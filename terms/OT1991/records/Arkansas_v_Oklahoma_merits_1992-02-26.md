## Event and chronology

Arkansas v. Oklahoma; No. 90-1262; No. 90-1266 (EPA v. Oklahoma); OT1991. Historical reporter anchor: 503 U.S. 91 (1992). Merits decision, 1992-02-26. Argued December 11, 1991. On writs of certiorari to the Tenth Circuit, 908 F.2d 595. Both petitions challenge the judgment invalidating EPA’s federal discharge permit for Fayetteville’s sewage-treatment plant.

Questions: Whether EPA may condition this upstream federal permit on downstream state standards; whether existing downstream degradation categorically bars the discharge; and whether EPA’s compliance interpretation and supported technical findings survive judicial review.

Relief sought and material record: Fayetteville applied in 1985 for a permit; effluent travels through creeks and the Illinois River for about 39 miles to Oklahoma. The permit includes numerical conditions and modification if a study requires tighter limits for Oklahoma compliance. EPA’s Chief Judicial Officer replaced an undue-impact test with a preponderance showing of no actual detectable violation. The ALJ made component-specific findings meeting that direction, sustained by EPA. Arkansas contests an obligatory downstream rule; EPA defends its authority and actual determination; Oklahoma seeks protection of scenic-water standards. The Tenth Circuit imposed an already-degraded-waters prohibition not advanced by a party, without displacing the no-detectable-violation finding. The separate White River discharge is outside review.

Stage before: plenary merits review. Stage after: Supreme Court review ends in both dockets. EPA’s permit determination is sustained with its existing compliance, study and modification conditions. ## Law of the decision

### EPA may require downstream standards in this federal permit

**Holding and operative rule:** The Clean Water Act authorizes EPA, in issuing this federal permit under §402(a), to require compliance with applicable downstream state water-quality standards through its valid regulations. A downstream State’s limited direct role in permitting does not restrict that federal authority; whether the Act itself commands identical compliance in every permitting setting remains open.

**Authority:** Stevens’s opinion, joined by all other Justices; unanimous statutory-authority holding.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that EPA acted within Congress’s delegation when it protected Oklahoma’s water standards through the federal permit. Section 402(a)(2) gives the Administrator substantial authority to prescribe permit conditions, and §301(b)(1)(C) identifies attainment of applicable water-quality standards as a statutory objective. Regulation §122.4(d), requiring assurance of affected-state compliance, reasonably carries out that scheme. Milwaukee II identifies the Act’s displacement of federal nuisance law, while Ouellette limits an affected State’s independent common-law and direct permitting role. Neither case bars the federal agency from imposing conditions Congress authorized. Arkansas’s contrary argument confuses limits on a State’s own authority with limits on EPA’s delegated power. Chevron supports this reasonable exercise of that power; expertise alone would not create it. Because the actual federal permit lies within the authorized approach, the Court does not decide whether Congress compels the same formulation in every federal or state permit, or whether an independent source-state remedy applies.

**Precedent treatment:**

- *Milwaukee v. Illinois*, 451 U.S. 304 — preserved: the statutory water-pollution scheme displaces federal nuisance common law.
- *International Paper Co. v. Ouellette*, 479 U.S. 481 — distinguished: affected-state common-law and direct-input limits do not constrain this delegated EPA condition.
- *Chevron U.S.A. Inc. v. Natural Resources Defense Council, Inc.*, 467 U.S. 837 — applied within Congress’s actual delegation; no overhaul of agency deference.
- *Lechmere, Inc. v. NLRB*, simulated January 27, 1992 — preserved: deference operates within controlling statutory precedent, and no contrary construction is displaced here.

### Existing degradation is not a categorical statutory prohibition

**Holding and operative rule:** The Clean Water Act does not categorically forbid a new discharge merely because its effluent will reach waters already failing a water-quality standard, irrespective of whether it will cause a detectable violation under the applicable permit standards. Courts may not substitute that categorical prohibition for the Act’s actual permitting and pollution-reduction scheme.

**Authority:** Stevens’s unanimous opinion rejects the independent statutory basis on which the court of appeals invalidated the permit.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that the court of appeals added a prohibition Congress did not enact. The Act contains duties directed toward meeting water-quality standards and provisions for reducing existing pollution, including §303(d)’s framework for allocating reduction burdens. It does not say that every additional source must be barred whenever receiving waters already are degraded. Section 402(h), on which the lower court relied, concerns additional pollutants accepted by a treatment plant violating its own permit; it does not create the broader downstream prohibition. Milwaukee II’s recognition of a comprehensive statutory program is not authority to replace that program with a court’s preferred environmental policy. Existing degradation remains relevant when the governing standards make it so, but it does not eliminate the need to evaluate the proposed discharge under those standards. EPA applied the required no-detectable-violation approach here. The Court neither excuses a detectable violation nor decides later permit conditions on facts outside this record.

**Precedent treatment:**

- *Milwaukee v. Illinois*, 451 U.S. 304 — its description of a comprehensive program does not authorize an additional judicial categorical ban.

### Reasonable construction of the incorporated downstream standards

**Holding and operative rule:** For this interstate federal permit, EPA’s valid regulation incorporates the applicable, federally approved downstream standards into federal permitting law, and its reasonable, consistently held construction receives substantial deference. EPA permissibly construed Oklahoma’s no-degradation requirement to prohibit an actual detectable or measurable violation, rather than every infinitesimal theoretical effect.

**Authority:** Stevens’s unanimous opinion controls the interpretation of the standards as used in this federal permit; it does not decide state law for unrelated purposes.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that the reviewing court must respect EPA’s permissible construction of the standards governing its federal permit. The standards were developed with federal guidance, approved by EPA and incorporated through Regulation §122.4(d); in this interstate permitting setting, they operate as part of federal water-pollution law. Chevron and the received regulatory-deference principle reflected in Seminole Rock support deference to a reasonable, consistently held interpretation within that framework. Requiring a detectable or measurable violation provides a means to determine compliance and does not make every theoretical molecule of additional effluent a downstream veto. NCIR’s source-specific acceptance of a supported regulatory construction is consistent with that approach. The contrary categorical reading lacks support in the statutory or regulatory text. The Court preserves actual standards and their enforcement: it does not announce a general exemption for pollution described as trivial, excuse unreliable science, or confer authority to disregard a demonstrated detectable violation.

**Precedent treatment:**

- *Chevron U.S.A. Inc. v. Natural Resources Defense Council, Inc.*, 467 U.S. 837 — applied to the authorized federal permitting construction.
- *Bowles v. Seminole Rock & Sand Co.*, 325 U.S. 410 — its received regulatory-interpretation principle remains operative within valid delegated authority.
- *INS v. National Center for Immigrants’ Rights, Inc.*, simulated December 16, 1991 — consistent source-specific regulatory construction is applied without importing immigration rules.

### Supported findings govern review of this permit

**Holding and operative rule:** A reviewing court must accept agency adjudicative findings supported by substantial evidence on the record as a whole and may not replace them merely because competing findings also could be supported. Here EPA’s supported finding that Fayetteville carried the administrative preponderance burden of no actual detectable violation sustains the permit; the court’s contrary policy does not establish arbitrary agency action.

**Authority:** Stevens’s unanimous opinion controls both the appellate-review rule and its application to the reviewed permit.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that the permit survives review on the record actually decided by EPA. Universal Camera requires substantial evidence supporting the agency’s findings on the whole record, not evidence from which the reviewing court could make different findings. The ALJ addressed nutrient enrichment, appearance, dissolved oxygen and metals under the corrected no-detectable-violation standard and found the city’s preponderance burden satisfied. The identified errors concerning phosphorus in streams and the standards’ edition are harmless here: the findings addressed the more vulnerable lake, and the relevant editions did not materially differ. State Farm requires attention to important aspects of the problem, but the lower court made existing degradation decisive only by adopting its erroneous categorical rule. It could not then label EPA arbitrary for failing to follow that invented rule. The existing study and modification conditions remain safeguards for further information. No new discharge limit, scientific threshold or finding about later water quality is imposed.

**Precedent treatment:**

- *Universal Camera Corp. v. NLRB*, 340 U.S. 474 — applied: supported agency findings are not displaced by merely permissible alternative findings.
- *Motor Vehicle Manufacturers Association v. State Farm Mutual Automobile Insurance Co.*, 463 U.S. 29 — applied to the actual governing problem, not a court-created categorical policy.

## B. JUDGMENT AND REMEDY

* **Stone's Controlled Judgment or Disposition:** Reverse the Tenth Circuit in both Nos. 90-1262 and 90-1266.
* **Stone's Remedy and Remand Position:** Remove the categorical already-degraded-waters bar and sustain the reviewed permit determination under its existing compliance and modification conditions. Do not prescribe new numerical limits or adjudicate the unrelated White River discharge.

## C. ISSUE POSITIONS

### Issue 1: Enforce the delegated interstate safeguards

* **Threshold and Merits Reach:** Decide authority for EPA's actual conditions and the court-imposed prohibition.
* **Question Stone Resolves:** Whether EPA was authorized to use the downstream-compliance approach here, not whether every permit must use an identical formulation.
* **Stone's Legal Position:** Apply the enacted scheme and entering administrative doctrine. The sources independently establish EPA's authority, so no overhaul of Chevron or regulatory deference is necessary.
* **Stone's Proposed Holding:** EPA may impose downstream state water-quality compliance through this federal permit; the Act does not supply the Tenth Circuit's categorical prohibition irrespective of detectable violation.
* **Essential Reasoning and Record Application:** Interstate effluent predictably crosses boundaries, and Congress provided a federal scheme in which affected-state standards and participation constrain permits. Recognizing those authorized conditions respects the downstream State without converting its common law into direct extraterritorial control. The scheme does not say that existing degradation automatically forbids every additional source regardless of the permit's demonstrated effect. A court cannot manufacture that prohibition from a broad environmental goal after the agency has applied the governing standards. This conclusion depends on enacted authority, not an assumption that technical expertise creates power.
* **Boundary or Reserved Question:** Reserve mandatory compliance in all conceivable settings and any distinct source-state tort remedy preserved by law.

### Issue 2: Respect supported technical findings within the rule

* **Threshold and Merits Reach:** Reach the administrative determination necessary to resolve the reviewed permit.
* **Stone's Legal Position:** Apply the existing proof requirement and whole-record review; change neither burden nor scientific threshold.
* **Stone's Proposed Holding:** The supported no-detectable-violation determination cannot be displaced by the reviewing court's separate categorical policy judgment.
* **Essential Reasoning and Record Application:** EPA corrected the initial undue-impact formulation and required the city to carry a preponderance burden concerning actual detectable violations. The ALJ made component-specific findings meeting that instruction. The Tenth Circuit did not identify an evidentiary defect sufficient to overturn them. Technical uncertainty still matters through the permit's study and modification provisions; it does not authorize a court to invent a different statutory prohibition.
* **Boundary or Reserved Question:** No blanket tolerance for pollution labeled de minimis; detectable violations, unreliable findings and failure to apply valid standards remain reviewable.
