## Controlling holdings

### The original categorical prohibition presents a reviewable earlier-period claim

**Holding and operative rule.** A later discretionary permit procedure does not bar review of compensation for an earlier categorical building prohibition where permission under that prohibition was unavailable and the state supreme court finally rejected the compensation claim on its merits. Any compensable taking must be remedied for its actual duration; the later procedure and unresolved factual issues remain relevant to future use, duration and valuation, and the permanent-value award is not automatically reinstated.

**Authority.** Scalia's Opinion of the Court, Part II, joined by Stone-Zsela, White, O'Connor and Thomas, controls with five votes. Kennedy separately concurs in the judgment and agrees that the earlier interval is reviewable, but does not join the Court's writing. The holding rests on the express five-Justice opinion without combining separate rationales.

**Controlling explanation.** The Court holds that a new opportunity to seek future permission cannot eliminate a completed deprivation from judicial review. Williamson County and MacDonald ordinarily require a final determination of permitted development so that a court knows the restriction it must assess. Here the Council stipulated that the original Act allowed no building permit, and the state supreme court rejected compensation on a categorical merits ground after the amendment existed. First English establishes that ending a restriction does not eliminate compensation for a period in which it effected a taking. The Council's request for further permitting therefore cannot dispose of the earlier interval. Yee preserves the difference between a distinct unpresented claim and an argument supporting the claim actually decided; the earlier deprivation belongs to the compensation dispute already presented. These conclusions do not establish that a taking occurred or fix its value. The Court leaves the new permit avenue, the extent and duration of actual loss, and the defenses the state supreme court did not decide for further proceedings.

**Precedent treatment.**

- Williamson County Regional Planning Commission v. Hamilton Bank, 473 U.S. 172 (1985): preserves ordinary finality requirements; distinguishes an absolute prohibition for an already elapsed period.
- MacDonald, Sommer & Frates v. Yolo County, 477 U.S. 340 (1986): preserves the need to determine permitted development; an unavailable original permit need not be sought to test the earlier prohibition.
- First English Evangelical Lutheran Church v. County of Los Angeles, 482 U.S. 304 (1987): applies compensation for an actual temporary taking; does not decide whether this regulation effected one or set the amount.
- Yee v. City of Escondido, simulated April 1, 1992: applies the distinction between a presented claim and a distinct unpresented taking theory; no new physical-taking rule follows.

### Complete economic deprivation requires compensation unless background ownership law already foreclosed the use

**Holding and operative rule.** A land-use regulation that removes all economically beneficial use of the relevant property effects a taking requiring just compensation unless the prohibited use was already outside the owner's title under background state property or nuisance law. After the owner establishes complete deprivation, the State must identify and substantiate an applicable background limitation that would independently prohibit the claimed use in the property's circumstances; a new legislative harm declaration alone does not suffice, and ordinary adjudication supplies the proof requirements without a special heightened quantum.

**Authority.** Scalia's Opinion of the Court, Part III, joined by Stone-Zsela, White, O'Connor and Thomas, supplies five votes for the complete rule and its explanation. Kennedy supports reversal through an expectations-sensitive analysis, without joining this categorical formulation. No Marks combination is required or used.

**Controlling explanation.** The Court holds that public authority to restrict land and responsibility to pay for its complete economic loss are separate questions. Pennsylvania Coal recognizes that otherwise valid regulation can impose a compensable taking. Agins supplies the complete-use formulation, which the Court now specifies as a categorical rule with an ownership limitation. Penn Central continues to govern ordinary partial burdens. A purpose-only exemption would let the State describe the same sacrifice as preventing harm or conferring a benefit and thereby determine its own compensation duty. Mugler and the older noxious-use cases do not establish that exemption for proved elimination of all beneficial land use. Roth and Scranton explain why the owner's entitlement remains bounded by existing law: prohibiting an entitlement never held takes nothing. The trial finding supplies the premise of this review, but does not settle the Council's preserved factual objections. South Carolina must identify genuine background principles and their application on remand. Changed knowledge may affect nuisance law's application; a new legislative label cannot replace it. The Court decides no parcel-definition rule or percentage shortcut.

**Precedent treatment.**

- Pennsylvania Coal Co. v. Mahon, 260 U.S. 393 (1922): applies its recognition that regulation can go too far despite valid public purposes; does not attribute today's exact categorical rule to that decision.
- Agins v. City of Tiburon, 447 U.S. 255 (1980): specifies and extends its complete-economic-use formulation into the rule and necessary exception stated here; does not adjudicate its distinct legitimate-interest formulation.
- Penn Central Transportation Co. v. New York City, 438 U.S. 104 (1978): preserves contextual analysis for partial deprivation, including economic impact, expectations and the character of governmental action; no percentage automatically defeats or establishes compensation.
- Mugler v. Kansas, 123 U.S. 623 (1887), Hadacheck v. Sebastian, 239 U.S. 394 (1915), Miller v. Schoene, 276 U.S. 272 (1928), and Goldblatt v. Hempstead, 369 U.S. 590 (1962): limits a broad noxious-use reading that would make any legislative harm characterization conclusive despite complete beneficial-use deprivation; preserves their actual outcomes and lawful protective regulation.
- Keystone Bituminous Coal Association v. DeBenedictis, 480 U.S. 470 (1987): distinguishes its productive-enterprise and partial-interest setting; no reversal of its actual holding or adoption of a new denominator follows.
- Board of Regents v. Roth, 408 U.S. 564 (1972): applies existing rules and understandings to identify the protected property interest; does not decide a procedural due-process claim.
- Scranton v. Wheeler, 179 U.S. 141 (1900): applies its antecedent-title-limit principle; a previously existing servitude is distinct from a newly imposed public burden.
- Loretto v. Teleprompter Manhattan CATV Corp., 458 U.S. 419 (1982), and Kaiser Aetna v. United States, 444 U.S. 164 (1979): preserve physical-occupation and exclusion protections and illuminate the difference between existing limitations and a new imposed servitude; this decision concerns regulatory loss.
- Webb's Fabulous Pharmacies, Inc. v. Beckwith, 449 U.S. 155 (1980): applies the prohibition on converting private property into public property by governmental assertion alone.

**Limits and questions not reached.** The Court assumes the trial court's total-loss finding to decide the presented legal issue; it makes no new appraisal and does not resolve the Council's preserved challenge to that finding. No unconditional entitlement to the trial award, final application of state nuisance law, right to anticipated profits, new parcel rule, or disposition of post-amendment permission is adopted. Legitimate environmental purposes remain sufficient to justify regulation; compensation is a distinct consequence. The Court overrules no earlier decision.

## Published noncontrolling positions and continuity

Kennedy agrees that the earlier interval is reviewable and reversal appropriate, but would test complete-loss restrictions against objectively reasonable investment-backed expectations drawn from the whole legal tradition. Common-law nuisance alone is too narrow, particularly for vulnerable coastal land; actual ability and intent to develop and the duration of loss remain for remand. Blackmun disputes finality and the unsupported premise of complete economic extinction, and rejects the categorical rule in favor of the protective public purpose and contextual precedent.
