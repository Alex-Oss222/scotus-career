## Law of the decision

**Holding and operative rule:** Section 2674 bars damages whose legal nature is punishment for culpable wrongdoing, not every compensatory award alleged to be excessive, duplicative, or difficult for the injured person to perceive. Rejecting the federal bar does not establish entitlement under the governing state law or determine an amount.

**Authority:** Thomas's unanimous opinion for all nine Justices.

**Controlling explanation:** The FTCA generally makes the United States liable like a private person under the law of the place while withholding “punitive damages.” Congress used an established tort term associated with punishment for culpable misconduct. *Morissette* supports reading borrowed legal language against that settled background, and *Massachusetts Bonding* confirms the distinction between punitive recovery and compensation in explaining the Act's special wrongful-death provision. The Government's proposed “punitive effect” test would authorize federal courts to revise state compensation rules whenever an award seems excessive or imperfectly tied to perceived loss, without statutory criteria. Its concession that compensation may be reasonably approximate underscores the absence of a workable boundary. These claims arise from admitted simple negligence and seek categories labeled compensatory under state law; alleged duplication or unconsciousness does not alone change their legal nature. Wisconsin law must still determine whether future care, lost enjoyment, survival, and offsets permit any recovery. The Court neither authorizes punitive payment nor directs an award.

**Precedent treatment:** *Massachusetts Bonding*, *Morissette*, *Muniz*, *Richards*, and *Sheridan* are applied and preserved.

**Law after decision:** The FTCA punitive-damages exclusion uses the traditional punitive category; state-law entitlement and federal classification remain distinct.
