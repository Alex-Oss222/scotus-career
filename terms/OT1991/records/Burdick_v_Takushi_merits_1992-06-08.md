Certiorari to the United States Court of Appeals for the Ninth Circuit, reviewing its replacement opinion filed June 28, 1991, 937 F.2d 415. The March 1, 1991 opinion at 927 F.2d 469 was withdrawn. Hawaii’s Supreme Court construed state law on July 21, 1989 to prohibit write-ins. The District of Hawaii entered summary judgment and an injunction for Burdick on May 10, 1990, 737 F.Supp. 582; that injunction was stayed and the Ninth Circuit reversed.

The questions are whether Hawaii’s complete prohibition violates First and Fourteenth Amendment electoral-choice rights when considered within its access system, and whether the Constitution independently requires an official tally of arbitrary protest votes. Burdick seeks prospective relief from the prohibition; Hawaii seeks affirmance. The Court reaches primary and general-election applications within the challenged system, but no independent challenge to every surrounding access rule. The lower court rejected standing and certification/full-faith-and-credit objections; no supplied defect prevents certiorari review under 28 U.S.C. § 1254(1).

In 1986 only one candidate filed for Burdick’s legislative district, and he wished to vote for someone who had not filed. Hawaii requires new parties to obtain signatures from one percent of registered voters 150 days before the primary; qualifying established parties retain access. Party and nonpartisan candidates file 15 signatures for legislative or county office, or 25 for statewide or federal office, 60 days before the primary. A nonpartisan candidate advances with ten percent of the primary vote or the vote sufficient to nominate a partisan candidate, whichever is lower. A voter chooses one party or nonpartisan primary ballot for all offices. These combined routes and restrictions frame the constitutional burden; the Court does not separately adjudicate every access provision.

## Controlling holdings

### 1. Review of election regulations follows the actual burden

**Holding and operative rule.** A court reviewing a restriction on voting or candidate access must weigh the character and magnitude of the injury to First and Fourteenth Amendment rights against the State’s precise asserted interests and the extent to which those interests require the burden. Severe restrictions must be narrowly drawn to advance a compelling interest; reasonable, nondiscriminatory restrictions ordinarily are justified by important regulatory interests, so strict scrutiny does not apply to every election rule.

**Authority.** Stone-Zsela’s Opinion of the Court, joined by Blackmun, Stevens, Kennedy, and Souter, supplies five votes for the complete rule and controls. White’s dissent, joined by O’Connor, Scalia, and Thomas, also accepts this calibrated framework; its agreement does not make its contrary application controlling.

**Controlling explanation.** The Court holds that constitutional review must account for the regulation and the election system in which it operates. Anderson v. Celebrezze supplies the inquiry into the actual injury, the precise state interests, and the need for the particular burden. Norman v. Reed preserves that inquiry while recognizing that States may require support in the electorate a candidate or party seeks to represent. Storer v. Brown explains why orderly elections require substantial regulation of candidate selection. Treating every restriction as severe would disable the primary procedures and qualification rules those decisions permit. The claimed injury here therefore cannot be measured simply by observing that Hawaii forbids write-ins. The Court must examine the other means of obtaining access and the function the prohibition serves within that system. This approach preserves demanding review when access is severely restricted and meaningful scrutiny when a lesser burden is asserted; it does not make ordinary administration immune from constitutional review.

**Precedent treatment.**

- Anderson v. Celebrezze, 460 U.S. 780 (1983): applied as the governing burden, interest, and necessity inquiry; no categorical strict-scrutiny rule replaces it.
- Norman v. Reed (January 14, 1992): applied for actual access review and permissible electorate-specific support requirements; its protection against cross-component exclusion remains effective.
- Storer v. Brown, 415 U.S. 724 (1974): applied for the State’s authority to organize candidate selection; severe barriers remain reviewable.

### 2. Hawaii’s complete write-in prohibition leaves no effective route for a genuine alternative candidate

**Holding and operative rule.** When a State closes its ordinary nomination routes before voters can respond to the actual field of candidates, it must leave an effective route by which voters may support a genuine, legally eligible alternative candidate. A reasonable registered write-in procedure is one such route; the State may require eligibility, the candidate’s consent and declaration, and a reasonable registration date. Hawaii’s complete prohibition, operating after filing closes 60 days before the primary and alongside an all-office primary-ballot choice and nonpartisan advancement conditions, leaves no such route, and its asserted interests do not justify that full reach. The electoral-choice challenge succeeds as applied to genuine, legally eligible candidates.

**Authority.** Stone-Zsela’s Opinion of the Court, joined by Blackmun, Stevens, Kennedy, and Souter, controls with five votes. White, O’Connor, Scalia, and Thomas disagree with this rule, its application and the resulting judgment. This is a majority rationale; Marks analysis is unnecessary.

**Controlling explanation.** The Court holds that Hawaii’s prohibition imposes a substantial burden within its system. Fifteen signatures make initial filing accessible, a fact favoring Hawaii, but every ordinary route closes before the primary, and the all-office primary-ballot choice and advancement conditions govern how an alternative reaches the election that chooses the officeholder. Burdick faced an actual single-candidate contest and wished to support someone else. Anderson v. Celebrezze therefore requires asking what the complete ban itself contributes to the State’s interests; the independent validity of the surrounding rules cannot answer that question. Storer v. Brown supports preventing sore-loser candidacies, but that interest cannot explain a primary ban and reaches only some general-election candidates. Rosario v. Rockefeller permits timely measures against raiding, yet nothing here explains barring genuine support for a candidate on the selected primary ticket. An uncontested-winner rule leaves no general-election ballot to protect. Munro v. Socialist Workers Party excuses proof of past disruption, not a showing of fit. Hawaii keeps its deadlines, support rules and qualifications; it must only leave an effective route for genuine eligible candidates. The Constitution guarantees neither a competitive contest nor every late candidacy.

**Precedent treatment.**

- Anderson v. Celebrezze, 460 U.S. 780 (1983): applied to the complete Hawaii system; the challenged prohibition’s own contribution to the asserted interests must be examined, and no new scrutiny tier is created.
- Storer v. Brown, 415 U.S. 724 (1974): applied; primary finality and sore-loser prevention remain legitimate interests and reasonable selection rules remain valid, but they do not justify this prohibition’s full reach.
- Jenness v. Fortson, 403 U.S. 431 (1971): applied to permit reasonable candidate-support requirements; their validity does not make a separate prohibition presumptively valid.
- Munro v. Socialist Workers Party, 479 U.S. 189 (1986): applied; preventive regulation needs no proof of a particular past disruption, but it must still fit the interest it serves.
- Rosario v. Rockefeller, 410 U.S. 752 (1973): applied; timely measures against party raiding remain available, but it does not justify excluding genuine support for a candidate on the selected primary ticket.
- Lubin v. Panish, 415 U.S. 709 (1974): consistent; a write-in option is neither an automatic cure for a defective printed-ballot scheme nor the only route that satisfies this holding.

### 3. The ballot need not serve as an official tally of arbitrary political messages

**Holding and operative rule.** The First and Fourteenth Amendments do not require the State to count and publicize write-in votes for fictional figures or arbitrary protest messages as an independent form of political expression. Elections select public officials, and a desire to use the ballot for such messages does not establish a constitutional entitlement to a write-in option.

**Authority.** Stone-Zsela’s Opinion of the Court, joined by Blackmun, Stevens, Kennedy, and Souter, controls. White’s dissent, joined by O’Connor, Scalia, and Thomas, expressly agrees in rejecting this broader claim. All nine reject this component, although four would deny relief on genuine electoral choice as well.

**Controlling explanation.** The Court holds that the ballot’s purpose limits the claimed expressive entitlement. Voting carries political meaning, but an official election need not become a general forum in which the State records and disseminates any message a voter wishes to send. Storer v. Brown recognizes elections as a means of selecting officeholders through an orderly process, and the access inquiry under Anderson v. Celebrezze protects the ability to participate in that process. Neither principle requires an official tally for fictional figures or arbitrary statements. Burdick’s interest in expressing dissatisfaction therefore does not itself invalidate the prohibition. His distinct interest in supporting an actual alternative candidate is protected by the preceding holding, and the relief it supports is confined to votes for genuine, legally eligible candidates. Rejecting the broader theory does not diminish political expression outside the ballot.

**Precedent treatment.**

- Storer v. Brown, 415 U.S. 724 (1974): applied to the office-selecting function of elections; the ballot is not a general expressive forum.
- Anderson v. Celebrezze, 460 U.S. 780 (1983): distinguished from the demand for an arbitrary-message tally; meaningful electoral participation remains protected.

## Law after the decision and reserved questions

Effective June 8, 1992, Anderson’s calibrated review assesses a write-in prohibition by its own contribution to the State’s interests within the complete access system; the validity of surrounding access rules does not make the prohibition presumptively valid. When a State closes its ordinary nomination routes before voters can respond, it must leave an effective route for genuine, legally eligible alternative candidates. A reasonable registered write-in procedure is one such route, and the State may require eligibility, consent, declaration and a reasonable registration date. Hawaii’s complete prohibition is invalid as applied to such candidates in the reviewed system. No absolute right to a write-in option, arbitrary-message tally, competitive contest or every late candidacy exists, and no new scrutiny tier is created.

Norman’s protection against unjustified name and cross-component disqualification survives; Hawaii’s write-in prohibition presents a different application. Burson’s strict-scrutiny proposition concerns campaign-content restrictions on ordinary public sidewalks and is neither extended to all ballot rules nor displaced. Burson’s fractured judgment does not supply a controlling validity exception here. No precedent is overruled, limited in force beyond these distinctions, or restored after displacement. Candidate qualifications, the design of a qualifying route, narrow anti-raiding and sore-loser restrictions, other States’ materially different systems, and independent challenges to other Hawaii provisions remain open.

## Published separate writings and complete noncontrolling continuity

Kennedy, joined by Blackmun and Stevens, joins the Court’s opinion in full and concurs. He considers the total prohibition a substantial burden in the actual nomination system: low signature requirements do not answer the need to select one party or nonpartisan primary ballot for all offices, the advancement rules, or the inability to support a real alternative in an unopposed contest, and a presumption derived from the surrounding rules’ validity cannot sustain the ban. Sore-loser prevention does not explain a primary ban and reaches only some general-election candidates; party-raiding and uncontested-primary interests do not justify the prohibition’s entire reach. He would describe the available relief more broadly: if Hawaii provides no qualifying route in time for an upcoming election, the lower courts may relieve votes for real, legally eligible persons from the prohibition itself, leaving eligibility to ordinary canvassing rather than awaiting a registration procedure. His position does not establish a general constitutional write-in entitlement or an arbitrary-message tally.

White, joined by O’Connor, Scalia, and Thomas, dissents. He accepts calibrated Anderson review and agrees in rejecting the arbitrary-message claim, but would affirm. Where the complete ballot-access system imposes only reasonable burdens, he would treat a write-in prohibition as presumptively valid and sustainable by proportionate interests. Small signature requirements and party and nonpartisan routes permit access before the election; Jenness permits reasonable demonstrations of support, Munro permits primary winnowing without proof of a particular past disruption, and Storer supports the primary’s role in settling candidacies. In his view the prohibition supports primary finality, prevents candidacies that would evade the selection process and primary raiding, and complements the designation of certain unopposed primary victors, and the Constitution does not require the State to accommodate every preference arising after its lawful selection deadlines. He would not decide whether the same prohibition would survive materially more restrictive access rules.

Both writings are noncontrolling. The dissenters’ agreement on contextual scrutiny and rejection of general protest expression is identified in the Holdings block; Kennedy’s broader remedial view does not enlarge the Court’s remedy.
