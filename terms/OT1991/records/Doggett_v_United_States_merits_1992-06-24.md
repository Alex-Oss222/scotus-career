## Material record and lower judgments

Doggett was indicted on February 22, 1980 on conspiracy charges. His one-count conditional conviction is the judgment under review. He left before attempted arrest, was detained in Panama, went to Colombia and returned on September 25, 1982. He thereafter lived openly under his own name. Investigators failed to verify increasingly doubtful assumptions about his foreign whereabouts; a routine credit check located him in 1988, and he was arrested September 5. The accepted findings establish negligent pursuit and ignorance of the indictment before arrest. He asserted the right after arrest. The Eleventh Circuit required actual prejudice unless its first three Barker factors weighed heavily, and treated negligence as insufficiently heavy without bad faith; Clark dissented. Claims of lost tape evidence and lost youthful-offender treatment did not establish particularized prejudice. The Court's relief does not depend on finding those claims proved or attributing every foreign period to American fault.

## Law of the decision — controlling holdings

#### An outstanding indictment protects fair adjudication before arrest

**Holding and operative rule:** Once an indictment or other official accusation triggers the Sixth Amendment, the speedy-trial inquiry must consider impairment of the defense even if the accused remains free and unaware of the charge. Absence of incarceration or anxiety eliminates those possible injuries on this record, but it does not exclude defense impairment from the Barker balance.

**Authority:** Part II of Souter's opinion for the Court, joined by Stone-Zsela, White, Blackmun, Stevens and Kennedy; six Justices adopt the scope rule.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that an outstanding indictment brings the fairness of the eventual trial within the speedy-trial guarantee. Barker identifies defense impairment as a protected interest distinct from detention and anxiety, and Moore rejects a rigid insistence upon affirmative proof of trial prejudice. The Government's narrower account would remove that protection whenever an accused remained unaware and unrestricted, although the pending prosecution could still erode his ability to defend himself. Marion addresses delay before formal accusation; MacDonald and Loud Hawk address periods outside an operative prosecution and the consequences of justified proceedings. Those decisions do not erase Barker's protection after accusation. Doggett's indictment remained pending throughout the delay, so his defense interest must enter the balance even though two other kinds of injury are absent. The Court does not constitutionalize a limitations period running from the offense, decide an independent right to repose, or withdraw due process protection against unfair delay before or after accusation.

**Precedent treatment:**

- Barker v. Wingo, 407 U.S. 514 — applied: fair adjudication is an independent protected interest in the existing four-factor inquiry.
- Moore v. Arizona, 414 U.S. 25 — applied: affirmative proof of trial prejudice is not indispensable in every claim.
- United States v. Marion, 404 U.S. 307 — distinguished as to preaccusation delay; its accusation boundary remains effective.
- United States v. MacDonald, 456 U.S. 1 — distinguished as to periods after dismissal; its separate due process protection is preserved.
- United States v. Loud Hawk, 474 U.S. 302 — distinguished as to absence of an operative prosecution and justified interlocutory delay; no categorical restriction to custody or anxiety is adopted.

#### Extraordinary negligent delay and unrebutted impairment require dismissal here

**Holding and operative rule:** Under Barker, extraordinary post-indictment delay caused by sustained governmental negligence can violate the Sixth Amendment without identified trial prejudice when the accused did not knowingly acquiesce and the resulting inference of defense impairment is not persuasively rebutted. The eight-and-one-half-year delay here, including approximately six avoidable years while Doggett lived openly, establishes that violation; Strunk requires dismissal of the indictment and vacatur of the conditional conviction, not a sentence adjustment.

**Authority:** Part III of Souter's opinion for the Court, joined by Stone-Zsela, White, Blackmun, Stevens and Kennedy; six Justices adopt the rule, application and dismissal consequence.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that the Government's prolonged neglect cannot be excused merely because Doggett cannot identify everything time has destroyed. Barker first requires delay sufficient to prompt examination and then weighs its duration, reasons, assertion of the right and prejudice together. Its warning that forgotten evidence can rarely be identified explains why presumptive impairment matters beyond the initial threshold. Negligence weighs less heavily than deliberate obstruction, but its weight grows when it persists for years without adequate justification. The accepted findings establish that ordinary investigation could have located Doggett while he lived openly, and that he did not know of the indictment before arrest. His prompt assertion therefore supplies no knowing acquiescence. Moore forecloses treating particularized prejudice as an extra mandatory element. The Government's answers to specific allegations of harm do not persuasively rebut the accumulated inference here. Diligent pursuit, justified obstacles, evasion or materially shorter neglect may yield another result. Strunk supplies dismissal once this constitutional violation is established; no fixed deadline or general transfer of the ultimate constitutional burden is announced.

**Precedent treatment:**

- Barker v. Wingo, 407 U.S. 514 — applied and specified for extraordinary negligent delay; the threshold inquiry remains distinct from the ultimate balance and knowing acquiescence remains relevant.
- Moore v. Arizona, 414 U.S. 25 — applied against a categorical actual-prejudice prerequisite.
- United States v. Loud Hawk, 474 U.S. 302 — preserved for the significance of justified delay and the insufficiency of possible prejudice considered alone.
- Strunk v. United States, 412 U.S. 434 — applied: dismissal and vacatur, rather than sentence credit or a new trial, implement an established speedy-trial violation.

#### The express conditional plea preserves effective review

**Holding and operative rule:** A guilty plea entered under Federal Rule of Criminal Procedure 11(a)(2), with the Government's express consent to reservation of the adverse speedy-trial ruling, preserves that claim and its defense-impairment argument for appellate review. Doggett's plea does not waive the claimed consequence of unconstitutional delay merely because he ultimately admitted guilt.

**Authority:** Part I of Souter's opinion for the Court, joined by Stone-Zsela, White, Blackmun, Stevens and Kennedy; six Justices adopt this threshold application.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that the Government must honor the appellate reservation it accepted. Rule 11(a)(2) permits a conditional plea preserving review of an adverse pretrial ruling. Doggett's agreement expressly reserved the denial of his motion to dismiss for post-indictment constitutional delay, and the Government consented to that reservation. The impairment claim was part of that motion. Broce addresses the consequences of an ordinary guilty plea; it does not transform an expressly conditional plea into an unconditional surrender of the very issue reserved. Accepting the Government's position would permit an appeal in name while disabling the principal argument supporting it. The Court therefore reviews the preserved speedy-trial issue as effectively as it could have been reviewed after trial. This application does not excuse an unreserved objection or reopen the factual admissions establishing guilt. Relief follows because the preserved constitutional claim succeeds and Strunk requires the conviction to be set aside with dismissal of the prosecution.

**Precedent treatment:**

- Federal Rule of Criminal Procedure 11(a)(2) — applied according to the express reservation and prosecutorial consent; no new exception to plea waiver.
- United States v. Broce, 488 U.S. 563 — distinguished: an unconditional plea's consequences do not defeat this expressly preserved issue.
- Strunk v. United States, 412 U.S. 434 — applied to the consequence of success on the preserved constitutional claim.

## Current law, limits and reserved questions

Effective June 24, 1992, Barker's existing four-factor balance expressly reaches extraordinary negligent post-indictment delay with unrebutted presumptive defense impairment despite an accused's freedom and ignorance. The threshold delay needed to trigger inquiry is distinct from the growing weight of presumed impairment in the ultimate balance. Negligence remains less culpable than deliberate obstruction but weighs increasingly against the Government as unjustified delay persists. No fixed time limit, automatic presumption of constitutional violation, universal prosecutorial burden transfer, rule governing preaccusation delay or independent right of repose is created. Strunk's dismissal consequence and Rule 11(a)(2)'s express reservation operate without change. Six Justices support the Court's entire opinion; no fractured-decision or Marks issue arises.

### Material published noncontrolling positions
