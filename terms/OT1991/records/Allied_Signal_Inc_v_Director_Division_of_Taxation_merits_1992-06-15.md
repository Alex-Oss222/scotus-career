## Controlling holdings

### Holding 1: The unitary-business limitation and operational-asset route remain governing law

**Holding and operative rule.** A State taxing a nondomiciliary corporation may apportion income connected to its unitary business, but may not include discrete, unrelated investment value merely because the corporation does business in the State or owns the asset. A unitary relationship between income payor and recipient is sufficient but not necessary: an income-producing asset or capital transaction serving an operational function in the taxpayer's unitary business may qualify without that relationship, subject to the required territorial connection and fair apportionment.

**Authority.** Kennedy's Opinion of the Court, joined in full by White, Stevens, Scalia, and Souter; these five Justices support both the territorial limit and the operational qualification. Stone separately supports those principles subject to his stated reservations, and the dissent agrees with retention and an operational route; neither separate writing is needed to establish the Court opinion's controlling authority.

**Controlling explanation.** The Court holds that the power to tax a corporation does not establish power to tax every item it owns. Mobil Oil v. Commissioner of Taxes requires a connection with the State and a rational relationship between the attributed income and values there. ASARCO v. Idaho Tax Commission prevents a general corporate profit purpose from replacing that relationship; F. W. Woolworth v. Taxation and Revenue Department requires actual integration beyond investor oversight. Container Corporation v. Franchise Tax Board permits concrete transfers of operational value even without exchanges of goods. Those decisions allow States practical apportionment methods while preserving a territorial boundary. An asset serving the taxpayer's operations can satisfy that boundary even when its issuer belongs to a separate enterprise. Common ownership alone cannot. The established rule remains workable despite fact-sensitive disagreements, and businesses and States have organized investments and tax systems around it. The Court therefore declines to overrule ASARCO or Woolworth. Because neither is overruled, the conditional questions about retroactivity and replacement constitutional principles do not arise.

**Precedent treatment.**

- Mobil Oil Corp. v. Commissioner of Taxes, 445 U.S. 425 (1980): followed for the minimum connection, rational relationship, and unitary-business framework; a separate enterprise can remain outside the base.
- ASARCO Inc. v. Idaho Tax Commission, 458 U.S. 307 (June 29, 1982): reaffirmed; generalized corporate profit purpose does not establish a unitary connection, and capital gains receive the same constitutional treatment as dividends.
- F. W. Woolworth Co. v. Taxation and Revenue Department, 458 U.S. 354 (1982): reaffirmed; functional integration, centralized management and economies of scale remain practical indicia, while investor oversight alone is insufficient.
- Container Corp. of America v. Franchise Tax Board, 463 U.S. 159 (1983): followed and clarified; concrete operational value can support apportionment without a flow of goods or a unitary payor/payee relationship, but passive investment is insufficient.

### Holding 2: The stipulated record requires exclusion of the ASARCO gain

**Holding and operative rule.** Where the stipulated facts establish unrelated enterprises without functional integration, centralized management or economies of scale, and do not show that the stock itself performed an identifiable operational function in the taxpayer's unitary business, a general acquisition strategy and intended later use of sale proceeds cannot justify apportioning the capital gain. Bendix has met its burden on this record; New Jersey must exclude the approximately $211.5 million ASARCO gain from the challenged apportionable tax base.

**Authority.** Kennedy's Opinion of the Court, joined in full by White, Stevens, Scalia, and Souter; five Justices adopt this application and the resulting reversal and remand. Stone separately reaches the same disposition on his separately stated territorial/operational ground. Blackmun, O'Connor, and Thomas reject the application and would affirm.

**Controlling explanation.** The Court holds that the agreed facts exclude the ASARCO gain from this tax base. ASARCO v. Idaho Tax Commission supplies the rule that an investment's contribution to corporate profitability is insufficient, and applies the same constitutional boundary to capital gains and dividends. F. W. Woolworth v. Taxation and Revenue Department makes potential control and ordinary investor oversight insufficient. Bendix and ASARCO shared no operational management, facilities, financing or meaningful integrated dealings; their stipulated separation negates the ordinary indicia of a unitary enterprise. Container Corporation v. Franchise Tax Board preserves an operational-capital route, but this record shows no working-capital use or other identifiable operational function for the shares. New Jersey's acquisition-and-divestiture theory merely restates the general business-purpose rationale. Intended later expenditure on a related acquisition does not establish the function of the ASARCO holding while it produced the gain. The stipulations satisfy the taxpayer's burden of showing unrelated value. The Court reserves genuine operational investments and fair-apportionment challenges involving a properly constituted tax base; calculations and appropriate refund treatment remain for the state courts.

**Precedent treatment.**

- ASARCO Inc. v. Idaho Tax Commission, 458 U.S. 307 (1982): applied to reject the business-purpose rationale and to treat capital gain under the same nexus rule as dividend income.
- F. W. Woolworth Co. v. Taxation and Revenue Department, 458 U.S. 354 (1982): applied; no actual integration is shown, and two noncontrolling board seats do not establish centralized operational management.
- Container Corp. of America v. Franchise Tax Board, 463 U.S. 159 (1983): distinguished on the facts; its operational finance and services are absent. Its taxpayer burden and review for use of the correct legal standard remain governing; duration alone is not made a constitutional test.
