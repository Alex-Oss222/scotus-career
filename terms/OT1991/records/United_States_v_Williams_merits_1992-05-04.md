## Controlling holdings

### Review of the question passed upon below

**Holding and operative rule.** The Court may review an important federal question expressly decided by the court of appeals even though a party did not renew its challenge to binding circuit precedent in this case, where adherence to that precedent did not concede its correctness. This is a permissible exercise of review, not an obligation to decide every unpressed question or a rule excusing an affirmative waiver.

**Authority.** Stone’s reviewability opinion, joined by White, Scalia, Kennedy and Souter: five Justices adopt the rule and explanation. The four Justices who prefer dismissal of the writ do not join this rule.

**Controlling explanation.** The Court holds that the question is properly before it because the court of appeals actually decided the legal duty now challenged. Stevens v. Department of Treasury permits review of a question passed upon below even when the party did not properly press it. The Tenth Circuit expressly applied Page’s disclosure obligation and used it to sustain dismissal. The Government’s recognition that Page bound the panel did not concede that Page correctly stated federal law; the Government had opposed that rule in the proceeding establishing it. Requiring a renewed demand that the panel disregard its recent controlling precedent would not improve the presentation of this already decided legal issue. The contrary vehicle argument treats compliance with binding precedent as abandonment of a legal objection, although no affirmative abandonment is established. The Court exercises its discretion on this federal appellate record. It does not displace a governing trial-objection requirement, decide the effect of an express concession, or prescribe a broader rule for review of state judgments.

**Precedent treatment.**

- Stevens v. Department of Treasury, 500 U.S. 1 — applied: actual decision below can satisfy the pressed-or-passed-upon practice.
- United States v. Page, 808 F.2d 723 — its binding force within the Tenth Circuit explains the litigation posture; adherence does not establish agreement with its correctness.

### Disclosure of known evidence directly negating guilt

**Holding and operative rule.** Before seeking an indictment, a federal prosecutor must present or otherwise disclose to the grand jury personally known, substantial evidence directly negating the charged offense when its omission would materially distort the grand-jury presentation. This duty requires neither an investigative search for undiscovered favorable evidence nor presentation of every possible defense; a violation alone does not establish entitlement to dismissal.

**Authority.** Stevens’s Parts II and III, joined by Blackmun, O’Connor and Thomas, adopt the personally-known/direct-negation duty. Stone’s separate merits opinion adopts that duty where omission materially distorts the presentation. Under Marks, these five judgment supporters establish the stated common requirement; the four-Justice formulation does not remove Stone’s material-distortion qualification from the controlling rule.

**Controlling explanation.** The Court holds that a prosecutor may not use the grand jury’s independent role to justify the materially misleading omission described here. Calandra recognizes both the grand jury’s protection against unfounded prosecution and enforceable limits on its process. Bank of Nova Scotia recognizes judicial remedies for prejudicial misconduct while separating an actual violation from its effect on an indictment. The obligation here regulates the prosecutor’s presentation of substantial evidence already personally known; it does not require the prosecutor to investigate the defense. Costello prevents challenges to the competence or sufficiency of evidence in an otherwise lawful presentation, and that protection remains. The contrary institutional argument correctly rejects a preliminary trial of every defense, but does not dispose of this confined misconduct question. The Government’s categorical objection to authority therefore fails. The character of the particular accounting documents is outside the question accepted for review. Dismissal requires a separate remedial showing, and constitutional duties and negligent discovery remain open.

**Precedent treatment.**

- United States v. Calandra, 414 U.S. 338 — its protective function and enforceable process limits support the confined duty; ordinary evidentiary freedom remains.
- Bank of Nova Scotia v. United States, 487 U.S. 250 — its misconduct and prejudice inquiries remain separate; violation does not automatically warrant dismissal.
- Costello v. United States, 350 U.S. 359 — preserved for competence and sufficiency challenges to an otherwise lawful presentation; distinguished from the specified prosecutorial omission.
- Marks v. United States, 430 U.S. 188 — applied to the judgment supporters’ shared direct-negation duty with the material-distortion qualification retained.

### Confined supervisory power over a materially misleading presentation

**Holding and operative rule.** Federal supervisory authority permits dismissal without prejudice when a prosecutor withholds personally known, substantial evidence directly negating the charged offense, the omission materially distorts the presentation, the evidence would plainly preclude a finding of probable cause, and the violation substantially influenced the indictment or leaves grave doubt that the indictment was free from that influence. This establishes the stated sufficient ground for dismissal; it requires neither a search for undiscovered favorable evidence nor presentation of every potential defense.

**Authority.** Stevens’s merits Parts II and III, joined by Blackmun, O’Connor and Thomas, supply the narrower judgment-supporting rule. Stone concurs in affirmance under the broader material-distortion formulation, covering known evidence substantially undermining an essential element with established Bank of Nova Scotia prejudice. Under Marks v. United States, 430 U.S. 188, the four-Justice rule is the controlling logical subset: every known, directly negating omission that plainly defeats probable cause, materially distorts the presentation and causes the stated prejudice also satisfies Stone’s broader rule. The five judgment supporters together establish its force. Stone is not represented as formally joining the narrower qualification. The four-Justice and broader one-Justice applications are not combined to remove that qualification from the sufficient ground recognized here. Neither the plurality nor the five judgment supporters adopt an exclusive rule forbidding relief in every other circumstance.

**Controlling explanation.** The Court recognizes a confined power to prevent a prosecutor from materially misleading the grand jury while preserving its independent charging function. Bank of Nova Scotia requires actual charging prejudice before dismissal for nonconstitutional misconduct; that safeguard remains necessary here. Calandra recognizes the grand jury’s protective function and legally enforceable limits on its process. The disclosure duty concerns personally known, substantial evidence directly negating guilt. Evidence plainly defeating probable cause supplies a sufficient case for dismissal when the separate prejudice condition is met. Costello protects an otherwise lawful presentation from judicial reassessment of the quality or quantity of the evidence, and Calandra preserves the grand jury’s freedom from ordinary trial restrictions. Neither requires acceptance of the materially distorted presentation covered by this narrow rule. The institutional objection limits supervision but does not eliminate it. Here the accepted question concerns authority; the specific evidentiary application is not reviewed. A lawful new presentation remains possible. Applications outside this sufficient dismissal class, constitutional duties, state grand juries and negligent discovery remain unresolved.

**Precedent treatment.**

- Bank of Nova Scotia v. United States, 487 U.S. 250 — its prejudice requirement is retained; limited supervisory power is extended to the specified known-evidence omission. Its evidence-reliability prohibition survives for lawful presentations.
- Costello v. United States, 350 U.S. 359 — distinguished: the confined misconduct inquiry is not a challenge to the competence or sufficiency of evidence in an otherwise lawful presentation. Its hearsay and ordinary adequacy rules remain.
- United States v. Calandra, 414 U.S. 338 — preserved for grand-jury independence and freedom from trial evidence rules; no exclusionary-rule extension occurs.
- United States v. Mechanik, 475 U.S. 66 — its completed-trial harmlessness holding is not extended to erase a prejudicial violation challenged and resolved before trial; the charging-prejudice formulation adopted in Bank remains operative.
- Brady v. Maryland, 373 U.S. 83 — not transplanted into grand-jury proceedings; its trial disclosure duty remains unchanged.
- Marks v. United States, 430 U.S. 188 — applied to the genuine logical subset of the five judgment supporters’ rules; the shared authority supports the sufficient dismissal ground, without creating a single majority merits opinion or an exclusive boundary on other relief.
- United States v. Page, 808 F.2d 723 — the judgment applying it in Williams is sustained against the categorical no-authority challenge; Page’s full disclosure formulation and any rule requiring every fact relevant to a possible defense are not adopted.

### Application, limits and questions not reached

The affirmed dismissal binds this case. The Government presented the existence of supervisory authority, rather than a renewed challenge to the nature of the omitted evidence. The four-Justice writing does not independently find that these accounting documents plainly preclude probable cause; it preserves its doubt about the lower courts’ reasonable-doubt formulation while rejecting the categorical no-power answer on the question granted. Stone separately finds that the supported hearing findings satisfy his broader material-distortion and prejudice rule. Accordingly, affirmance does not establish that any evidence bearing on intent necessarily meets the narrower controlling test, nor does it impose a new finding that the prosecutor knowingly fabricated evidence. No determination of guilt or innocence follows. A distinct negligent-discovery claim, state-grand-jury duty, constitutional disclosure rule and immunity or fabrication claim remain open.

When a prosecutor conducting the grand-jury inquiry personally knows of substantial evidence directly negating the subject's guilt, the prosecutor must present or otherwise disclose that evidence to the grand jury before seeking an indictment. The obligation does not require searching out and presenting all evidence that might create a reasonable doubt at trial, does not give the target a general right to present a defense, and does not convert charging into a trial.

**Comparison support:** Stevens Part III, 504 U.S. 69–70, joined by Blackmun, O'Connor, and Thomas, adopts the stated 1988 United States Attorneys' Manual §9-11.233 formulation. The Manual's quoted policy is evidence of the formulation adopted by those writers; the Manual alone does not establish a judicially enforceable Supreme Court rule. Their source-of-authority reasoning is in Part II, including Berger, 295 U.S. 78, 88, on the prosecutor's public duty; Calandra, 414 U.S. 338, 343, 346, on the grand jury's protection and legally enforceable limits; Brown, 359 U.S. 41, 49, on judicial assistance; and Bank of Nova Scotia, 487 U.S. 250, 256, 261, on prejudicial grand-jury misconduct.

### Dismissal authority and prejudice

The four recognize judicial authority to dismiss an indictment for withholding evidence that would plainly preclude a finding of probable cause. That sufficient class for judicial relief is distinct from the personally-known/direct-negation formulation of the disclosure duty. Do not shorten the duty into a requirement to disclose only evidence that plainly precludes probable cause, or make every disclosure violation an automatic entitlement to dismissal. Bank of Nova Scotia's separate requirement of prejudicial effect on the indictment remains: substantial influence on the charging decision or grave doubt that the decision was free of such influence.

**Comparison support:** 504 U.S. 64–65 distinguishes prejudicial misconduct from harmless error, and page 70 identifies the plain-preclusion case. The writing's own page 70 note 13 doubts that the lower courts' more lenient reasonable-doubt standard establishes the necessary evidentiary character. Neither this reconciliation nor the historical writing resolves that factual question. No deliberate-falsity, perjury, intentional-deception, or guilt finding is supplied.

- An affirmative rule recognizing dismissal authority within the plain-preclusion class does not, without a separately adopted proposition, establish a universal prohibition on dismissal outside that class. A conditional affirmance also does not nationalize Page's full substantial-exculpatory-evidence standard.
- Marks, 430 U.S. 193–194, concerns the narrowest judgment-supporting position; it cannot turn support for relief in a specified class into agreement forbidding relief everywhere else. Engine §9's logical-subset aid does not authorize joining opposite propositions or counting DIG-only positions as merits rationales. No Marks result for an assembled Court is determined here.
- Costello's rule against evidentiary-adequacy challenges and Calandra's rejection of extending the exclusionary rule remain material counterarguments. The four distinguish regulation of prosecutorial misconduct from imposing general evidentiary completeness; they do not overrule those cases or impose a general defense-presentation right.
