## 9. Separate Writings

White, joined by O'Connor, Scalia and Thomas, regards the Authority's transport facilities as nonpublic and the immediate-collection rule as a reasonable response to congestion, traveler pressure and monitoring difficulties. Public access and shops do not establish a traditional or deliberately opened forum on that view.

Stone assumes public-forum protection and sustains only the conjunctive immediate-payment rule under Ward. Repeated personal transfers interrupt travelers and create operational and fraud concerns. Advocacy and requests for later support inside remain essential alternatives, supplemented by outside immediate solicitation; his analysis does not assume connecting passengers can conveniently reach the sidewalk. He reserves classification and rejects any extension to all financial appeals or distribution.

Kennedy, with Blackmun, Stevens and Souter in Part I, classifies the accessible common areas through their actual physical character, public access and compatibility with expression. Kennedy alone concludes that the narrow immediate-money transaction restriction satisfies Ward while leaving interior advocacy and later-contribution requests available.

O'Connor emphasizes that reasonableness must fit the actual multipurpose environment. She distinguishes immediate monetary collection from passively accepting a leaflet; her classification does not authorize every exclusion.

Souter, joined by Blackmun and Stevens, would invalidate even the immediate-collection ban. On their public-forum view the record does not justify complete interior exclusion where conduct and location rules could address actual obstruction or fraud, and later-payment devices do not adequately replace the live fundraising opportunity.
