## Law of the decision

**Holding and operative rule:** Qualified immunity from a claim of arrest without probable cause is a legal question for the judge under ordinary summary-judgment constraints. A court must evaluate whether a reasonable officer could believe the arrest lawful on the supported nonmovant version of the complete contemporaneous record; choosing the “more reasonable” interpretation is not the test, but a generalized security concern alone does not establish reasonable belief that the elements of the offense were satisfied.

**Authority:** Stone's opinion joined by Stone, Blackmun, O'Connor, Kennedy, and Souter; five of eight participants and therefore controlling.

**Controlling explanation:** *Harlow*, *Anderson v. Creighton*, and *Malley* protect reasonable mistakes and require early judicial resolution where supported facts permit it. Rule 56 nevertheless requires the court to identify genuine disputes about historical predicates and draw supported inferences for the nonmovant. The agents could consider the assassination references, reported gesture, oral statements, confusing interview, and refusal to elaborate. They also had the letter itself, which repeatedly attributed the supposed plot to an outside group; that feature matters to whether Bryant communicated a serious threat prohibited by §871 or reported an imagined danger. *Watts* supplies the contextual distinction between a true threat and protected expression. The Ninth Circuit erred by asking which interpretation was more reasonable and by suggesting the legal immunity issue belonged generally to a jury. But the opposite error would treat presidential-security concern as dispositive without connecting the accepted facts to the offense. The remand court must decide immunity promptly after specifying any supported material dispute; the Court finds neither guilt, innocence, bad faith, nor a constitutional violation.

**Precedent treatment:** *Harlow*, *Anderson v. Creighton*, *Malley*, *Mitchell*, *Beck*, *Watts*, and *Liberty Lobby* are applied and preserved. No qualified-immunity rule is revised.

**Law after decision:** Qualified immunity remains judge-decided and protects reasonable probable-cause mistakes, subject to Rule 56's supported-fact constraints and offense-specific analysis.

### Historical reconciliation, September 15, 2026 hand correction

This section is a dated correction to the audit annex above, made directly at the user's authorization rather than through a fresh Run, to supply the commitment-change audit Engine §8 requires. It changes no vote, judgment, or remedy stated above; it documents the basis for O'Connor's, Kennedy's, Souter's, and Blackmun's recorded moves from their provisional commitments.

The official report, 502 U.S. 224, confirms the per curiam disposition directing qualified immunity on the stated ground that "a reasonable officer could have believed [the arrest] to be lawful, in light of clearly established law and the information the officers possessed." White, Blackmun, O'Connor, Kennedy, and Souter joined that opinion without a separate writing of their own; that undifferentiated join, on that stated ground, is each of their recorded positions after reconciliation with the comparator, and Engine §8 governs its revision. Scalia separately concurs in the judgment on his own narrower ground, agreeing with immunity but stating the Court of Appeals applied the correct standard and reached the wrong factual result. Stevens alone dissents, disputing that probable cause existed on this record. Thomas took no part.

**The prior legal barrier.** Each of O'Connor's, Kennedy's, and Souter's reconciled commitments rests on the per curiam's own reasonable-officer application to the agents' information: the letter's assassination references, the reported gesture, Bryant's oral statements, his refusal to elaborate, and the security context. That application did not separately identify or resolve a factual dispute about what that information actually showed before concluding a reasonable officer could believe the arrest lawful.

**The minimum revision.** Stone's circulated opinion does not depart from the reasonable-officer standard those three Justices already applied; it applies the same standard under Rule 56's separate, undisputed requirement to draw genuinely disputed factual inferences for the nonmovant before that legal question is resolved on summary judgment. The specific disputed predicate Stone's opinion identifies — that the letter itself repeatedly attributed the supposed assassination plot to an outside group rather than to Bryant personally, a feature bearing directly on whether the agents' own information supported a reasonable belief that Bryant himself communicated a serious threat under §871 — was not addressed by the per curiam's undifferentiated application. That is the minimum revision resolving the barrier: not a new legal rule, but the identification of a specific, record-based factual dispute the per curiam's own stated ground had to pass over to reach summary immunity. O'Connor, Kennedy, and Souter join Stone's narrower remand because it resolves that predicate under the same governing law they already accepted, without conceding error in the standard itself; White accepts the same predicate identification but concludes immunity remains directable notwithstanding it, narrowing to concurrence in the judgment; Scalia rejects the predicate's materiality and dissents.

Blackmun's provisional placement against directed immunity, made from the neutral packet alone before the comparator was opened, is superseded here: the comparator confirms he too joined the undifferentiated historical per curiam directing immunity, on the same stated ground as White, O'Connor, Kennedy, and Souter. His reconciled commitment therefore moves on the identical basis stated above — the letter's outside-group attribution as an unresolved Rule 56 predicate — placing him with O'Connor, Kennedy, and Souter in Stone's remand rather than in his originally guessed position.

White's narrowing to a concurrence in part and judgment, and Scalia's dissent favoring directed immunity, remain each Justice's own recorded final position and are unaffected by this reconciliation.
