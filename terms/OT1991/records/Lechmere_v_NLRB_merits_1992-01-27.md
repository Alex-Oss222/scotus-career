The ALJ found reasonable alternatives but ordered access under Fairmont's balancing; the Board instead applied Jean Country and found no reasonably effective alternative.

## Law of the decision

### Nonemployee access requires demonstrated inaccessibility before balancing

**Holding and operative rule:** For a nondiscriminatory exclusion policy, nonemployee organizers must show that employees are practically beyond reasonable offsite communication before the Board may balance a need for entry against the employer's property rights; greater convenience or organizing success does not satisfy that threshold. This workforce's community residence, public approaches and substantial direct contacts defeat the asserted exception, while discriminatory exclusion, employees' own solicitation and genuinely inaccessible workforces remain governed by their distinct rules.

**Authority:** Stone's opinion, joined by O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter and Thomas; six Justices control both the statutory rule and denial of enforcement.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that the Board cannot replace Babcock's threshold with general balancing. Section 7 protects employee self-organization; access for outside organizers serves that right derivatively and is limited. Babcock distinguishes employees' solicitation from nonemployee entry, and Central Hardware requires the need for entry to be shown before accommodation. Hudgens's flexible language does not erase that distinction, while Sears confirms the demanding burden to establish that reasonable alternatives are unavailable. The Board's expertise remains relevant to actual access obstacles, but an established statutory construction fixes the legal inquiry. These employees live in the surrounding community. Organizers used public approaches and obtained substantial direct contacts; the small number of signed cards does not establish inability to communicate. Jean Country improperly treats alternatives as merely one factor in balancing rather than an antecedent requirement here. The Court demands neither organizational success nor exhaustion of every imaginable technique. It preserves the separate rules for discriminatory exclusion, employee solicitation and workers isolated by their living or working location.

**Precedent treatment:**

- *NLRB v. Babcock & Wilcox Co.*, 351 U.S. 105 — applied and reaffirmed: employee and nonemployee activity differ, and demonstrated inaccessibility precedes balancing under a nondiscriminatory rule.
- *Republic Aviation Corp. v. NLRB*, 324 U.S. 793 — employees' own organizing rights remain distinct and are not narrowed here.
- *Central Hardware Co. v. NLRB*, 407 U.S. 539 — applied for the prior showing of a need for nonemployee access.
- *Hudgens v. NLRB*, 424 U.S. 507 — accommodation language is preserved within Babcock's nonemployee threshold, not treated as authorization for universal balancing.
- *Sears, Roebuck & Co. v. Carpenters*, 436 U.S. 180 — applied for the heavy burden concerning alternative access and the separate discrimination route.
- *Chevron U.S.A. Inc. v. Natural Resources Defense Council, Inc.*, 467 U.S. 837, and *Maislin Industries, U.S., Inc. v. Primary Steel, Inc.*, 497 U.S. 116 — general deference remains; a later agency approach must respect a governing judicial statutory construction.
- *Jean Country*, 291 N.L.R.B. 11 — rejected insofar as its universal balancing replaces the antecedent inaccessibility inquiry for nondiscriminatory exclusion of nonemployee organizers; other contexts are not adjudicated.

**Authority check / Marks:** Every holding above has a named majority at its stated generality. Judgment-only agreements and dissents are not counted as joins. No Marks-derived rule is needed.

**Law after decision / before-and-after operation:** Babcock's nonemployee threshold controls: demonstrated practical inaccessibility precedes balancing, and a more productive organizing location does not establish necessity. The Board may assess actual obstacles within that rule; it may not replace the rule through Jean Country. Discriminatory exclusion, employee activity, public-property advocacy and genuinely isolated workforces retain distinct treatment. No new general deference or constitutional-entry rule is adopted.
