## Law of the decision

**Holding and operative rule:** A timely document styled as an appellate brief may serve as a notice of appeal if it objectively identifies the appealing party, the judgment or order appealed, and the appellate court as Rule 3(c) requires. Filing it in the court of appeals is governed by Rule 4's mistaken-filing provision; informal form or title alone cannot require dismissal.

**Authority:** O'Connor's opinion joined by eight Justices supplies the controlling rationale; Scalia agrees with the judgment and the express form-or-title ground.

**Controlling explanation:** *Torres* makes Rule 3 compliance mandatory while recognizing functional compliance with the information the Rule actually requires. *Foman* directs courts to read a notice in context where the intent to seek review is manifest. Rule 3(c) itself prohibits dismissal for informality of form or title, so an administrative expectation that a notice and brief arrive as separate papers cannot add a jurisdictional condition. Smith returned the clerk's informal-brief form within the valid filing period after attempting to appeal; responding to that form does not negate the objective notice his document may convey. Rule 4 also treats a notice mistakenly filed in the appellate court as filed in the district court upon receipt. The first notice remains ineffective under the then-current posttrial-motion rule. The Fourth Circuit must still determine whether the May 4 document actually contains each required item and what judgment or orders it fairly identifies.

**Precedent treatment:** *Torres*, *Foman*, and *Griggs* are applied; *Ibarra* remains confined to timely criminal reconsideration motions.

**Law after decision:** Rule 3 looks to timely objective content, not a document's caption, while preserving every required item.
