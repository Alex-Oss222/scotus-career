## Controlling holdings

### Inherent distinctiveness suffices without acquired secondary meaning

**Holding and operative rule.** Under the pre-1988 Lanham Act §43(a), nonfunctional restaurant trade dress that is inherently distinctive may receive protection without proof of acquired secondary meaning, provided the remaining infringement requirements, including likelihood of confusion, are met. Protection does not expire after an unspecified period merely because secondary meaning has not developed.

**Authority.** White's Opinion of the Court, joined by Stone-Zsela, Blackmun, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy and Souter, supplies seven votes at this level of generality. Stevens and Thomas agree with the judgment on separate statutory accounts; the Court's rule does not depend on combining their writings.

**Controlling explanation.** The Court holds that a source identifier need not acquire distinctiveness twice. Inwood distinguishes acquired public association from functionality and recognizes the broader protection available under §43(a). Park 'N Fly explains the statutory distinction between descriptive marks requiring acquired meaning and inherently distinctive marks. The same source-identifying distinction applies to the nonfunctional restaurant presentation accepted here. The jury found inherent distinctiveness and likely confusion, yet found no acquired secondary meaning; those findings concern different propositions and are not contradictory. Chevron Chemical properly applies the familiar Abercrombie categories to trade dress, whereas a universal secondary-meaning requirement has no basis in the operative statute. Requiring an initial period of unprotected copying would obstruct source identification and goodwill. The concern that protection might exhaust useful designs is answered by the independent requirements of distinctiveness, nonfunctionality and confusion. A temporary exemption followed by an unwritten deadline is equally unsupported. The Court accepts the findings for this limited review and leaves the excluded functionality challenge and other forms of product design undecided.

**Precedent treatment.**

- Inwood Laboratories, Inc. v. Ives Laboratories, Inc., 456 U.S. 844 (1982): applied for secondary meaning, functionality and §43(a)'s protection beyond registered marks; its distinct contributory-infringement holding is unchanged.
- Park 'N Fly, Inc. v. Dollar Park & Fly, Inc., 469 U.S. 189 (1985): applied for the statutory distinction between inherent and acquired distinctiveness and protection of source-identifying goodwill; no registration requirement is imported into §43(a).
- Kellogg Co. v. National Biscuit Co., 305 U.S. 111 (1938): its acquired-source-significance concept is preserved; it does not impose secondary meaning on accepted inherently distinctive restaurant dress.
- Abercrombie & Fitch Co. v. Hunting World, Inc., 537 F.2d 4 (2d Cir. 1976): its classification is used to distinguish inherent from acquired distinctiveness; this circuit authority is not represented as an earlier Supreme Court trade-dress holding.
- Chevron Chemical Co. v. Voluntary Purchasing Groups, Inc., 659 F.2d 695 (5th Cir. 1981): its treatment of inherently distinctive trade dress is approved on the question presented.
- Vibrant Sales, Inc. v. New Body Boutique, Inc., 652 F.2d 299 (2d Cir. 1981), and the contrary categorical reading of the Second Circuit trade-dress cases: rejected insofar as they require acquired secondary meaning despite accepted inherent distinctiveness; their distinct functionality applications are not decided.
