## Record, question and reachability

After his November 1987 arrest Riggins requested treatment for voices and sleep difficulties; Mellaril eventually reached 800mg daily, with Dilantin also prescribed. He was found competent after examinations in early 1988. In June he sought suspension through trial; the July14 hearing produced differing predictions, including testimony that he could remain competent without Mellaril and questions concerning dosage. The July hearing initially preceded a September 26 trial setting. A September 19 defense continuance moved trial to November 7; the later delay is not treated as a period the judge already knew was available at the hearing. Jurasky’s report predicting regression and management difficulty was part of the record, but its prediction is not an adopted necessity finding. The court denied relief in a one-page unexplained order; treatment continued through his November1988 jury trial, insanity defense and testimony. Nevada's majority treated expert explanation of medication as sufficient; Rose concurred while questioning necessity, and Springer dissented. Medical appropriateness is assumed for this review, not newly adjudged; need, actual impairment and harmlessness are not invented factual findings. Other Nevada appeal issues are not independently granted questions.

### Issue 1: Justification and trial protection

* **Threshold and Merits Reach:** Reach the preserved objection after consent was withdrawn. Exclude the separately unpreserved Eighth Amendment claim.
* **Stone's Legal Position:** Apply Harper's recognized liberty and fair-trial doctrine to a distinct trial setting. Stone proposes the necessary clarification that the State bears justification for continuing treatment over objection: medical appropriateness plus a demonstrated necessity sufficient for the intrusion, considering reasonable less intrusive means. He does not import a later competency-medication test or invent a proof quantum.
* **Stone's Proposed Holding:** An unexplained order continuing antipsychotic medication through trial cannot stand where the State has not established the need for that compelled course and the supported risk to defense participation cannot be found harmless beyond a reasonable doubt.
* **Essential Reasoning and Record Application:** Medical appropriateness addresses clinical acceptability, not the legal need to override refusal. Several witnesses did not identify loss of competence upon withdrawal, and one questioned the dosage. Stone cannot replace the absent necessity determination with his own medical finding. The State's strongest position, that serious charges deserve a competent trial, is legitimate but leaves unanswered why this treatment was required to obtain one. Demeanor testimony informs jurors only about one possible effect; it cannot reconstruct impaired consultation or attention. Under Chapman the State, rather than Riggins, carries harmlessness. This record does not discharge that burden. The rule uses existing hearings and medical evidence, demands no new documentary ritual, and leaves appropriate safety treatment possible.
* **Boundary or Reserved Question:** Reserve a categorical ban on competency-restoration medication, a general structural-error classification, and the Eighth Amendment sentencing theory. Stone neither declares Riggins incompetent nor assumes every medicated defendant receives an unfair trial.

## Holdings
### 1. The State must justify antipsychotic medication continued over objection

**Holding and operative rule.** Once a pretrial detainee objects to continued antipsychotic medication, the State must establish medical appropriateness and a need sufficient to override the protected refusal interest, considering reasonable less intrusive means. The unexplained order here supplies neither an essential justification nor findings about alternatives and cannot authorize the compelled treatment through trial.

**Authority.** O'Connor's Part II, joined by Stone-Zsela, White, Blackmun, Stevens and Souter: six of nine participating Justices adopt this rule and application.

**Controlling explanation.** The Court holds that clinical acceptability does not itself establish legal authority to override refusal. Washington v. Harper recognizes the substantial liberty interest affected by unwanted antipsychotic medication and permits justified treatment of dangerous, mentally ill prisoners. An accused awaiting trial retains the protected refusal interest recognized in Harper. Riggins initially sought treatment, but continued administration became involuntary after his objection. Medical appropriateness is accepted for this review; necessity is not. The hearing contained testimony that he could remain competent without Mellaril and questions about the high dosage. The trial court's unexplained denial establishes neither an essential safety need nor that less intrusive means could not permit adjudication. The State's legitimate interest in a competent trial cannot replace those missing determinations. The Court does not impose a new documentary ritual, universal proof quantum or strict-scrutiny formula. Medically appropriate treatment essential to safety after considering alternatives remains possible. The complete conditions for competency-restoration medication, and whether a defendant may insist on becoming incompetent, remain undecided.

**Precedent treatment.**

- Washington v. Harper, 494 U.S. 210 (1990): applied for the protected refusal interest and requirement of overriding justification and medical appropriateness; its convicted-prisoner procedures do not automatically govern trial medication.
- Illinois v. Allen, 397 U.S. 337 (1970): relied on for the State's legitimate trial interest and the possibility of necessary courtroom measures; no comprehensive competency-medication exception is adopted.

### 2. This unsupported medication order requires a new trial

**Holding and operative rule.** When unjustified compelled antipsychotic medication through trial creates a supported, substantial risk of impairing the defense that the record cannot reliably reconstruct, the defendant need not demonstrate exactly how an unmedicated trial would have differed. The risk here extends beyond visible demeanor to testimony, comprehension and communication with counsel; explanatory expert testimony does not cure it, and the convictions and dependent sentence must be vacated for a new trial.

**Authority.** O'Connor's Part III, joined by Stone-Zsela, White, Blackmun, Stevens and Souter: six of nine participating Justices adopt this record-specific rule and remedy. Kennedy concurs in the reversal and new-trial judgment on a distinct trial-integrity ground.

**Controlling explanation.** The Court holds that this trial record cannot establish that the unsupported treatment left the defense intact. Illinois v. Allen and Estelle v. Williams recognize that compelled practices can affect an accused's trial in ways a transcript cannot measure. The doctors' testimony identifies a strong possibility that this dosage affected attention, communication and the substance of testimony, not merely the appearance the jury observed. Expert explanation may help jurors interpret demeanor, but cannot restore a conversation with counsel or a response that medication changed. The contrary demand for a precise account of the trial that would have occurred without treatment would therefore require speculation. Allen permits necessary measures to protect the trial, but this record contains no finding establishing an essential justification for this treatment. Reversal and a new trial follow from this combination of unjustified treatment and supported, unreconstructable trial risk. The Court neither finds Riggins incompetent nor decides that every medication error is structural. Renewed compelled treatment requires a constitutionally adequate contemporaneous justification.

**Precedent treatment.**

- Illinois v. Allen, 397 U.S. 337 (1970): applied by analogy to potentially prejudicial compelled trial measures and the need for an essential justification.
- Estelle v. Williams, 425 U.S. 501 (1976): applied by analogy to effects of compelled trial appearance that cannot be fully reconstructed from a transcript; its particular waiver disposition is not extended.

## Published separate writings

Kennedy concurs in the judgment. Thomas, joined by Scalia except in Part II-A, would affirm.
