## 1. Event

Fort Gratiot Sanitary Landfill, Inc. v. Michigan Department of Natural Resources, No. 91-636. Argued March 30, 1992; decided June 1, 1992. On writ of certiorari to the United States Court of Appeals for the Sixth Circuit, reviewing 931 F.2d 413, decided May 1, 1991, with rehearing denied July 16, 1991. The district court denied the operator's summary-judgment request and dismissed the complaint; the Sixth Circuit affirmed.

The question is whether Michigan may prohibit private landfills from accepting outside-county waste unless a county plan expressly authorizes it, despite treating other Michigan counties like other States. Petitioner seeks declaratory and prospective injunctive relief. The challenged provisions are §§ 299.413a and 299.430(2), enacted December 28, 1988. Its February 13, 1989 request offered to reserve twenty years of county disposal capacity but was denied by the planning committee.

## 6. Holdings

### County-origin exclusions from private disposal facilities

**Holding and operative rule:** A State's prohibition on a private landfill's acceptance of outside-county waste unless a county plan affirmatively authorizes it discriminates against interstate commerce even when it also excludes waste from other counties within that State and some counties choose to admit imports. The restriction cannot stand unless the State establishes a legitimate local purpose that reasonable nondiscriminatory alternatives cannot adequately serve; Michigan has not made that showing for these Waste Import Restrictions, and regulation of a private facility does not become market participation because government plans waste services.

**Authority:** Stevens's Opinion of the Court, joined in full by Stone, White, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter and Thomas. Eight Justices adopt the same rule, application and limits. Blackmun supports a different remedial course and supplies no controlling join. No fractured-decision analysis is necessary.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that a county boundary does not remove a barrier to interstate trade. Philadelphia establishes that waste disposal is commerce and that local capacity may not be reserved by origin alone. Brimmer and Dean Milk establish that excluding other intrastate suppliers does not cure a geographic preference against interstate suppliers; New Energy likewise rejects a requirement of widespread commercial disadvantage. Wyoming preserves the distinction between the existence and the extent of discrimination. Michigan's planning obligations are substantial, but the challenged veto still gives local waste access that equivalent outside waste lacks. Neutral quantity limits, appropriate charges and facility standards can preserve planning without that preference. Hunt and Maine require justification of the discriminatory means, and respondents identify no distinct imported hazard or inadequacy of those alternatives. They have offered no further showing or requested an evidentiary remand that would change that conclusion. Government is regulating a private disposal transaction, not selling its own service. These alternative tools illustrate the failed justification rather than impose a replacement program. The Court leaves public facilities, neutral planning requirements and unconditional operating entitlement undecided.

**Precedent treatment:**

- Philadelphia v. New Jersey, 437 U.S. 617 — applied to county-origin restrictions on private disposal facilities; its rule against origin-only exclusion is unchanged.
- Brimmer v. Rebman, 138 U.S. 78 — applied: a substate boundary does not cure a burden on interstate trade merely because some intrastate trade also suffers.
- Dean Milk Co. v. Madison, 340 U.S. 349 — applied to the claimed neutrality of the county line; municipal exclusion remains subject to Commerce scrutiny.
- New Energy Co. of Indiana v. Limbach, 486 U.S. 269 — applied: geographic discrimination need not disadvantage every outside supplier or produce a statewide exclusion.
- Hunt v. Washington State Apple Advertising Commission, 432 U.S. 333 — the State's burden concerning purpose and adequate nondiscriminatory alternatives is preserved.
- Maine v. Taylor, 477 U.S. 131 — distinguished on the absence of a distinctive imported hazard and unavailable adequate alternatives; its environmental exception remains intact.
- Wyoming v. Oklahoma, simulated January 22, 1992 — applied for express geographic preferences; the volume affected does not define whether discrimination exists.
- Hughes v. Alexandria Scrap Corp., 426 U.S. 794, and Reeves, Inc. v. Stake, 447 U.S. 429 — their distinction between government participation in a market and government regulation is preserved; public planning does not transform this regulation of a private facility into proprietary action.
- Pike v. Bruce Church, Inc., 397 U.S. 137 — its incidental-burden branch does not govern the express county-origin exclusion; the separate standard for evenhanded regulation remains.

**Limits and questions not reached:** Only the 1988 Waste Import Restrictions as applied to privately owned and operated landfills are invalidated. The earlier comprehensive planning statute, public-facility practices, neutral health and safety rules, neutral capacity limits, different waste-risk classifications and unconditional facility operation are not adjudicated. No hazardous-waste issue arises. No genuinely applicable congressional authorization is displaced; none supplies an adjudicated defense here. The Court does not reopen the distinct due-process, taking or ownership-transfer licensing matters described below. No showing that every Michigan county bars outside waste is required.
