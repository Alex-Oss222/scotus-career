## 1. Event, sources, posture and reach

**Yee v. City of Escondido** — No. 90-1947; OT1991.

Argued 1992-01-22; decided 1992-04-01. Certiorari under 28 U.S.C. §1257 after California Supreme Court denial of review. The granted question concerns physical taking; a related regulatory theory is not fairly included.

**Question:** Do these restrictions on voluntarily created tenancies impose a permanent physical occupation requiring compensation?

**Material record and posture:** Park owners voluntarily rented pads to mobile-home owners. California limits termination and interference with in-place sales, but permits withdrawal through a change of land use. Escondido’s 1988 ordinance rolled rents back to 1986 levels and requires approval for increases. Owners alleged that reduced rents and constrained replacement of tenants create premiums in home resale prices. They did not attempt the statutory change-of-use process. The trial court sustained a demurrer, the Court of Appeal affirmed, and state discretionary review was denied. The due-process claim appeared only in the request for discretionary review; no state court adjudicated it. No as-applied denial of withdrawal is established.

## 6. Controlling holdings

### 1. Voluntarily created tenancies

**Holding and operative rule.** Regulating rents and continuation of voluntarily created pad tenancies does not impose a permanent physical occupation where the law permits the owner to change the land’s use and no contrary application is established. A tenant’s resale premium and the owner’s reduced ability to select incoming tenants do not themselves convert that regulation into a compelled occupation.

**Authority.** O’Connor, I, joined by Stone, White, Stevens, Scalia, Kennedy, Thomas: seven votes control. Blackmun and Souter separately agree with rejection of the physical-taking claim.

**Controlling explanation.** The Court holds that the owners invited the occupation for which they now seek automatic compensation. Florida Power distinguishes that invitation from a government license forcing an unwanted occupant onto property; regulation of rent does not erase the distinction. Loretto requires compensation for a compelled permanent occupation, but preserves ordinary regulation of the landlord-tenant relationship. Here the challenged framework permits a change of use, and the owners have not alleged an actual unsuccessful effort to invoke that process. Their loss of rent and tenants’ ability to capitalize favorable terms describe economic effects, not governmental compulsion to surrender possession. The right to exclude remains important, but it does not turn every limit on an invited tenancy into physical appropriation. Nollan’s compelled easement presents a different premise. The Court leaves a law actually forbidding withdrawal, its particular application, and the separate regulatory-taking merits for appropriate proceedings.

**Precedent treatment.**

- FCC v. Florida Power Corp., 480 U.S. 245 — applied: invitation to occupy remains distinct from compelled occupation despite regulated rent.
- Loretto v. Teleprompter Manhattan CATV Corp., 458 U.S. 419 — applied and distinguished: its permanent-occupation rule requires compulsion absent here.
- Nollan v. California Coastal Commission, 483 U.S. 825 — distinguished: a required easement cannot substitute for proof of forced occupation here.
- Kaiser Aetna v. United States, 444 U.S. 164 — preserved: the right to exclude is important, but the facial scheme has not compelled this occupation.

**Authority.** O’Connor, II-A, joined by White, Stevens, Scalia, Kennedy and Thomas: six formal joins; Blackmun separately agrees that the claim is not properly raised. Stone’s limited merits reach supplies no additional procedural rationale.

**Controlling explanation.** The Court holds that the due-process claim is not properly presented for this review. The owners did not put that distinct federal claim in their complaint or litigate it in the Court of Appeal. The distinction matters because due process is not merely a new supporting argument about an already presented taking. Whether the rule for reviewing state judgments is jurisdictional or prudential need not be settled: the Court would follow it in either event here. This disposition decides no due-process merits and gives no view on the ordinance’s validity under that separate constitutional theory.

### 3. Preserved taking claim and facial ripeness

**Holding and operative rule.** A party that properly presents a federal taking claim below may offer a different supporting argument on review, subject to the separate limitation imposed by the question presented. Under the then-governing standards, this facial allegation that the ordinance never substantially advances a legitimate state interest is ripe without a rent-increase request because it does not depend on the particular property’s economic loss or compensation.

**Authority.** O’Connor, II-B–C, joined by White, Stevens, Scalia, Kennedy and Thomas: six formal joins control these procedural propositions; no Stone join is attributed.

**Controlling explanation.** The Court holds that preservation of a federal claim and preservation of every supporting argument are different inquiries. Bankers Life and Gates permit a new argument supporting the taking claim actually presented in the state courts. The owners’ physical and regulatory theories invoke that same federal claim, unlike their separate due-process claim. The city’s ripeness objection also does not bar the facial theory asserted here. Williamson County would require the pertinent final determination for a claim dependent on how regulation affects particular property. This theory instead alleges that the ordinance never advances a legitimate state interest, a question independent of any requested rent increase. Agins and Keystone supply that distinction under the law governing this case. These conclusions establish neither a regulatory taking nor permission to disregard the grant’s limits. Rule 14.1(a) still prevents consideration of the regulatory merits in this proceeding.

**Precedent treatment.**

- Bankers Life & Casualty Co. v. Crenshaw, 486 U.S. 71 — applied: new arguments may support a preserved federal claim.
- Illinois v. Gates, 462 U.S. 213 — applied: argument and claim preservation are distinct.
- Williamson County Regional Planning Commission v. Hamilton Bank, 473 U.S. 172 — distinguished: the asserted facial theory does not require a property-specific rent determination.
- Agins v. Tiburon, 447 U.S. 255 — applied only to the then-current facial-ripeness proposition; no merits determination follows.

### 4. Question presented limits merits review

**Holding and operative rule.** Under Rule 14.1(a), a question asking whether the ordinance causes a physical taking does not fairly include the related but independent question whether it causes a regulatory taking. No exceptional urgency or economy justifies departing from that prudential limit here, so the regulatory merits remain undecided.

**Authority.** O’Connor, II-D, joined by Stone, White, Stevens, Scalia, Kennedy, Thomas: seven votes; this section adopts no regulatory merits or ripeness conclusion.

**Controlling explanation.** The Court holds that the question chosen in the petition defines the issue for decision. The petition sought resolution of disagreement over physical occupation. A regulatory taking is related to that issue, but neither question is subsidiary to the other. Rule 14.1(a) gives the respondent notice of the proposed grounds for review and permits the Court to select and decide focused questions. Stone v. Powell recognizes departure only in exceptional circumstances, and none calls for expanding this case. The lower courts’ physical-taking disagreement explains review of that issue; it does not supply a developed conflict or record on the regulatory theory. Lytle counsels against deciding a new application without adequate lower-court consideration. The owners’ preservation of a general taking claim therefore does not remove the separate restriction imposed by their petition. The Court leaves regulatory-taking merits to an appropriate proceeding.

**Precedent treatment.**

- Stone v. Powell, 428 U.S. 465 — applied: departure from the question-presented rule remains exceptional.
- Lytle v. Household Manufacturing, Inc., 494 U.S. 545 — applied: developed arguments and lower-court consideration support disciplined review.
- American National Bank & Trust Co. v. Haroco, Inc., 473 U.S. 606 — applied by analogy: related questions are not necessarily fairly included within one another.
