## Reno v. Condon, 528 U.S. 141

Source: https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/USREPORTS-528/pdf/USREPORTS-528-141.pdf. Saved PDF/text: `chunk2-control-sources/USREPORTS-528-141.pdf` and `.txt`. Full reading: 11 pages, pp. 141–151.

No. 98-1464; argued November 10, 1999; decided January 12, 2000. Certiorari to Fourth Circuit, 155 F.3d 453. Reversed. Rehnquist wrote for all nine; no separate writing.

The historical Court treats personal driver information sold or released into interstate business as an article of commerce; it does not decide the alternative substantial-effects theory for each State's intrastate activity. It rejects commandeering because the Act regulates States as database owners, requires no legislation, and does not enlist state officers to enforce federal law against private people. South Carolina v. Baker supplies the distinction between regulation of state activities and direction of how States regulate others; compliance time and expense do not alone establish commandeering. General applicability is present because the Act covers States and private resellers/redisclosers, so whether it is constitutionally required remains open. The Government abandoned its Fourteenth Amendment §5 ground. South Carolina sought facial invalidation even as applied to its commercial sales; it did not assert nonparticipation in the interstate information market.

Historical Printz reasoning cannot be imported into the simulation's differently reasoned Printz. The factual direct-regulation distinction remains a subject for independent modeling and reconciliation, not an automatic historical assignment or join.

## Martinez v. Court of Appeal, 528 U.S. 152

Source: https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/USREPORTS-528/pdf/USREPORTS-528-152.pdf. Saved PDF/text: `chunk2-control-sources/USREPORTS-528-152.pdf` and `.txt`. Full reading: 15 pages, pp. 152–166.

No. 98-7809; argued November 9, 1999; decided January 12, 2000. Certiorari to California Supreme Court after denial of writ of mandate. Affirmed, unanimous judgment. Stevens's opinion joined by Rehnquist, O'Connor, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg, and Breyer. Kennedy wrote a solo concurrence. Breyer wrote a separate solo concurrence. Scalia alone concurred in judgment. The original comparator incorrectly combines Kennedy and Breyer into one writing.

The Court confines Faretta to self-representation at trial. The Sixth Amendment does not establish a right to appeal or appellate self-representation. The sparse history of appeals provides no equivalent historical entitlement. Due-process autonomy interests do not require a State to sacrifice the fairness and efficiency of appellate review; the posture changes from defending against prosecution to seeking reversal of a conviction. States remain free to recognize a right under their constitutions or permit self-representation or supplemental filings. The opinion does not overrule Faretta.

Kennedy: the appeal/trial distinction is sufficient while accepting Faretta as sound; casting doubt on its rationale is unnecessary. Breyer: he joins the Court, notes judges' concerns about practical consequences, but finds no empirical foundation for reconsidering Faretta's constitutional premises. Scalia: he rejects skepticism about Faretta and protects informed individual choice even when disadvantageous, preferably grounded in due process. He nevertheless finds no appellate self-representation right because there is no constitutional right to appeal; a State could supply nonadversarial review, making counsel-based review permissible. His broad appellate-review rationale is not the Court's holding.

## Adarand Constructors v. Slater, 528 U.S. 216

Source: https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/USREPORTS-528/pdf/USREPORTS-528-216.pdf. Saved PDF/text: `chunk2-control-sources/USREPORTS-528-216.pdf` and `.txt`. Full reading: 9 pages, pp. 216–224.

No. 99-295; decided January 12, 2000, on petition for certiorari to Tenth Circuit, 169 F.3d 1292. No argument date is reported. Certiorari granted; reversed and remanded by per curiam opinion; no separate writing or noted dissent. The official disposition is reversal, not vacatur. The supplied metadata says 9-0; the primary opinion itself is unsigned and notes no dissent rather than publishing a named roll call.

The historical court distinguishes initial standing from voluntary-cessation mootness and puts the heavy no-recurrence burden on respondents. Colorado's certification did not conclusively remove the dispute: DOT had not approved Colorado's revised certification procedure; federal rules required valid certification and individualized determinations for persons outside presumptively disadvantaged groups; the certification could face third-party or agency challenge. Vacating the district judgment also removed the basis on which the applicant had been deemed disadvantaged. The Court does not decide the contracting program's constitutionality, the validity of certification, or the disputed scope of the district court's remedial order. It restores further proceedings. Historical Adarand I and Laidlaw propositions must be replaced by their actual simulated counterparts where the timelines differ.

## Weeks v. Angelone, 528 U.S. 225

Source: https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/USREPORTS-528/pdf/USREPORTS-528-225.pdf. Saved PDF/text: `chunk2-control-sources/USREPORTS-528-225.pdf` and `.txt`. Full reading: 25 pages, pp. 225–249.

No. 99-5746; argued December 6, 1999; decided January 19, 2000. Fourth Circuit, 176 F.3d 249, affirmed 5-4. Rehnquist's Court opinion joined by O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Thomas. Stevens dissented; Ginsburg and Breyer joined all; Souter joined all except Part I. No other writing.

The Court applies Boyde's reasonable-likelihood test, not certainty or any-possibility review. Buchanan had sustained the operative Virginia pattern instruction; Weeks also received a separate mandatory mitigation-consideration charge. Referring the jury to the exact paragraph responsive to its question sufficed on this record. The majority relies on presumptions of following and understanding instructions, the absence of a repeated question, further deliberation, verdict/polling and counsel's explanation. Bollenbach's erroneous instruction and Eddings's refusal to consider mitigation are distinguished. It separately holds that AEDPA §2254(d)(1) bars relief because Virginia's merits adjudication was neither contrary to nor an unreasonable application of clearly established Supreme Court law. The original comparator omits this independent federal-habeas gateway.

Stevens's dissent begins from AEDPA and Boyde, finding the actual question defeats an assumption of understanding. Part I, not joined by Souter, addresses ambiguity in the longer charge and the failure to distinguish mitigation's role in disproving an aggravator from its independent role in choosing life after an aggravator. Parts II–IV, joined by all four dissenters, address the unanswered direct question, why counsel's earlier statement and further deliberation do not establish comprehension, the verdict forms' failure expressly to show life with an aggravator, and the polling record. The dissent treats the possibility that jurors were deliberating over the same unresolved meaning as at least sufficient under Boyde; it rejects a requirement to ask repeatedly. Its emotional inference from jurors' tears is contested by the majority and is not an established fact about their mental process.

The factual existence of the question, response, charge, mitigation evidence, verdict and polling is distinct from each writing's inference about juror understanding. Reconciliation must preserve Souter's Part I nonjoin and separately address actual entering Buchanan/AEDPA law.

## Gutierrez v. Ada, 528 U.S. 250

Source: https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/USREPORTS-528/pdf/USREPORTS-528-250.pdf. Saved PDF/text: `chunk2-control-sources/USREPORTS-528-250.pdf` and `.txt`. Full reading: 9 pages, pp. 250–258.

No. 99-51; argued December 6, 1999; decided January 19, 2000. Ninth Circuit, 179 F.3d 672, reversed and remanded. Souter wrote for all nine; no separate writing.

The Organic Act's majority denominator is votes cast in the gubernatorial election, not all general-election ballots. Context repeatedly refers to Governor/Lieutenant Governor and distinguishes the simultaneous general election. Congress also distinguishes votes from ballots elsewhere. Requiring a new contest despite an expressed majority would entail a cost absent for unsuccessful referendums. The recall provision supports the office-specific denominator. Later Delegate-election language does not establish a contrary meaning; redundancy avoidance yields to the stronger contextual reading, and the disputed words can cover both initial and successive elections. The opinion resolves the disputed statutory denominator, not every invalid-ballot or election-contest issue. Numerical recitations identify 609 double-voted ballots; do not invent a general rule for them beyond the question decided.

## United States v. Martinez-Salazar, 528 U.S. 304

Source: https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/USREPORTS-528/pdf/USREPORTS-528-304.pdf. Saved PDF/text: `chunk2-control-sources/USREPORTS-528-304.pdf` and `.txt`. Full reading: 16 pages, pp. 304–319.

No. 98-1255; argued November 29, 1999; decided January 19, 2000. Ninth Circuit, 146 F.3d 653, reversed; unanimous judgment. Ginsburg's Court opinion joined by Rehnquist, Stevens, O'Connor, Souter, Thomas, Breyer. Souter wrote a solo concurrence. Scalia concurred in judgment, joined by Kennedy. A seven-Justice opinion controls; judgment-only joins do not make its holding merely a plurality.

A defendant who elects to remove an erroneously retained juror for cause with an allotted peremptory, receives the full number of challenges and is tried by an impartial jury suffers no Rule 24 or constitutional deprivation. Ross supplies the distinction between impartial-jury protection and auxiliary peremptories. The Court refuses the Government's proposed compulsory curative-use rule; the defendant's difficult choice is still a choice. Rule 24 provided ten joint petit-jury challenges and one alternate challenge here. No deliberate trial-court manipulation was asserted; no biased juror sat. The remedy for actual substantial impairment is reserved, and Swain's automatic-reversal language is questioned as unnecessary and predating harmless-error rules.

Souter joins but reserves refusal of a requested makeup peremptory after a showing that the defendant otherwise would use every challenge for noncurative purposes. This defendant made no such showing, request, or objection to a seated juror. Scalia and Kennedy agree that voluntary use leaves the full allotment intact; they withhold join because the opinion unnecessarily addresses the counterfactual right to appeal after declining to remove a biased juror. Ordinary waiver principles might foreclose such a challenge, and they would leave that issue open. No contrary holding on waiver follows from their concurrence.
