## 2. Chronology, the state adjudication, and the federal judgment

1. The penalty trial occurred October 21–22, 1993. The jury returned the death verdict October 22. The respondent reports a January 13, 1994 post-trial sentencing hearing and final trial judgment January 14, 1994 (respondent pp. 13–15). 2. The Supreme Court of Virginia decided the direct appeal November 4, 1994, reported at 248 Va. 460, 450 S.E.2d 379. ## 3. Preserved threshold disputes: what the parties actually asserted

**Fair presentation and default.** Assignment 41 requested a mitigation-effect instruction; its argument invoked Woodson, Penry and the federal constitutional provisions. Assignment 44 specifically challenged the refusal to clarify that life remained an option after aggravators; its argument incorporated assignment 41 and expressly invoked the Fifth, Sixth, Eighth and Fourteenth Amendments. The state brief's fact statement also described the later jury question, requested answer, refusal and reference to the second paragraph (JA 265–270). These papers and the state merits language are reproduced below.

Respondent pp. 21–26 nevertheless asserts that the incorporated argument challenged only the facial charge and failed fairly to present the distinct response-to-question claim. It invokes Picard v. Connor, Anderson v. Harless, Smith v. Murray, the Virginia briefing rules and state decisions. It reads the state no-merit references as at most rejecting the initial-charge claim. Respondent expressly preserved this defense; it may not be treated as waived merely because the federal courts rejected it. Petitioner's appellate reply, JA 315–317, invokes the actual merits language and Harris v. Reed, disputes the premise of a procedural ruling, and complains that fair-presentation and Teague assertions were newly made on appeal. The source record thus contains both the defense and its rebuttal; it does not establish a new waiver ruling by this Court.

**Teague.** Respondent pp. 26–33 proposes that requiring a trial judge to supply new clarifying language, after a legally accurate charge and a correct reference to it, would be a new rule. It cites Teague, Butler and Saffle, and a series of federal appellate cases recognizing discretion to refer jurors to accurate original instructions. It distinguishes Bollenbach as federal direct review of an affirmatively erroneous supplementary instruction, not a state-capital constitutional requirement to rephrase accurate language. It treats the finality date, not the later federal appeal date, as the relevant Teague time. Its historical Graham, Johnson, Gray and other post-divergence citations are advocacy sources only; the simulated holdings control where supplied.

Petitioner pp. 21–29, especially note 13, describes the requested rule as application of the established prohibition against a capital sentencer deeming itself legally barred from considering relevant mitigation. It invokes Lockett, Eddings, Penry, Mills, McKoy and Boyde and compares facial approval with an unconstitutional application. The petition and appellate reply also invoke Bollenbach's requirement of accurate response to an expressed difficulty. The Fourth Circuit's substantive Teague discussion concerning the separate expert-assistance claim is not a Teague disposition of this instruction claim. No general new-rule determination, Teague exception, waiver or foreclosure of that defense is established by this extraction.

**Section 2254(d).** The certiorari petition p. 20 and merits brief p. 25 n.11 say the state adjudication contradicted and unreasonably applied the established mitigation rules. Petitioner's p. 17 n.9 additionally invokes the perfunctory nature of the state disposition and Cardwell v. Greene as a basis for independent review. Respondent p. 34 n.10 argues that a challenge to applicability of §2254(d) was not presented by the question or petition, which instead invoked the statute's contrary-to/unreasonable-application terms. The underlying merits and the statutory authority to grant a writ are separate source questions; the supplied statute contains both the merits-adjudication trigger and distinct alternatives. No subsequent Williams formulation is imported.

## 4. Record facts bearing on the question

The written charge consisted of instruction 1 (credibility/evidence), instruction 2 (eligibility and punishment alternatives), instruction 3 (aggravated battery), and B1 (mitigation definition and duty). The full written texts are copied below, together with the material oral charge. B1 expressly required consideration of a supported mitigating circumstance and left its weight to the jury. The original proposed final sentence forbidding refusal to consider mitigation was deleted; the granted B1 retained the express consideration command. C1, separately refused, expressly said that life could be chosen to give effect to mitigation after one or both aggravators were proved. The sources establish refusal of C1 and a renewed request after the question; the ordinary instruction's use does not itself establish waiver of the later objection.

The jury had the written instructions and exhibits. At approximately noon it asked about parole and received the quoted instruction to consider punishment under the evidence and instructions, without considering what might happen afterwards. This is context, not an additional merits question supplied for this event. The judge later permitted the jury to continue deliberating during the court's lunch recess if all twelve were together; the transcript does not justify assuming a full one-hour interruption of jury deliberations.

At approximately 3:15 p.m. the jury asked the precise life-or-death question reproduced below. The judge referred it specifically to instruction 2's second paragraph. Defense counsel asked for express clarification that life remained available after one or both factors, and objected when refused. At 5:31 p.m. the jury returned the death verdict based on vileness; the form recited consideration of mitigation and each juror assented when polled. The record excerpt shows no intervening question in that sequence. Respondent expressly states none was asked. A vileness-only verdict proves the stated basis of the verdict; a particular reason for not using future dangerousness remains an inference, not a jury finding. Tears recorded during the poll likewise do not establish the jurors' legal understanding or reasons.

Both briefs describe ten mitigation witnesses including Weeks. They identify youth (twenty at the offense), loss of his father, his mother's drug involvement and absence, upbringing by his grandmother in a violent and poor neighborhood, church and basketball participation, graduation and college prospects, leaving that support network, his girlfriend's pregnancy, later criminal associations, the circumstances surrounding the shooting, and expressions of responsibility and remorse. Those are accounts of testimony, not findings that every explanation was true. The State presented the offense facts, criminal record/probation violations and victim impact, and challenged credibility. The briefs identify JA 104–175 and relevant trial pages; the corresponding volume is recovered but not certified as fully inspected here.

The merits briefs and JA 207–215 agree that defense counsel corrected an initial claim that both aggravators were required and then said life remained possible even if both were found. The prosecutor said the death penalty did not have to be imposed and urged the jury to judge whether Weeks' testimony warranted life. Those arguments are material trial context, not judicial instructions. Petitioner's argument is that the later question exposed unresolved misunderstanding despite this context; respondent's argument is that the specific paragraph reference, whole charge, testimony, arguments, subsequent deliberation and verdict resolved or negate the asserted prohibited operation. Those competing inferences are presented as party arguments, not resolved facts.

response to see the second paragraph of Instruction # 2, 
which read as follows: 


If you find from the evidence that the Com- 
monwealth has proved beyond a reasonable 
doubt either of the two alternatives, and as to 
that alternative you are unanimous, then you 
may fix the punishment of the defendant at 
death or if you believe from all the evidence that 
the death penalty is not justified, then you shall 
fix the punishment of the defendant at life 
imprisonment or imprisonment for live [sic] and 
a fine of a specific amount, but not more than 
$100,000.00. 


(J.A. at 264 (emphases added).) The trial court overruled 
Weeks’s objection and request that the jury be instructed 
that it could impose a life sentence upon finding one or 
both aggravating factors based upon its belief that the 
jurors “just have to be drawn to that paragraph to find 
their answer.” (J.A. at 1344.) Following several more 
hours of deliberation, the jury sentenced Weeks to death. 


The Supreme Court of Virginia rejected Weeks’s 
claim, contained in assignment of error no. 44, that the 
trial court’s actions prevented the jury from considering 
relevant mitigating evidence on the ground that Weeks

“effectively presents no argument in support of” his 
claim. Weeks v. Commonwealth, 248 Va. 460, 450 S.E.2d 379,

is procedural and that Weeks has procedurally defaulted 
any objection to the trial court’s response to the jury’s 
question about its sentencing options. 


We disagree with the Commonwealth's characteriza- 
tion. First, in the sentence at the end of the same para- 
graph that states that Weeks “effectively presents no 
argument in support of,” inter alia, assignment of error 
no. 44, the Supreme Court of Virginia concluded, “We 
have considered these so-called arguments and find no 
merit in any of [them].” Id. (emphasis added). Second, the 
Supreme Court of Virginia stated the following near the 
end of its opinion: “[Djefendant raises a number of mis- 
cellaneous issues dealing with evidence, jury instruc- 
tions, and inquiries by the jury during its deliberations. 
We have considered all the arguments in support of these 
issues and conclude that none has any merit.” Id. at 390 
(emphasis added). Because Weeks’s jury made two 
inquiries and Weeks’s brief to the Supreme Court of 
Virginia made two assignments of error (nos. 43 and 44) 
regarding the court’s response to jury instructions, the 
Supreme Court of Virginia's use of the term “inquiries,” 
must have referred to both assigned errors nos. 43 and 44. 
The Supreme Court of Virginia therefore adjudicated 
assigned error no. 44 on the merits, allowing us to review 
this claim in a federal habeas proceeding.4 See Wright v.

Where, as here, the state supreme court has adjudi-

how it reached its decision, a federal habeas court must 
still apply the AEDPA standards of review. See id. A state

least minimally consistent with the facts and circum-

federal law as determined by the Supreme Court or an

“that reasonable jurists would all agree,” Green v. French, 
143 F.3d 865, 870 (4th Cir.1998), was not “ ‘minimally 
consistent with the facts and circumstances of the case,’ ” 
Wright, 151 F.3d at 157 (quoting Hennon, 109 F.3d at 335). 


The Eighth Amendment requires that a capital jury 
be able to consider and give effect to all relevant mitigat- 
ing evidence offered by the petitioner. See Eddings v. 
Oklahoma, 455 U.S. 104, 112-14, 102 S.Ct. 869, 71 L.Ed.2d 1 
(1982); Lockett v. Ohio, 438 U.S. 586, 604, 98 S.Ct. 2954, 57 
L.Ed.2d 973 (1978). Evidence about the petitioner’s back- 
ground and character is necessary for the sentencer “to 
make an individualized assessment of the appropriate- 
ness of the death penalty.” Penry v. Lynaugh, 492 U.S. 302, 


343 


319, 109 S.Ct. 2934, 106 L.Ed.2d 256 (1989). In 

the validity of a challenged instruction, the instruction 
“may not be judged in artificial isolation, but must be 
viewed in the context of the overall charge.” Cupp v. 
Naughten, 414 U.S. 141, 147, 94 S.Ct. 396, 38 L.Ed.2d 368 
(1973). In Boyde, 494 U.S. 370, 110 S.Ct. 1190, 108 L.Ed.2d 
316, the Supreme Court fleshed out these principles, 
holding that the proper inquiry in cases where a capital 
sentencing instruction allegedly prevents the consider- 
ation of mitigating evidence is “whether there is a reason- 
able likelihood that the jury has applied the challenged 
instruction in a way that prevents the consideration of 
constitutionally relevant evidence.” Id. at 380. Applying 
this rule to the petitioner’s case in Boyde, the Supreme 
Court concluded that there was not a reasonable likeli- 
hood that the challenged instruction prevented the con- 
sideration of mitigating evidence, in light of the fact that 
the instruction told the jury that “you shall” consider 
“[a}]ny other circumstance which extenuates the gravity of 
the crime” and that the jury was presented with four days 
of evidence at the penalty phase relating to Boyde’s back- 
ground and character. Id. at 381; see id. at 381-84. 


Applying the Boyde inquiry in Buchanan v. Angelone, 
522 U.S. 269, 118 S.Ct. 757, 139 L.Ed.2d 702 (1998), the 
Supreme Court upheld the constitutionality of the Vir- 
ginia pattern capital sentencing instruction, the very 
instruction at issue in this case, against a challenge on 
Eighth and Fourteenth Amendment grounds. The 
Supreme Court first noted that “the sentencer may not be 
precluded from considering, and may not refuse to con- 
sider, any constitutionally relevant mitigating evidence. 
However, the State may shape and structure the jury's

consideration of mitigation so long as it does not pre- 
clude the jury from giving effect to any relevant mitigat- 
ing evidence.” Buchanan, 118 S.Ct. at 761 (internal 
citations omitted). The Court further held, in pertinent 
part: 
The instruction did not foreclose the jury’s con- 
sideration of any mitigating evidence. By direct- 
ing the jury to base its decision on “all the 
evidence,” the instruction afforded jurors an 
opportunity to consider mitigating evidence. 
The instruction informed the jurors that if they 
found the aggravating factor proved beyond a 
reasonable doubt then they “may fix” the pen- 
alty at death, but directed that if they believed 
that all the evidence justified a lesser sentence 
then they “shall” impose a life sentence. The 
jury was thus allowed to impose a life sentence 
even if it found the aggravating factor proved. 


Id. at 762. The Supreme Court concluded that “[e]ven 
were we to entertain some doubt as to the clarity of the 
instructions,” the two days of testimony related to mit- 
igating evidence and the extensive arguments of the 
defense and the prosecution on the effect such evidence 
should be given in the sentencing determination demon- 
strated that there was “not a reasonable likelihood” that 
the instructions had precluded the jury’s consideration of 
Buchanan's mitigating evidence in violation of Boyde. Id. 
at 762-63. 


Given the Supreme Court's decision in Buchanan, 
Weeks is reduced to arguing that the trial court's failure 
to clarify a constitutionally sufficient instruction violated 
his Eighth and Fourteenth Amendment rights because the 
jury’s question placed the trial court on notice that it was 


345 


reasonably likely that the jury would apply the instruc- 
tion in a way that prevented the consideration of mitigat- 
ing evidence. We find this argument to be without merit. 
Following the jury’s finding of guilt on the capital mur- 
der charge, Weeks presented mitigating evidence as to his 
religious upbringing, the abrupt manner in which the 
events surrounding the shooting unfolded, and his feel- 
ings of remorse. Defense counsel argued extensively that 
the jury should give this mitigating evidence great weight 
in the sentencing determination. “[W]e think it unlikely 
that reasonable jurors would believe the court’s instruc- 
tions transformed all of this favorable testimony into a 
virtual charade.” Boyde, 494 U.S. at 383 (internal quota- 
tion marks omitted). In fact, the verdict form the jury 
returned specifically noted that in fixing Weeks’s punish- 
ment at death, the jury “considered the evidence in mit- 
igation of the offense.” (J.A. at 258, 1345-46.) At the 
conclusion of the penalty phase, following the announce- 
ment of the verdict in open court, each member of the 
jury was polled and confirmed the verdict. 


Under these circumstances, we believe that no rea- 
sonable juror would have understood the sentencing 
instruction to preclude the consideration of mitigating 
evidence even upon a finding of an aggravating factor. 
The Supreme Court of Virginia’s summary disposition of 
this claim, therefore, was neither contrary to federal law 
as interpreted by the Supreme Court of the United States 
nor an application or interpretation of Boyde “that reason- 
able jurists would all agree,” Green, 143 F.3d at 870, was

not “minimally consistent with the facts and circum- 
stances of the case,” Wright, 151 F.3d at 157.5
