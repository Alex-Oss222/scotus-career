# United States v. Martinez-Salazar: neutral record clarification

Source: official United States Reports record recitation, https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/USREPORTS-528/pdf/USREPORTS-528-304.pdf, pages 308–310. The full source is control-only. This paraphrase supplies objective proceedings, lower-court grounds and party positions; it omits the target Supreme Court action, lineup and reasoning. A fresh neutral context must validate the framing before modeling.

Martinez-Salazar and a codefendant faced narcotics and weapons charges in the District of Arizona. The court allotted ten joint peremptory challenges for the twelve-person petit jury under Rule 24(b), and one separate challenge for selecting an alternate under Rule 24(c). Gilbert's questionnaire and answers expressed a predisposition toward the prosecution. The defendants challenged him for cause; the government opposed the challenge, and the court declined it.

After twice objecting unsuccessfully, Martinez-Salazar used a peremptory to remove Gilbert. The defendants later exhausted their allotted challenges. They requested no additional petit-jury challenge. Rule 24(b) permitted the court to allow additional challenges in a multiple-defendant case. When the court read the final jurors' names and invited objections, counsel stated none. The record does not present a seated juror whose impartiality was challenged.

The Ninth Circuit found abuse of discretion in refusing the cause challenge; the government does not contest that finding on review. The court found no Sixth Amendment violation because Gilbert was removed and the seated jury's impartiality was not challenged. It nevertheless found a Fifth Amendment violation from curative consumption of a challenge, treating the full-allotment right as impaired and requiring automatic reversal.

Judge Rymer's dissent in part read Rule 24(b) as granting the stated opportunities to remove jurors without a guarantee that none would be used to correct a cause error. She noted that the defendants identified no other juror they would have struck and made no final impartiality objection. Her alternative argument rejected turning an assumed Rule violation into a due-process rule of automatic reversal. These are lower-court positions, not adopted Supreme Court holdings.

The parties' competing positions include whether the rule grants a full number of challenges or a full number after correction of all judicial cause errors; whether curative use amounts to legal compulsion; and what constitutional or rule-based interest remains when the full numerical allotment was received and no biased juror sat. The government's proposed curative-use requirement and its narrower no-impairment argument are distinct. An erroneous cause ruling, an actually denied allotment, deliberate manipulation and a biased seated jury remain distinct possible circumstances. No finding of deliberate manipulation is supplied.

Ross v. Oklahoma, 487 U.S. 81 (1988), is surviving pre-divergence authority: it distinguishes the constitutional right to an impartial jury from statutory peremptories and treats the state-created right according to its governing state rules. Its footnote 4 leaves the federal-rule question open. Its state-law result alone does not answer the construction of federal Rule 24. Swain's earlier language about impairment and reversal and the federal harmless-error provisions should be considered only if an impairment is found; no impairment or remedy is established by this packet.
