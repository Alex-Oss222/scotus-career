## Governing enactment and material findings

Proposition 198 was adopted in March 1996. Elections Code §2001 gives otherwise qualified electors, including unaffiliated voters, the right to vote for candidates regardless of political affiliation, subject to other law. Conforming amendments affect §§2151, 13102, 13203, 13206, 13230, 13300, 13301 and 13302. Each voter receives the same ballot, candidates appear with party identification, and the voter can cross party lines office by office. Section 15451 makes each party's highest vote recipient its nominee at the general election. Thus this is a partisan nomination system, distinct from a nonpartisan first round advancing the highest candidates without choosing an official nominee for each party. Party committee elections remain limited to registrants of the relevant party. All four plaintiff parties—the Democratic, Republican, Libertarian, and Peace and Freedom parties—had bylaws opposing outsider voting in their primaries.

The trial court found a significant but nonsevere associational burden. That is a constitutional characterization to be reviewed, not a binding historical fact deciding the present legal issue. Its underlying findings included:

- Crossover voting would increase. Evidence from Washington suggested roughly ten percent per office, sometimes twenty to twenty-five percent; some studies distinguished independents from members of competing parties. The California prediction was necessarily uncertain. Do not mix study denominators or turn those estimates into established California election totals.
- Some races over time probably would be decided by crossover voters, especially where competition was asymmetric. The possibility could influence campaigns and officeholders even without changing a particular winner. Campaign costs could rise, discipline could weaken, and activists' morale could decline.
- Organized raiding to nominate a weak opposing candidate was not shown likely to be important. Sincere voting for a preferred candidate and strategic voting for a second choice were separate phenomena. Rare raiding does not establish absence of outsider influence.
- Major parties remained vigorous in Washington. The court did not expect serious organizational debilitation; endorsements, campaigning, financial support, platforms, registration and turnout work remained available. Nominees' participation in governing committees could be addressed by party rules. Those alternatives do not literally restore control of the legal nomination.
- The court found the special effects alleged for minor parties inadequately proved, noting infrequent contested primaries and the different incentives of major-party voters. This was a finding on the trial showing, not a rule that minor-party autonomy has no protection.
- Proposition 198 received substantial voter support, but the court expressly rejected the contention that polling of party registrants constituted the parties' consent. Party rules prescribe their own deliberative amendment procedures; inferred popular approval is not their replacement.

## Reasoning below and both sides

The lower courts applied the Anderson–Burdick balancing inquiry, relying on the actual system and evidence rather than treating every outsider vote as automatically fatal. They characterized primaries as the public first stage in selection of officeholders, distinguished party governance from state election machinery, and treated compulsory registration as already an imperfect measure of political association. They found the burden significant but short of severe and the aggregate state interests sufficient: representativeness, broader debate, participation of independents and minority-party voters in safe districts, greater choice, competition and turnout, perceived fairness, political stability, and privacy. Their description of certain voters as effectively disenfranchised meant limited practical influence in safe districts, not legal ineligibility to register or vote. Empirical support for increased competitiveness and turnout was qualified. The courts rejected mere state paternalism about what would help a party, yet accepted electorate-centered representativeness and participation interests.

The parties' supported argument is that selection of the official standard bearer defines their political message and is a core associational act. Compulsory participation by people who retain an opposing affiliation can change that act even when their votes are sincere. Tashjian, Eu and La Follette protect a party's choice of participants; ordinary election regulation and the actual Timmons fusion restriction do not necessarily transfer the selection decision to outsiders. Endorsing a different candidate cannot undo the statutory nominee's status. The change in the nominating electorate is the asserted injury, so lack of proof that parties will collapse is not dispositive. A government desire to moderate a party's positions can itself be the burden challenged. The parties acknowledge lawful administration and primary requirements, subject to constitutional limits; their private-association analogy does not erase the white-primary cases.

California and the defending intervenors argue that public, state-financed nomination is an integral governmental electoral function carrying ballot-access advantages, not simply a private club's meeting. State authority to structure that function and electors' participation interests deserve weight. Tashjian addressed a party's invitation to independents, Eu internal governance and endorsements, and La Follette national-convention delegate rules; none automatically decides this blanket primary. California stresses the trial findings, the survival of party advocacy, the small practical differences among blanket, open and easily changed registration systems, the nondiscriminatory application to parties, and privacy without a public party declaration. Its merits brief asserts later participation improvements; those are litigation assertions unless their evidentiary basis is independently established, not substituted trial findings. The trial court's rejection of implied party consent must accompany any reliance on popular approval.

Analytical possibilities under the entering law include sustaining the system after actual-burden review; finding severe compelled association and testing precise interests and fit; or finding an insufficient fit even on a less-than-severe classification. These are available arguments, not vote predictions. The lawful distinction between electoral administration and control of a party's own official nominee must be addressed whichever path is selected. A source showing conventional open primaries or nonpartisan systems exist is not their constitutional adjudication in this case.

## Lease and statutory obligations

Mobil and Marathon each acquired approximately one-third interests in five North Carolina leases and paid more than $78 million in bonuses, with annual rentals separately due. Four leases were part of the Manteo Unit; the fifth had no submitted exploration plan. Each lease had a ten-year primary term, continued production/drilling provisions, and conditional exclusive exploration/development rights. It was not a guarantee of discovery, profitable production, unconditional plan approval, or every other permit.

Section 1 incorporates the Outer Continental Shelf Lands Act (OCSLA), §§302–303 of the Department of Energy Organization Act, regulations issued under those statutes existing when the lease took effect, future regulations under those statutes for prevention of waste, conservation of OCS natural resources and protection of correlative rights, and other applicable statutes and regulations. Preserve the named sources and future-regulation qualifications; do not paraphrase this as immunity from all future regulation. Sections 9–10 require operation under approved plans and compliance with exploration/development/production regulations and orders. Section 13(a) permits suspension or cancellation pursuant to OCSLA §5 and compensation where the Act provides it. Section 13(b) separately addresses wartime/national-emergency suspension on the Defense Secretary's recommendation with just compensation. Section 19 reserves ungranted rights, with particular noninterference and national-defense qualifications. Those reservations do not themselves answer whether the specified contracted approval process could be replaced without contract consequences.

Under 43 U.S.C. §1340(c)(1), an exploration plan must comply with OCSLA, its regulations and lease provisions; following necessary modifications, approval is due within thirty days of submission unless the statutory serious-harm condition, not avoidable by modification, requires disapproval. The harm categories include life (including aquatic life), property, minerals, national security/defense, and marine, coastal or human environment. Separate provisions authorize suspension/cancellation on their own predicates. Section 1334(a)(1)(A)'s national-interest/proper-development/transportation suspension is at the lessee's request; §1334(a)(1)(B) addresses threatened serious, irreparable or immediate harm. Do not remove the lessee-request condition from (A) or make every general environmental concern a finding satisfying (B). The Government also relies on the environmental-analysis/NEPA regulation, former 30 C.F.R. §250.10(b)(4), later recodified §250.110(b)(4), and the inordinate-permitting-delay provision (b)(6). Whether these incorporated powers authorize the actual disputed postponement is contested.

Approval of a plan is distinct from permission to carry it out. Under OCSLA §1340(c)(2) and CZMA §307(c)(3), operations requiring federal licenses/permits and affecting the coastal zone need state consistency concurrence, conclusive presumed concurrence under the actual statutory conditions, or a Commerce override based on consistency with CZMA objectives or national security. No presumed concurrence is established here. Discharges separately require an EPA NPDES permit and the applicable CZMA process; a drilling permit and later development/production approval also remain necessary. The plan-approval claim cannot silently become an entitlement to those permits. ## Chronology and competing governmental determinations

In July 1989 Mobil, North Carolina and Interior entered an MOU for additional environmental review of a draft exploration plan and lease suspensions. The MOU promised additional suspensions upon application for specified state objections, administrative/judicial challenges, appeals or regulatory delays, with two drilling seasons after their resolution. The draft plan was submitted in 1989; Interior's extensive report was completed in June 1990. Interior found the proposed well had no significant environmental impact under its review and deemed the plan approvable under its criteria. Those determinations do not erase North Carolina's distinct data and coastal-consistency objections.

North Carolina formally objected to the NPDES consistency certification in July 1990. Some sources date the letter July 16 and the lower opinion says July 17; no decisive one-day timing question is presented. On August 18, 1990 Congress enacted OBPA. Mobil submitted its final plan August 20. North Carolina objected to the plan's CZMA certification November 19. Both state objections remained unresolved during the material period, and Commerce declined override on September 2, 1994 because the requisite findings were unsupported. Whether OBPA materially deprived the companies of the separately purchased process, despite those barriers, is the contract question, not a settled causal fact.

OBPA §6003(c) prohibited new lease sales/issuance, exploration-plan and development/production-plan approval, drilling-permit approval and drilling on the defined North Carolina OCS. It lasted until the later of October 1, 1991, or forty-five days of continuous congressional session after an Interior report, following consideration of the scientific panel, certifying sufficient information for OCSLA responsibilities and explaining/justifying differences from the panel. Continuous session has its own adjournment exclusions; this was not simply forty-five calendar days. The panel recommended studies and information; it was not granted final permit authority. The Act required further environmental studies and consultation, but allowed the Secretary to disagree with panel recommendations through the stated explanation process.

The panel reported in January 1992. Interior's April 1992 report certified information sufficient for the first proposed exploratory well while withholding approval pending two additional studies. The lower majority treated the statutory bar for that plan as lifted after the required congressional period and later work as the Secretary's own initiative. Petitioners dispute that later work was independent of OBPA and emphasize future stages still needing statutory certification. The Government stresses that no other plans were before Interior and denies an indefinite ban on every future proposal. Studies were transmitted in July 1994. A general suspension was terminated in November 1994 after the studies and Commerce decision. OBPA was repealed in 1996. Repeal is a subsequent event, not proof that any prior breach was or was not material or retracted in time.

## Lower judgment and strongest litigation positions

It concluded no breach or basis for restitution had been established. It rejected a general OCSLA right to compensation whenever exploration proves unavailable. Newman dissented because the purchased timely-and-fair consideration entitlement had been negated, and ordinary contract/restitution principles did not permit the Government to retain the consideration after preventing the promised performance. Petitioners argue that the timely original approval process was a material part of what the bonuses bought; OBPA imposed new prerequisites across all six regulated activities, initially barring performance for at least thirteen months and practically prolonging it. ## Judgment, parties and posture

The Eighth Circuit's December 29, 1999 judgment, 200 F.3d 1128, No. 98-3439, affirmed Judge Charles A. Shaw's summary judgment for the defendants in the Eastern District of Missouri. Chief Judge Wollman wrote, joined by Richard S. Arnold; District Judge Charles R. Wolle, sitting by designation, dissented. Adam Henerey sued through his father under 42 U.S.C. §1983. Named defendants were the City of St. Charles School District, Principal Jerry Cook, and Assistant Principal David Watson, the individuals in both capacities. The appeal was submitted April 21, 1999. The summary-judgment standard requires favorable treatment of the nonmovant's supported evidence and reasonable inferences; the majority and dissent disagree over its application here.

The lower court did not decide that the condom distribution lacked any expressive content. It identified a material dispute over protected expression but held the rule constitutional and the sanction reasonably related to pedagogy. The approved grant addresses classification of election speech and the permissibility of genuinely viewpoint-neutral campaign enforcement; it does not supply a factual stipulation that enforcement was neutral.

## Facts and source status

Henerey was a sophomore seeking election as junior class president in March 1997. Candidacy was open to class members subject to meeting the council adviser and signing a contract to obey school rules. A council member told him flyers and posters needed advance approval. The school approved his campaign slogan. The campaign began April 7. After complaints that his posters covered competitors' posters and demeaned others, adviser Mary Stodden told him all materials required administrative approval. The opinion reports these complaints and the warning; it does not establish that the alleged posting misconduct was the actual ground of disqualification.

On April 10, election day, Henerey distributed condoms attached to campaign stickers in the school halls without seeking specific approval for condoms or indicating that the campaign would concern sex. The majority describes about eleven distributed; the dissent describes an offer of about twenty to requesting students. These accounts need not be converted into a single exact quantity. While Stodden was counting votes, a student complained; she informed Cook, who disqualified Henerey for failure to comply with Rule KJ-R. The subsequent count showed he had a majority. Thus do not write that Cook first learned he had won and then removed him in response to the result.

Other candidates distributed candy, gum or buttons without specific advance approval. The school said candy distribution was a longstanding practice with tacit approval. The majority found no record evidence disputing that practice and therefore no evidence of selective content enforcement. The dissent thought a jury could infer that the safe-sex message, rather than the approval process, caused the sanction. No trial resolved this disagreement. The only stated principal's ground identified by the dissent was failure to obtain approval.

## Rule KJ-R and educational function

The official published opinion quotes only relevant portions of the policy, with omissions. Those quoted portions are script-copied into `preparation/OT_1999CHUNK9_HENEREY_RULE_SOURCE.md`; that extract is not represented as the complete policy. The text requires a principal-approved distribution location; approval by the principal or assistant principal the previous day or earlier; more than one school day for material not readily classified or approved; and an approval stamp. It identifies unacceptable hate/hostility/violence material, pornography, obscenity, material unsuitable for school distribution, libel, commercial promotion, unapproved fundraising, and material the principal concludes would materially disrupt classwork, cause substantial disorder, or invade others' rights. The fundraising clause contains superintendent-or-assistant approval as its own exception. All material outside the unacceptable categories is acceptable. Do not flatten these distinct provisions into one general disruption exception or characterize the policy as guaranteeing a decision in exactly twenty-four hours: the lower court used that shorthand, while the quoted text expressly allows longer for difficult material.

The lower court classified the election as a school-sponsored curricular activity in a nonpublic forum. It relied on faculty supervision, a school-defined campaign period, the eligibility/contract requirements, prior approval, and instruction in leadership and democratic participation. It found no policy or practice opening campaign resources to indiscriminate public discourse. It reasoned that observers could attribute approved campaign material to the school. These are the lower court's legal classifications and record-based inferences, not a universal rule that every student campaign message is the school's own speech.

## Grounds below, including the alternative and dissent

The majority's principal ground was ordinary pedagogical enforcement of a known advance-approval requirement. It accepted tacit approval of conventional candy as a reason that different treatment did not establish content selection, rejected the facial prior-restraint and vagueness objections in this secondary-school setting, and found disqualification a reasonable sanction. Its generality analysis invoked the nature of indecency, vulgarity and disruption; it did not hold every unguided restriction on student speech valid.

The majority separately assumed protected expression and disagreement with its content, and still upheld disqualification. It reasoned that condom distribution could be read as encouraging teenage sexual activity and could carry apparent school approval, whereas candy was a conventional modest gratuity. It invoked maturity, decorum, curriculum, avoiding association with controversial sexual topics, and advance notice allowing accommodation of parents' objections. These are an independent appellate rationale and possible legal inferences, not findings that Henerey's actual message urged irresponsible sex, caused disruption, or produced a documented parental confrontation.

Wolle's dissent argued that a jury could find pretext, selective application, insufficient notice that condoms needed approval when other campaign objects did not, and disproportionate disqualification. Approval of the slogan and differential treatment could support the inference that the safe-sex message was the real cause. He rejected controversy over safe sex as enough by itself to suppress discussion in the campaign. He sought reversal of summary rejection so possible damages against one or more defendants could be adjudicated, not automatic election installation or a damages award.

## Strongest supported arguments and lawful paths

Henerey's record-supported position is that his individual campaign advocacy is protected; sponsorship of an election does not necessarily attribute every candidate's message to the institution. He challenges forum classification, prior restraint and discretion, the asserted neutrality of implementation, actual pedagogical grounds, notice, and sanction. Even if Hazelwood governs, genuine school purposes must explain the decision; controversy or a merely imaginable rationale cannot resolve disputed motive and enforcement at summary judgment under the current Settle holding. The school responds that a faculty-supervised election teaching leadership and democratic procedures is part of its program; approval rules protect orderly participation, maturity and attribution; candidates agreed and received warnings; and traditionally approved candy is a different comparator. It may defend the judgment on the principal process rationale without insisting that every controversial topic is prohibited.

Analytical paths include applying Hazelwood and sustaining a supported neutral sanction, applying Hazelwood but vacating for a material inference about actual grounds or permissible sanction, or treating the specific expression as independent student advocacy and applying Tinker. B. The proposed intersovereign offshore boundary is fixed by Exhibit A, from Point Hope in the Chukchi Sea to the United States-Canada border in the Beaufort Sea. The parties' memorandum states that they agreed to extend the continuous line westward to Point Hope using the resolved issues and uncontroverted principles. The United States would have exclusive exploration and natural-resource rights seaward of the line against Alaska and those claiming under it, with a reciprocal noninterference injunction. The exception preserves Alaska's section 8(g), 43 U.S.C. 1337(g), revenue rights and Paragraph C's lease-administration rules. Alaska would have the corresponding rights shoreward against the United States and those claiming under it, except within the Petroleum Reserve and Arctic Refuge, subject to 43 U.S.C. 1313 and Paragraph C. The reciprocal injunction has that same qualified scope.

Section 1313 preserves lawfully and expressly acquired federal parcels and their accretions, resources and improvements; lands lawfully held under state law; lands expressly retained or ceded at statehood, excluding a merely general marginal-sea retention; lands acquired in a proprietary capacity by eminent domain, purchase, cession, gift or otherwise; lands filled, built up or reclaimed for federal use; existing federal rights in lands actually occupied under claim of right; lands or interests held for tribes, bands, groups or individual Indians; and structures or improvements constructed under the federal navigational servitude. No new parcel-specific exception finding is requested.

Section 1337(g) concerns federal leasing and coastal-state revenue sharing, including its qualified Alaska treatment for tracts partly within the three-mile band, escrow treatment during boundary disputes, later adjustment at settlement, and specified interest and payment rules. It does not give Alaska the seaward title the proposed decree otherwise denies. The decree preserves the section's operation without calculating a dollar award or adjudicating every statutory application. Its exact text is supplied so that the preservation is not mistaken for an unqualified 27-percent award over all offshore revenues.

Section 1301(b) authorizes an offshore boundary fixed by coordinates in a final Supreme Court decree to remain immobilized despite subsequent coastal change. Exhibit A uses NAD 83/WGS 84 as authoritative. NAD 27 coordinates are conversion aids, not equal alternative authority. The schedule is preserved in the original PDF; no coordinate should be retyped or silently changed from OCR. The proposal does not ask the Court to conduct a new survey.

C. Three distinct lease categories govern accounting and administration. For leases subject to section 7 agreements, escrow funds are to be distributed under those agreements within 180 days after decree entry, and their applicable terms govern administration. Existing leases outside section 7 agreements but intersected by the new fixed line remain administered by their original lessor, who keeps all past and future lease revenues; after expiration, relinquishment or termination, Paragraph B governs the area's exploration and exploitation rights. The fixing does not change distribution from former leases in that category that expired before decree entry. Where an existing lease both falls under a section 7 agreement and is intersected by the line, the agreement controls escrow distribution and administration; Paragraph B governs the area after expiration, relinquishment or termination. Fixing the line does not change distribution from former intersected agreement leases that expired before entry.

Paragraph C(2) independently preserves each sovereign's rights and obligations toward lessees and third parties, whether arising under section 7 agreements or otherwise.
