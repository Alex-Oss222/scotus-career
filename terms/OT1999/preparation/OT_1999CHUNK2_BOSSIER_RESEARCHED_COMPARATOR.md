## Historical controlling holdings, explanations, and limits

1. **Mootness.** The 1992 plan remained relevant as the likely preclearance benchmark for the next plan, despite the 1998 election having occurred and a new census and plan expected before the scheduled 2002 election. Absent a successful subsequent section 2 challenge, the 1992 plan, rather than the materially different 1980 predecessor, would supply the baseline. The Court did not decide whether possible vacancies, failure to agree on a future plan, or opponents' claimed opportunity to seek a special election independently kept the controversy alive; it called those possibilities speculative and unnecessary to resolve.

2. **Purpose limited to retrogression in this vote-dilution setting.** Section 5 does not prohibit preclearance of a redistricting plan enacted with a discriminatory but nonretrogressive purpose. A jurisdiction still bears persuasion on both no prohibited purpose and no prohibited effect. Beer had construed the effect prong's shared phrase concerning abridgment as confined to retrogression. The majority declines to give the same phrase different meanings in the same sentence when it modifies purpose. Purpose has independent work: intended but failed retrogression is covered, and evidence of retrogressive intent can defeat preclearance without litigating the often complex effect question. The Court does not assert that purpose is invariably easier to prove (p.332 n.1).

3. **Distinct baselines, distinct proceedings.** In section 5's change-review context, abridgment is measured against the existing plan; in section 2 or a constitutional action the challenged system is measured against the proper nondiscriminatory alternative. Preclearance is a limited no-backsliding determination, not general approval or constitutional immunity. An independent section 2 or constitutional challenge remains available; section 5 expressly preserves subsequent injunction actions. Denying a nonretrogressive but ameliorative plan could itself preserve a worse existing system. The majority also cites section 5's federalism costs and possible constitutional concerns as support for the limited construction, without holding section 5 unconstitutional.

4. **Expressly bounded to dilution/abridgment.** The Court states at p.338 n.6 that its holding does not extend to violations consisting of an outright denial of an individual's right to vote, as opposed to an abridgment in dilution cases. It does not decide a general rule for all voting-rights denials. It notes that section 4 independently bars specified tests and devices. It does not hold that Fifteenth Amendment vote dilution is actionable: pp.334–335 n.3 expressly says the Court has never so held. It also expressly intimates no holding on section 3(c)'s scope (pp.333–334 n.2).

5. **Unreached discriminatory-purpose fact question.** Appellants challenged the district court's conclusion concerning discriminatory but nonretrogressive intent as clearly erroneous; they did not challenge the finding of no retrogressive intent. The statutory holding made the former challenge unnecessary to decide. Affirmance therefore does not endorse the district court's disputed record assessment as a separate Supreme Court holding. Nor does the opinion establish a distinct finding that the Board would have adopted the identical plan absent racial motivation. It recounts legitimate-purpose findings (prompt preclearance; implementation without precinct-line changes) and the lower court's intent conclusions; those are not interchangeable with a same-decision finding.

## Material precedent treatment

- **Beer v. United States:** Followed and extended from nonretrogression under the effect prong to the purpose prong for vote dilution. Its statement that an ameliorative plan may violate section 5 if it violates the Constitution is treated as dictum not previously applied to deny preclearance. The majority suggests that dictum more plausibly concerned outright denial rather than dilution and does not conclusively resolve that different situation.
- **Bossier Parish I:** Followed for section 2/section 5 separation, burdens, and the proposition that the nonretrogressive-purpose question had been left open. Its direction to consider effect evidence bearing on intent is not displaced as to a legally relevant intent inquiry.
- **Richmond v. United States:** Distinguished as a special necessity limiting effect analysis in annexations, preventing all annexations of areas with smaller minority populations from being blocked. In the majority's view the approved effect and disapproved hypothetical purpose were both retrogressive; Richmond does not establish broadly different meanings for the purpose and effect prongs.
- **Pleasant Grove v. United States:** Construed as reaching present intent to impair the future voting strength of future minority residents, including when there are no current minority voters. It is treated as anticipated retrogression in operation compared with the status quo, not as authority for prohibiting every nonretrogressive dilutive purpose.
- **Shaw I, City of Lockhart, Allen:** Used to preserve the distinction between section 5 preclearance and subsequent constitutional challenges and to confirm that a precleared plan can remain discriminatory or unconstitutional.
- **Miller v. Johnson and Lopez v. Monterey County:** Relied on for the limited preclearance function and federalism concerns; no new free-standing constitutional holding is entered.
- **Administrative practice:** Majority declines to defer to Justice Department views inconsistent with its reading of Beer, noting the Department also advanced the unsuccessful broader effect theory in Beer. It gives no deference to the Department's construction of the Court's own precedent.

## Every separate writing

**Thomas.** His short concurrence observes that the Board first sought preclearance almost seven years earlier. He characterizes the opponents as maintaining that a safe majority-minority district was necessary to elect a black Board member, and says three black members had meanwhile been elected from majority-white districts. He expressly acknowledges that these election results are outside the record and nevertheless sees them as illustrating that the intervention was unnecessary. This is not an adjudicated evidentiary finding and must not be inserted into the neutral record as proved fact. Souter expressly objects that these results and whether those members were minority voters' choices are outside the record.

**Souter, joined by Stevens, Ginsburg, Breyer.** He agrees on mootness and would deny preclearance because section 5 reaches intentionally discriminatory vote dilution even without retrogression. He considers Beer wrongly decided but accepts its effect holding under especially strong statutory stare decisis; respecting that precedent does not require enlarging its error into a distinct purpose provision. Section 5 uses the Fifteenth Amendment's unqualified language of denial or abridgment, and the legislative history shows Congress sought to stop successive substitutions of discriminatory devices that stymied litigation, not merely worsening the status quo. Covering changes does not logically require measuring all illegality against the old discriminatory system. Intent to subordinate minority political power can be identified without computing the difficult hypothetical nondilutive baseline required for an effects claim, so retaining Beer's limited effect inquiry alongside a broader purpose inquiry is coherent. The Department's consistent interpretation receives particular weight because of its central section 5 role.

Souter applies Arlington Heights in depth. Stipulated resistance to school desegregation; acknowledged dilutive effects and possible compact majority-black districts; polarization evidence; fracturing communities; ignoring ordinary school districting and incumbent-protection considerations; ignored requests for public participation; the changed pace after the NAACP proposal; and inconsistent precinct-splitting explanations together show intentional dilution. He considers the supposed preclearance safe harbor explanation implausible because the Board ignored that plan for more than a year before minority pressure rose. He regards the record as sufficient to deny preclearance under the Board's burden to negate intent and also as clearly erroneous under the contrary lower-court factual approach, even if opponents bore the burden. He distinguishes Miller's rejection of maximization from refusal even to consider any majority-black district. He does not treat the expert's limited voting data as unlimited proof; the cited record includes qualifications about elections in Air Force base districts and disputed statements.

On precedent, Souter reads Richmond as recognizing independent discriminatory purpose even where annexation fairly reflects the resulting electorate, not as an arbitrary annexation exception. He reads Pleasant Grove as explicitly condemning dilution of future minority votes where present minority voting strength was nonexistent and thus regards the majority as effectively overruling it. Federalism cannot justify withdrawing the very protection Congress prescribed. He acknowledges that the Court has not expressly decided Fifteenth Amendment dilution coverage, while considering the text and cases to support it; his view is not a Court holding.

**Stevens, joined by Ginsburg.** He stresses the ordinary text and the Department's quarter-century construction. The future phrasing of effect might plausibly invite temporal comparison, but the present phrase concerning purpose naturally addresses intentional racial denial or abridgment, without a retrogression limitation. He agrees with the earlier Beer dissents but says one need not disavow Beer to preserve the independent ordinary meaning of purpose. He also adopts Souter's fuller grounds.

**Breyer.** He agrees with Souter with one express qualification: he would not reconsider whether Beer was correct because it is an effects case and does not resolve intentional unconstitutional deprivation. He emphasizes the moving-target problem that prompted section 5. Where discrimination had already reduced black registration nearly to zero, additional retrogression could be practically impossible even though substitution of a new device perpetuated racial exclusion. His historical illustration is Forrest County's registration practices and Mississippi's later moral-character requirement; a hypothetical small registration improvement would not cure a deliberate discriminatory device. The majority responds that outright denial is outside its dilution holding and section 4 separately bars tests and devices. Breyer's discussion therefore must not be flattened into agreement that Beer should be overruled, or that he rejects its correctness on the same grounds as Souter.
