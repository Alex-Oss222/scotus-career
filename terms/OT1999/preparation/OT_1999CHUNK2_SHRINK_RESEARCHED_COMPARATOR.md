## Court's historical grounds and limits

The Court applies Buckley's contribution/expenditure distinction to state elections: contributions face the closely-drawn/sufficiently-important-interest standard, without treating a dollar in 1976 as a constitutional floor. The historical reasoning treats the threat as extending beyond consummated bribes to undue compliance with large contributors and the appearance of improper influence. That breadth must be tested against the actual entering simulated Russell rule; historical language supplies no silent amendment to simulated law.

The historical Court finds the Missouri evidence sufficient, rejecting a fixed new-study prerequisite while disallowing mere conjecture. It relies on Senator Goode's affidavit, reported contributions and Missouri scandals, the Proposition A vote as evidence of public perception, and conflicting academic work. It does not hold that a referendum overrides a constitutional right, or specify a universal minimum empirical showing. It reserves the possibility that a record undermining the proffered premises could demand fuller proof.

The operative challenged statewide ceiling was $1,075, adjusted biennially; other statutory tiers are background. The Court relies on District Court findings of adequate campaign funding and contributor-count data. Its historical test asks whether regulation makes association ineffective and prevents adequate campaign resources, rather than constitutionalizing a fixed real-dollar floor. Footnote 8 states that no access claim concerns another office. The individual challenger identified one would-be larger contributor. The Court does not adjudicate all future applications, and it refuses the dissenters' invitation to overrule Buckley when these respondents did not request that step. Footnote 7 rejects disclosure and bribery laws as automatically sufficient alternatives under Buckley; footnote 4 addresses the incumbency/equal-protection argument.

## Separate writings, with qualifications

Stevens (398–399) distinguishes money/property and liberty interests from direct speech, while accepting substantial constitutional protection for using resources to communicate. He would not give paid proxy expression the same protection as speaking directly. His Meyer footnote expressly preserves First Amendment protection where a payment prohibition forecloses a channel of communication. Do not reduce his position to an unlimited power to regulate money.

Breyer, joined by Ginsburg (399–405), places political communication and association alongside electoral integrity, participation and public confidence. He favors close independent review of proportional burdens, with deference to informed legislative empirical judgment but no deference that insulates incumbents from meaningful challenge. The record, office, indexing and postreform funding persuade him. He treats the low nominal ceiling as a real concern rather than dismissing it. He permits consideration of comprehensive reforms and would reconsider Buckley if it denied adequate room for constitutional solutions. This is his qualified additional rationale, not a separate holding of the six.

Kennedy (405–410) faults Buckley's split regime for pushing candid contributions toward less accountable soft money and issue advocacy. He sees the resulting distortion and incumbent advantage as undermining the state's asserted justification; contemporary disclosure technology matters to his alternative. He would overrule Buckley, but expressly does not foreclose a new comprehensive system limiting both contributions and expenditures. He substantially agrees with Thomas while retaining that reservation; he did not join Thomas's dissent.

Thomas, joined by Scalia (410–430), would overrule Buckley's lesser protection and apply strict scrutiny. Contributions enable chosen candidate speech and association; the additional intermediary does not justify reduced protection. He attacks aggregate campaign-health reasoning for disregarding an individual outsider's voice, objects to broadening corruption beyond quid pro quo, and demands attention to much lower inflation-adjusted ceilings and equal PAC/individual limits. His footnote 10 reports contrary record figures: spending in five statewide races fell from $21.599m to $9.337m; statewide primary spending from $14.249m to $1.625m; contested primaries from ten to one; participants from 32 to 11. He concedes those figures do not conclusively establish causation but regards them as substantial contrary evidence. He favors bribery enforcement and disclosure as less restrictive remedies and rejects efficiency as a ground to suppress protected speech.
