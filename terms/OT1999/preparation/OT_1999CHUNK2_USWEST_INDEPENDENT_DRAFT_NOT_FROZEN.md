## Actual statutory and regulatory setting

Section 222(a)'s confidentiality duty and section 222(c)(1)'s words govern. Except as required by law or with customer approval, individually identifiable CPNI obtained through providing a telecommunications service may be used, disclosed or accessed for the service from which it derives or services necessary to or used in providing it, including directories. Approval's general method is not specified in (c)(1). Aggregate customer information and subscriber lists have different statutory treatment; subscriber-list information is excluded from CPNI, so this is not a restriction on all customer data.

The pre-amendment section 222(d) permissions remain separate: initiating, rendering, billing and collecting for telecommunications services; protecting the carrier's rights/property or users and other carriers from fraudulent, abusive or unlawful use/subscription; and inbound telemarketing, referral or administrative services only during the customer-initiated call and with the customer's approval to use that information for that service. The inbound-call provision does not supply indefinite permission for later marketing.

The challenged 1998 total-service rule allows CPNI use to provide or market local, interexchange and commercial-mobile services within categories the customer already buys from that carrier. It permits sharing among affiliates providing service to a customer who subscribes to more than one category; it denies that affiliate-sharing permission when the customer does not. Cross-category marketing into an unsubscribed category requires approval, subject to the specified exceptions. The inside-wiring installation/maintenance/repair, mobile-service health research, and former adjunct-to-basic service permissions retain their distinct functions; the latter includes speed dialing, directory assistance, monitoring/tracing/blocking, return/repeat dialing, tracking/waiting, caller identification, forwarding and specified centrex features.

The original equipment/information-service restriction, competitor-tracking prohibition and winback prohibition are distinct provisions, not synonyms for the general approval method. No ruling in this draft independently validates each one or supplies an approval exception to language that does not contain it. Intervening changes to those provisions are preserved separately below.

Affirmative approval can be written, oral or electronic. Oral approval carries the carrier's burden to demonstrate compliance. Approval remains until revoked or limited, and notification/approval records must be kept for at least one year. Prior notice must describe the CPNI, specific recipients and purposes; state the customer's rights and carrier's confidentiality duty; explain precise granting/denying steps and that denial does not affect subscribed services; and be comprehensible and nonmisleading. Written notice must be legible, sufficiently large and readily apparent; translation of part requires translation of all. Notice may describe benefits of tailored offerings and the customer's affirmative-written-request option; it may not encourage freezing third-party access. It must state the duration of approval or denial until affirmative revocation or limitation. Solicitation must be proximate to notice. The former same-document provision is not treated as restored following the later removal.

## Adversarial paths

### First Amendment trigger and substantial interest

The speech-protective path prevails at the threshold for every Associate. Lanphere and United Reporting require review where use is selected against truthful, lawful marketing. Targeted communication is protected even though other marketing remains possible. Neither corporate form, carrier status, custody of information nor the statute's unchallenged baseline makes the agency's chosen approval method nonexpressive. The lower dissent's conduct-only alternative cannot displace the controlling trigger.

The interest is protecting confidential calling information from unknowing use or disclosure outside the existing chosen service relationship. It is not limited to embarrassment, receipt of unwanted mail or a judicially recognized constitutional property/privacy right. Call destinations, timing and service patterns supply concrete confidentiality interests. Paul P. recognizes a different limited constitutional residential-information claim and does not create ownership of all CPNI. Condon sustains a different information-distribution law on Commerce and structural grounds while expressly reserving speech; it is no shortcut around Central Hudson.

All eight recognize the interest as substantial. The six do not require competition to carry the result and would leave its independent sufficiency unresolved. Stevens and Kennedy likewise would not deny all privacy regulation, but require the particular selected use and consent rule to satisfy the remaining requirements.

### Advancement

The six would find the challenged approval mechanism materially advances confidentiality. It prevents a carrier from treating an unread notice as permission to repurpose confidential service records for a new service category. The customer's chosen service categories supply an administrable boundary for the initial relationship; affirmative approval enlarges that relationship without preventing the customer from receiving offers or the carrier from seeking permission. This responds to a supported confidentiality mechanism rather than a speculative chain from offensive mail to eventual harm.

The strongest contrary argument is the Commission's own statement that sharing inside an integrated firm does not present significant privacy concerns, together with permissions for marketing within existing categories and specified adjunct services. The six would not ignore that statement, invent outside disclosures, or characterize all internal handling as a proved injury. They would distinguish the physical recipient of the information from its purpose: a firm already has the data for the subscribed service, while the challenged choice concerns use for additional service markets. The total-service permission approximates the relationship already chosen; sharing between affiliates already serving that relationship has the same relevant limit. Inside-wiring and adjunct services are tied to the existing service, and health research is a distinct nonmarketing purpose. These limited permissions do not reproduce the same unconsented cross-category marketing the approval rule addresses. The fit need not be perfect or regulate every information source.

Stevens and Kennedy would find that the record does not adequately support this particular line. The agency's express assessment of integrated-firm disclosure and the breadth of permissible within-category marketing weaken the claim that the same internal information becomes a materially different privacy harm when used for a new category. Sensitivity alone does not establish every selected use as harmful. Their own United Reporting position and Kennedy's Edenfield/Lanphere reasoning require support for the actual differential treatment, not a general demand for empirical studies or a finding that no privacy interest can exist. They would not turn this into a universal all-sources-at-once requirement.

### Independent fit inquiry and the alternatives

The six would independently find a reasonable, proportionate fit. The Commission actually considered written-only permission, oral/written/electronic express approval and notice with opt-out. It rejected written-only permission as too restrictive, and selected the flexible express route because silence after unread notices may not reflect an informed choice. Approval can last until revoked; solicitation through several forms and other marketing remain available. The government need not prove that every nonrespondent opposes the use or eliminate every conceivable alternative.

The carrier's study is treated exactly: 33 percent expressly refused, 28 percent approved, and 39 percent hung up or asked not to be called again. The last group is not counted as a demonstrated privacy refusal. Seventy-two percent approved when approached after initiating contact; this shows a usable route, not consent from all other customers. Residential mail response was 6–11 percent and business response 5–9 percent; these are response rates, not express-refusal rates. The six would regard these mixed results as consistent with the agency's concern that inaction is ambiguous, while recognizing the informational burden and expense of soliciting express choices. They would not infer indifference or refusal categorically.

Stevens and Kennedy would independently find the fit insufficient even assuming advancement. The inability to read every silence as informed permission does not itself demonstrate that a sufficiently clear notice-and-opt-out alternative inadequately protects the relevant internal-use interests. The limited affirmative response is not evidence of widespread informed opposition; substantial numbers of willing recipients can lose effective tailored communication. They recognize that the FCC discussed opt-out and permitted oral/electronic approval; their objection concerns the adequacy of the supported comparison, not failure to consider an alternative or a written-consent-only premise. They would prescribe no replacement system and adopt no least-restrictive-means test.

Went For It's actual decision invalidated a public-record mail waiting period while reserving confidential-record misuse. The six would distinguish that record; the two would apply its insistence on concrete harm and proportionate actual burden without declaring this setting identical. Discovery Network, Rubin and Greater New Orleans require comparison of genuinely comparable permissions, not automatic invalidity whenever a statute has an exception.

### Statutory approval, avoidance and independent review

All eight would conclude that the general words approval of the customer, read with the confidentiality duty, permit an affirmative customer decision without unambiguously compelling this exclusive method for every general CPNI use. Chevron governs the statutory gap; it does not decide the First Amendment merits. AT&T v. Iowa Utilities Board preserves actual delegations and enacted limits rather than making every FCC choice valid.

For the six, the independent constitutional application just described defeats the asserted serious-doubt ground, and requiring oral, written or electronic affirmative permission is a reasonable construction of the general approval language. They would not hold opt-out constitutionally forbidden. For Stevens and Kennedy, statutory permission cannot save the challenged unconstitutional application, and deference is unavailable to the extent it would do so. Their textual agreement neither authorizes the actual burden nor adds a third independent invalidity theory.

Public Law 106-81 section 5, effective October 26, 1999 without a section 5 delay, is a different express rule: wireless call-location information requires express prior authorization except the enacted section 222(d)(4) uses; automatic-crash-notification information requires it except use in operating an automatic-crash-notification system. The three section 222(d)(4) permissions are: (A) provision to a public safety answering point, emergency medical service provider, emergency dispatch provider, public safety official, fire service official, law-enforcement official, or hospital emergency or trauma-care facility in order to respond to the user's call for emergency services; (B) informing the user's legal guardian or members of the user's immediate family of the user's location in an emergency involving risk of death or serious physical harm; and (C) provision to information- or database-management-service providers solely to assist delivery of emergency services in response to an emergency. These permissions do not create a general marketing exception. The amendment also inserts location into CPNI's definition, moves definitions to subsection (h), and adds separate emergency-service information duties. The Court need not construe the enacted subsection (g) cross-reference to subsection (i)(3)(A) here. Its existence neither retroactively establishes the 1998 agency's evidentiary support nor makes opt-out available for every CPNI category. No constitutional judgment on those new express rules is proposed.

## Remedy and intervening instruments

The six would reverse the identified constitutional and statutory grounds and remand for the takings and arbitrary-and-capricious grounds the lower majority did not reach. The lower dissent's takings analysis does not become a holding. No property ownership, compensation, arbitrary-and-capricious compliance, violation or sanction is decided.

The two would retain relief against the adjudicated approval application, without requiring invalidation of every collateral part of FCC 98-27 or the statute itself. They would return any necessary tailoring of relief to the agency instrument actually before the court and the intervening sources, without treating unresolved alternative claims as rejected.

FCC 99-223 retained affirmative approval in paragraph 95 and therefore did not remove the core controversy. Paragraphs 185/190 condition amended information-collection rules on OMB approval and a later effective-date announcement; none is admitted here. Paragraphs 187/188 separately remove winback and the same-document notice rule. Paragraph 189 postpones enforcement of amended safeguards (a)/(c) until eight months after September 3 release. The changed equipment/information-service treatment and safeguards cannot simply be declared immediately effective as a whole. Nor may reversal restore every old rule irrespective of those changes. No present noncompliance or sanctions follow. The statutory amendment's express wireless protections remain independently operative.
