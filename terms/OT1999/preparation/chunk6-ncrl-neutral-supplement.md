## Corrected facts and actual lower judgment

NCRL, NCRLPAC and Barbara Holt sought declaratory and injunctive relief. NCRL is a nonprofit issue organization, separate from its PAC, which expressly supports candidates. The opinion says NCRL is not associated with a candidate, party or campaign committee and does not have a major electoral purpose. It distributes voter guides discussing public issues and candidates' positions. Holt supervises both entities and is a registered lobbyist. These different entities and activities must remain separate.

The first sample voter guide sent to the Board inadvertently included a PAC endorsement sheet. The second omitted endorsements. The official told NCRL that the second guide still violated the corporate political-purpose expenditure prohibition. The opinion reports that NCRL refrained from distributing its guide and that NCRLPAC stopped soliciting without a disclaimer. The State's later litigation position limiting the laws to express advocacy was not a promulgated binding rule; local prosecutors' nonenforcement was not established. The Fourth Circuit found a credible threat, rejected lack of controversy, and rejected abstention. It noted no state supreme court certification procedure and no statutory route for federal-court certification to the Elections Board. See opinion paragraphs 2–18 and footnote 1.

The political-committee definition in section 163-278.6(14) covers a primary **or incidental** purpose to support or oppose a candidate or party **or** to influence an election. Covered entities register, maintain contribution/expenditure records and file organizational and financial reports under sections 163-278.7(b), .8, .9 and .11, backed by criminal sanctions. The Fourth Circuit affirmed facial invalidation of the committee definition. It refused the State's narrowing construction because that would remove the incidental-purpose language and the independent influence-election limb. See paragraphs 19–25; the saved text preserves the exact statutory quotation.

The lower decision also affirmed invalidation of sections 163-269, -278.6(16) and -278.19's corporate contributions/expenditures prohibition. It treated NCRL as protected under MCFL despite zero to eight percent corporate revenues from 1993–1995: the money came through customers directing phone refunds, and NCRL had no profit-making activity, shareholders or economic claimants. The lower judgment reversed invalidation of the session-time lobbyist contribution and solicitation restrictions in section 163-278.13B. Its donor-identification declaration under section 163-278.20(a) remained; the State challenged jurisdiction but did not argue those merits on appeal. See paragraphs 26–56 and footnotes 2–3.

The two granted questions concern committee burdens and the electoral-advocacy boundary. They do not themselves reopen every corporate-contribution, lobbyist, solicitation or disclaimer ruling. The corporate-ban discussion supplies context for the independent-expenditure boundary, but no independent direct-gift or session-ban disposition should be created from it. ## Entering law that the short brief omitted

Use `terms/OT1999/entering-law/OT_1999CHUNK6_NCRL.md`. The copied rules and their operative conditions govern rather than the brief's generic statement that no earlier same-term decision exists.

* Akins (June 25, 1997) rejects a categorical organization-wide major-purpose exemption for genuinely covered candidate financing. Actual cash transfers, conduits and other supported candidate-connected financing cannot be immunized by the organization's principal mission. Its statutory thresholds and distinct exclusions cannot be transplanted into North Carolina's text. Independent expression remains protected and particular organizational burdens remain reviewable.
* Maine Right to Life (September 14, 1998) permits a tightly confined contextual statutory express-advocacy construction: communication as a whole; only limited external context; unmistakable, unambiguous electoral direction; and no reasonable nonelectoral interpretation. It reserved constitutional vagueness. It rejects an automatic magic-words requirement without authorizing regulation of any election-related issue speech.
* The separate Maine Right to Life voter-guide decision requires real candidate connection. Asking candidates for public positions does not itself establish coordinated campaign spending or a contribution.
* Day's actual-nonprofit independent-expenditure protection and Stengel's distinct direct-candidate contribution rule must not be collapsed. McIntyre concerns truthful individual leaflets and supplies no universal exemption for organizations or campaign finance.
* Nixon/Shrink (January 24, 2000) supplies the actual proof-and-fit approach for candidate contribution limits. It does not decide political-committee coverage or validate the State's different burdens by analogy. Christensen, another May 1 event, is not entering law for this uncoordinated same-day matter.

## Strongest supported paths for independent assessment

These are analytical alternatives under Engine section 14, not claims that additional briefs were filed or preserved.

The issue-advocacy organization can defend relief because the North Carolina text expressly sweeps incidental influence on elections into a comprehensive committee regime, despite the absence of any genuine candidate financing in the record. A binding electoral boundary cannot rest on revocable litigation assurances. Even contextual express advocacy under Maine Right to Life requires a determinate electoral command that this endorsement-free guide has not been found to contain. The strongest counterargument is that a categorical major-purpose exemption would contradict Akins, while a literal listed-word boundary would contradict Maine Right to Life's statutory holding. A lawful affirmance must separate the concrete burden and vague coverage from those overbroad formulations.

The State can seek a narrowing construction and sustain tailored reporting for genuinely electoral activity even when incidental to an organization's overall work. Buckley's narrowing method, Akins's transaction-specific coverage and Maine Right to Life's contextual boundary support that legal possibility. Its strongest obstacle is the actual text's separate incidental-purpose and influence-election clauses, the lack of binding state narrowing, and the threat directed at a guide without endorsements. A federal court cannot simply rewrite the State's law or treat mere electoral influence as unambiguous advocacy. Any remand for a lawful narrower construction must identify the unresolved state-law task and avoid restoring burdens the Court has found constitutionally invalid.

A narrower merits path can reject both categorical propositions, sustain relief against the actual issue-advocacy coverage, and leave bona fide candidate financing, exact sponsor disclosure and distinct corporate-contribution restrictions governed by their own predicates. Remedy must specify whether the committee definition remains facially invalid, whether only the challenged application is barred, or whether an actual construction question is remanded. No registration order, donor list, fine, damages award or rewritten statute follows automatically.
