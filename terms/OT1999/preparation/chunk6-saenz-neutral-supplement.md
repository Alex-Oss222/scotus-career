The Fifth Circuit reviewed summary judgment for Deputy Beto Gonzalez and Brooks County, resolving genuinely disputed facts and inferences for the plaintiffs. On that account, Gonzalez and Reserve Deputy Antonio Martinez stopped behind Zuniga's pickup at a stop sign on June 19, 1995. The truck stayed still about fifteen minutes. Martinez wanted to investigate. Gonzalez, the senior officer, identified the driver, described his repeated drunkenness, and instructed Martinez to leave him alone. A footnote records Gonzalez's stated concern about smell, soiling or vomiting in the patrol car. At that point the officers had suspicion, rather than concrete proof, of intoxication; the account included the long stop and a wide turn. The saved primary text preserves the exact statements, without requiring this summary to recast them as adjudicated findings.

Minutes later the officers were called to the collision. Zuniga had struck an oncoming vehicle, killing Graciela Saenz and Jose Hinojosa and injuring three others. His blood alcohol concentration was .21. Plaintiffs allege that Gonzalez used his authority to prevent an available police response and that Brooks County tolerated his interference with junior officers' drunk-driving enforcement. They did not allege that Gonzalez knew these particular victims were on the road or personally restrained them. No private-rescue undertaking was obstructed on this record, and neither victims nor driver were in police custody when he was left at the intersection.

The district court found no constitutional injury, granted Gonzalez immunity on that predicate, and rejected the county claim for the same absence of a federal deprivation. It severed and remanded state-law claims. The court of appeals affirmed the constitutional merits ground. It distinguished Ross, involving suppression of a known drowning child's available rescue, and Salas; it stated that its circuit had not adopted the proposed general danger theory. It treated the instruction as nonenforcement and stressed the unknown victims, absence of a targeted deprivation and private source of the danger. It did not conduct a separate detailed June 1995 notice analysis after finding no right. That matters: individual immunity and absence of a federal injury have different consequences for the county.

The private Heldenfels entity in the caption is not one of the two appellees on this federal summary-judgment appeal. Its presence supplies no governmental-action finding or independent federal judgment for this Court to decide. The state-law remand remains distinct.

## Entering law and competing paths

Pinder preserves the distinction between omission of police protection and an official's own affirmative deprivation of otherwise available protection, but does not hold that every affirmative assurance or instruction creates a constitutional injury. Stemler/Chipman controls knowing actual restraint and involuntary transfer into appreciated danger; it expressly reserves a general noncustodial danger rule. Lewis applies a harmful-purpose standard to rapid, pressured pursuit choices and does not mechanically govern a fifteen-minute deliberative interval. None supplies a general right to arrest a private wrongdoer. DeShaney, Daniels and the actual Collins holding remain essential.

The plaintiffs' strongest supported path treats the senior officer's command as effective use of public authority to stop an independently willing junior officer's immediate investigation, causally increasing exposure rather than merely failing to respond. The absence of victims' names need not alone determine whether the official created a foreseeable danger. The available deliberation and stated nonprotective concern may be relevant to culpability. The strongest counterargument is that Martinez's proposed intervention was itself discretionary public protection; stopping it need not deprive road users of a preexisting private safeguard, cause the driver's intoxication or force anyone into danger. Foreseeability and callousness do not alone establish an unconstitutional deprivation.

The defendants' strongest path treats a supervisory choice not to investigate as nonprotection within DeShaney, without converting a grammatical affirmative command into the State's creation of danger. No victim was restrained, transferred into danger, deprived of private rescue or known to need immediate assistance. The strongest counterargument is Pinder's refusal to treat lack of custody as dispositive of every affirmative-deprivation theory: coercive interference with an actual available response cannot be dismissed solely because the future victim was unidentified. The controlling question remains what protection was actually removed, through what attributable act, with what causal effect and constitutional culpability.

A narrower path can reject an automatic known-victim or custody prerequisite without finding that this record establishes liability. If the merits ground is vacated, causation, culpability and any independent summary-judgment ground must retain their actual procedural footing. Conversely an affirmance may rest on the particular nonprotection characterization without declaring every noncustodial danger claim impossible.

An independently sufficient individual-immunity path must use law available on June 19, 1995. Pinder (1996), Lanier (1997), Lewis (1998) and Stemler (1998) may supply current analytical rules but cannot themselves have given notice earlier. Elder (1994), Harlow, Anderson, DeShaney, Ross and other actual date-eligible decisions may bear on the notice question; no factual-twin or Supreme-Court-only requirement applies. A merits reserve plus personal immunity does not automatically defeat the county claim. Monell requires the county's own policy/custom and attribution; those allegations are not proved merely because the original dismissal used a different rationale.
