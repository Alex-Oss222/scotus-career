## Weisgram v. Marley Co., No. 99-161

**Chronology and route.** Argued January 18, 2000; event placement February 22, 2000. Certiorari to the Eighth Circuit, 169 F.3d 514 (1999). No participation disqualification was identified in the public source.

**Record.** Bonnie Weisgram died from carbon monoxide poisoning during the December 30, 1993 fire at her home. Her son, individually and for her heirs, brought a diversity wrongful-death action in the District of North Dakota, alleging that a defect in Marley's electric baseboard heater caused the fire and death. The suit was consolidated below with State Farm's property-insurance recovery action; State Farm was dismissed from the Supreme Court appeal after certiorari was granted. These allegations are not new findings that the heater was defective or caused the fire.

**Preservation and judgment below.** Marley objected to the reliability of three experts before and during trial. It sought Rule 50(a) judgment at the close of the plaintiffs' evidence and again at the close of all evidence, then renewed its request after the jury verdict and alternatively sought a new trial under Rules 50 and 59. The District Court denied those motions. The Eighth Circuit held the expert evidence inadmissible, examined the remaining evidence favorably to the verdict winner, found it insufficient under state law, and directed judgment for Marley. Its characterization that the plaintiffs had a fair opportunity and that the case was not close is a lower-court assessment, not an independent new factual finding.

**Granted scope and relief.** The granted question concerns appellate power to direct judgment after deletion of erroneously admitted evidence. The Eighth Circuit's exclusion determination and its conclusion that the remaining evidence could not sustain a state-law claim are outside that grant. The request is for further trial-court proceedings or a new-trial determination rather than a new Supreme Court appraisal of the three experts. The public record recitation reports that Weisgram's rehearing petition pressed state-law error, lack of appellate power, and insufficient deference to the trial court's evidentiary rulings, rather than a specific additional evidentiary proffer or other independently specified new-trial ground. That reported litigation history may be used as such; it is not an inferred forfeiture arising from missing research documents.

**Preexisting terms.** The companion reproduces the then-operative Rule 50 in full, including its ten-day renewed-motion and new-trial periods, the specified-grounds requirement, conditional new-trial treatment, and subdivision (d)'s permission for an appellee to assert alternative new-trial grounds. Rule 50(d) does not say that a new trial must be granted. Section 2106 authorizes an appellate court to alter a judgment and to direct appropriate further proceedings as justice requires. No present-day amendment to Rule 50 supplies the applicable deadline.

**Preexisting authority.** *Neely v. Martin K. Eby Construction Co.*, 386 U.S. 317, 322–330 (1967), recognized appellate authority to direct judgment after reversal of a trial court's denial of a properly preserved Rule 50 motion, while requiring attention to grounds for a new trial better evaluated by the trial judge. It identified opportunities for the verdict winner to present new-trial grounds when the posttrial motion is made, in the appellate proceeding, and by seeking rehearing after appellate reversal. That prior decision addressed insufficient proof; whether the same authority extends to a record made insufficient by appellate exclusion is the live question here. [Prior decision](https://tile.loc.gov/storage-services/service/ll/usrep/usrep386/usrep386317/usrep386317.pdf).

**Source and limits.** Official U.S. Reports, 528 U.S. 444–448 & nn.1, 3–4; 450 n.6; 455–456 & nn.11–13, used here only for the procedural recitation and quoted preexisting text. [Official source](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/USREPORTS-528/pdf/USREPORTS-528-440.pdf). The collection's original petition, merits briefs, and joint appendix were located; no absent-source assumption is adopted. The official recitations support this bounded question. A proposed reopening of exclusion, state-law sufficiency, or a claimed specific new proffer would require the pertinent original record and authorized expansion of scope.

## Hunt-Wesson, Inc. v. Franchise Tax Board, No. 98-2043

**Chronology and route.** Argued January 12, 2000; event placement February 22, 2000. Certiorari to the California Court of Appeal, First Appellate District, No. A079969, judgment December 11, 1998; California Supreme Court review was denied. No participation disqualification was identified.

**Record and statutory operation.** Hunt-Wesson is successor to a corporation domiciled outside California. During the tax years in dispute, California calculated its apportioned business-income share by averaging the California-to-total property, payroll, and sales ratios. The corporation incurred interest expense and received nonunitary interest and dividend income. California reduced the interest deduction by reference to that income. The parties accepted that the nonunitary receipts at issue had no independent nexus to California.

**Material qualifications.** The relevant version of section 24344 first allows interest expense against interest income subject to formula allocation. It then allows the balance of interest expense only insofar as that balance exceeds interest and dividend income not subject to formula allocation. Dividends deductible under section 24402 are expressly excepted; section 24402 concerns dividends declared from income already included in the measure of the specified California taxes upon the declaring taxpayer. The remaining interest expense is directly offset against the nonformula interest and dividend income. These are distinct statutory steps, not a flat denial of every interest deduction whenever any exempt receipt exists. Both provisions are reproduced in the companion.

**Dispute and litigation positions.** Hunt-Wesson maintained that the forms used for the relevant years required the corporation first to separate interest relating to nonunitary activities, leaving only unitary-business interest subject to the challenged offset. California acknowledged how the forms worked but disputed that construction of the statute, asserting that the statute covered all interest expense. Hunt-Wesson reported no nonunitary interest expense for the years at issue. California defended the deduction limit as an allocation response to fungible money and tax arbitrage, rather than conceding that it taxed the nonunitary receipts. Hunt-Wesson challenged the offset under both the Due Process and Commerce Clauses. The state appellate judgment sustained the assessment.

**Source and limits.** Official U.S. Reports, 528 U.S. 460–465, 468–469 statutory appendix; only objective procedural facts, acknowledged disputes, and statutory text are carried here. [Official source](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/USREPORTS-528/pdf/USREPORTS-528-458.pdf). No audit amount, final refund sum, tracing finding, or choice between the parties' disputed statutory constructions is supplied. Any such premise would need separate support. The original collection item was located; the present bounded issue can use the published recitation without assuming unavailable papers or taxpayer default.

## Baral v. United States, No. 98-1667

**Chronology and route.** Argued January 18, 2000; event placement February 22, 2000. Certiorari to the D.C. Circuit; judgment order at 172 F.3d 918 (1999), affirming federal summary judgment for the United States. No participation disqualification was identified.

**Undisputed dates and amounts.** Baral's employer withheld $4,104 from his wages during 1988. Baral remitted a further $1,100 as estimated income tax in January 1989. The ordinary filing date was April 15, 1989; he obtained an extension to August 15, 1989. He filed on June 1, 1993, and the IRS assessed the reported liability on July 19, 1993. The return claimed a $1,175 overpayment and asked that it be credited to his outstanding 1989 tax obligations. This is a requested credit of a claimed overpayment, not a request to disregard the ordinary remittances entirely.

**Narrow dispute.** The IRS did not dispute that the claim was filed within the applicable section 6511(a) filing period. It denied recovery under the separate ceiling in section 6511(b)(2)(A): the amount cannot exceed tax paid during the immediately preceding three years plus any filing extension. The parties' relevant lookback runs from February 1, 1990 through June 1, 1993, counting the four-month extension. The government treated both remittances as paid April 15, 1989 under section 6513(b). Baral argued payment occurred no earlier than filing the return and more precisely on the July 19 assessment. He also argued withholding and estimated tax were separate taxes converted to income tax upon the return. These are opposing legal positions, not fresh conclusions.

**Preexisting terms.** The companion reproduces section 6513(b)(1) and (2), including the chapter 24/section 31 qualifications on withholding and the express disregard of filing extensions when fixing the estimated-income-tax deemed payment date. Section 6513's dates apply for sections 6511 and 6512. Section 6151(a)'s payment direction operates without assessment or notice and demand; section 6315 describes estimated income-tax payment as payment on account of subtitle A income taxes. These provisions must be kept distinct from section 6511's claim-filing deadline and amount ceiling.

**Preexisting payment/deposit authority.** *Rosenman v. United States*, 323 U.S. 658, 659–663 (1945), involved an estate-tax remittance made under protest to avoid interest and penalties, placed in a suspense account before assessment. The Court treated that arrangement as a deposit resembling a cash bond; the relevant payment occurred when the balance was applied to the later deficiency. It rejected treating the same transaction as a deposit for government-interest purposes but as a payment to bar the taxpayer's refund claim. Baral invokes that prior case for his assessment-based argument; how its estate-tax arrangement relates to the two specified income-tax remittances and section 6513 is the present interpretive dispute, not a neutral conclusion. [Prior decision](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/USREPORTS-323/pdf/USREPORTS-323-658.pdf).

**Source and limits.** Official U.S. Reports, 528 U.S. 432–435, 436–437, used for undisputed facts, opposing submissions, and quoted preexisting provisions. [Official source](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/USREPORTS-528/pdf/USREPORTS-528-431.pdf). No general fact about every tax remittance, estate-tax deposit, or remittance during audit follows from this record. Those are not the two remittance types before the Court. The original collection item was located. No reconstructed assessment, payment, or tolling fact is adopted.

## Rotella v. Wood, No. 98-896

**Chronology and route.** Argued November 3, 1999; event placement February 23, 2000. Certiorari to the Fifth Circuit, 147 F.3d 438 (1998), affirming summary judgment on limitations grounds. No participation disqualification was identified.

**Record and concessions.** Rotella entered Brookhaven Psychiatric Pavilion in February 1985 with a diagnosis of major depression and was discharged in 1986. In 1994 the parent company and a director pleaded guilty to criminal fraud connected with improper company-doctor relationships; Rotella learned of that plea in 1994. He filed the civil RICO action in 1997, alleging that doctors and related businesses had admitted, treated, and retained him to generate profits rather than for medical reasons. Alleged injuries included unnecessarily extended confinement, loss of personal items, and fraudulent charges for unnecessary care. No finding that the defendants committed those alleged acts is added here.

Rotella conceded that he knew of the asserted injury by 1986 at the latest and did not dispute that the elements of his civil RICO cause of action were complete by then. The lower courts applied injury discovery, producing a 1990 expiration. Rotella sought an accrual rule awaiting discovery, actual or reasonable, of both injury and a racketeering pattern. An amicus requested injury-occurrence accrual; the parties did not focus their advocacy on that alternative. A claim whose injury precedes completion of the pattern is not the record presented by this concession.

**Preexisting law and textual limits.** Section 1961(5)'s minimum two predicate acts and ten-year interval form part of the statutory pattern definition; they are not themselves a ten-year period for filing a civil action. Section 1964(c) concerns injury to business or property and authorizes treble damages and fees. *Agency Holding Corp. v. Malley-Duff*, 483 U.S. 143 (1987), supplied a uniform four-year civil RICO period. Any operative simulated *Klehr* rule must be taken from the entering-law slice, not imported from the historical recitation. The current packet does not establish actual diligence, fraudulent-concealment tolling, a separate later injury, or a new predicate act that caused new injury; it also does not establish their absence through research silence.

**Source and limits.** Official U.S. Reports, 528 U.S. 551–555 & nn.1–2, 558–559 n.4, used only for recorded facts, concessions, prior statutory terms, and advocacy scope. [Official source](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/USREPORTS-528/pdf/USREPORTS-528-549.pdf). The original collection item was located. No reconstruction premise is needed to frame the conceded-complete 1986 claim; a proposed tolling award or independent later-injury disposition would require the supporting record.

## Rice v. Cayetano, No. 98-818

**Chronology and route.** Argued October 6, 1999; event placement February 23, 2000. Certiorari to the Ninth Circuit, 146 F.3d 1075 (1998), affirming summary judgment for the Governor in his official capacity; District Court opinion at 963 F. Supp. 1547 (D. Haw. 1997). No participation disqualification was identified.

**Challenged electoral provision.** OHA was created by a 1978 amendment to Hawaii's Constitution and is administered by nine trustees. Article XII, section 5 requires trustees to be Hawaiians elected by qualified voters who are Hawaiians. The statutory electorate is the broader section 10-2 category, not only the narrower category called native Hawaiians. The companion reproduces both definitions in full, including the sovereignty, subsistence, continued-residence, and blood-quantum language. The distinction between those classes must survive any account of the election or funds.

**Funds and governmental arrangements.** The Admission Act section 5(f) public trust encompasses five purposes, reproduced in the companion: public education; betterment of native Hawaiians as defined in the Hawaiian Homes Commission Act; widespread farm and home ownership; public improvements; and lands for public use. OHA administers a 20% share of revenue from the approximately 1.2 million acres granted under section 5(b), earmarked by state law for native-Hawaiian betterment, together with state/federal appropriations and private donations for native Hawaiians and/or Hawaiians. The approximately 200,000 acres under the Hawaiian Homes Commission Act are administered by a different agency. OHA's statutory responsibilities also include coordinating programs, evaluating other agencies' effects, advocacy, grants and donations, and receiving reparations. These statutory arrangements do not resolve the contested legal characterization of the electorate or the scope of congressional power.

**Record and litigation scope.** Rice is a Hawaii citizen descended from preannexation island residents but not from the pre-1778 people required by the statute. In March 1996 he applied to register for OHA trustee elections, struck the form's assertion that he was Hawaiian, and was denied registration. He challenged the voting exclusion under the Fourteenth and Fifteenth Amendments. His separate challenge to the August 1996 sovereignty election had been rejected in 941 F. Supp. 1529 and is not before the Court. He did not challenge the legality of OHA's underlying programs or administrative trusts in the appealed case.

**Positions below and presented.** The District Court treated the federal and state relationship with native Hawaiians as analogous to the guardian-ward relationship in Indian law, and used *Morton v. Mancari* rational-relationship analysis. The Ninth Circuit likewise relied on the connection between beneficiaries and fiduciaries, while describing the provisions as facial racial classifications. Hawaii defended the electorate as ancestry/political-community based; alternatively it invoked the federal Indian-law relationship and delegated responsibility, special-purpose-district voting precedents, and beneficiary selection of trustees. Those legal arguments are not findings that native Hawaiians are a federally recognized tribe, that ancestry is constitutionally race-neutral, or that Congress can authorize the particular voting qualification.

**Source and limits.** Official U.S. Reports, 528 U.S. 507–511, reproducing the statutes and lower proceedings; 514 and 518–523 for the State's presented arguments only. [Official source](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/USREPORTS-528/pdf/USREPORTS-528-495.pdf). Historical accounts of Hawaii and the existence or exact scope of a federal trust relationship are not converted into adjudicated neutral facts. No resolution of program-benefit eligibility, tribal membership, tribal internal elections, or general federal recognition power is supplied. The original collection item was located. No routine or material reconstruction premise is adopted.

## Roe v. Flores-Ortega, No. 98-1441

**Chronology and route.** Argued November 1, 1999; event placement February 23, 2000. Certiorari to the Ninth Circuit, 160 F.3d 534 (1998), in a section 2254 habeas case from the Eastern District of California. No participation disqualification was identified.

**Plea and missed notice.** With appointed counsel Nancy Kops and a Spanish interpreter, Flores-Ortega pleaded guilty in October 1993 to second-degree murder under *People v. West*, permitting denial of the act while acknowledging sufficient evidence to convict. The prosecutor moved to dismiss two assault counts and strike the weapon enhancement. On November 10 he received 15 years to life. The trial judge told him he could appeal within 60 days and receive appointed appellate counsel if indigent. Counsel's file recorded a reminder to bring appeal papers, but no timely notice was filed. Flores-Ortega attempted to file on March 24, 1994; the clerk rejected the notice as late. State habeas efforts failed.

**Findings must remain distinct.** Federal habeas alleged that counsel had promised to file an appeal. The magistrate held an evidentiary hearing limited to that promise and found that Flores-Ortega had not proved it by a preponderance. The magistrate also found no consent to nonfiling, little or no understanding of what an appeal meant, and that a conversation with counsel had occurred. Counsel had no specific recollection of a promise. The District Court adopted the findings. Failure to prove a promise is not a finding that counsel gave a complete explanation, that Flores-Ortega knowingly declined an appeal, or that no conversation occurred. Conversely, the note and conversation do not by themselves establish the contents of any advice or an express instruction to appeal.

**Procedural ground and remedy below.** Flores-Ortega filed the federal habeas petition on July 27, 1995, before AEDPA's enactment. The date is affirmatively stated in the original certiorari petition, the joint appendix's reproduced petition at page 46, and the magistrate's account at page 153. The District Court denied relief because it viewed the Ninth Circuit's *Stearns* no-consent rule as new under *Teague*. The Ninth Circuit held *Stearns* tracked the earlier *Lozada* decision, reversed, and directed a conditional writ unless the state court allowed a new appeal. Its operative rule allowed relief upon proof that failure to file occurred without the defendant's consent. The granted setting is failure to file a notice after a guilty plea; the record is not an established instruction-to-appeal case. The requested habeas remedy concerns restoration of an appeal, not a Supreme Court determination of the underlying murder conviction's merits.

**Open factual limits.** The supplied public recitations do not establish the full content of the counsel-client conversation. They describe roughly 90 days of post-sentencing lockup/evaluation and inability to communicate as apparent circumstances, not as a separately established legal excuse. No express appeal waiver is asserted in those recitations. Do not infer from the guilty plea alone a waiver of all appellate claims, an agreed precise sentence, frivolous appellate prospects, or the absence of such prospects. Ordinary *Strickland*, *Jones v. Barnes*, *Rodriquez*, *Hill*, and *Penson* authorities must be read in their own scope; any completed simulated *Peguero* or *Smith v. Robbins* must come from the entering-law slice, not the historical same-matter source.

**Source and limits.** Official U.S. Reports, 528 U.S. 473–476 and the quoted magistrate findings; 488 n.1 supplies the reported absence of an asserted waiver and the different sentencing requests, without importing any Justice's conclusions. [Official source](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/USREPORTS-528/pdf/USREPORTS-528-470.pdf). The original petition, merits briefs, and joint appendix were located. The filing date was also checked in the [original collection item](https://archive.org/details/micro_IA40385014_0265), using its petition and joint appendix text; those originals were searched and the pertinent affirmative passages read, not read in full. Published findings sufficiently support evaluation of the applicable standard and the distinction between proven and unresolved facts. An adjudication of a disputed conversation's precise content would require record evidence and appropriate factfinding, not a reconstruction premise.
