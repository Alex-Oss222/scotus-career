I. STATEMENT OF THE CASE 


Plaintiff is a convicted felon, currently incarcerated at 
Ware State Prison in Waycross, Georgia, in the custody of 
the Georgia Department of Corrections. In his lawsuit, 
brought against five current and former members of the 
State Board of Pardons and Paroles, Plaintiff raises a 
single issue (it is Defendants’ understanding that all 
remaining issues were dismissed by the Court, pursuant 
to 28 U.S.C. § 1915, prior to service of process). 


Plaintiff alleges that the Board’s decision to defer his 
next reconsideration for parole for a maximum of eight 
years, as authorized by Georgia law, violates his ex post 
facto rights by adversely effecting his potential for 
parole. Plaintiff contends that Akins v. Snow, 922 F.2d 
1558 (11th Cir.), cert. denied, 501 U.S. 1260 (1991), pro- 
hibits the application of this rule to him. 


37 


Il. STATEMENT OF FACTS 


On July 23, 1974, Plaintiff was sentenced following 
conviction on murder charges in the Superior Court of 
Fulton County. Plaintiff was sentenced to life imprison- 
ment for the murder of Jack Bell. On November 24, 1979, 
Plaintiff escaped. While on escape, Plaintiff killed Frances 
Tutt Bell. Plaintiff was convicted of her murder and sen- 
tenced to life imprisonment, again in the Superior Court 
of Fulton County, on August 20, 1982. 


Plaintiff was initially considered for, and denied, 
parole on September 29, 1989. He was scheduled for 
reconsideration in August, 1997. Plaintiff was considered 
for a second time, and denied parole, on September 1, 
1992. He was scheduled for reconsideration in August, 
1995. On September 29, 1995, Plaintiff was reconsidered. 
He was denied parole and advised that his next recon- 
sideration would come within eight years. Plaintiff there- 
upon filed suit. 


III. ARGUMENT AND CITATION OF AUTHORITY 


THE “SET-OFF” OF PLAINTIFF FOR FUTURE 
RECONSIDERATION OF PAROLE HAS NO 
MEANINGFUL EFFECT ON THE POSSIBILITY 
OF PAROLE FOR PLAINTIFF; THEREFORE, 
THE APPLICATION OF THE RULE TO PLAIN- 
TIFF DOES NOT VIOLATE THE EX POST 
FACTO CLAUSE. 


In Akins v. Snow, 922 F.2d 1558 (11th Cir.), cert. 
denied, 501 U.S. 1260 (1991), the United States Court of 
Appeals for the Eleventh Circuit ruled upon the applica- 
tion of Ga. Admin. Comp. Ch. 475-3-.05(2) to prisoners 


38 


whose crime had been committed prior to the amend- 
ments to that regulation. The Court found that a retroac- 
tive application would violate the ex post facto clause. 


Akins has not received majority support among the 
circuits; indeed, it is the minority rule. See Bailey v. 
Gardebring, 940 F.2d 1150, 1157 (8th Cir. 1991). Following 
the decision of the United States Supreme Court in Cali- 
fornia Department of Corrections v. Morales, __ US. 
__, 115 S.Ct. 1597 (1995), the Eleventh Circuit itself noted 
that the continued vitality of Akins was “questionable.” 
Jones v. Georgia Board of Pardons and Paroles, 59 F.3d 
1145, 1149 (11th Cir. 1995). The Court did not, however, 
overrule Akins at that time, as the issue was not squarely 
before the Court. 


In Morales, the Supreme Court outlined the factors 
which the court should apply in making the determina- 
tion. The Court ruled that it is not the mere possibility of 
increased punishment which creates an ex post facto vio- 
lation; rather, there is no violation if the change “creates 
only the most speculative and attenuated risk of increas- 
ing the measure of punishment attached to the covered 
crimes.” Morales, 115 S.Ct. at 1604. See also Hill v. Jack- 
son, 64 F.3d 163, 169-70 (4th Cir. 1995) (similar Virginia 
provision does not violate the ex post facto clause). As is 
demonstrated infra, the amendment in question falls 
within this category. Therefore, no violation is present. 


First, the Court noted that the amendment applied 
only to a class of prisoners for whom the likelihood of 
release on parole is quite remote. Morales, 115 S.Ct. at 
1603. In Hill, the Fourth Circuit found that a provision 
applicable to: (1) inmates serving a life sentence; (2) 


39 


inmates with a single sentence of seventy years or more 
for a violent offense, and at least ten years to serve before 
mandatory release; or (3) inmates with multiple consecu- 
tive sentences totalling at least 70 years, one of which is 
for a violent offense, and at least ten years to serve before 
mandatory release was sufficiently limited to satisfy 
Morales. Hill, 64 F.3d at 169. Georgia’s regulation is more 
limited; it is limited in application solely to life sentence 
inmates. Only those who commit capital felonies or who 
are recidivists may receive a life sentence in Georgia. By 
definition, those who commit a capital felony or are 
recidivists are unlikely to receive parole. In Plaintiff's 
case, he has been convicted of multiple capital felonies 
(two counts of murder) in Georgia, including one murder 
committed after having escaped from custody. Thus, 
Plaintiff has been convicted of multiple capital felonies, is 
a recidivist, is an escape risk, and has a significant history 
of violence. Plaintiff clearly fits into the category of 
inmates “for whom the likelihood of release on parole is 
quite remote.” Id. Thus, the first aspect of the Morales 
test is satisfied. 


Secondly, the Morales court found that the Board’s 
authority was carefully tailored because it had no effect 
upon initial eligibility, but affected only the timing of a 
subsequent review. Thus, the amendment would have no 
effect upon a prisoner unless the Board concluded, not 
only that parole should not be granted at that time, but 
that it would not be reasonable to expect that parole 
would be granted during the following years. Morales, 
115 S.Ct. at 1604; Hill, 64 F.3d at 169. The same is true in 
Plaintiff's case. The amended regulation has no effect on 
Plaintiff's eligibility for parole; he first became eligible 


40 


for parole in 1989, and has remained eligible since that 
date. The only effect upon Plaintiff is upon the timing of 
subsequent reconsiderations. As in Morales, the Board 
has concluded in Plaintiff’s case that, in light of the fact 
that Plaintiff has been convicted of multiple capital felo- 
nies, is a recidivist, is an escape risk, and has a significant 
history of violence, it is not reasonable to expect that he 
will be paroled in the near future. 


The Morales court found significant the fact that the 
California statute did not preclude the California Board 
from expediting the prisoner’s release date in appropriate 
circumstances. Morales, 115 S.Ct. at 1605; Hill, 64 F.3d at 
169. At first blush, this might appear to distinguish the 
Georgia rule from the California statute, as the Eleventh 
Circuit in Akins stated that the Georgia regulation ren- 
ders the inmate “effectively ineligible for parole between 
two parole reconsideration hearings.” Akins, 922 F.2d at 
1561. However, the Court was mistaken as to the effect of 
the regulation. As shown in Mr. Amideo’s affidavit 
attached hereto, the “set-off” merely fixes the maximum 
time that will pass prior to reconsideration; the inmate is 
not ineligible for parole during this time, and may be 
reconsidered at an earlier time if circumstances warrant 
or if additional information is received by the Board. 
Thus, this aspect of the Morales test is also satisfied. 


Finally, the Morales court found significant that the 
California Board retained the authority to establish a 
reconsideration schedule tailored to the circumstance of 
an individual inmate’s case. Morales, 115 S.Ct. at 1604; 
Hill, 64 F.3d at 169. The same is true of the Georgia 
regulation. The regulation authorizes, but does not 


41 


require, the Board to delay reconsideration for a maxi- 
mum of eight years. The Board could have scheduled a 
reconsideration at any time prior to the expiration of 
eight years, if it had seen fit to do so, It has chosen not to 
[sic] so in this case; however, because the Board retained 
that authority, the third Morales factor is also satisfied. 
Of course, should new information or changed circum- 
stances warrant an earlier reconsideration, the Board has 
in place an existing means for doing so. Hill, 64 F.3d at 
169. 


Ultimately, then, the lesson of Morales is that pro- 
cedural modifications by a Parole Board exercising dis- 
cretionary authority do not violate the ex post facto 
clause when, as a practical matter, the changes create 
“only the most speculative and attenuated risk of increas- 
ing the measure of punishment attached to the covered 
crimes.” Morales, 115 S.Ct. at 1605. That is precisely the 
situation present in the case at bar. Plaintiff has been 
convicted of multiple capital felonies, is a recidivist, is an 
escape risk, and has a significant history of violence. The 
Board has concluded, after extensive review of his case 
and three separate considerations for parole by the mem- 
bers of the Board, that it is unlikely that Plaintiff will be 
granted parole during the eight years following the latest 
reconsideration. Having made that determination, the 
Board may legitimately pursue the twin goals of (1) 
focusing the time-consuming and costly efforts of parole 
consideration on those with a reasonable chance of parole 
in the near future and those who have just become eligi- 
ble for. consideration and (2) provide the inmate with a 
realistic assessment of his chance for parole, rather than 


42 


inflating, then dashing, his hopes each year when he has 
no reasonable chance of parole at that time.

Defendants’ Motion for Summary Judgment and enter 
judgment in their favor.

B. AS TO PLAINTIFF'S CLAIMS FOR DAMAGES, 
DEFENDANTS ARE ENTITLED TO QUALIFIED 
IMMUNITY. 


Qualified immunity since the decision of the United 
States Supreme Court in Harlow v. Fitzgerald, 457 U.S. 800 
(1982), has been limited to an objective inquiry into the 
state of the law at the time the allegedly unconstitutional 
actions of the defendants were taken. When a defendant 
has asserted the defense of qualified immunity, the com- 
plaint must be dismissed unless the plaintiff can demon- 
strate that the legal norms allegedly violated by the 
defendant were clearly established at the time he acted. 
Id., at 818. Once the defendant has invoked the defense of 
qualified immunity, the burden shifts to the plaintiff to 
demonstrate a violation of clearly established constitu- 
tional rights. Waldrop v. Evans, 871 F.2d 1030 (11th Cir. 
1989). 


In Lassiter v. Alabama A & M University, 28 F.3d 1146 
(11th Cir. 1994) (en banc), the Eleventh Circuit stated that 
the usual rule is that qualified immunity protects govern- 
ment actors and that only in exceptional cases will gov- 
ernment actors not be shielded in cases against them in 
their individual capacities. Id., at 1149. Lassiter also 
teaches that for the law to be clearly established to the 
point that qualified immunity does not apply, the law 
must have “earlier been developed in such a concrete and 
factually defined context to make it obvious to all reason- 
able government actors, in the defendant’s place, that 
‘what he is doing’ violates federal law.” Id., at 1149. 


The Court went on to state that general propositions 
have little to do with the concept of qualified immunity 


75 


and that if the case law, in factual terms, has not staked 
out a bright line, qualified immunity almost always pro- 
tects the defendant. Id., at 1150. 


The line is not to be found in abstractions — to 
act reasonably, to act with probable cause, and 
so forth — but in studying how these abstrac- 
tions have been applied in concrete circum- 
stances. Id., at 1150 (quoting Barts v. Joyner, 865 
F.2d 1187, 1194 (11th Cir. 1989)). 


Further, public officials are not obligated to be cre- 
ative or imaginative in drawing analogies from previ- 
ously decided cases. For qualified immunity to be 
surrendered, pre-existing law must dictate, that is, truly 
compel (not just suggest or allow or raise a question 
about), the conclusion for every like-situated, reasonable 
government agent that what defendant is doing violates 
federal law in the circumstances. Id., at 1150. Addi- 
tionally, because qualified immunity is a doctrine of prac- 
tical application to real-life situations, Courts should 
judge the acts of the government officials against the law 
and facts at the time Defendants acted and not by hind- 
sight, based on later events. Id., at 1150. 


In the case at bar, Plaintiff relies upon Akins v. Snow, 
922 F. 2d 1558 (11th Cir., cert. denied, 501 U.S. 1260 (1991). 
Clearly, that case supports his claim (and, considered in 
isolation, would require rejection of Defendants’ qualified 
immunity defense). However, since that decision, the 
United States Supreme Court has decided California 
Department of Corrections v. Morales, ___ U.S. __, 115 S.Ct. 
1597 (1995). The Eleventh Circuit itself has termed the 
holding in Akins “questionable.” Jones v. Georgia Board of 
Pardons and Paroles, 59 F.3d 1145, 1149 (11th Cir. 1995). 


76 


The Fourth Circuit has, in considering a similar Virginia 
provision in light of Morales, rejected the Akins rationale. 
Hill v. Jackson, 64 F. 3d 163, 169-70 (4th Cir. 1995). Thus, 
Defendants submit that their decision cannot be said to 
have violated clearly established law, and they are there- 
fore entitled to qualified immunity from suit for dam- 
ages.

475-3-.05 Parole Consideration. Amended. 


(1) Consideration is automatic for all offenders 
serving sentences imposed by a court of this State and 
who are in the custody of the Department of Offender 
Rehabilitation. No application is required. Inmates serv- 
ing misdemeanor county sentences of sufficient duration 
to make them meet the time-served requirements of Rule 
475-3-.06(1) will be considered upon submission of a 
request to the Board. The request may be in any written 
form and must contain name under which convicted, 
where incarcerated, offense(s), date and court of convic- 
tion, and length of sentence(s). The request should be 
submitted as early as possible to allow enough time for 
necessary investigations. 


(2) Reconsideration of those inmates who have been 
denied parole shall take place at least every three years. 
The Board will inform inmates denied parole of the rea- 
sons for such denial without disclosing confidential 
sources of information or possibly discouraging diagnos- 
tic opinions. 

(3) Inmates who have escaped will not be consid- 
ered for parole until their return to custody of penal 
authorities of this State. If initial parole consideration was 


given prior to the escape, the next consideration will be 
scheduled for one to three years after recapture. 


(4) A person who is returned to prison because of 
violation of the conditions of parole or other conditional 
release will be scheduled for parole consideration one to 
three years after revocation unless the Board directs oth- 
erwise in its order of revocation, votes to consider the 
case earlier, or unless a new sentence supersedes the 


87 


revoked sentence for the purpose of computing parole 
eligibility. 

(5) When considering a case for parole, the Board 
will apply Parole Decision Guidelines. The Guidelines 
will take into account the inmate’s crime severity level 
and his likelihood of success on parole. Parole success 
factors significantly associated with successful parolees 
will be tabulated from the inmate’s criminal and social 
history. A third element for consideration will be the 
inmate’s behavior in prison. The Board will follow the 
Guidelines in all parole cases except life-sentence cases to 
arrive at its decision or, for a specific and stated reason, 
may depart from the Guidelines. After the Board notifies 
the inmate of the decision, he may appeal the decision, 
but he must do so within 30 days. The basis for an appeal 
must be limited to the assessment of the crime severity 
level or to the computation of the parole success likeli- 
hood score. The inmate should address his appeal to the 
Appeals Officer in the Board’s central office.

475-3-.05 Parole Consideration. Amended. 


(1) Consideration is automatic for all offenders 
serving sentences imposed by a court of this State and 
who are in the custody of the Department of Corrections, 
except that no parole consideration shall ever be given 
to offenders serving sentences of life imprisonment 
without parole. No application is required. Persons 
ordered to serve consecutive county misdemeanor con- 
finement sentences exceeding 12 months must request 
consideration. The request may be in any written form 
and must contain name under which convicted, where 
incarcerated, offense(s), date and court of conviction, and 
length of sentence(s). The request should be submitted as 
early as possible to allow enough time for necessary 
investigations. 


(2) Reconsideration of those inmates serving life 
sentences who have been denied parole shall take place at 
least every eight years. The Board will inform inmates 
denied parole of the reasons for such denial without 
disclosing confidential sources of information or possible 
discouraging diagnostic opinions. 


(3) Inmates who have escaped will not be consid- 
ered for parole until their return to custody of penal 
authorities of this State. If initial parole consideration was 
given prior to the escape of a life sentence inmate, the 
next consideration will be scheduled for one to eight 
years after recapture. 


(4) A persor. who is returned to prison because of 
violation of th: conditions of parole cr other conditional 
release will be scheduled for parole consideration six 
months to one year after revocation unless the Board

directs otherwise in its order of revocation, votes to con- 
sider the case earlier, or unless a new sentence super- 
sedes the revoked sentence for the purpose of computing 
parole eligibility. 


(5) In considering parole for persons who will 
becomes statutorily eligible for parole consideration and 
who are serving less than a life sentence, the Board shall 
review a recommendation as to months to serve. This 
recommendation will be obtained from the Parole Deci- 
sion Guidelines system which accounts for the severity of 
the crime and the inmate’s likelihood of success on 
parole. The inmate’s likelihood of success on parole is 
measured by weighted factors concerning the inmate's 
criminal and social history which the Board has found to 
have value in predicting the probability of further crimi- 
nal behavior and successful adjustment under parole 
supervision. The Parole Decision Guidelines system is an 
aid to the Board in making more consistent, soundly 
based and explainable parole decisions and does not cre- 
ate a liberty interest. The Board specifically reserves the 
right to exercise its discretion under Georgia Law to 
disagree with the recommendation resulting from appli- 
cation of the Parole Decision Guidelines and may make 
an independent decision to deny parole or establish a 
Tentative Parole Month at any time prior to sentence 
expiration. After the Board notifies the inmate of their 
decision, the inmate may contest either the Crime Sever- 
ity Level or Parole Success Factor scores by writing 
within 30 days the Parole Guidelines Director in the 
Board’s Central Office. Prior to an inmate being paroled 
the inmate’s institutional conduct will be reviewed and 
institutional misconduct may result in a delay in parole 


release or a decision to deny parole. Any decision ren- 
dered under the Parole Decision Guidelines may be 
changed at the discretion of the Board at any time. The 
Board may modify any part of the Parole Decision Guide- 
lines system at any time.
