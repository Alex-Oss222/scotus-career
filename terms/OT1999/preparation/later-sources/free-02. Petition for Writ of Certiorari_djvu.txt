QUESTION PRESENTED 


Whether the supplemental jurisdiction statute, 28 U.S.C. 
§ 1367, overrules Zahn v. International Paper Co., 414 U.S. 
291 (1973), and thus expands federal subject matter 
jurisdiction in a class action to encompass class members 
whose claims do not satisfy the amount-in-controversy 
requirement of 28 U.S.C. § 1332, as long as diversity 
jurisdiction exists over the claims of one named plaintiff.

STATUTES INVOLVED 
28 U.S.C. § 1367 (1994) provides in part: 


(a) Except as provided in subsections (b) and (c) 
or as expressly provided otherwise by Federal 
statute, in any civil action of which the district 
courts have original jurisdiction, the district courts 
shall have supplemental jurisdiction over all other 
claims that are so related to claims in the action 
within such original jurisdiction that they form 
part of the same case or controversy under Article 
III of the United States Constitution. Such 
supplemental jurisdiction shall include claims that 
involve the joinder or intervention of additional 
parties. 


(b) In any civil action of which the district courts 
have original jurisdiction founded solely on 
section 1332 of this title, the district courts shall 
not have supplemental jurisdiction under 
subsection (a) over claims by plaintiffs against 
persons made parties under Rule 14, 19, 20, or 
24 of the Federal Rules of Civil Procedure, or over 
claims by persons proposed to be joined as 
plaintiffs under Rule 19 of such rules, or seeking 
to intervene as plaintiffs under Rule 24 of such 
rules, when exercising jurisdiction over such 
claims would be inconsistent with the 
jurisdictional requirements of section 1332. 


(c) The district courts may decline to exercise 
supplemental jurisdiction over a claim under 
subsection (a) if — 


4 


(1) the claim raises a novel or complex 
issue of State law, 


(2) the claim substantially predominates 
over the claim or claims over which the 
district court has original jurisdiction, 


(3) the district court has dismissed all 
claims over which it has original 
jurisdiction, or 


(4) in exceptional circumstances, there 
are other compelling reasons for 
declining jurisdiction.

STATEMENT OF THE CASE 


Petitioners Robin and Renee Free are retail purchasers 
of infant formula. They filed a class action petition on 
October 14, 1993 in the Eighteenth Judicial District Court 
of the State of Louisiana against infant formula 
manufacturers Abbott Laboratories, Bristol-Myers Squibb 
Company, and Mead Johnson & Company. The Frees allege 
that the defendants conspired to raise the price of infant 
formula, in violation of the Louisiana antitrust statute and 
other state law; no federal claims are presented. 51 F.3d at 
525, App. J at 77a. They claim that, as a result of defendants’ 
conduct, retail purchasers of infant formula in Louisiana paid 
higher prices than they otherwise would have paid. The 


5 


petition seeks relief on behalf of themselves and a class of 
purchasers in an amount not to exceed $20,000 each. /d. at 
526, App. J at 79a. 


The defendants removed the case to the United States 
District Court for the Middle District of Louisiana, alleging 
diversity and federal question jurisdiction. The Frees moved 
for remand to Louisiana state court, arguing that neither their 
claims nor the claims of unnamed class members satisfied 
the then-applicable $50,000 amount-in-controversy 
requirement of diversity jurisdiction and that there was no 
federal question. The district court agreed there was no 
federal question jurisdiction and, after determining that the 
amount-in-controversy requirement was met for the Frees’ 
claims due to their entitlement to attorney’s fees as named 
plaintiffs, the district court held that 28 U.S.C. § 1367 allows 
supplemental jurisdiction over the unnamed class members, 
none of whose claims satisfy the amount-in-controversy 
requirement. 1994 U.S. Dist. LEXIS 4563, at *6-10, App. L 
at 104a. However, acting under Section 1367(c), the district 
court declined to exercise supplemental jurisdiction because 
the case presents novel issues of state law. /d. at *10-11, 
App. L at 104a-05a. With respect to the named plaintiffs, 
the district court declined jurisdiction under an abstention 
doctrine. 1994 WL 422298, at *2, App. K at 95a-97a. 


Defendants took an interlocutory appeal of the remand 
order. The Frees again argued that their claims do not exceed 
the jurisdictional minimum and further that federal 
supplemental jurisdiction under 28 U.S.C. § 1367 does not 
encompass class members whose claims do not satisfy the 
jurisdictional minimum. The Fifth Circuit held that the Frees’ 
claims satisfy the jurisdictional minimum because of their 
entitlement to attorney’s fees. 51 F.3d at 527, App. I at 82a. 


6 


The Fifth Circuit then held that the line of cases culminating 
in Zahn was overruled by Section 1367 and therefore 
affirmed supplemental jurisdiction over unnamed class 
members even though their claims do not satisfy the 
jurisdictional minimum. /d. at 528-29, App. J at 89a. Finally, 
the court reversed the district court’s decision to abstain with 
respect t» the Frees’ claims and held that to decline 
supplemental jurisdiction under Section |367(c) with respect 
to unnamed class members was an abuse of discretion. /d. at 
529-30, App. J at 90a-91a. In light of its holdings on diversity 
and supplemental jurisdiction, the Fifth Circuit declined to 
address the federal question issue. It denied rehearing and 
rehearing en banc. 65 F.3d at 33, App. I at 75a. 


When the case returned to federal district court, two 
defendants moved to dismiss based on Louisiana antitrust 
law. Prior to a ruling on the motion, all defendants agreed to 
settle the case for $4.35 million. 953 F. Supp. at 752, App. 
G at 55a. The district court preliminarily approved the 
settlement and certified a settlement class. App. H. However, 
following notice to the class and a hearing, the court declined 
to grant final settlement approval. 953 F. Supp. at 756, App. 
G at 65a. Defendants moved for reconsideration of the denial 
of settlement approval but the district court denied the 
motion. App. F at 52a. The third defendant then joined the 
pending motion to dismiss. The district court ordered the 
case dismissed for failure to state a claim. 982 F. Supp. at 
1218-19, App. D at 44a. 


The Frees appealed and continued to assert the lack of 
federal subject matter jurisdiction because of failure to satisfy 
the amount-in-controversy requirement. On January 19, 
1999, a new three-judge panel of the Fifth Circuit ruled that, 
under the law of the case doctrine, it would not consider

further the issue of subject matter jurisdiction. 164 F.3d at 
272-73, App. C at 15a. The Court of Appeals then certified 
two questions of state antitrust law to the Louisiana Supreme 
Court. /d. at 276-77, App. C at 24a-25a. However, on March 
19, 1999, the Louisiana Supreme Court denied certification. 
1999 WL 194425, at *1, App. B at 9a. On June 3, 1999, the

plaintiffs’ claims. 176 F.3d at 301, App. A at 8a. 


REASONS FOR GRANTING THE WRIT 


This Court has consistently required, in diversity cases, 
that the claims of each plaintiff satisfy the jurisdictional 
minimum set forth in 28 U.S.C. § 1332 (1994). The Court 
has applied this principle in a class action where some class 
members satisfied the minimum and others did not. Zahn v. 
International Paper Co., 414 U.S. 291, 300 (1973) (“any 
plaintiff without the jurisdictional amount must be dismissed 
from the case, even though others allege jurisdictionally 
sufficient claims”). It also has applied the principle in a class 
action in which no plaintiff satisfied the minimum and the 
class members sought to aggregate their claims. Snyder v. 
Harris, 394 U.S. 332, 335 (1969) (“The traditional judicial 
interpretation under all of these [diversity jurisdiction] 
statutes has been from the beginning that the separate and 
distinct claims of two or more plaintiffs cannot be aggregated 
in order to satisfy the jurisdictional amount requirement.”). 
And, the Court always has applied the same principle in non- 
class actions where one plaintiff satisfied the jurisdictional 
minimum but another plaintiff did not. See, e.g., Clark v. 
Paul Gray, Inc., 306 U.S. 583, 589 (1939). This principle 
requiring the claims of each plaintiff to satisfy the 
jurisdictional minimum (referred to herein as “the Zahn 
principle”) is firmly rooted in case law dating to 1832. Zahn, 


414 US. at 515 & n.3 (citing Oliver v. Alexander, 31 U.S. 
(6 Pet.) 143 (1832)). 


THE CIRCUITS EXPRESSLY DISAGREE WHETHER 
THE SUPPLEMENTAL JURISDICTION STATUTE 
ELIMINATES OR PRESERVES THE RULE IN 
DIVERSITY CASES THAT THE CLAIMS OF EACH 
PLAINTIFF MUST SATISFY THE JURISDICTIONAL 
MINIMUM. 


The Court of Appeals’ decision in this case has been 
followed by the Seventh Circuit but expressly rejected by 
the Tenth and Third Circuits. Here, the Court of Appeals 
held that the supplemental jurisdiction statute, 28 U.S.C. 
§ 1367, overrules the Zahn principie. According to the Court 
of Appeals, “under § 1367 a district court can exercise 
supplemental jurisdiction over members of a class, although 
they did not meet the amount-in-controversy requirement, 
as did the class representatives.” Jn re Abbott Labs., 51 F.3d 
524, 529 (Sth Cir. 1995), App. J at 89a. The Seventh Circuit 
has followed this holding. Stromberg Metal Works, Inc. v. 
Press Mechanical, Inc., 77 F.3d 928, 930 (7th Cir. 1996) 
(“We follow Abbott Laboratories, which has strong support 
from the statutory text.”); Jn re Brand Name Prescr. Drugs 
Antitrust Litig., 123 F.3d 599 (7th Cir. 1997) (“At least one 
named plaintiff must satisfy the jurisdictional minimum. If 
he does, the other named plaintiffs and the unnamed class 
members can, by virtue of the supplemental jurisdiction 
conferred on the federal district courts by 28 U.S.C. § 1367, 
piggyback on that plaintiff's claim.”). 


However, in Leonhardt v. Western Sugar Co., 160 F.3d 
631, 640 (10th Cir. 1998), the Tenth Circuit concluded that 


9 


“a literal and textually faithful reading of § 1367(a) leads to 
the opposite conclusion from that of the Fifth and Seventh 
Circuits” and held that each plaintiff must satisfy the 
jurisdictional minimum. The Third Circuit also disagreed 
with the Fifth and Seventh Circuits in Meritcare Inc. v. St. 
Paul Mercury Ins. Co., 166 F.3d 214 (3d Cir. 1999), in which 
it held that Section 1367 “preserves the prohibition against 
aggregation outlined in Zahn v. International Paper Co. and 
Clark v. Paul Gray, Inc., and thus maintains the traditional 
rules governing diversity of citizenship and the amount-in- 
controversy under 28 U.S.C. § 1332.” See also Casteel v. 
Sara Lee Corp., No. Civ. 99-40117, 1999 WL 382375, at *6 
(E.D. Mich. June 7, 1999) (“This Court duly notes that the 
circuits are in disagreement on the issue of whether 28 U.S.C. 
§ 1367 overrules Zahn.”). 


These circuit cases reached opposite conclusions because 
they took different meanings from the text of Section 1367 
and applied different methods of statutory interpretation. To 
reach its result in this case, the Fifth Circuit analyzed Section 
1367 and found it neither unclear nor ambiguous: 


The statute’s first section vests federal courts with 
the power to hear supplemental claims generally, 
subject to limited exceptions set forth in the 
statute’s second section. Class actions are not 
among the enumerated exceptions. 


In re Abbott Labs., 51 F.3d at 528, App. J at 87a. While 
acknowledging that the legislative history cites Zahn as a 
case “untouched by the Act,” the Fifth Circuit held that “[w]Je 
cannot search legislative history for congressional intent 
unless we find the statute unclear or ambiguous” and “[h]ere 
it is neither.” Jd. The court also noted that the omission of 


10 


class actions from the list of exceptions in § 1367(b) “may 
have been a clerical error,” but held that “the statute is the 
sole repository of congressional intent where the statute is 
clear and does not demand an absurd result.” /d. at 528-29, 
App. J at 87a-88a. Finding that overruling this Court’s 
decision in Zahn would not be an absurd result, the court 
held there is supplemental jurisdiction over class members 
whose claims do not satisfy the jurisdictional minimum. 


Following the Fifth Circuit’s lead, the Seventh Circuit 
in Stromberg stated “we are reluctant to create a conflict 
among circuits on a jurisdictional issue” and agreed with 
the Fifth Circuit’s reading of Section 1367. Stromberg, 77 
F.3d at 930. In Stromberg, two plaintiffs asserted diversity 
jurisdiction but only one of them presented claims satisfying 
the jurisdictional minimum. The court held there was 
supplemental jurisdiction. In Brand Name Prescription 
Drugs, defendants in a class action alleging violations of 
state antitrust law removed the case from state court; 
plaintiffs argued the case should have been remanded for 
failure to satisfy the jurisdictional amount. Based upon 
Abbott and Stromberg, the Seventh Circuit held that as long 
as one named plaintiff satisfied the jurisdictional minimum, 
the other named plaintiffs and unnamed class members could 
“piggyback” by virtue of the supplemental jurisdiction statute 
“even though their own claims are for less than the 
jurisdictional minimum amount.” Jn re Brand Name Prescr. 
Drugs Antitrust Litig., 123 F.3d at 607. Finding no plaintiff 
satisfied the jurisdictional amount, the court ordered a 
remand of the case to state court. 


In Leonhardt (the Tenth Circuit case rejecting the 
holdings of the Fifth and Seventh Circuits), a class action 
was brought that initially presented both federal and state 


11 


claims. Following dismissal of the federal claims, the district 
court held there was no supplemental jurisdiction over the 
state-law claims because only one plaintiff's claim satisfied 
the jurisdictional minimum. The Tenth Circuit affirmed, 
reasoning that the other circuits had wrongly focused on the 
absence of Fed. R. Civ. P. 23 from the exceptions listed in 
Section 1367(b): 


Those exceptions, however, concern only the 
exercise of supplemental jurisdiction over claims 
against defendants who are made parties to a 
diversity action under certain rules and claims by 
plaintiffs who seek to be added to an on-going 
diversity action. We are concerned, however, with 
whether Congress intended to change the rules 
about when a plaintiff can bring an initial 
diversity-based class action under Rule 23, where 
the court’s original jurisdiction is based upon 
§ 1332. The omission of Rule 23 from § 1367(b) 
has no bearing on that question. 


Leonhardt, 160 F.3d at 639-40 (footnote omitted). Further, 
because Section 1367 commences by addressing “any civil 
action of which the district courts have original jurisdiction,” 
the Tenth Circuit concluded that the statute expressly 
preserves the well-accepted rules of original jurisdiction, 


including the Zahn principle. Jd. at 640. The Tenth Circuit 
stated: 


Thus, in our view § 1367(a) and (b) can be read 
literally, and unambiguously, to require each 
plaintiff in a class action diversity case to satisfy 
the Zahn definition of “matter in controversy” and 
to individually meet the $75,000 requirement. 


12 


Id. Finally, the court held that even assuming the statute was 
ambiguous, legislative history supported the court’s 
interpretation because the House Judiciary Committee Report 
specifically referred to Zahn as a jurisdictional requirement 
undisturbed by the statute. /d. at 640-41. 


In Meritcare (the Third Circuit case rejecting the 
holdings of the Fifth and Seventh Circuits), defendants 
removed a case based on diversity. It later became apparent 
that one plaintiff's claim satisfied the jurisdictional minimum 
under 28 U.S.C. § 1332 but a co-plaintiff's claim did not. 
After noting the circuit split, id. at 221 (“[t}he Court of 
Appeals for the Tenth Circuit, however, did not hesitate to 
take issue with both Abbott and Stromberg’), the court held 
Section 1367 to be ambiguous and therefore that resort to 
legislative history was appropriate. /d. at 222 (“we conclude 
that there is sufficient ambiguity in the statute to make resort 
to legislative history appropriate”). Further, the court held 
that reference to legislative history was appropriate even were 
it to conclude that Section 1367 was unambiguous because 
this was a rare case in which “ ‘the literal application of the 
statute will produce a result demonstrably at odds with the 
intentions of its drafters.’ ” Jd. (quoting United States v. Ron 
Pair Enters., Inc., 489 U.S. 235, 242 (1989)). 


The Third Circuit reviewed the legislative history and 
observed that a House Judiciary Committee Report “flatly 
states that Zahn’s validity was not to be affected,” id. at 220. 
It concluded: 


Our review of the text, legislative history, and 
origins of Section 1367 leads us to hold that it 
preserves the prohibition against aggregation 
outlined in Zahn v. International Paper Co. and 


13 


Clark v. Paul Gray, Inc., and thus maintains the 
traditional rules governing diversity of citizenship 
and the amount-in-controversy under 28 U.S.C. 
§ 1332. 


Id. at 222. The court ordered a remand to state court. 


In short, faced with the question whether 28 U.S.C. 
§ J 367 abrogates the Zahn principle, the Fifth and Seventh 
Circuits have answered “yes,” and the Tenth and Third 
Circuits have answered “no.” Two circuits hold the statute 
unambiguously overrules Zahn, one circuit holds the statute 
unambiguously does not overrule Zahn, and one circuit finds 
the Statute to be ambiguous and therefore looks to the 
legislative history to hold the statute does not overrule Zahn.

THE COURT OF APPEALS’ DECISION RAISES 
FEDERALISM CONCERNS AND SIGNIFICANTLY 
EXPANDS FEDERAL JURISDICTION IN DIVERSITY 
CASES. 


Under the rule adopted by the Fifth and Seventh Circuits 
and some district courts, a state-law claim now may be 
brought in or removed to federal court even though it does 


17 


not satisfy the jurisdictional minimum for diversity 
jurisdiction as long as diversity jurisdiction exists over 
another plaintiff's claim. Where, as here, at least one named 
plaintiff in a class action is held to have satisfied the 
jurisdictional minimum, the case can encompass the state- 
law claims of hundreds of thousands of class members even 
though the amount of each claim is only a few dollars. 


Whether to interpret a federal jurisdiction statute broadly 
or narrowly implicates serious federalism concerns: “Due 
regard for the rightful independence of state governments, 
which should actuate federal courts, requires that they 
scrupulously confine their own jurisdiction to the precise 
limits which the statute has defined.” Healy v. Ratta, 292 
U.S. 263, 270 (1934), quoted in Snyder v. Harris, 394 U.S. 
332, 340 (1969) (class members cannot aggregate claims to 
meet diversity jurisdictional minimum); see also Kokkonen 
v. Guardian Life Ins. Co. of Am., 511 U.S. 375, 377 (1994) 
(federal court jurisdiction is limited and “it is to be presumed 
that a cause lies outside this limited jurisdiction” unless the 
party asserting jurisdiction establishes the contrary). The 
holding of the Fifth and Seventh Circuits that Section 1367 
overrules Zahn results in federal courts adjudicating 
significantly more state-law claims, an encroachment upon 
the States’ traditional role in the federal system. Compare 
Free v. Abbott Labs., 176 F.3d 298 (Sth Cir. 1999), App. A 
at 8a (federal court holding in a case of first impression that 
state antitrust statute does not provide a cause of action to 
indirect purchasers of infant formula) with Hyde v. Abbott 
Labs., Inc., 473 S.E.2d 680, 688 (N.C. Ct. App. 1996) (state 
court holding that state antitrust statute does provide a cause 
of action to indirect purchasers of infant formula). By 
contrast, the holding of the Tenth and Third Circuits that 
Zahn is not overruled respects the States’ role in determining 


18 


state-law issues except where Congress has specifically and 
clearly authorized federal jurisdiction. 


Further, the Court of Appeals’ decision in this matter 
significantly shifts to federal courts state-law claims that do 
not satisfy the jurisdictional minimum. The Seventh Circuit 
in Stromberg conceded that its interpretation of Section 1367 
greatly expanded the federal caseload, but felt constrained 
by its reading of the statute’s text. See Stromberg, 77 F.3d 
at 931 (overruling Zahn allows “thousands of small claims 
into federal court via the class device”). The federal district 
courts have expressed concern at the Court of Appeals’ 
holding that Zahn is overruled due to the holding’s substantial 
impact on their resources. See, e.g., Daniels v. Philip Morris 


Cos., 18 F. Supp. 2d 1110, 1114 (S.D. Cal. 1998) 


(“A disavowal of Zahn would have profound implications 
for district courts.”); Allendale Mut. Life Ins. Co. v. Excess 
Ins. Co., No. 95 Civ. 10970 (SAS), 1999 WL 608788, 
at *14 (S.D.N.Y. Aug. 12, 1999) (“Nor do I believe it 
advisable to allow such aggregation in light of the demands 
upon federal judicial resources already stemming from 
diversity actions.”). This Court, in adhering to the rule that 
the claims of each plaintiff must satisfy the jurisdictional 
minimum, has observed that a contrary rule would greatly 
increase the number of state-law cases in federal courts: 


To overrule the aggregation doctrine at this late 
date would run counter to the congressional 
purpose in steadily increasing through the years 
the jurisdictional amount requirement. That

regard to the federal courts’ diversity of 
citizenship jurisdiction. ... [Overruling the

the purpose of the jurisdictional amount 
requirement. The expansion of the federal 
caseload could be most noticeable in class actions

Not only has the holding of the Fifth and Seventh 
Circuits extended federal jurisdiction to plaintiffs whose

jurisdiction has the potential to move from 
complete to minimal diversity.

THE COURT OF APPEALS’ DECISION EXPANDING

WAS WRONG. 


The Court of Appeals’ determination that Section 1367 
overruled Zahn was wrong. In addition to the Tenth and Third 
Circuits, numerous courts have addressed the Fifth Circuit's 
error. Judge Louis Pollak, in a thorough opinion that has 
been widely cited, aptly described the error: 


To retain this case in this court is to say to 
Congress: “We know what you meant to say, but 
you didn’t quite say it. So the message from us in 
the judicial branch to you in the legislative branch 
is: Gotcha! And better luck next time.” Such a 
message is not required by the separation of 
powers. Nor is it in harmony with the fact that 
Congress and the courts, however different their 
respective roles, are parts of a single government. 


Russ v. State Farm Mut. Auto. Ins. Co., 961 F. Supp. 808, 
820 (E.D. Pa. 1997), quoted in Meritcare, 166 F.3d at 221;

references to diversity jurisdiction and § 1332

conduct from those not so caused. See, e.g., Spectrum Sports,

Viewed from this perspective, the purposes of § 137 and

to proscribe conduct promoted by one enterprise that may

“unqualified” right of recovery. In addition, the courts’ role 
in policing conduct violative of Louisiana antitrust policy

direct purchasers; on the contrary, the remedy would become 
more effective. The Louisiana Power decision, on the other

divest courts of authority under the antitrust laws. See 493 
So.2d at 1159. Finally, the political goal of the antitrust laws

DISCUSSION 
A. Jurisdiction 


Undeterred by this court’s previous decision, appellants 
again contend that the federal courts’ exercise of diversity 
jurisdiction was improper because their claims did not satisfy 
the amount in controversy requirement of 28 U.S.C. § 1332. 
The prior panel allegedly erred, according to the Frees, by 
crediting to them the class attorneys’ fees for purposes of 
calculating the amount in controversy and by applying 
Louisiana’s “procedural” attorneys’ fee law (Art. 595) in a 
federal diversity case. Although appellants’ argument bears 
on federal courts’ jurisdiction, this is not an issue that we 
will reconsider. 


Once a panel of this court has decided an issue of law or 
fact, the decision continues to govern all subsequent stages 


13a 
Appendix C 


of the same case. See United States v. Becerra, 155 F.3d 
740, 752 (Sth Cir.1998); Copeland v. Merrill Lynch & Co. 
Inc., 47 F.3d 1415, 1423 (Sth Cir.1995). Known as the law 
of the case doctrine, this principle is necessary to bring 
finality to litigation and to discourage “panel shopping” by 
litigants. See Becerra, 155 F.3d at 752. While application of 
the doctrine is discretionary, this court will generally refuse 
to revisit a prior panel’s decision unless “(i) the evidence on 
a subsequent trial was substantially different, (ii) controlling 
authority has since made a contrary decision of the law 
applicable to such issues, or (iii) the decision was clearly 
erroneous and would work [sic] manifest injustice.” /d. at 
752-53. (quoting North Miss. Comms., Inc. v. Jones, 951 
F.2d 652, 656 (Sth Cir.1992)). 


The Frees dispute application of the law of the case 
doctrine on two grounds. First, based on a recent en banc 
decision of this court, they urge that federal courts must 
constantly examine each case for subject matter jurisdiction, 
and if it does not exist, must remand the case to state court. 
See Marathon Oil Co. v. Ruhrgas, 145 F.3d 211 (1998), cert. 
granted, _U.S. _, 119 S.Ct. 589, _ L.Ed.2d __, 67 
U.S.L.W. 3273 (1998). The Marathon rule does not apply. 
Certainly, a federal court must always be satisfied that subject 
matter jurisdiction exists and must even raise the issue sua 
sponte, Steel Co. v. Citizens For A Better Env't, 523 U.S. 
83, 118 S.Ct. 1003, 1012, 140 L.Ed.2d 210 (1998), but 
Marathon dealt with whether courts must initially determine 
subject matter jurisdiction before reaching the issue of 
personal jurisdiction. The Frees advocate a quite different 
rule of perpetual re-examination of precisely the same issue 
of subject matter jurisdiction. Other circuits have, however,

the case doctrine. See Ferreira v. Borja, 93 F.3d 671, 674

contractual relationship must be alleged.* As indirect 
purchasers, plaintiffs cannot assert the existence of any such 
obligation. Plaintiffs argue, however, that an action for fraud 
may also be alleged in tort under Article 2315 which would 
not require plaintiffs to establish such elements as privity, 
consideration, or breach of contract. Because the court has 
concluded that plaintiffs have failed to state a cause of action 
for liability for antitrust activity under Article 2315, the court 
holds the same for a cause of action for fraud. 


The Other Argument of Defendants 


The defendants also advance the argument that the 
Louisiana statute, La. R.S. 51:122, applies only to intrastate 
price fixing scheme and that the conspiracy as alleged by 
plaintiffs is clearly interstate, not intrastate. Hence, 
defendants argue, the Louisiana statute has no application 
to their alleged activities. 


In view of the conclusion of the court as to the 
construction of section 137, the court does not express any 
view on this argument. 


Accordingly, for the reasons set forth above, defendants’ 
motion to dismiss for failure to state a claim is hereby 
GRANTED and this action will be dismissed.

A. DIVERSITY JURISDICTION: THE NAMED 
PLAINTIFFS’ CLAIMS 


The court found it had diversity jurisdiction over the 
named plaintiffs’ claims even though each named and 
unnamed plaintiff claimed only $20,000, less than the 
$50,000 minimum for diversity jurisdiction. 28 U.S.C. 
§ 1332(a). The district court found that Louisiana law 
attributed all of a class’s attorney’s fees to the named 
plaintiffs. It held that the claim of the named plaintiffs for 
$20,000 — once swelled by attorney’s fees — met the 
$50,000 amount-in-controversy requirement. 


Plaintiffs argue that Louisiana statutes distribute the fees 
pro rata to all members of the class, with the result that none 
meets the amount-in-controversy requirement. 


The distribution of attorney’s fees centers on two 
Louisiana statutes. The first, Article 595 of the Louisiana 
Code of Civil Procedure, provides: 


| The court may allow the representative parties 
their reasonable expenses of litigation, including

Appendix J 


attorney’s fees, when as a result of the class action 
a fund is made available, or a recovery or 
compromise is had which is beneficial, to the 


class. 


Official Revision Comments 


(a) It is intended, in the first paragraph, that 
the reasonable expenses of litigation allowed the 
successful representative parties is to be paid out 
of the fund or benefits made available by their 
efforts. 


The second key Louisiana statute is Section 51:137 of 
the Louisiana Revised Statutes, which provides: 


Any person who is injured in his business or 
property by any person by reason of any act or 
thing forbidden by this Part may sue in any court 
of competent jurisdiction and shall recover 
threefold the damages sustained by him, the cost 
of suit, and a reasonable attorney’s fee. 


Article 595, plaintiffs contend, supports their argument 
that the fees are to be distributed among all class members. 
See, e.g., White v. Board of Trustees, 276 So.2d 714, 719

attorney’s fee from the awards due to each plaintiff), writ 
ref'd, 279 So.2d 694 (La.1973).

We disagree. Defendants pay attorney’s fees and

IV. ABSTENTION AND DISCRETIONARY 
EXERCISE OF SUPPLEMENTAL 
JURISDICTION 


Colorado River abstention is to be used only sparingly, 
see Colorado River, 424 U.S. at 813, 96 S.Ct. at 1244, and 
this case is a poor candidate. The district court acknowledged 
that “several of the [Colorado River] factors are either neutral 
or weighing in favor of the exercise of [federal] jurisdiction.” 
It rested its decision on two concerns: that remanding only 
the class members’ claims would split the action, and the 
novel and complex questions of state law. 


The first of these two concerns — the risk of piecemeal 
litigation — is a problem only under the district court’s view 
of abstention. The second consideration — that novel and 
complex state law issues govern the action — has more merit. 
Cf. Moses Cone, 460 U.S. at 23-24, 103 S.Ct. at 941 
(disfavoring abstention where federal question controls). 
These state law issues included whether indirect purchasers 
can state a claim under Louisiana antitrust law, and whether 


the claims in this case were preempted by federal antitrust 
law. 


90a 


Appendix J 


We agree that these may prove to be difficult questions. 
Standing alone, however, the novelty or complexity of state 
law issues is not enough to compel abstention. See, e.g., 
Rougon v. Chevron, U.S.A., Inc., 575 F.Supp. 95, 97 
(M.D.La.1983) (denying motion to remand to state court even 
though “the issues presented, involving previously undecided 
matters of Louisiana ... law, are peculiarly suited to 
disposition by the state courts of Louisiana”). Only 
“ ‘exceptional’ circumstances, the ‘clearest of justifications,’ 
_..can suffice under Colorado River to justify the surrender 
of [federal] jurisdiction.” Moses Cone, 460 U.S. at 25-26, 
103 S.Ct. at 942 (emphasis omitted). This is not one of those 
truly rare and exceptional cases in which Colorado River 
abstention is proper. 


The district court remanded the claims of other class 
members because they presented “novel issues of state law,” 
including whether indirect purchasers could state a claim 
under Louisiana antitrust law and whether the antitrust claim 
was preempted by federal law. 


Refusing to exercise supplemental jurisdiction over the 
unnamed plaintiffs’ claims reflects respect for considerations 
of comity, but it assumes that the claims of the class 
representatives were to be remanded to state court. The court 
must now adjudicate claims of the class representatives — 
including the same novel and complex state law issues the 
district court preferred to leave to Louisiana. So the interests 
of comity will not be served by declining to exercise 
supplemental jurisdiction over the class members whose 
claims do not meet the jurisdictional amount. 


9la 


Appendix J 


In short, the entire case should remain in federal court. 
The district court had diversity jurisdiction over the named 
plaintiffs’ claims; § 1367 granted it supplemental jurisdiction 
over the claims of the unnamed plaintiffs; and, considering 
that it must try the named plaintiffs’ claims, it abused its 
discretion on the facts here in declining supplemental 
jurisdiction over the unnamed plaintiffs’ claims. It is not 
necessary to decide the problematic contention that the 
district court also had federal question jurisdiction, and we 
do not. We VACATE the district court’s remand order, and 
REMAND to the district court for further proceedings. The 
petition for mandamus is DENIED.
