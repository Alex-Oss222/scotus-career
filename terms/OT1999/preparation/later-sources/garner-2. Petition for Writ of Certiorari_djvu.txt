STATEMENT 
A. Claims and Facts 


The State of Georgia, through its State Board of Par- 
dons and Paroles, makes provision for the exercise of 
executive clemency in the case of individuals convicted of 
violations of Georgia’s criminal statutes. Both the Supreme 
Court of Georgia and the United States Court of Appeals 
for the Eleventh Circuit have surveyed the relevant Geor- 
gia statutory and constitutional provisions and have con- 
cluded that the Board, in the exercise of the clemency 
power, exercises “virtually unfettered discretion.” Jones v. 
Georgia State Board of Pardons and Paroles, 59 F.3d 1145, 1150 
(11th Cir. 1995); see also Vargas v. Morris, 266 Ga. 141, 465 
S.E.2d 275, cert. denied sub nom Vargas v. Garner, __ US. 
__, 116 S.Ct. 1329, 134 L.Ed.2d 480 (1996). 


Except as otherwise provided by law, inmates serving 
sentences of life imprisonment become eligible for the 
exercise of the Board’s powers after the service of seven 
years’ imprisonment. O.C.G.A. § 42-9-45(b). The Board is 
authorized to provide, by regulation, tor eligibility for 
reconsideration of those inmates considered for, but 
denied, parole. O.C.G.A. § 42-9-45(a). 


Prior to 1979, the Board’s regulations provided that 
life sentenced inmates considered for, but denied, parole 


would be reconsidered on an annual basis. In 1979, due to 
increases in the prison population, the Board amended its 
regulation so as to provide for reconsideration of these 
inmates no less than every three years (while retaining 
the discretion to consider the inmate sooner). This 
amendment was applied by the Board to all life-sen- 
tenced inmates, including those whose crimes had been 
committed prior to 1979. 


In 1985, as Georgia’s prison population continued to 
increase, and in an attempt to provide life sentenced 
inmates with a more realistic assessment of their chances 
for release on parole, the Board again amended its policy 
to provide for parole reconsideration no less than every 
eight years. Again, the Board retained the discretion to 
consider the prisoner more frequently. This amendment 
was likewise applied to all life-sentenced inmates, includ- 
ing those whose crimes had been committed prior to 
1979. Ga. Admin. Comp. Ch. 475-03-.05(2). 


Prisoners convicted of crimes committed prior to 
1985 challenged the retroactive application of the 
amended regulation. The United States Court of Appeals 
for the Eleventh Circuit found that the retroactive appli- 
cation of the amended regulation violated the ex post 
facto clause. Akins v. Snow, 922 F.2d 1558 (11th Cir.), cert. 
denied, 501 U.S. 1260 (1991). 


In 1995, this Court decided the case of California Dep't 
of Corrections v. Morales, 514 U.S. 499 (1995). In Morales, 
this Court ruled that a retroactive application of a revi- 
sion to the schedule for the reconsideration of prisoners 
previously denied parole was not an ex post facto viola- 
tion, so long as the change created no real, practical effect 


upon the prisoner’s chances for parole. Based upon 
advice from the Attorney General of Georgia that the 
Court's decision in Morales was inconsistent with the 
Eleventh Circuit’s holding in Akins, the Board resumed 
retroactive application of its amended regulation. 


On July 23, 1974, Respondent had been sentenced to 
life imprisonment following his conviction for the murder 
of Jack Bell in the Superior Court of Fulton County, 
Georgia. On November 24, 1979, Respondent escaped 
from custody. While on escape, Respondent murdered 
Frances Tutt Davis. Respondent was convicted of her 
murder, and again sentenced to life imprisonment, in the 
Superior Court of Fulton County on August 20, 1982. 


Respondent was initially considered for, and denied, 
parole in September, 1989. He was reconsidered, and 
again denied, in September, 1992. Respondent was con- 
sidered a third time, and again denied parole, in Septem- 
ber, 1995. At that time, Respondent was advised that his 
next reconsideration would come within the next eight 
years. 


B. Proceedings Below 


Respondent brought suit, contending that retroactive 
application of the amendment violated the ex post facto 
clause of the United States Constitution. Respondent 
sought damages, as well as a declaratory judgment that 
the application of the amended regulation in his case 
violated the ex post facto clause. 


Following a period of discovery, the parties filed 
cross-motions for summary judgment. The District Court 


granted summary judgment to the Board, finding that, as 
in Morales, the amended regulation “creates ‘only the 
most speculative and attenuated possibility of producing 
the prohibited effect of increasing the measure of punish- 
ment.’ ” 


On appeal, a panel of the Eleventh Circuit reversed 
and remanded. The Court held that Morales reinforced its 
earlier holding in Akins, that the record did not support a 
finding that the amendment did not have the purpose or 
effect of increasing the quantum of punishment, and 
accordingly reversed the grant of summary judgment to 
the Board. The Court subsequently denied the Board’s 
Motion for Rehearing with Suggestion for Rehearing en 
banc.

REASONS FOR GRANTING THE WRIT 


I. THE ELEVENTH CIRCUIT PLACED THE BURDEN 
OF PROOF UPON PETITIONERS, CONTRARY TO 
THE HOLDINGS OF THIS COURT, THE FOURTH 
CIRCUIT, AND THE NINTH CIRCUIT. 


The Eleventh Circuit placed upon Petitioners the bur- 
den of showing that the application of Ga. Admin. Comp. 
Ch. 475-3-.05(2) would not pose a risk of increasing Plain- 
tiff’s sentence. Jones, 164 F.3d at 596. This was plain error. 
In any ex post facto inquiry, the burden is on the prisoner 
to demonstrate that the law in question will, as applied to 
his case, violate the ex post facto clause. Lynce v. Mathis, 
519 U.S. 433, 117 S.Ct. 891, 897-98 (1997) explaining Cali- 
fornia Dep't of Corrections v. Morales, 514 U.S. __, 115 S.Ct. 
1597 (1995). See also Roller v. Gunn, 107 F.3d 227, 237 (4th 


Cir.), cert. denied, 522 U.S. 874 (1997), quoting Johnson v. 
Gomez, 92 F.3d 964, 968 (9th Cir. 1996), cert. denied, 520 
U.S. 1242 (1997) (after Morales, the prisoner bears the 
burden of demonstrating “... ‘with assurance’ ... that 
he would have received parole under the old system”). 
The Eleventh Circuit erred in shifting this burden to the 
Board. The transfer of the burden of proof was plain error 
and conflicts squarely with this Court’s own explanation 
of its holding in Morales. This decision also conflicts with 
the decisions of the Fourth and Ninth Circuits, which 
have squarely placed the burden of proof on the prisoner. 


Il. THE DECISION OF THE COURT OF APPEALS IS 
INCONSISTENT WITH THE RECENT DECISIONS 
OF THIS COURT AND THE CONSTRUCTION OF 
THOSE DECISIONS BY THE OTHER CIRCUITS 
AND THE APPELLATE COURTS OF THE SEV- 
ERAL STATES. 


This Court has recently illustrated the proper 
approach to applying the ex post facto clause in California 
Dep't of Corrections v. Morales, 514 U.S. 499, 115 S.Ct. 1597, 
131 L.Ed.2d 588 (1995), and Lynce v. Mathis, 519 U.S. 433, 
117 S.Ct. 891, 137 L.Ed.2d 63 (1996). When “[t]he amend- 
ment creates only the most speculative and attenuated 
possibility of increasing the measure of punishment for 
covered crimes,” Morales, 514 U.S. at 509, and “the change 
at issue hals] neither the purpose nor the effect of 
increasing the quantum of punishment,” Lynce, 117 S.Ct. 
at 897, then the retroactive application of a new statute, 
regulation, or procedure does not violate the ex post facto 


clause. 


In the case at bar, it is clear that application of the 
“set off” regulation to Respondent will not affect his 
ultimate date of release. Once an inmate becomes eligible 
for parole, the Board exercises unfettered discretion in 
determining whether or not to grant parole in an individ- 
ual case and, if so, when release is to be granted. Stated 
bluntly, the Board has determined that, based upon the 
facts known to the Board about his case, Respondent may 
not reasonably expect to be paroled within the next eight 
years. It defies reason to suggest, as the Eleventh Circuit 
has done, and as proper ex post facto jurisprudence 
would require if that decision be sustained, that mere 
repetitive viewing of his file during the interim would 
change that conclusion.

reconsideration schedules as early as 1992 and, as they 
also predicted, the issue arises frequently. Bailey v. Noot, 
503 U.S. 952, 112 S.Ct. 1516, 117 L.Ed.2d 652 (1992) 
(White, J., dissenting). Subsequent to Morales, the First,”

Il. Discussion 


The Constitution provides that “[n]o State shall 
... pass any ... ex post facto Law. ...” U.S. Const. art. L, 
§ 10, cl. 1. At the time of the drafting of the Constitution, 
the phrase “ ‘ex post facto law’ was a term of art with a 
well-established meaning.” Akins v. Snow, 922 F.2d at 
1560. In Calder v. Bull, 3 U.S. (Dall.) 386 (1798), the 
Supreme Court “identified several legislative acts that 
clearly implicated the core concerns” with respect to ex 
post facto laws that existed at the time the Constitution 
was written. Akins, 922 F.2d at 1560-61. Included in such 
legislative acts was any law ” . . . that changes the punish- 
ment, and inflicts a greater punishment, than the law 
annexed to the crime, when committed.” Akins, 922 F.2d 
at 1560-61 (quoting Calder, 3 U.S. (Dall.) at 390 (emphasis 
in original)). The Supreme Court's ex post facto rulings 
have continued to adhere to the original principle that 


5a 


laws with this effect violate the Constitution’s ex post 
facto prohibition. See id. (collecting cases).? 


Aikins v. Snow 


In Akins, this Court considered an ex post facto chal- 
lenge to the retroactive application of the amendments to 
Georgia's regulations governi: , parole consideration at 
issue here, see Ga. Comp. R. & Regs. r. 475-3-.05.(2), and 
held such application to violate the proscription against 
laws “ ‘that change| | the punishment, and inflict[ | a greater 
punishment than the law annexed to the crime when com- 
mitted.’ ” Akins, 922 F.2d at 1560-61 (quoting Calder, 3 
U.S. (Dall.) at 390 (emphasis in original)). When the 
crimes for which the petitioners in Akins were convicted 
were committed, “the Board’s rules required the Board to 
initially consider an inmate serving a life term for parole 
after serving seven years of his life sentence.” Id. at 1560. 
If the inmate was denied parole at that time, he would 
receive reconsideration every year thereafter. See id. The 
regulation was subsequently amended so that “[parole 
rjeconsideration of those inmates serving life sentences 
who have been denied parole shall take place at least 
every eight years.” Ga. Comp. R. & Regs. r. 475-3-.05.(2). 
Applying the two-pronged test for Ex Post Facto Clause 
violations established by the Supreme Court in Weaver v.

BACKGROUND 


The facts in this case are not in dispute. In July 1974 
Jones was sentenced to life in prison following conviction 
in the Superior Court of Fulton County on a murder 
charge. He escaped in November 1979. In August 1982, he 
was again sentenced to life in prison following conviction 
in the Superior Court of Fulton County on a second 
murder charge. 


The rules and regulations which were in effect at the 
time of Jones’ second offense required that he be initially 
considered for parole after seven years and that he be 
reconsidered every three years. Prior to the time he was 
initially considered for parole, the rules were amended to 
require reconsideration “at least every eight years.” 


Jones was initially considered for parole in 1989, 
seven years after his conviction. The Board of Pardons 
and Paroles (Board) denied parole because of Jones’ mul- 
tiple offenses, and he was scheduled for reconsideration 
in 1997 (eight years). Because of the intervening decision 
in Akins v. Snow, 922 F.2d 1558 (11th Cir.), cert. denied, 501 
U.S. 1260 (1991), Jones was actually reconsidered for 
parole in 1992 (three years). He was again denied parole, 
both because of his multiple offenses and because of the 
“circumstances and nature of the offense,” and was 
scheduled for reconsideration in 1995. He was recon- 
sidered in 1995 and, for a third time, denied parole, again 
because of his multiple offenses and because of the “cir- 
cumstances and nature of the offense.” Because of the 


22a 


intervening decision in Morales, the amended regulation 
was applied, and Jones was scheduled for reconsideration 
in 2003 (eight years). 


Jones contends that applying the amended regulation 
to him so that he will not be reconsidered for parole for 
eight years violates his rights under the ex post facto 
clause of the United States Constitution.

In Akins, the Court of Appeals held that reducing the 
frequency of parole reconsideration hearings could “as a 
matter of law violate the ex post facto clause.” Akins, 922 
F.2d at 1565. The Court of Appeals further held that the 
actions of the Board in retroactively applying the changes 
and denying annual reconsideration hearings to the 
plaintiffs did violate their rights under the ex post facto 
clause. Id. Before reaching its decision, the Court of 
Appeals determined that: (1) the rules and regulations 
promulgated by the Board pursuant to authority dele- 
gated by the Georgia legislature “to enact rules and regu- 
lations concerning parole reconsideration” were “laws” 
within the meaning of the ex post facto clause, id. at 1561 
(citing O.C.G.A. § 42-9-45 (1989)); (2) parole reconsidera- 
tion hearings were a part of parole eligibility because “an 
inmate is effectively ineligible for parole between two 
parole reconsideration hearings,” id. at 1562 (emphasis in 
original); and (3) the ex post facto clause’s restrictions 
applied to a change in parole eligibility because parole 
eligibility is part of the sentence imposed as punishment 
for a crime, id. at 1563. The Court of Appeals then applied 
the two-prong analysis developed by the Supreme Court, 
id. (citing Weaver v. Graham, 450 U.S. 24 (1981)), determin- 
ing (1) that the Board’s actions were “retrospective” 
because “the Board effectively altered the consequences 
of [the plaintiffs’] crimes after they were completed,” id. 
at 1563-64, and (2) that the amendments “disadvantage[d] 
the [plaintiffs]” because the change, by increasing the

the time an inmate must spend in prison before he can 
again become eligible for parole,” id. at 1564. In Morales, 
the Supreme Court held that California did not violate

relieved the Board of the necessity of holding parole

being released. Id. 


The Court emphasized that determining whether a 
change implicates the ex post facto clause is a matter of 
degree — “whether [the change] produces a sufficient risk 
of increasing the measure of punishment attached to the 
covered crimes.” Id. at 509. The Court found that the 
California amendment created “only the most speculative 
and attenuated possibility of producing the prohibited 
effect of increasing the measure of punishment.” Id. 
Although the Court declined to establish a formula for 
determining when a change implicates the ex post facto 
clause, it found the following factors significant in reach- 
ing the Morales decision: (1) “the amendment applie[d] 
only to a class of prisoners for whom the likelihood of 
release on parole [was] quite remote,” id. at 510; (2) “[t]he 
amendment [had] no effect on the date of any prisoner's 
initial parole suitability hearing [as] it affect[ed] the tim- 
ing only of subsequent hearings,” id. at 511 (emphasis in 
original); (3) “the Board retain[ed] the authority to tailor 
the frequency of subsequent suitability hearings to the 
particular circumstances of the individual prisoner,” id.; 
(4) the possibility existed for an expedited hearing where 
a change in circumstances altered the prisoner’s suit- 
ability for parole, id. at 512-13; and (5) “the practical 
effect of a hearing postponement [was] not significant”

since the actual release date was frequently several years 
after a finding of suitability for parole, id. at 513. 


The Morales decision supports the preliminary hold- 
ing in Akins that the rules and regulations concerning 
parole reconsideration are “laws” within the meaning of 
the ex post facto clause, that parole reconsideration hear- 
ings are a part of parole eligibility, and that the ex post 
facto clause’s restrictions apply to a change in parole 
eligibility. As was the case in Akins, applying the 
amended regulation so that Jones will be reconsidered for 
parole only after eight years is a retrospective applica- 
tion. This Court must determine whether, in light of 
Morales, the amendments disadvantage Jones by increas- 
ing the time between hearings. 


The Georgia Administrative Code currently requires 
that “inmates serving life sentences who have been 
denied parole” be given reconsideration hearings “at 
least every eight years.” Ga. Comp. R. & Regs. 
475-3-.05(2). The Board’s policy statement specifies that 


all Life Sentence Cases denied parole may be set 
for reconsideration up to a maximum of eight 
years from the date of last denial when, in the 
Board’s determination, it is not reasonable to 
expect that parole would be granted during the 
intervening years. Inmates set-off under this 
policy may receive expedited parole reviews in 
the event of a change in their circumstance or 
where the Board receives new information that 
would warrant a sooner review.

Here, as in Morales, the amendment of which Jones 
complains changes only the timing between reconsidera- 
tion hearings, relieving the Board of the necessity of 
holding parole hearings for prisoners who have no rea- 
sonable chance of being released. It does not change any 
prisoner’s sentencing range, the substantive formula for 
evaluating a prisoner for release, or the standards for 
setting a prisoner’s initial date of eligibility for parole. 
The change applies only to prisoners serving life sen- 
tences who have been denied parole at least once, “a class 
of prisoners for whom the likelihood of release on parole 
is quite remote,” Morales, 514 U.S. at 510; it affects only 
the timing of reconsideration hearings; the Board retains 
the authority to set subsequent hearings at a time suitable 
to the “particular circumstances of the individual pris- 
oner,” id. at 511; the Board’s policies specifically provide 
for an expedited reconsideration hearing if a prisoner’s 
circumstances change or if the Board receives new infor- 
mation that might affect a prisoner's chance of parole. 


The Court finds that, as in Morales, the change to 
which Jones objects creates “only the most speculative 
and attenuated possibility of producing the prohibited 
effect of increasing the measure of punishment.” Id. at 
509. It does not disadvantage Jones and therefore does 
not violate his rights under the ex post facto clause.
