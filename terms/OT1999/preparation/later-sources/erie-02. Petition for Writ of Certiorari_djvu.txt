The Pennsylvania court committed fundamental error 
by analyzing Ordinance 75-1994 under strict scrutiny. It is 
well settled that expressive conduct is not entitled to full First 
Amendment protection. Where the conduct has both speech 
and non-speech elements, incidental limitations on First 
Amendment freedoms may be tolerated. United States v. 
O'Brien” provides the correct analytical framework. The 
O'Brien test permits some burden on expressive conduct 
where the burden is justified by a “sufficiently important 
governmental interest.””° 


The Pennsylvania court expended its efforts solely on 
disagreeing with the result in Barnes. This Honorable Court 
has previously addressed the respective roles of state and 
lower federal courts in federal constitutional analysis.

When determining whether a preliminary injunction 
should be granted, the court must consider the following 
standard: (1) is the injunction necessary to prevent 
immediate and irreparable harm not compensable in damages, 
(2) would greater harm result from denying the injunction 
than from granting it; (3) is the plaintiff's right to relief clear 
(a likelihood of success on the merits); and (4) will the status 
quo be restored if the injunction is granted. Com. v. Danny’s 
Bookstore, 155 Pa. Cmwlth. 281, 625 A.2d 119 (1993) citing 


Norwood A. McDaniel Agency v. Foster, 117 Pa. Cmwlth. 
227, 543 A.2d 155 (1988). 


First, before a preliminary injunction can be entered, 
the plaintiff must show that such injunction is necessary to 
prevent immediate and irreparable harm not compensable in 
damages. Kandyland’s argument, and one which the court 


l4a 


accepts, rests on the principle that nude dancing is afforded 
constitutional protection under the First Amendment to the 
U.S. Constitution. 

of Akron, 816 F. Supp. 1249 (N.D. Ohio 1993). Generally 
speaking, where violations of constitutional rights are alleged, 
a further showing of irreparable injury may not be required 
if more than merely money is at stake. Milwaukee County 
Pavers Ass'n. v. Fiedler, 707 F. Supp. 1016, as modified in 
710 F. Supp. 1532 (W.D. Wis. 1989). Thus, the court finds 
Kandyland has met the first requirement necessary to allow 
entry of a preliminary injunction. 


ve The second criteria requires that the preliminary 
injunction maintain the status quo. There is no doubt that 
issuing a preliminary injunction would satisfy this 
requirement for status quo would be maintained. 
Kandyland’s business could proceed as usual, as there was no 
such prohibition prior to Ordinance 75-1994.? 


The third prong to be established is whether the 
plaintiff's right to relief is clear, or otherwise expressed, 
whether plaintiff is likely to succeed on the merits of the 
case. This extremely high standard is required to justify the 
entry of a preliminary injunction, because a preliminary 
injunction is an extraordinary remedy and must be exercised 
only with extreme caution. After all the arguments presented 
before it, the court finds this to be the most perplexing issue 
to resolve. The U.S. Constitution and the Bill of Rights form 
the cornerstones of our government. Unfortunately,

75-1994 addresses public indecency.

sometimes the rights afforded by the Bill of Rights are not 
defined with sufficient specificity. As noted by the 
Honorable Justice Benjamin Cardozo, "[b]ills of rights give 
assurance to the individual of the preservation of his liberty. 
They do not define the liberty they promise.” M. E. HALL, 
SELECTED WRITINGS OF BENJAMIN NATHAN 


CARDOZO (1947). 


The Pennsylvania courts have not been directly 
presented with the constitutionality of ordinances as enacted 
in the City of Erie. The parties have cited two major cases 
in support of their respective positions. Not coincidentally, 
all the ordinances in question are almost identical to the 
ordinance enacted in Barnes, infra. On Kandyland’s side is 
Triplett Grille, Inc. v. City of Akron, 816 F. Supp. 1249 
(N.D. Ohio 1993). Triplett is currently on appeal to the Sixth 
Circuit. In Triplett, the District Court held that the ordinance 
enacted by the City of Akron was overbroad and violated the 
First Amendment right to free speech and expression. 
Further, the District Court found that the Akron ordinance 
failed to consider any of the secondary effects of adult 


entertainment in the community. 


The Triplett court relied upon Justice Souter’s 
concurring opinion in Barnes v. Glen Theatre, Inc., 111 S. Ct. 
2456 (1991), in finding the Akron ordinance invalid. The 
Triplett court interpreted Justice Souter’s opinion as the 
narrowest grounds available in analyzing the high court's 
plurality opinion. See Marks v. United States, 430 U.S. 188 


(1977) cited in Triplett, supra. Justice Souter’s concurrence 
centered on the secondary effects of adult entertainment. 


Justice Souter found the secondary effect argument to meet 
the criterion set forth in United States v. O’Brien, 391 U.S. 
367 (1968). 


16a 


Turning to the defendants’ position, the court is 
directed to Barnes, supra, a plurality decision of the nation’s 
highest court. The Barnes court applied the four-part test of 
O'Brien, supra, and determined the Indiana statute to be 
constitutional. Specifically, the court stated that (a) nude 
dancing is expressive conduct within the outer perimeters of 
the First Amendment, Id. at 2460, and (b) Indiana’s public 
indecency statute is justified despite the incidental limitations 
on some expressive activity. The statute was viewed as 
within the constitutional powers of the powers of the state 
and one which furthers substantial governmental interests 
(protecting order and morality, Barnes at 2461). Further 
analyzing the O’Brien test, the court ruled that governmental 
interest is unrelated to the suppression of free expression, 
since public nudity is the prohibited act (whether or not it is 
combined with expressive activity). Finally, the restriction 
placed on the First Amendment freedom was found not to be 
greater than essential to further the governmental interest (the 
act of nude dancing and the messages it intends to convey are 
not being deprived, but rather the messages conveyed would 
be slightly less graphic). Barnes at 2463. 


Given these two opposing positions, this court cannot 
state that plaintiff's right to relief is clear or that the plaintiff 
is likely to sticceed on the merits of the case. On the basis 
of Triplett, the Erie ordinance may very well be invalid. 
However, Ordinance 75-1994 specifically enumerates the 
intentions of the legislature and the purpose for its enactment, 
thus providing the secondary effects argument Triplett lacks. 
The determination that the Akron statute is overbroad is not 
yet final, inasmuch as Triplett has been appealed to the Sixth 


Circuit. Barnes, on the other hand, upheld the statute as 
applied. 


At this time, the issues of preemption and overbreadth 


17a 


are not yet ripe for review, as the court's review is strictly 
limited to the question of the issuance of a prelir inary 
injunction. With the plurality decision of the U.S. Supreme 
Court and the conflicting District Court decision in mind, the 
court cannot say the plaintiff's right to relief is clear. This is 
not to say with certainty that upon further hearing and 
argument, the ordinance will be found valid. This order is 
being entered without prejudice to Kandyland, after a full 
hearing, to seek a permanent injunction. In fact, the oar 
Kandyland will have to pull in a final hearing to obtain an 
injunction is much lighter than the one they must wield in 
obtaining a preliminary injunction. Also, the court agrees, 
upon the request of either party, to expedite this case and 
dispose of it as soon as possible after all pleadings are filed, 
testimony presented and arguments held. 


The court, having found that plaintiff has not met the 
burden with respect to proving a clear right to relief, declines 
to address the fourth prong of the preliminary injunction 
standard, that being whether greater harm would result by 
denying the injunction than by granting it. 


Finally, the court would like to note that it has 
considered all the case law presented on behalf of Kandyland. 
These cases, however, can be distinguished from the case sub 
judicia, both factually and/or in the relief they seek, and thus 
they lend little insight in the issue of a preliminary injunction.

Discussion 


Before the court attempts to analyze the underlying 
facts of this case as they relate to the applicable law, the 
court would remind the parties of the purpose of this case. 
It is not to reveal the personal opinions of one man. If that 
were the case, since nude dancing does not meet with the 
court's approval, its task would be much easier, for the court 
would just concur with the City. Nevertheless, the role of 
this judiciary is not to question the wisdom of the actions of 
a legislative body. The court's responsibility is only to see 
that legislation passes constitutional muster. Finucan v. 

ia Mi 136 Pa. Cmwith. 272, 582 
A.2d 1152 (1990). 


One would hope modern society~sees its judges as 
examining issues from all angles, balancing objectives among 
the various interests, applying fundamental principles 
neutrally, and seeking to attain a delicate balance between 
majority rule and the basic rights of the individual. Aharon 
Barak, Judicial Discretion (1989). 


Initially, the court must address the City’s allegation 
that Pap’s A.M. lacks standing to challenge the Ordinance. 


27a 


Traditionally, a person who is not adversely affected by the 
matter he seeks to challenge is not “aggrieved” and therefore 
has no standing to seek judicial review. American 
Booksellers Ass’n., Inc. v. Rendell, 332 Pa. Super. 537, 481 
A.2d 919 (1984). This rule has, however, been modified for 
First Amendment issues. A litigant may challenge the 
validity of an ordinance on the grounds of overbreadth as it 
applies to persons not before the court. “The doctrine of 
overbreadth accords standing by reason of the chilling effect 
that a particular law might have upon the exercise of first 
amendment rights.” Id. at 555, 481 A.2d 919 (1984). This 
is so because the very existence of the statute may cause 
others not before the court to refrain from constitutionally 


protected speech or expression. American Booksellers, supra, 


citing Broadrick vy. Oklahoma, 413 U.S. 601 (1973); Doran v. 
Salem Inn Inc., 422 U.S. 922 (1975). Among other theories, 


plaintiff is challenging Ordinance 75-1994 on the basis of 
overbreadth. Thus, the court finds plaintiff to have standing 
in which to challenge the City’s Ordinance. In addition, 
plaintiff has attained standing by the Ordinance itself. The 
Ordinance imposes penalties upon those who promote or 
maintain public indecency as a property owner, proprietor or 
manager of a business. Consequently, as the provider of 
nude adult entertainment, Pap’s A.M. is aggrieved by the

CONCLUSIONS OF LAW 


1. This court has jurisdiction over the parties to 
this action and the subject matter thereto. 


2. Plaintiff has standing in which to bring this 
action. 


3. The City of Erie Ordinance 75-1994, as 
drafted, violates Article 1, Section 7, of the Pennsylvania 
Constitution and the First Amendment of the United States 
Constitution in that the Ordinance is overbroad as it prohibits 
all nudity, whether or not said nudity is related to adult 
entertainment. 


4. A permanent injunction is hereby GRANTED 
in favor of plaintiff against defendants and defendants shall 
be barred from enforcing Ordinance 75-1994. The effective 
date of this permanent injunction is January 18, 1995. 


5. City of Erie Ordinance 75-1994 is hereby 
declared unconstitutional and so shall be stricken.

It is well established that a First Amendment

Osborne v. Ohio, 495 U.S. 103 (1990); Triplett Grille, Inc. v.

Broadrick_v. Oklahoma, 413 U.S. 601 (1973); City of

inhibited. Broadrick.

expression to be violative. Id. The instant case does not

U.S. 601 (1973). The overbreadth doctrine is “strong 
medicine” which is used-only sparingly and only as * ‘ast

not overbroad merely because its application implicates

In Council of Middietown Township v. Benham, 514 
Pa. 176, 523 A.2d 311 (1987), the court stated that merely 
because a state has legislated in an area of law does not 
denote that the state law preempts the field. “The General 
Assembly must clearly show its intent to preempt a field in 
which it has legislated. The test for preemption . . . . is well 
established. Either the statute must state on its face that local 
legislation is forbidden, or ‘indicate[] an intention on the part 
of the legislature that it should not be supplemented by 
municipal bodies."” jd. at 180-81, 523 A.2d at 313 (citations 
omitted) (alterations in the original). 


The obscenity statute at issue does not state that it 
preempts the field, and in fact, subsection (k) of the statute 
permits local governments to enact laws in the area of 
obscenity as long as they are consistent with the statute. The 
indecency Ordinance and obscenity statute may overlap in 
some areas, but they regulate different activities. The 
Ordinance was enacted to prohibit public nudity, not obscene 


behavior,'* and was designed to protect the public from the 
secondary effects associated with nude dancing, not lewd 
behavior. It was not enacted for the purpose of 

obscene behavior; in fact, the Ordinance does not mention 
obscenity."* 


Accordingly, we hold that the Ordinance is not 
preempted by the Pennsylvania obscenity statute.

I agree with the majority that Sections 1(c) and 2 of 
the ordinance at issue must be stricken. However, because a 
majority of the United States Supreme Court has upheld the 
constitutionality of a virtually identical ordinance under the 
First Amendment to the Federal Constitution, and because 
this Court is bound by the United States Supreme Court's 
interpretations of the Federal Constitution, I must disagree 
with the majority’s conclusion that the provisions of the 
ordinance at issue fail under the First Amendment to the 
Federal Constitution. Instead, I believe that the provisions of 
the ordinance at issue here must be stricken under Article I, 
§ 7 of the Pennsylvania Constitution. Accordingly, I concur 
only in the result reached by the majority in this matter.

Justice White's dissent forcefully articulates the

purposes of our interpretation of Article I, § 7 of the

as the plurality and Justice Scalia believed, was merely to 
“enforce the traditional moral belief that people should not 
expose their private parts indiscriminately,"* then the 
ordinance presumably would not allow people to display 
indiscriminately their private parts to innumerable relatives, 
friends, and Uthers whom they care to invite into their homes. 
Since the ordinance does not prohibit such behavior, the 
moral justification must be deemed illusory. The true 
purpose of the ordinance, as applied to appellant and others 
similarly situated, is to prevent the customers in appellant's 
and like establishments from being exposed to the distinctive 
communicative aspects of nude dancing. One might call this 
a moral justification, insofar as the drafters of the ordinance 
seem to be expressing their moral condemnation of those 
citizens of the Commonwealth who are attracted to this 
distinct form of communication, but lawmakers cannot invoke 
the sword of morality specifically to attack a form of 
protected expression. The fact that this particular form of 
protected expression may not ascend to the level of a high art 
form is irrelevant.’ Lawmakers may not categorically 
proscribe any form of protected expression simply because 
they are not at ease with its content.
