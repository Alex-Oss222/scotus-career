## Statutory distinctions and lower holdings

Section 510(c), 30 U.S.C. §1260(c), first requires a three-year schedule of notices. Its separate permit-block rule applies when the schedule **or other information** shows that an operation owned or controlled by the applicant is **currently** violating the relevant laws. Issuance remains barred until proof that the violation is corrected **or is in the process of being corrected** to the satisfaction of the authority having jurisdiction. Neither three-year schedule coverage nor the date a violation first arose automatically limits that current-condition duty.

The separate former-control provision permits withholding where the applicant controls **or has controlled** operations with a demonstrated pattern of willful violations of the chapter, of such nature and duration and with such resulting irreparable environmental damage as to indicate an intent not to comply. The lower court held that ordinary past ownership/control alone cannot bypass that condition. It did not abolish every consequence after control ends. The IFR did not expressly announce the past-control application, but OSM had used that construction at least once. Paragraphs 1 and 6 and footnote 4 preserve the exact text and source status.

The appellate court sustained indirect downstream control through intermediary entities. It invalidated presumptions based solely on officer/director status and ten-to-fifty-percent equity, but sustained the asset-control and general-partner presumptions. Operating and coal-right presumptions were not specifically challenged and were not decided. Due-process objections and the APA persuasion-burden challenge belonged to a separate proceeding over the 1994 procedural rules and were not adjudicated here. See paragraphs 5 and 7–9 and footnotes 3, 5–10.

The court rejected the five-year section 2462 argument because it read the current-violation command as an exception supplied by Congress. It expressly did **not** decide whether the permit consequence is punitive or remedial. It nevertheless held the IFR retroactive for violations **and control transactions** predating November 2, 1988. Its premise was that the indirect-control reading was permissible but not compelled before the 1988 rule. Footnote 12 distinguishes a later acquisition of control over an operation with an older violation: that post-rule control transaction is not within its retroactivity holding. See paragraphs 10–13 and footnotes 11–12.

The lower court sustained additional reasonably necessary schedule information and recognized inability to obtain information as a way to rebut presumed control. It sustained general suspension/rescission authority for improvidently issued permits under section 201(c)(1), 30 U.S.C. §1211(c), and implied correction authority. It separately rejected circumvention of state-primacy procedures: for ordinary non-imminent violations the statutory notice, abatement opportunity, public-hearing opportunity, written unabated-violation finding and specified corrective steps remain necessary before the relevant federal cessation action. Imminent-danger procedures under section 521(a)(1)–(2) remain distinct. See paragraphs 14–18 and footnotes 13–14.

## Review scope and supported alternatives

The supplied two-question grant centers on post-control consequences, retroactivity and asserted reopening. That description does not erase the distinctions above or automatically bring every presumption, information demand, state-primacy rule or separate procedural appeal before the Court. The actual source **sustains** improvidently issued permit correction authority. No record of a particular finally adjudicated permit, vested title or completed agency merits adjudication is supplied by this facial rule challenge. Do not assume such a finality premise or create a general reopening prohibition. The Court may state that no particular final adjudication is before it and decide the statutory rule question at its actual scope.

The industry's strongest supported path reads current ownership/control as a substantive limit and the willful-pattern provision as the deliberately narrower route for former relationships. It characterizes application of a newly chosen indirect-control rule to completed pre-1988 violations and control transactions as a new disability requiring express delegation under Bowen, reinforced by Landgraf's actual-operation inquiry. Its strongest counterargument is that an application for a new permit and a presently unabated operation are current conditions, and a statute already reaching control can be clarified without retroactively creating liability. Smiley prevents a rule that every later authoritative construction of ambiguity is retroactive.

Interior's strongest supported path treats permit eligibility as prospective regulation, uses indirect actual control to make abatement possible, and relies on section 1211(c)'s express suspension/revocation authority to correct unlawfully issued permits. Current financial or operational control need not be direct title ownership. Its strongest obstacles are the statute's different treatment of former control, the statutory current-violation and cure language, legitimate reliance and actual new-disability effects, and the separate state-enforcement procedures. A new application cannot by label make every antecedent liability prospective; an interpretation cannot amend contrary statutory text.

A component-specific path may sustain present indirect-control regulation and ordinary lawful permit correction while rejecting unsupported former-control applications or genuinely retroactive new disabilities. Retroactivity must be tested for the precise transaction and disability, preserving the pre-rule-violation/post-rule-acquisition distinction. Any remand must identify the defective rule or application and statutory correction task rather than rescind a named permit, create a damages claim, declare all old violations unenforceable, or adjudicate the separate procedural-rule case.

## Entering law and chronology

Landgraf supplies the actual new-consequences inquiry; Bowen's pre-divergence rule constrains retroactive legislative rulemaking. Smiley distinguishes use of a lawful current statutory interpretation for antecedent transactions from backdating a legislative rule, while reserving displacement of clear prior guidance. Brown & Williamson requires agency authority within the connected enacted scheme. Christensen is effective by May 8 and distinguishes legislative authority, persuasive advisory interpretation and regulatory construction.
