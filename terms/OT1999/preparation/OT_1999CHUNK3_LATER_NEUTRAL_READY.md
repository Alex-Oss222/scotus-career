### Village of Willowbrook v. Olech

The question is whether this pleaded intentional unequal easement demand states an equal-protection claim and whether ill will adds a separate requirement. One path treats intentional materially unequal treatment without a conceivable legitimate explanation as sufficient, although the plaintiff is one person. Its counterargument is the risk of making ordinary errors and discretionary distinctions federal constitutional cases. The competing path requires an additional limiting characteristic such as illegitimate animus, or confines review where a genuinely discretionary comparison cannot establish material similarity; its counterargument is that equal protection protects persons and existing rational-basis doctrine does not universally require proof of subjective hostility.

Beach preserves conceivable grounds, while Heller tests their relationship to the actual disadvantage; Romer did not rest on a finding about every actor's subjective motive. Thomas's Beach reasoning, Souter's Heller and Romer reasoning, and Kennedy's distinct Heller family-participation ground provide actual contrasting applications without deciding this pleading question. The source addendum fixes the Rule 12(b)(6) posture, pleaded 33-foot demand against other owners' 15 feet, approximately three months' delay and subsequent 15-foot acceptance. Intentional deprivation and reckless disregard are pleaded alternatives, not a finding that recklessness alone satisfies any intentional-treatment rule. The earlier successful suit and ill will are pleaded, not proved; other affected owners mean “class of one” does not certify that literally one person was affected. No liability, damages, final irrationality or comparator finding follows from pleading sufficiency. No new requirement to plead evidence or prove every conceivable fact may be smuggled into this review.

### United States v. Johnson

One path reads release in §3624(e) as actual departure from imprisonment and treats supervised release as a distinct period of community supervision. Its strongest counterargument is that continued custody after the lawful sentence ends is not imprisonment in connection with a valid conviction in the sense Congress intended, so an actual-release rule may compound an unlawful restraint. The competing path treats the legally required release date as commencement, or interprets the confinement tolling clause to exclude vacated convictions, crediting excess custody. Its counterargument is the statutory commencement language, the separate purposes of custody and supervision, and the difference between failing to toll an already commenced term and commencing it in the first place.

The exact historical §§3624(e) and 3583(e) are now supplied in the source excerpts. Section 3624(e) separately provides commencement, concurrency, imprisonment tolling, a less-than-30-consecutive-days exception, and a fine-installment condition; its brief-imprisonment exception is not by itself an excess-custody credit rule. Section 3583(e)(1) permits termination after one year of supervised release, considering the enumerated §3553(a) factors and the required procedure, when the released defendant's conduct and the interest of justice warrant it. Section (e)(2) separately permits modification/reduction/enlargement of conditions before expiration or termination and extension when less than the maximum was imposed; it does not permit immediate discretionary shortening of every supervision term. Revocation and its proof/term limits are another distinct branch, not this claim. These powers are discretionary and cannot become automatic credit or unconditional relief. The supplied vacatur of Johnson's two §924(c) convictions is litigant history, not authority importing a displaced historical statutory holding. His drug convictions support the three-year supervision term under review.
