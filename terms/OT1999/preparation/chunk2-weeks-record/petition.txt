STATEMENT OF THE CASE 

A. The Crime and the Trial 

On February 24, 1993, Lonnie Weeks, Jr. was a passenger in a speeding car traveling 
southbound on Interstate Route 95 in Northern Virginia. A State Trooper pulled the vehicle over 
on an off-ramp in Prince William County. The Trooper approached and stood near the rear driver's 
side of the car, and he directed Weeks to get out of the passenger side of the vehicle. After Weeks 
stepped from the car, he shot the Trooper more than once in rapid succession while standing near 
the car’s front passenger door. The Trooper died as a result of at least one of the gunshot wounds. 
Weeks was subsequently arrested and charged with the capital murder of a law enforcement officer 
in the performance of his duties. VA. CODE §18.2-31(6). 

Weeks admitted causing the Trooper's death, but he pleaded not guilty to capital murder on 
the ground that he did not act with premeditation. In support of this theory, and also to support his 
penalty-phase arguments, Weeks moved the trial court to appoint an independent pathologist and a 
firearms expert to assist the defense. The trial court denied this motion.’ The jury returned a verdict 


of guilty of capital murder. 


' The court's stated reason for denying Weeks’ motion was that pathologists and firearms 
experts deal with issues of fact on which experts do not differ, rather than issues of opinion; in 
the court's view, independent experts appointed to assist the defense therefore would not reach 
any different conclusion than the prosecution’s experts. (Record on Appeal to the United States 
Court of Appeals for the Fourth Circuit ("R.") at 641- 43.) Weeks notes that regardless of its 
reasons, the Circuit Court of Prince William County did not grant independent experts in any 
field — except for mental health experts whose appointment is mandated by statute — in any of 
the 10,000 felony cases that it processed between 1985 and 1995. Testimony to this effect was 
presented by Richard Conway, an Assistant Commonwealth’s Attorney in Prince William 
County, on behalf of the Commonwealth in Smith v. Angelone, No. 93CV710 (E.D. Va. Nov. 8, 
1995). Weeks has lodged a copy the transcript of Conway’s testimony with the Court. 


3 


In the penalty phase, Weeks presented evidence in mitigation. In addition to his own 
testimony, Weeks offered testimony from family, church members, and friends regarding his 
personal history and character. After presenting this evidence, Weeks proffered instruction C1 and 
asked that the jury be instructed, in addition to Virginia’s pattern capital sentencing instruction, as 
follows: 

Even if you find that the Commonwealth has proved one or both of the aggravating 

factors beyond a reasonable doubt, you may give effect to the evidence in mitigation 

by sentencing the Defendant to life in prison. 

(App. at 85.) The trial court denied the instruction. (R. at 1254-55.) Instead, the court provided the 
jury with four brief instructions pene ee their duties in assessing Weeks’ sentence. (App. at 86- 
89.) Only one of those instructions, Instruction #2, described the process by which jurors were to 
arrive at a sentence selection determination. That pattern instruction, which this Court found to be 
facially constitutional in Buchanan v. Angelone, 118 S. Ct. 757 (1998), reads, in pertinent part, as 


follows: 


If you find from the evidence that the Commonwealth has proved beyond a 
reasonable doubt, either of the two alternatives, and as to that alternative you are 
unanimous, then you may fix the punishment of the defendant at death; or, if you 
believe from all the evidence that the death penalty is not justified, then you shall fix 
the punishment of the defendant at life imprisonment, or imprisonment for live [sic] 
and a fine of a specific amount, but not more than $100,000. 


(App. at 87.) 

This instruction correctly shows that Virginia is a nonweighing state in which the sentencer 
must make two independent determin ations — death eligibility and sentence selection — in sequence: 
In assessing the penalty of any person convicted of an offense for which the death 
penalty may be imposed, a sentence of death shall not be imposed unless the court 
or jury shall (1) after consideration of the past criminal record of convictions of the 
defendant, find that there is a probability that the defendant would commit criminal 


4 


acts of violence that would constitute a continuing serious threat to society or that his 

conduct in committing the offense for which he stands charged was outrageously or 

wantonly vile, horrible or inhuman in that it involved torture, depravity of mind or 

an aggravated battery to the victim [death eligibility decision); and (2) recommend 

that the penalty of death be imposed [sentence selection decision). 

Va. Cope § 19.2-264.2. The pattern instruction also reflects the fact that Virginia juries are 
constitutionally required to consider mitigating evidence in making each of these determinations. 
See, e.g., Tuggle v. Netherland, 516 U.S. 10, 14 (1995) (per curiam) (holding that trial court's failure 
to appoint psychiatrist to develop mental health mitigating evidence deprived Virginia defendant not 
only of ability to rebut state’s evidence of future dangerousness, but also "may well have affected 
the jury's ultimate decision, based on all of the evidence before it, to sentence petitioner to death 
rather than life imprisonment”). 

Weeks’ jury was given a written copy of the instructions as well as the verdict form to take 
with them and consult during deliberations. 

Eighty minutes after the jury retired to deliberate on Weeks’ sentence, it sent out a written 
question asking whether a sentence of life imprisonment would include the possibility of parole. (R. 
at 1340.) The trial court refused to answer this question, instead advising the jury that it should 
“impose such punishment as you feel is just under the evidence, and within the instructions of the 
Court. You are not to concern yourselves with what may happen afterwards." (App. at 90; R. at 
1340.) The jury . .curned to its deliberations.

B. The Fourth Circuit’s Decision Cannot Be Reconciled with This Court’s 
Decisions in Bollenbach, Boyde, and Buchanan and Undermines the

The rule for more than half a century has been that when a jury instruction is facially 
defective and the "jury makes explicit its difficulties a trial judge should clear them away with 
concrete accucsacy.” Bollenbach v. United States, 362 U.S. 607, 612-13 (1946). There is no 
principled reason for applying a different rule when the jury makes explicit its inability to 
comprehend an instruction that lawyers and judges deem to be unambiguous. Jurors, after all, "do 
not sit in solitary isolation booths parsing instructions for subtle shades of meaning in the same way 
that lawyers might." Boyde, 494 U.S. at 380-81. If the jurors don’t get it, they don’t get it — 
regardless how clear the instruction may appear to trained legal minds or even to different jurors. 
The Virginia Supreme Court's decision was contrary to and an unreasonable application of this 
Court's clearly established precedents, and the Fourth Circuit erred in upholding the state court's 
ruling. 

This Court’s more recent decisions in Boyde and Buchanan do not dilute the Bollenbach 
principle. Boyde and Buchanan both involved challenges to jury instructions that allegedly were 
ambiguous on their face. In neither case did the jurors request clarification or otherwise indicate that 
they were interpreting the instructions in a manner that precluded the consideration of mitigating 
evidence. In both cases, therefore, this Court was required to predict what reasonable jurors would 
infer from the language of the instructions and the context of the proceedings. Indeed, this Court 
indicated that the rule in Boyde is predicated on the absence of any overt evidence of jury 
misunderstanding. See Buchanan, 118 S. Ct. at 763 (finding no reasonable likelihood "in this 


context” that jury would misunderstand Virginia's pattern instruction).

Weeks eligible for death, without engaging in the sentence selection process and thus without

II. THE FEDERAL COURTS OF APPEALS ARE DEEPLY DIVIDED ON 
WHETHER READING AKE V. OKLAHOMA TO PROVIDE INDIGENT 
CRIMINAL DEFENDANTS WITH A RIGHT OF ACCESS TO 
NONPSYCHIATRIC EXPERTS WOULD ANNOUNCE A NEW RULE 
This Court long has acknowledged that states must afford indigent criminal defendants with 

a mean’ngful opportunity to present a defense. See, e.g, Douglas v. California, 372 U.S. 353 

(1963); Griffin v. Illinois, 351 U.S. 12 (1956). Although the state need not purchase for an indigent 

defendant all of the services that the wealthy may buy, see Ross v. Moffitt, 417 U.S. 600 (1974), the 

State must provide the defendant with the “basic tools of an adequate defense.” Britt v. North 

Carolina, 404 U.S. 226 (1971). 

In Ake v. Oklahoma, 470 U.S. 68, 80 (1985), this Court held that a psychiatric expert could 
constitute such a basic tool when the issue to be addressed by the psychiatrist would be a significant 
issue at trial or necessary to an adequate defense. Since the decision in Ake, the state and federal 
courts have been divided on whether the rule in that case is narrowly limited to the right to a 


psychiatrist, or if it also establishes the rule for an indigent defendant's access to other kinds of 


experts. 


In Weeks, the Fourth Circuit squarely held that to apply the principles of Ake to 
nonpsychiatric experts would announce a “new rule,” and thus that the antiretroactivity doctrine bars 
federal habeas review of Weeks’ federal constitutional claim.'' (App. at 15-18.) See Teague v. 
Lane, 489 U.S. 288 (1989). The only other court to impose this doctrinal straightjacket on Ake is 
the Ninth Circuit. The position of the Fourth and Ninth Circuits is in direct conflict with several 
other courts of appeals. The stance of the Fourth and Ninth Circuits also is inconsistent with the 
position of the highest court in every state that has directly addressed whether Ake establishes a 
federal constitutional right to nonpsychiatric experts. This Court should grant certiorari to resolve 
the conflict among the courts of appeals, and to provide guidance to the state courts that have not 
rendered an opinion about the full scope of Ake. 

A. AFederal Court Does Not Create a New Rule When It Applies a General 

Rule That Was Specifically Designed for the Purpose of Evaluating a 
Myriad of Factual Contexts 

In Teague, a plurality of this Court adopted the second Justice Harlan’s retroactivity approach 

and held that a “new rule” of federal constitutional law will not be applied or announced on collateral 


review unless the rule falls within one of two narrow exceptions.'? See Teague, 489 U.S. at 299-316 


''Because the new rule doctrine exists to “validate[] reasonable, good-faith interpretations of 
existing precedents made by state courts even though they are shown to be contrary to [other] 
decisions,” Butler v. McKellar, 494 U.S. 407, 414 (1990), there is good cause to question the 
application of the doctrine in the first instance when the defendant presented the issue to the state 
court on direct review but that court somehow failed to address it. Cf, 28 U.S.C. § 2254(d), as 
amended by the AEDPA (imposing limitations on federal habeas review only if claim actually 
“was adjudicated on the merits in State court proceedings"). 


"The first exception is for new rules that place “certain kinds of primary, private individual 
conduct beyond the power of the criminal law-making authority to proscribe.” Teague, 489 U.S. 
at 311 (plurality opinion) (internal quotation marks omitted). The second exception is for 
"*watershed rules of criminal procedure’ implicating the fundamental fairness and accuracy of 
the criminal proceeding." Saffle v. Parks, 494 U.S. 484, 495 (1990). 


23 


(plurality opinion). The new rule doctrine is predicated on interests of comity and the finality of 
criminal convictic .. Gilmore v. Taylor, 508 U.S. 333, 340 (1993). This principle “serves to ensure 
that gradual developments in the law over which reasonable jurists may disagree are not later used 
to upset the finality of state convictions valid when entered.” Sawyer v. Smith, 497 U.S. 227, 231 
(1990). 

The Court has emphasized, however, that when a rule announced in one factual circumstance 
is designed to apply to diverse factual circumstances, federal courts may safely apply that rule to the 
kind of new facts that were within the intended scope of the original rule does not announce a new 
rule. See, e.g., Penry v. Lynaugh, 492 U.S. 302 (1989) (emphasizing critical distinction between 
_extension of existing precedent on collateral review to announce new rule, and mere application of 
existing general principle to new set of facts); Wright v. West, 505 U.S. 277, 309 (1992) (Kennedy, 
J., concurring in the judgment) (when “a rule [is] designed for the specific purpose of evaluating a 
myriad of factual contexts, it will be the infrequent case that yields a result so novel that it forges a 
new rule, one not dictated by precedent”); see also id. at 304. 

In Weeks’ view, the decision in Ake established precisely this kind of rule. The standard 
announced in Ake was designed for evaluating myriad requests for access to experts, not merely 
requests for appointment of a psychiatrist. This Court likely did not, and does not, anticipate 
granting certiorari in dozens (if not hundreds) of cases to decide whether an indigent defendant has 


a constitutional right to each separate kind of expert.

B. The Decisions of the Federal Courts of Appeals Are in Conflict 

The Fourth Circuit has held squarely that Ake did not announce a rule for courts to employ 
in other factual contexts and that the principles enumerated in Ake do not apply beyond the narrow 
and specific facts of Ake: 

We interpret Ake and Caldwell together to stand for the proposition that due process 

as of the time Weeks’s conviction became final only required that an indigent 

defendant be appointed psychiatric experts when his sanity is at issue in the trial. 
(App. at 17.) See also, Gray v. Thompson, 58 F .3d 59, 66 (4" Cir. 1995) (indigent defendant has no 
constitutional right of access to private investigator), aff'd on other grounds, 518 U.S. 152 (1996). 

The Ninth Circuit shares this narrow reading of Ake. In Jackson v. YIst, 921 F.2d 882, 885- 
86 (1990), the defendant claimed that he had a federal due process right to appointment of an 
eyewitness identification expert. The Court of Appeals concluded that such a holding “would create 
a ‘new rule’ which would ‘impose[] new obligation on the [s}tates.’ Teague v. Lane, 109 S.Ct. at 
1070.” Jackson, 921 F.2d at 886. Like the Fourth Circuit, the Ninth Circuit said that Ake should be 
limited to its factual context — i.e., that due process requires only that “a trial court must appoint 
a psychiatrist for an indigent if his or her sanity will “be a significant factor at trial."” Jd at 885 
(c.*ation omitted). 

The Eighth Circuit has reached the opposite conclusion. In Little v. Armontrout, 835 F.2d 
1240 (8th Cir. 1987) (en banc), a rape victim identified Little as her assailant after she was placed 
under hypnosis by a police officer. Little, an indigent defendant, requested the state to provide him 
with an expert in hypnosis so he could intelligently challenge the qualifications of the state's 
hypnotist and his methodology. The Missouri Supreme Court found no due process error in the 
denial of the expert, concluding that Little could have challenged the identification procedures by

using the iibrary or by consulting with psychologists from a local university “at little or no expense.” 
Id. at 1242 The en banc Eighth Circuit, relying upon this Court’s decisions in Britt, Ake, and 
Caldwell, held that the “rule of Ake” applied to nonpsychiatric experts as well as psychiatrists: 

There is no principled way to distinguish between psychiatric and nonpsychiatric 

experts. The question in each case must be not what field of science or expert 

knowledge is involved, but rather how important the scientific issue is in the case, 

and how much help a defense expert could have given. 

Id. at 1243. 

The Sixth Circuit likewise concluded that the rule of Ake should be applied to requests for 
nonpsychiatric experts. Relying on this Court’s decision in Ake, that court concluded in Terry v.

The Fifth Circuit has not directly ruled on the question but appears to have accepted the

227 (5" Cir. 1993), that the trial court's denial of a request for forensic and firearms experts did not

The Tenth and Eleventh Circuits have been asked on numerous opportunities to address the

D. The Right to Nonpsychiatric Experts Comes Within the 
“Watershed” Exception to the Antiretroactivity Doctrine 


Federal habeas courts are not barred from announcing new “‘watershed rules of criminal 
procedure implicating the fundamental fairness and accuracy of the criminal proceeding.’” Graham 
v. Collins, 506 U.S. 461, 478 (1993) (quoting Teague, 489 U. S. at 311). The Fourth Circuit held 
that the rule Weeks sought to apply did not come within this “watershed” exception. 

The right to counsel articulated in Gideon v. Wainwright, 372 U.S. 335 (1963), is the most 
commonly cited example of a rule so fundamental that it would fit the “watershed” category. An 
indigent defendant’s right of access to nonpsychiatric experts is very similar to the right to counsel 
established in Gideon. Just as an ordinary individual cannot intelligently investigate and present a 
defense to a criminal charge without the assistance of an expert in the substantive and procedural 
law, nether the defendant nor his counsel can intelligently investigate and present a defense to the 
state’s forensic evidence without the assistance of a person with appropriate expertise. Few criminal 


defense lawyers are also experts in pathology or toolmarks or molecular genetics or metallurgy or 


32 


poison chemistry or any of the myriad other technical subjects that may arise in a criminal 
prosecution. Experts in all of these areas are freely available to the prosecution from state crime 
labs, the FBI, and specialized laboratories, and thus may appear in any criminal prosecution. 
Furthermore, as Weeks argued below, to hold that indigent criminal defendants have a federal 
constitutional right to the appointment of a psychiatrist in appropriate circumstances but do not have 
a corresponding right to appointment of a nonpsychiatric expert would be fundamentally unfair. 
Therefore, the Court should grant certiorari to determine whether an indigent defendant's right of 


access to nonpsychiatric experts comes with the “watershed” exception to Teague.
