| Writing | Author | Joined by | Relationship to judgment | Scope joined |
|---|---|---|---|---|

## Holdings

**Emergency authority does not become general investigative authority.** Police may enter to render emergency aid, locate victims or a dangerous person, and take supported protective or evidence-preservation measures within their actual justification. Once that justification ends, the occurrence of a homicide does not authorize a prolonged warrantless evidentiary search.

**Authority:** The unanimous per curiam opinion supplies the controlling rule and its application.

**Controlling explanation:** Mincey and Thompson reject an exception based on the crime's gravity. Here the cabin had been secured before the extended evidence-gathering search, and the trial court authorized a search of everything at the scene without tying it to an existing emergency. Dickerson likewise confines a lawful initial intrusion to its authorized purpose. The accepted emergency entry therefore cannot sustain the later categorical search. This conclusion preserves properly supported emergency action on its own facts.

**A closed briefcase requires separate search authority.** Seeing an unlocked but closed briefcase does not expose the photographs and negatives concealed within it. Opening it requires a warrant or a separately established exception.

**Authority:** The same unanimous opinion controls.

**Controlling explanation:** Hicks distinguishes observation from an additional search. The briefcase's location near the body and unlocked condition did not reveal its contents or create authority to inspect them. The trial court made no findings adequate for this Court to resolve implied consent, standardized inventory, another exigency, or harmlessness. Those preserved questions remain for the state courts; absence of findings does not establish that every alternative fails.

## Precedent Treatment

Mincey v. Arizona and Thompson v. Louisiana are applied to reject a homicide-scene exception. Minnesota v. Dickerson is applied for the limit imposed by an intrusion's actual authorization. Arizona v. Hicks is applied to distinguish viewing a container from searching concealed contents. Chapman v. California remains the standard for any properly presented harmless constitutional error. No precedent is overruled or broadened into an unconditional suppression rule.

## Law After Decision

The warrant default and recognized, fact-dependent exceptions remain unchanged. A secured homicide scene and a closed container cannot be searched on a categorical crime-scene rationale; independent justifications and harmlessness retain their separate requirements.

## Earlier public event: Texas v. Lesage; No. 98-1111; 1999-11-29

## Event

Texas v. Lesage, No. 98-1111. Summary merits disposition, November 29, 1999. Submitted without oral argument. On writ of certiorari to the United States Court of Appeals for the Fifth Circuit. The question is what a same-decision showing defeats when a rejected applicant alleges an unconstitutional admissions process, and what distinct retrospective, statutory, and prospective claims remain.

## Participation

Chief Justice Stone-Zsela and Justices Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg, and Breyer participate.

Reversed in the stated respect and remanded, 9–0. Supporting: Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg, and Breyer. The Fifth Circuit must assess Texas's showing under the correct school-borne burden and a specified constitutional process, with the genuine-dispute requirements of summary judgment preserved. The entire defense judgment is not reinstated. Claims under §§1981 and 1983 and Title VI, proper defendants, immunity, nominal/declaratory relief and continuing prospective exposure require separate treatment.

## Opinion Topology

| Writing | Author | Joined by | Relationship to judgment | Scope joined |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Per curiam opinion | The Court | Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg, Breyer | Unanimous bounded reversal and remand | Entire opinion |

## Holdings

**Same-decision proof limits relief for the rejection without erasing a completed process injury.** Once an unconstitutional admissions process has been established, the school must prove by a preponderance that a specified constitutional process would have produced the same rejection. Success defeats admission and compensation for that denial and its consequences; it does not erase the separately established unequal-process injury.

**Authority:** The unanimous per curiam opinion controls every proposition stated here.

**Controlling explanation:** Hopwood separates unequal opportunity to compete from entitlement to a seat and supplies the school-borne defense. The lawful counterfactual must allow any race use that controlling law permits; it cannot silently become a race-neutral-only requirement. Texas offered academic and qualitative evidence supporting the same rejection, but the Fifth Circuit treated its causal significance as irrelevant instead of testing the specified lawful process and burden. Its legal premise was mistaken. The evidence is not newly declared conclusive, and the Court does not decide whether the challenged program was unconstitutional merely because it considered race.

**Relief follows the particular injury and lawful remedial channel.** An established unconstitutional process may support nominal or declaratory recognition even if Texas proves the same lawful rejection, but only under an available cause against a proper defendant and subject to immunity. Prospective relief additionally requires concrete continuing or imminent exposure to unequal competition and an equitable basis.

**Controlling explanation:** Carey distinguishes recognition of a completed violation from compensation for proved loss; Northeastern Florida distinguishes unequal competition from guaranteed selection. Sections 1981 and 1983 and Title VI retain their separate requirements. Possible cessation of the practice or abandonment of prospective relief must be resolved, not assumed. No admission, damages amount, immunity waiver, personal liability or broad future-program injunction follows from this decision.

## Precedent Treatment

Hopwood v. Texas is applied to the specified lawful counterfactual, preponderance defense, independent process injury, source-specific remedies and future-exposure requirement; its racial-classification standard and permitted-use boundaries remain unchanged. Mt. Healthy City Board of Education v. Doyle supplies the causal same-decision distinction through Hopwood. Carey v. Piphus supplies the distinction between compensable harm and nominal recognition. Northeastern Florida Chapter v. Jacksonville supplies the competitive-injury principle without guaranteeing admission.

## Law After Decision

Existing admissions law remains controlling. A successful same-decision defense defeats relief caused by the lost admission, while an independently proved unconstitutional process and any continuing unequal competition retain their own causes, defenses, standing requirements and remedies. Statistical comparisons alone do not replace the required lawful counterfactual.

No particular relief is awarded.

## Holdings

### Public conviction information and a protective notification purpose

**Holding and operative rule.** This informational-privacy claim does not confer a right to conceal public conviction information. New Jersey may pursue the legitimate and substantial objective of communicating accurate risk information to persons with a concrete safety need, subject to the separate protection and safeguards governing nonpublic residential information.

**Authority.** Part I of Kennedy's opinion, joined by all eight other Justices, controls both propositions. Their agreement does not extend to every disclosure method or to all of the opinion's later parts.

**Controlling explanation.** The Court holds that a public conviction does not become confidential because notification repeats it or causes adverse attention. Paul v. Davis rejects a constitutional privacy entitlement to conceal the public record of an arrest; its distinction likewise forecloses treating public conviction facts themselves as private. That conclusion does not decide the different injury alleged from deliberate dissemination of a nonpublic current residential location. Protecting persons likely to encounter an offender is a legitimate governmental function. The present holding approves that objective, not every audience, datum or later use selected in its name. Recognition of the objective leaves the Court's separate residential-information inquiry necessary.

### Limited protection for compiled nonpublic residential information

**Holding and operative rule.** When the State compels collection and deliberately disseminates compiled nonpublic current home-address and associated identifying information in a manner exposing the home and household to outside contact, the Fourteenth Amendment protects a limited personal privacy interest against disclosure lacking a sufficient connection among the information, its recipients and the protective purpose. The protection does not extend merely by reason of government possession to public convictions, every identifying datum, every address available elsewhere, or all government-held information; actual public availability matters, but theoretical inspectability alone does not establish prior dissemination.

**Authority.** Part II of Kennedy's opinion is joined by Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Souter, Ginsburg and Breyer. These seven Justices directly adopt this limited extension and its boundaries; no combination of separate rationales is needed.

**Controlling explanation.** The Court holds that exposing a nonpublic household location differs from repeating the public fact of conviction. Sheets v. Salt Lake County protects intimate marital and family information against intentional official dissemination without sufficient governmental justification. Its attention to actual exposure, rather than possession or ownership alone, supports this limited extension to the deliberate dissemination of compiled residential information. Doe v. SEPTA assumed a medical-information interest and rejected a claim arising from confined benefits administration; it neither established a general right nor authorized public dissemination detached from the governmental function. Its reservation leaves this outward disclosure question open. The objection that addresses are often readily found does not answer whether these current residential locations are nonpublic and what exposure the State's compilation and distribution produce. Conversely, privacy does not follow from an individual's preference alone. The Court recognizes no general secrecy entitlement in identifiers, no universal constitutional balancing formula, and no independent constitutional rule for the effort needed to assemble otherwise public scattered data. The interest here rests on nonpublic residential exposure, not embarrassment about a conviction.

### Notification requires safeguards fitted to its protective function

**Holding and operative rule.** Disclosure of protected residential information through Tier Two or Tier Three notification must reasonably fit the notification's protective function: the actual information and recipient audience must have a sufficient safety connection, and safeguards must constrain unnecessary disclosure and redistribution. The protective objective alone does not establish that every distribution or subsequent use is justified; the District Court must consider the adequacy of safeguards on the actual record, without a universal least-restrictive-means requirement, a guarantee of perfect containment, a prescribed assurance form, or a newly established proof quantum.

**Authority.** Part III of Kennedy's opinion is joined by Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Souter, Ginsburg and Breyer. These seven Justices adopt both the rule and retention of the safeguards remand.

**Controlling explanation.** The Court holds that a proper reason to notify one audience is not a sufficient reason to expose the same information to everyone. Sheets requires a governmental justification for the actual disclosure; Doe preserves the distinction between functionally confined use and outward dissemination. Those principles permit practical public-safety notification while requiring attention to recipients, scope, use, confidentiality and redistribution. The danger that information cannot be recalled supports a concrete safeguards inquiry, not a ban on all notification. The motions tendered first to the Court of Appeals belong before the District Court for consideration of the challenged applications and any appropriate response; their contents are not adjudicated facts. The Court does not determine whether particular existing precautions are adequate, whether particular alleged harm occurred, or whether every recipient's later speech is attributable to the State. It prescribes no restriction on independently protected private expression. W.P.'s independent state-entitlement and ordinary advance-process requirements remain at their actual scope; this decision neither enlarges its class nor converts its four-Justice notification-plan proof rationale into a general controlling standard.

### The notification provisions remain available while application-specific safeguards are examined

**Holding and operative rule.** Recognition of the limited residential interest does not invalidate the notification provisions as a matter of law: properly directed public-safety notification may disclose protected information. The summary judgment rejecting the challenge to those provisions is affirmed, while the distinct remand permits the District Court to examine whether the ways notification is applied provide adequate protection against unnecessary disclosure.

**Authority.** Part IV of Kennedy's opinion is joined by Stevens, O'Connor, Souter, Ginsburg and Breyer. These six Justices supply the controlling legal explanation; Scalia and Thomas add judgment-only votes to make the information-disclosure affirmance 8–1, on their no-established-substantive-right ground. Stone-Zsela joins the substantive protection and safeguards rule but opposes this affirmance and does not join its rationale. The two judgment-only votes add no support to Part IV's reasoning.

**Controlling explanation.** The Court holds that the protected interest and the public-safety function can coexist. The challenge to notification as a matter of law cannot succeed merely because a properly selected safety audience receives residential information. The State's substantial protective interest supports that limited legal conclusion; it is not a finding that all present applications are sufficiently confined. The Court of Appeals separately required examination of the information offered about actual distribution and safeguards, so affirmance does not extinguish that inquiry. The objection that summary judgment must be vacated until safeguards are adjudicated treats the permitted notification function and its challenged implementation as inseparable. The six Justices preserve the judgment against the former challenge and the remand concerning the latter. They do not resolve the contents of the sealed motions, approve any unproved dissemination, or bar relief that the safeguards proceedings lawfully establish. No independently sufficient alternative ground, categorical denial of privacy, or generalized seven-factor test is adopted.

## Law After Decision

This extends Sheets beyond its intimate marital-information setting without establishing a general right to control government-held information. A sufficient connection among information, safety audience and protective function, together with reasonably fitted safeguards against unnecessary disclosure and redistribution, governs the notification at issue.

Public conviction information remains outside this secrecy claim. Properly directed notification remains permissible, and the eight-Justice affirmance of the information-disclosure summary judgment, supported by a six-Justice controlling rationale, coexists with the seven-Justice safeguards remand. No general heightened plan-proof standard, automatic secrecy for all compiled public records, liability for private redistribution, or categorical restraint on notification follows.

## Holdings

**Federal lien law reaches the taxpayer's existing power to accept or redirect inherited value.** A legally effective state-created power to accept an inheritance or channel its value to another by disclaimer is a right to property within 26 U.S.C. §6321 on this record. State law defines the control, but federal law determines whether the lien reaches it; state relation back cannot erase control that already existed for federal lien purposes.

**Authority:** Souter's opinion, joined by all eight other Justices, states the unanimous rule and application.

**Controlling explanation:** National Bank of Commerce separates the existence of a state-law interest from its federal tax treatment. Romani requires respect for Congress's specific priority choices and supplies no broad exemption for a disclaimer. ## Holdings

**1. A speech-selected access and use condition requires First Amendment review.** When the State makes covered records available but denies access or restricts use because the requester intends truthful, lawful commercial solicitation or the conceded commercial address-distribution activity, the condition is subject to the governing commercial-speech standard. The absence of a general right to government information does not exempt that selection: the State must identify a substantial interest, establish real harm and material advancement, and show reasonable fit.

**Authority:** Part I of Breyer's opinion, joined by all eight other Justices, is unanimous on this rule. It does not resolve the disputed commercial application.

**Controlling explanation:** Lanphere separates a general demand for records from a condition selecting among intended speech uses. Houchins supplies no universal right to information; it does not answer the different condition question. Central Hudson supplies review of lawful, nonmisleading commercial activity; Edenfield requires real harm and material advancement; Fox requires reasonable fit without demanding the least restrictive means. The lawful, nonmisleading premise accepted below remains operative for that activity and does not require production of each future letter. Privacy is a substantial interest, but its existence does not itself prove the validity of this selected line. No right to creation of lists, universal criminal-file access, protection for deception or coercion, or exemption from neutral confidentiality follows. Profit motive alone does not settle the classification of the Register's editorial or mixed material. Commercial overbreadth cannot replace proof of a violation, but the existence of valid regulation against deception, coercion or harassment does not by itself establish the validity of this categorical condition; any relief must fit the established violation and equitable need.

**Authority:** Part II of Breyer's opinion, joined by Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas and Ginsburg, supplies eight votes. Stone-Zsela does not join this part.

**Controlling explanation:** Lanphere's application-specific adjudication and relief require attention to the actual claimant and burden. Petitioners acknowledged facial and as-applied pleading; the publisher continued to assert its own denial and actual address-service activity. The Register also contains substantive editorial material. Neither the lower courts' facial description nor a contested cross-petition argument establishes abandonment. These facts require consideration of the actual claims; they do not establish their merits, classify every communication, or prove that the signed decree exceeds lawful bounds.

The Court decides no vagueness, equal-protection, due-process, state-constitutional or separate statutory-entitlement question. ## Holdings

**I. The direct appeal is within the Court's jurisdiction.** The heard denial of injunctive relief by the required three-judge court in this Commonwealth legislative-apportionment action is reviewable under 28 U.S.C. §1253. Covenant §902's intergovernmental consultation procedure does not displace these residents' judicial remedy for asserted personal vote dilution.

**Controlling explanation:** Sections 1821(c) and 1824 of Title 48 apply the relevant federal judicial provisions to the Commonwealth; Calero-Toledo supports treating an autonomous territorial commonwealth's government as a State for the relevant three-judge-court function. Section 2284 applies to this general legislative-apportionment challenge, and the actual panel, hearing and merits denial satisfy the direct-review route. The two Saipan residents allege diminished weight of their own votes. Section 902 establishes political consultation, while §202 expressly preserves judicial consistency review. Neither a new certiorari grant nor an exclusive political-remedy rule is needed.

**II. The particular federal territorial settlement may preserve one equal-island chamber.** The constitutional requirements for this unincorporated territory permit Congress's express accommodation of equal municipal representation in one chamber alongside a population-based other chamber. This specific protected territorial arrangement does not violate equal protection merely because it would fail the population rule governing both chambers of an ordinary State legislature.

**Authority:** The merits portion of Kennedy's opinion is joined by all seven other Associates. Stone-Zsela dissents from this rule and its application.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court recognizes the severe voting disparity and the Senate's general legislative power. Reynolds and Board of Estimate explain why those facts would defeat ordinary state or local geographical representation, but they did not adjudicate this distinct federal territorial settlement. The territorial constitutional framework, reflected in Verdugo-Urquidez, requires attention to the particular institutional relationship while retaining constitutional protection for residents. Covenant §203(c) expressly requires equal municipal representation. Section 501(a) makes Fourteenth Amendment §1 applicable as if the Commonwealth were a State, to the extent it does not apply of its own force; §501(b) preserves the validity of, and Congress's power to consent to, §203. The statutory terms defeat simple transplantation through §501(a), although Congress's constitutional power still requires independent decision. Here the expressly protected allocation, local self-government and population-based other chamber support the confined accommodation. ## Holdings

**Common authority:** Thomas's opinion is joined in full by all eight other Justices. Each rule, application and reservation below has direct unanimous support.

**Civil RICO requires a direct relation, and these ordinary expenditures are too remote.** Section 1964(c) requires factual causation and proximate cause, including a direct relation between the asserted business-or-property injury and the alleged violation. A fund's alleged medical payments arising through participants' smoking, illness and treatment do not satisfy that requirement on this pleaded chain, even when defendants allegedly intended the fund to bear the cost.

**Controlling explanation:** Holmes supplies a contextual inquiry informed by attribution, overlapping recovery and the availability of directly injured plaintiffs; those considerations are not three independent elements. Here the fund suffers a real monetary loss, but only after the participant's conduct and bodily injury generate treatment and payment. Alleged targeting and foreseeable costs do not eliminate those links. Attribution depends on separating loss produced by deception from illness and costs that would have occurred despite accurate information or from other causes. Limited duplication favors the funds, and participants cannot recover bodily-injury damages under RICO, but neither fact makes this expenditure chain direct. McCready involved a reimbursement denial that itself implemented the scheme, rather than a payment arising through another person's illness. The Court accepts the allegations and does not demand a damages model at the pleading stage. Statistical methods can quantify loss; they cannot supply an absent direct relation.

**The cessation-program theory independently fails on its pleaded chain.** Describing concealment as interference with the funds' cost-control decisions does not make the claimed medical payments direct when loss still depends on different fund action, changed participant behavior, avoided illness and avoided treatment.

**Controlling explanation:** This theory receives separate consideration because the funds identify their own decisionmaking. The alleged loss nevertheless remains downstream healthcare expenditure; no independently pleaded property loss occurs through the fund decision without the intervening illness chain. The Court finds no particular program ineffective and imposes no evidentiary damages burden now. A different direct fraud upon a fund, and whether any amendment could state one, are not presented.

**The funds' property and legal capacity remain distinct from participants' bodily injuries.** Benefit payments reduce the funds' own property, but their legal form and payment duty neither transfer participants' bodily injuries nor create an exemption from RICO's causal requirements.

**Controlling explanation:** ERISA and the trusts' Taft-Hartley form identify their benefit function and capacity; they establish neither a substantive recovery right against these manufacturers nor a categorical inability to protect their own property. The District Court's no-cognizable-property-injury rationale is not adopted. Entity status does not alter the same directness rule applied to the actual asserted interest.

**The Clayton Act claim is independently too remote.** Under AGC's antitrust inquiry, these healthcare expenditures following participants' purchases, smoking and illness are not sufficiently direct injuries from the alleged suppression of safer tobacco products.

**Controlling explanation:** The funds are not consumers or competitors in the cigarette market, but those labels are not an exclusive test. Their payments were consequences of illness, not the mechanism implementing the alleged market exclusion as reimbursement denial was in McCready. This directness defect suffices without defining every antitrust-injury boundary. RICO receives no imported antitrust-injury, special racketeering-injury or purchaser-seller requirement.

**Limits:** No exceptionless third-party-payer bar, categorical prohibition of aggregate proof, state-law disposition or ruling on an unpresented amendment is adopted.

## Holdings

**I. The enacted provisions unmistakably authorize the private federal action.** Sections 630(b), 626(b)–(c), and the incorporated FLSA provisions, read together, authorize private ADEA actions against state employers in federal court. That authorization establishes neither constitutional power, State consent, discrimination nor entitlement to relief.

**Authority:** Part I of Ginsburg's opinion is joined by Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Souter, Ginsburg, and Breyer, with the author included in that seven-Justice coalition. Kennedy and Thomas disagree.

**Controlling explanation:** Section 630(b) expressly includes States and agencies. Section 626(b) incorporates specified FLSA powers, remedies and procedures, excluding §216(a), and treats amounts owing from an ADEA violation as unpaid wages for enforcement purposes. Section 216(b) expressly permits actions against public-agency employers in federal or state court, and §203(x) includes state government and agencies. Section 626(c) also provides the aggrieved person's action. Treating its competent-court language as the sole authorization while reducing the incorporation to payment calculations or incidental procedure fails to give the express incorporation of remedies its full operation. Seminole Tribe and Florida Prepaid require clear authorization separately from constitutional amenability; this combined text supplies it.

**Operative qualifications:** Section 626(b) permits liquidated damages only for willful violations. Section 626(c)(1) ends the individual's right to bring the action when the EEOC commences an action enforcing that employee's right. The separately incorporated §216(b) terminates its action/party right upon the Secretary's §217 complaint seeking restraint of delay in the employee's unpaid minimum wages or overtime, or legal or equitable relief for alleged violations of §215(a)(3), the prohibition on retaliation. These distinct provisions are not collapsed into one identical rule, and no such governmental filing is adjudicated here.

**II. Article I independently sustains this authorized enforcement measure.** Congress may enforce the valid federal age-employment duty against States through this expressly authorized private federal action when both the duty and its enforcement measure fall within Congress's Article I authority. The Eleventh Circuit's categorical immunity dismissal therefore cannot stand.

**Authority:** Parts II–III of Ginsburg's opinion have direct support from Stone-Zsela, Stevens, Souter, Ginsburg, and Breyer.

**Controlling explanation:** Florida Prepaid Part III states the governing Article I enforcement principle, rather than a patent-only permission. EEOC v. Wyoming and Garcia support the commerce basis for regulating covered state employment. Allowing the injured employee to prove the enacted violation and obtain the specified relief directly enforces that valid duty and is an appropriate measure within the present threshold inquiry. Changing the regulated subject from patents to employment does not remove the general principle. The contrary distinction between substantive duty and treasury suit is the constitutional objection rejected by that precedent, not an existing limitation that defeats this action. Alden's separate state-court holding does not require resort there first, and College Savings means continued employment is not constructive consent. The action rests on congressional authority, not a new waiver finding.

**Limits and questions not reached:** The Court reserves the ADEA's §5 validity. Age classifications receive rational-basis constitutional review, while the statute supplies broader protection; this distinction neither establishes a §5 holding nor imposes an individual constitutional-tort prerequisite. Coverage, defenses, proof and award-specific statutory and constitutional limits remain for ordinary adjudication. No officer action, state exhaustion requirement or universal authorization of every federal remedy is created.

## Event

New York v. Hill, No. 98-1299. Plenary merits decision, January 11, 2000; October Term 1999, chunk 1. Argued November 2, 1999. On writ of certiorari to the New York Court of Appeals, 92 N.Y.2d 406, 704 N.E.2d 542 (1998).

## Public Action

The Court reverses the judgment of the New York Court of Appeals and remands.

## Judgment & Remedy

**Reversed and remanded, 9–0.** **Supporting:** Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer.

The conviction is reinstated as against the Article III(a)/Article V(c) dismissal ground, and the case is remanded for any preserved state or federal issue the court below did not resolve. ## Opinion Topology

Justice Stevens delivers the Opinion of the Court, joined in full by Chief Justice Stone-Zsela and Justices O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer. All nine join every controlling proposition and the bounded remedy. No separate writing issues.

## Holdings

**Common authority:** Stevens's Opinion of the Court, joined in full by Stone-Zsela, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer, controls all four propositions below. Each has nine direct votes at the stated level of generality; no combination of separate rationales is needed.

### Counsel may bind a represented prisoner to an expressly agreed trial date

**Holding and operative rule:** When defense counsel expressly agrees, before expiration of Article III(a)'s period, to a specified trial date beyond that period, the represented prisoner may not obtain dismissal because the agreed date exceeded the deadline, absent a contemporaneous objection or reservation when the date is set, a conflict, or a showing that counsel lacked authority in the particular proceeding. The prisoner need not personally attend, repeat the agreement, or undergo a waiver colloquy for this scheduling act; the rule does not authorize counsel to override an express timely objection.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that choosing the date of a contested trial ordinarily belongs to counsel's management of the case. Taylor v. Illinois supplies the distinction between professional trial management, which binds the represented accused, and basic personal choices that counsel cannot surrender. Jones v. Barnes likewise preserves the accused's ultimate choices while allowing counsel to conduct the litigation. Article III(a) contains no command that the prisoner personally make this scheduling decision. Requiring a personal colloquy for each genuine accommodation would impair counsel's coordination of preparation, witnesses, competing proceedings and the court's calendar.

That classification supplies the authority to agree. The absence of a statutory prohibition, standing alone, does not make every statutory protection waivable. Mezzanatto concerns a particular knowing and voluntary impeachment agreement; it supplies no universal waiver rule. Olano requires attention to the right and distinguishes affirmative relinquishment from mere inaction. Brookhart preserves the accused's basic choice to contest guilt against counsel's contrary surrender; a date agreement does not abandon that defense.

The holding is confined to genuine, unqualified agreement within counsel's authority. It neither permits wholesale surrender of the compact nor approves indefinite or bad-faith delay or delay beyond the agreed date. The agreement here preceded expiration; the effect of consent first given afterward is not presented or decided. Coercion, misunderstanding, ineffective assistance and an actual conflict remain distinct questions on their own predicates. No such predicate is established here, and the Court imposes no new hearing, personal-advice requirement or burden of proof.

### Counsel's January 9 agreement supplies the required assent

**Holding and operative rule:** Counsel's express January 9, 1995 agreement to the May 1 trial date binds Hill as to that date. Express acceptance has the same effect as proposing the identical date; neither an Article III citation, proof of deadline-specific advice nor separate personal authorization is required for this scheduling decision.

**Controlling explanation:** Hill initiated the Article III process while imprisoned in Ohio. The trial court found that 167 nonexcludable days had elapsed when counsel agreed to the date. The agreement therefore preceded expiration on the accepted computation. Hill's initial request established his entitlement to the statutory timetable; it did not place every later calendar decision beyond counsel's authority.

The Court of Appeals permitted explicit waiver or an affirmative request for treatment inconsistent with the Agreement, but distinguished counsel's acceptance of a date the prosecutor proposed. That distinction cannot govern the legal effect of the same affirmative scheduling choice. Counsel did more than remain silent: he expressly accepted May 1 in court. The rule rests on that act, not an assumed strategy, knowledge of the precise deadline, or Hill's personal attendance or assent.

Hill's April 17 motion was made before trial and preserves his legal challenge to the agreement's effect. It was not a contemporaneous objection or reservation when the date was set, and does not restore a dismissal entitlement defeated by the valid agreement. The Court rejects the motion on that ground, not for failure to preserve it. The Court decides no general rule governing withdrawal of consent or a later request to change the schedule on an independently available ground.

### A date agreement does not replace the compact's statutory conditions

**Holding and operative rule:** An effective agreement to a particular trial date is distinct from an Article III(a) continuance or Article VI(a) tolling. Article III(a) permits a necessary or reasonable continuance only for good cause shown in open court with the prisoner or counsel present; Article VI(a) separately tolls time while the prisoner is unable to stand trial, as determined by the court with jurisdiction.

**Controlling explanation:** Fex v. Michigan supplies Article III(a)'s commencement rule: for a prisoner serving a term of imprisonment who seeks final disposition of an untried charge supporting a detainer in another participating State, the 180 days begin only upon actual receipt of the required written notice and disposition request by both the prosecuting officer and the appropriate court. Delivery to the custodian alone does not start the period. Its separate continuance and inability provisions remain operative.

The statutory permission for either prisoner or counsel to be present is consistent with counsel's scheduling role. Presence is not itself good cause. The Court finds neither a qualifying continuance nor inability to stand trial as an independent justification for the January-to-May interval. The proviso governing judicial extensions does not displace counsel's ordinary authority to agree to this particular date. The compact's interests in prompt disposition, cooperation and rehabilitation neither eliminate the proviso's conditions nor make every affirmative agreement ineffective.

The accepted exclusions for pending defense motions are part of the trial court's computation in this case; they establish no rule of automatic motion-pendency tolling. The Court supplies no new receipt or expiration date and no grace period. This prisoner-initiated Article III case decides no Article IV(c) question. Other compact protections, including anti-shuttling requirements, retain their own predicates and are not surrendered by this date agreement.

### The agreement defeats this dismissal ground, while the statutory remedy remains mandatory for an actual violation

**Holding and operative rule:** Article V(c) requires dismissal with prejudice when the applicable trial period is violated without an effective agreement or applicable statutory exception; no additional showing of defense impairment or bad faith, and no harmlessness exception, is imposed. Because Hill's agreement defeats the asserted Article III(a)/Article V(c) ground, the Court reverses the judgment dismissing the indictment, reinstates the conviction as against that ground, and remands for any preserved state or federal issue not resolved below.

**Controlling explanation:** Article V(c)'s mandatory remedy does not answer the antecedent question whether the agreed date can constitute the violation asserted. It cannot do so here. The provision's separate refusal-or-failure-to-accept-temporary-custody ground remains intact and is not presented. When dismissal is required, the detainer based on the dismissed charge also loses force.

Reed v. Farley addresses collateral relief for an assumed compact violation. Its collateral threshold supplies no prejudice prerequisite for this preserved direct-review claim and no independent waiver finding. The Court removes the legal ground on which the Court of Appeals dismissed the indictment; it does not decide other preserved challenges to the conviction or order unconditional reinstatement free of their consideration.

## Precedent Treatment

- **Taylor v. Illinois, 484 U.S. 400, 417–418 (1988):** Its distinction between counsel's trial management and the accused's basic personal choices is applied to the express selection of a trial date. Its particular discovery-misconduct facts are not attributed to Hill.
- **Jones v. Barnes, 463 U.S. 745, 751–754 (1983):** Its division between professional litigation management and the accused's ultimate choices supports counsel's scheduling authority; the accused retains the decisions whether to plead guilty, waive a jury, testify and take an appeal.
- **Brookhart v. Janis, 384 U.S. 1, 7–8 (1966):** Its prohibition on counsel surrendering the practical substance of a not-guilty defense over the accused's express objection remains intact; this date agreement does not make that surrender.
- **Fex v. Michigan, February 23, 1993:** Its actual-receipt trigger, qualified continuance power, separate inability tolling and mandatory dismissal rule remain unchanged. Today's decision adds the effect of an authorized express date agreement; Fex itself did not decide that question.
- **United States v. Olano, April 26, 1993:** Its distinction between intentional relinquishment and inaction, with right-specific waiver requirements, is applied. Its Rule 52(b) prejudice allocation does not govern Hill's preserved motion.
- **Godinez v. Moran, June 24, 1993:** Its separation of competence from informed and voluntary personal waiver remains intact. Neither competence nor representation supplies a finding of Hill's personal assent, and personal plea or counsel-waiver requirements do not govern routine scheduling.
- **Reed v. Farley, June 20, 1994:** Its recognition of the compact as federal law is applied; its statutory collateral-relief threshold is distinguished from direct review. Reed supplies neither a merits waiver nor a defense-impairment requirement here.
- **United States v. Mezzanatto, January 18, 1995:** Its limited enforcement of a knowing and voluntary impeachment agreement is consistent with giving lawful consent its actual effect, but is not expanded into a general statutory-waiver presumption or a rule allocating this decision to counsel.
- **Libretti v. United States, November 7, 1995:** Its limits on consent remain: an agreement cannot enlarge statutory punishment or replace a required substantive predicate. Its particular forfeiture-verdict waiver does not require the prisoner's personal colloquy for this separately classified scheduling act.

## Holdings

### The uncertain certificate does not end this established competitive controversy

**Holding and operative rule:** When an able and ready bidder with concrete continuing exposure challenges unequal competitive treatment, the party relying on postfiling cessation must adequately establish that the challenged disadvantage will not reasonably recur. Adarand's state certification does not carry that showing because its federal acceptance and durable effect remain unsettled; this conclusion does not relax initial standing or stage-appropriate proof, and a supported durable elimination of prospective disadvantage could require a different mootness determination.

**Authority:** The per curiam opinion, joined in full by all nine participating Justices identified in Opinion Topology, supplies a direct unanimous holding on the threshold question.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that the certificate does not establish an end to the controversy. Adarand v. Peña recognizes injury to equal competitive opportunity without requiring proof of a lost contract and requires concrete renewed exposure for prospective relief. Adarand's repeated bidding supplies that exposure. Northeastern Florida distinguishes a change that actually ends the challenged exclusion from one that leaves it in place; City of Mesquite supplies the continuing voluntary-cessation principle. Lesage keeps injury distinct from entitlement to particular relief.

Colorado accepted self-certification under its changed procedure, but the federal government has not approved that procedure or endorsed this certification. It retains review if a third-party challenge reaches DOT, and DOT may require withdrawal proceedings upon reasonable cause to believe federal eligibility criteria are unmet. The government's reliance on existing certification therefore leaves the certificate's federal effect unresolved. The conclusion rests on these combined facts and continuing bidding exposure, not on a rule that every reviewable certificate creates permanent jurisdiction or that withdrawal is inevitable. No actual third-party challenge, withdrawal order or present ineligibility is found.

The appellate mootness judgment must be reversed. That result removes mootness as the ground for terminating this action and vacating the favorable district judgment; it does not affirm that judgment's constitutional reasoning or automatically reinstate all disputed relief. The remaining preserved issues and the decree's lawful effect return for ordinary proceedings under the June 12, 1995 mandate. That mandate requires application of the common substantial-justification standard and did not finally uphold the program. The Court orders no new complaint, injunction, award, program redesign or special evidentiary procedure.

**Precedent treatment:** Peña's competitive-injury holding and bounded remand govern; Northeastern Florida and City of Mesquite supply the continuing-injury and cessation distinction; Lesage preserves separate remedial predicates. None is changed.

## Holdings

**Common authority:** The standing, deterrent-redress and bounded-remand holdings in O'Connor's opinion have direct support from Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Kennedy, Souter, Ginsburg, and Breyer. Scalia and Thomas disagree. Stevens's additional penalty-survival theory is not a holding of the Court.

**Concrete interference with members' own river use supplies injury.** Supported curtailment of identified local recreational and aesthetic use because of reasonable concern about ongoing mercury discharges is a concrete personal injury; Article III does not require separate proof of environmental damage when the asserted injury is this loss of use and enjoyment. General interest in compliance or unexplained subjective concern alone remains insufficient.

**Controlling explanation:** Lujan requires personal interests and stage-appropriate support, rather than a generalized desire for lawful government. Curtis identified nearby downstream activities and a present wish to return. His childhood cessation was not caused by pollution; that fact does not erase the distinct present-use assertion. Moore's two visits do not become a history of frequent recreation, but her desired activities likewise must be evaluated accurately. Patterson's earlier local recreation and present curtailment independently connect CLEAN's claim to the river. These accounts differ from indefinite intentions to visit distant places. The later findings of adequate water quality and no demonstrated significant environmental harm deserve weight, but do not conclusively resolve reasonable lost enjoyment during ongoing discharge violations at filing. The Court does not treat a permit breach as injury to everyone downstream, decide every property valuation, or separately establish Sierra Club's standing when injured members of the other organizations support the same joined request.

**Deterrent penalties can redress supported threatened personal injury.** A statutorily authorized civil penalty payable to the Treasury can provide Article III redress when it is likely to deter continuing or reasonably recurring violations affecting the plaintiff's supported personal interests. Payment to the plaintiff is unnecessary for that preventive effect, but punishment of wholly past conduct without a remaining remedial connection, satisfaction from punishment and fees alone are insufficient.

**Controlling explanation:** Steel Co. dismissed a suit filed after cure with no continuing or imminent violation alleged, while expressly leaving deterrent redress available for supported threatened injury. This action began amid mercury exceedances; the record contains fourteen on or after June 12, 1992 and thirteen strictly after that filing date. The penalty can affect compliance with those same obligations and therefore the use interests at risk. Government enforcement and other litigation exposure do not eliminate the supported deterrent effect on this record. The Court does not demand certainty or hold that every public penalty necessarily supplies redress.

**Later compliance does not automatically establish mootness; the closure evidence must be assessed.** A defendant relying on voluntary cessation must make it absolutely clear that the challenged injurious conduct cannot reasonably be expected to recur. Substantial compliance sufficient to defeat an injunction does not automatically satisfy that burden or erase standing that existed at filing.

**Controlling explanation:** The Fourth Circuit treated the remaining Treasury penalty as incapable of affecting a live controversy; that premise conflicts with the supported deterrent connection. Later closure presents a different factual question. The affidavit identifies the last discharge on September 1, 1998, distinct closure-plan approvals and dismantling/sale status as of May 21, 1999. It also admits permit retention. The 1997 agreement required incineration to cease, while allowing specified equivalent treatment capacity subject to approval, permits, reconstruction conditions and cessation of incineration before use. Neither a retained permit nor dismantling alone decides recurrence. The lower court must evaluate those facts and legal restraints without presuming resumed operations or permanent impossibility. An asserted 1999 reporting notice, known through a declaration's database/conversation account, establishes neither resumed mercury discharge nor conclusive mootness.

**Limits and questions not reached:** The Court does not newly affirm or increase the $405,800 penalty, adjudicate the preserved diligent-prosecution defense, award fees, reopen the unappealed denial of an injunction, or decide the unpresented Article II question. The dispute over whether the liability ruling itself was declaratory relief does not establish a separate declaratory decree in the record. Fees neither cure missing standing nor alone prevent mootness. Broader survival of a properly initiated penalty claim after proven permanent cessation is reserved by the Court.

## Holdings

**I. Suspicion must remain individualized when flight and location are considered together.** Terry requires objective, particularized reasonable suspicion under the whole circumstances. Flight may contribute to that assessment, and a demonstrated connection between the location and crime may supply context, but location alone is insufficient and neither any flight nor every flight in a broadly described high-crime area creates a universal permission to stop.

**Authority:** Part I of Stone-Zsela's opinion has all nine Justices' direct support as the governing law applied here.

**Controlling explanation:** Terry permits grounded inferences rather than proof of an offense, while Brown v. Texas rejects substituting a neighborhood label for an individualized basis. A refusal to cooperate or departure from police is not automatically criminally suspicious. Ornelas requires independent review of ultimate constitutional sufficiency with respect for historical findings and due weight to supported local inferences; it permits no supplementation of testimony. The later discovery of a firearm cannot justify the earlier stop. This common rule leaves the Justices' disagreement about its application intact.

**II. This record does not establish the required suspicion.** Wardlow's observed departure and route, considered with the credited narcotics setting and a permissible police-recognition inference, do not supply a sufficiently particularized basis for suspecting him of wrongdoing on this record. The initial stop was therefore unlawful, and the Illinois judgment sustaining suppression is affirmed.

**Authority:** Part II of Stone-Zsela's opinion has direct support from Stone-Zsela, Stevens, Souter, Ginsburg, and Breyer.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court credits the state high court's finding about narcotics activity and does not exclude flight from consideration. Nolan saw Wardlow look toward the officers and run through a gangway and alley, but observed no law violation or transaction. His description of customers and lookouts concerned what normally occurred in such areas; it did not place a crowd or such activity beside Wardlow on this occasion. Nolan's uniform is established, while vehicle markings and Wardlow's observation of the whole caravan are not. Even allowing an inference that he recognized police, the flight's significance and the generalized setting remained too ambiguous to connect this person adequately to suspected criminal activity. The contrary reading gives those circumstances more particularizing force than this testimony supports. The Court finds neither an innocent motive nor actual guilt, requires no observed completed offense, and adds no categorical third factor.

**Questions not reached:** The separate safety basis and scope of the frisk and bag search are not adjudicated. A stronger flight-related record remains governed by Terry's existing totality inquiry.

### Trial self-representation does not establish an appellate Sixth Amendment right

**Holding and operative rule:** Faretta's right to conduct one's own defense at trial does not give a convicted defendant a Sixth Amendment right to discharge appointed counsel and personally conduct a first appeal as of right. Faretta remains fully effective at trial; this decision does not diminish an existing trial choice because a judge thinks counsel would serve the defendant better.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that the trial and appellate questions have different constitutional settings. Faretta protects the accused's personal conduct of the defense in the proceeding determining guilt. An appeal instead examines a completed record through legal claims and the procedures for reviewing alleged error. Price distinguishes permission to participate in appellate proceedings from a constitutional entitlement to conduct them personally. The importance of the appellant's liberty and preference for personal control does not make the Sixth Amendment trial guarantee coextensive with every later review proceeding.

The Court does not question Faretta's trial holding or find Martinez unable to represent himself. Godinez separates competence to choose from an actual knowing, intelligent and voluntary waiver when that choice is legally available; capacity to waive cannot create an appellate entitlement absent from the governing right. Hill concerns a confined scheduling agreement and likewise supplies no rule that counsel controls every basic personal choice. Because the asserted Sixth Amendment entitlement does not extend to this appeal, California's refusal to discharge counsel violates no such right.

**Precedent treatment:** Faretta is distinguished by proceeding and preserved at trial; Price's appellate-participation distinction is applied. Godinez's competence/actual-waiver separation and Hill's bounded scheduling rule remain intact.

### Fair first-appeal access permits represented advocacy

**Holding and operative rule:** The Fourteenth Amendment's guarantees of meaningful access and effective assistance on a first appeal as of right do not confer a personal right to reject appointed counsel and conduct that appeal entirely without counsel. A State may require represented advocacy while remaining free to allow self-representation or supplemental personal submissions; this decision requires neither option and resolves no independent competence, conflict, ineffective-assistance or access-to-record claim.

**Controlling explanation:** Douglas and Evitts require fair appellate access and effective assistance when a State provides a first appeal as of right. Those protections do not create a converse entitlement to reject the representative through whom the State provides meaningful review. The appellant's autonomy interest is substantial, but the State may structure its record-based error review through represented advocacy. That conclusion does not rest on a prediction that Martinez would perform poorly, an empirical claim that every lawyer outperforms every litigant, or a premise that conviction extinguishes all personal constitutional decisions.

California may therefore deny this request to displace appointed counsel. Martinez's asserted right is resolved without relying on an unestablished claim that counsel already filed a brief or that supplemental submissions were actually available to him. The California Supreme Court's denial of mandate is affirmed, appointed counsel remains in place, and the direct appeal proceeds under lawful state procedures. No compulsory supplemental-brief process, new waiver colloquy or substitute appointment is ordered. Whether another defect in representation or access exists is outside the question decided.

**Precedent treatment:** Douglas and Evitts are applied within their fair-access and effective-assistance scope, without treating the assistance guarantee as a compulsory personal-advocacy option. Price supports the appellate distinction; Faretta's trial rule is unchanged.

## Precedent Treatment

- Faretta v. California: preserves the trial self-representation right and distinguishes its Sixth Amendment setting from a first appeal; no reconsideration of the trial holding is undertaken.
- Price v. Johnston: applies the distinction between permitted appellate participation and a constitutional entitlement to conduct appellate advocacy personally.
- Douglas v. California: applies its protection of meaningful, equal access to counsel on a first appeal as of right; no converse right to reject represented review follows.
- Evitts v. Lucey: preserves effective appellate assistance as part of meaningful review; independent deficient-representation claims remain available on their own grounds.
- Godinez v. Moran (June 24, 1993): preserves the distinction between capacity and a knowing, intelligent and voluntary actual waiver; it does not itself establish a self-representation choice in every proceeding.
- New York v. Hill (January 11, 2000): preserves the confined rule for an authorized, unqualified scheduling agreement and its protection of basic personal choices; it does not decide appellate self-representation or authorize counsel to override every personal decision.

Faretta's trial right, meaningful appellate access and effective assistance, and independent competence and valid-waiver requirements retain their scope. The State's permission to accept personal submissions remains distinct from a federal duty. The two controlling propositions have eight direct joins; Scalia's distinct appellate-forum rationale is noncontrolling and needs no aggregation with the Court's rationale.

**Justice Kennedy, concurring; no other Justice joins.** He joins the entire Court opinion and affirms Faretta's soundness. ### Congress may regulate this information-distribution market

**Holding and operative rule:** Congress may regulate disclosure and redistribution of the DPPA's defined personal motor-vehicle information because the enacted distribution class participates in the supported interstate information market. ### The majority is calculated from votes cast for gubernatorial slates

**Holding and operative rule:** Under 48 U.S.C. §1422, a gubernatorial slate obtains the required majority when it receives more than half the votes cast for Governor and Lieutenant Governor jointly; general-election ballots expressing no choice in that contest are excluded from this denominator. If no slate receives a majority of those votes, the statute requires a runoff on the fourteenth day between the slates receiving the highest and second-highest vote totals; the ruling does not decide invalid candidate votes, fraud or materially different statutory language.

**Authority:** Justice Souter's opinion of the Court, joined in full by all eight other participating Justices, supplies a direct unanimous construction of §1422 and its application to the supplied election.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that the paragraph counts votes in the gubernatorial contest. Section 1422 provides for one joint vote for Governor and Lieutenant Governor and links the majority calculation to the votes candidates receive. A ballot used to vote in another contest but making no gubernatorial choice is not that joint vote. The reference to people qualified to vote for members of the Legislature identifies who may vote; the reference to the general election identifies when the gubernatorial election occurs. Neither changes the object being counted.

The competing reading would require a majority of everyone participating in the simultaneous general election, on the theory that a runoff secures a broader mandate. But the statutory mechanism speaks of cast votes received by candidates, not participation in unrelated races. An abstention from this contest cannot become a vote against each slate without a textual direction to count it that way. The candidate-vote construction leaves the runoff clause fully operative whenever the gubernatorial votes themselves produce no majority.

The established slate received a majority of the votes cast for gubernatorial candidates. The all-ballot denominator therefore cannot sustain the Ninth Circuit's runoff order. The Court reverses that order and remands under the correct denominator; the decision supplies no new numerical returns, fraud finding or rule for invalid candidate votes.

## Holdings

### I. Equivalent safeguards may replace the precise Anders format

**Holding and operative rule:** A State may use a procedure different from the precise filing, withdrawal and labeling arrangements described in Anders if it preserves meaningful assistance by an appellate advocate and supplies an equivalent means of determining whether the first appeal as of right is wholly frivolous. The Constitution protects the function, not a mandatory title or verbatim filing form.

**Authority:** Part I of Justice Stevens's opinion is joined by all nine Justices. Chief Justice Stone-Zsela and Justices Souter, Ginsburg and Breyer also join Parts II–IV; Justices O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy and Thomas join Part I only.

**Controlling explanation:** Douglas protects counsel on the first appeal a State supplies, and Evitts requires effective assistance in that appeal. Anders applies those protections when counsel believes that further advocacy would be frivolous. McCoy explains the reviewing court's two tasks: determining that counsel diligently searched for possible grounds and that the conclusion of frivolousness is sound. Those functions permit genuinely equivalent safeguards without requiring every State to reproduce each procedural detail. Neither counsel nor the court must advance a frivolous claim. The agreement on permissible alternatives does not settle whether California's particular procedure is equivalent.

### II. Wende omits necessary counsel-assisted legal screening

**Holding and operative rule:** Before a first appeal as of right is disposed of as frivolous, appointed counsel must supply meaningful legal assistance concerning issues that might arguably support the appeal, or participate in a genuinely equivalent safeguard allowing the court to test the conclusion of frivolousness. Wende is inadequate because it permits counsel to identify no such issue or legal ground and shifts that examination to the court and indigent appellant; a cited factual narrative, continued appointment, independent judicial review and a personal submission opportunity do not supply the missing advocate's function.

**Authority:** Part II of the Court's opinion, joined by Stone-Zsela, Stevens, Souter, Ginsburg and Breyer, controls with five direct votes.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that an advocate must assist the legal assessment that determines whether further represented appellate consideration is withheld. Anders rejects counsel's unsupported conclusion followed by judicial review as an adequate substitute. McCoy makes that duty concrete: the court must be able to evaluate both the diligence of counsel's search and the legal soundness of the frivolousness conclusion. Penson preserves those screening functions independently of its additional circumstance that the court had found arguable claims.

Counsel here performed real work, reviewed the record, supplied a cited narrative, remained appointed and available, and consulted an appellate project. Wende also permits Robbins to submit his own points, requires judicial examination of the record and calls for further briefing if the court discovers a nonfrivolous issue. The defect is therefore not literal withdrawal or the absence of every form of assistance. It is that the tribunal receives no required legal guidance from counsel about potentially supportive grounds before it makes the decisive screening determination. Further advocacy depends on the court first identifying an issue without that assistance. Actual project consultation does not establish that the tribunal received an equivalent legal assessment.

The State's safeguards have value, but neither nominal retention nor judicial issue-spotting supplies the missing adversarial contribution. No particular Anders form, merits argument for a frivolous claim or finding that Robbins will prevail is required. The lower courts identified possible library-access and counsel-waiver-withdrawal issues; this Court makes no finding that either is legally available, nonfrivolous or likely to succeed. The State's library-preservation argument remains unresolved and distinct from the other possible ground.

### III. The defined protection and restoration of its process apply as old law

**Holding and operative rule:** Teague does not bar relief restoring the counsel-assisted appellate screening required here because Anders, McCoy and Penson already dictated that specific protection and its restoration before Robbins's conviction could have become final. This application creates neither a new procedural template nor a new exception to nonretroactivity.

**Authority:** Part III of the Court's opinion is joined by Stone-Zsela, Stevens, Souter, Ginsburg and Breyer. Five Justices reject the asserted bar on its terms, assuming it properly entertainable; no presentation waiver is found.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that the relevant old rule is the advocate's meaningful legal assistance in determining whether an indigent first appellant may be denied further represented consideration. It is not merely the general right to a lawyer. Anders required adequate assistance in that determination and restored appellate consideration without requiring this Court first to find an available nonfrivolous issue. McCoy explained the necessary legal-screening functions; Penson reinforced them before addressing its separate finding of arguable claims. All three preceded Robbins's 1990 conviction and thus any possible later finality date.

The State argues that Wende's retained counsel and substantial review distinguish those cases and that restoration without an established nonfrivolous ground extends their remedies. Those distinctions inform the merits disagreement but do not change the function the prior cases required. Counsel's legal contribution is necessary to test the very conclusion on which further advocacy depends. Requiring Robbins to establish the issue through the deficient process before obtaining that process would withhold the earlier protection.

Teague and Caspari require comparison of the particular protection and remedy with earlier law; disagreement alone does not establish novelty, and genuine new extensions remain subject to the bar. Schiro and Caspari keep presentation history distinct from novelty. The Court assumes proper consideration of the defense and rejects it without inventing its presentation history. No watershed or substantive exception is invoked, and later appellate-assistance cases supply no missing prefinality entitlement.

### IV. The conditional appellate-process relief and remaining-claims remand stand

**Holding and operative rule:** The omission of the required advocate-assisted screening function warrants restoration of constitutionally adequate appellate consideration without requiring Robbins first to prove that a particular omitted issue probably would have changed the appellate outcome. The Ninth Circuit's judgment affirming the conditional writ and remanding the remaining exhausted trial claims is affirmed; adequate screening may still find all available appellate issues frivolous.

**Authority:** Part IV of the Court's opinion is joined by Stone-Zsela, Stevens, Souter, Ginsburg and Breyer and supplies the five-Justice remedy.

**Controlling explanation:** Anders supplies the restoration of adequate appellate consideration. McCoy and Penson explain the necessary function that is missing, while Strickland and Cronic distinguish that deprivation from defective performance within an otherwise supplied function. The remedy restores counsel's role at the screening stage; it is not an award of a successful appeal or a declaration that an identified issue must receive merits briefing regardless of lawful preservation rules.

The State's strongest remedial objection is that counsel remained appointed and performed substantial work, making any defect an individual performance-and-prejudice question. But the Court's holding concerns the absence of the specific legal-assistance function necessary to the screening decision. Robbins need not prove the outcome of the process that must now be supplied. This conclusion does not transform every technical departure from Anders into a deprivation requiring this remedy.

The State's unresolved library objection receives its proper treatment if that issue is relied upon. The separate counsel-waiver-withdrawal issue and the eleven remaining exhausted trial claims retain their own predicates. Relief on those claims may make a further appellate process unnecessary. No immediate release, new deadline, mandatory evidentiary hearing or merits finding on those claims is ordered.

## Precedent Treatment

- Douglas v. California and Evitts v. Lucey — applied to meaningful assistance on a first appeal as of right; neither requires advocacy for a frivolous claim.
- Anders v. California — its advocacy and adequate-screening protections, and restoration of the missing process, are applied; its precise procedural format is not exclusive.
- McCoy v. Court of Appeals of Wisconsin — applied to counsel's assistance in testing diligent search and legal frivolousness; a screening submission remains distinct from a merits brief.

- Schiro v. Farley — presentation of a nonjurisdictional nonretroactivity defense remains distinct from its merits; no late assertion or waiver is found here.

Lawful preservation rules, the merits of the eleven remaining trial claims, and any materially distinct nonretroactivity question retain their independent force.

### Corrective use followed by an impartial jury causes no Sixth Amendment deprivation

**Holding and operative rule:** When a defendant preserves a cause objection, uses an allotted peremptory challenge to remove the juror and is tried by an impartial jury, that corrective use does not itself deny the Sixth Amendment impartial-jury guarantee. The erroneous cause ruling remains an error; this holding does not decide a case in which a biased juror sits or an independent discriminatory-selection injury occurs.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that Gilbert's removal and the impartiality of the jury that sat defeat the claimed Sixth Amendment deprivation. Ross distinguishes the constitutional entitlement to an impartial tribunal from the statutory opportunities for peremptory exclusion. Its distinction applies here even though its Oklahoma-law compulsory-use condition does not govern this federal case. The Court accepts the uncontested determination that Gilbert should have been removed for cause; exercising the peremptory does not make that ruling correct.

The defense's strategic loss is real: the strike might otherwise have been used against a different prospective juror. But a possible change in the jury's composition is not itself the constitutional injury asserted when the challenged juror did not sit and the seated jury's impartiality is not contested. Gray's capital exclusion of a qualified juror is a different setting. The distinct federal-rule entitlement requires its own construction below; impartiality is not treated as satisfying every independent selection right. Batson, McCollum and J.E.B. retain their protections against discrimination, which this case does not present.

**Precedent treatment:** Ross's impartial-jury distinction is applied while its state-law curative condition remains confined to that law. Gray is distinguished at its capital-exclusion scope. The independent discrimination protections of Batson, McCollum and J.E.B. remain intact.

### The defendants received the Rule 24 entitlement they exercised

**Holding and operative rule:** On this record, Rule 24(b)'s ten joint ordinary peremptory challenges were fully afforded and exercised; using one to remove a juror erroneously retained over a cause objection does not deny that allotment or create a Fifth Amendment deprivation merely because the remaining strikes are exhausted. Rule 24 itself imposes no general duty of corrective use and promises no automatic replenishment; its permission for additional challenges in a multiple-defendant case is not a command, and the separate alternate challenge is not interchangeable with an ordinary one.

**Controlling explanation:** Rule 24 grants the opportunities to exclude prospective jurors, not ten further exclusions after every cause ruling has been correctly resolved. The defendants received and exercised all ten ordinary strikes. Removing Gilbert used one of those opportunities for its intended purpose. Practical pressure following the adverse cause ruling does not establish that the court denied the exercise of a strike. Because the claimed rule-based entitlement was received, the Fifth Amendment deprivation theory built on its loss fails as well.

The strongest contrary construction treats all for-cause removals as logically prior to the full peremptory allotment. That concern recognizes the value of an exclusion without legal cause, but neither the rule nor this record establishes the additional entitlement asserted. Defendants asked for no further strike, identified no other juror they wished to remove and challenged no seated juror's impartiality. Those facts limit this result; they do not create an exclusive preservation checklist or erase the prior cause objections.

Ross expressly reserved the impairment question beyond Oklahoma's particular rule, so the Court answers the federal-rule question independently. Swain's language about reversal for impairment cannot establish the antecedent impairment itself. Finding none, the Court does not choose automatic reversal or harmless-error review for an actual deprivation. The Government's proposed compulsory-use rule is rejected, but that rejection supplies no assurance that a defendant who leaves a challenged juror seated may later appeal without regard to ordinary waiver or preservation rules. That different situation remains open.

**Express reservations:** The Court does not decide whether reversible error occurs when a defendant uses a strike to cure a cause error, shows that the full complement otherwise would be used for noncurative exclusions, and requests but is refused a makeup challenge beyond the otherwise applicable maximum. This record contains no such showing or request. Deliberate judicial manipulation, denial of the prescribed allotment, a biased seated juror and independent discrimination likewise remain distinct. No right to replenishment, categorical no-waiver rule or general harmlessness rule follows.

**Precedent treatment:** Ross's express reservation requires an independent Rule 24 construction; its state-law exhaustion condition is not imported. Swain's actual-impairment language is left unresolved as a remedy question because no impairment is established. Hill's bounded scheduling rule supplies no waiver determination here.

Avoiding splits does not explain the refusal to consider any effective minority option or the timing of the actual choice. The legitimate concerns do not dispel the demonstrated purpose or establish independently sufficient reasons for these identical lines.

The Court accounts for the polarization data's limits and other-office successes' Air Force base and effective-electorate qualifications. Musgrove denied the attributed statement; that disputed account is not indispensable to the conclusion and is not treated as an admission. If the lower opinions contain a finding of no broader discriminatory purpose, it is clearly erroneous on this aggregate record; if they failed to perform the mandated inquiry, the developed proof and the Board's burden still require no further opportunity to establish the same entitlement. The first remand neither predetermined purpose nor guaranteed repeated remands. Both appeals therefore receive reversal with directions to deny clearance, without a replacement map, added district or election order.

## Event

U.S. West, Inc. v. Federal Communications Commission. On writ of certiorari to the United States Court of Appeals for the Tenth Circuit; certiorari granted November 1, 1999; submitted January 10, 2000; decided February 7, 2000. The Tenth Circuit's August 18, 1999 judgment, No. 98-9518, 182 F.3d 1224, reviewed FCC 98-27 under 28 U.S.C. §2342(1) and 47 U.S.C. §402(a). It vacated the CPNI order on First Amendment and constitutional-doubt grounds, without deciding the takings and other arbitrary-and-capricious objections. The questions are whether the challenged affirmative-approval method for marketing unsubscribed service categories satisfies commercial-speech scrutiny and is permitted by the general approval language of 47 U.S.C. §222(c)(1).

## Participation

Chief Justice Stone-Zsela and Justices Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer participate at submission and decision.

## Public Action

The Court rejects the First Amendment and general approval-construction grounds for vacatur and returns the unresolved claims for review.

## Judgment & Remedy

**Reversed in the identified respects and remanded, 7–2.**

**Supporting:** Stone-Zsela, O'Connor, Scalia, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer.

**Opposing:** Stevens and Kennedy, who would retain constitutional invalidity confined to the challenged approval application, with relief conforming to the intervening instruments.

The Tenth Circuit's judgment is reversed insofar as it vacated the challenged approval mechanism on the First Amendment and general approval-construction grounds decided here. ## Holdings

### Part I. The marketing condition receives independent commercial-speech review

**Holding and operative rule:** A rule conditioning use of information on its intended use for truthful, lawful commercial solicitation receives commercial-speech review even when the carrier lawfully possesses the information and other marketing remains available. Protecting confidential calling records against unknowing use outside the customer's chosen service relationship is a substantial interest, but the government must independently establish material advancement and reasonable fit.

**Authority:** Justice Ginsburg's Part I is joined by all eight other Justices. Stevens and Kennedy join this part while rejecting the constitutional application and judgment.

**Controlling explanation:** Lanphere and United Reporting require review of this marketing-selected condition. Targeted communication remains protected; permission to address an indiscriminate audience does not remove its burden. Neither corporate form, common-carrier status, custody of information nor the unchallenged statutory baseline turns the chosen approval method into unprotected conduct. Constitutional validity is the Court's independent responsibility.

The asserted confidentiality interest concerns information about whom a customer calls, when and for how long, acquired through providing telephone service. Its protection does not depend on finding a new constitutional ownership right in every record or confining harm to embarrassment. Went For It recognized substantial privacy interests while invalidating the particular public-record mail restraint; it reserved confidential-record misuse. The substantial interest therefore warrants the next inquiries, not automatic validation. Paul P.'s limited residential-information right and Condon's commerce and structural holdings do not decide this commercial-speech question. The Court does not rely on competition as an independently sufficient interest.

**Precedent treatment:** Lanphere and United Reporting supply the speech-selected-use trigger. Went For It preserves both substantial privacy and the need for supported application. Paul P. and Condon remain confined to their different questions.

### Part II. Affirmative choice materially advances the identified confidentiality interest

**Holding and operative rule:** On this record, requiring approval before using confidential service-derived CPNI to market an unsubscribed service category directly and materially protects against unknowing repurposing outside the existing relationship. The service-related and distinct research permissions do not defeat that application; this holding neither treats all internal handling as harmful nor validates every collateral CPNI restriction.

**Authority:** Part II is joined by Stone-Zsela, O'Connor, Scalia, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer. Stevens and Kennedy disagree with its application.

**Controlling explanation:** The approval mechanism addresses the proposed new use itself. Confidential calling records come to the carrier through services the customer selected. Requiring an affirmative choice before extending their marketing use to another category prevents an unread notice from being treated as informed permission. Edenfield permits supported prediction without requiring an empirical study in every setting; the service source, actual proposed use and available customer choice provide the concrete connection here.

The Commission's statement that sharing within an integrated firm raises no significant privacy concern is substantial counterevidence. It does not answer the different question of purpose. Possession by the same firm does not make every later marketing use part of the relationship already chosen. The within-category permission and sharing among affiliates already supplying subscribed categories approximate that existing relationship. Inside wiring and adjunct services relate to it; mobile-service health research serves a distinct nonmarketing purpose. The statutory operational, protective and limited customer-initiated-call permissions likewise do not authorize unrestricted new-category marketing.

Discovery Network, Rubin and Greater New Orleans require examination of comparable permissions, not automatic invalidity whenever exceptions exist. These permissions do not reproduce the unconsented new-category use at issue. Unlike the public records in Lanphere and Went For It, the underlying information here is subject to a service-derived confidentiality duty. The Court finds no actual outside disclosure or individual injury and recognizes no universal data-ownership right. Protecting confidentiality with informed expansion, rather than keeping lawful buyers ignorant to influence purchases, is indispensable to the holding.

**Precedent treatment:** Edenfield's real-harm and material-advancement burden is applied without a mandatory-study rule. Discovery Network, Rubin and Greater New Orleans require the actual permission comparison; Lanphere and Went For It are distinguished at their public-information setting without weakening them.

### Part III. The flexible approval mechanism reasonably fits this use

**Holding and operative rule:** Oral, written or electronic affirmative approval, lasting until revoked or limited and preceded by intelligible notice, reasonably and proportionately fits the confidentiality interest in this challenged new-category use. The government need not select opt-out merely because it burdens the carrier less, but neither opt-out's general constitutional invalidity nor a least-restrictive-means requirement follows.

**Authority:** The same seven Justices join Part III. Stevens and Kennedy independently reject its fit conclusion even assuming material advancement.

**Controlling explanation:** Fox requires reasonable proportional fit, not the least restrictive conceivable method. The FCC considered written-only consent, flexible express consent and notice with opt-out. It rejected written-only consent as unduly restrictive and chose the flexible route because unread notices can leave silence uninformed. Approval continues until revoked or limited; the carrier can seek it through several methods and can market through other information. Those alternatives do not erase the burden of losing effective targeting, but they make the consent requirement proportionate to the confidentiality choice.

The study supplies mixed evidence. In outbound calls, 33 percent expressly refused, 28 percent approved, and 39 percent hung up or requested no further calls; the last group is not an established informed privacy refusal. Seventy-two percent approved when approached after initiating contact, showing a usable avenue without establishing others' consent. Residential direct-mail response was 6–11 percent and business response 5–9 percent; response is not refusal. The informational burden and solicitation expense are real. These results neither prove that every nonrespondent opposes use nor establish indifference or informed permission.

The strongest objection is that clearer notice with opt-out could preserve willing recipients' access to tailored offers. The FCC addressed that choice, and its explanation concerning unknowing repurposing, considered with the service boundary and flexible methods, meets the government's burden here. Mere discussion of an alternative would not suffice; this actual mechanism and justification do. No perfect consent system, universal study, public-information restriction or permanent suppression of truthful product knowledge is approved.

**Precedent treatment:** Fox supplies proportional fit without least restrictive means. Edenfield, Lanphere and Went For It retain the burden of a supported application; Greater New Orleans does not transform every less burdensome alternative into a constitutional command.

### Part IV. The general approval text permits this construction, with residual claims preserved

**Holding and operative rule:** Section 222(c)(1), read with the confidentiality duty, permits the FCC to require an affirmative customer decision through oral, written or electronic means without unambiguously compelling that exclusive method for every general CPNI use. After independent rejection of the presented speech challenge, that choice receives ordinary Chevron deference; the asserted speech-based constitutional doubt cannot sustain vacatur, while the unreached takings and other arbitrary-and-capricious claims remain for review.

**Authority:** The same seven join Part IV and the reversal/remand. Stevens and Kennedy agree that the general text leaves room for affirmative approval but withhold deference to this application because of their constitutional conclusions; that textual agreement is not a join in Part IV or reversal.

**Controlling explanation:** Congress required customer approval for the covered uses but left the general approval method unspecified. Within the confidentiality duty, an affirmative indication is a reasonable way to establish a knowing choice. The text need not command that method exclusively for Chevron to permit it. AT&T v. Iowa Utilities Board requires an agency to remain within actual delegation and enacted limits; it supplies no blanket approval of FCC choices.

Statutory deference cannot decide First Amendment validity. The Court has independently applied the governing speech standard, including the government's burden, the adverse evidence and alternatives. That analysis defeats the speech-based serious-doubt ground invoked below. It does not declare every constitutional objection resolved: the lower majority did not reach takings, and its dissent's contrary view does not decide that claim. The remaining arbitrary-and-capricious objections likewise receive their own review.

This construction concerns the general text. The later express wireless-location and automatic-crash-notification commands and their distinct exceptions retain their enacted operation; their constitutionality and the emergency-information cross-reference are not adjudicated. The FCC's reconsideration also has distinct removals, effective-date conditions and enforcement timing. Reversal therefore removes the grounds decided here and returns the residual claims and proper implementation; it does not restore every former regulation or decide compliance, sanctions, ownership or compensation.

**Precedent treatment:** Chevron is applied to the general approval gap after independent constitutional review. AT&T preserves statutory limits and separate review of unresolved objections. No avoidance principle permits assumed speech invalidity to replace the analysis required by the governing cases.

## Law After Decision

The challenged new-category approval method satisfies independent commercial-speech review on this record and is a permissible reading of the general approval text. No conduct-only exemption, universal opt-out prohibition, exclusive statutory opt-in rule for every general CPNI use, constitutional data-ownership right or general reduction in corporate speech protection follows. Takings and other administrative-law claims remain unresolved.

### Scope of the approval determination

The decision concerns confidential, individually identifiable customer proprietary network information obtained through the telecommunications service relationship, including calling patterns and billing information. Subscriber-list information is excluded, and aggregate customer information has separate treatment. It does not establish a rule for all data or all targeted marketing.

Under the general statutory text, use required by law or approved by the customer is distinct from use for the service from which the information derives or services necessary to or used in providing it, including directories. Section 222(d)'s original permissions separately cover initiating, rendering, billing and collecting for telecommunications services; protecting carrier rights or property and users or other carriers against fraudulent, abusive or unlawful service use or subscription; and inbound telemarketing, referral or administrative services during a customer-initiated call when the customer approves use of the information to provide that service. The last permission does not extend beyond that call or authorize indefinite later marketing.

The challenged total-service rule permits use to provide or market local, interexchange and commercial-mobile services within categories already subscribed to from the carrier. Sharing among affiliates supplying those subscribed categories is permitted when the customer subscribes to more than one category; the corresponding permission is absent when the customer does not. Approval is required for marketing an unsubscribed category, with distinct permissions for inside-wiring installation, maintenance and repair; mobile-service health-effects research; and former adjunct-to-basic services. Illustrative adjunct services include speed dialing, computer-provided directory assistance, monitoring, tracing, blocking, return, repeat dialing, tracking, waiting, caller identification, forwarding and certain centrex features. This is not an exhaustive list.

Approval can be written, oral or electronic; the carrier bears the burden of demonstrating compliant oral approval. It continues until revoked or limited; notification and approval records must be kept at least one year. Prior one-time notice identifies the information, specific recipients, purposes, customer rights and carrier duty, precise granting or denying steps, rights to disapprove or withdraw, and the continuing effect of approval or denial until affirmative revocation or limitation. It must state that refusal does not affect subscribed services. Notice must be comprehensible and nonmisleading; written notice must be legible, sufficiently large and readily apparent, and translation of part requires translation of all. Notice may describe tailored-offering benefits and possible compelled disclosure on the customer's affirmative written request; it may not encourage freezing third-party access. Solicitation must be proximate to notice. These conditions are distinct from the later removal of the same-document requirement.

The original equipment/information-service restriction, competitor-tracking prohibition and former-customer winback prohibition are separate provisions. No approval exception is inserted into a prohibition that lacks it, and none receives an independent constitutional ruling merely from this approval-method decision.

### Intervening enactment and agency action

Public Law 106-81 section 5 took effect October 26, 1999 without a section 5 delay. Section 222(f) separately requires express prior authorization for wireless call-location information except the specified section 222(d)(4) emergency uses, and for automatic-crash-notification information except use in operating an automatic-crash-notification system. The location exceptions permit provision to a public safety answering point, emergency medical or dispatch provider, public safety, fire-service or law-enforcement official, or hospital emergency or trauma-care facility to respond to the user's emergency-services call; informing the legal guardian or immediate family of the user's location in an emergency risking death or serious physical harm; and provision to information- or database-management providers solely to assist delivery of emergency services in response to an emergency. Those exceptions do not permit ordinary marketing; the crash-notification exception remains separate.

The amendment adds location to CPNI, moves definitions to subsection (h), and imposes a separate emergency-service information duty. The Court neither construes subsection (g)'s enacted cross-reference nor adjudicates those later provisions' constitutionality. They do not retroactively establish the FCC's 1998 explanation or permit a general assertion that opt-out is available for every CPNI category.

FCC 99-223, adopted August 16, released September 3 and published October 1, 1999, retains affirmative approval in paragraph 95. Paragraphs 185 and 190 condition amended information-collection rules on OMB approval and a later effective-date publication; no such publication is established in this record. Paragraphs 187 and 188 separately remove the winback and same-document notice restrictions. Paragraph 189 postpones enforcement of amended section 64.2009(a) and (c) safeguards until eight months after release. Equipment/information-service and safeguard amendments are not all treated as effective on publication, and reversal does not resurrect removed provisions. No compliance, default, sanction exposure or expired transition is determined.

## Separate Writings

**Chief Justice Stone-Zsela, concurring; no other Justice joins.**

Stone-Zsela joins the Court's entire opinion and separately explains the carrier's capacity to claim constitutional protection. Capacity follows the particular right: commercial-speech protection serves listeners as well as speakers, so the corporation may invoke it; legal personhood alone does not settle the scope of that right. Its charter supplies its existence, and authorization to provide telephone service supplies its service position and access to calling records. That access carries lawful confidentiality terms under section 222, subject to the independent commercial-speech scrutiny the Court applies.

Telephone service is not consent to every later profitable use of its records. But neither charter nor licensing permits government to demand surrender of independent constitutional protections. A penalty for viewpoint or editorial choice would receive the scrutiny governing that discrimination; this rule selects neither. The carrier's wealth or size reduces no protection, natural persons acting through it retain their own speech, and customers retain their interests in their records. Marketing and requests for consent remain available.

Lanphere's marketing trigger and Central Hudson/Fox, rather than a new entity-capacity test, govern the rule's validity. This additional explanation supplies no general charter-condition power, categorical inferiority of corporate speech or new constitutional ownership of all CPNI. No other Justice joins the concurrence.

**Justice Stevens, concurring in part and dissenting in part, joined by Justice Kennedy.**

Stevens and Kennedy join Part I's independent speech review and substantial confidentiality interest. They would retain invalidity of the challenged approval application on two independently sufficient grounds, with relief confined to that application and reconciled with the intervening instruments.

First, the agency has not adequately justified the selected distinction under Discovery Network, Edenfield, Lanphere and their United Reporting position. Confidential calling records materially differ from public records, but that difference does not establish every internal marketing use as a new harm. The Commission's own integrated-firm assessment and broad permission for existing-category marketing weaken its explanation for treating the selected new-category use differently. Sensitivity alone cannot supply advancement, and the dissent demands no empirical study in every case or regulation of all information sources.

Second, even assuming advancement, fit independently fails. The FCC considered opt-out and permits oral or electronic approval; neither fact is disputed. Its concern about unread notices does not adequately show why sufficiently clear notice with opt-out would fail to protect the actual internal-use interest. Low affirmative response is not widespread informed opposition; willing recipients may lose effective tailored communication. Fox does not impose least restrictive means, but still requires a supported comparison proportionate to the burden. The additional marketing channels do not eliminate that burden.

The general statutory text can permit affirmative approval, but statutory permission and Chevron deference cannot cure this unconstitutional application. The dissent would not invalidate section 222, require opt-out universally, adjudicate the later express wireless commands, adopt a takings conclusion or treat unresolved administrative claims as rejected. It does not defend blanket vacatur of every collateral provision.
