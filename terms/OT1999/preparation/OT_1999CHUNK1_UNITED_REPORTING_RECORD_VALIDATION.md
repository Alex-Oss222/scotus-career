# United Reporting — neutral original-record validation

**Matter:** Los Angeles Police Department v. United Reporting Publishing Corp., No. 98-678.

**Purpose and boundary:** Validate the pleaded and maintained claims, the relief below, and United Reporting's actual business and proposed uses. This is a factual source refresh before assembly, not an adjudication or a revision of a frozen handoff. No current-case Supreme Court opinion, comparator, Stone material, model commitment, reconciled draft, or Canonical Decision Record was consulted. The reviewed materials are original filings predating adjudication, including the August 23, 1999 merits reply. No conclusion about the Supreme Court's disposition, lineup, or rationale is supplied.

## Pleadings and respondent's maintained own-injury claims

**Both facial and as-applied allegations are affirmatively confirmed by the adverse party's brief.** Petitioners' merits brief 6 n.2 expressly acknowledges that United Reporting alleged unconstitutionality both facially and as applied, citing Court of Appeals Record 6, paragraph 26. The original complaint itself is not reproduced in the materials reviewed, so this is an express petitioner recitation of its adversary's pleading, not an independent inspection of that pleading. It contradicts a factual premise that respondent pleaded only a challenge to other persons' applications.

The district court's reproduced opinion identifies a declaratory and injunctive action under 42 U.S.C. §1983, with a First Amendment first cause and Fourteenth Amendment second, third, and fourth causes. Pet. App. 11a. Its opinion describes the case as a facial challenge and, after granting the First Amendment claim, does not reach the other causes. Id. 10a, 22a–23a. The reviewed record does not permit assigning each numbered Fourteenth Amendment cause an individual label from the original pleading. The appellate opinion identifies equal protection, due process, and overbreadth arguments as unnecessary to reach. Id. 36a n.6.

**Respondent continued to assert injury to its own activities in its merits presentation.** Respondent's brief 14–15 expressly defends facial and as-applied claims as well as claims concerning subscribers and arrestees. At page 15 it identifies its publication of the Register and provision of addresses to subscribers, says its owners and employees were denied addresses because they could not make the required certification concerning journalistic purpose and direct or indirect sales, and separately states that United Reporting itself desires to use arrestee addresses to sell its own products and services directly. These are respondent's affirmative factual and legal positions, not judicial findings that every use qualifies for access or that standing is established.

Respondent also maintains that the Register and its information service involve noncommercial or mixed speech. Resp. Br. 30–34. It presses an equal-protection argument concerning its own publication and explains its concern about signing the certification under possible criminal exposure, citing ER 192 and SER 441–442. Id. 45–46 & n.31. Vagueness and the state constitutional theory also remain in its presentation. Id. 47–48. Accordingly, neither a description of the judgment as facial nor the presence of broader arguments establishes that respondent abandoned its own-injury position.

**The narrower Register theory was disputed, not adjudicated as abandoned.** Petitioners' merits brief 8 n.4 says the district court did not reach the alternative First Amendment theory concerning publication of the Register because it granted broader relief. The reply argues that the narrower theory presents obstacles to resolution, including the absence of a cross-petition and the possibility of access through the statutory journalism exception. Reply Br. 16–18. Its page 17 n.17 cites the complaint's prayer for a declaration that the Register falls within the amendment's provisions (Court of Appeals Excerpts of Record 334, paragraph 5) and a declaration concerning informative, journalistic, and scholarly purpose (id. 699, paragraph 4). Those are petitioners' recitations and preservation arguments. They are not a lower-court ruling that respondent waived or relinquished an as-applied claim.

Respondent reports an initial May 15, 1996 complaint, followed by July 1 denials when agencies required certifications its employees could not sign, and an amended complaint against those agencies. Resp. Br. 4–5, citing ER 1–14, 258, 370–374, 378–387, and 486–523. These citations support its account of actual denials; the underlying declarations and amended pleading are not reproduced here. The record therefore distinguishes an asserted actual denial for inability to certify from an agency's adjudication of a completed request confined to qualifying journalistic use. The latter cannot be assumed from the former.

## Judgment and relief below

The district court's November 27, 1996 opinion appears in full at Pet. App. 9a–23a. It grants United Reporting summary judgment and denies defendants' motion after holding the address restriction unconstitutional under its commercial-speech analysis. Id. 22a–23a. Its operative direction is to lodge a proposed declaratory and injunctive judgment within twenty days, with ten days for objections and subsequent revision and signature. Id. 23a. **That direction is not the separately signed permanent injunction.**

The petition at 4, petitioners' merits brief at 6, and the relevant docket entry at JA 1 characterize the ensuing relief as permanently enjoining enforcement of §6254(f)(3). The briefs also state that the California Attorney General remained subject to the injunction although not an appellant. Pet. 2; Pet. Br. 1 n.1. These descriptions establish the reported nature of the relief. The exact final decretal wording, including its precise coverage of applicants and uses, is not reproduced in the four complete core documents or the merits reply. No universal or plaintiff-only scope beyond those descriptions should be invented.

The Ninth Circuit's June 25, 1998 opinion is reproduced in full at Pet. App. 24a–36a. Only the Los Angeles Police Department appealed. Id. 26a n.1. The court treats the sale of the arrest information as commercial speech, considers the privacy rationale, and affirms the district judgment invalidating the restriction as an infringement of United Reporting's First Amendment rights. Id. 26a–29a, 30a–31a, 36a. It does not reach the fourth Central Hudson prong or respondent's equal-protection, due-process, and overbreadth arguments. Id. 36a nn.5–6. The opinions' broad facial framing and their discussion of United Reporting's own rights must both be retained. The breadth of relief affirmed is a separate issue from whether respondent pleaded and maintained its own-injury theory.

## Actual business and original exhibits

The opinions below describe United Reporting as a private publishing service selling recently arrested persons' names and addresses to clients including attorneys, insurance companies, drug and alcohol counselors, religious counselors, and driving schools. Pet. App. 11a, 25a–26a. Petitioners' merits brief 5 cites the complaint, Court of Appeals Record 5, paragraph 19, for its description of the business and subscribers. Respondent describes distributing the Register to subscribers and arrestees and providing legal and political articles along with arrest information. Resp. Br. 4.

The original exhibits substantiate the coexistence of editorial material and the address service:

- **May 9, 1996 Register, JA 2–5:** articles on speed traps and field sobriety testing, an editorial criticizing the pending access legislation, a request for articles useful to the legal community, and an arrest blotter. The blotter supplies names, addresses, other arrest information, and an offer to obtain data through a dial-up subscription. JA 5 also cautions that arrest does not establish guilt.
- **June 1, 1996 Register, JA 6–9:** articles concerning DUI defense and malpractice, crash reconstruction, and blood, breath, and urine testing, followed by another arrest blotter and dial-up offer. The original exhibits therefore do not support treating the Register as an address list devoid of other content. They also do not establish the legal classification of the publication or its entitlement to the statutory journalism exception.

No individual arrestee's identifying information is reproduced in this validation. The exhibits' editorial claims about the benefits of solicitation or the consequences of legislation are the publisher's claims, not judicial findings.

Respondent's descriptions of subscriber mailings and arrestees' reactions cite declarations and exhibits outside this short appendix. Resp. Br. 5–6 (ER 206, 215–231, 240–245, 249–250, 252). The district court separately recites favorable arrestee affidavits. Pet. App. 21a. These recitations do not justify finding the absence of privacy evidence throughout the legislative record. JA 10–12 reproduces an earlier-version Senate floor analysis containing proponents' and opponents' positions; it is not proof of United Reporting's actual use in every statutory application or a substitute for the final statute.

## Bounded conclusion and remaining source limit

The material affirmative refresh is that United Reporting pleaded both facial and as-applied unconstitutionality and expressly maintained its own business-related injury in the merits briefing, including its own proposed direct sales use. The original newspapers confirm substantive editorial content alongside the address business. These facts do not themselves resolve standing, preservation, exhaustion, the proper breadth of constitutional adjudication, or the merits of any particular application.

The original complaint, amended complaint, cited record declarations, and separately signed final injunction have not independently been recovered. They are not reproduced in the complete documents listed above. This is a limit on the recovered source set, not a claim that those documents never existed, that the arguments were forfeited, or that their contents can be reconstructed from the eventual Supreme Court decision. The explicit adverse-party pleading acknowledgment and respondent's affirmative merits presentation are sufficient to reject an unsupported factual assertion that no own-injury/as-applied position was presented; the legal consequence remains for the authorized model context.
