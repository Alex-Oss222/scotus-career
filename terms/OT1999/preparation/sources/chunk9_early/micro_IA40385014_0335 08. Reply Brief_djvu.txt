INTRODUCTION 


The briefs of the parties clearly frame the issue before 
this Court. The political party petitioners assert that the 
First Amendment protects their right to choose nominees 
who best represent each party’s ideology and prefer- 
ences, and that that right is obliterated by Proposition 
198, which allows all voters, “regardless of political affil- 
iation[,]” to cross party lines and to vote in several par- 
ties’ primaries at the same election. See Cal. Elec. Code 
§ 2151.! 


On the other hand, the State sees the partisan pri- 
mary as an election, not a nominating event, and thus 
claims wide latitude to regulate it. In the State’s view, the 
blanket primary is a sort of mini general election 
designed to avoid “discrimat[ion] based on party affilia- 
tion[.]” Resp.Br. 11. The State sees few if any associational 
rights involved. Rather the partisan primary, like the 
election process as a whole, begins to look something like 
a laboratory experiment that can be fine tuned to produce 
officials who “stand closer to the median policy positions 
of their districts[.]” Resp.Br. 40. 


What the State overlooks is that the winner of each 
party primary becomes “the nominee of that party at the 
ensuing general election.” § 15451 (emphasis added). The 
State has no right to commandeer parties during the 
primary election and attempt to steer them toward 
median policy positions. The First Amendment protects 
the right of a political party to choose its own standard

bearer, not somebody else’s standard bearer. Eu v. San 
Francisco Democratic Central Comm., 489 U.S. 214, 224 
(1989); Timmons v. Twin Cities Area New Party, 520 U.S. 
351, 359 (1997). The First Amendment guarantees a 
party’s right to choose a liberal standard bearer or a 
conservative standard bearer or any other kind of stan- 
dard bearer it chooses. This is an area where “a State, or a 
court, may not constitutionally substitute its own judg- 
ment for that of the Party.” Democratic Party of U.S. v. 
Wisconsin ex rel. La Follette, 450 U.S. 107, 123-124 (1981). 


It is for this reason that Proposition 198 is unconstitu- 
tional. There is no doubt that Proposition 198 will flood 
California partisan primaries with voters who have no 
affiliation whatsoever with the party in whose primary 
they are voting. The presence of these voters will inevita- 
bly affect primary election results, the nature of the 
debate within the parties, and ultimately the parties’ 
ideologies. This is the very objective of Proposition 198. 
This the State cannot do.

I. TASHJIAN CONTROLS THE RESULT HERE. 


Respondents read Tashjian v. Republican Party of Con- 
necticut, 479 U.S. 208 (1986) as recognizing the right of a 
political party to prevent “having the party’s electoral 
base restricted contrary to the party’s wishes.” Resp.Br. 
21. From this perspective, the right of political association 
becomes a one-way ratchet that protects a party’s right to 
invite non-members to join in the nomination process, but 
does not protect a party’s right to limit the nomination 
process to its own members. 


Respondents misread Tashjian. The specific result in 
Tashjian was that a closed primary statute was held 
invalid as applied to a political party that sought to open 
its primary election to independents. But that result was 
based on a principle, the principle that “the freedom to 
join together in furtherance of common political beliefs 
‘necessarily presupposes the freedom to identify the peo- 
ple who constitute the association.’ ” Id. at 214 (quoting 
La Follette, supra, 450 U.S. at 122). Tashjian made clear that 
this principle applies full bore when a party performs the 
“basic function” of choosing its nominees. Id. at 216. It is 
this principle that petitioners rely on in the present pro- 
ceeding. Petitioners do not claim “unfettered control over 
who can vote in primary elections[.]” Resp.Br. at 19. 
Rather they assert that the associational right asserted 
here is stronger than that asserted in Tashjian, and the 
burden imposed by Proposition 198 is greater than the 
burden considered in Tashjian. 


Il. PROPOSITION 198 SEVERELY BURDENS PARTY 
ASSOCIATIONAL RIGHTS. 


As petitioners set out in their opening brief, Proposi- 
tion 198 severely burdens the associational rights of polit- 
ical parties by forcing them to accept “nominees” who are 
supported by neither a majority nor a plurality of party 
members. Pet.Br. at 27-33. Proposition 198 is designed to 
make crossover voting effortless and to give non-party 
members an equal role in choosing party nominees. This 
strikes at the very heart of the First Amendment, which 
protects the right of a political party to choose its own 
nominees. Eu, supra, 489 U.S. at 224; Timmons, supra, 520 
U.S. at 359. 


Respondents’ Brief offers several arguments why 
Proposition 198 does not severely burden the parties, and 
therefore does not trigger strict scrutiny. None of these 
arguments has merit. 


First, respondents contend that the harm inflicted by 
the blanket primary will be insubstantial because 90 to 95 
percent of crossover voters are “sincere”? and because 
crossover voting “generally does not change the outcome 
of elections[.]” Resp.Br. at 27-31. Even if this were true 
(which petitioners dispute), it misses the point. The First 
Amendment protects the right of a political party to 
choose its own nominees. The motivation of outsiders 
who wish to join in the nominating process is irrelevant. 
Even “sincere” crossover voters have different prefer- 
ences from party members and therefore will skew the 
results in an unknown number of nominating elections. 
P1.Ex. 8 [Tables 1 and 3 of Addendum to Field Report].

Post-trial events further illustrate the effect of Propo-

were kept of the results of that primary: one tally

Republican voters only, Mr. Bush beat Mr. McCain by 
more than 700,000 votes. Respondents’ Second Lodging at 
13. Among crossover voters, Mr. McCain beat Mr. Bush 
by a margin of 340,000 votes. Approximately 31% of the 
votes cast in the Republican primary were cast by non- 
Republicans. Ibid. While the difference in voting in that 
particular race was not outcome determinative, the enor- 
mous numbers of crossover votes in that race belies any 
argument that petitioners’ concerns over the effects of 
crossover voting are “overdrawn.” Resp.Br. at 27. 


As a second example, recently-completed vote 
recounts in two Southern California races demonstrate 


that crossover votes did, in fact, change the results of two 
Republican Assembly races in the 2000 primary.? See 
Recounts Clinch GOP Suspicions, Los Angeles Times, 
Orange County Edition, April 11, 2000, at B4; Analysis 
proves point for open-primary foes, Orange County Register, 
April 11, 2000, at B7. In both cases, one candidate was 
favored by the majority of voters in his party, but a large 
number of nonparty voters caused another candidate to 
be selected as the nominee of that party. Whether this was 
the result of “sincere” or “strategic” voting, the fact 
remains that the party members of those districts lost the 
ability to select their own nominee.

primary systems allow some crossover voting, and that a 
decision invalidating Proposition 198 would inevitably 
lead to the invalidation of those systems as well. Resp.Br. 
at 31-33. Here the State paints with much too broad a 
brush.

III. PROPOSITION 198 PARTICULARLY BURDENS 
THE MINOR PARTIES, WHO DO NOT SEEK TO 
PURSUE “MEDIAN POLICY POSITIONS.” 


The District Court concluded that the major parties 
“have been characterized by a fluidity and overlap of 
philosophy and membership” and at different times have 
been “composed of various elements reflecting most of 
the American political spectrum.” 986 F.Supp. at 1296; 
Pet.App. 65a-66a; quoting La Follette, supra, 450 U.S. at 
133 (Powell, J., dissenting). Leaving aside the question 
whether this is an accurate characterization of the major 
parties, it is certainly not an accurate characterization of 
the minor parties. The Libertarian Party and the Peace 
and Freedom Party have distinct ideologies at opposite 
ends of the political spectrum. Compare J.A. 76-79 [Liber- 
tarian Party Statement of Principles} with J.A. 82-83 
[Peace and Freedom Party 1996 Platform]. They have no 
desire to be moved back to the middle of the spectrum.

They are at the greatest risk that the “inclusion of persons 
unaffiliated” with them will “seriously distort” their col- 
lective decisions. See. La Follette, supra, 450 U.S. at 122. 


The State suggests that the minor parties use the 
primary ballot only for “expressive purposes.” Resp.Br. 
36. This is nonsense. The minor parties use the primary to 
choose the nominees who will best represent them at the 
ensuing general election. Their only expressive purpose 
is to make sure that the nominees — who will be described 
on the general election ballot by the labels “Libertarian” 
or “Peace and Freedom” - are really chosen by the Liber- 
tarian and Peace and Freedom parties.? The State also 
suggests that the impact of Proposition 198 on the minor 
parties is lessened because few minor party primaries are 
contested.* Resp.Br. 37. But the frequency with which an 
event occurs has no relationship to its importance or to 
the extent of the constitutional protection that attaches to 
it. It is reasonable to assume that minor party contested 
primaries occur when there are serious differences of 
opinion within the party. The selection of a nominee is 
the mechanism by which such disputes are resolved. This 
is precisely the situation in which the freedom of associa- 
tion is most important.

IV. PROPOSITION 198 IS NOT NARROWLY TAI- 
LORED TO ADVANCE A COMPELLING STATE 
INTEREST. 


The State now asserts that Proposition 198 advances 
seven compelling state interests, an increase of two over 
the number claimed in the Court of Appeals. We will 
briefly address the two new interests. 


Affording voters more choice. On a purely quantitative 
level, a blanket primary increases the number of candi- 
dates a voter can choose from in a primary election. But 
in California the partisan primary is a nominating event. 
The State has no compelling interest in allowing non- 
party members to choose party nominees. The State’s 
power to regulate elections and set voter qualifications 
must be exercised without infringing upon “basic consti- 
tutional protections.” Kusper v. Pontikes, 414 U.S. 51, 57 
(1973). 


Any primary election system narrows the range of 
available candidates. A partisan primary does this in a 
particular way — it allows an unlimited number of candi- 
dates to compete for party nominations, but each party 
may only advance one candidate to the general election. 
The electorate is then presented with a range of candi- 
dates who represent the ideological spectrum of the par- 
ties participating in the process. To the extent that 
Proposition 198 produces more centrist candidates, voters 
will be presented with fewer choices from a narrow ideo- 
logical range. J.A. 70-71 [Cain Report]. 


Promoting “fairness.” This is a predictive judgment 
that is not susceptible to proof. It can just as easily be 
argued that Proposition 198 promotes “unfairness”

because it produces “nominees” who are not supported 
by the party they “represent.”
