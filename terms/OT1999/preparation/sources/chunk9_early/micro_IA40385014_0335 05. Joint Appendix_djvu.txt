JURISDICTION 


1. Plaintiffs, including the California Democratic 
Party and certain of its officers and members, originally 
brought this action to protect their rights of political 
association, political expression, free speech, and their 
right to petition the government for the redress of griev- 
ances. These rights are guaranteed by the First and Four- 
teenth Amendments to the United States Constitution, by 
42 U.S.C. section 1983, and by Article One, Section Three, 
and Article Two, Section Five of the California Constitu- 
tion. Plaintiffs in intervention/intervenors include the 
California Republican Party and certain of its officers and 
members who are so situated that disposition of this 
action will as a practical matter impair or impede their 
ability to protect those same rights and interests. Accord- 
ingly, intervenors have the right to intervene on the side 
of plaintiffs under Federal Rule of Civil Procedure 24(a). 
Intervenors’ claims also involve questions of law and fact 
common to those raised by the pending lawsuit. FRCP 
24(b). Jurisdiction of the federal claims is conferred upon 
this Court by 28 U.S.C. sections 1331, 1343(a)3), 2201, and 
2202. Jurisdiction of the state claims is conferred upon 
this Court by 28 U.S.C. section 1367. 


VENUE 


2. Venue for this action properly lies within the 
Eastern District pursuant to 28 U.S.C. sections 1391(b) 
and 1392(a) in that defendant resides in the State of 
California and in the Eastern District, and plaintiffs’ and 
intervenors’ claims arose in the Eastern District. 


PARTIES 


3. Intervenor CALIFORNIA REPUBLICAN PARTY 
(“Republican Party”) is the duly authorized and officially 
recognized Republican Party of the State of California. It 
is an unincorporated association of individuals who have 
joined together to advance common political beliefs. The 
Republican Party nominates candidates for public office 
by primary election. Voting in the Republican Party pri- 
mary elections is restricted by California Republican 
Party Rules to Republicans. 


4. Intervenor MICHAEL SCHROEDER (“Schroe- 
der”) is the duly elected Vice-Chair of the Republican 
party and intervenor SHAWN STEEL (“Steel”) is the duly 
elected Treasurer of the Republican Party. Intervenors 
Schroeder and Steel are registered to vote in California as 
Republicans. Said intervenors do not want to allow non- 


Republicans to vote for and choose Republican nominees 
for public office. 


5. Intervenor DONNA SHALANSKY is a registered 
Republican voter in Sacramento County and regularly 
votes in the primary election. She does not want to allow 
non-Republicans to vote for or choose Republican nomi- 
nees for public office. 


6. Defendant BILL JONES (“Jones”) is the Secretary 
of State of California. He is California’s chief elections 
officer and administers the provisions of California’s 
Elections Code. Among his duties is the enforcement of 
state election laws. He is sued in his official capacity only. 


FACTUAL ALLEGATIONS 


7. In March 1996, the California voters passed Prop- 
osition 198, which is a statutory initiative that adds or 
amends nine sections of the Elections Code. A true and 
correct copy of Proposition 198 is attached as Exhibit A. 


8. Proposition 198 dramatically changes how pri- 
mary elections are conducted in California including who 
may vote in them. It requires that in such electior.s any 
registered voter may vote for a candidate regardless of 
the voter’s political affiliation. Each voter will be given 
the same primary ballot on which all candidates will 
appear. All candidates for a particular office will be listed 
together and identified by party affiliation. Voters will be 
able to vote for a candidate from one party for one 
particular office and, if they wish, for a candidate from 
another party for a different office. There is one excep- 
tion: only party members will be allowed to vote for 
party central committee members. 


9. The California Republican Party prohibits non- 
Republicans from voting in Republican primaries. In this 
regard article, I section 1.00(a) of the California Republi- 
can Party By-Laws states in pertinent part: 


The Committee shall recognize only those parti- 
san nomination processes under California law 
that limit the electorate for partisan nomination 
elections to registered Republican voters, and 
which do not impose upon the Committee, with- 
out its express concurrence, a nomination pro- 
cess open to voters registered in other parties or 
as decline to state. 


(See Exhibit B, By-Laws of the California Republican 
Party, attached hereto and incorporated by reference.) 


10. The Republican National Committee also only 
recognizes Republican candidates nominated through a 
primary election at which only Republicans may vote, or 
if there is a non-partisan or open primary only those 
candidates “endorsed by convention held under the 
authority of the state Republican Party. . . . ” See Exhibit 
C, the Rules of the Republican Party attached hereto and 
incorporated herein by this reference. 


11. Unless this Court takes the actions requested 
herein, the Republican Party and its members will be 
deprived of their right to select their own candidates. 
Proposition 198 allows non-Republicans to cross party 
lines and choose Republican nominees. Accordingly, 
Proposition 198 will allow the nomination of “Republi- 
can” candidates who are not supported by a majority of 
Republican voters. Proposition 198 conflicts with the 
rules and by-laws established by the state and national 
Republican parties to govern their selection of candi- 
dates. 


12. Proposition 198 is invalid and unenforceable 
because it violates intervenors’ rights of political associa- 
tion, political expression, free speech, and their rights to 
petition government for redress of grievances. Defendant 
maintains that Proposition 198 is valid and enforceable 
and that plaintiffs and intervenors are bound by its terms. 
Thus, an actual controversy has arisen and now exists 
between the parties within the scope of 28 U.S.C. section 
2201, in which the preservation of the constitutional 
rights of intervenors requires a declaration of the rights 


and other legal relations of the parties, and specifically a 
declaration that Proposition 198 is unconstitutional. 


13. Intervenors have suffered and will continue to 
suffer irreparable injury as a result of Proposition 198 and 
defendant's conduct. Intervenors have no plain, speedy, 
or adequate remedy at law. Accordingly, intervenors are 
entitled to injunctive relief which is necessary to preserve 
the status quo and their constitutional rights.

15. Proposition 198 violates the First and Fourteenth

SECOND CAUSE OF ACTION

16. Intervenors reallege and incorporate paragraphs 
1-15 above. 


17. Proposition 198 violates Article One, Section 
Three, and Article Two, Section Five of the California 
Constitution because it denies intervenors their rights of 
association, political expression, free speech, and their 
right to petition government for the redress of grievances.

RELIEF 


WHEREFORE, intervenors pray for relief as follows: 


1. That this Court declare that Proposition 198 vio- 
lates the First and Fourteenth Amendments to the United 
States Constitution, and Article One, Section Three, and 
Article Two, Section Five, of the California Constitution. 


2. That defendant and all others acting in concert 
with him be enjoined from enforcing Proposition 198. 


3. That intervenors be awarded their costs, expenses, 
and attorneys’ fees. 


4. That this Court grant such other and further 
relief as the Court may deem just and proper.

I. BACKGROUND: MINOR PARTIES IN CALIFORNIA 


California has a rich history of minor party participation 
in elections. Minor parties appeared on the statewide 
ballot in every partisan statewide California election in 
the last 120 years, except only two elections. Only seven 
other states can say the same.? 


Sixteen minor parties have nominated by primary in Cali- 
fornia. They are described below. The years in paren- 
theses, following the party name, indicate the years the 
party placed nominees on the California general election 
ballot. The direct primary in California started in 1910. At 
the time, a party was qualified if it had polled at least 3% 
of the vote for any statewide candidates in the last gen- 
eral election, or if it submitted a petition signed by 3% of 
the last gubernatorial vote. The 1929 legislature (by a 


1 In the last 120 years, the only states in which a minor 
party has appeared on the statewide ballot, in all years in which 
a regularly-scheduled election for presidential electors, 
governor or U.S. Senator, was held, are Michigan, Minnesota, 
New Jersey, New York, and Wisconsin. In Pennsylvania minor 
parties missed just one such election (1990), and in 
Massachusetts and California they missed two such elections 
(1978 and 1986 in Massachusetts, and 1964 and 1966 in 
California).

unanimous vote in both houses) lowered the petition 
requirement to 1% of the last gubernatorial vote, and 
added an alternate registration membership of 1% of the 
last gubernatorial vote. The 1937 legislature raised the 
petition alternative to 10% of the last gubernatorial vote, 
but kept the registration alternate at 1%. 


1. Socialist, 1900-1938. The Socialist Party elected a few 
state legislators in California during the 1910’s decade, 
and was, of course, the California branch of the Socialist 
Party, organized throughout the United States from 1900 
to the present day. It stood for public ownership of the 
means of production, recognition of collective bargaining 
rights for labor unions, and a public social welfare sys- 
tem. The Socialist Party went off the ballot in California 
after the 1938 election because it failed to poll as much as 
3% of the vote for any statewide office that year. 


2. Prohibition, 1875-1964. The Prohibition Party elected a 
congressman from California in 1914, Charles Randall, 
and re-elected him in 1916 and 1918. The party also 
elected an Assemblyman in California in 1914. In 1932, 
the party’s nominee for U.S. Senate, Rev. Robert P. Shuler, 
polled 25.8% of the vote and carried Orange and River- 
side Counties. The party was the California branch of the 
Prohibition Party, organized throughout the United States 
from 1869 to the present day. The party favored a ban on 
the manufacture and sale of beverage alcohol. The party 
always believed that any law imposing prohibition would 
fail, unless a political party determined to enforce the law 
were in power. The party also felt that the governing 
political party should be composed of dedicated Chris- 
tians, and argued that most Democratic and Republican 


41 


Party politicians are not truly dedicated to Christian prin- 
ciples. The party went off the ballot in January 1964, for 
failing to keep its registration above 1/15th of 1% of the 
statewide registration total. 


3. Independence League, 1906-1910. The Independence 
League was financed by William Randolph Hearst to 
fight for progressive ideas, such as direct election of U.S. 
Senators, nomination by primaries, the initiative, the 
eight-hour workday, prohibition of child labor, workers 
compensation laws, a federal reserve bank, and anti-trust 
legislation. It polled 14.4% of the vote for Governor in 
1906, but it fared poorly afterwards and had been aban- 
doned by 1910. 


4. Progressive, 1914-1918. The Progressive Party was 
formed in 1912 by former President Theodore Roosevelt, 
after Roosevelt failed to get the Republican nomination 
for president. The party stood for the initiative, recall, 
presidential primaries in all states, women’s suffrage, 
limitations on campaign contributions, prohibition of 
child labor, the eight-hour workday, minimum wage 
laws, workers compensation laws, a federal reserve bank, 
and stronger protection for natural resources. The party 
wasn’t formed in California until 1914 (there was no need 
for the party in California in 1912, since the California 
Republican Party bolted the Republican national ticket 
and declared Roosevelt to be its own nominee). In 1914 
the Progressive Party nominated Hiram Johnson as its 
gubernatorial candidate, even though he was the incum- 
bent and had been elected as a Republican in 1910. In 
1914 Johnson defeated his Republican and Democratic 


42 


opponents. The Progressives also elected several mem- 
bers of the U.S. House and California legislature in 1914. 
The party ceased to be active after the 1916 election. 


5. Liberty, 1932-1934. This party existed nationally only 
in 1932. It was organized to fight the depression by 
expanding the number of silver coins in circulation. It 
also advocated government ownership of banks, a five- 
year moratorium on mortgage foreclosures, demonitariz- 
ation of gold, and a n‘:- dum before Congress could 
declare the nation to be at war. The party ceased to be 
organized after the 1932 election. 


6. Communist, 1934-1944. Across the nation, the Commu- 
nist Party ran candidates in elections from 1921 until the 
present. During the 1920's it was called the Workers 
Party. It was unable to qualify for the California ballot 
before 1934 because, before that year, it was unable to 
successfully obtain the signatures of 1% of the last guber- 
natorial vote (it had tried unsuccessfully in previous elec- 
tions). Its 1932 platform called for unemployment 
insurance, emergency relief for the poor, a moratorium on 
mortgage foreclosure, equal rights for African-Americans 
and self-determination for that portion of the U.S. South 
which had African-American majorities, and for the cre- 
ation of a workers soviet which would rule the nation. In 
1943 the California legislature, motivated by a desire to 
_ eliminate the party, added a registration test (to be met in 
addition to the vote test) of one-tenth of 1% of the total 
state registration (no other party had registration under 
one-tenth of 1%). The Communist Party ceased to be 
qualified in March 1944 because it failed the new registra- 
tion. test. Nationally, the Communist Party continues to 


43 


exist, although it has not run a presidential candidate 
since 1984. 


7. Progressive, 1934-1940. This Progressive Party (unlike 
Progressive Parties of the 1910's, 1920's, and 1940's 
decades) existed only in California. It was formed by U.S. 
Senator Hiram Johnson in 1933 because he feared that he 
could not be re-nominated by the Republican Party when 
he was up for re-election in 1934. As it turned out, he was 
more popular than he anticipated, and he won the nomi- 
nation of three parties (Republican, Democratic and Pro- 
gressive) in 1934. The Progressive Party elected Franck 
Havenner congressman from San Francisco in 1936, the 
last time a minor party won a partisan election in Califor- 
nia. Havenner later switched his membership to the Dem- 
ocratic Party and was re-elected as a Democrat. 
Technically, the Progressive Party failed to be qualified 
after the November 1938 election, because it didn’t poll as 
much as 3% for any of its statewide candidates, but due 
to an error by the California Secretary of State, it also 
appeared on the ballot in 1940. 


8. Commonwealth, 1934-1938. Like the Progressive Party 
of 1934, this party was formed only in California, and for 
the specific purpose of backing a single candidate, Ray- 
mond L. Haight, for Governor in 1934, Haight, who was 
cross-endorsed by the Progressive Party, won the 
endorsement of the McClatchy newspaper chain and car- 
ried El Dorado, Fresno and Stanislaus Counties, but 
placed third with 13.0% of the vote. He presented himself 
as the moderate choice, standing between the conserva- 
tive Republican incumbent and the Democratic nominee, 
Upton Sinclair, who had been the Socialist Party nominee 
for Governor in 1930. The Commonwealth Party had no 


44 


candidates in 1936 and only one in 1938. It ceased to exist 
because it failed to poll 3% of the vote in 1938. 


9. Townsend, 1938-1942. It existed only in California and 
Connecticut; there was no national Townsend Party. The 
party was organized to support the Townsend Plan, 
advocated by Frances Townsend. The plan advocated 
pensions for every elderly person of $30 per month 
(nicknamed “Ham and Eggs”). Townsend argued that the 
recipients of the money would spend it, thus stimulating 
commerce and ending the depression. The party went off 
the ballot after 1942 for failing to meet the vote test. 


10. Independent Progressive, 1948-1954. It was formed by 
former vice-president Henry Wallace, who became its 
first presidential candidate. It is the only party in Califor- 
nia history that ever qualified by submitting a petition 
signed by 10% of the last gubernatorial vote (at that time, 
275,100 signatures were required). It advocated a negoti- 
ated settlement of differences with the Soviet Union, an 
end to the peacetime draft, abandonment of military 
bases abroad, the outlawing of the atomic bomb, indepen- 
dence for Puerto Rico, a federal anti-lynching bill, a ban

Il. MINOR PARTIES IN THE U.S.: WHY DO THEY 
RUN? 


As the preceding section suggests, people join minor 
parties because they have strong ideas about what public 
policy should be, and they observe, to their regret, that 
neither the Democratic nor Republican Parties support 
those ideas. Most people who choose to be active in 
minor parties are reconciled to the likelihood that those 
minor parties will win few, if any elections to important 
policy-making positions. Nevertheless, activists in minor 
parties believe that by running for office, or supporting 
the election campaigns of their fellow party members, 
they are helping to move public policy in the desired 
direction. Minor party activity is a means by which advo- 
cates of new ideas, propagate those ideas. 


There is substantial historical evidence to support the 
conclusion that persistent minor party campaigning can 
eventually influence public policy. The first minor party 
to advocate that slavery be abolished, the Liberty Party 
(which was launched in 1840), enjoyed very little support. 
Its presidential candidate aroused such hostility that he 
was forcibly prevented from speaking, not only in the 
South, but in the North. He only polled seven-tenths of 
1% of the 1840 presidential vote total. But his campaign 
served to spread the abolition idea, to bring people who 
agreed with that idea into communication with each 
other, and to change the thinking of many ordinary peo- 
ple who otherwise would not have been exposed to the 
party’s arguments. 


One may note, upon reading Section 1 of this report, that 
many of the ideas advocated by the early minor parties, 


49 


have long since been adopted as public policy. Collective 
bargaining rights, women’s suffrage, direct election of 
U.S. Senators, the eight-hour day, abolition of child labor, 
anti-monopoly legislation, an end to segregation, the ini- 
tiative, and other ideas, faced overwhelming hostility and 
opposition when they were first advocated. All these 
ideas were advocated by minor parties, decades before 
the major parties accepted them, yet now we take them 
for granted. 


III. NOMINATION OF MINOR PARTY CANDIDATES 


Minor parties desire to nominate as candidates, those 
people who are most skilled at explaining the party’s 
positions. Consequently, minor parties, especially ideo- 
logical minor parties (and most minor parties are ideolog- 
ical), generally prefer to nominate candidates by 
convention, rather than by primary, since party officers 
and activists (the kind of people likely to attend a nomi- 
nating convention) are more familiar with the personal 
characteristics of various potential candidates. Most 
states accommodate this desire. In 41 states, new or small 
political parties are permiiied to place candidates on the 
general election ballot, with the party label, without nom- 
inating those candidates in a primary election. Only 
Alaska, California, Hawaii, Louisiana, Mississippi, North 
Dakota, Ohio, Oklahoma, and Tennessee, require that all 
parties must nominate all their candidates by primary 
(technically, the Louisiana primary doesn’t even provide 
for party nomination of candidates; it functions as a 
general elected, with a later run-off if no one gets 50% in 
the first election). And Hawaii, one of these eight states, 


50 


at least provides that minor parties have the power to 
block anyone from entering the party’s primary, if the 
party feels that the proposed candidate is not sympa- 
thetic to the party’s views (for example, in 1994, the 
Hawaii Green Party blocked two candidates from run- 
ning in the party’s primary because the candidates were 
pro-life on the abortion issue, a stand which contradicted 
the party platform). 


Since minor parties are forced to nominate by primary in 
California, they (or the candidates) attempt to mail cam- 
paign literature, describing differences between the can- 
didates in important contested primaries, to all their 
registered voters, or to those registered voters who 
(based on past performance) are most likely to vote in the 
party’s primary. Major newspapers and broadcasters in 
California generally do not cover contested minor party 
primaries at all. Therefore, rank-and-file registrants in 
minor parties (especially those who are not active in the 
party organization and do not attend party meetings, or 
receive the party newsletter) would have almost no basis 
for making a decision as to whom to vote for in the 
primary, if it weren't for direct mailings from the party or 
from the candidates. 


Such mailings are very expensive, and minor parties 
don’t have large budgets. Since before 1940, no minor 
party in California has held registration membership just 
prior to a primary, of as much as 1% of the number of 
registered voters in the state, with the sole exception of 
the American Independent Party (that party exceeded 1% 
in 1968 and also the period of 1978 to the present). 
Relatively small registration has meant that it is at least 
possible for the minor party, or a candidate in a contested 


51 


minor party primary, to communicate by direct mail with 
most of the party’s registrants.

CONCLUSION AND OPINION 


In my opinion, if the new California primary law goes 
into effect, the vote for minor party candidates in primary 
elections will be much higher than it has been in the past. 
The new voters will not be party members. As a general 
proposition, I believe that minor party members voting in 
their own primary will be outnumbered by non-party 
members. Even where they are not outnumbered, the 
proportion of non-party voters will be very high — much, 
much higher than in major party primaries. 


This will have devastating effects on the minor parties. It 
will, in the most fundamental way, deny party members 
the right to choose their own nominees. If the non-party 
voters have malicious intent, they will frequently be able 
to alter the result of a minor party primary because, as 
the chart set forth in section IV above shows, those pri- 
maries are frequently decided by a small number of 
votes. Even if the non-party voters are sympathetic to the 
party, they will almost certainly lack knowledge of the 
minor party candidates because they will not have 
received mail from the party. Even a small number of 


such voters could easily defeat the choice of party mem- 
bers. 


Thus the new primary law will dilute the message of 
minor parties. One reaction to this might be to say “So 
what? The minor parties seldom win partisan elections 
anyway.” As I have shown above, minor parties are gen- 
erally ideological entities that have played an important 
role in introducing new and (at least initially) unpopular 


54 


ideas into our political culture. I believe that any restric- 
tion of that role strikes at the very heart of the minor 
parties’ reason for existence. 


In my opinion, Proposition 198 will harm California’s 
minor parties by flooding their primaries with voters 
who lack knowledge of their candidates.

REPORT ON BLANKET AND OPEN PRIMARIES 


Bruce E. Cain 
UC Berkeley 


With the passage of Proposition 198 in March 1996, 
California adopted an unusual method for nominating 
partisan candidates - the blanket primary. The blanket 
primary allows independent and partisan voters to vote 
for candidates from any party that qualifies for the ballot. 
This means, for instance, that Democrats can vote in 
Republican party primary races, or that voters from large 
parties can vote in primary races for small parties such as 
Peace and Freedom. Only two other states employ this 
system specifically - Alaska and Washington - and one 
other state, Louisiana, uses a slight variant, the nonpar- 
tisan primary (so-called because the top two vote-getters 
advance to the general election regardless of their party 
affiliation unless one of them gets more than 50% of the 
vote). 


The blanket primary lies at the far “open” end of a 
procedural spectrum that ranges from closed candidate 
selection by party activist caucuses at the one end to open 
elections by primary voters at the other. Because it pre- 
vents the parties from protecting themselves against 


56 


encroachments from opposing party voters, the blanket 
primary is the most burdensome of the ostensibly parti- 
san nominating procedures with respect to party rights 
and operations. Allowing voters from other parties to 
determine another party’s : ominee infringes upon that 
party’s basic right to determine its own ideas and poli- 
cies. Only a purely nonpartisan process that eliminated 
party labels entirely would infringe to a greater degree on 
the parties’ rights to control their own political view- 
points. Moreover, there is no evidence that the blanket 
primary affords indisputable advantages over other less 
burdensome forms of primary selection. California voters 
could adopt any number of other forms of primary pro- 
cedures (e.g. open, semi-open, semi-closed) that would 
open up the process to independent voters without vio- 
lating the first amendment freedom of parties so 
extremely. 


ASSESSING THE EFFECT OF THE BLANKET PRIMARY 
ON CALIFORNIA'S POLITICAL PARTIES 


The problematic nature of the alleged benefits of the 
blanket primary system would matter less if this unique 
procedure did not so substantially and unnecessarily 
infringe upon the autonomy of California political par- 
ties. Political parties are ideally teams of reasonably like- 
minded individuals who come together to cooperate and 
coordinate their activities in order to achieve their politi- 
cal ends. Whether the origins of political parties are inter- 
nal (i.e. develop out of legislative factions) or external 
(i.e. develop out of electoral coalitions or extra-political 
organizations such as labor unions), they exist to perform 


57 


certain key functions (Duverger, 1954). First, political par- 
ties nominate individuals as official candidates in order 
to represent their ideas and interests in the election. In 
the cases of minor parties and major parties in hopeless 
seats (i.e. where the result in November is inevitable), the 
nomination act is more for the purpose of making a 
statement about principles and ideals than it is about 
picking a likely winning candidate. Such issue can- 
didacies serve an important role in fostering educative 
debate during an election; a contest between candidates 
espousing essentially similar centrist views does not fur- 
ther the ideal of J.S. Mill’s “free market place of ideas” as 
effectively as one that has at least a few issue-oriented 
candidates who place principles ahead of winning. Nomi- 
nation is a form of political message. In this sense, the 
closed primary allows for the purest expression of the 
party’s message. Allowing members of other parties to 
participate in a given party’s primary surrenders control 
over that message to the party’s opponents and threatens 
the vitality of political debate. Most members of minor 
parties do not join these organizations because they think 
their party will win. Rather, they belong to parties 
because they want to support candidates who will give 
voice to their ideas and ideologies. 


Secondly, parties give nominated candidates a desig- 
nation in the form of a party label that provides an 
extremely important information cue for voters. Even in 
the current era, a candidate’s party label is one of the 
most important pieces of information that a voter can 
have about candidates and their likely actions in office 
(Campbell et al., 1960; Miller and Levitan, 1976). Party 


58 


labels evoke images in voters’ minds about what candi- 
dates stand for and how they will behave once in office. 
Voters in California typically face a potentially bewilder- 
ing array of candidates. Few, if any, have the time and the 
resources to inform themselves fully about each and 
every issue position candidates may be taking (Fiorina, 
1981; Lupia and McCubbins, 1996). Knowing that Mr. C is 
a Democrat gives the voter a basis for expecting where 
Mr. C stands both on various issues and on the role of 
government more generally. The right to bestow the offi- 
cial party label upon candidates is critical to its ability to 
control who gets to represent the party and what that 
party label means to voters over time. 


The third function of parties is to mobilize resources 
and supporters to help their nominated candidates win 
election. The centrality of the official party organization 
in providing resources to candidates fluctuates over time. 
In the last two decades, candidates themselves raised 
much of the money they needed for election, and field 
activities by party loyalists became less important. How- 
ever, aspects of the federal campaign finance law and the 
recently enacted Proposition 208 seem unintentionally to 
be putting parties back in the center of the campaign 
resource picture. The 1996 Presidential election brought 
record amounts of so-called “soft money” contributions 
to state parties, and Proposition 208 has given the parties 
the opportunity to replace individual contributors as 
major fund-raising sources in the state. Aside from spe- 
cial interests that give to further self-interested policy 
goals, the other major motivation for giving is ideological 


- i.e. people or groups giving because they believe in the — 


party’s ideals and policies (Sorauf, 1988). Undercutting 


59 


the role of party members in the nomination of their own 
party’s candidates potentially weakens the incentive for 
ideological giving and leaves the system even more vul- 
nerable to special interest donors. 


Finally, political parties provide a framework for 
cooperation among elected officials once in office. When 
the primary ballot restricts shopping across party lines, 
voters have to think about elected officials as a team, 
fostering a sense of collective responsibility for policies 
and positions (Haeberle, 1985). Although denied the 
option to choose at will in the primary, voters are still free 
to pick and choose across party lines in the November 
election. By constraining themselves to party slates in the 
primary, voters preserve the option of picking a reason- 
ably like-minded team of elected officials in the fall that 
will work together to realize certain policy ends. If voters 
can cross party lines in the primary, it weakens the ability 
of party members to select a coherent team, and iron- 
ically, takes away an option for voters in the fall cam- 
paign. 

The blanket primary affects all of the essential party 
functions in important, adverse ways. Because it opens 
the selection to independents and supporters of other 
parties, it is inevitable that parties will be forced in some 
circumstances to give their official designation to a candi- 
date who is not preferred by a majority or even plurality 
of party members. For example, assume an Assembly 
district with 35,000 registered Democrats, 80,000 Republi- 
cans and 20,000 independent and other party identifiers. 
Assume also that there are two candidates running in the 
Democratic primary - Mr. A, a liberal, and Mrs. B, a 
conservative. By themselves, the Democrats might prefer 


60 


Mr. A 70% to 30%, but if only a quarter of the non- 
Democrats cross over to the Democratic primary 
(because, for instance, the race on the Republican side is 
uncontested) in order to support Mrs. B, the Democratic 
party could be forced to give its official party label to a 
candidate who is opposed by a vast majority of registered 
Democratic voters in the constituency. This is called 
“swamping.” 


The inability to control who makes the party's selec- 
tion of candidates then affects the second essential party 
function - i.e. the right to control the party label. Years of 
political science research reveals that the party label is 
used by many voters as an information short-cut or 
heuristic (i.e. it helps voters know what candidates stand 
for without investing in information about all of their 
specific policy positions. See Lupia and McCubbins, 
1996). Hence, losing control over who gets the label 
amounts to losing control in an important way over what 
the party stands for and what is communicated to voters 
about the parties. Under the circumstances of my hypo- 
thetical example, it would not be surprising if many of 
the 80% of the Democratic registered voters who sup- 
ported Mr. A became disillusioned and refused either to 
work for or give resources to the election of the party’s 
nominee whom they did not support. In turn, the third of 
the four essential party functions discussed above would 
be impaired. Finally, the fact that Mrs. B, if elected, owes 
her nomination to independents and Republicans 
weakens her incentive to cooperate or associate closely 
with other Democratic legislators, eroding party disci- 
pline in the legislature and weakening norms of collective 
responsibility (i.e. the fourth party function). 


61 


The blanket primary procedure is more violative of 
party rights than other forms of open primary, because it 
substantially lowers the costs of strategic actions. All 
other forms of open primaries require voters at some 
point to declare in which party’s primary they wish to 
participate in. The cost of crossing over to influence one 
race is that the voter can only vote in that one party’s 
primary. However, in the blanket system, the voter does 
not lose the opportunity to vote in his or her preferred 
party primary just because he or she wants to cross-over 
in any particular political race. Hence, the temptation to 
cross-over unaided by elites is greater in the blanket and 
nonpartisan races than in other open primary procedures. 
If in addition, political consultants and party leaders 
provide encouragement and direction, the incentive to 
vote strategically will be enhanced significantly. 


There are two major types of cross-over behavior. 
The first is “shopping” which is when voters of one party 
cross-over to another party’s primary to support the can- 
didate whose positions they most prefer. Thus, if Republi- 
cans cross-over to the Democratic primary to support the 
more conservative candidate, or if Democrats cross-over 
to the Republican primary to support the more liberal 
candidate, then these would be cases of what I would 
term “shopping.” ‘If enough shoppers cross-over to deter- 
mine the outcome of the party’s primary, shopping can be 
decisive, and it becomes “swamping.” The other form of 
cross-over behavior can be called “sabotage” or “raid- 
ing,” which is when voters in one party cross-over to 
participate in another party’s primary in order to elect 
the “weaker” candidate so that their party's candidate 
can prevail in November. I call it sabotage for the obvious 


62 


reason that the purpose of crossing over is not to support 
a preferred candidate, but to influence the outcome by 
forcing the other party into a weaker position. 


The conditions that are most ripe for both “shop- 
ping” and “sabotage” are similar, but not identical. The 
key strategic condition is what I call “asymmetric com- 
petitive conditions.” If one party has an uncontested race 
and the other a contested race, then voters who would 
normally participate in the uncontested race will feel 
more tempted to put their vote where it counts. Both 
shopping and sabotage are forms of strategic behavior in 
the sense that voters are motivated by their expectations 
of where their votes will count the most and not just by 
their policy preferences per se. Most studies of cross-over 
voting suggest that “shopping” is more prevalent than 
“sabotage” (Hedlund and Hedge, 1982; Hedlund and 
Watts, 1986). Recent Field Poll studies on the upcoming 
1998 Senate and Gubernatorial races suggest that as many 
as a third of the voters may cross-over (Field Poll, Feb. 25, 
1997). As discussed earlier, if enough voters “shop” 
across party lines, it is inevitable that parties will be stuck 
with nominees that their members did not prefer. 


Instances of “sabotage,” while less frequent, are 
important and especially violative of party autonomy and 
rights. For instance, in the Alaska Gubernatorial primary 
of 1974, a large number of Democrats and independents 
abandoned a Democratic primary that featured a vir- 
tually unopposed Democratic incumbent in favor of vot- 
ing in a contested Republican primary that featured a 
former Governor, Walter Hickel, and a liberal Republican, 
Joey Hammond. Even though there were 49,000 regis- 
tered Democrats, there were only 22,370 votes cast in the 


63 


Democratic primary. By comparison, there were 60,107 
votes cast in the Republican primary even though there 
were only 26,500 registered Republicans. Hammond pre- 
vailed over Hickel 28,602 to 20,728. This can be viewed 
either as a case of liberal shopping that saddled the 
Republicans with a choice they would not have made 
themselves or as sabotage in the sense of helping the 
candidate with a weaker background. Either way, it raises 
serious questions about party rights and autonomy under 
the blanket system. 


Looking at California, we can ask how likely the 
conditions are that encourage these types of cross-over 
behavior. First, I would note that we have seen attempts 
to sabotage other party prospects in California in recent 
years. Scott Baugh, a Republican Assemblyman from 
Newport Beach, was accused of trying to encourage a 
Democratic candidacy in a special election in order to 
divide the Democratic vote and protect Republican pros- 
pects. Or to take another example, Republican opponents 
of Brian Setencich may have encouraged the candidacy of 
a euphonious competitor, Brian Steiner, in order to con- 
fuse potential write-in voters. Given the perceived stakes 
of controlling the legislature, it is clear that candidates 
and consultants will play “hard ball” tactics when they 
have to in order to win. 


Second, the specific conditions that are ripe for cross- 
over voting occur frequently enough that the temptation 
will be present at every primary election. Those condi- 
tions are: (1) Asymmetric competitive conditions (i.e. one 
competitive primary and one uncompetitive), and (2) A 
likely competitive race in November. In the 1996 Assem- 
bly primaries, I identified ten races that fulfilled the first 


64 


condition and four that also fulfilled the second condi- 
tion: Districts 1, 8, 43 and 53. Of the ten asymmetric cases, 
one featured an uncontested race in the party that was 
certain to win in November (AD 65), and hence there was 
no reason to interfere in the contested primary. In five 
other cases (ADS 11, 14, 30, 52, 71), the uncontested race 
was in the party of the likely loser in November and the 
contested race was in the party of the likely winner. 
These cases are ripe for “shopping,” in which voters 
might try to get the best deal they can in the party that is 
likely to win in November. Those in the likely losing 
party could be assured of having a standard-bearer for 
their party while at the same time having the chance to 
influence the outcome of the opposition party primary. 
The remaining four cases feature both asymmetric com- 
petitive primary conditions and a likely competitive race 
in November. Party members in the uncompetitive pri- 
mary could have interfered in the decision of the party 
with the competitive primary to help elect the weaker 
candidate if they chose to. In all but one case, the out- 
come could have been changed by the intervention of less 
than 5% of the other party’s registered voters. In other 
words, a small degree of strategic coordination could 
have tipped the outcome in the contested primary. This 
would have given the party with the uncontested pri- 
mary the power to dictate the candidates of both their 
own and the opposition party. This is hardly what we 
mean by increasing choice in a democracy. 


It seems clear to me that the potential harms to 
parties under the blanket primary are quite severe. It is 
not surprising that both major and minor parties would 
resist rules that allow members of opposing parties to 


65 


vote in their primaries. Parties will likely be forced to 
accept as their nominated candidate individuals who are 
not preferred by their members, or worse, candidates that 
were dictated to them by the opposing party in order to 
weaken their prospects in the November election. Given 
that the alleged benefits of the blanket primary are dis- 
putable and the potential harms are significant, it is rea- 
sonable to ask whether the aims that voters sought to 
achieve through the passage of Prop 198 could not be 
achieved through mechanisms that are less burdensome 
to the parties. 


ASSESSING THE ALLEGED BENEFITS OF THE BLAN- 
KET PRIMARY 


Those who defend the blanket primary system typ- 
ically assert that is has advantages over other forms of 
selection: that it produces more moderate candidates; it 
enhances voter participation; it gives voters a wider 
range of choices; and it promotes the secrecy of the ballot. 
However, I find that these claims are neither empirically 
proven nor normatively indisputable. 


With respect to the tendency to produce more “cen- 
trist” candidates, there is clear political science evidence 
that rule changes can affect the positions that party candi- 
dates take. A recent study by Barbara Norrander suggests 
that there may be a higher tendency for states that 
employ caucus nomination systems to give greater sup- 
port to more ideologically distinct candidates than states 
with primary elections (Norrander, 1993). Another study 
by Elizabeth Gerber and Rebecca Morton suggests that 


66 


the same is true for closed versus open primaries (Gerber 
and Morton, 1997). 


However, there is no consensus in democratic theory 
‘that additional ideological moderation is needed in a 
system that already encourages centrism in many ways, 
especially given that Prop 198 would seek to achieve this 
moderation by allowing opposition party members to 
interfere in another party’s nomination process at will. 
Those who argue for additional moderation assume with- 
out proof that there is a need in California to reduce 
further the risks of November elections between candi- 
dates with ideologically extreme views. Occasionally, can- 
didates that are less centrist than others win their 
primary nominations, but usually they lose in the 
November election if the other party nominates a centrist 
candidate of their own. In fact, there are many informed 
observers who think that the problem in California is 
exactly the opposite: i.e. they lament the lack of distinct 
choices in American politics and feel that the system 
shuts out legitimate points of view that do not fall in the 
middle of the ideological spectrum. 


Since the publication of Anthony Downs’ seminal 
book, An Economic Theory of Democracy, political scientists 
have come to appreciate the strength of the median voter 
effect in American politics (Downs, 1957). Median voter 
theorems suggest that candidates who take positions 
closer to the median (i.e. center or mid-point) of an 
ideological spectrum will tend to defeat those who do 
not, assuming single-member, simple plurality voting 
rules and voters who prefer candidates whose positions 
are closest to theirs. Sometimes this means that primary 
voters will choose candidates who seem to be more 


67 


potentially electable in November based on how main- 
stream their views are (see, for instance, the 1990 Demo- 
cratic Gubernatorial primary between Feinstein and Van 
de Kamp, as described in Lubenow, 1991). In other 
instances, it means that candidates will try to move to the 
center after the primary in order to appeal to the swing 
voters in November (e.g. as Herschensohn did against 
Boxer in the 1992 US Senate contest). My point quite 
simply is that pressures to move to the center are already 
quite strong in California politics, and hence, it is not 
obvious that there is a need for additional centrist pres- 
sures. 


Moreover, the mechanism that Prop 198 uses - cross- 
over voting by opposition party members - interferes 
with the impacted party’s first amendment right to deter- 
mine what it stands for. Whether the opposition party 
members are merely shopping or engaging in sabotage, 
they will determine by their cross-over vote what the 
impacted party stands for. The impacted party has lost its 
ability to determine its message. 


A second purported benefit is that the blanket pri- 
mary would increase the level of participation in primary 
elections. Here, we need to make a critical distinction to 
avoid confusion; namely, between overall turnout in a 
primary (i.e. the number of individuals who participate 
in the election) and race-specific turnout, or dropoff (i.e. 
the number of individuals who vote a specific office such 
as President, Governor, etc.). Even in a completely open 
system, race-specific turnout will typically be less than 
overall turnout for any number of reasons (e.g. disin- 
terest in the race, ballot fatigue, lack of suitable alterna- 
tives, etc). It would seem almost tautological to state that 


68 


race-specific turnout should increase with the addition of 
independents. However, because it is possible that core 
party activists under a blanket primary system could 
become so alienated by the prospect of independents 
swamping the nomination process, decreases in activist 
participation might offset the gains in independent par- 
ticipation. But short of that depressing scenario, the 
number of individuals voting in partisan primary races 
should go up. But will it go up dramatically? A study of 
Senate races found that turnout in states with blanket and 
nonpartisan rules only had a turnout 7.2% higher than 
closed primary systems and 2.3% higher than those with 
other open systems. (Kenney, 1986). Lack of enthusiasm 
among the partisans may not completely offset the addi- 
tion of independents, but it may diminish the potential 
effect considerably. 


But the more interesting and important question is 
whether overall participation will increase. There have 
been many studies of participation over the last thirty 
years (Wolfinger and Rosenstone, 1980; Nagler, 1991; 
Leighly and Nagler, 1992; Texeira, 1989). None of them 
has ever asserted that the nomination process type is as 
important a determinant of whether people vote as are 
socioeconomic factors such as age and education, or 
structural factors such as the ease of registration require- 
ments in a given state. This means that there are much 
more direct, effective ways to increase voter participation 
(e.g. mandatory voting, same-day registration, etc.) that 
are less burdensome with respect to party rights and 
autonomy (see, for mandatory voting, Hirczy, 1994). 


For all of the above reasons, having a blanket pri- 
mary system does not guarantee a high primary turnout 


69 


in either the general or race-specific sense. If we compare 
across Gubernatorial primaries in 1994, for instance, we 
find that Alaska with a blanket primary had a turnout of 
32% while California with its closed system had a turnout 
of 31%. States with closed primary systems had a wide 
variance, ranging from a high of 42% in New Mexico's 
Gubernatorial primary to a low of 12% in New York’s. 
Clearly, there are many other factors at work in determin- 
ing the turnout level, and at best, the type of primary is a 
marginal factor. 


Moreover, there is a good reason to suspect tha: the 
effect of changing from a closed to a blanket system will 
be even more marginal in California, especially with 
regard to overall primary participation. Unlike many 
other states, California offers voters reasons to go to the 
polls other than the chance to participate in partisan 
elections per se. For instance, primary ballots usually 
contain a number of state and local initiatives and refer- 
endums. In addition, some cities and counties hold their 
nonpartisan elections on the day of the statewide pri- 
mary. As a consequence, the number of independent 
voters who participate in California primary elections is 
already significant. 


One suggestion of this comes from the Field Insti- 
tute’s pre-election polls. In pre-election polls prior to the 
1994 and 1996 primaries in California, the Field Institute 
found that 74% and 75% respectively of those registered 
as decline to state indicated that they were absolutely or 
fairly certain that they would vote in the upcoming pri- 
mary election. This is somewhat less than what those 
registered as Democrats (90% and 85% respectively), and 
as Republicans (88% and 85% respectively) indicated, but 


70 


it impossible to say whether even this modest difference 
is due to the exclusion of independents from partisan 
races or to other factors such as their overall lower levels 
of interest in and attentiveness to politics (Keith et al., 
1992). While pre-election polls do not tell us directly who 
actually voted in the primaries, it does gives us evidence 
about the intentions of decline-to-state voters. Judging by 
the gap between votes cast at the top of ticket in the 
partisan primary races and the total votes cast in 1994, I 
estimate that nonpartisan turnout was about 10% less 
than the overall average. Opening up partisan races to 
independent voters may provide a marginally stronger 
incentive to participate, but the increases are unlikely to 
be dramatic. 


The last two common arguments in favor of the blan- 
ket primary are not essentially empirical claims. The first 
is that the blanket primary offers a wider range of choices 
to voters since Democrats are not restricted to voting for 
Democratic candidates, Republicans to Republican candi- 
dates, etc. In a certain purely quantitative sense, this is 
indisputable. If a voter can vote in any primary that he or 
she wants to, then it follows that he or she will have more 
choices than a person who is restricted to voting in one 
party’s primary. However, in another more qualitative 
sense which I discussed earlier, blanket and open pri- 
maries could actually work to restrict choices in Novem- 
ber more than closed primary systems do. If, as Gerber 
and Morton’s work suggests the likelihood of nominating 
candidates who are further from the median is lower in 
open than in closed primary systems, whatever gains 
voters get in terms of the number and variety of choices 
in the primary will be offset by the lack of distinct and 


71 


meaningful choices in November. It is important to bear 
in mind that the right that the blanket primary preserves 
is the right to pick and choose between party races over 
the entire ballot. If California wanted to preserve the 
right to enter a given party’s primary without violating 
the integrity of the party ballot as severely as the blanket 
primary does, the state could adopt the completely open, 
partially open or partially closed primary systems. Under 
these systems the voter can either change his/her party 
registration on election day (i.e. partially closed), declare 
a party on the day of the election (partially open) or not 
choose until he or she is in the voting booth (i.e. com- 
pletely open). All of these systems are less vulnerable to 
— of swamping and raiding that I discussed 
earlier. 


The last of the alleged benefits of the blanket primary 
is the greater secrecy it affords voters with respect to 
their partisan affiliations. Since the voter does not need to 
declare an affiliation in order to vote in any party’s 
primary, voters who wish to maintain their privacy with 
respect to partisanship can best do so under the blanket 
system, or so it is alleged. However, once again, I would 
point out that the same level of privacy can be main- 
tained in far less burdensome ways. The “completely 
open” primary requires the voters to choose one party 
over the others, but the voter does not have to choose 
until he or she is in the polling booth. Such a system 
would better preserve the party’s autonomy and protect 
it from strategic intrusions from other parties, but still 


72 


afford maximum protection for voters who wish to keep 
their partisan preferences secret. 


PARTY SOVEREIGNTY AND THE PROP 198 VOTE 


The passage of Prop 198 raises an interesting political 
science question: if a majority of the registered Democrats 
and Republicans who voted in the 1996 primary favored 
the blanket primary, does this mean that the parties have 
assented to these rules? I do not presume to give a 
constitutional interpretation, but I do have thoughts from 
a political science perspective. Since the seminal work of 
the scholar V.O. Key, parties are thought to consist of 
three components: the party in government, the party in 
the constituency and the party as organization. The party 
in government refers the formal and informal ties 
between elected officials sharing the same party label. 
The party in the constituency is the term of those who 
register or identify with the party. The party as organiza- 
tion is the official party structure. 


Universally, political parties experience tensions 
between the different components. Elected officials tend 
to be more responsive to what they perceive as electorally 
popular. Members of the party organization tend to put 
greater stock in ideological principle. Aside from the 
activists and more politically motivated voters, large por- 
tions of the electorate may be tangentially connected to 
the party, and only then because of the need to register or 
declare a party preference. A compromised synthesis of 
these perspectives best represents the balanced ideal of 
party government (Bibby, 1981). 


73 


In the past in California, the party as government has 
heavily regulated the party as organization or official 
party by statute, dictating party structure and activities 
through the election code. Recent court decisions have 
given the parties as organizations more autonomy over 
their affairs (e.g. SF County v. Eu). Now, with the passage 
of Proposition 198, we have an attempt by the party as 
constituency to regulate the party as organization by 
imposing blanket primary rules. The critical question in 
political science terms is where should party sovereignty 
lie? Which sense of the term party should decide the 
omy of assigning the party label to potential candi- 

ates 


From a political science perspective, the official party 
or party as organization is the most appropriate locus of 
sovereignty. The party as organization is the only setting 
that brings all three components together at regular inter- 
vals for deliberative purposes. Party conventions are 
forums that convene party officials, registered voters/ 
activists and elected officials in order to talk about mat- 
ters of policy and party governance. When elected offi- 
cials make decisions about party governance in their 
capacity as legislators, they do not have to have the 
support of either the official party or the party in the 
electorate. Similarly, when the party as the electorate 
dictates rule changes by initiative, it does not have to 
consult or get the approval of the official party of the 
elected officials. From the perspective of consent, the 
official party most legitimately represents the synthesis of 
these components. 


Just as the imposition of rules by elected officials was 
an intrusion in the autonomy of the parties, so the 


74 


imposition of rules by voters does not imply the true 
consent of the parties. Political participation studies iden- 
tify registration and voting as the least costly forms of 
political activity (Verba and Nie, 1992). The regulations 
imposed by the party in the electorate upon the official 
party weaken the party’s ability to express an untainted 
message (i.e. untained by the viewpoints of other party 
members) and to field a slate of candidates that can act 
effectively as a team. The concerns of proponents — such 
as increasing participation in partisan races —- could have 
been addressed by other open primary systems that bet- 
ter preserved the notion of collective responsibility and 
party automony. Instead, voters were offered an all-or- 
nothing chance to open up the system in the peculiar and 
burdensome fashion of the blanket primary. There is no 
way of telling whether they fully understood the implica- 
tions of the system they chose, or whether they would 
have chosen the blanket primary system over other open 
systems if given the chance. 


The associational rights of parties seem to me to best 
preserved when the official party decides its own pro- 
cedures and rules subject to basic prohibitions against 
nondiscrimination. As long as the party does not exclude 
individuals on an inappropriate and unconstitutional 
basis such as race or gender, any system that restricts 
voters to choices within a slate offered in a primary 
preserves basic party autonomy. But when the threshold 
of strategic behavior is lowered considerably, as in the 
case of the blanket primary, the dangers of swamping and 
sabotage become considerable. The party’s essential func- 
tion to field teams of candidates with common goals is 
attacked and its ability to associate effectively for the 


75 


purpose of furthering political goals is seriously 
abridged.

Analysis by the Legislative Analyst 
Background 


In general, California has three types of elections: 
primary, general, and special. Primary elections are held 
for both partisan offices, where candidates are identified 
on the ballot with a political party, and nonpartisan 
offices, where candidates are not identified with a politi- 


cal party. 


In order to vote in primary elections for partisan 
offices, a voter must have identified a political party 
affiliation when registering to vote and can vote only for 
candidates of that party. Voters who have not identified a 
political party affiliation can not vote for candidates run- 
ning for partisan office in primary elections. These voters, 
however, can vote for candidates for nonpartisan offices 
and on propositions. 


For every primary election, the elections official in 
each county is required to prepare a partisan ballot for 
each political party and a nonpartisan ballot for voters 
who have not identified a political party affiliation. 


Proposal 

This measure allows all persons who are entitled to 
vote in primary elections, including those not affiliated 
with a political party, to vote for any candidate regardless 
of the candidate’s political party affiliation. Thus, voters 
in primary elections would be allowed to vote for candi- 
dates across political party lines. Furthermore, the initia- 
tive provides that county elections officials prepare only 


one ballot for all voters. The candidates for an office 
would be listed randomly on the ballot and not grouped 
by political party affiliation. The candidate of each polliti- 
cal party who receives the most votes for a state elective 
office becomes the nominee of that party at the next 
general election. 


These provisions do not apply to elections of political 
party committee members. In these elections, voters 
would be restricted to voting for candidates of their own 
political party affiliation. 


Fiscal Effect 


This measure would have no direct fiscal impact on 
the state. Counties could realize unknown, but probably 
minor savings statewide, primarily due to the prepara- 
tion of fewer ballots.

I. Overview of the Project 


In March of 1996, California voters passed Proposition 
198, the Open Primary Law. Proposition 198 requires that 
California replace its current system of closed primaries 
for nominating candidates for state and federal office 
with an open or “blanket” primary system. This entails 
moving from a system in which participation in primary 
elections is limited to registered party members, to a 
system in which voters participate in the nominating 
primaries without regard to their party registration. Pro- 
ponents of Proposition 198 argued that allowing regis- 
tered voters to participate in whichever primary they 
choose, regardless of their party registration, would 
result in more competitive elections, increased voter par- 
ticipation, and more responsive elected representatives 


SE OF GR OO RE I PO TN . 


Secretary of State, 1996). Opponents argued that opening 
candidate nominations to nonpartisans and members of 
the other parties would result in raiding of the party’s 
nomination process and a weakening of the parties (Sec- 
retary of State, 1996). 


While we will only know the ultimate consequences of 
Proposition 198 after blanket primaries are put into place 
and several elections are held under the new system, it is 
instructive to analyze the experience other states have 
had with similar laws. Electoral laws, like other political 
institutions, create incentives for candidates, voters, and 
parties to engage in predictable patterns of behavior. 
These patterns of behavior result in predictable electoral 
outcomes. Certainly, there are also numerous other fea- 
tures of each state’s unique political environment that 
also shape the electoral outcomes in that state. It is, 
however, reasonable to expect that there will be broad 
similarities between the electoral outcomes that result 
from states that have similar electoral laws and institu- 
tions. 


This report summarizes research that analyzes the rela- 
tionship between the primary system used in each of the 
American states and some of the outcomes produced by 
the electoral system. I find that in states that use blanket 
primaries, the congressional elections were more compet- 
itive by several measures, including the winning candi- 
date’s average vote share, number of uncontested races, 
partisan balance, average number of candidates in pri- 
mary races (for Democrats only), and general election 
turnout. There were fewer minor party candidates in 
blanket primaries, and a far lower share of the general 
election vote went to minor party candidates, indicating

On the basis of these experiences in states with different 
primary election laws, I conclude that when the blanket 
primary is put into place in California, electoral outcomes 
in the state are likely to become more like those in states 
that already have blanket primaries. In other words, we 
can expect blanket primaries to make California elections

state. Since the data are limited in some ways that are

A. Primary System Type 


The US Constitution empowers individual states to deter- 
mine the laws by which they conduct elections. To nomi- 
nate candidates for congressional, state legislative, and 
state executive offices, all states use some form of pri- 
mary elections.! These election laws vary in terms of 
restrictions on who can participate in the nomination 
process. The following are the main types of primary 
election laws currently used in the American states. 


' Note that a state’s laws regulating the nomination of 
congressional, state legislative, and state executive candidates 
often differs from its laws regulating presidential nominations. 
This research focuses strictly on the former, however, we would 
expect many of the patterns (i.e. competitiveness) to hold for 
presidential elections as well. Also, a state’s laws for 
nominating minor party candidates often differ from its laws for 
nominating major party candidates.

V. Summary and Conclusion 


Analysis of recent congressional and state legislative elec- 
tions reveals important and systematic differences 
between the electoral outcomes resulting from states that 
use different primary election systems to nominate candi- 
dates for office. The following patterns emerge: 1) elec- 
tions are more competitive in blanket primary states; 2) 


132 


the parties are more permeable; and 3) the elected offi- 
cials are more reflective of the majority of voter prefer- 
ences in their states. On the basis of the experience from 
other states, it is reasonable to conclude that election 
outcomes in California will become more similar to those 
in states with open or blanket Primaries under Califor- 
nia’s new primary election law. In my opinion, any or all 
of those changes would improve the health of California’s 
two party electoral system.

Until passage by public vote of Proposition 198 in 
California in 1996, only two states in the U.S. had 
adopted the “blanket primary” for conducting primary 
elections in the U.S. There are thirteen Western States, six 
of which employ the closed primary, while seven utilize 
the open or blanket primary (Benedict and Hrebenar, 
1991, 107). Only Washington state (beginning in 1936) and 
Alaska (beginning in 1960) used the blanket primary 
prior to its adoption in 1996 by citizen initiative in Cali- 
fornia. The present statement addresses the uses and 
consequences of the blanket primary in the state with the 
longest experience with this unique form of the direct 


primary election. 


Direct primary elections first appeared in the U.S. 
shortly after the turn of the current Century as an alterna- 
tive method for political parties to select their nominees 
for the general election, replacing the more restrictive 
methods of the party convention and the party caucus. 


134 


The drive for adoption of the direct party was led by the 
Progressive Movement, which found fertile soil for its 
ideas among Western states (Hofstadter, 1955). The direct 
primary varies in form between the closed primary, on 
one hand, where voters choose party nominees from 
among candidates within the party within which the 
voter is registered, and the blanket primary, on the other 
hand, where voters do not have to declare a preference 
for one or another political party, and where they are free 
to choose nominees for each office among candidates of 
all parties. The open primary exhibits elements of both 
the closed and blanket primaries, where voters can cast 
votes in either party, but they cannot split their ballots 
between the parties in a given primary. 


Opponents of the blanket primary argue that it 
intrudes on the associational rights of a party organiza- 
tion to choose candidates who will represent the platform 
stands of the party, and it allows candidates to run in the 
general election under the name of the party without 
being beholden to the party or its views. Blanket primary 
supporters respond that this nomination method removes 
the selection of nominees from the hands of a closed 
circle of party stalwarts, maximizes voters’ freedom of 
choice over candidates, encourages voter turnout, and 
ensures that elected officials are representative of the 
people to be governed by forcing the parties to have a 
broad, cross-section of support from voters. 


At the base of differences between opponents and 
Supporters of the blanket primary lie two competing phi- 
losophies of democracy in the U.S. Advocates of strong 
Parties see democracy best served by elected officials 
who adhere closely to Party principles and platforms and 


135 


who abide by strong party discipline while holding office. 
Those less concerned with state guarantees of strong 
parties view democracy as best served by widely dis- 
persed and active involvement among eligible voters in 
the selection of nominees who stand for election in gen- 
eral elections. In the end this is, of course, a question of 
political choice between competing philosophies about 
how the goal of genuine democratic self government is 
best attained by the alternative methods available for its 


realization. 


In what follows, I present empirical evidence drawn 
from Washington state’s six-decade experience with the 
blanket primary to address a series of issues that have 
been raised about the uses and consequences of this 
unique form of the direct primary. The discussion is 
informed by the findings of research conducted by politi- 
cal scientists, my own direct scholarly research results, 
and a quarter century of close observation and involve- 
ment in state politics in Washington. The format of pre- 
sentation is to raise a series of six questions, and to 
provide answers to them.

The potential for raiding under the blanket primary 
generates wide media attention and spawns electioneer-

Technically defined, raiding would occur where partisan 
voters of party X cross-over in sufficient numbers to help 
select the weakest or most vulnerable candidate in party 
Y. The requirements for this form of electoral subterfuge 
are: 1) the absence of a meaningful primary contest in 
party X; 2) provisions of sufficient information to identify 
the weaker and most vulnerable potential opponent in 
party Y; 3) concerted action by a large enough bloc of 
voters in party X to influence the outcome in the primary 
of party Y; and 4) the abandonment of traditional party 
loyalties by partisan voters to enter the opponent's pri- 
mary.

Political Parties are organizations that run candidates 
or office with the goal of winning elections. The major 


139 


parties in Washington are the Republican and Democratic 
parties, but a number of other parties run candidates 


from time to time. 


Washington exhibits a wide-open and freewheeling 
style in its politics. The inherited legacy is a “lusty, 
gutsy” politics with pronounced strains of populism and 
radicalism (Bone, 1985, 7). The state’s radicalism includes 
ideologies of both the left and the right which are deeply 
embedded in the state’s populist political practices. Ten- 
dencies toward radical and populist politics are reified in 
the use of the initiative, referendum, and recall. Washing- 
ton voters tend to be highly independent and its political 
parties are commonly regarded as not strong (although, 


see below). 


What parts of these political attributes and behaviors 
can be attributed to the influence of the blanket primary? 
This question involves a tricky “chicken and egg” issue of 
causality: does the blanket primary create weaknesses in 
the political parties; or, did weak parties create the blan- 
ket primary? The direction of the flow of causality is as 
important as it is complex; most likely the causal influ- 
ences pass in both directions. In order to sort out these 
complexities, it is helpful to inventory the state of health 
in Washington’s two party system under the blanket pri- 
mary. 

A foremost goal of political parties is to win elec- 
tions. The Republican and Democratic parties have 
thrived in their quest for capturing public offices on a 
more or less even basis. By any criterion of measurement, 
Washington state is an evenly divided, two party compet- 
itive system. The Democratic and Republican parties 


140 


were remarkably evenly matched in electoral outcomes, 
according to Hrebenar and Benedict (1991, 142), through- 
out the seventies and in the period from 1982 to 1986 
more so than any of the other dozen mountain and Pacific 
states of the West. This finding is consistent with Ran- 
ney’s (1976) earlier research on the 1962-1973 time period 
which placed Washington as one of only six states in the 
West in the two party competitive category. And, Walker 
(1992, 45) concludes that: “Washington has been ranked 
as a highly competitive state. Over the years the differ- 
ence between the winner and loser in gubernatorial races 
~ whether Democrat or Republican - is slight.” There is 
ample evidence from multiple sources that Washington 
state’s two party system is highly competitive and evenly 
balanced in electoral outcomes. It lacks the more common 
pattern in the West where one or the other party domi- 
nates electoral outcomes, and thereby creates one very 
strong and one very weak party. Two party competitive 
Status thus may be viewed as a central indicator of the 
well being of a party system in regularly offering voters a 
viable choice among candidates for elective office. The 
competitiveness of the parties directly contributes to 
Washington’s relatively high levels of voter turnout. In 
fact, during the 1980s, Washington ranked 15th nationally 
in voter turnout (Nice, 1992, 73). To the extent that the 
blanket primary results in Producing candidates whose 
appeal is to the median voter (see Kelley and Weir, 1981) 
it can be seen as contributing to and sustaining the highly 
competitive party system in Washington state.

5 Cross Over Voting 
5.1 Definition: Open vs. Closed Primaries 


Most primaries in the United States are commonly classi- 
fied in one of two categories: open or closed. 


In most states where voters declare their party 
preference when they register to vote, ‘closed’ 
primaries are held; only voters registered with a 
particular party are allowed to participate in 
that party’s primary. Some states have modified 
this system and allow citizens who register as 
independent or state no party preference when 
they register to select the party ballot of their 
choice. Selecting a party ballot usually puts 
these voters on the registration rolls as members 
of the party. In states without party registration, 
‘open’ primaries are held. Voters either choose a 
party ballot before they enter the polling booth 
or in states where the Republican and Demo- 
cratic primary races are on the same ballot, 
voters may cast a ballot in either party primary 
once in the polling booth. (McGillivray, 1993: 
vii) 


We used McGillivray’s classification of states in deter- 
mining whether they had open or closed primaries. Thus 
a closed primary is one in which only voters registered as 


160 


party members are eligible to cast ballots. An open pri- 
mary 1s one in which voters are not required to be regis- 
tered as party members to cast ballots. 


5.2 Definition: Crossover Voting 


We consider a crossover voter to be when a Democratic 
voter votes in a Republican primary, or when a Republi- 
can voter votes in a Democratic primary. We do not 
consider votes in either primary by independent voters to 
be crossover votes. We feel that any definition of cross- 
over voting that counts independents is not appropriate 
for American politics, where an increasing number of 
voters now claim to be independents. The American 
National Election Studies has been asking respondents 
“Generally speaking, do you consider yourself a Republi- 
can, a Democrat, an Independent, or what?” since 1952. 
In the 1990s the percentage of respondents claiming to be 
Independent has been over 35%.

While the amount of crossover voting necessarily puts an 
upper bound on the amount of strategic behavior b 

voters, we still want to know the motivation of the i 
over voters. The most. pernicious attempt at strategic

voting would be for voters to attempt to ‘interfere’ and 
‘sabotage’ the primary of the other party by crossing over 
and voting in the opposing party’s primary in an attempt 
to give the other party the weakest general election nomi- 
nee. We looked for raiders in the 1988 Super Tuesday 
primaries. This is an appropriate election to search for 
raiders because the requisite circumstances for raiding 
were present: the Republican primary was largely a fore-

Bush was looking unbeatable), and the Democratic pri-

whom were probably not likely to be strong candidates in 


a general election. 


To determine if a crossover voter is attempting to act 
as a raider we need to know if the voter is intentionally 
voting for a candidate that they perceive to be a loser in 
the general election. Here are the requirements a voter 
had to meet for us to classify them as a raider: 


1. They had to crossover and vote in the pri- 
mary of the party that they did not identify 
with. 

2. There had to be a candidate available in the 
primary they voted in whom they did not 
vote for, and: 


(a) Who they preferred to the candidate 
they did vote for, and | 


(b) Who they felt had a better chance of 
winning the general election than the 
candidate they voted for. 


3. They had to want the party they identified 
with to win the general election.

7 Conclusion of Individual Analysis 


We have demonstrated that open primaries do not lead to 
a substantively large increase in crossover voting over 
closed primaries. And we have argued that the existing 
literature on voter behavior suggests that any crossover 
voting that occurs will be motivated by voters’ desire to 
vote for their first-choice candidate, or to a lesser extent, 
to avoid wasting their vote. Voters will simply not have 
the information necessary to engage in raiding behavior 
in primaries. We find that our empirical analysis of the 
1988 Super Tuesday primary provides overwhelming evi- 
dence to support this claim. By carefully examining the 
voters’ evaluations of the available candidates, and the 
voters’ perceptions of the candidates’ chances of winning 
both the primary and general election, we have been able 


170 


to demonstrate that fewer than 2% of voters in the pri- 
mary engaged in raiding behavior. 


It is our view that the blanket primary will not lead 
to large amounts of strategic behavior by voters. The 
possibility of strategic behavior in a primary is signifi- 
cantly different than in multi-candidate general elections.

8.3 What has the aggregated electoral data demon- 
strated? 


To conclude, we have shown that there was not a great 
deal of crossover voting in the aggregated data we exam- 
ined using 1992, 1994, and 1996 county-by-county elec- 
toral returns from Ohio and Washington, across a number 
of different primary elections. In general, we found in the 
ecological estimates that crossover voting averaged 12% 
for the thirteen races we examined. 


This analysis of the ecological data, then, leads us to 
have much more confidence in our hypothesis that the 
incidence of crossover voting in open and blanket pri- 
maries is not very great. The evidence presented here 
cross-validates our empirical results using the exit poll 
data earlier in this report. Here, we do estimate that the 
extent of crossover voting in open and blanket primaries 
is slightly greater than we found using the exit poll data 
from a number of recent presidential primaries across 
many states. This could be the result of one of two 
phenomenon. On one hand, our ecological analysis might 
be overestimating the incidence of crossover voting. If 
that is the case, then the amount of crossover voting in 
these races is much lower. On the other hand, if our 


171 


ecological analysis is correct but the exit poll analysis is 
incorrect, then the amount of crossover voting in these 
races is correct. But in either case, we are very confident, 
on the basis of two fundamentally different types of 
empirical evidence and statistical approaches, that the 
amount of crossover voting in open and blanket pri- 
maries is no higher than the level we have estimated 
using the ecological data. Hence, the amount of crossover 
voting in open and blanket primaries is relatively slight. 


Additionally, the ecological results in this section of 
our report help to provide answers to two other impor- 
tant questions. The first concerns the incidence of cross- 
over voting in non-presidential primary elections. We 
have shown above (and just discussed in the previous 
paragraph) that we find little support for the argument 
that the incidence of crossover voting is higher in sub- 
presidential primaries. Also, using the ecological data, we 
have found that the incidence of crossover voting in 
visible state-wide races is not any greater or lesser than 
for less prominent state-wide races. 


Last, we have provided some evidence here that 
crossover voting in blanket primaries is not dramatically 
higher than in other types of open primary systems. 
Washington's blanket primary process is very similar to 
what might be instituted in California; using the ecologi- 
cal data we have provided evidence that the blanket 
primary in Washington does not produce levels of cross- 
over voting which are much different from levels of 
crossover voting in other types of open primaries (for 
example, in Ohio).

9 Conclusion 


In this report we have presented an extensive array of 
data analysis: spanning a variety of primary election 
years, different states, various types of primary election 
institutions, different types of data and different types of 
statistical analysis. Our purpose has been to determine 
what the extent of crossover voting is in states with open 
and blanket primaries. Additionally, we have also probed 
into the data further to understand what motivates indi- 
vidual voters to crossover and vote for candidates of the 
opposing party - in particular, whether these voters are 
doing so for strategic reasons. 


We began this report by discussing the relevant aca- 
demic literature on strategic voting in political behavior 
and on crossover voting in primary elections. There we 
noted that in this literature the estimated incidence of 
strategic political behavior, of crossover voting, and of 
primary election “raiding”, is relatively slight. Addi- 
tionally, we briefly mentioned some of the problems 
which we have observed with the empirical analysis in 
the literature. 


We then produced our own extensive analysis of both 
the publicly available survey data and of some aggre- 
gated electoral statistics from Ohio and Washington. 
Using different statistical approaches to analyzing these 
different databases, we have developed three conclusions 
about crossover voting in open and blanket primaries and 
about the potential for strategic voter behavior in open 
and blanket primaries: 


173 


1. there is very littler crossover voting in gen- 
eral in primary elections in the United 
States; 


2. the difference in the amount of crossover 
voting between states with open primaries 
and closed primaries is not substantively 
large; 


3. the amount of strategic behavior on the part 
of voters in primary elections is small. 


We believe that these conclusions shed light on what will 
happen when California voters encounter the new open 
primary system in the future. There is no reason to 
believe that the amount of crossover voting will be any 
different in California than in the many cases we have 
examined. Also, we believe that our results show that 
most of the crossover voters will be motivated to cast 
ballots for opposing party primary candidates simple 
because they prefer those candidates to the candidates 
offered in their own party’s primary, or they view their 
own party primary as a foregone conclusion and want the 
best possible set of candidates to choose from in the 
general election. We believe that few California primary 
election voters will engage pernicious raiding in the 
opposing party’s primary.

The purpose of this memorandum is to look at Proposi-

CALIFORNIA’S HISTORY OF ANTI-PARTYISM 


California is known as an “anti-party” state. It has a 
political System that gives its political parties form but 
little function. The state prefers weak political parties and 
has purposely kept them weak. Professors Bell and Price 


in California Government Today describe the situation very 
well: 


In theory the British monarch presides over the 
British Commonwealth of nations; and in theory 
California's political parties are directed and 
coordinated by a three tiered System of state 
conventions, state central committees, and 
county central committees. But in reality none of 
this happens. For the most part, California’s 


181 


official party organizations play only a minor 
role in influencing and shaping state politics.! 


Bell and Price go on to note that “The main reason 
California’s political parties have traditionally been so 
weak and ineffective hinges largely on key historical 
features.” California began as a Republican state in the 
years following the Civil War, largely because California 
has sided with the Union and was populated by people 
from Republican leaning states. As political machines 
developed in the late 19th Century, they developed 
within the context of the Republican party. California also 
experienced much of the Gilded Age political corruption 
associated with late 19th Century urban organizations, 
especially the political machine dominated by the South- 
ern Pacific Railroad. Muckraking journalist Frank Norris 
described the railroad in The Octopus as “the leviathan, 
with tentacles of steel clutching into the soil, the soul less 
force, the iron charted power, the monster, the octopus.” 


In the early 20th Century California became imbued with 
the Progressive Reforms that promised to end political 
control by the railroad. When the San Francisco district 
attorney was assassinated in the courtroom while pros- 
ecuting the city’s notorious boss, Abe Ruef, his task was 
taken over by Hiram Johnson, an active member of the 
Lincoln Roosevelt League of Republican reformers. Most 
political contests took place within the Republican party, 
and Johnson captured that party’s nomination for gover- 
nor in 1910, and went on to be elected. No more anti- 
party California politician every existed; he set out to

Approximately one quarter of California voters are effec- 
tively precluded from participation in the election of their 
state legislator or Member of Congress because they are 
not a member of the dominant political party in a district 
“safe” for that party. 


In these districts, the decision as to who will be elected is 
made at the primary election. Under the closed primary 
system, voters who do not belong to the dominant party 
cannot vote in the primary. Their vote is meaningless in 
the general election because the district is not competitive 
between the parties. 


A district may be considered “safe” if it meets several 
criteria: 


e The district has not been contested between the par-

e The area covered by the district has been historically 
dominated by one party,

One measure of a “safe” district is its rating by the 
California Target Book, a nonpartisan analysis of all Cali- 
fornia legislative and Congressional districts. The Target 
Book rates districts as “target”, “strong Democrat/strong 
Republican”, and “safe Democrat/safe Republican”. All 
districts considered “safe” in this report were rated 
“safe” in both 1994 and 1996 by the Target Book, and are 
rated “safe” for the incumbent party in the 1998 election’.

districts can be rated as “safe.” There are several reason

ACTIVITIES OF THE POLITICAL PARTIES 


Despite historically weak party organizations, the parties 
do have one advantage in California politics. Because of 
their tax status, they are able to mail political literature at 
reduced mail rates. Therefore, it has been a common 


195 


practice of political consultants to use the party’s mailing 
permit for much of their direct mail campaign activity. A 
party paid mailer is also able to give a candidate some 
cover if the mailer is a negative mail piece critical of the 
opponent. In the 1996 election, the Democratic State Cen- 
tral Committee paid for a large number of negative 
mailers in targeted districts. 


The parties are also effective fundraisers; presidents and 
governors often appear at party sponsored fund raising 
dinners. 


Among the other activities undertaken by state and 
county central committees are: 


e Voter registration efforts 
e Get out the vote drives on election day 
e Conventions and the adoption of party platforms 


e Mailing of materials urging a vote for the party’s 
candidate and ballot positions 


e Sponsorship of debates, periodical meetings, organiza- 
tional efforts. 


None of these activities constitute exercise of any political 
authority; the fund raising and campaign spending is 
largely a “passthrough” in support of the party’s candi- 
dates. All these activities are organizational in nature, 
intended to increase the electability of the nominees of 
the party, and none are interfered within any way by 
Proposition 198. 


What then is the burden imposed by Proposition 198 on 
the political parties? There is none; the party central 
committees may still meet, debate issues, raise funds and 


196 


do the other non-policy chores they have done for a 
century. Their political power is not diminished by Prop- 
osition 198 because they have no political power. The 
burden, if there is any, falls on statewide candidates and 
legislators who must appeal more broadly in the primary 
election, and thus campaign to a larger constituency than 


was the case under the closed primary. This burden, 
however, does not fall on the parties.
