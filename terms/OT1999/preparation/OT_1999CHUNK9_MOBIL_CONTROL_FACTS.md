## Review posture and decisions below

Certiorari under 28 U.S.C. §1254(1) reviews the Federal Circuit's revised May 13, 1999 opinion, 177 F.3d 1331, No. 97-5146. It supersedes the October 15, 1998 opinion at 158 F.3d 1253. Plager wrote for himself and Rader; Newman dissented. The revised judgment reversed the Court of Federal Claims' liability judgment at 35 Fed. Cl. 309 (1996) and subsequent July 24, 1997 restitution judgment. The actual ruling under review is not an affirmance of contractual liability with only arithmetic left.

On cross-motions for summary judgment, with neither side identifying a material factual dispute for that decision, the trial court found an undertaking to consider properly submitted exploration plans timely and fairly, a material breach/repudiation through the Outer Banks Protection Act (OBPA), and no sovereign-acts defense. After a benefits hearing it awarded return of the bonus payments, exceeding $156 million for both plaintiffs, but denied annual rental recovery. It found no legally cognizable offset established by the Government. The Federal Circuit did not reach the Government's challenges to calculation of restitution because it found no breach warranting relief. Reported rounded figures of $156 million and $158 million should not be converted into an exact final award; the precise judgment and any proper offsets govern.

The Court of Federal Claims litigation originally involved other lessees and areas; settlements left these plaintiffs' North Carolina OBPA claims. An originally pleaded takings theory is not before the Court. A separate District of Columbia action reviewing Commerce's CZMA override decision is not this contract appeal. Do not decide that administrative challenge or require Commerce to override North Carolina as contract relief.

## Lease and statutory obligations

Mobil and Marathon each acquired approximately one-third interests in five North Carolina leases and paid more than $78 million in bonuses, with annual rentals separately due. Four leases were part of the Manteo Unit; the fifth had no submitted exploration plan. Each lease had a ten-year primary term, continued production/drilling provisions, and conditional exclusive exploration/development rights. It was not a guarantee of discovery, profitable production, unconditional plan approval, or every other permit.

Section 1 incorporates the Outer Continental Shelf Lands Act (OCSLA), §§302–303 of the Department of Energy Organization Act, regulations issued under those statutes existing when the lease took effect, future regulations under those statutes for prevention of waste, conservation of OCS natural resources and protection of correlative rights, and other applicable statutes and regulations. Preserve the named sources and future-regulation qualifications; do not paraphrase this as immunity from all future regulation. Sections 9–10 require operation under approved plans and compliance with exploration/development/production regulations and orders. Section 13(a) permits suspension or cancellation pursuant to OCSLA §5 and compensation where the Act provides it. Section 13(b) separately addresses wartime/national-emergency suspension on the Defense Secretary's recommendation with just compensation. Section 19 reserves ungranted rights, with particular noninterference and national-defense qualifications. Those reservations do not themselves answer whether the specified contracted approval process could be replaced without contract consequences.

Under 43 U.S.C. §1340(c)(1), an exploration plan must comply with OCSLA, its regulations and lease provisions; following necessary modifications, approval is due within thirty days of submission unless the statutory serious-harm condition, not avoidable by modification, requires disapproval. The harm categories include life (including aquatic life), property, minerals, national security/defense, and marine, coastal or human environment. Separate provisions authorize suspension/cancellation on their own predicates. Section 1334(a)(1)(A)'s national-interest/proper-development/transportation suspension is at the lessee's request; §1334(a)(1)(B) addresses threatened serious, irreparable or immediate harm. Do not remove the lessee-request condition from (A) or make every general environmental concern a finding satisfying (B). The Government also relies on the environmental-analysis/NEPA regulation, former 30 C.F.R. §250.10(b)(4), later recodified §250.110(b)(4), and the inordinate-permitting-delay provision (b)(6). Whether these incorporated powers authorize the actual disputed postponement is contested.

Approval of a plan is distinct from permission to carry it out. Under OCSLA §1340(c)(2) and CZMA §307(c)(3), operations requiring federal licenses/permits and affecting the coastal zone need state consistency concurrence, conclusive presumed concurrence under the actual statutory conditions, or a Commerce override based on consistency with CZMA objectives or national security. No presumed concurrence is established here. Discharges separately require an EPA NPDES permit and the applicable CZMA process; a drilling permit and later development/production approval also remain necessary. The plan-approval claim cannot silently become an entitlement to those permits. The statutory cancellation-and-compensation route is conditional; the Federal Circuit found it inapplicable and said the lessees did not claim its predicates were met, while Newman's dissent invoked it by analogy/support. No actual statutory cancellation finding is supplied.

## Chronology and competing governmental determinations

In July 1989 Mobil, North Carolina and Interior entered an MOU for additional environmental review of a draft exploration plan and lease suspensions. The MOU promised additional suspensions upon application for specified state objections, administrative/judicial challenges, appeals or regulatory delays, with two drilling seasons after their resolution. The draft plan was submitted in 1989; Interior's extensive report was completed in June 1990. Interior found the proposed well had no significant environmental impact under its review and deemed the plan approvable under its criteria. Those determinations do not erase North Carolina's distinct data and coastal-consistency objections.

North Carolina formally objected to the NPDES consistency certification in July 1990. Some sources date the letter July 16 and the lower opinion says July 17; no decisive one-day timing question is presented. On August 18, 1990 Congress enacted OBPA. Mobil submitted its final plan August 20. North Carolina objected to the plan's CZMA certification November 19. Both state objections remained unresolved during the material period, and Commerce declined override on September 2, 1994 because the requisite findings were unsupported. Whether OBPA materially deprived the companies of the separately purchased process, despite those barriers, is the contract question, not a settled causal fact.

OBPA §6003(c) prohibited new lease sales/issuance, exploration-plan and development/production-plan approval, drilling-permit approval and drilling on the defined North Carolina OCS. It lasted until the later of October 1, 1991, or forty-five days of continuous congressional session after an Interior report, following consideration of the scientific panel, certifying sufficient information for OCSLA responsibilities and explaining/justifying differences from the panel. Continuous session has its own adjournment exclusions; this was not simply forty-five calendar days. The panel recommended studies and information; it was not granted final permit authority. The Act required further environmental studies and consultation, but allowed the Secretary to disagree with panel recommendations through the stated explanation process.

The panel reported in January 1992. Interior's April 1992 report certified information sufficient for the first proposed exploratory well while withholding approval pending two additional studies. The lower majority treated the statutory bar for that plan as lifted after the required congressional period and later work as the Secretary's own initiative. Petitioners dispute that later work was independent of OBPA and emphasize future stages still needing statutory certification. The Government stresses that no other plans were before Interior and denies an indefinite ban on every future proposal. Studies were transmitted in July 1994. A general suspension was terminated in November 1994 after the studies and Commerce decision. OBPA was repealed in 1996. Repeal is a subsequent event, not proof that any prior breach was or was not material or retracted in time.

## Lower judgment and strongest litigation positions

The Federal Circuit agreed that the catchall lease language did not generally incorporate a future enactment such as OBPA; it declined an interpretation making promised rights illusory. It nonetheless held that North Carolina's independent, unresolved objections prevented exploration, that the lessees assumed the original permit risks, and that OBPA was not the operative cause of their inability to drill. It concluded no breach or basis for restitution had been established. It rejected a general OCSLA right to compensation whenever exploration proves unavailable. That is the complete lower path: its agreement on the future-statute clause is not a concession of material breach.

Newman dissented because the purchased timely-and-fair consideration entitlement had been negated, and ordinary contract/restitution principles did not permit the Government to retain the consideration after preventing the promised performance. She distinguished exploratory failure after performance from withholding the contracted process, and did not assert that every proposed plan must be approved. Her opinions' original and revised versions must not be conflated; the revised May 1999 dissent is the operative one.

Petitioners argue that the timely original approval process was a material part of what the bonuses bought; OBPA imposed new prerequisites across all six regulated activities, initially barring performance for at least thirteen months and practically prolonging it. They seek restoration of payments for a repudiated bargain, rather than expectation profits requiring proof that oil would have been found or permits ultimately obtained. Independent state objections, the possibility of a losing bargain and post-suit repeal therefore do not automatically defeat restitution. On the fifth tract, they invoke anticipatory repudiation of the same approval undertaking rather than a fiction that an application was submitted.

The Government argues that the leases already permitted environmental study and suspension, that Congress merely required the contracting agency to use existing powers, and that the priority to seek eventual development—not an inflexible timetable for one administrative step—was the essential bargain. It separately argues no material deprivation because the CZMA/NPDES barriers would have blocked operations regardless of OBPA. It distinguishes breach/materiality from causation of expectation damages and argues that restitution cannot follow every technical delay. Its reliance on broad national-interest suspension must be tested with the lessee-request qualification above. The source packet does not convert either party's construction into an operative rule.

## Waiver, continued performance and remedy defenses

The Government's independent waiver/election defense relies on submission of the final plan after enactment, administrative and judicial efforts to overcome the CZMA objections, and requests for continued suspensions and extensions. It says these demanded and accepted performance, induced continued governmental expense and affirmed the contracts, leaving at most partial-breach damages. Petitioners distinguish urging performance/retraction from acceptance of materially useful performance, invoke the preexisting MOU obligation, deny that an unapproved plan or unsuccessful override delivered the promised approval process, and identify their 1992 suit as an election once the scope of the breach was clear. Mobil also disputes preservation of some newly framed waiver grounds and reliance on correspondence outside the trial record. These are arguments requiring resolution at the available record's actual scope, not an established forfeiture or a presumed intentional waiver.

The suspension benefits require particular care. The Government's January 2000 brief broadly characterizes the extensions as continuously rent-free. Its own appended June 2, 1995 MMS letter, however, approves new suspensions effective November 10, 1994 pending final nonappealable judgment, caps them at five years under §250.10(e), and expressly requires rentals under §218.154(b)(2). Those express terms qualify the narrative characterization. Do not assume that the 1995 letter itself established continuing suspension or rental relief through June 2000. A distinct renewal or actual payment history would be needed if that proposition determines waiver or an offset. Earlier directed suspensions did waive rentals, and extensions of term may have value independently of rent. The distinction can be reserved if the legal waiver ground fails even treating the documented requests/extensions as given.

If breach and restitution entitlement are found, the Government expressly requests remand for its unaddressed challenges to the amount. Restoration must account for actual returned rights and legally cognizable benefits; the trial court's bonus-only award and rental denial are distinct. No profits, interest, automatic return of all rentals, guaranteed permit, legislative injunction or order nullifying North Carolina's objection follows from the process claim. Interest against the United States requires separate legal authority. A narrow liability or entitlement decision can leave only genuinely unresolved monetary issues; it need not order a new evidentiary hearing if the existing record suffices. Affirmance can rest on a valid no-breach, no-material-breach or independently supported waiver ground, with any remand matched to the ground actually adopted. No such path is selected here.

## Law entering and each Associate's own sources

The focused law slice copies actual simulated Winstar and the reusable rule, effective July 1, 1996. An authorized undertaking and its exact risk allocation are essential; general regulation or fairness supplies no contract. Congress retains regulatory power, while an actual assumed risk may carry monetary responsibility. Causation, loss, terms, duration and remedies remain separate. The majority does not establish a universal unmistakability exemption for money claims or a comprehensive substantial-contractual-effect exception to sovereign acts.

Souter, Stevens, O'Connor and Breyer adopted Winstar's limited unmistakability threshold account; Souter, Stevens and Breyer also adopted its separate substantial-effect account, which is noncontrolling. Scalia's own concurrence, joined by Kennedy and Thomas, applies unmistakability and reads the whole contract to find the particular undertaking; it recognizes that immediate regulatory permission could be a different bargain. Ginsburg's dissent would require contract-by-contract reconsideration of continuing change-of-law risk under unmistakability rather than treating valuable immediate permission as necessarily a longer promise. Seven Associates nevertheless joined the common assumed-risk holding; Ginsburg accepted actual-authorization requirements but disputed the identified continuing promise. These exact prior public positions are appended to the slice and support individual examination without supplying current votes.
