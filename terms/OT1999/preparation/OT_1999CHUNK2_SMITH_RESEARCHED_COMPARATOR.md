## Historical controlling propositions and limits

1. **Anders is not an exclusive procedural prescription.** Anders supplies a prophylactic framework protecting the underlying Douglas right to appellate counsel; states may adopt different procedures that adequately safeguard it. The Court treated Anders' concluding procedural description as unnecessary to its holding; McCoy approved a variation, Finley calls the framework prophylactic rather than an independent constitutional command, and Penson's invalid procedure failed Douglas without resolving whether every Anders detail was mandatory. Federalism and Griffin leave states procedural discretion within Fourteenth Amendment limits. The Court did not overrule Anders, Douglas, McCoy, or Penson in terms.

2. **Wende satisfies the Fourteenth Amendment.** Adequate and effective review reasonably ensures resolution related to the appeal's merits. The right to appellate counsel does not require subsidy or merits advocacy of a frivolous appeal, but every indigent still has a right to a lawyer zealous for his interests who evaluates the case and seeks nonfrivolous arguments (p. 278 n.10). Wende requires counsel's procedural/factual summary with record citations and attestations; counsel remains available rather than withdrawing; the court independently examines the entire record; both find no arguable/nonfrivolous issue before affirmance; and the court orders briefing if it finds one. Those protections distinguish a mere no-merit letter, the denial of counsel during decisional review, and a single review tier. Wende need not be optimal policy; the Court did not resolve competing policy assessments of judicial workload, counsel's ethical duties, or resource allocation. Substantial equality does not require absolute equality (p.277 n.9).

3. **Individual ineffectiveness remains open under both Strickland prongs.** Validity of the procedure does not determine that Robbins' appeal actually was frivolous. He must show that counsel unreasonably failed to discover a nonfrivolous issue and file a merits brief, and a reasonable probability that he would have prevailed on appeal absent that failure. A finding of one nonfrivolous issue a reasonably competent lawyer would have discovered suffices on performance when no merits brief was filed; this differs from selecting among issues in a merits brief under Jones v. Barnes. Prejudice remains the same in both settings. Either Strickland prong may be considered first (p.286 n.14).

4. **No automatic prejudice on this record.** Appointed counsel complied with a constitutionally valid procedure and never withdrew; there was no complete actual or constructive denial, no state interference, and no actual conflict shown. Penson involved a materially different deprivation. Actual conflict ordinarily requires adverse effect even where prejudice is presumed. The Court expressly left open whether supervisory rules might justify per se prejudice for analogous errors on direct appeal from federal convictions (pp.288–289 n.16).

5. **Remand scope matters.** The district court and Ninth Circuit identified prison-library adequacy and refusal to permit withdrawal of the trial-counsel waiver as potentially arguable issues, but the term could mean either nonfrivolous or merely the weaker Anders formulation. Their strength therefore remained to be clarified. The State's contention that the library issue was unpreserved because Robbins failed to object at trial was not addressed below; the Court did not resolve it. The habeas petition predated AEDPA and its amendments do not govern (p.267 n.3). The Ninth Circuit had sent the case back to consider eleven trial-error claims because success on a trial claim could make a new appeal unnecessary. The Supreme Court did not decide any of those claims on its merits.

## Every separate writing

**Stevens, joined by Ginsburg.** He joins Souter fully and adds stare decisis and institutional reasons. In his view the majority's broad merit-related criterion would also validate procedures already rejected in Anders and Penson and therefore effectively overrules them. California had defended the old procedure by invoking counsel's and the court's record review, so that feature cannot distinguish it. McCoy's requirement that counsel write reasons for rejecting arguable issues is a safeguard: articulating a legal analysis can reveal overlooked merit, require diligence, and aid judicial review. A factual and procedural summary does not perform that task. Review by a judicial law clerk does not give an indigent substantially equal advocacy to retained counsel. He rejects the majority's suggestion that McCoy's reason-giving requirement provided less effective advocacy.

**Souter, joined by Stevens, Ginsburg, and Breyer.** He accepts that the Constitution does not require an appeal and does not demand briefing of frivolous issues. But when a state permits first appeals and retained advocacy, equal protection and due process demand substantially equal partisan review for indigents. Counsel must first examine the record as an interested advocate and then brief any nonfrivolous claim; the first service is an irreducible obligation even when the second proves unnecessary. Anders' issue-spotting brief tests counsel's diligence and gives judges a focused basis to review both that diligence and the frivolity assessment. Souter does not claim every Anders detail is exclusive: it is the constitutional benchmark. Wende neither requires an affirmative, reviewable product of partisan analysis nor enables meaningful review of it; supervision by a project attorney cannot replace that product. A judge's independent review is an inquisitorial substitute, and the amicus description of delegation to staff attorneys reinforces the concern; he does not decide whether judicial workload independently rises to constitutional significance.

On remedy, Souter would require reinstatement of the appeal without requiring proof that particular issues would prevail. A Wende-only summary makes counsel function as amicus rather than advocate and is a constructive denial closer to Cronic/Penson than deficient performance under Strickland. The state likewise could not cure this deprivation by harmless-error proof under Chapman or Brecht. This does not promise relief whenever any Wende-labeled brief was filed: a brief actually meeting Anders standards would receive ordinary Strickland analysis, and limitations periods remain (p.302 n.7). Teague does not bar applying already announced Griffin/Douglas/Anders principles; their application to a new violation is not itself a new rule. He similarly considers the rule clearly established for AEDPA purposes, while acknowledging this petition is pre-AEDPA.
