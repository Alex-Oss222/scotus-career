## Governing enactment and material findings

Proposition 198 was adopted in March 1996. Elections Code §2001 gives otherwise qualified electors, including unaffiliated voters, the right to vote for candidates regardless of political affiliation, subject to other law. Conforming amendments affect §§2151, 13102, 13203, 13206, 13230, 13300, 13301 and 13302. Each voter receives the same ballot, candidates appear with party identification, and the voter can cross party lines office by office. Section 15451 makes each party's highest vote recipient its nominee at the general election. Thus this is a partisan nomination system, distinct from a nonpartisan first round advancing the highest candidates without choosing an official nominee for each party. Party committee elections remain limited to registrants of the relevant party. All four plaintiff parties—the Democratic, Republican, Libertarian, and Peace and Freedom parties—had bylaws opposing outsider voting in their primaries.

The trial court found a significant but nonsevere associational burden. That is a constitutional characterization to be reviewed, not a binding historical fact deciding the present legal issue. Its underlying findings included:

- Crossover voting would increase. Evidence from Washington suggested roughly ten percent per office, sometimes twenty to twenty-five percent; some studies distinguished independents from members of competing parties. The California prediction was necessarily uncertain. Do not mix study denominators or turn those estimates into established California election totals.
- Some races over time probably would be decided by crossover voters, especially where competition was asymmetric. The possibility could influence campaigns and officeholders even without changing a particular winner. Campaign costs could rise, discipline could weaken, and activists' morale could decline.
- Organized raiding to nominate a weak opposing candidate was not shown likely to be important. Sincere voting for a preferred candidate and strategic voting for a second choice were separate phenomena. Rare raiding does not establish absence of outsider influence.
- Major parties remained vigorous in Washington. The court did not expect serious organizational debilitation; endorsements, campaigning, financial support, platforms, registration and turnout work remained available. Nominees' participation in governing committees could be addressed by party rules. Those alternatives do not literally restore control of the legal nomination.
- The court found the special effects alleged for minor parties inadequately proved, noting infrequent contested primaries and the different incentives of major-party voters. This was a finding on the trial showing, not a rule that minor-party autonomy has no protection.
- Proposition 198 received substantial voter support, but the court expressly rejected the contention that polling of party registrants constituted the parties' consent. Party rules prescribe their own deliberative amendment procedures; inferred popular approval is not their replacement.

## Reasoning below and both sides

The lower courts applied the Anderson–Burdick balancing inquiry, relying on the actual system and evidence rather than treating every outsider vote as automatically fatal. They characterized primaries as the public first stage in selection of officeholders, distinguished party governance from state election machinery, and treated compulsory registration as already an imperfect measure of political association. They found the burden significant but short of severe and the aggregate state interests sufficient: representativeness, broader debate, participation of independents and minority-party voters in safe districts, greater choice, competition and turnout, perceived fairness, political stability, and privacy. Their description of certain voters as effectively disenfranchised meant limited practical influence in safe districts, not legal ineligibility to register or vote. Empirical support for increased competitiveness and turnout was qualified. The courts rejected mere state paternalism about what would help a party, yet accepted electorate-centered representativeness and participation interests.

The parties' supported argument is that selection of the official standard bearer defines their political message and is a core associational act. Compulsory participation by people who retain an opposing affiliation can change that act even when their votes are sincere. Tashjian, Eu and La Follette protect a party's choice of participants; ordinary election regulation and the actual Timmons fusion restriction do not necessarily transfer the selection decision to outsiders. Endorsing a different candidate cannot undo the statutory nominee's status. The change in the nominating electorate is the asserted injury, so lack of proof that parties will collapse is not dispositive. A government desire to moderate a party's positions can itself be the burden challenged. The parties acknowledge lawful administration and primary requirements, subject to constitutional limits; their private-association analogy does not erase the white-primary cases.

California and the defending intervenors argue that public, state-financed nomination is an integral governmental electoral function carrying ballot-access advantages, not simply a private club's meeting. State authority to structure that function and electors' participation interests deserve weight. Tashjian addressed a party's invitation to independents, Eu internal governance and endorsements, and La Follette national-convention delegate rules; none automatically decides this blanket primary. California stresses the trial findings, the survival of party advocacy, the small practical differences among blanket, open and easily changed registration systems, the nondiscriminatory application to parties, and privacy without a public party declaration. Its merits brief asserts later participation improvements; those are litigation assertions unless their evidentiary basis is independently established, not substituted trial findings. The trial court's rejection of implied party consent must accompany any reliance on popular approval.

Analytical possibilities under the entering law include sustaining the system after actual-burden review; finding severe compelled association and testing precise interests and fit; or finding an insufficient fit even on a less-than-severe classification. These are available arguments, not vote predictions. The lawful distinction between electoral administration and control of a party's own official nominee must be addressed whichever path is selected. A source showing conventional open primaries or nonpartisan systems exist is not their constitutional adjudication in this case.

## Scope, remedy and source limits

The case concerns state-office and congressional nominations.
