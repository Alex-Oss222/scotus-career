# Playboy lower-opinion findings — source preparation only

This draft records the contents and limits of the December 28, 1998 district court opinion, *Playboy Entertainment Group, Inc. v. United States*, 30 F. Supp. 2d 702 (D. Del. 1998), No. Civ.A. 96-94-JJF. It is not a validated neutral handoff, a new adjudication, or a recommendation. A fresh source validator must check the bounded primary text before substantive use. The lower court's historical discussion of Supreme Court precedent does not establish the force of that precedent under the authorized entering law.

## What blocking required and what the court found

1. **Separate mechanisms.** The district opinion describes §505 as requiring full video-and-audio scrambling or blocking for the covered sexually oriented channels, with FCC-selected time channeling until that requirement is met. It describes §504 as requiring, on a cable subscriber's request and without charge to that subscriber, complete blocking of any programming the subscriber wants blocked, sexually explicit or otherwise. The operator bears the cost under both arrangements. The court treated §504's subscriber selection as content-neutral. Its statutory discussion does not make its later publicity proposal part of Congress's text. See findings 1, 10, 20; conclusions under “Least Restrictive Alternative Analysis”; notes 2, 8, and 18.

2. **Technology is specific.** Finding 8 says analog RF/baseband scrambling could allow signal bleed. In the systems described, adding positive or negative traps to that scrambling prevented bleed, as did channel-mapping converters and digital transmission. These are findings about the identified arrangements, not a finding that every device marketed as a lockbox always works. Finding 24 separately records Government contentions that fulfillment may take weeks, a device may fail, and a channel reassignment can defeat an existing block. The concluding discussion does not supply a quantified failure rate, a maximum fulfillment deadline, or a separate factual resolution of each example. Modern TV/VCR lockout and V-chip features are mentioned in finding 10, without making them substitutes for the operator's statutory duty.

3. **Outside installation matters.** Finding 7(c) gives $12–$15 as the negative-trap device cost. Note 21 distinguishes the installed-cost assumption used in the economic evidence and rejects Playboy's suggestion that mailing a negative trap would avoid installation labor: the experts agreed these traps were installed on the outside cable or pole. The record therefore does not establish a universally effective remedy consisting simply of mailing a cheap device for household self-installation.

4. **Evidence of exposure.** Findings 11–12 distinguish estimated households with the potential for bleed from proved actual exposure. The Government did not quantify actual exposed households and produced limited complaints. The complaint recitation says the operators offered or provided free correction in every listed case except one, at varying speeds; it does not say every complaint was promptly or successfully resolved. One recited incident concerned a child exposed at a friend's house despite the parent's home lockout. The court ultimately found sufficient risk of harm to susceptible minors to warrant protection while describing considerable weaknesses in the harm evidence. Neither universal exposure nor absence of harm is its finding.

5. **Actual systemwide response.** Finding 18 says the large majority of surveyed operators chose time channeling and neither side identified an operator adopting double scrambling to comply with §505. The main text gives 69% in one survey; note 14 describes 68% of surveyed operators carrying adult programming. Preserve that modest numerical inconsistency rather than inventing a precise reconciled figure. Finding 19 treats daytime restriction as a significant loss of speaking and viewing opportunity, with 30–50% of adult programming viewed before 10 p.m. The opinion does not make the disputed dollar-loss estimates essential to its analysis. Note 23's unanimous-choice statement addresses operators with incomplete scrambling facing the specified expensive alternatives, not every operator nationwide.

## The observation period and its limitations

Finding 21 describes an approximately fourteen-month period during which §504 operated without §505 enforcement. The Government survey reported devices **distributed** to fewer than one-half of one percent of subscribers. That is not, in the opinion, a direct measurement of requests received, requests refused, parents notified, failures to install, or children protected. The survey covered 79 operators of roughly 6,000, with 62 answering the distribution question. Note 19 says asking for distributions since the earliest known date could include devices distributed under an earlier paid-device provision and thus slightly overstate §504's observed effect.

The court identifies two reasons the low distribution rate did not establish ineffectiveness: it was consistent both with ineffective implementation and with limited parental concern, and the record did not clearly establish that adequate notice had been given. Playboy's claim that parents knew of bleed and chose not to act is identified as its argument, not adopted as a proven general fact about parents. The source supports a conditional comparison after adequate publicity, not an empirical finding that a well-publicized fourteen-month trial had already succeeded.

The opinion's procedural/date recitations themselves require care. The procedural account says the temporary restraining order was entered March 7, 1996, while finding 21 describes a March 6 start. Finding 21 also describes both provisions as taking effect March 9, 1996. This preparation does not use those recitations to override the separately supplied enacted effective dates in entering law, which distinguishes §504 from §505. The independent enacted text has not been re-researched here.

## The exact notice arrangement

The court says adequate notice is crucial and **suggests** the following content: identify channels transmitting sexually oriented programming; explain that partially discernible video and full audio can appear through signal bleed; explain that children can encounter it without parental knowledge or permission; state that blocking devices are free; and state that a subscriber may telephone the operator to request a free device for the offending channel.

Its examples of appropriate media are billing inserts, barker or preview channels, and advertisements on channels other than the sexually explicit channel. These are examples, not a command to use every listed medium simultaneously. Notice should recur regularly at reasonable intervals. When an operator changes the channel carrying sexually explicit programming, the court specifies a special mailed notice to subscribers who requested a lockbox. It does not specify automatic reconfiguration, replacement, testing, or an installation deadline; those should not be added to its arrangement by inference.

The court expressly says it lacks jurisdiction over the cable operators to command notice directly. Instead, it states that it will require Playboy, in its contracts with operators, to ensure adequate notice of §504 devices. It conditions §504's status as a viable alternative on that adequate notice. The opinion does not establish that every contract had already been changed, that every household had already received the notice, or that the contractual requirement independently binds nonparty programmers. Nor does its statement that §504 would cease to be viable without notice specify an automatic restoration or enforcement procedure for §505.

## Comparative effectiveness and economic qualifications

The court compares §504 **with adequate notice** to §505, not an unpublicized request right alone. It says parents can decide whether bleed concerns them, that the arrangement is effective for a concerned parent, and that it would appear as effective as §505 **for those concerned about bleed**, while less restrictive of speech. It acknowledges dependence on parental vigilance, determined minors' access, and the absence of a perfect solution under either regime. Its observation about access during safe-harbor hours should not be converted into a finding that §505 permits daytime bleed when full blocking or time channeling is obeyed.

The economic evidence in finding 25 is materially limiting. On the specified revenue/cost assumptions, the Government expert calculated that distributing devices to roughly 3% of subscribers would exhaust the adult-channel revenue used in the first calculation, rising to 6% on the five-year revenue calculation. Playboy's witness also supplied much higher costs for a hypothetical wide distribution than the estimated adult-network revenues. These are feasibility calculations under assumptions, not a prediction that precisely 3%, 6%, or 56% of households would request devices after adequate notice. The court preserves operators' right to stop carrying the programming if uneconomic and states that Playboy and other providers have incentives to make device distribution feasible. It does not find that unlimited device distribution is costless or guaranteed profitable.

The source also states, in finding 22, that Playboy must demonstrate efficacy to establish its alternative claim, while the conclusions place the constitutional least-restrictive-means burden on the Government. Both passages are preserved in the copied excerpts. They should not be silently rewritten into a different source allocation of burden. The district court ultimately declares §505 unconstitutional and enjoins enforcement on the less-restrictive-alternative ground; it expressly does not decide equal protection or vagueness. This reports only the lower judgment under review.
