## Boy Scouts of America v. Dale - No. 99-699

**Event and question.** Merits consideration June 28, 2000; argued April 26, 2000. Certiorari to the New Jersey Supreme Court. The federal question concerns compelled retention of James Dale as an adult assistant scoutmaster under New Jersey's Law Against Discrimination (LAD), and the claimed freedoms of speech and association. The petition's own wording is copied in the source extracts. The approved question is expressive association; direct compelled-expression and intimate-association arguments were actually advanced and need their appropriate scope addressed. This is not an abstract facial challenge to all public-accommodations laws.

**Law and judgment below.** Dale v. Boy Scouts of America, 160 N.J. 562, 734 A.2d 1196 (Aug. 4, 1999), affirmed the Appellate Division and remanded. New Jersey construed its LAD to cover BSA as a place of public accommodation notwithstanding the absence of one fixed location; broad solicitation, educational/recreational activities, public partnerships and actual admission practices supported that conclusion. The court rejected the distinctly-private exemption and held that sexual-orientation discrimination in the adult-leader privilege violated the statute. That state-law coverage determination is distinct from whether the federal Constitution forbids this application. The common-law claim remained dismissed as duplicative; the federal question does not independently revive it.

The state court rejected intimate association in light of the organization's actual openness, recruitment, size and relationships; it rejected expressive association because, in its assessment, admitting Dale would not significantly impair members' shared expressive purposes. It relied on the absence of a communicated anti-homosexuality teaching in the ordinary program, the direction to leave sex education to parents and spiritual advisers, and diversity among sponsors and members. It alternatively regarded the State's interest in eliminating discrimination in public accommodations as compelling and treated Dale's inclusion as status-based access rather than insertion of a message into a parade. These are the state court's legal and record characterizations, subject to the appropriate First Amendment review; they are not unchallengeable independent findings that settle expression or material burden.

Justice Handler separately concurred, emphasizing genuine admission selectivity and the distinction between disclosure of identity and advocacy imposed through an organizational role. At the intermediate appellate stage Judge Landau agreed with membership restoration but dissented from compelled appointment to leadership, emphasizing communication by adult example. BSA disputes that membership-only relief is feasible because adult membership exists for leadership service. Preserve this remedial and expressive-role dispute rather than silently substitute general youth membership for the adult position presented.

**Material record and disputed inferences.** Dale joined Cub Scouts in 1978, became a Boy Scout, attained Eagle Scout, and was accepted as an assistant scoutmaster in March 1989. While at Rutgers he became co-president of the Lesbian/Gay Alliance. A July 1990 newspaper article identified that position and his participation in a seminar about gay teenagers' needs. BSA revoked his adult membership that month. The initial explanation invoked standards forbidding membership to homosexuals; later correspondence and litigation emphasized avowed homosexuality, leadership example and BSA's moral position. The newspaper article did not identify Dale as a Scout leader or state that he proposed to teach a contrary message within Scouting. This does not establish a categorical rule that public self-identification can never affect a leadership message.

BSA's oath requires members to remain morally straight; its law includes being clean. Adult leaders subscribe to those commitments and the religious-principle declaration. BSA's materials describe teaching values through activity and adult example. BSA relies on official position statements from 1978, 1991, 1992 and 1993 and officer testimony that homosexual conduct conflicts with its moral code. The petitioners' brief candidly states that materials directed to boys do not mention homosexuality or denounce homosexual conduct; it argues that positive teaching and example need not catalogue forbidden conduct. The 1991 statement predates this suit, while some other statements postdate the expulsion. Do not flatten these different dates into either a wholly invented litigation position or a universally communicated historical teaching. The record permits a dispute about what the documents establish, who received them, and what the particular leader role communicates.

**Actually supported competing positions.** BSA contends that courts may not revise the group's own moral message or demand universal member agreement, continual public advocacy, or a single founding purpose before protecting it. A uniformed adult entrusted with teaching by example is different from an ordinary customer; forced leadership communicates acceptance contrary to the organization's chosen values. It invokes Hurley, Roberts (including O'Connor's discussion of education and values), Rotary, and the troop-level relationships of parents, leaders and children. It argues that a membership-only remedy misdescribes the adult role.

Dale contends that a large civic organization's assertion of a discriminatory policy is not itself proof that excluding him protects a genuine message or that admitting him materially changes attributable expression. The ordinary materials, sex-education instructions, diversity and absence of proposed contrary Scouting advocacy undermine the claimed expressive conflict. He invokes Roberts, Rotary and New York State Club Association, distinguishes Hurley's identified bannered contingent, and supports neutral antidiscrimination enforcement with the State's concrete access interest. His brief also invokes public entwinement as strengthening that interest. Do not convert public assistance or a federal charter alone into a finding that BSA is a state actor.

**Entering-law forks and limits.** The actual Hurley holding protects composition of a private expressive presentation and expressly reserves ordinary identity-based access and materially different associational disputes. It also preserves independent review of intertwined expression characterizations with credibility deference. Determine (a) the actual expressive activity, (b) the particular role and claimed attributable burden, (c) the applicable constitutional justification if a material burden is established, and (d) the proper remedy. Neither uncritical deference to any exclusion nor judicial substitution of approved values follows from Hurley. Affirmance, reversal of the compelled-leadership application, or a bounded remand each requires its own supported premise; no vote is proposed here. The June 26 Jones public holding must be added and its nomination setting distinguished before modeling.

**Justice-specific date-eligible sources.** Souter's unanimous simulated Hurley opinion, including all eight current Associates' joins, directly supplies the common prior commitment and its limits. For O'Connor's own earlier analysis, Roberts v. United States Jaycees, 468 U.S. 609, 631-640 (1984), discusses genuinely expressive associations, quiet persuasion, instruction of the young and commercial activities. Roberts, Rotary and New York State Club Association remain pre-divergence sources at the scope preserved by Hurley. These sources identify legal forks; they do not predetermine a vote in Dale.

**Sources and bounded gaps.** [New Jersey opinion](https://static.case.law/nj/160/html/0562-01.html); [IA docket collection, No. 99-699](https://archive.org/details/micro_IA40385014_0345), petition, appendix, petitioners' merits brief, respondent's merits brief and three joint-appendix volumes. ## Hill v. Colorado - No. 98-1856

**Event and question.** Merits consideration June 28, 2000; argued January 19, 2000. Certiorari to the Colorado Supreme Court's February 16, 1999 judgment in Hill v. Thomas, 973 P.2d 1246. Petitioners seek declaratory and injunctive relief against section 18-9-122(3). Their initial complaint asserted prior restraint, content discrimination, vagueness and overbreadth. The state court treated the challenge as facial because there had been no enforcement. The case is not review of convictions, damages, or a defendant-specific injunction.

**Operative law.** The copied statutory text contains all six subsections. Subsection (3) penalizes a knowing approach within eight feet, without consent, for the listed communicative purposes in public-way or sidewalk areas within 100 feet of any entrance door to a health-care facility. It covers leaflets/handbills, signs, oral protest, education and counseling. Subsection (4) defines the facility by legal authority to administer medical treatment. Subsection (2)'s obstruction offense is distinct; subsection (6) adds civil liability by cross-reference. The constitutional review of (3) does not automatically invalidate (2) or resolve all civil-remedy elements.

**Authoritative state construction and judgment.** The Colorado Supreme Court affirmed summary judgment for the State. It construed the statute to require both knowing conduct and an approach by the speaker. A stationary speaker need not retreat when someone comes within eight feet; a third party's movement does not by itself create liability. The rule does not require advance consent for all words, signs or presence in the 100-foot area. It regulates the specified knowing nonconsensual approach. The court considered ordinary conversation and signs possible at eight feet and regarded stationary, consensual and more distant communication as alternatives. That assessment is part of the constitutional conclusion below, not a conclusive empirical finding that every leaflet can be delivered as effectively.

The court applied Ward's rule for general time/place/manner legislation, distinguishing Madsen/Schenck's closer fit for injunctions and Sabelko's retreat requirement. It treated the law as content neutral and the access, safety and medical-treatment interests as significant. It relied on legislative testimony of blocking entrances, intimidation, crowding and confrontation. These are reported legislative materials; they are not findings that these individual petitioners committed violence. The record supports ordinary sidewalk counseling as petitioners' claimed activity.

**Preservation caution.** The Colorado opinion says petitioners did not contest content neutrality in that state appeal and records a concession about the applicable time/place/manner measure. The initial complaint did raise content discrimination, and the state court itself decided neutrality. This extraction supplies neither a new waiver nor a conclusion that every argument is preserved. If disposition turns on forfeiture, verify the actual current petition and briefing and apply the ordinary rule to the issue passed upon; do not silently discard the granted challenge based on an isolated characterization.

**Competing arguments.** Petitioners can press that peaceful personal leafleting and conversational initiation in a traditional public forum receive protection; an advance-consent approach rule covers protected conduct without obstruction or a prior refusal. The statute selects protest, education and counseling by communicative purpose, and its coverage of passers-by and all health facilities may burden more speech than the demonstrated access problem warrants. The distinct obstruction law and other ordinary conduct remedies are practical alternatives. Madsen's protection of peaceful initial approach and Schenck's moving-zone difficulties are relevant, although their injunction standard and larger distances cannot simply be transplanted.

The State relies on generally applicable legislation regulating the mechanics of close approach, neutral as to views and covering all health-care entrances; its knowledge and approach elements avoid forced retreat and accidental violations. The eight-foot measure and 100-foot setting preserve seeing, hearing, stationary offers and willing encounters while serving access and treatment-related interests. Under Ward a rule need not be the least restrictive imaginable. The State still must justify the actual burden and available channels; neither the word safety nor discomfort at a message answers every application.

**Distinct paths and remedy.** Decide classification before applying the corresponding review. If content neutral, test advancement, fit and actual alternative channels under Ward, accounting for the binding no-retreat construction. If content based, identify the classification and apply the proper stricter protection. Address vagueness and overbreadth separately, preserving knowledge, consent and purpose elements. A listener's lack of consent is not automatically a censorship power over all public speech. Any invalidation must match the actual subsection/application; no substitute distance, new conduct finding, conviction reversal or damages award follows automatically.

**Justice-specific sources.** O'Connor authored simulated Madsen's controlling fit rule; Souter and Ginsburg joined its provision-specific analysis, and Souter separately addressed actual-concert limits. Stevens adopted an individualized equitable analysis and sustained the approach term only for the identified enjoined actors, expressly distinguishing a general rule. Scalia's Madsen and Schenck writings, joined by Kennedy and Thomas, emphasize operation, legally supported relief and protected initiation. Breyer's Schenck separate opinion favored first-instance clarification of the disputed mobile terms, without approving every literal floating zone. The majority joins and precise differences are copied from the earlier public renders. Ward v. Rock Against Racism, 491 U.S. 781 (1989), authored by Kennedy, supplies ordinary regulation review; Stevens joined Marshall's dissent. No target-case reasoning supplies a commitment.

**Sources and limits.** [Colorado Supreme Court opinion and reproduced statute](https://static.case.law/p2d/973/html/1246-01.html); [OpenJurist parallel](https://openjurist.org/973/p2d/1246/hill-v-thomas-1301771). The IA collection's own listing was searched by docket and case name: no matching 98-1856 item was returned; the earlier 95-1905 item is not a substitute for the current merits briefs. Lower judgment, operative construction and statutory text suffice for the basic merits paths; exact current preservation or an asserted argument concession needs a specific source before use. Historical Supreme Court proceedings recited in the lower opinion supply no additional simulated order.

## Mitchell v. Helms - No. 98-1648

**Event, question and adopted premise.** Merits consideration June 28, 2000; argued December 1, 1999. Certiorari to the Fifth Circuit. The parties' question concerns federal Chapter 2's publicly purchased, loaned instructional materials and equipment for students in public and private schools, with allocations independent of religious identity. The approved neutral packet separately adopts an operational premise that the program as administered actually finances religious instruction or demonstrated religious use under recipient control. Its exact text is copied in the source extracts. That premise is neither an inference from affiliation/fungibility nor a recovered historical finding; it supplies no school, quantity, incident, nationwide prevalence or remedial scope beyond its words.

**Statute and operation.** The lower opinion reproduces 20 U.S.C. sections 7351(b)(2), 7372(a)(1), 7371(b), 7372(c)(1)-(2) and distribution provisions. Chapter 2 provides block grants, including materials, library resources, computer hardware/software and equipment. Private-school services/materials must be secular, neutral and nonideological; federal aid must supplement rather than supplant nonfederal funds; a public agency retains control of funds and title to property and administers them. Service providers must be public agencies or independent contractors. Public title and administration are statutory requirements; whether particular classroom operation complies and what government finances are separate inquiries.

The lower court described local requests, review of book titles, equipment loans, state/local monitoring, secular-use assurances and the absence of direct cash payments to nonpublic schools. It reported changes in monitoring after a federal review and a recall of approximately 191 book titles found inconsistent with Chapter 2 guidelines. These descriptions do not establish that all materials were devotional, that all safeguards were ineffective, or that the adopted premise is limited to those recalled titles. They also do not erase the adopted actual-use premise.

**Judgment and reasons below.** Helms v. Picard, 151 F.3d 347 (5th Cir. Aug. 17, 1998), Part III, reversed the district court's summary judgment for defendants and rendered a declaration that federal Chapter 2 and its Louisiana counterpart were unconstitutional as applied in Jefferson Parish insofar as they permitted educational/instructional equipment loans to sectarian schools, including library books. The court regarded Meek and Wolman as directly controlling and rejected the argument that later cases had implicitly displaced their equipment/textbook distinction. It preserved the textbook category and did not rest this categorical disposition on a particular quantified finding of actual devotional use. The current Court must evaluate the stated lower ground against actual simulated Agostini, Jackson and Strout; a changed operational premise does not silently narrow the lower decree.

The litigation also included special education, transportation and a Louisiana state program. Those distinct programs and their dispositions cannot all be imported into No. 98-1648 merely because they share the Fifth Circuit opinion. The federal Chapter 2 question is the supplied grant; identify any genuinely dependent state provision before directing relief beyond it. A separate historical Supreme Court action in another docket is not part of this event.

**Competing arguments.** Petitioners and the Secretary of Education support neutral access to secular educational resources. Public title, secular-content restrictions, neutral per-student allocations and controls can preserve a lawful benefit; religious location, institutional savings and potential divertibility alone cannot establish religious sponsorship. They challenge categorical reliance on Meek/Wolman and preserve genuinely segregated secular services and independent private benefits. Under the adopted premise, they must address the actual financed use rather than simply deny that fact; the scope of violations and appropriate severable remedy remain available arguments.

Helms and the plaintiff taxpayers argue that equipment controlled and used by a religious school's teachers for its own integrated instruction differs from the separately supervised public service in Agostini or the independently edited printing benefit in Rosenberger. Actual religious instructional financing is not cured by neutral eligibility, public title or routing. Their historical merits brief also relies on potential diversion and institutional support; those broader theories must be tested against the actual current rule rather than accepted as controlling. No requirement that every religious school be excluded follows merely from proving a defective use.

**Entering-law forks and remedy.** Agostini's updated Lemon inquiry asks what governmental act and function are performed, not whether every act of a recipient is government speech. It permits identifiable secular work with enforceable controls and rejects religious-location and incidental-benefit presumptions. Jackson establishes that genuine parental choice does not itself cure demonstrated financing of integrated devotional instruction; Strout applies the funded-function distinction in tuition's actual public-substitute setting. Rosenberger remains an actual controlled financing benefit for independently edited private expression, not a general declaration that all public payments are forums. Southworth's independent university-expression program and Santa Fe's official religious-choice mechanism retain their distinct applications.

Apply the adopted premise, then identify the actual scope of relief it supports. An as-administered violation may require correction while independently lawful loans remain; reversal of an overbroad categorical rationale does not establish that every administered use is constitutional. Conversely, actual religious financing cannot be treated as only a risk merely because the historical record is disputed. Vacatur and a targeted remedial remand, narrower affirmance if the lawful reviewed scope permits it, or another disposition must explain both the legal ground and treatment of the actual lower declaration. No new audit regime, all-provider exclusion, recoupment or school closure is assumed.

**Justice-specific sources.** O'Connor's Jackson opinion and Stevens/Souter/Ginsburg/Breyer joins adopt the actual funded-function rule and confined remedial inquiry; their earlier Agostini positions are separately preserved. Kennedy's Jackson dissent, joined by Scalia and Thomas, treats the genuine child-selected benefit as important while denying that neutrality or a signature alone is universally sufficient. The three later joined Kennedy's Strout dissent distinguishing capped ordinary tuition from provider operating/debt costs. Breyer did not join Souter's affirmative direct-aid objection in Agostini, although he joined its precedent and procedural objections. Souter and Ginsburg authored those separate Agostini grounds. These distinctions, not a generic church/state grouping, support individual analysis. Current Santa Fe positions are copied for attribution/official-function continuity.

**Sources and limits.** [Fifth Circuit opinion](https://static.case.law/f3d/151/html/0347-01.html); [IA record, No. 98-1648](https://archive.org/details/micro_IA40385014_0270), especially petition and appendix, petitioners' brief, Secretary of Education brief, plaintiffs' brief and joint appendix. The full Part III was read, with selected briefing passages. The adopted premise resolves the actual-use fact for this application but does not resolve source-specific quantity or remedy. Do not require nonexistent simulated findings or treat unavailable historical detail as litigant default.

## Stenberg v. Carhart - No. 99-830

**Event and questions.** Merits consideration June 28, 2000; argued April 25, 2000. Certiorari to the Eighth Circuit, 192 F.3d 1142 (Sept. 24, 1999). The state merits brief's two questions concern statutory construction/federalism and the asserted undue burden of the method prohibition. The supplied neutral grant expressly includes both reach to standard D&E and absence of a health exception. Respondent advances health, bodily-integrity, purpose and vagueness grounds supporting the existing protection. The petition's additional fetal-personhood and wholesale-reconsideration questions were not carried into the petitioners' two merits questions; this packet does not enlarge the supplied grant into either subject.

**Operative statute.** Nebraska LB 23, codified at sections 28-326(9) and 28-328, forbids its defined partial-birth procedure unless necessary to save the patient's life endangered by a physical disorder, illness or injury, including a pregnancy-caused life-endangering physical condition. The definition covers deliberate and intentional vaginal delivery of a living fetus or a substantial portion for a procedure the performer knows will kill it and does kill it, before completion of delivery. Intentional and knowing prohibited performance carries a felony sanction and physician-license consequences. The patient may not be prosecuted under this section or for conspiracy to violate it. The definition, conduct fault, patient immunity, life exception, absence of an independent health exception and no viability limitation must remain distinct. The source extracts reproduce the available operative terms, retaining the brief's ellipsis at the end of its licensing recitation; no ruling on the separate licensing procedures is supplied. No invented D&X-only definition, clinical safe harbor or good-faith defense is inserted.

**Material findings and competing medical evidence.** Dr. Carhart performs abortions before viability and uses D&E and, in some circumstances, an intact variant. The lower courts describe ordinary D&E as frequently drawing an arm or leg through the cervix while the fetus is living before dismemberment occurs; they rejected the State's claim that the intent terms necessarily exclude those acts. In some instances fetal demise precedes removal, which differs under the statutory living-fetus element. No claim of unconstitutional restriction on viable-fetus procedures was necessary to the Eighth Circuit's disposition.

The district court credited Carhart, Hodgson, Stubblefield and the State's Boehm while finding the other state expert generally unreliable on procedure knowledge. It particularly credited Stubblefield's experience. The district court found that Carhart's intact method, used in roughly 10-20 cases annually, reduced particular surgical risks in those circumstances compared with available alternatives; it identified reduced instrumentation, retained parts, trauma, blood loss, operating time and infection risks. It did not make a universal finding that every intact procedure is always safest. The State's evidence disputes comparative superiority, stresses the absence of controlled comparative studies and availability of alternatives, and invokes ethical and professional interests. Boehm acknowledged that intact removal might reduce certain mechanical risks but considered comparative safety insufficiently studied. Preserve the evidence/finding distinction and review standard rather than substitute a professional association's conclusory veto or categorical legislative factual immunity.

**Exact lower relief and independent grounds.** Carhart v. Stenberg, 11 F. Supp. 2d 1099 (D. Neb. July 2, 1998), expressly declined to decide whole-statute facial invalidity. It declared LB 23 unconstitutional as applied to Carhart, his patients and similarly situated persons, concerning his D&E/D&X methods on nonviable fetuses; enjoined defendants and persons bound under Rule 65(d) to that extent; and directed service on Nebraska county attorneys. Its three reasons were the health/access effect of prohibiting Carhart's intact method in the identified circumstances, the reach to ordinary D&E, and vagueness of substantial portion.

The Eighth Circuit affirmed because the language reaches ordinary D&E, the predominant second-trimester method, creating a substantial obstacle under its stated Casey analysis. It held that avoiding constitutional doubt cannot give words a meaning they cannot bear, and that intentional/knowing elements do not exclude an ordinary D&E performed intentionally. It expressly did not decide the separate D&X-only burden or vagueness grounds. It also affirmed the county attorney's joint/several fee liability in his distinct appeal; the supplied merits question does not independently place that fee dispute before the Court. Do not describe an affirmance here as a new adjudication of all fees or an automatic nationwide/facial injunction.

**Competing statutory and constitutional arguments.** Nebraska argues that the words partial vaginal delivery, context, statutory purpose and deliberate sequence distinguish the intact procedure from dismemberment D&E. It invokes constitutional avoidance, federalism, legislative materials, the Attorney General's expressed D&E nonenforcement position, and appropriate respect for state construction. It argues that alternative methods preserve access and that a distinct procedure can be prohibited to protect potential life and medical ethics without an exception that would allow any physician preference to defeat the prohibition. The Attorney General's litigation position is not itself a binding state supreme-court construction or a finding that every county prosecutor is bound.

Carhart argues that the enacted substantial-portion definition reaches limb extraction in ordinary D&E; an aspirational purpose and nonbinding assurance cannot replace the text or eliminate penal exposure. Even a narrower intact-only rule, he contends, lacks usable protection where alternatives impose significant clinical risks. He supports those arguments with the district findings, disputes the claimed state interests and presses separate notice/enforcement concerns. He does not need to prove that the intact method is preferable in every case to support relief for an established affected group; he still must prove the relevant interference, causation and scope under actual Casey.

**Entering-law forks and remedy.** Actual Casey's full six-step standard, Fargo and Voinovich govern; the historical lower courts' obstacle-only or numerical language does not replace them. Distinguish (1) permissible construction and actual coverage, (2) independent necessary-care protection, (3) substantial obstacle, (4) remaining severe/meaningful interference and justification, (5) notice/fault, and (6) severability and relief. Voinovich forbids categorical invalidity merely because medical labels overlap or every clinical judgment lacks an express fault term; it equally requires genuine substantive access and necessary-care analysis. It rejected a universal fault cure and left method applications to supported inquiry. Its Ohio affirmative defenses are not Nebraska law.

If the D&E reach ground is sufficient, explain its actual affected procedures and remedy without manufacturing an additional medical holding. If an independently sufficient health ground is reached, state the clinical trigger, support and limits. A narrower lawful construction cannot be invented; the Court may assess whether one is legally available and how much it resolves. Any remand or affirmance must account for the existing bounded protection and independent grounds; it does not automatically dissolve the injunction, prohibit all regulation of a distinct procedure, or award damages. No later medical or abortion decision may enter.

**Justice-specific sources.** Souter's simulated Voinovich opinion, joined fully by Stevens, O'Connor, Ginsburg and Breyer, supplies their common complete-framework, separate notice/fault, health and method-remand positions. Kennedy joined its notice rule, serious necessary mental-health protection and lawful construction inquiry, but dissented from residual lesser-burden review and would reverse the method injunction under his reasonable-regulation account. Scalia's separate opinion, joined by Thomas, agreed on notice/severability and opposed the constitutional abortion framework and health holding. The actual Casey retained-choice coalition and whole-framework coalition differ; the copied sources preserve that distinction. None of those prior positions decides the factual application here.

**Sources and bounded gaps.** [Eighth Circuit opinion](https://static.case.law/f3d/192/html/1142-01.html); [IA record, No. 99-830](https://archive.org/details/micro_IA40385014_0347), especially the supplemental appendix reproducing both district opinions, merits briefs and three joint-appendix volumes. The medical findings and exact relief above are from the district opinion's actual text; the petition's main appendix also reproduces other litigation, including Hope Clinic, which is not a Nebraska finding. No inference of absent evidence is drawn from unreviewed appendix portions. If a proposed opinion depends on a precise claimed clinical condition, quantitative comparison, state-law construction, certification option or severability clause not established here, verify that narrow source before relying on it.
