## Village of Willowbrook v. Olech — No. 98-1288

**Dates and vehicle.** Argument January 10, 2000; scheduled event February 23, 2000. Review is of the Seventh Circuit's judgment at 160 F.3d 386 (1998), which reversed a Rule 12(b)(6) dismissal. This is a sufficiency-of-the-complaint case, not a finding of unconstitutional conduct or an award of damages.

**Allegations.** Grace and Thaddeus Olech requested connection to municipal water. The Village initially demanded a 33-foot easement; the complaint says other similarly situated owners were required to provide only 15 feet. After approximately three months the Village accepted 15 feet and supplied water. The complaint characterizes the larger demand as irrational and wholly arbitrary and alleges ill will arising from an earlier successful, unrelated lawsuit against the Village. The complaint alleges intentional deprivation or reckless disregard; those alternatives must not be collapsed into a finding of intent. The complaint also concerns Rodney and Phyllis Zimmer and Howard Brinkman, who had participated in the earlier litigation and encountered the same initial demand. A description as a single-person claim is therefore an analytical shorthand, not proof that only one person was affected.

**Scope.** The Seventh Circuit relied on its spiteful-ill-will theory. Whether the allegations also suffice without proof of subjective ill will is a question for this Court, not an established fact. Rational basis, comparability, motive, liability, and damages have not been factually adjudicated in the posture supplied.

**Support.** Objective recitation at 528 U.S. 562, 563–564 and footnote; [official report](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/USREPORTS-528/pdf/USREPORTS-528-562.pdf). The report's adjudicative discussion is excluded from this addendum.

## Strout v. Albanese — review of 178 F.3d 57

**Dates and vehicle.** The First Circuit heard No. 98-1986 February 4, 1999, and decided it May 27, 1999. The District of Maine decided cross-motions for summary judgment August 11, 1998, reported at 13 F. Supp. 2d 112. The parties agreed there were no genuine issues of material fact. The approved calendar supplies the grant November 1, 1999, argument January 24, 2000, and merits event February 28, 2000. Those are adopted simulation scheduling premises, not recovered historical merits proceedings. Ordinary submission and circulation on that authorized schedule may be reconstructed under Engine §14(3); no new factual concession, filing, or waiver follows.

**Program and applicants.** Three sets of parents resided in Minot, part of School Union No. 29. Neither Minot nor the union operated a secondary school. The parents chose St. Dominic's Regional High School, a private Catholic sectarian school in Lewiston. Their requested benefit was tuition for the ordinary school program. It was not an application for a separately identified interpreter, remedial teacher, textbook, transportation service, or private-expression forum. Under the program, the local school unit paid the selected eligible school directly, while the parents selected the school. The statutory maximum was reported as $4,900 for 1996–1997; St. Dominic's tuition was approximately $3,850, so the requested tuition would fall below that ceiling. The petition describes St. Dominic's as meeting basic approval and incorporation requirements and excluded from tuition approval because it was sectarian. The State's opposition likewise describes full tuition coverage and calls the payments unrestricted. The nature and attribution of that aid remain legal questions.

**Statutory conditions.** The historical appendix reproduces 20-A M.R.S.A. §2951 and §5204(4). Section 2951 requires basic school approval, nonsectarian status in accordance with the First Amendment, incorporation, reporting/auditing and adopted requirements, participation in statewide assessment when publicly funded enrollment reaches 60% under the specified two-month average, and transfer of student records upon a school unit's request. Section 5204(4) applies when the resident unit neither maintains a secondary school nor contracts for secondary-school privileges; receiving-school permission and tuition approval govern the enumerated alternatives. Payment is mandatory up to the legal tuition rate. The exact copied text is in the neutral excerpts, including conditions omitted from the compact brief.

**Record limits and preserved claims.** The district court states that, on its summary-judgment record, the Department accepted schools' self-identification and no dispute over identification had occurred. That is the finding underlying rejection of the separate excessive-entanglement argument; it is not statutory text. The First Circuit reports differing parental motivations and that no parent claimed parochial attendance was a central tenet. The parents disputed the legal relevance of a centrality requirement in their reply. Neither religious affiliation nor those motivations independently proves the contents of every class. The approved packet's description of integrated sectarian schooling identifies the benefit under review; this addendum does not invent a devotional-hours count, curricular segregation mechanism, or school-specific factual finding beyond the recovered record.

The historical litigation presented Establishment, Free Exercise, Equal Protection, substantive due process, and speech theories. The authorized granted questions in the neutral packet frame the present review; the existence of additional theories below does not enlarge that grant. The local school defendants were dismissed below, and the State's opposition says their dismissal was not being challenged. The judgment under review upheld the exclusion; one circuit judge concurred without deciding whether broader funding would itself violate the Establishment Clause. No historical Supreme Court action supplies this merits event or its result.

**Support.** [Archive docket item](https://archive.org/details/micro_IA40386005_1011), located by the collection's own name and docket searches. Petition pp. 1–5 and appendices A–C; opposition pp. 1–7, 12; reply pp. 1–6. The recovered petition includes both complete lower-court opinions and the historical statutory appendix. A [published First Circuit text](https://openjurist.org/178/f3d/57/strout-v-albanese) cross-checks the primary appendix. No historical Supreme Court disposition is incorporated.

## Shalala v. Illinois Council on Long Term Care — No. 98-1109

**Dates and posture.** Argument November 8, 1999; scheduled event February 29, 2000. An association of approximately 200 Illinois nursing homes sued under 28 U.S.C. §1331. The district court dismissed for lack of jurisdiction on March 31, 1997; the Seventh Circuit's judgment is at 143 F.3d 1072 (1998). The case concerns the route to judicial review, not the validity of the challenged regulations.

**Claims and limits.** The amended complaint attacked (1) allegedly vague terms such as substantial compliance and minimal harm; (2) alleged inconsistency with statutory requirements and the Medicare mandate; (3) allegedly deficient administrative procedures under due process; and (4) alleged legislative rules issued without APA notice and comment or a statement of basis and purpose. Medicaid is outside the present review. The Seventh Circuit held the APA notice-and-comment challenge ripe and the vagueness challenge unripe, and required further ripeness consideration of other claims. The Secretary did not seek review of the APA ripeness determination. The approved premise does not reopen the vagueness or Medicaid issues.

**Program text and competing factual accounts.** Medicare nursing facilities must enter provider agreements and meet substantive requirements. Inspections produce deficiency findings. The sanction system distinguishes immediate jeopardy, less serious deficiencies, and substantial compliance. Immediate jeopardy requires termination or temporary management; lesser deficiencies permit lesser sanctions; deficiencies creating no more than a potential for minimal harm fall within the stated substantial-compliance definition. These categories describe the published program, not findings about a particular member.

Section 1395cc(h)(1) provides the specified administrative and §405(g) judicial review for dissatisfaction with provider-status determinations or determinations described in subsection (b)(2), subject to paragraph (h)(2). Section 405(h)'s three sentences must be kept distinct: binding effect of findings and decision after hearing, exclusive review as provided, and the bar on specified federal-jurisdiction actions to recover on claims arising under the subchapter. Section 1395ii incorporates designated provisions to the same extent as applicable in the Social Security subchapter, with substitutions for the Secretary and Department. These texts are reproduced mechanically from the report's statutory appendix in the neutral excerpts. This extraction does not adjudicate their scope.

Historically, the Council asserted that correction plans foreclosed review and that obtaining a reviewable sanction risked termination. The Secretary disputed those asserted practices and described access by incurring a minor penalty. Those were opposing descriptions of agency practice; this addendum adopts neither as a new finding. **Adopted simulation premise:** the approved neutral packet expressly supplies that, for the properly presented claims, there is no safe, practical route to meaningful review and a reviewable vehicle requires catastrophic exposure rather than ordinary cost, delay, or exhaustion. That premise governs this proceeding and is not derived from the source opinion. It establishes no violation on the merits and no entitlement to relief on an otherwise unripe claim.

**Support.** Objective record recitation at 529 U.S. 1, 6–9; competing practice descriptions at 21–22; procedural description at 51 n.14; statutory appendix at 25–30; [official report](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/USREPORTS-529/pdf/USREPORTS-529-1.pdf). Source extraction excludes the Court's answers to the jurisdictional question.

## United States v. Johnson — No. 98-1696

**Dates and posture.** Argument December 8, 1999; scheduled event March 1, 2000. The Sixth Circuit judgment under review is 154 F.3d 569 (1998). The requested relief is a reduction of supervised release corresponding to excess custody.

**Sentence and claim.** Johnson's 1990 convictions included two drug counts, two §924(c) firearm counts, and one felon-in-possession count. An initial 171-month sentence comprised concurrent 51-month terms and consecutive 60-month firearm terms; appellate correction reduced the sentence to 111 months. On May 2, 1996, the district court granted an unopposed §2255 motion vacating the §924(c) convictions and reduced the term to 51 months. Johnson had already served more than that and was ordered immediately released. A three-year supervised-release term remained attached to the drug convictions. In June 1996 he sought approximately two-and-a-half years' credit against that term. The district court rejected automatic credit; the Sixth Circuit treated supervised release as beginning when lawful custody should have ended. The vacatur is part of this litigant's supplied history; this addendum does not import any historical Supreme Court construction of §924(c) over the simulated baseline.

**Text and remedial distinctions.** Section 3624(e) addresses commencement on release, concurrency with other probation, supervised-release, or parole terms, tolling while imprisoned in connection with a conviction, and its exception for imprisonment of less than 30 consecutive days. The exception is not a general excess-custody credit rule. Section 3583(e)(1) permits termination after one year of supervised release, following the specified factors and procedure, when warranted by conduct and the interest of justice. Section 3583(e)(2) separately permits modification of conditions before expiration or termination and permits extension where less than the maximum was imposed. Section 3583(e)(3) addresses revocation for a proved violation and has different limits. None is a mandatory grant of the reduction requested. Exact historical statutory text, including §3624(e)'s fine-installment sentence, is copied in the neutral excerpts.

**Support.** Objective history at 529 U.S. 53, 54–56, and description of imposed conditions at 60; [official report](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/USREPORTS-529/pdf/USREPORTS-529-53.pdf). [1999 §3624](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/USCODE-1999-title18/pdf/USCODE-1999-title18-partII-chap229-subchapC-sec3624.pdf); [1999 §3583](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/USCODE-1999-title18/pdf/USCODE-1999-title18-partII-chap227-subchapD-sec3583.pdf).

## United States v. Locke / Intertanko v. Locke — Nos. 98-1701, 98-1706

**Dates, posture, and requested remedy.** Argument December 7, 1999; scheduled event March 6, 2000. Intertanko sought declaratory and injunctive relief against enforcement of Washington's best-achievable-protection regulations. Environmental groups intervened to defend them. The district court upheld the regulations, 947 F. Supp. 1484 (1996). The United States intervened on appeal; the Ninth Circuit sustained the rules except WAC 317-21-265's navigation/towing equipment requirements, 148 F.3d 1053 (1998). Petitions for rehearing were denied, 159 F.3d 1220 (1998). The scope of the remaining challenges cannot be treated as a single indivisible state rule.

**State measures.** The source appendix reproduces the Ninth Circuit's descriptions of sixteen provisions: event reporting (-130); watch practices (-200); navigation (-205); engineering (-210); prearrival tests (-215); emergency procedures (-220); preservation of records after events (-225); training (-230); drugs/alcohol (-235); personnel evaluation (-240); work hours (-245); language (-250); training records (-255); management (-260); navigation/towing technology (-265); and advance entry/safety reports (-540). Their complete copied descriptions appear in the neutral excerpts.

In particular, general watch practices require two licensed deck officers, a helmsman, and a lookout; a distinct restricted-visibility requirement exists and must not be silently treated as identical. Training includes approved programs and recurring drills. Language requirements extend to all licensed deck officers and the master, subordinate communication, and written instructions. Event reporting reaches specified incidents anywhere in the world during the five years preceding the prevention plan and subsequent incidents. The broader regime permits penalties, operating restrictions, and denial of entry; no source-derived inference that the State currently enforced or would imminently enforce a particular rule is adopted here.

**Federal texts and analytical boundaries.** PWSA Title I concerns traffic and local port/waterway measures; Title II's §3703(a) directs regulations for the enumerated vessel subjects and expressly permits different rules for domestic trade and rules exceeding international standards. Section 3703(b) requires attention to cargo types/grades, and subsection (c) requires consultation procedures including state/local officials, port authorities, environmental groups, and other knowledgeable parties. These are distinct from OPA Title I's liability/compensation scheme and §2718's saving language. The saving text preserves the specified authority concerning discharge/removal, funds, additional liabilities/requirements/penalties, and contains its own named-act limits. Exact §3703 and §2718 text appears in the neutral excerpts. The interpretation of those texts and the application of the entering-law slice's Ray, Gade, and Myrick authorities remain for neutral modeling; no rule announced in the pending Locke matter is supplied as entering law. The United States also advanced treaty-based arguments; this addendum does not make a treaty independently dispositive.

**Support.** Objective recitation at 529 U.S. 89, 95–99; regulatory terms at 112–115; appendix at 117–119; [official report](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/USREPORTS-529/pdf/USREPORTS-529-89.pdf). [1999 §3703](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/USCODE-1999-title46/pdf/USCODE-1999-title46-subtitleII-partB-chap37-sec3703.pdf); [1999 §2718](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/USCODE-1999-title33/pdf/USCODE-1999-title33-chap40-subchapI-sec2718.pdf). The descriptions in the appendix are judicial recitations of regulations, not a substitute for exact regulatory text if an unquoted qualification becomes material.

## Portuondo v. Agard — No. 98-1170

**Dates and vehicle.** Argument November 1, 1999; scheduled event March 6, 2000. Agard was convicted February 25, 1991. The Appellate Division modified the judgment December 20, 1993, reversing one weapons conviction and affirming the remaining sodomy and weapons convictions, 199 A.D.2d 401. Leave was denied April 14, 1994, 83 N.Y.2d 868. His federal habeas petition was filed in June 1995, before AEDPA. The district court gave reasons and dated its order March 15, 1996; the reproduced judgment refers to the order's filing March 20, and the circuit opinion gives entry March 21. The Second Circuit decided July 3, 1997, 117 F.3d 696, and denied rehearing October 23, 1998, 159 F.3d 98. Its remedy directed grant of the writ unless the State afforded a new trial within sixty days of its mandate. Do not substitute a direct criminal appeal or automatically apply amended §2254(d).

**Comments and trial context.** The trial turned substantially on credibility. The complainant and a friend described assault, sexual crimes, and gun threats; Agard described consensual intercourse, admitted striking the complainant once, and denied the charged sexual assault and gun threats. Defense summation attacked the two women's truthfulness. The prosecutor attacked Agard's credibility, including his interest, record, alleged rehearsed answers, and his opportunity to hear other testimony. The challenged passage expressly contrasted him with the other witnesses because he could sit through their testimony before testifying and consider how his account would fit the evidence. Defense counsel objected and later moved for a mistrial, specifically asserting that an adverse credibility inference punished his constitutional right to attend. The trial judge rejected that objection and motion. The neutral excerpts copy the complete relevant trial passage and ruling from the joint appendix; they do not infer an additional concession or fabricate a quotation.

**Preservation and scope.** On rehearing the Second Circuit narrowed its rationale: it allowed arguments based on particular evidence or fit between testimony, and barred a general summation argument that discredited the defendant solely for exercising trial presence. The panel expressly declined to entertain the State's Teague argument because it was first raised on rehearing, and expressly did not decide whether its rule was new or available retroactively to other petitioners. These are procedural facts about the judgment under review, not a fresh ruling here. Respondent's merits brief p.43 asserts that the State challenged neither the circuit's harmfulness determination in the certiorari petition nor in its merits brief, and cites the Solicitor General's corresponding description of the grant. The petition's question concerns extending Griffin to presence-based comment. Treat the unchallenged-harmlessness point as respondent's documented scope contention, and evaluate it under the authorized grant and simulated review rules. This addendum does not declare that historical Brecht supplies the simulation's governing standard in all habeas cases.
