## Illinois v. Wardlow

**Read in full:** the petition for certiorari (including its lower-court appendices), Joint Appendix (34 numbered pages), petitioner's merits brief (44 numbered pages), and respondent's merits brief (39 numbered pages), from [Internet Archive item micro_IA40385014_0250](https://archive.org/details/micro_IA40385014_0250). Saved originals and complete OCR texts are in `../research/wardlow-record/`. These are the four files numbered 02, 05, 06, and 18. The initial whole-JA display truncated its middle; JA 16–22 was separately reread in full, completing the document. OCR defects in incidental passages are not treated as missing record evidence. No current-case Supreme Court opinion was read.

The actual suppression testimony, JA 3–12, confirms the factual supplement with these precise source and attribution limits:

| Proposition | Original-record support and qualification |
|---|---|
| Police identification | Nolan testified that he was in uniform, but could not recall whether his car was marked because it changed from day to day (JA 4; transcript A4–A5). He confirmed a full police uniform, including badge, name tag, and Chicago Police Department patch, when he exited the car (JA 9; A11). There is no separate testimony that Harvey or the other six officers were uniformed, nor that any of the four vehicles was marked. The intermediate appellate opinion describes Nolan and Harvey as uniformed (JA 28–29); that description must not be represented as additional testimony from Nolan. The Illinois Supreme Court's account identifies Nolan's uniform only (JA 16). |
| What Wardlow saw | Nolan said Wardlow looked in their direction and began running; when pressed, he said Wardlow was looking at them, then agreed he was looking in their direction (JA 5; A6). On cross-examination he again described looking toward them followed by flight (JA 9; A10). Wardlow did not testify, and no admission of police recognition appears. Actual recognition is an inference from the observation and setting. |
| Trial-court inference | The trial court reasoned that almost anyone could identify a marked or an obviously unmarked police car and referred to a white officer in a black area when inferring recognition and explaining its suppression denial (JA 14; B13). Petitioner invokes that inference at merits brief 4 and 35 n.14. This is the judge's stated inference, not testimony proving markings, the visibility of an officer's uniform from Wardlow's position, or what Wardlow actually recognized. |
| Location and police assignment | Nolan first saw Wardlow standing approximately in front of 4035 West Van Buren (JA 7; A8). Asked why the officers went to that location, he described it as an area in the 11th District with high narcotics traffic and confirmed an assignment to investigate narcotics sales there (JA 8; A9–A10). Eight officers in four cars were traveling eastbound on Van Buren, with Nolan and Harvey last (JA 8). The Illinois Supreme Court accepted the testimony as sufficient to establish a high-crime area (JA 19–20), rejecting the intermediate court's narrower reading (JA 31–33). Preserve both the testimony's actual detail and the state supreme court's finding; do not add an observed sale, information about this address, or an identified suspected offense involving Wardlow. |
| Crowd and caravan details | Nolan explained the number of officers by what normally occurs in these areas: many people, including customers or lookouts (JA 8; A9). He did not testify to a crowd beside Wardlow on this occasion. Neither core brief nor the petition supplies additional witness testimony establishing the number of people actually present, the caravan's speed, whether Wardlow noticed all four cars, or whether the other cars had passed him. Respondent's possible-provocation explanations (merits brief 20–24) and petitioner's claims about obvious recognition and departmental intelligence (35–36, 41–42) remain arguments, not additional evidence. |
| Conduct and route | Nolan denied observing any law violation when he first saw Wardlow, including when the running began (JA 5). He followed south by car while Wardlow ran through a gangway and alley; the encounter ended on the street near 4036 West Congress, where Wardlow ran toward the officers (JA 6, 9). The opaque white bag stayed under Wardlow's arm when Nolan could see him (JA 9). The petition's description of running at full speed (11) does not add a measurement or corresponding testimony. |
| Stop, frisk, arrest | Nolan exited the car and performed the protective pat-down without first announcing his office or asking questions (JA 6, 9). Squeezing the opaque bag revealed a heavy, hard, gun-shaped object; only after opening it and finding the loaded .38-caliber handgun did he arrest Wardlow (JA 7, 10–11). His general experience linked weapons with narcotics locations (JA 11), but the gun was not seen before the stop. Keep the three stages distinct. Petitioner expressly treats the subsequent search as outside the question presented (merits brief 10 n.5). |
| Time and call history | No time is given in Nolan's suppression testimony. The state opinions attribute 12:15 p.m. to the arrest report (JA 17 n.1, 29; the petition's clearer printing is app. 2 n.1). Petitioner's merits brief 4 n.1 cites the arrest report, CLR C4, and the prosecutor's argument, R B5. The Illinois Supreme Court states that the officers were not responding to a call or report of suspicious activity (JA 24). These sources are identified recitations; the arrest report itself and the complete arguments are not reproduced in the JA. |

**Readiness and actual limits:** the preceding supplement's sequence and record-limits qualifications are confirmed, subject to the distinctions above. The four complete core documents do not supply the missing objective details; no further testimony should be inferred from either party's characterization. The recovered JA supplies the actual suppression testimony, so that testimony is no longer an unrecovered source. The arrest report and omitted portions of counsel's suppression arguments were not independently recovered in these four documents; their content is used only to the extent expressly recited. No innocent or criminal motive is established by those limits.

## Friends of the Earth v. Laidlaw

**Read in full in clean research contexts:** the petition for certiorari, both merits briefs, and Joint Appendix (211 numbered pages), from [Internet Archive item micro_IA40385014_0244](https://archive.org/details/micro_IA40385014_0244). Saved originals and complete OCR texts are in `../research/laidlaw-record/`, files 02, 09, 10, and 12. The JA was read completely in a separate clean context; its original PDF was checked for the discharge table and printed page numbers. This validation also read the complete June 9, 1999 Suggestion of Mootness and its appendices (11), petitioners' June 15 response (13), respondent's June 21 reply (14), and petitioners' merits reply and appendices (15). No current-case Supreme Court opinion was read.

| Proposition | Original-record support and qualification |
|---|---|
| Member-use assertions | Petitioners' merits brief 6 and n.6 identifies the Curtis, Moore, McCullough, Sharp, Patterson, Lee, and Pruitt affidavits and paragraph citations, locating them in the October 5, 1992 preliminary-injunction submission and June 28, 1993 supplemental standing opposition. Its account distinguishes use, desired use, avoided use, concern during use, and claimed economic effects. Merits reply 11–12 adds Pruitt's avoidance of downstream recreation and fish, Lee's and Curtis's asserted property-value effects, and Patterson's foregone home purchase, each attributed to an affidavit. These are the parties' evidence descriptions, not independent findings of harm or valuation. They support retaining the member-specific accounts already supplied, with their source limits. |
| Adverse deposition evidence and original-document limits | Neither the four complete core documents nor the additional filings reproduce the full Curtis or Moore affidavits or the requested deposition passages. The JA contains pleadings, docket entries, orders, a different witness's trial excerpt, the state complaint and decree, district-court findings, and tables. The earlier clean addendum therefore remains the source for Moore's two visits (Record 62, deposition 23–24), Curtis's childhood cessation and answer that pollution did not cause that cessation (deposition part 2, 38–39), and the reported absence of depositions of the other affiants. Those are supported published recitations, not independently recovered deposition text. Preserve them alongside the present-use assertions; this original-record review supplies no contrary testimony or new corroboration. |
| Permit, filing, and exceedance counts | The permit effective date is January 1, 1987, not the petition's isolated 1986 starting-date description (JA 141–142; petitioners' merits brief 4). The citizen complaint was filed June 12, 1992 (JA 1). The original discharge table at JA 195 resolves the briefs' 13/14 variation: entry 476 is June 12 itself, and entries 477–489 are thirteen later exceedances. There are fourteen on or after the filing date and thirteen strictly after June 12. The last three listed exceedances are November 3, 1993, November 26, 1994, and January 30, 1995. The table and JA 178's inclusive June 12–October 1992 account are consistent on that distinction. |
| Monitoring and reporting counts | JA 159 finds 420 monitoring and 503 reporting violations, with thirteen monitoring and ten reporting violations since the complaint. The later discussion of recent compliance at JA 178 instead says one alleged monitoring violation and no reporting violations since June 1992. This is an unresolved inconsistency in the district opinion's own text, not a reason to erase either passage or infer zero postfiling violations. The referenced Revised Exhibits 77–79 and 97 and full 1995 trial transcript, 181–182 and 187, are not reproduced in the appendix. The mercury dates remain independently shown in the table. |
| State enforcement proceeding | The state complaint preceded the citizen suit; the reproduced consent decree is expressly ordered June 10, 1992 (JA 83). Its $100,000 penalty and compliance undertakings appear at JA 81–82. Distinguish that judicial approval date from the June 9 filing/settlement chronology. The federal district court's findings concerning Laidlaw's initiation and preparation of the state case and the inadequate economic-benefit recovery remain supported (JA 93–94, 116–120, 134–136). Its footnote expressly rejects petitioners' additional fraud/collusion accusation (JA 117 n.14); deficient diligent prosecution must not be restated as an adjudicated finding of fraud or collusion. |
| Environmental findings and judgment | The court independently found adequate water quality for fish, wildlife, and recreation and no demonstrated significant environmental harm from the mercury discharges at issue, while acknowledging mercury's toxicity (JA 150, 156–157, 160–164). That lower-court finding is distinct from the members' reported avoidance and concerns. The appendix also recites a 1988 fish kill from a pH upset (JA 88); non-mercury discharge claims were removed as not ongoing at filing (JA 139–140, 198–199). Do not turn the mercury finding into a finding that no discharge ever harmed the river. The court assessed a $405,800 penalty against a calculated $1,092,581 economic benefit and denied injunctive relief on substantial compliance (JA 165, 180–184). Its findings were signed January 21, 1997 (JA 184); the separate judgment was filed January 22 (JA 198–199). Thus January 22 is the judgment date already supplied. |
| Appealed decision and later closure | The Fourth Circuit's July 16, 1998 opinion assumed initial standing and continuing injury without deciding them, and relied on the remaining Treasury-paid penalty remedy (petition app. 7a–9a). Its account says the denial of declaratory and injunctive relief was not appealed. Petitioners dispute that characterization of declaratory relief, arguing that the liability determination itself was declaratory relief (merits brief 40–42); that is their argument, not a separate recovered declaratory decree. The later closure could not have been the basis of this appellate judgment. |

**The closure record supplies dates and qualifications missing from the earlier supplement.** McLeod's May 21, 1999 affidavit in the Suggestion of Mootness, app. 4a–6a, paragraphs 3–8, states that the last NPDES discharge occurred at 7:00 a.m. on September 1, 1998; no customer waste was received after August 31. It distinguishes approval of an incinerator closure plan on September 15, 1998 from approval of the remaining-operations closure plan on April 13, 1999. The assertion that the entire facility was closed and for sale describes its status at the affidavit's date, not an exact earlier whole-facility closure day. The incinerator and feed system had been removed, the first and third treatment stages removed or sold, and the second stage and filter press remained for sale. These are sworn assertions, not findings made below. Do not date the entire facility's closure or dismantling to the last discharge or assume all of it preceded certiorari.

The affidavit expressly admits retaining the NPDES permit to make the site attractive to a buyer while denying an intention to resume discharges (paragraph 8). The actual June 2, 1997 consent agreement, Suggestion app. 7a–15a, required incineration to cease by August 31, 1998, terminated incineration interim status, required closure planning, and continued compliance with existing permits and orders. It also allowed conversion to specified equivalent treatment capacity subject to agency design approval, required permits, reconstruction requirements, and cessation of incineration before use (operative paragraphs 1–5, 8–9). It did not itself revoke the NPDES permit or prohibit every conceivable future operation. Its schedule-adjustment provision required notice and agency concurrence (paragraph 12); no adjustment is assumed. The separate groundwater-remediation system was to continue until its regulatory requirements were met (finding 4). Petitioners' June 15 response, 6–8, disputes the asserted legal permanence of closure; respondent's June 21 reply, 2–4, relies on dismantling and the affidavit. Neither position becomes an adjudicated recurrence finding here.

Petitioners' merits reply also presents a postclosure reporting allegation (6–7; Clark declaration, app. 11a–13a, paragraph 8, executed August 13, 1999). Clark recounts a database indication and an August 4 conversation in which a DHEC employee reportedly said that a June 1999 notice addressed the missing April 1999 seven-day toxicity report, due at the end of May and still unreceived on August 4. This is a declaration under penalty of perjury reporting a conversation and database research; the notice itself is not attached. It is not an independently verified or adjudicated violation and does not establish resumed discharge.

**Readiness and actual limits:** original-record review confirms the principal filing, judgment, permit-retention, and post-appellate-closure sequence, with the date, attribution, count, and decree-scope refinements above. The full member affidavits, Curtis/Moore deposition passages, and June 30, 1993 oral standing ruling are not reproduced in the core documents; petitioners' merits brief 6 identifies the latter as hearing transcript page 40. Their nonrecovery here is not a finding that they never existed or that the parties failed to supply them. The earlier clean member and adverse-deposition recitations remain usable on their stated published-source basis. No finding about constitutional standing, recurrence, mootness, or the proper disposition is supplied by this validation.

The JA independently corroborates the June 30, 1993 motion-denial date in its docket (JA 6; transcript listing at JA 25). Its December 1992 order had discussed nearby residents' affidavits when denying preliminary relief for lack of irreparable injury (JA 61–62); that ruling is not an express determination there of no Article III injury. The final findings also distinguish later permit terms from liability under the former permit: the daily mercury maximum became 10 ppb and monthly average 1.25 ppb effective June 1, 1996, subject to the state challenge, without retrospectively excusing prior exceedances (JA 148–153, 178, 182–183). The 1997 order revises earlier equipment-necessity findings (JA 166–167 nn.15–16); do not use the 1995 findings as its unchanged final position.
