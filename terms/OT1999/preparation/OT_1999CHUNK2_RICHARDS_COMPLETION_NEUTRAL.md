## 3. Posture and every material lower-court ground

The complaint was filed March 26, 1997. On March 27, 1998, the District Court of the Virgin Islands dismissed for lack of subject-matter jurisdiction under Rule 12(b)(1), 1 F. Supp. 2d 498. It did not decide the government's alternative Rule 12(c) or Rule 56 motions, which also invoked Feres. App. 13a–15a & n.2. The Third Circuit affirmed on May 5, 1999, reviewing Feres's legal application de novo. App. 1a–3a, 10a. Rehearing was denied June 30, 1999. App. 11a–12a.

The district court considered duty status, location, and activity together. It regarded the factors as favoring a bar but, because of disagreement among lower courts concerning similar travel, treated that examination as inconclusive. It then relied on each Feres rationale: military decisionmaking, the distinct federal relationship, and compensation. Its discipline analysis was concrete: the negligent-maintenance, unlicensed-driver, and negligent-order allegations would call military maintenance and personnel decisions into question. It contrasted driving-only litigation with the actual supervision allegations before it and relied on Shearer and Sanchez, 878 F.2d 633. Those are its grounds; no Supreme Court holding that all maintenance or supervision litigation is immune follows. App. 16a–23a.

The panel treated the circumstances as incident to service because Richards remained in active service, was traveling from his duty station, and was still on the base. It acknowledged the personal travel premise and public highway. Thomason, 539 F.2d 955, supplied the circuit's prior on-base accident rule. It considered Flowers, 764 F.2d 759, more analogous than Troglia, 602 F.2d 1334: Flowers concerned personal travel on a public easement within a base, whereas Troglia's accident occurred outside the gate and the base. It also invoked Stewart's travel between duty activities and Lauer's departure/return discussion. These circuit authorities supply lower-court reasoning and comparisons, not additional binding Supreme Court rules. App. 5a–8a.

Part II-B of the panel opinion separately questioned the fit between that result and military discipline. A civilian could have been struck by the same truck and could raise similar questions; the panel doubted that this suit implicated the special superior–subordinate relationship in the manner contemplated by Shearer and Muniz. The reservation did not alter the judgment or find negligence. App. 8a–10a. Judge Rendell, joined by Chief Judge Becker and Judges Nygaard and McKee, dissented from denial of rehearing, criticizing Feres's statutory foundation and its use to bar recovery for essentially civilian activity and urging Supreme Court reconsideration. App. 11a–12a. That writing is a lower-court separate position, not current Supreme Court law.

## 4. Governing law, authority boundaries, and distinctions

**FTCA text.** Section 1346(b)(1) covers claims for injury, death, or property loss caused by a government employee's wrongful or negligent act within employment when a private person would be liable under the law of the place. Section 2671 includes military personnel and defines their scope of employment by line of duty. This definition concerns the alleged tortfeasor, not whether the injured servicemember's activity triggers Feres. Section 2674 preserves the private-person comparison and denies prejudgment interest and punitive damages, with its separate compensatory rule where local wrongful-death law supplies only punitive damages. The petition reproduces these provisions and §2680 in App. 24a–27a.

**Feres, 340 U.S. 135, 138–146 (1950).** The continuing rule bars FTCA recovery for a servicemember's injury arising out of or in the course of activity incident to service. Feres involved an active-duty barracks death and active-duty medical negligence. It distinguished Brooks's furlough travel, absence of orders or duty, and lack of military mission. Its stated rule is not a universal bar based on enlistment, a public-road exception, or a requirement that each injury arise from a specifically assigned task. Its references to status and furlough do not themselves create the later lower courts' mandatory three-factor formula. [Full opinion](https://www.law.cornell.edu/supremecourt/text/340/135).

**Brooks, 337 U.S. 49, 50–54 (1949).** The Court allowed the servicemembers' highway claims for injury unrelated to service and rejected a bar resting solely on military membership. It reserved service-incident injury. It rejected an unprovided exclusivity or election rule; its remand concerning benefit adjustments preserved the questions of applicable statutory/regulatory treatment and preservation, and did not establish a blanket deduction of every benefit or insurance payment. The supplied official-report text was read completely. Feres and Brown provide the explicit leave/furlough descriptions of Brooks; the Brooks majority itself should not be cited as if it contains every later factual characterization.

**Brown, 348 U.S. 110, 111–113 (1954).** A separate postdischarge medical injury was actionable even though the veteran entered the hospital because of a service injury and received veterans' compensation. The Court preserved both Brooks and Feres. Its rejection of causal history alone does not establish that Richards had civilian status or that every private purpose ends the service relationship. [Full opinion](https://www.law.cornell.edu/supremecourt/text/348/110).

**Shearer, 473 U.S. 52, 57–59 (1985), Part II-B.** An off-duty, off-base victim's status did not permit a claim challenging Army supervision, control, and retention of a dangerous soldier. The type of command decision challenged mattered; broad policy disagreement was not required. The Court expressly distinguished Brooks's negligent vehicle operation from allegations calling military discipline and management into question. The controlling Feres reasoning must be kept separate from Part II-A's nonmajority intentional-tort analysis. No assault claim is presented in Richards. [Full opinion and separate writings](https://www.law.cornell.edu/supremecourt/text/473/52).

**Johnson, 481 U.S. 681, 686–692 (1987).** The Court applied Feres to a Coast Guard rescue death despite alleged negligence by civilian FAA controllers. The controlling opinion relied on federal relationship, compensation, and discipline; the nature of the tortfeasor alone did not defeat the service-incident bar. It involved an undisputed military mission. It does not supply a factual finding that Richards's accepted homeward journey was such a mission. Scalia's dissent, joined by Stevens, Brennan, and Marshall, criticized the statutory foundation and declined an extension, expressly reserving whether to overrule Feres because that request was not before the Court. The dissent is date-eligible published material, not controlling law. [Full opinion and dissent](https://www.law.cornell.edu/supremecourt/text/481/681).

**Stencel Aero, 431 U.S. 666, 669–674 (1977).** A contractor could not recover indemnity for the same service-incident injury that the servicemember could not recover directly. Its rationales included the federal relationship, compensation limitation, and military decisionmaking. The holding does not decide whether Richards's injury was incident to service in the first place. [Full opinion and dissent](https://www.law.cornell.edu/supremecourt/text/431/666).

**Simulated Minns, February 25, 1999.** The focused entering-law copy supplies the controlling eight-Justice §2680(a) proposition: actual governmental choice susceptible to protected policy judgment remains outside the waiver; a prescribed course, choice alone, or military subject matter does not automatically establish that exception. The whole-dismissal judgment had nonnested grounds and creates no majority Feres extension to every military causal connection. Feres, Stencel, Shearer, Johnson, and Brown retain their stated force. A protected-policy bar and Feres remain distinct. The material alleged conduct must be identified; neither the truck's ownership nor the word supervision proves protected discretion, and neither the word operational nor the allegation of negligence conclusively defeats it. Berkovitz and Gaubert supply the inherited distinction through the authorized Minns slice. No independently adjudicated §2680(a) defense appears in the supplied Richards lower judgments.

## 5. Source-supported alternatives for fresh neutral evaluation

These are the competing questions and remedial possibilities supplied by the record and authorities. They are not a recommendation or a new holding.

1. **Sustain the incident-to-service dismissal on the accepted personal-travel premise.** The supporting lower-court grounds are continuing active service, absence of pass/furlough, departure from the duty station, base location, the federal relationship and benefits, and the challenged maintenance/personnel decisions. The opposing sources are Brooks, the release from work and personal purpose, public-road access, and the panel's own discipline reservation. The disputed Education Center account cannot be substituted for the accepted premise.
2. **Reject the Feres dismissal under the existing boundary.** The petition relies on private homeward travel, authorized release, ordinary public-road danger, Brooks, and Shearer's distinction for vehicle-operation negligence. The contrary material includes Richards's nonfurlough status, travel immediately from work within the base, the complaint's command/supervision allegations, and Johnson's refusal to make the military character of the tortfeasor or direct discipline review indispensable in every service-incident case. Any rule would have to state why these actual facts fall outside the surviving service relationship, without making every public road or absence of an assigned task conclusive.
3. **Distinguish the pleaded theories.** The complaint separately alleges negligent operation, unsafe equipment/maintenance, and knowing assignment or permission to an allegedly unqualified driver. Shearer's driving/command distinction and the district court's actual rationale make claim-specific treatment a supported subject of inquiry if a general service-incident rule does not dispose of all of them. A partial disposition must identify which pleaded theory survives, its legal ground, and what remains below. The supplied materials do not establish that the theories are legally inseparable or that every supervision allegation necessarily presents the same command question as Shearer.
4. **Remand for a specified legal or factual reason.** The personal destination is assumed, not finally found. A court deciding the legal adequacy of the dismissal on that premise need not determine the destination against the claimant. If the operative legal rule makes a genuinely unresolved destination or command fact decisive, it may leave its determination below. A changed standard or need to separate theories may also produce a bounded remand. The earlier summary's lack of research is not a merits ground, and remand may not be justified merely by calling duty status, permission, pass/furlough, or road character wholly unknown when the recovered record supplies them.
5. **Broader reconsideration of Feres.** The petition and rehearing dissent expressly seek it, and the Johnson dissent supplies pre-divergence criticism. That advocacy must remain separate from current law and the narrower simulation question. It cannot be selected through an assumed broadened grant. The current extraction supplies no outcome on scope or overruling.

If any claim proceeds, negligence, causation, applicable state tort law, damages, the pleaded contributory-negligence defense, and any properly presented independent FTCA defense remain for appropriate proceedings. Potts's admitted scope of employment must be preserved rather than described as entirely unestablished. No damages award, special evidentiary hearing, new production order, automatic benefit offset, or new filing deadline follows from the source record alone. An affirmance would sustain the Feres dismissal; a reversal or partial reversal removes only the identified ground and cannot silently adjudicate remaining liability questions.
