## Event and question

No. 98-2060, review under §1254(1) of Carpenter v. Mohr, 163 F.3d 938 (6th Cir. December 18, 1998), rehearing denied March 24, 1999. Argument February 28, 2000; scheduled decision April 25, 2000. The supplied grant asks whether a procedurally defaulted ineffective-assistance claim may supply cause for another federal claim's default without itself satisfying cause and prejudice. The State seeks reversal/remand of the Sixth Circuit's contrary rationale. All nine members were seated; no recusal is established; normal quorum and majority rules apply.

## Record and lower dispositions

Carpenter entered an Alford guilty plea to aggravated murder and robbery while maintaining innocence. The three-judge culpability hearing received a prosecutor's factual recitation, with defense counsel neither admitting nor denying it; no witnesses or exhibits were introduced at that hearing. The separate mitigation hearing and other investigative evidence must not be confused with the culpability-hearing record. Direct appellate counsel challenged the parole-eligibility sentence rather than sufficiency of the evidence. The direct appeal was affirmed and Carpenter did not seek Ohio Supreme Court review then.

After collateral proceedings, Carpenter applied July 15, 1994 to reopen direct review under Ohio Appellate Rule 26(B), alleging ineffective appellate assistance for omitting sufficiency. The rule allowed application within ninety days of journalization unless good cause supported later filing. Because the direct judgment preceded the rule, Ohio used the rule's July 1, 1993 effective date as the starting point. The state court rejected the application as late without good cause; Ohio's Supreme Court affirmed on January 31, 1996 by reference to State v. Reddick.

The federal petition was filed May 3, 1996. It asserted evidentiary insufficiency, ineffective appellate assistance, and lack of a meaningful state opportunity under Rule 26(B). The district court accepted that the sufficiency claim was defaulted, but found Rule 26(B)'s good-cause practice inadequate to bar the separate ineffective-assistance claim because it was inconsistently applied. It found ineffective assistance and ordered reinstatement of the direct appeal within ninety days or issuance of a writ. It treated the remaining claims as moot.

The Sixth Circuit affirmed the finding of cause but substituted a conditional new culpability hearing for the appellate remedy. It held that an ineffective-assistance claim used only as cause required exhaustion, not freedom from procedural default. Ohio conceded exhaustion of the ineffective-assistance claim. The court therefore did not decide adequacy of Rule 26(B), although it commented on inconsistent application. It found appellate counsel constitutionally deficient and prejudicial under Strickland-based principles, yet expressly did not decide the sufficiency claim itself. Footnote 11 reserved whether defense counsel's stance toward the prosecutor's recital constituted consent to its evidentiary use; neither side thoroughly briefed that question and the district court had not decided it. The State's direct appeal was treated as moot after disposition of Carpenter's cross-appeal.

Thus the premise under review is the Sixth Circuit's categorical exemption for cause claims. It is inaccurate to say no lower court addressed the second default or that Carpenter's claim already fails under an established adequate rule. The district adequacy ruling survives as an issue requiring review if the Sixth Circuit's categorical rationale is rejected.

## Entering law and supported alternatives

Murray v. Carrier distinguishes external cause from ordinary attorney error and recognizes constitutionally ineffective assistance as potential cause, normally requiring state presentation of that independent constitutional claim. Coleman v. Thompson preserves adequate, independent state default, cause plus actual prejudice and the miscarriage-of-justice route. Strickland, Evitts v. Lucey and Jones v. Barnes require constitutional appellate-performance analysis, not a duty to raise every available argument.

The entering slice supplies Gray's claim-specific preservation and genuinely applicable statutory-gate limits; Lambrix/Trest's distinction between jurisdiction and nonjurisdictional default and the absence of a universal default-first order; O'Sullivan's one complete ordinary state-review round (effective June 7, 1999); and Schlup's distinct innocence gateway. Relevant Standards titles are **Sequencing nonretroactivity and procedural default on habeas review** (Trest December 9, 1997; Lambrix May 12, 1997) and **One complete ordinary state-review round under former §2254** (O'Sullivan June 7, 1999). Their precise limits remain in the slice. O'Sullivan's source application was pre-AEDPA; Carpenter's May 1996 application must not be casually classified as pre-AEDPA. April 18 Williams decisions are a pending entering-law dependency: refresh their effective public law before modeling Edwards, especially for statutory conditions, ineffective-assistance analysis or hearing consequences. This packet adopts no guessed Williams rule.

State's path: treating a constitutional claim as cause still entails adjudicating that claim, and exhaustion alone cannot defeat the independent-and-adequate-ground principle. A defaulted ineffective-assistance claim could be used after the default is excused under cause and prejudice; the rule is conditional, not an absolute prohibition. The strongest objection is Carrier's specific language about exhaustion and the difference between seeking relief on the ineffective-assistance claim and using it to establish the reason another claim was not properly presented. The existing district adequacy ruling also prevents automatic final denial.

Carpenter's path: the State received the opportunity Carrier requires and has conceded exhaustion; a claim rejected on state procedural grounds may serve a distinct explanatory function even where it cannot independently support a writ. The strongest objection is that a procedural rejection supplies no actual state merits consideration and that constitutional adjudication remains the necessary intermediate step. A cause claim cannot gain immunity from defaults merely through relabeling.

Independent narrower path: even if the State prevails on the general rule, affirmance on an adequately preserved alternative adequacy ground might be possible only if the appellate record and necessary legal inquiry support it. The supplied question does not itself settle that ground. The safer legally supported remand leaves adequacy/independence and actual application, any excuse for the ineffective-assistance default, prejudice and any properly raised miscarriage-of-justice showing to ordinary determination. No finding of innocence, ineffective assistance, ultimate sufficiency or adequacy should be newly inferred from resolving the abstract nested-default question.

## Remedy and limits

Rejection of the Sixth Circuit's categorical rule removes its basis for the conditional culpability-hearing remedy; remand permits adjudication of the preserved state-rule and other genuinely applicable questions. It does not order immediate release, acquittal, retrial, a new evidentiary hearing or final habeas denial. Affirmance on the exhaustion-only rule would preserve the appellate remedy subject to the actual judgment's terms; affirmance on a distinct adequacy ground needs support at that ground's proper scope. Attorney error that does not satisfy the constitutional standard is not automatically cause. Lack of a constitutional right to counsel at a particular collateral stage cannot be bypassed by calling every failure ineffective assistance. The narrow actual-innocence gateway remains distinct from ordinary merits prejudice and does not itself grant relief.
