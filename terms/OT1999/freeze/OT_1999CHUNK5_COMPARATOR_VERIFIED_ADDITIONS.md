## Edwards v. Carpenter — complete

April 25, 2000, No. 98-2060, argued February 28, 2000; 529 U.S. 446. The Sixth Circuit's judgment, 163 F.3d 938, was reversed and remanded unanimously. Scalia wrote for Rehnquist, O'Connor, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas and Ginsburg. Breyer, joined by Stevens, concurred in the judgment and rejected the majority's additional default requirement.

The seven-Justice reasoning treats a constitutional ineffective-assistance claim used as cause as subject to its own state procedural requirements. If an independent and adequate state ground bars it, the prisoner must establish cause and prejudice for that default, unless the recognized fundamental-miscarriage exception applies. Merely submitting the claim in an untimely application does not satisfy the concern that the State have a fair opportunity to consider it. Murray v. Carrier supplies the constitutional-quality and state-presentation requirements; Coleman connects exhaustion with procedural default. The historical opinion's reliance on historical O'Sullivan and Stewart v. LaGrand must be checked against the corresponding effective simulated law, not imported.

The disputed cause claim concerns appellate counsel's failure to challenge evidentiary sufficiency. The state reopening application was rejected under Rule 26(B)'s ninety-day requirement and good-cause exception. The District Court found the rule insufficiently regular to bar federal review and ordered a conditional reopening of the appeal; the Sixth Circuit bypassed the rule's adequacy and directed a conditional new culpability hearing. The Supreme Court left adequacy, cause and prejudice for the counsel-claim default, counsel's constitutional effectiveness and ultimate relief unresolved. It did not find Ohio's rule adequate.

Breyer and Stevens reasoned that once the counsel claim was exhausted or unavailable in state court, its function as cause remained a federal question like other causes. They opposed the extra layer of default litigation. They nevertheless required remand because the prisoner still had to establish constitutional ineffectiveness or another cause and his underlying constitutional claim before receiving relief. Their judgment agreement is not a join in the sequencing rule.

## Nelson v. Adams USA, Inc. — complete

April 25, 2000, No. 99-502, argued March 27, 2000; 529 U.S. 460. Ginsburg wrote for a unanimous Court reversing and remanding the Federal Circuit's judgment, 175 F.3d 1343. No separate writing or partial join.

The holding addresses personal liability entered at the same instant leave was granted to add Nelson as a party. Nelson did not challenge the permission to amend. Due process and the then-effective Rules 12 and 15 required a meaningful opportunity to contest personal liability before judgment. Rule 15(a) then allowed ten days after service of the amended pleading, in the circumstances described; it is not a constitutional ten-day minimum, and the Rule itself is not constitutionalized. No amended pleading had actually been framed, filed or served. Notice of a motion and an appeal after judgment did not supply the missing opportunity. Predicted failure on the merits could not replace it.

The Court rejected preservation and waiver objections to this opportunity-to-defend claim. Personal-jurisdiction or service objections were distinct; waiver of service did not waive time to answer the claim. The substance was fairly raised and passed on below without requiring particular verbal labels. Adams disclaimed veil piercing, and sole ownership plus control of corporate litigation was not held to create personal liability automatically. The Court distinguished deliberate pretrial concealment in Fromson; it did not adjudicate Nelson's ultimate liability. Issue preclusion remained open for a properly timed argument after pleading and response. The corporation's original liability was not vacated by the personal-process ruling.

## Beck v. Prupis — complete

April 26, 2000, No. 98-1480, argued November 3, 1999; 529 U.S. 494. The Eleventh Circuit's summary judgment, 162 F.3d 1090, was affirmed, 7–2. Thomas wrote for Rehnquist, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Ginsburg and Breyer. Stevens dissented, joined by Souter. No partial joins or additional writings.

The majority reads sections 1964(c) and 1962(d) together through common-law civil-conspiracy principles: the private injury must result from racketeering or an act otherwise independently wrongful under RICO, not simply any act furthering the unlawful agreement. Beck's termination does not meet that requirement even accepting his summary-judgment evidence as true. The rule is not limited to listed predicate acts when a different kind of injury-producing conduct is independently wrongful under a substantive RICO provision. Nor must each conspirator personally commit the injurious act. Conspiracy recovery still reaches agreeing participants who did not themselves commit the substantive violation. Salinas's criminal agreement rule remains distinct from the private injury requirement.

Footnote 9 reserves the investment-injury rule under subsection (a). Footnote 10 separately reserves whether the conspiracy plaintiff must show an actionable completed violation of subsections (a)–(c), or an agreement plus at least one injury-causing racketeering act suffices. The unchallenged disposition of Beck's substantive RICO theories is not reopened. The decision does not declare the termination lawful under every other source or deny injury to business or property.

Stevens and Souter would permit recovery for business/property injury proximately caused by an overt act furthering an unlawful RICO agreement, even if the act is not separately a predicate or RICO violation. They read the statutory text as complete and disputed both the asserted common-law rule and the analogy between a tortious act and an independently wrongful RICO act. They preserved Holmes proximate causation and distinguished civil injury from criminal conspiracy's lack of an overt-act requirement.

## Christensen v. Harris County — complete

May 1, 2000, No. 98-1167, argued February 23, 2000; 529 U.S. 576. The Fifth Circuit's judgment, 158 F.3d 241, was affirmed, 6–3. Thomas wrote; Rehnquist, O'Connor, Kennedy and Souter joined the entire opinion. Scalia joined except Part III (agency deference), concurring separately in part and in the judgment. Souter also concurred. Stevens dissented with Ginsburg and Breyer; Breyer separately dissented with Ginsburg. Stevens's footnote expressly agreed with Breyer's Chevron comments, without a formal join in Breyer's writing.

The statutory coalition finds no prohibition on compelled use in section 207(o) or the regulations where employees have validly accepted compensatory time and no agreement on compelled use is assumed. Employee-request rights under (o)(5) assure timely use, subject to undue disruption, and are not the exclusive method of reducing the balance. Accrual limits, overtime compensation after the cap, permissible cash-out and termination payment remain distinct. The Court does not adjudicate a binding agreement restricting compelled use; its assumption is that no relevant agreement exists. It does not permit loss of accrued value or denial of protected requests.

Part III has five historical joins. The informal opinion letter, lacking force of law, receives persuasive respect under Skidmore, not controlling Chevron weight. The regulation permits additional agreement provisions; it does not require a compelled-use provision. Auer does not let an agency add a condition to an unambiguous regulation. Existing simulated Auer, NationsBank, Koray and other controlling law must be checked separately before adopting that reasoning.

Scalia accepts the statutory result but would give an authoritative agency interpretation Chevron treatment without requiring a particular formal format; the Secretary's position as represented in the Government's brief established authority. He nevertheless considered this interpretation unreasonable. Souter joins on the express understanding that the decision does not foreclose authority to issue future regulations limiting compelled use. Stevens's dissent treats compensatory time as a consensual exception to the cash-overtime rule and requires agreement over its use; employers can instead cash out or follow their agreement. He finds the agency's consistent position persuasive. Breyer preserves Skidmore's vitality when delegated interpretive authority is doubtful and regards the agency's interpretation as reasonable and persuasive under either possible deference framework. No plurality treatment of the five-Justice Part III is warranted.

## Slack v. McDaniel — complete

April 26, 2000, No. 98-6322, argued October 4, 1999, reargued March 29, 2000; 529 U.S. 473. Reversed and remanded, 7–2; Scalia and Thomas would affirm denial of appellate certification. Kennedy wrote. Part I was unanimous. Part II (post-AEDPA certificate requirements for a post-enactment appellate proceeding) was joined by Rehnquist, O'Connor, Scalia, Thomas and Ginsburg. Parts III and IV were joined by Rehnquist, Stevens, O'Connor, Souter, Ginsburg and Breyer. Stevens, with Souter and Breyer, concurred in part and in the judgment, rejecting Part II while joining the balance. Scalia, with Thomas, concurred in part and dissented in part. His writing expressly joins except the discussion in Parts III and IV of whether the renewed petition is second or successive; it does not reject the two-component certificate standard merely because the syllabus lists seven whole-part joins.

The 1995 district proceeding remained governed by pre-AEDPA law, but the 1998 appellate application triggered amended section 2253(c) under the historical majority's distinct-proceeding construction. The notice of appeal should be treated as a certificate application. A procedural dismissal requires reasonable debatability of both a valid constitutional claim and the procedural ruling; after merits denial, the constitutional assessment must be debatable or wrong. These are threshold showings, not merits adjudication. A court may first address the more apparent component; an indisputably correct procedural bar defeats certification. Neither a merely statutory underlying claim nor a bare claim of procedural error meets the constitutional showing.

A renewed petition after a mixed petition was dismissed without any merits adjudication solely for exhaustion is treated as a first petition, including new claims. Rose v. Lundy and the historical Martinez-Villareal discussion supplied the majority's grounds. Ordinary exhaustion, proper state procedural bars, applicable AEDPA provisions and lawful conditions against repetitious litigation remain available. A known violated return condition can have its own dismissal consequence; Slack's initial dismissal was unconditional and without prejudice. The holding does not decide the merits or find all renewed claims exhausted. The Court corrected the successive-petition ground but left Slack's constitutional certificate showing to remand, because the issue had not been addressed under the right appellate standard.

Stevens, Souter and Breyer would apply the pre-AEDPA certificate regime to an appeal from a proceeding commenced before enactment; they nevertheless joined the remaining rules and reversal. Scalia and Thomas accepted temporal application of amended section 2253 and the certificate framework, but would limit post-exhaustion renewal to the original claims. The added new and still-unexhausted claims made this petition successive on their view. They distinguished Martinez-Villareal's renewal of an originally raised premature claim and rejected erasing the initial petition from the successive-petition inquiry. Their disagreement was not a general bar on appellate review of procedural dismissals.
