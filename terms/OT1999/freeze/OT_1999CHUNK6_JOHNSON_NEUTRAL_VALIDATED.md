# Johnson v. United States — fresh neutral validation

Engine stage 3A only; no current-case vote or proposed disposition. No. 99-5153; argued February 22, 2000, verified through the separate clean official-header receipt. Authorized merits event May 15, 2000, reviewing the Sixth Circuit's affirmance of imprisonment followed by renewed supervised release after revocation. The original Class D felony occurred in October 1993, the original sentence in March 1994, and §3583(h) was enacted September 13, 1994. The supplied nine-member Court has no identified nonparticipation. Only the eight Associates are modeled in the next stage.

## Validated record and distinct questions

The original sentence imposed twenty-five months' imprisonment and three years of supervised release. Supervision began August 14, 1995. About seven months later Johnson was arrested and subsequently convicted of state forgery-related offenses; he also left the district without permission. Revocation produced eighteen months' imprisonment and twelve months' further supervision. The district court did not identify the statutory authority for renewed supervision. The Sixth Circuit sustained it by treating the later violations as the relevant conduct for applying §3583(h).

The questions are separate: which event controls the temporal inquiry; whether Congress directed the 1994 provision to reach this earlier offense; and whether offense-date §3583 independently authorized the prison-plus-supervision disposition. A constitutional Ex Post Facto question is not necessarily reached merely because statutory nonretroactivity excludes subsection (h). Conversely, a finding of no increased punishment under an adequately supported construction of old law cannot itself supply Congress's temporal direction.

## Exact-version discipline and entering law

The 1994 Code reproduces the amended §3583 and quotes former (e)(3) in its amendment notes. Former (e)(3) referred to all or part of the actual supervised-release term, not the later phrase referring to the term authorized by statute for the offense. It permitted revocation and imprisonment without credit for time already served on supervision, on a preponderance finding of a violation and subject to the applicable procedure and policy statements. The Class C/D cap was two years. Former (e)(1) permitted termination and discharge after one year when justified by conduct and justice; (e)(2) permitted extension if less than the authorized maximum had been imposed and modification, reduction or enlargement of conditions before expiration or termination. Their terms cannot be silently conflated.

Subsection (a) authorizes supervision as part of a sentence to imprisonment, with mandatory supervision where another statute requires it. Its first-domestic-violence-conviction addition postdates this offense. Enacted 1994 (h) permits further supervision when revocation imprisonment is less than the (e)(3) maximum; further supervision cannot exceed the original-offense authorized term less imprisonment imposed on revocation. The eighteen-month term is below the two-year Class D prison cap, and twelve further months are below the eighteen-month remainder obtained from the three-year authorized supervision limit under (h). That arithmetic establishes no retrospective authority and is not a judicial construction of former (e)(3).

Landgraf/Rivers requires actual temporal direction before applying the presumption against new consequences for completed conduct. Corrective purpose does not alone establish retrospective reach. Granderson requires text and structure first and lenity only for genuine residual penal uncertainty; it does not announce a universal lowest-punishment rule. Its probation statute does not decide supervised release. Young v. Harper protects existing conditional community liberty but does not create sentencing authority. Cavanaugh/Morales/Garner distinguish a legally increased burden from every procedural disadvantage; their parole-opportunity rules do not by themselves resolve this statute. No later amendment or post-event sentencing doctrine is available.

The Court's effective March 1 United States v. Johnson decision supplies an additional, distinct rule: under §3624(e), supervision begins on actual release, and excess prison time does not automatically reduce its length. It identifies §3583(e)'s separate discretion concerning conditions and termination after one year when the statutory requirements are met; condition modification cannot accomplish an early termination that the termination provision forbids. That decision does not decide renewed supervision after revocation and cannot make the later text of (h) applicable to a 1993 offense. Its supervision-purpose reasoning is available, but this case still requires independent construction of the offense-date provisions.

Carmell, effective May 1, preserves Calder's separate prohibition on reducing offense-date legal proof sufficient for conviction while distinguishing competence, admissibility and ordinary procedure. It treats the rule's operation, not its label or unchanged reasonable-doubt instruction, as decisive, and leaves parole doctrine intact. That holding does not classify a new postrevocation supervision term or answer this statute's temporal reach. It reinforces the need to identify the actual retrospective legal change; the majority's proof rule and the dissent's witness-credibility analysis cannot be mechanically converted into sentencing positions. Their public reasons are available in the appended clean projection without predetermining this case.

## Supported competing paths

**Temporal path favoring offense-date law:** Revocation enforces conditions attached to the original criminal sentence. Violation findings use revocation procedures and a preponderance standard and may concern conduct not independently criminal. Attributing the renewed penalty to the original sentence avoids treating those findings as a new criminal prosecution and preserves the independent prosecution of new crimes. The strongest objection is that Johnson's new misconduct occurred after enactment and he could have conformed his conduct to the new supervision consequences; the model must explain why those facts do or do not make the violation the statutory relevant event. The original packet's bare attribution assertion cannot replace that analysis.

**Later-violation path:** The government can defend the Sixth Circuit's temporal reference by treating the new violation as the event generating postrevocation consequences. The strongest contrary argument is the continuing statutory connection to the original offense and sentence, not merely a generalized unfairness assertion. If Congress supplied no retrospective instruction and the provision attaches a new consequence to the original offense, the enacted-date barrier must be addressed even if the violation occurred later.

**Former-law authorization path:** The government can argue that revocation and imprisonment for all or part of the supervised-release term leave authority over an unserved remainder, read with subsection (a)'s supervision-after-imprisonment structure and the distinct use of termination in (e)(1). The purpose of supervised release may support a continuing transition after prison rather than requiring imprisonment to eliminate useful supervision. The strongest contrary argument is that revocation ordinarily cancels the term, that (e)(3) expressly authorizes prison but not a replacement release term, and that (e)(2) limits modification to a surviving term. Purpose cannot create missing penal authority.

**Former-law nonauthorization path:** Johnson can argue that the separately enumerated alternatives permit ending the term, modifying an existing term, or revoking it for imprisonment, with no enacted power to create a new term after revocation. The new (h) supplied that omitted power prospectively, and residual ambiguity cannot enlarge punishment. The strongest objection is that this reading may overread revocation as extinguishing every residual aspect despite the text's all-or-part formulation, and that later clarification does not conclusively prove the earlier statute lacked the power. The model must interpret the earlier statute itself, not simply count the competing circuit readings.

These are analytical legal alternatives, not assertions that all were briefed in a particular formulation. A claim that the old and new regimes produce identical permissible punishment requires an actual old-law construction and calculation, not an assumption. No challenge to the violation findings or original sentence is introduced.

### Former drug-possession probation revocation and the imprisonment range

**Current rule:** For controlled-substance possession triggering the former proviso, with an initially available zero-to-six-month Guidelines imprisonment range and no established lawful departure, revocation requires imprisonment of at least one-third of that range’s six-month maximum and no more than that maximum. ## Standards and Tests selection: Retrospective withdrawal of mandatory parole reconsideration

### Retrospective withdrawal of mandatory parole reconsideration

**Governing question:** Whether a later law may withdraw mandatory discretionary-parole reconsideration for an earlier offender by doubling hearing intervals or permitting individualized postponement for up to three years.

**Current rule:** The Ex Post Facto Clause forbids applying to a pre-enactment offender a later statute that removes every alternate mandatory parole-reconsideration opportunity by doubling the post-denial interval from twelve to twenty-four months, where ordinary parole depends on consideration and no retained ordinary means of obtaining intervening review is established. That binding loss of a meaningful release opportunity makes punishment more onerous despite unchanged initial eligibility, maximum sentence and ultimate release discretion; the prisoner need not prove the omitted hearing would have produced release. Relief restores the consideration opportunity, not an entitlement to parole. An individualized initial prediction of continued unsuitability and review of that prediction do not preserve later mandatory opportunities to present rehabilitation, changed circumstances or a materially mistaken forecast. Where the actual three-year deferral removes meaningful annual consideration while parole is legally available and no ordinary substitute is established, it increases punishment. The prisoner must show the earlier entitlement, retrospective actual withdrawal and a concrete connection to a legally available avenue to earlier release; inconvenience alone is insufficient. Once shown, the State must substantiate a substitute it invokes through its legal basis, practical availability and timely capacity to provide that consideration. The prisoner retains the ultimate burden of proving an unconstitutional increase in punishment.

**Authority by component:**

- Mandatory annual-to-biennial withdrawal, proof and relief boundary: Cavanaugh v. Roller — The binding loss of annual reconsideration increases punishment on this record, 1993-11-30. Part III of O'Connor's opinion, joined by Stone-Zsela, Blackmun, Stevens, O'Connor, Kennedy, and Souter; six Justices adopt the rule, its application to Roller, and the prospective remedy. Ginsburg's conditional agreement in her separate opinion adds no vote to this controlling coalition.

- The initial prediction does not preserve the meaningful later opportunity lost here: The initial prediction does not preserve the meaningful later opportunity lost here, 1995-04-25. Part II of O’Connor’s Opinion of the Court, joined by Stone-Zsela, Stevens, Souter, and Ginsburg; five Justices adopt the same rule, application and relief.
- The claimant proves meaningful loss; the State substantiates a substitute it invokes: The claimant proves meaningful loss; the State substantiates a substitute it invokes, 1995-04-25. Part III of O’Connor’s Opinion of the Court, joined by Stone-Zsela, Stevens, Souter, and Ginsburg; five Justices expressly adopt this bounded evidentiary direction and its application. Recognition of a source limitation in the dissent is not an additional join.

## Standards and Tests selection: Statutory temporal reach and new consequences for completed conduct

### Statutory temporal reach and new consequences for completed conduct

**Governing question:** How courts decide whether new legislation governs events preceding enactment.

**Current rule:** First determine whether Congress prescribed the statute’s temporal reach. If it did, that direction governs subject to independent constitutional limits. Otherwise ask whether applying the provision would attach new legal consequences to completed events by impairing existing rights, increasing liability for past conduct or imposing new duties concerning completed transactions. Such operation requires clear congressional intent. Pending litigation alone does not answer the question. Procedural, jurisdictional and prospective-relief labels are not substitutes for examining the provision’s actual operation. Legislative correction of a judicial construction, or a purpose to restore an earlier understanding, does not by itself establish retrospective reach; this legislative inquiry differs from the temporal operation of judicial decisions.

**Authority by component:**

- Sequential temporal inquiry: Landgraf v. USI Film Products — Determine statutory temporal reach before applying the presumption against new retrospective consequences, 1994-04-26. Stevens’s general-framework portion, joined by Stone-Zsela, O’Connor, Souter and Ginsburg; five Justices adopt this organizing rule. Scalia, Kennedy and Thomas concur on a distinct relevant-activity approach, not as additional joins at this level of generality.
- Corrective purpose and distinction from judicial interpretation: Rivers v. Roadway Express, Inc — Corrective legislative purpose does not itself give an amendment retrospective reach, 1994-04-26. Stevens’s opinion, joined by Stone-Zsela, O’Connor, Souter and Ginsburg, controls by five votes at this explanatory level. Scalia, Kennedy and Thomas accept the judicial/legislative distinction through their separate textual method, not through a presumed full join.

**Present operation effective:** Rivers v. Roadway Express, Inc, 1994-04-26.

**Holdings navigation:** Determine statutory temporal reach before applying the presumption against new retrospective consequences; Corrective legislative purpose does not itself give an amendment retrospective reach.

**Exceptions and limits:** An express congressional direction concerning the temporal operation of a statute resolves the threshold statutory question, subject to applicable constitutional limits. Such a direction does not alone determine whether Congress may constitutionally require reconsideration of an already completed Article III judgment. Landgraf concerns statutory consequences for earlier conduct in litigation properly before the courts and does not decide that separate final-judgment issue.

**Related current rules:** Congressional reopening of completed private federal judgments supplies Morgan Stanley's and Plaut's separate Article III rule for §27A(b).

### Conditional community release and revocation

#### Young v. Harper

**Docket or dockets:** No. 95-1598.
**Decided:** March 18, 1997.

##### Whether preparole community freedom is protected existing liberty

**Holding:** A prisoner released to live at home, work and participate in ordinary community life under parole-like conditions possesses conditional liberty protected by Morrissey. Legal custody, the preparole label, an overcrowding purpose or denial of formal parole does not dispense with revocation process.

**Proposition-level authority:** Opinion of the Court (Breyer), Parts II–IV, unanimous.

**Material application:** The August 1991 procedure cannot by itself establish a condition communicated at the October 1990 release or validate the March 1991 telephone return order. Silence in the earlier procedure does not establish all release conditions, prove compliance or make release irrevocable.

**Limits and questions not reached:** Five months is not a constitutional minimum. Formal parole entitlement, short furloughs, work details, ordinary institutional transfers and the later procedure’s prospective validity are not decided.

**Treatment of earlier authority:** Morrissey protects freedom already conferred; Greenholtz concerns initial release and Meachum ordinary internal transfer. Sandin preserves direct constitutional liberty alongside its sufficient, nonexclusive state-entitlement route; no exhaustive program-entitlement finding is required.

##### Which preliminary and final safeguards govern withdrawal of that liberty

**Holding:** Revocation of this community liberty requires Morrissey’s preliminary and final protections, with their distinct qualifications. Initial arrest and detention need not await a completed final hearing, but final revocation must rest on constitutionally adequate process.

**Proposition-level authority:** Opinion of the Court (Breyer), Part III, unanimous.

**Material application:** For arrest and detention pending revocation, a reasonably prompt inquiry at or reasonably near the alleged violation or arrest determines probable cause or reasonable ground to detain for a release-condition violation. The decisionmaker must be uninvolved, but may be a different parole officer rather than a judge. Notice must identify the inquiry, its purpose and alleged violations; the participant may appear, speak, present relevant documents and witnesses and, on request, question adverse informants in person. An officer’s finding that identification would expose an informant to harm permits withholding confrontation of that informant. A summary must state the evidence and reasons without formal judicial findings; supported probable cause permits detention and institutional return pending final decision.

The State must offer a final hearing if desired, within a reasonable time after custody and before effective final revocation, to resolve disputed relevant facts and whether circumstances justify revocation, including denial and mitigation. Minimum safeguards are written notice of violations or grounds, disclosure of adverse evidence, a personal opportunity to present witnesses and documents, confrontation and cross-examination unless specifically withheld for good cause, a neutral and detached decisionmaker, and written evidence and reasons. The decisionmaker need not be a judge or lawyer. Informal letters and affidavits may be considered; matters already decided adversely in another forum, including a relevant conviction, need not be retried.

**Limits and questions not reached:** Preliminary informant-danger and final-hearing good-cause exceptions are separate permissions, not commands to withhold confrontation. No exception, waiver, dispositive admission, prior determination or supported probable-cause decision is established here. Morrissey’s two-month discussion establishes no universal deadline or safe harbor; no new evidentiary burden, automatic-release rule or separate counsel holding is adopted.

**Treatment of earlier authority:** Morrissey’s flexible administrative safeguards govern in full, without converting revocation into a criminal trial or replacing it with a telephone instruction.

##### What relief follows from the failure to provide revocation process

**Holding:** The Tenth Circuit correctly reversed denial of habeas relief because this termination required Morrissey process. Oklahoma may still establish a legally available revocation ground through adequate procedures or rely on an independent lawful basis for custody.

**Proposition-level authority:** Opinion of the Court (Breyer), Part IV, unanimous.

**Limits and questions not reached:** The procedural violation proves neither sentence expiration, formal-parole entitlement nor compliance with every possible condition.

**Operative remedy or transition:** Affirmance returns the case for proceedings under the protected-liberty and process holdings; it orders no unconditional release, release date or new state deadline. Permissible interim detention remains subject to the preliminary and final safeguards.

**Treatment of earlier authority:** Lynce supplies no unproved exhaustion defect or waiver; Gonzales’s concurrency construction supplies no authority for summary revocation.

For the eight-Justice accepted-rehearing holding in Young v. Harper, No. 95-1598, decided March 18, 1997, see Federal Courts, “Accepted rehearing and certiorari finality.”

### Garner v. Jones — 2000-03-28

#### Event and question

Garner v. Jones, No. 99-137. Merits decision, March 28, 2000. Argued January 11, 2000. On writ of certiorari to the United States Court of Appeals for the Eleventh Circuit, reviewing Jones v. Garner, 164 F.3d 589, which reversed summary judgment for the parole officials and remanded. The question is whether Georgia may apply Jones's eight-year reconsideration deferral retrospectively in place of his offense-date three-year opportunity, given the asserted expedited-review mechanism.

Jones received life sentences for murders committed in 1974 and, after escape, 1982. The offense-date rule supplied initial consideration after seven years and reconsideration after denial at three-year intervals. The later rule permits intervals no longer than eight years. Following consideration in 1989, 1992 and 1995, the Board denied parole in 1995 and set reconsideration for August 2003, citing the nature and circumstances of the offenses and multiple offenses. Those facts establish neither suitability for release nor legal impossibility of earlier parole.

The Board's 1996 policy permits a deferral up to eight years when parole cannot reasonably be expected in the intervening years. Eligible life-sentence cases, including cases involving parole later revoked, may receive expedited review when changed circumstances or new information warrant earlier consideration. Members must indicate the interval when voting to deny parole. A custodian's affidavit describes full individual review, an individualized prediction and an existing expedited procedure. The regulation separately requires reasons for denial while protecting confidential sources and potentially discouraging diagnostic opinions.

**Authority:** Both holdings are supplied by Justice O'Connor's Opinion of the Court, joined throughout by Stone-Zsela, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas and Breyer. Seven directly join this application of controlling Morales. Stevens and Ginsburg agree with the governing opportunity protection but do not join the vacatur disposition or its justification.

#### Jones has shown the meaningful reconsideration opportunity withdrawn

**Holding:** Jones's actual eight-year deferral retrospectively removed the offense-date three-year occasion for consideration while parole remained legally available, satisfying Morales's initial showing of a meaningful lost opportunity. He need not prove that the omitted occasion would have produced release; the original prediction of continued unsuitability does not itself preserve consideration of later rehabilitation, changed circumstances or a materially mistaken prediction.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that Morales governs this withdrawal. That decision requires an earlier legal entitlement, its actual retrospective removal, and a concrete connection to legally available earlier release; the prisoner retains ultimate constitutional proof. Cavanaugh protects the meaningful consideration opportunity, not a promise of favorable parole. Weaver explains why a legally available avenue to reduced custody can matter despite discretion over the outcome, and Miller rejects unchanged outer sentencing limits as a complete answer. Collins confines the inquiry to punishment rather than every procedural disadvantage. Jones's multiple murders, escape and repeated adverse assessments bear on suitability, but do not establish that earlier release was legally impossible. The Court does not decide suitability, impose an actual-release probability requirement or invalidate every longer interval. It applies Morales to the established individual withdrawal and turns separately to the asserted substitute.

#### The asserted expedited procedure requires assessment under the actual substitute rule

**Holding:** A written discretionary policy and sworn description of an existing expedited-review procedure must be assessed for legal basis, practical availability and timely capacity to provide the consideration withdrawn; neither the discretionary wording alone nor an assertion that the procedure exists decides its adequacy. Because the appellate court rejected the arrangement on a broader categorical premise and the district court applied a different Morales premise, the judgment must be vacated for the proper inquiry under ordinary procedure.

**Controlling explanation:** Morales requires the State to substantiate a replacement it invokes without shifting the prisoner's ultimate burden. Georgia supplies a policy and actual affidavit evidence, which materially differ from an advocate's hypothetical power to advance review. Courts must consider that evidence and supported practice. But an assertion that expedited review exists does not itself establish an ordinarily usable route and timely consideration as a matter of law. Conversely, the policy's permission to advance review does not establish that such a route is impossible simply because it is discretionary or lacks an invariably enforceable hearing demand. The Court rejects both shortcuts. The remand preserves Jones's established initial showing and does not replace it with a requirement to prove likely release. It guarantees no opportunity to cure every evidentiary failure, orders no administrative study or automatic discovery, and prescribes no filing form or response deadline. If an adequate substitute is not established, the relief restores timely consideration under lawful rules while leaving the Board's lawful suitability judgment intact.

#### Precedent treatment

- California Department of Corrections v. Morales governs the actual opportunity and substitute inquiries. Its original authority, burden allocation and consideration-only remedy remain; this decision addresses the materially different Georgia arrangement it left open.
- Cavanaugh v. Roller retains protection against the binding loss of a meaningful avenue to earlier release without proof of the omitted hearing's result.
- Weaver v. Graham supplies the distinction between a legally available opportunity and a guaranteed favorable outcome; theoretical discretion does not establish a compensating substitute.
- Miller v. Florida supplies scrutiny of binding adverse operation despite unchanged sentence ceilings or a potentially identical outcome.
- Collins v. Youngblood preserves the punishment boundary and supplies no categorical exemption for parole procedure.

#### Resulting law and limits

Morales continues to govern retrospective withdrawal of mandatory parole consideration. Actual discretionary practices may establish a materially adequate replacement, but their practical availability and timely capacity require support. The Court establishes neither a general significant-risk substitute for that inquiry nor a rule invalidating every discretionary or longer reconsideration arrangement.

#### Source and record limits

[Jones v. Garner, 164 F.3d 589](https://static.case.law/f3d/164/html/0589-01.html) supplies the lower judgment, record history and policy account. The custodian's affidavit and Board instruments appear in the [record and briefs](https://archive.org/details/micro_IA40385014_0323). The affidavit establishes an assertion of an existing procedure, not an adjudicated finding that the procedure is ordinarily available and timely. No request by Jones for expedited review, rejection of such a request, new rehabilitation finding or statewide operational rate is established. The policy permits expedited review; it does not guarantee a hearing on every request or a response within a fixed period.

## Event

**United States v. Johnson**; No. 98-1696. Argued December 8, 1999; decided March 1, 2000. On writ of certiorari to the United States Court of Appeals for the Sixth Circuit, 154 F.3d 569 (1998).

The question is whether excess imprisonment after convictions were vacated automatically reduces the remaining supervised-release term. Johnson's original sentence included drug, firearm and felon-in-possession convictions. Following an appellate reduction from 171 to 111 months, the District Court granted an unopposed §2255 motion on May 2, 1996, vacated the two §924(c) convictions and reduced imprisonment to 51 months. Because Johnson had already served longer, it ordered immediate release. A three-year supervision term remained attached to the drug convictions. The District Court denied his request for roughly two-and-a-half years' automatic credit; the Sixth Circuit treated supervision as commencing when lawful custody should have ended.

## Holdings

### Actual release begins the supervision term

**Holding and operative rule:** Under 18 U.S.C. §3624(e), supervised release commences on actual release from imprisonment. Excess custody served before that event neither constructively begins supervised release nor automatically reduces its duration.

**Authority:** Kennedy's opinion of the Court, joined by Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg, Breyer; all nine adopt both holdings directly.

**Controlling explanation:** Congress selected release from imprisonment as the commencement event. Johnson remained imprisoned after the lawful custody term should have ended, but a legal entitlement to release is not the release that occurred. The separate sentence stopping supervision during imprisonment connected with a conviction addresses whether an already commenced term runs; it cannot commence a term that has never begun. Thus even the contention that the vacated convictions no longer support the excess confinement does not establish the requested credit. Imprisonment and supervised release also perform different functions: community supervision assists the transition after custody and is not automatically performed by serving additional prison days. That function confirms the text rather than supplying an exception to it. The Court therefore rejects the Sixth Circuit's constructive-commencement rule. It does not decide whether invalid custody tolls a supervision term that had already begun, or foreclose an independently available remedy for unlawful custody.

### The separate statutory adjustment powers remain available on their own terms

**Holding and operative rule:** Excess custody may inform the district court's discretionary exercise of §3583(e), but creates no automatic adjustment. After considering §3553(a)(1), (a)(2)(B)–(D), and (a)(4)–(6), the court may terminate supervision under §3583(e)(1) only after one year of supervised release and when the released person's conduct and the interest of justice warrant termination; §3583(e)(2) separately permits modification, reduction or enlargement of conditions before expiration or termination, and extension of the term if less than the authorized maximum was imposed.

**Authority:** Kennedy's opinion of the Court, joined by Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg, Breyer; all nine adopt both holdings directly.

**Controlling explanation:** The hardship of excessive incarceration does not disappear because automatic credit is unavailable. Congress supplied distinct discretionary means of addressing an individual's circumstances. Termination under paragraph (1) must follow the Federal Rules' procedure for probation modification and satisfy its one-year prerequisite and substantive conditions. Paragraph (2) uses the applicable probation-modification procedure and the provisions governing initial terms and conditions; reducing a condition does not authorize terminating the term before paragraph (1) permits it. Johnson may invoke the appropriate authority, but the Court directs no particular exercise of discretion. The enacted distinction answers the equitable objection without replacing the actual-release command or converting imprisonment into supervision.

### Statutory qualifications and issues not decided

Section 3624(e) separately makes supervision concurrent with any federal, state or local probation, supervised-release or parole term for another offense to which the person is or becomes subject during supervision. Its conviction-connected imprisonment tolling excludes imprisonment for less than thirty consecutive days. Its fine provision conditions release on agreement to an installment schedule for the imposed fine, ordinarily no longer than two years unless special circumstances exist. None supplies precommencement credit.

The other §3583(e) branches are not invoked. Paragraph (3) permits revocation under the applicable Federal Rules upon proof by a preponderance that a supervision condition was violated, with imprisonment without credit for previous postrelease supervision and limits of five years for a class A felony, three for class B, two for class C or D, and one year otherwise. Paragraph (4) permits confinement at the residence during nonworking hours, with telephone or electronic monitoring if ordered, only as an alternative to incarceration. The Court orders neither branch, creates no new sentence, and adjudicates no damages or retrospective sentencing-credit remedy.

## Precedent Treatment

The decision construes §§3624(e) and 3583(e) directly. No earlier Supreme Court holding is overruled or limited. The prior vacatur of Johnson's firearm convictions is accepted as the procedural history of his case; the Court does not decide the substantive §924(c) question.

## Law After Decision

From March 1, 2000, actual release is the operative commencement event under §3624(e); there is no automatic conversion of precommencement excess custody into supervised release. The different §3583(e) powers and their procedural, timing and substantive predicates remain independently applicable. Other remedies for unlawful imprisonment and invalid-custody tolling after supervision has begun remain unresolved here.

## Event

Carmell v. Texas, No. 98-7540. Plenary merits decision, May 1, 2000. Argued November 30, 1999. On writ of certiorari to the Texas Court of Appeals, Second District. The question is whether applying the 1993 amendment to Texas Code of Criminal Procedure Article 38.07 to earlier offenses violates the Ex Post Facto Clause by reducing the evidence legally sufficient to convict.

The indictment contained fifteen sexual-offense counts involving conduct from February 1991 through March 1995. Counts 7–10 concern conduct from June 1992 through July 1993, when the complainant was fourteen or fifteen. Before September 1, 1993, uncorroborated testimony permitted conviction if the complainant informed a person other than the defendant within six months; the reporting requirement did not apply when the complainant was younger than fourteen at the offense. The amendment extended that exception to complainants younger than eighteen and the reporting period to one year. The March 1995 report met neither period for the challenged counts.

The state appellate court sustained retrospective application while assuming absence of corroboration for its legal ruling. Texas preserves its contention that the trial record contained corroboration. The six counts involving conduct while the complainant was under fourteen and five post-amendment counts, including the life-sentence counts, are outside this challenge.

## Participation

Chief Justice Stone-Zsela and Justices Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer participated at argument and decision.

## Public Action

The Court reverses the judgment concerning counts 7–10 and remands for application of the former Article 38.07 requirement to the existing trial record.

## Judgment & Remedy

**Reversed and remanded as to counts 7–10, 6–3.**

**Supporting:** Stone-Zsela, Stevens, Scalia, Souter, Thomas and Breyer.
**Opposing:** O'Connor, Kennedy and Ginsburg.

The State's preserved contention that corroborating evidence existed remains for determination under the former law. If the existing proof fails that law's sufficiency requirement on an affected count, the consequence is acquittal on that count, not a new opportunity to supply deficient prosecution proof. The Court does not order immediate acquittal on an assumed absence of corroboration. The other eleven counts remain undisturbed.

## Holdings

#### A State may not retrospectively reduce the evidence legally required for conviction

**Holding and operative rule:** When the offense-date law makes already admissible testimony legally insufficient to convict without additional evidence unless its stated age or timely-report condition is satisfied, a later law may not retrospectively remove that additional-proof requirement. Ordinary changes in witness competence, admissibility or trial procedure remain permissible when they do not reduce the legal sufficiency required for conviction.

**Authority:** Stevens's Opinion of the Court, joined throughout by Stone-Zsela, Scalia, Souter, Thomas and Breyer. The same six Justices adopt both holdings and their stated limits.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that this amendment changes the proof the law requires, not merely the means of presenting proof. Calder v. Bull identifies retrospective reduction of the evidence required to convict as a distinct forbidden category. Collins v. Youngblood preserves that category while rejecting a general rule against every loss of a substantial procedural protection. Hopt v. Utah permits removal of a witness's incompetency because the amount or degree of proof required remains unchanged. Thompson v. Missouri permits additional handwriting-comparison evidence without directing that a formerly deficient evidentiary basis is sufficient.

Here the complainant could testify under either law. For counts 7–10, neither the former under-fourteen exception nor the six-month reporting condition applied; the March 1995 report also fell outside the amended one-year period. The expanded under-eighteen exception therefore permits her testimony alone to support conviction where the former law required corroboration. The continued reasonable-doubt instruction does not answer that distinct reduction in legally required evidence. A witness-specific reliability purpose likewise does not eliminate the former condition's binding sufficiency operation. The Court does not require proof of innocence, prediction of a different jury verdict or distrust of this complainant. Nor does it constitutionalize corroboration independently of the offense-date law or restore the broader procedural theories rejected by Collins.

#### The preserved corroboration question must be decided under the former law

**Holding and operative rule:** The constitutional ruling sustaining retrospective application to counts 7–10 must be reversed, and Texas's preserved corroboration contention must be determined on the existing trial record under former Article 38.07. Failure of that record to satisfy the former sufficiency requirement requires acquittal on the affected count; the remand does not authorize a new opportunity to supply missing prosecution evidence.

**Authority:** The same six-Justice Court opinion.

**Controlling explanation:** The appellate court assumed an absence of corroboration to decide the constitutional issue; it did not finally resolve Texas's contention that corroboration existed. The Court cannot turn that assumption into a finding for either side. Its constitutional holding restores the former legal condition, leaving the actual record to be measured against it. The accepted state-law consequence of noncompliance is evidentiary insufficiency and acquittal, rather than a mere admission error permitting the prosecution to repair its proof. The Court therefore neither orders immediate acquittal nor directs an unconditional new trial. The eleven unchallenged counts, including those supporting life sentences, supply no reason to deny review of these four convictions and receive no relief from this decision.

## Precedent Treatment

- Calder v. Bull, 3 U.S. (3 Dall.) 386, supplies the evidence-sufficiency category as retained and bounded by the later controlling cases; it does not freeze every evidentiary rule.
- Collins v. Youngblood, 497 U.S. 37, preserves that category and the rejection of a free-standing substantial-procedural-protection theory. Its overruling of Kring and Thompson v. Utah remains intact.
- Hopt v. Utah, 110 U.S. 574, is distinguished because removal of witness incompetency did not lessen the amount or degree of proof essential to conviction; its competence holding remains in force.
- Thompson v. Missouri, 171 U.S. 380, is distinguished because admission of handwriting exemplars added information for the jury without reducing the proof legally required. Its admissibility holding remains in force, subject to Collins's treatment of the older procedural formulations it cited.
- Cavanaugh v. Roller, California Department of Corrections v. Morales and Garner v. Jones retain their separate rules governing meaningful parole-consideration opportunities. They supply neither a trial-proof probability test nor a new burden allocation here.

## Law After Decision

The Ex Post Facto Clause protects the offense-date legal quantum of proof as well as the other retained Calder categories. Retrospective removal of this corroboration requirement crosses that boundary when the former age and reporting conditions are unsatisfied. The decision leaves ordinary competence and admissibility reforms available, creates no universal corroboration requirement and alters no parole doctrine. No precedent is overruled.

## Separate Writings

O'Connor, joined by Kennedy and Ginsburg, dissents. She would affirm because the age and reporting conditions regulate the credibility of a particular class of testimony, leaving the charged acts, lawful punishment and ultimate burden of persuasion unchanged. Hopt permits a formerly disqualified witness to supply evidence for an earlier offense; Thompson permits formerly excluded comparison evidence. For the dissent, removal of this special credibility disability has the same constitutional function. The State's sufficiency terminology and acquittal remedy show the rule's importance but do not alone determine its federal character: permissible evidence restrictions can also decide whether a prosecution succeeds.

The dissent does not exempt every rule called procedural or abolish Calder's evidence category. A true reduction in the required proof or burden remains distinct. It disagrees with the Court's classification of this age-and-report rule, makes no finding that corroboration actually existed, and does not treat the unchallenged sentences as eliminating the controversy. Its position would defeat the presented federal challenge without deciding unpresented state-law claims.

## Procedure After Action

The case returns through the Texas Court of Appeals for determination of the preserved corroboration contention from the existing record under the former rule. If an affected count lacks the legally required proof, the state sufficiency consequence applies. No release from the untouched sentences, automatic retrial or new evidence-gathering order is directed. Ordinary mandate procedure applies.
