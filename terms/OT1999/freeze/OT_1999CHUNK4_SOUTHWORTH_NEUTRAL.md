## Actual program, stipulated matters, and claim scope

In 1995–96 a full-time Madison student paid a mandatory $165.75 segregated fee each semester. Students who refused payment could not receive grades or graduate. About 80 percent supported nonallocable services such as health, athletics and student-union operations; that portion is not challenged. The plaintiffs also did not challenge the general authority to levy a fee, ordinary student government, nonideological organizations, or expenditure of nonobjectors' money. The case is not a demand to abolish every student activity.

The allocable program supported registered student organizations and other qualifying services through student-government processes subject to University approval. ASM governed the student allocation system, with a general grant route and the GSSF route, the latter involving a services-finance committee. Student organizations determined their messages and activities; the University disclaimed ownership of their expression. Most support operated by reimbursement. WISPIRG was a lump-sum exception, and the available record did not establish all uses of its money. The lower opinion recites concrete political and ideological activity by the challenged groups, including lobbying and off-campus advocacy. Those activities do not themselves prove that every dollar went to the same use or that the University adopted each viewpoint.

**The parties stipulated that the ordinary SGAF/GSSF allocation review and approval was administered without viewpoint discrimination and that the University did not use it to advocate a particular viewpoint. The referendum procedure is outside that stipulation.** Control's objective extraction reports the parties' express oral-argument confirmation of this limit. ASM treated referendum results as binding; a referendum could allocate, defund, or veto funding for a group. The record includes a WISPIRG funding vote and an October 1996 termination vote. The lower source reports $49,500 for WISPIRG, while control's objective recitation reports a $45,000 referendum allocation in 1995–96. The sources do not establish that these are the same accounting measure; no exact sum is necessary to the constitutional issue, and neither sum is used to calculate relief.

Policies described in the record restrict gifts/donations/contributions, legal services, and politically partisan or religious activities; a separate GSSF policy excludes primarily political groups and lobbying uses. The descriptions are not proof that all policies were effective, revised, abandoned, uniformly enforced, or facially constitutional. Respondents later questioned the partisan restriction while continuing the ordinary-allocation stipulation at argument. That is a party act with a defined scope, not a finding validating all written restrictions. No presumed policy revision or actual finding of comprehensive viewpoint neutrality may close the referendum issue.

The lower opinion explains that free-exercise/RFRA and state claims were initially pleaded and removed from the then-live appeal after the first premature appeal; its note 15 does not decide free exercise. The presently granted question and judgment address speech and association. The district court's constitutional decision did not reach free exercise. No adjudicated Establishment claim or finding of a particular payment for worship or devotional instruction is established. The initial neutral's phrase possible devotional uses names a hypothetical limit, not a supported violation or independently presented claim. Do not invent a devotional payment, revive dismissed claims, or treat religious viewpoints as identical to a devotional governmental function.

## Judgment below and remedy already narrowed

The Seventh Circuit applied Abood, Keller and Lehnert to reject compelled support by objecting students for the challenged private ideological organizations. It did not accept the University's general educational mission as sufficiently bounded germaneness, or its appeal to shared benefit/majority choice as a justification for forced ideological financing. It considered earmarking for organizational expenses insufficient to cure support for the ideological activity and did not regard lack of personal attribution as dispositive.

The lower court did **not** retain every part of the district court's broad injunction. It limited relief to objecting students' allocable fees and vacated the excess reaching all students and the prescribed detailed disclosure/arbitration/opt-out mechanism. It required a constitutionally adequate alternative without itself fixing a particular refund formula or comprehensive administrative design. A purely retrospective reimbursement would not necessarily cure the interim compulsory support on its reasoning. The overall lower judgment was affirmed in part, reversed in part and vacated in part. The initial packet's phrase broad constitutional liability and relief omits these operative limits.

## Correct entering law and the open question

Current Rosenberger requires religious-viewpoint equality within its defined privately edited, independently attributed, vendor-funded printing program. It leaves compulsory-fee challenges unresolved. Its lawful benefit does not prove that every compulsory allocation is valid; conversely, its reservation does not establish a right to an ideological opt-out. It does not authorize all devotional financing or require religious groups to lose otherwise available private-speech benefits.

Current Glickman is O'Connor's six-Justice holding for germane, generic, nonideological product promotion within a substantially collective agricultural regulatory scheme, without personal attribution and with independent speech preserved. Compelled funding can implicate speech even without ideology or attribution; shared benefit or majority approval alone is insufficient. An advertising-only program, ideological content, viewpoint penalties or interference with independent speech is outside that holding. The historical Glickman formulation recited by the lower court is not the governing simulated rule. Glickman's common-function reasoning is a material analogy, but the educational/private-ideological forum differs from its integrated economic arrangement.

Abood and Keller establish protection against compelled nongermane ideological support in exclusive representation and integrated-bar settings. Lehnert's Court opinion applies germaneness, the vital labor-peace/free-rider interests and additional expressive burden in the union setting; its fractured applications and separate statutory-duty theory cannot be collapsed into a universally agreed rule for universities. Gibson's current rule preserves timely protection of disputed nongermane dues, adequate explanation, the chargeability burden and prompt impartial review while permitting appropriate reduction or escrow alternatives. It neither requires universal opt-in nor decides whether student-forum fees are nongermane. Air Line Pilots v. Miller distinguishes offering impartial arbitration from making nonconsenting objectors exhaust it. These remedy principles become relevant only after identifying a violation and its scope.

Agostini's unified Lemon inquiry and effective Strout preserve the distinction between private expression and actual governmental financing of a devotional function. Strout permits refusal to pay integrated devotional tuition on that program's facts; it does not create a compulsory-student-fee rule. The Court may preserve a specific devotional-funding question without deciding an unestablished payment or claim. The initial neutral's assertion that no new reusable rule is required does not decide the question: the extension or distinction of compelled-financing doctrine in a broad university expression program remains open.

## Strongest competing analytical paths

One supported path treats a general, viewpoint-neutral private-expression program as a common educational function that a university may fund with a generally applicable fee. Its purpose is to make diverse student expression available, rather than appoint an exclusive ideological representative or make dissenters profess agreement. On this path, requiring the university to choose only subjects germane to an official orthodoxy or allow selective ideological vetoes risks defeating the forum's very purpose. Disclaimed private ownership, independent expression and viewpoint-neutral administration matter together. The argument must explain why Abood/Keller's compulsory-support objection and Glickman's express ideological limitation do not govern this distinct educational forum; mere lack of attribution, benefit, student majorities or compulsory enrollment is not a sufficient answer.

That general-program path must analyze the referendum separately. A binding vote that funds or eliminates a private group's speech can select viewpoints by popularity even if ordinary committees satisfy the stipulation. The record establishes the procedure and exclusion from the stipulation, but not a universal finding concerning every referendum's ballot wording or operation. A lawful bounded remedy may require further proceedings concerning neutrality and preserved policy questions rather than affirming the entire system. A rule allowing committees to use objective mission, accountability and anti-fraud criteria is not a permission to approve or reject ideological positions.

The strongest contrary path applies compelled-subsidy protection to actual financing of private political/ideological organizations. The students are not represented by a legally obligated bargaining agent or integrated bar, and broader education/shared benefit cannot automatically make all organizational advocacy chargeable. A varied set of favored views does not necessarily cure compulsory support for each, and no-attribution disclaimers do not remove the financial expressive burden. This path must address the University's distinct pedagogical forum, the consequences of subject-by-subject ideological exemptions, and the available line between a common forum expense and direct ideological organizational support. It cannot equate student access with compelled profession of belief or reject a program solely because private expression is religious.

If objector protection is required, relief must match the objectors, allocable fees, actually covered uses and procedural posture. The lower court has already removed the all-students injunction and detailed mandatory scheme. The Court may preserve a supported narrowed injunction, correct its legal standard and remand, or leave choice among adequate prospective procedures; it may not silently reinstate vacated district-court details, award an unsupported amount, or prohibit all student organizations. If the general program is permissible, reversal or vacatur of the general liability ground does not automatically validate the referendum, every policy exclusion or a hypothetical devotional payment.

## Justice-specific source map, without modeled votes

Stevens joined Lehnert's controlling germaneness framework and Blackmun's narrower political-lobbying analysis, and joined current Glickman's bounded collective-program rule. He also joined the current Rosenberger dissent's objection to direct devotional-production funding. The educational-forum argument therefore must be reconciled with his actual common-function and financed-expression limits rather than inferred from general ideology.

O'Connor joined Scalia's Lehnert statutory-representation-duty approach, authored current Glickman's limited collective-program ground, and separately concurred in current Rosenberger with particular emphasis on independence, disclaimers, vendor payment, a vigorous competing forum and the availability of a properly presented compulsory-fee objection. That last safeguard is a reserved challenge, not an existing opt-out holding. Scalia joined the same Lehnert approach, current Rosenberger's bounded printing holdings, and Souter's current Glickman dissent; his latter position rejects treating shared benefit as a sufficient ground for forced expressive financing.

Kennedy joined Scalia's Lehnert framework except its strike-preparation part and wrote separately to examine whether an expense reasonably serves a representative duty rather than decide by rigid labels. He authored current Rosenberger and joined Glickman. These sources support attention to actual program function and private control but do not decide a student fee or eliminate the compelled-funding burden.

Souter joined Scalia's Lehnert approach, dissented in current Rosenberger against direct devotional-production financing, and authored the current Glickman dissent against a separate compulsory expressive expenditure unsupported by a legally required representative function. His Glickman Central Hudson discussion is a separate commercial-speech framework and does not automatically govern political student speech. Thomas joined only Glickman dissent Part I's compulsory-subsidy ground, not Souter's Central Hudson part; his own separate writing, joined in Part I by Scalia, rejects loss of protection merely because financing uses money or commercial content. He joined current Rosenberger's specific holding and wrote a broader civic-benefit/history concurrence, which is not Court law. No Lehnert join is attributed to Thomas, who was not on the Court.

Ginsburg and Breyer joined current Glickman's nonideological collective-program rule and the current Rosenberger dissent's concern with direct devotional-production financing. Neither sat in Lehnert. Their actual Glickman joins must be tested against the ideological and educational character of this record, and their religious-financing positions cannot be turned into a disposition of a specific devotional claim that is not established here. Current Strout's majority and dissents, copied from the neutral projection, supply the effective religious-function distinction if that limiting issue becomes material.
