## Event and reach

No. 99-312, merits review of the Sixth Circuit's April 13, 1999 judgment, 173 F.3d 386, No. 96-6371, affirming a diversity judgment from the federal district court. Argument: March 1, 2000, as supplied by the control context's sanitized procedural-source extraction. Decision placement: April 17, 2000 under the authorized neutral calendar. The granted question is whether federal railroad-safety regulations preempt an inadequate-warning-device tort theory where federal funds participated in installation and the installed devices were operating. All nine Justices are seated; no disqualification is established.

Norfolk Southern seeks removal of the inadequate-device theory and relief consistent with preemption. The Court reviews that issue rather than the merits of every railroad duty. The judgment below also addressed comparative-fault sufficiency and included other negligence theories. Those facts constrain the remedy even if the granted warning theory fails.

## Supported facts, claim boundaries and lower judgment

Eddie Shanklin died on October 3, 1993 when his vehicle crossed the Oakwood Church Road crossing in Gibson County, Tennessee and collided with a Norfolk Southern train. Tennessee installed reflectorized crossbucks there in 1987 through a federally funded minimum-protection project covering 196 crossings in eleven counties. The lower court found no evidence that a federal employee individually selected crossbucks rather than active warnings for this crossing. Evidence described high-speed trains with restricted sight distance, moderately high highway and rail traffic, hazardous-material trucks and a prior collision. These are trial evidence recitations, not a new federal finding that all regulatory conditions existed at installation or that a diagnostic team made a particular decision.

The district court entered summary judgment against the excessive-speed and ditch-light theories on April 17, 1996. Four theories went to the jury: delayed braking, inadequate horn warning, vegetation, and inadequate crossing devices. The jury found both sides negligent, allocated 70 percent to the railroad and 30 percent to Shanklin, and found total damages of $615,379. Judgment was $430,765.30. The court denied the railroad's motion for judgment as a matter of law or new trial, and the Sixth Circuit affirmed. The published recitation does not allocate this verdict's damages among the four theories. It therefore supports a confined remand to determine the effect of withdrawing an erroneous theory, not an automatic judgment for the railroad on the entire action or automatic preservation of the existing award.

The lower court also held that the evidence permitted a reasonable comparative-negligence verdict under Tennessee law: sight obstruction, vehicle speed, horn audibility, the train crew's observations and expert testimony prevented judgment for the railroad as a matter of law. This is separate from the federal preemption issue. Federal review of the warning-device question does not itself disturb or adopt every factual characterization or state-law sufficiency ruling.

## Federal text and entering law

The applicable statutory framework is former 45 U.S.C. §434, recodified without material change as 49 U.S.C. §20106 in 1994. The 1998 official Code confirms national uniformity to the extent practicable; State requirements may remain until a federal regulation or order covers the subject. An additional or more stringent covered requirement survives only if necessary to eliminate or reduce an essentially local safety hazard, not incompatible with federal law, and not an unreasonable burden on interstate commerce. These are cumulative conditions. Local conditions alone do not establish the exception, and the packet supplies no separate established local-hazard claim.

Sections 646.214(b)(3)–(4) distinguish federally participating installations. Under (b)(3)(i), gates with flashing lights are required for the listed conditions: multiple main tracks; nearby tracks that can conceal another approaching train; high-speed trains with limited sight distance; high speed combined with moderately high highway and rail traffic; high highway or train volumes, substantial schoolbus or hazardous-material truck traffic, unusually limited visibility, continuing accidents or combinations; or a diagnostic-team recommendation. Under (b)(3)(ii), when a diagnostic team justifies gates being inappropriate, FHWA may determine those requirements inapplicable. That permission does not command omission of gates. Where (b)(3) does not apply, (b)(4) makes the device choice subject to FHWA approval. The separate (b)(2) route addresses crossings within or near the terminus of federal-aid highway construction/improvement projects and conditions unrestricted opening/project acceptance on installed functioning adequate devices. Do not collapse that route into every federally funded sign installation.

The entering-law register's exact title is **Federal railroad coverage and separate warning and speed duties**, effective April 21, 1993 through simulated Easterwood. Easterwood unanimously requires substantial subsumption of the actual duty and recognizes operative installation predicates for (b)(3)–(4); general planning and MUTCD requirements are insufficient. Its warning-device claim survived because abandoned gates, redirected funds and nonwarning circuitry failed to establish the predicate. Its actual holding does not already decide the present dispute over the legal effect of approved and completed federally funded crossbucks. The speed holding is separate and does not make the present warning analysis an all-purpose railroad immunity. Current-term Locke and other preemption precedents retain their subject-specific settings; neither overlap nor general regulatory importance replaces the actual coverage inquiry.

## Competing supported positions

The railroad's position is that federal approval and participation in the installation under the applicable regulations displace independent state selection of different warning devices once installation is complete. The device-choice scheme allocates responsibility federally; tort relitigation cannot make each jury a new device selector. The lower opinion identifies supporting circuit approaches and the railroad's reliance on 23 U.S.C. §109(e), under which funds cannot be approved without adequate devices meeting the Secretary's standards, and then-applicable 23 C.F.R. §630.114(b), which conditioned authorization on satisfaction of federal prerequisites. These provisions support an inference of approval in the railroad's argument; they are not independent findings that an actual diagnostic evaluation occurred.

Shanklin's strongest answer is that federal expenditure on minimum signage makes regulations applicable but does not establish that the agency applied their criteria and determined that passive protection was adequate at this crossing. The Sixth Circuit adopted Shots' two-stage approach, rejected constructive approval, and held the railroad had not demonstrated FHWA approval of passive devices. It stressed the presumption against displacement and the gap in accountability if minimum funding eliminated state duties before any adequacy assessment. That preserves a meaningful difference between funding and actual coverage, but must confront Easterwood's allocation of device selection and the enacted regulatory conditions.

The railroad also argued that §409's protection of safety reports made proof of individualized approval impracticable. The Sixth Circuit answered that reports, surveys, schedules, lists and data used for the statutory safety programs are protected, while the Secretary's actual approval is not identified in that list. This evidentiary issue supports the practical debate; the granted question does not demand a general new §409 holding. Amicus arguments described in the lower opinion are those of the Association of American Railroads and trial-lawyer advocates; no Supreme Court amicus position is invented.

Supported alternatives: (1) reverse the warning-device preemption ruling because approved federally funded installation meets the operative federal criteria without a separate individualized study requirement, then remand for effects on the actual verdict; (2) affirm because applicability alone is insufficient and the unproved specific approval is necessary; or (3) vacate and remand if a legally relevant approval/compliance distinction requires findings not resolved under the lower court's categorical rule. A remand cannot presume that the plaintiff separately pleaded and preserved a claim enforcing federal noncompliance. The intake says no such separate claim was identified. Treat a federal-standard violation, poor maintenance of an installed device, operation of the train, vegetation, and state demand for additional devices as distinct duties. The fact that other theories actually went to trial does not establish that each survives every independent defense.

## Validated neutral source additions

The control context supplied sanitized objective recitations in `OT_1999CHUNK5_NEUTRAL_FACTS_FROM_SOURCE_CHECK.md`, Shanklin section. They identify 99 percent federal funding, installed advance warning signs as well as reflectorized crossbucks, and conformity of the installed devices to their device standards. Those limited facts do not establish that every device allegedly required for this crossing was present. They also confirm that no FRSA local-hazard exception is asserted here.

Respondent's original merits brief, pages 11 and 19–22, independently confirms a material argument omitted by the compact intake: she contends required advance pavement markings were absent. Her citations identify a 55-mile-per-hour road, an asserted MUTCD requirement for markings at that speed, the funding request's omission of markings, the paved surface and testimony describing Tennessee's minimum protections where the roadway could take them. The railroad's position, quoted in that brief, treated the installation choices as evidence of crossing-specific attention. These are contested legal and evidentiary arguments, not a new finding of federal noncompliance. They must be considered in deciding whether the actual warning-device duty is covered. They do not by themselves establish a separate federal cause of action or a separately preserved federal-compliance damages count. Similarly, respondent's argument that (b)(3)'s dangerous conditions required active devices must be tested under the regulation's independent terms and exception, rather than erased by saying only crossbucks were installed.
