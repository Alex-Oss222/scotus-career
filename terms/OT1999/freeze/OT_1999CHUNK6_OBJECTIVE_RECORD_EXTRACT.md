## Carmell v. Texas

- The indictment contained fifteen sexual-offense counts involving conduct from February 1991 through March 1995, when the complainant was twelve through sixteen. Two aggravated-sexual-assault counts carried life imprisonment; the remaining counts carried concurrent twenty-year sentences. The challenged counts are 7–10, involving conduct between June 1992 and July 1993, when she was fourteen or fifteen.
- Before September 1, 1993, Article 38.07 permitted conviction on the complainant's uncorroborated testimony if she informed a person other than the defendant within six months. The reporting requirement did not apply if she was younger than fourteen at the offense. The September 1, 1993 amendment extended the age exception to persons younger than eighteen and extended the reporting period to one year.
- The report occurred in March 1995, outside either reporting period for counts 7–10. The State contends that corroboration existed; the defendant disputes it. The state appellate court accepted the absence of corroboration for purposes of its legal ruling. Absence of corroboration is therefore an assumption for the presented legal question, not a finally established evidentiary finding.
- Six other counts concern conduct while the complainant was under fourteen, and five concern conduct after the amendment took effect. Those eleven counts, including the life-sentence counts, are outside this challenge.
- Texas decisions cited in the record characterize noncompliance with Article 38.07 as insufficiency leading to acquittal, rather than an evidentiary-admission error allowing a new trial. The State accepted that description of state procedure at argument. No new opportunity to repair deficient prosecution proof is assumed.
- Sources: [official reported record, 529 U.S. 513, 516–521 and notes 1–4](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/USREPORTS-529/pdf/USREPORTS-529-513.pdf); [published source text](https://www.law.cornell.edu/supct/html/98-7540.ZO.html). Preserve the State's corroboration contention in any remedy analysis.

## Fischer v. United States

- Fischer was president and partial owner of a billing-audit business, Quality Medical Consultants. In 1993 he obtained a $1.2 million loan from West Volusia Hospital Authority, which operated two Medicare-participating hospitals. The Authority received $10–15 million in Medicare funds that year. The investigation identified diversion of loan proceeds, speculative investment losses, and a $10,000 kickback to the Authority's chief financial officer. A jury convicted Fischer on thirteen counts; sentence was sixty-five months, three years of supervised release, and $1.2 million restitution.
- The issue presented concerns whether payments to the Authority meet §666(b)'s benefits element. The amount received and Medicare's status as a federal assistance program are not disputed. Section 666(b) requires more than $10,000 in benefits during a one-year period; the offense provisions separately use a $5,000 property/transaction threshold. Section 666(c) separately excludes bona fide salary, wages, fees, other compensation, or paid/reimbursed expenses in the usual course of business.
- Medicare conditions hospital participation on licensing, staffing, quality assurance, recordkeeping, facilities, and other standards. The published statutory/regulatory recitation describes periodic payments, sometimes in advance, and reimbursement arrangements addressing treatment, overhead, education, capital depreciation, and liquidity. Some categories of hospitals may receive special payments. Those program provisions do not establish that this Authority received every kind of special payment.
- Material source limit: the specific Part A/Part B allocation and receipt of special-treatment payments were not established in the record identified in the published materials. Do not transform the program's general features into a finding that WVHA received a particular subsidy.
- Sources: [official reported record and statutory/regulatory quotations, 529 U.S. 667, 669–679; evidentiary limits at 683 n.2 and 691 n.3](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/USREPORTS-529/pdf/USREPORTS-529-667.pdf); [record recitation](https://www.law.cornell.edu/supct/html/99-116.ZO.html). The source of the last limitation is held in the internal exposure receipt; the neutral proposition is only absence of identified proof.

## United States v. Morrison / Brzonkala v. Morrison

- Brzonkala alleged a September 1994 sexual assault by two fellow Virginia Tech students, resulting emotional injury, treatment, and withdrawal from the university. Those are complaint allegations, not findings after a merits trial.
- The district court found the allegations sufficient to state the statutory claim against the alleged assailants but dismissed that claim on constitutional grounds. The Court of Appeals considered the case en banc. The constitutional validity of the civil remedy under the Commerce Clause and Fourteenth Amendment §5 is the question presented. The separate Title IX litigation against the university is outside that question.
- Section 13981 creates a civil remedy against the person committing gender-motivated violence, including a person acting under color of state law. It authorizes compensatory and punitive damages, injunctions, declarations, and other appropriate relief. Gender motivation requires conduct because of gender or on the basis of gender and at least partly due to gender animus. No prior criminal charge, prosecution, or conviction is required. The remedy does not extend to random violence unrelated to gender or conduct whose gender motivation is not demonstrated by a preponderance; federal jurisdiction over divorce, alimony, marital-property distribution, and child-custody decrees is excluded. State and federal courts have concurrent jurisdiction over the statutory action.
- Congress documented effects on travel, employment, economic participation, productivity, medical expense, and interstate goods, and documented bias in state justice systems. The statutory damages defendant here is the alleged private assailant. The provision has no case-specific interstate-commerce jurisdictional element and is not geographically limited to particular state justice systems.
- Sources: [official reported record and statutory text, 529 U.S. 598, 601–607](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/USREPORTS-529/pdf/USREPORTS-529-598.pdf); [record recitation](https://www.law.cornell.edu/supct/html/99-5.ZO.html). Legislative findings are Congress's findings, not independently adjudicated facts about each defendant.

## Johnson v. United States

- The initial Class D felony occurred in October 1993. The March 1994 sentence imposed twenty-five months of imprisonment and three years of supervised release. Johnson began supervised release on August 14, 1995. About seven months later he was arrested and later convicted of state forgery-related offenses; he also left the district without permission.
- After revocation the district court imposed eighteen months' imprisonment followed by twelve months of supervised release. It did not identify the authority for the renewed supervision. The Sixth Circuit sustained the sentence on the view that the subsequent violations supplied the relevant temporal reference for §3583(h).
- Section 3583(h) was enacted September 13, 1994, after the original offense and sentence. The earlier §3583(e)(3) authorized revocation and imprisonment for all or part of the supervised-release term, without credit for previously completed supervision. Its text, along with (e)(1) termination, (e)(2) modification/extension, and (a) initial sentencing, presents distinct interpretive questions. Do not substitute the amended version of (e)(3) for the offense-date text.
- Sources: [official record, 529 U.S. 694, 697–699; former statute at 703–704](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/USREPORTS-529/pdf/USREPORTS-529-694.pdf); [record recitation](https://www.law.cornell.edu/supct/html/99-5153.ZO.html). The official report governs clerical discrepancies in the preliminary online text concerning the amending Act's year and the certiorari citation.

## Public Lands Council v. Babbitt

- The facial statutory challenge concerns three 1995 rules: the definition of grazing preference and permitted use; deletion of the requirement that applicants be engaged in the livestock business; and title to future permanent improvements made under cooperative agreements. The conservation-use rule is not among the three questions presented. The Secretary did not seek review of the lower court's rejection of that separate rule. An arbitrary-and-capricious challenge to the new rules was raised below but was not presented for Supreme Court review.
- The new preference definition describes priority against others for a permit or lease, attached to controlled base property. The separate permitted-use definition expresses forage allocation under an applicable land-use plan in animal-unit months. The agency represented that suspended use would remain recognized with priority for additional grazing in the allotment, and that the definitions did not cancel existing preference.
- Crucial scope correction: deletion of the regulatory livestock-business phrase is not deletion of the statute's eligibility language concerning bona fide settlers, residents, and other stock owners. Statutory preference remains for landowners engaged in the livestock business, bona fide occupants or settlers, or water-right owners. Noncommercial stock ownership and no stock ownership are different propositions. The presented rule does not create a general entitlement for organizations owning no livestock to obtain grazing permits for conservation purposes.
- The regular permit rules require substantial grazing use, authorize cancellation after two consecutive fee years without substantial authorized use, and allow temporary nonuse for financial reasons only with annual approval and no more than three consecutive years. The Secretary may allocate temporarily unused forage to others.
- The title amendment applies to future permanent improvements under cooperative agreements, such as fences, wells, and pipelines. It does not change the separate rules for improvements made under permits, and removable improvements may remain privately owned. No existing title or particular compensation dispute is resolved by this facial proceeding.
- Sources: [official record, 529 U.S. 728, 738–742, 744–750](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/USREPORTS-529/pdf/USREPORTS-529-728.pdf); [record and regulatory text](https://www.law.cornell.edu/supct/html/98-1991.ZO.html). The absence of an APA issue is a review-scope fact only.

## Geier v. American Honda Motor Co.

- In 1992 Geier was injured when her 1987 Honda Accord collided with a tree. She wore its manual lap and shoulder belts; the car lacked airbags and other passive restraints. She and her parents sued under District of Columbia tort law, including a negligent/defective-design theory based on the absence of a driver's airbag. The district court dismissed; the D.C. Circuit sustained dismissal on conflict-preemption reasoning.
- The controlling regulation for the claim is the 1984 version of FMVSS 208 as applied to model year 1987, rather than a later airbag rule. The standard offered passive-restraint alternatives and phased implementation. The litigation concerns a manufacturer that complied with that standard. DOT's contemporaneous explanation identifies a mix of restraints, gradual introduction, cost, technical questions, innovation, and consumer acceptance. Whether those stated objectives create an actual legal conflict with this tort duty is the issue, not an assumed factual answer.
- The Safety Act contains both an express clause concerning nonidentical state safety standards on the same performance aspect and a common-law savings clause concerning compliance with federal standards. Their functions must remain distinct. The presented general no-airbag theory is distinct from a claim of actual federal noncompliance or a genuinely vehicle-specific design defect supported by a separate record.
- Sources: [official record and regulation history, 529 U.S. 861, 865–868, 875–881](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/USREPORTS-529/pdf/USREPORTS-529-861.pdf); [record recitation](https://www.law.cornell.edu/supct/html/98-1811.ZO.html). Treat the lower procedural ruling as dismissal, not a jury finding or a merits factual determination.

## Jones v. United States

- On February 23, 1998, Jones threw a Molotov cocktail into a Fort Wayne home owned and occupied by his cousin. No person was injured; the house suffered substantial damage. A jury convicted him under §844(i), §924(c), and §5861(f). The grant concerns the §844(i) conviction; the other two convictions are not presented for direct decision.
- The house was used for ordinary family life. The Government identified an Oklahoma mortgage lender, Wisconsin casualty insurer, and interstate natural gas; it did not allege a home office or other commercial undertaking. The source recitation does not establish that the particular residence had formerly been rented. Any general proposition about past commercial use must not become an invented fact about this house.
- Sources: [official record, 529 U.S. 848, 851–852 and n.1, 855–856](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/USREPORTS-529/pdf/USREPORTS-529-848.pdf); [record recitation](https://www.law.cornell.edu/supct/html/99-5739.ZO.html). The formal grant also presented the constitutional question conditionally if the statute reached this residence.

## United States v. Playboy Entertainment Group

- Section 505 requires operators of channels primarily dedicated to sexually oriented programming to fully scramble/block them or confine them to hours when children are unlikely to view; the FCC specified 10 p.m.–6 a.m. Imperfect analog scrambling can expose some sound or images to nonsubscribing households. Digital systems did not have this bleed problem but were not yet widespread. The litigation assumed the programming was protected and did not allege obscenity; the Government disclaimed an interest in preventing children from viewing it with parental consent.
- Section 504 separately requires free, complete blocking of an unwanted channel at a subscriber's request. The district court considered this household-specific alternative together with adequate recurring notice: which channels contain sexual programming, possible signal bleed and children's exposure, the availability of free blocking, and how to request it. It required Playboy to insist on notice in its operator contracts. Notice and §504's blocking command are distinct components; §505's time window is not an exception to §504.
- The record recites that many cable operators chose time channeling; one survey reported sixty-nine percent. The district court found thirty to fifty percent of adult-program viewing occurred before 10 p.m. A study reported requests for blocking by fewer than one-half of one percent of subscribers during the period when §504 operated without §505. This rate by itself does not establish the cause of nonrequest or the effectiveness of a different notice program. Extent and frequency of discernible bleed and children's exposure were contested.
- Sources: [official record, 529 U.S. 803, 806–813, 816–820](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/USREPORTS-529/pdf/USREPORTS-529-803.pdf); [record recitation](https://www.law.cornell.edu/supct/html/98-1682.ZO.html). The appeal is from a three-judge District of Delaware court under the Act's direct-review provision.
