## Record and lower judgment

NCRL, its separate political action committee NCRLPAC, and Barbara Holt sought declaratory and injunctive relief. The Fourth Circuit describes NCRL as a nonprofit issue organization unassociated with a candidate, party or campaign committee, without a major purpose of electing candidates. NCRLPAC expressly supports candidates through contributions and independent expenditures. Holt supervises both entities and is a registered lobbyist. Their different functions cannot be collapsed into an assumed transaction by NCRL.

NCRL submitted two sample voter guides to the Elections Board. The first accidentally included NCRLPAC's endorsement sheet. The second omitted endorsements. The official told NCRL that distribution of the second guide would still violate the corporate political-purpose expenditure prohibition. NCRL refrained from distribution; NCRLPAC ceased soliciting without a disclaimer. These are the lower opinion's record recitations. The exact second guide is not reproduced, so neither its complete wording nor a finding satisfying Maine Right to Life's contextual standard may be invented.

The State argued that its construction limited coverage to electoral advocacy and that no credible prosecution threat existed. The Fourth Circuit found a live pre-enforcement controversy: the criminal statutes facially reached the activity, an official had applied the corporate prohibition to the endorsement-free guide, and the State supplied only its litigation position rather than a promulgated binding rule or an established prosecutorial undertaking. It rejected abstention; the opinion identifies neither a state supreme court certification procedure nor authority for a federal court to certify the construction to the Elections Board. That disposition and its supporting facts are available for independent threshold review, without treating a lower court's general presumption as newly controlling Supreme Court law.

The Fourth Circuit affirmed facial invalidation of the section 163-278.6(14) political-committee definition. It reasoned that the primary-or-incidental language and the separate influence-an-election limb swept issue organizations into comprehensive registration, recordkeeping and reporting backed by criminal penalties. It rejected a narrowing construction requiring removal of the incidental-purpose language or the separate election-influence limb. Its account of Buckley as mandating an organization-wide major purpose and listed electoral words must be assessed against the intervening simulated authorities appended below.

The lower court also affirmed invalidation of sections 163-269, 163-278.6(16) and 163-278.19's corporate contribution/expenditure prohibition. It concluded that NCRL fit MCFL on its actual nonprofit characteristics: no profit-making activity, shareholders or other economic claimants, and zero-to-eight-percent corporate receipts in 1993–1995 arising from individual telephone customers' decisions to direct refunds to the organization. Those are facts supporting the lower court's conclusion, not an independent Supreme Court finding that every nonprofit or every direct candidate gift is exempt.

The lower court reversed invalidation of section 163-278.13B's session-time lobbyist and employing-PAC contribution/solicitation restrictions. The donor-identification declaration under section 163-278.20(a) remained; on appeal the State contested controversy but did not contest the declaration's merits. ## Standards and Tests selection: Candidate financing, political-committee thresholds and membership exclusions

### Candidate financing, political-committee thresholds and membership exclusions

**Governing question:** Whether predominantly issue-oriented groups are exempt from FECA political-committee duties for otherwise covered candidate financing.

**Current rule:** Buckley and MCFL supply no categorical organization-wide major-purpose exemption for genuinely covered candidate financing. Section 431(4)(A) reaches a committee, club, association or other group receiving contributions aggregating over $1,000 in a calendar year or making expenditures aggregating over $1,000 in a calendar year, subject to actual definitions, exclusions and constitutional limits. Establish membership, covered transactions, actual candidate connection and thresholds before imposing duties. Where a contribution theory requires cooperation, consultation, concert, request or suggestion, ideological alignment, lobbying or partisan identity alone is insufficient. Independent expression retains its protection.

**Authority by component:**
- No categorical major-purpose exemption: Akins, Akins v. FEC — III. Major purpose is not a categorical exemption for otherwise covered candidate financing, Souter Part III with Stevens, O'Connor, Ginsburg and Breyer (five for both candidate-cash/conduit and broader covered candidate-financing subparts; Kennedy joins only the former), 1997-06-25.
- Distinct exclusions and reporting conditions: Akins, Akins v. FEC — IV. Membership exclusions and their distinct reporting proviso govern the remand, seven-Justice Part IV, 1997-06-25.

**Present operation effective:** Akins, 1997-06-25.

**Controlling construction:** Section 431(9)(B)(iii) excludes membership-organization or corporate communications to members, stockholders, or executive or administrative personnel when the organization is not primarily organized to influence federal nomination or election. Its separate proviso requires reporting direct costs of communications expressly advocating a clearly identified candidate's election or defeat when costs exceed $2,000 per election, except communications primarily about other subjects, under §434(a)(4)(A)(i) and, for a general election, (ii). Section 441b(b)(2) separately excludes corporate communications on any subject to stockholders and executive or administrative personnel and their families, and labor communications to members and their families; its nonpartisan registration/get-out-the-vote exclusion covers those respective recipients. Its separate-fund exclusion covers establishment, administration and solicitation for a political separate segregated fund by a corporation, labor or membership organization, cooperative or corporation without capital stock. Its loan exception concerns ordinary-course national or State bank loans under banking law and regulations. Sections 431(8)(B)(vi) and (9)(B)(v) cross-reference activity allowed by §441b without importing the $2,000 proviso.

**Open question:** Particular organizational or donor-disclosure burdens retain supported constitutional challenges. The construction supplies no automatic membership, transaction, threshold or liability finding.

## Standards and Tests selection: Express advocacy through limited context under §100.22(b)

### Express advocacy through limited context under §100.22(b)

**Governing question:** When a communication lacking a listed electoral slogan is express advocacy under the FEC rule and §441b.

**Current rule:** Paragraph (b) requires all of the following: assessment of the communication as a whole, only limited reference to external events, an unmistakable and unambiguous electoral portion suggestive of only one meaning, and no reasonable difference over whether it encourages election or defeat of a clearly identified candidate rather than another action. A reasonable nonelectoral interpretation excludes coverage. Within these cumulative restrictions, limited context does not exceed §441b merely because the communication lacks a listed slogan.

**Authority by component:**

##### The rule's cumulative restrictions preserve the statutory express-advocacy boundary

**Holding:** Under 2 U.S.C. §441b, 11 C.F.R. §100.22(b) does not exceed the statute merely because it permits limited context rather than an exclusive list of electoral slogans. Its coverage requires a communication considered as a whole, only limited reference to external events, an unmistakable and unambiguous electoral portion suggestive of only one meaning, and no reasonable difference over whether it encourages election or defeat of a clearly identified candidate rather than another kind of action; a reasonable nonelectoral interpretation excludes coverage.

**Proposition-level authority:** Opinion of the Court (Stevens). Parts II–III of Stevens's Opinion of the Court are joined by O'Connor, Souter, Ginsburg and Breyer: five direct votes. Stone-Zsela, Scalia, Kennedy and Thomas dissent from the statutory disposition and do not join this construction.

**Material application:** The Court holds that Buckley v. Valeo and FEC v. Massachusetts Citizens for Life require express electoral advocacy without prescribing a single verbal formula. The lower court itself recognized that MCFL included a marginally less direct explicit appeal; the disagreement is whether paragraph (b)'s contextual route necessarily crosses the narrowed statute's boundary. Its cumulative conditions need not do so. Election proximity, expected electoral effect, policy agreement, hostility toward a candidate, or an agency's preferred reading cannot replace the required unambiguous electoral direction. Limited context may inform the communication's meaning, but cannot displace a reasonable call to issue debate or some other action. The contrary concern that unchanged issue speech will be reclassified whenever an election approaches is answered at this statutory level by enforcing every restriction together, not by deferring to the Commission. A court must independently test the communication against them. This interpretation rejects categorical statutory invalidity; it neither decides the unresolved constitutional vagueness claim nor holds the rule valid in every application. It finds no MRLC publication unlawful and does not order enforcement.

**Treatment of earlier authority:** Buckley and MCFL retain their narrowing construction and protection of genuine issue discussion; the rule is permissible only within that boundary. Akins v. FEC's insistence on actual statutory conditions and independent judicial resolution is applied, without enlarging its distinct coverage holdings.

**Holding:** The categorical declaration that §100.22(b) exceeds statutory authority is vacated, and the case is remanded for the unresolved vagueness challenge and any properly presented application issues under the restrictive construction stated above. Subsection (a), separate contribution rules, distinct membership exclusions, and nonprofit protection based on actual characteristics are not invalidated or generally approved by this disposition.

**Proposition-level authority:** Part III of Stevens's Opinion of the Court, joined by O'Connor, Souter, Ginsburg and Breyer; five direct votes.

**Material application:** The Court removes the statutory premise of the judgment below, not the separate questions that premise made unnecessary to decide. A statutory construction alone cannot establish constitutional clarity in every application. No injunction against an otherwise unsupported enforcement threat, replacement rule, or judicial supervision of rulemaking follows. Day v. Holahan and MCFL require attention to an organization's actual political resources and financing; the acceptance of some corporate contributions does not establish MRLC's ultimate category, and no such determination is needed here. Lawful contribution enforcement and the Attorney General's dismissal remain separate. ##### Asking a candidate about public issues does not itself finance the candidate

**Holding:** Under 2 U.S.C. §441b, 11 C.F.R. §114.4(c)(4) cannot prohibit a publisher's mere inquiry to a candidate about positions or recorded votes as a contribution or candidate-directed expenditure. The required connection concerns campaign spending: actual cooperation, consultation, concert, request or suggestion concerning the expenditure remains governed by its own statutory predicates.

**Proposition-level authority:** Opinion of the Court (Souter), unanimous, at the stated narrow statutory and review-specific scope.

**Material application:** The Court holds that gathering information is not itself spending on a candidate's behalf. Buckley v. Valeo distinguishes independent expression from candidate financing; Colorado Republican Federal Campaign Committee v. Federal Election Commission requires a real candidate connection rather than a label substituting for that connection; Akins v. FEC likewise requires actual covered financing. MRLC's effort to learn the meaning of public votes supplies no finding that a candidate directed the guide's content, collaborated in distribution, or received an agreed campaign service. The Commission may investigate such arrangements when supported. Its concern that a survey could conceal collaboration cannot make every inquiry collaboration. This holding protects neither staged questions supplying campaign work nor a paid political advertisement disguised as an independent guide.

**Treatment of earlier authority:** Buckley's contribution/expenditure distinction, Colorado Republican's actual-connection requirement and Akins's transaction-specific coverage remain in force and apply to this information-gathering setting.

##### The existing written-only and equal-space declaration stands

**Holding:** In this private-petitioner review, the declaration invalidating §114.4(c)(5)'s compulsory written questions and replies and equal space and prominence remains in place. General rulemaking and enforcement authority does not authorize treating every independently edited guide involving candidate inquiry as a prohibited contribution or imposing these indiscriminate contact and editorial conditions on that premise.

**Proposition-level authority:** Opinion of the Court (Souter), unanimous, at the stated narrow statutory and review-specific scope. Stevens and Ginsburg join only within this private-petitioner review and its limited statutory ground; they do not adopt a general constitutional ban on contact safeguards.

**Material application:** The Court's limited statutory ground respects the difference between a real contribution and dialogue used to compose independent speech. A written record can aid enforcement, but administrative convenience does not establish that an oral discussion transfers value to a candidate. Miami Herald Publishing Co. v. Tornillo protects editorial allocation; Hurley v. Irish-American Gay, Lesbian and Bisexual Group of Boston protects the speaker's selection of an expressive composition. Those principles reinforce the need for actual statutory authority before requiring a private publisher to give every candidate equal space and prominence. The petition seeks broader protection for the private plaintiffs; no established cross-petition seeks enlargement of the Commission's rights by restoring restrictions already invalidated. That review limit independently preserves the existing relief. The Court does not decide that Congress could never authorize a specifically fitted contact safeguard, that accurate disclosure could never be required under lawful authority, or that proved coordinated services are exempt. A government-authored guide or a condition on a properly defined public resource presents different questions.

**Treatment of earlier authority:** Tornillo and Hurley reinforce editorial protection without becoming a universal prohibition on regulation of campaign contributions. Austin v. Michigan Chamber of Commerce is neither overruled nor treated as a delegation of every restriction on corporate speech.

##### The unfinished provisions and organizational status remain for the existing remand

**Holding:** The Court does not expand the declaration to invalidate the whole regulation or uphold the unresolved electioneering-message clauses. Their operative meaning, ripeness and severability remain for the District Court; MRLC's entitlement to nonprofit constitutional protection remains undecided on its actual funding characteristics.

**Proposition-level authority:** Opinion of the Court (Souter), unanimous, at the stated narrow statutory and review-specific scope.

**Material application:** The Court cannot supply an operative definition the appellate record leaves unsettled or decide severability from the label alone. The existing remand permits those questions and any properly supported interim-relief request to be decided under their own standards. Day v. Holahan prevents automatic denial of MCFL protection solely because an ideological nonprofit accepts some corporate money; it does not establish that MRLC's funding is insignificant, noncommercial or free of conduit features. The Court therefore neither grants an organizational exemption nor assumes commercial-treasury status. Genuine-coordination restrictions, express-advocacy provisions not successfully challenged, and distinct statutory membership exclusions retain their lawful scope.

**Treatment of earlier authority:** Day and MCFL require actual organizational and funding characteristics; Austin's surviving corporate-treasury rule remains available in its materially different setting. No prior decision is overruled.

### Campaign financing, intermediary contributions and nonprofit advocacy

#### Day v. Holahan

**Docket or dockets:** No. 94-672.
**Decided:** May 15, 1995.

##### Authority of the responsive-financing judgment

**Authority of the judgment:** The section 10A.25(13) responsive-financing invalidation is affirmed 5–4: Stone-Zsela, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy and Thomas support the judgment. Kennedy's plurality, joined by O'Connor, Scalia and Thomas, treats the adverse proportional legal trigger as an expressive burden; Stone-Zsela's judgment concurrence rests on insufficient programmatic connection on this record and rejects categorical invalidity. Neither framework controls, and no controlling rationale is established under Marks. The judgment binds the parties; the three independently majority-supported holdings below do not supply a unified financing rule.

**Limits and questions not reached:** The invalidated section 10A.25(13) mechanism increases candidate limits by independent expenditures opposing the candidate plus expenditures for the candidate's major-party opponents, excluding an association's communications solely to its dues-paying members. The provision itself has no $100 threshold; that figure describes the lower court's operational reporting scenario. The Board must notify every candidate in the race within 48 hours after receipt of the report or notice under section 10A.20(3), (6) or (6b), and within three days after notice must pay half the expenditure only to a subsidy-eligible candidate who has raised twice the minimum match, from an appropriation from the general fund. The lower court's electoral-result-eligibility concern describes implementation; it is neither a statutory payment exception nor a finding that every subsidy is immediately payable.

##### The special immediate-notice obligation falls with this trigger

**Holding:** Section 10A.20, subdivision 6b's additional immediate affidavit and service obligation cannot be enforced independently of the invalid responsive-financing mechanism on this scheme's demonstrated function and legislative relationship. Under Minnesota section 645.20, the special mechanism is essentially and inseparably connected with the invalid provision such that the legislature cannot be presumed to have enacted it alone; ordinary campaign reports remain enforceable within their separate terms.

**Proposition-level authority:** Stone-Zsela's Opinion of the Court, Part II, joined by O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy and Thomas: five direct votes. Stevens, Souter, Ginsburg and Breyer dissent on this component.

**Limits and questions not reached:** The additional obligation is a twenty-four-hour affidavit and service on the candidates and campaign treasurers in the affected race. The holding rests on essential legislative connection under section 645.20, not physical impossibility of reporting. The independent vagueness and overbreadth theories are not reached.

**Treatment of earlier authority:**

- Buckley, 424 U.S. 1: preserves distinct disclosure interests and associational protections; no categorical disclosure invalidation follows.
- Gibson, 372 U.S. 539: applies the need for a substantial relation between information demanded and the legitimate subject; does not eliminate ordinary reports.
- Brown v. Socialist Workers, 459 U.S. 87: recognizes genuine disclosure interests with protection for demonstrated associational harms; no new general exemption is created.

##### This $100 intermediary-contribution ceiling lacks sufficient fit

**Holding:** Minnesota's $100 annual ceiling on contributions received by the covered political committees and political funds is unconstitutional on the established burden and insufficiently fitted corruption/circumvention justification. Contributions remain distinct from independent expenditures: realistic routes around valid candidate limits may be regulated, but a broadly restrictive ceiling must preserve meaningful collective political association and fit an identified corruption or circumvention mechanism; no permanent dollar minimum follows.

**Proposition-level authority:** Stone-Zsela's Opinion of the Court, Part III, joined by Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer: nine direct votes, at this stated functional application.

**Limits and questions not reached:** Section 10A.27(12) covers aggregate annual contributions from individuals, political committees and political funds, but excludes a candidate's principal campaign committee and a political party unit defined in section 10A.275 from its covered recipients. Higher limits, coordination controls, candidate transfers and demonstrated conduit restrictions remain open. The record's substantial pooling burden does not establish a permanent constitutional dollar floor or equate contributions with independent expenditures.

**Treatment of earlier authority:**

- Buckley: applies contribution/expenditure distinction, corruption fit and effective collective advocacy; its historical dollar figure is no minimum.
- California Medical, 453 U.S. 182: preserves candidate-transfer anticircumvention regulation; its plurality is not a categorical answer for every committee.
- Citizens Against Rent Control, 454 U.S. 290: applies protection of pooling for collective political expression; does not immunize a candidate-contribution conduit.
- NCPAC, 470 U.S. 480: preserves independent advocacy at its scope; contributions are not silently equated with expenditures.

##### MCCL receives protection on its actual nonprofit characteristics

**Holding:** Minnesota may not prohibit MCCL's independent expenditures solely because its incidental business activity and absence of a formal policy against significant corporate contributions fall outside section 211B.15, subdivision 15's literal safe harbor. On the undisputed minimal-business-income and insignificant-corporate-funding record, its relevant political-association characteristics support as-applied MCFL protection; material commercial accumulation, economic claimants or corporate/union conduit funding requires a different assessment.

**Proposition-level authority:** Stone-Zsela's Opinion of the Court, Part IV, joined by all eight Associates: nine direct votes on this as-applied holding. No general overruling or endorsement beyond Austin's surviving scope is adopted.

**Limits and questions not reached:** The section 211B.15(15) safe harbor requires no business activity, no economic shareholders or claimants, no establishment by a business corporation or union, and a policy against significant contributions from them. Protection rests on MCCL's actual minimal business income and undisputed insignificant corporate funding. That checklist cannot foreclose this as-applied protection; materially changed operations or corporate or union financing may require reassessment. Direct corporate candidate contributions and ordinary business-corporation treasury restrictions are not decided.

**Treatment of earlier authority:**

- MCFL, 479 U.S. 238: applies protection to actual political-resource, structure and funding characteristics; descriptive facts do not foreclose this narrow as-applied claim.
- Austin, 494 U.S. 652: distinguishes materially different commercially accumulated corporate wealth; its surviving corporate-treasury rule is not overruled.

**Operative remedy or transition:** Affirm the Eighth Circuit on all four components. Maintain the permanent restraints on section 10A.25(13)'s responsive financing, section 10A.20(6b)'s linked special notice and section 10A.27(12)'s $100 annual ceiling, and MCCL's bounded as-applied protection under section 211B.15(15). Ordinary reports and otherwise lawful corporate and contribution regulation remain; no damages, general public-financing prohibition or facial excision of corporate restrictions follows.

#### McIntyre v. Ohio Elections Commission

##### Compelled identification on these political leaflets requires exacting scrutiny

**Holding:** A content-defined requirement that an individual identify herself on ordinary political issue leaflets burdens core political expression, including the choice to speak anonymously. The State must satisfy exacting scrutiny by showing narrow tailoring to an overriding state interest; viewpoint neutrality or the election setting alone does not lower that standard.

**Proposition-level authority:** Opinion of the Court (Stevens), joined by Stone-Zsela, O'Connor, Kennedy, Souter, Ginsburg and Breyer: seven Justices; Thomas concurs in the judgment only.

**Treatment of earlier authority:**

- Burson v. Freeman, May 26, 1992: applies the independently majority-supported scrutiny distinction; its fractured polling-place validity judgment does not settle this leaflet requirement.
- Anderson v. Celebrezze, 460 U.S. 780: distinguishes the election-administration setting; it does not reduce scrutiny for every election-related speech restriction.

##### The asserted voter-information interest does not justify this identification demand

**Holding:** A general interest in helping voters evaluate a source does not justify requiring this ordinary individual to place her name and residence or business address on her independently produced, truthful political leaflet. The Court leaves candidate finance, campaign committees, organizational spending, and other materially distinct disclosure settings for separate analysis.

**Proposition-level authority:** Opinion of the Court (Stevens), joined by Stone-Zsela, O'Connor, Kennedy, Souter, Ginsburg and Breyer: seven Justices; Thomas concurs in the judgment only.

**Treatment of earlier authority:**

- Talley v. California, 362 U.S. 60: applies protection for the ordinary author's choice of anonymous publication.
- Buckley v. Valeo, 424 U.S. 1: distinguishes campaign-finance reporting and its specific interests; those disclosure holdings remain undisturbed.
- First National Bank of Boston v. Bellotti, 435 U.S. 765: distinguishes corporate-advertising source information; its observation does not resolve this private individual's leaflet.

##### Fraud prevention does not justify this application to truthful independent advocacy

**Holding:** Although preventing electoral fraud is a weighty interest, Ohio may not impose this categorical identification requirement on truthful, independently produced individual issue leaflets without the necessary narrow fit to that interest. The enforcement order and $100 fine must be set aside; properly tailored fraud, impersonation, and materially different disclosure rules remain available.

**Proposition-level authority:** Opinion of the Court (Stevens), joined by Stone-Zsela, O'Connor, Kennedy, Souter, Ginsburg and Breyer: seven Justices; Thomas concurs in the judgment only.

**Treatment of earlier authority:**

- Talley v. California, 362 U.S. 60: applies anonymous-publication protection while distinguishing measures directed at fraud.
- Buckley v. Valeo, 424 U.S. 1: leaves candidate-finance disclosure and corruption-related interests intact rather than treating them as dispositive of this ordinary leaflet.
- First National Bank of Boston v. Bellotti, 435 U.S. 765: leaves materially different corporate source-disclosure questions outside the individual sanction decided here.

**Operative remedy or transition:** Ohio's judgment is reversed, 8–1, and the enforcement order and $100 fine must be set aside. No damages or invalidation of every disclosure provision is ordered; candidate, committee, organizational and other materially different disclosure settings remain outside this application.

### Akins v. FEC, D.C. Circuit No. 94-5088

Merits decision, June 25, 1997

#### Chronology and Posture

On review under 28 U.S.C. §1254(1) of the en banc D.C. Circuit judgment of December 6, 1996, amended January 3, 1997. Merits heard and decided June 25, 1997. The questions concern voter informational standing, review of the Commission's complaint dismissal under FECA, the major-purpose limitation on political-committee status, and the membership and transaction predicates for disclosure.

Six politically active voters challenged dismissal of their complaint that AIPAC had to register and disclose as a political committee. The Commission treated a major electoral purpose as necessary despite its assessment of likely campaign payments above the statutory threshold. A separate corporate-prohibition enforcement decision rested on uncertainty about membership communications. The district court sustained the major-purpose construction; the en banc court rejected it and recognized standing. No final finding that all alleged payments are covered expenditures is made here.

All nine Justices participated in the merits hearing and decision.

#### Judgment

The en banc judgment stands at the bounded scope stated here. The case returns through the statutory review channel for the Commission's consideration of the actual coverage questions. #### Controlling Propositions and Authority

##### I. The continuing denial of particular electoral information is a concrete injury

**Holding and operative rule:** Voters denied particular campaign-financing information allegedly required by FECA, which they seek for their own electoral participation, have a concrete informational injury even when many voters share it. Here the Commission's legal ground blocks a statutory process capable of supplying information still sought; the possibility of a later lawful discretionary choice does not defeat traceability or redressability.

**Authority:** Part I of Souter's Court opinion is joined by Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Kennedy, Ginsburg and Breyer: seven direct votes.

**Controlling explanation:** Public Citizen recognizes concrete denial of useful information without requiring that the injury be numerically exclusive. Lujan still requires the plaintiffs' own concrete interest, causation and likely redress, supported as the litigation stage requires. These voters identify electoral use of particular information that remains withheld. They assert more than opposition to AIPAC's policies or a general demand that government obey the law. The contrary argument treats any elector's interest in campaign oversight as generalized. That characterization overlooks the specific information and practical use alleged here; complaint status alone would not suffice. Bennett requires examination of the actual remedial chain despite retained agency choice. Correction of the legal premise actually used can restore the disclosure process, although the Court guarantees neither prosecution nor ultimate classification. Steel Co. involved reports already supplied and a completed deprivation that the requested remedies did not redress. Its result does not govern information still sought here. Bennett does not establish the facts of this case, and standing does not decide whether every requested item is legally owed.

##### II. FECA permits review of this dismissal's legal premise

**Holding and operative rule:** Section 437g(a)(8) permits an aggrieved complainant to obtain judicial review of a complaint dismissal within sixty days and permits correction of a legally erroneous coverage premise. A court may declare the dismissal contrary to law and may direct conformity within thirty days; if the Commission fails to conform, the complainant may bring an action to remedy the violation involved in the original complaint.

**Authority:** Part II has the same seven-Justice coalition as Part I.

**Controlling explanation:** Congress expressly provided this channel, unlike a claim based only on a general wish to supervise executive enforcement. Brown Group distinguishes an enacted enforcement route from the separate Article III requirement; Bennett requires use of the channel that actually authorizes the claim. The political-committee dismissal rests on a legal construction. Lawful enforcement priorities do not insulate that construction from the review Congress provided. Conversely, review does not compel prosecution, disclosure or a penalty. The distinct 120-day inaction route is not invoked. The Commission's close-membership explanation for its separate corporate-prohibition decision cannot become an unexercised replacement rationale for this dismissal. Legi-Tech preserves actual lawful Commission decisionmaking and statutory prerequisites; it supplies neither an automatic remedy nor a generic review bar. Genuine statutory ambiguities and remaining lawful discretion remain for appropriate agency consideration. The Commission's reading of constitutional decisions is not conclusive merely because it administers FECA.

##### III. Major purpose is not a categorical exemption for otherwise covered candidate financing

**Holding and operative rule:** Buckley and MCFL do not establish an organization-wide major-purpose exemption from political-committee treatment for genuinely covered candidate financing under §431(4)(A). That provision reaches a committee, club, association, or other group of persons receiving contributions aggregating in excess of $1,000 in a calendar year or making expenditures aggregating in excess of $1,000 in a calendar year, subject to the actual definitions, exclusions and constitutional limits; it does not literally make every group that makes a contribution a political committee.

**Authority:** Part III, including III-A for actual candidate cash transfers and genuine conduits and III-B for other genuinely covered candidate-connected financing, is joined by Stevens, O'Connor, Ginsburg and Breyer: five direct votes with Souter. Kennedy supplies a sixth vote only for III-A. Stone-Zsela reserves both subparts. No combination of separate rationales is necessary to make the five-Justice rule controlling.

**Controlling explanation:** Buckley's narrowing protects independent electoral discussion from being swept indiscriminately into regulation, while distinguishing resources supplied to candidates. MCFL's independent-spending setting likewise does not give every predominantly issue-oriented organization immunity for its covered candidate financing. The strongest contrary argument concerns the organizational and associational burdens of political-committee status beyond a single transaction. Those concerns require the actual statutory predicates and preserve supported challenges to particular duties; they do not justify a categorical exemption merely because lobbying occupies most of a budget. A candidate payment may fall within §431(9)'s expenditure definition, but its purpose, amount and exclusions must be established. Colorado Republican requires the real statutory candidate connection: cooperation, consultation, concert, request or suggestion where that contribution theory is invoked. Ideological alignment, lobbying, policy cooperation or partisan identity alone is insufficient. Genuine independent expression remains protected. The Court neither classifies AIPAC conclusively nor sustains every organizational or donor-disclosure burden on every application. The Commission must examine actual transactions after the relevant exclusions, rather than stop at its categorical major-purpose premise.

##### IV. Membership exclusions and their distinct reporting proviso govern the remand

**Holding and operative rule:** The Commission must determine membership, covered transactions, candidate connection, calendar-year thresholds and applicable exclusions before imposing political-committee duties. Section 431(9)(B)(iii)'s membership-communication exclusion retains its separate reporting proviso; §441b(b)(2)'s exclusions and the §431 cross-references retain their different terms.

**Authority:** Part IV has the same seven-Justice coalition as Parts I and II. The statutory construction in Part III governs the Court's remand notwithstanding Stone-Zsela's and Kennedy's narrower preferences.

**Controlling explanation:** Section 431(9)(B)(iii) excludes communications by a membership organization or corporation to its members, stockholders, or executive or administrative personnel if it is not organized primarily to influence nomination or election to federal office. The proviso requires a membership organization, including a labor organization, or a corporation to report costs directly attributable to communications expressly advocating election or defeat of a clearly identified candidate when those costs exceed $2,000 per election, except a communication primarily devoted to other subjects. Reporting follows §434(a)(4)(A)(i) and, for a general election, §434(a)(4)(A)(ii). This is neither the $1,000 calendar-year committee threshold nor an exception transplanted into §441b. Section 441b(b)(2) separately excludes corporate communications on any subject to stockholders and executive or administrative personnel and their families, and labor-organization communications to members and their families. Its nonpartisan registration and get-out-the-vote exclusion covers the same respective recipients. Its separate-fund exclusion covers establishment, administration and solicitation of contributions to a separate segregated fund used for political purposes by a corporation, labor organization, membership organization, cooperative or corporation without capital stock. The loan exception concerns a national or State bank's ordinary-course loan made under applicable banking law and regulations. Sections 431(8)(B)(vi) and (9)(B)(v) cross-reference activity allowed by §441b without importing the $2,000 proviso. The agency must apply each provision to the actual record. No final membership finding, coordination finding, threshold calculation, later membership rule, donor list, liability or penalty is supplied by this decision.

#### Precedent Treatment

- Public Citizen supplies the informational-access analogy; a concrete injury does not become abstract because widely shared.
- Lujan supplies personal injury, causation, redressability and stage-appropriate support; statutory complaint permission alone remains insufficient.
- Brown Group preserves the distinction between statutory enforcement authorization and Article III.
- Bennett supports a real remedial opportunity despite retained lawful agency choice; its particular agency-compliance facts are not attributed to this case.
- Steel Co. requires the disputed Article III inquiry before these contested merits and is distinguished because its reports were already current and its asserted injury completed.
- Buckley is applied to distinguish candidate resources from independent expression; its major-purpose narrowing is not extended to categorically exempt otherwise covered candidate financing.
- MCFL retains its independent-expression protection and does not establish the categorical exemption claimed here.
- Colorado Republican retains genuine independent-expression protection and requires an actual candidate connection; neither organizational identity nor policy agreement supplies coordination.
- Legi-Tech preserves lawful agency reconsideration and statutory predicates without creating a general immunity from legal-error review.

#### Law After Decision

FECA complainants with the concrete continuing informational injury established here may obtain review of the Commission's actual legal ground through §437g(a)(8). A predominantly lobbying purpose does not alone exempt genuinely covered candidate financing from the political-committee inquiry. Membership, actual transactions and distinct statutory exceptions remain essential, and genuine independent expression remains protected. No automatic committee status, prosecution, disclosure award or penalty follows. The Court overrules no precedent.

#### Separate Positions Relevant to the Decision

Stone-Zsela concurs in Parts I, II and IV and dissents from the major-purpose disposition. The unresolved membership-communications exclusion may dispose of some or all of the controversy. He would affirm standing and reviewability, vacate the rest of the en banc judgment, and have the Commission decide membership first. He therefore reserves whether Buckley and MCFL's major-purpose limitation reaches qualifying contributions or coordinated transactions, while preserving Colorado Republican's actual candidate-connection rule and lawful agency discretion.

Kennedy concurs in Parts I, II, III-A and IV and dissents from the broader merits instruction. Actual candidate cash and genuine conduits differ from an association's own expression even when cooperation occurs. His Colorado Republican position preserves that distinction. He would reject a major-purpose shield for the former, but require identification of the actual transactions and application of membership exclusions before resolving the constitutional treatment of the latter. He neither endorses the Commission's blanket exemption nor assumes independence merely from AIPAC's lobbying purpose.

Scalia, joined by Thomas, dissents. Lujan requires a concrete stake personal to the litigants, and statutory permission to complain does not supply it. In their view, the supported interest in learning about AIPAC's electoral influence remains the public's undifferentiated interest in informed oversight. Public Citizen involved denied direct access; it does not eliminate the personal-stake inquiry when plaintiffs seek regulatory classification and future third-party reports. They would vacate the merits judgment and remand for jurisdictional dismissal. They do not hold every shared information injury abstract, prescribe an affidavit requirement, treat agency discretion as invariably fatal to redressability, or decide the major-purpose question.

The Court itself directs no registration, report, penalty or prosecution and determines no final statutory violation.

**End of entry: Akins v. FEC, merits decision, June 25, 1997.**

### Federal Election Commission v. Maine Right to Life Committee, Inc., No. 96-1818

Plenary merits decision on submission, September 14, 1998

#### Chronology and Posture

Review of the First Circuit's October 18, 1996 affirmance, 98 F.3d 1, of the District of Maine's judgment and reconsideration ruling, 914 F. Supp. 8. No exact oral-argument date is established.

The question is whether 11 C.F.R. §100.22(b)'s context-sensitive definition of express advocacy exceeds 2 U.S.C. §441b as construed in Buckley v. Valeo and FEC v. Massachusetts Citizens for Life (MCFL), and whether the association had to request an advisory opinion before bringing this pre-enforcement challenge. MRLC publishes recurring newsletters, candidate surveys and issue advocacy, and accepts some corporate money. The District Court found actual cessation of speech after the rule took effect October 5, 1995, with no disputed material adjudicative facts. It invalidated paragraph (b) on statutory grounds, reserved standalone vagueness, dismissed the Attorney General, and denied further unnecessary relief.

All nine Justices participate.

#### Controlling Propositions and Authority

##### 1. This live challenge does not require an advisory opinion first

**Holding and operative rule:** An association whose recurring issue communications are subject to an effective FEC regulation and whose speech has actually ceased because of its credible application may seek pre-enforcement review of the rule's legal reach. Section 437f's permissive advisory-opinion procedure is not a compulsory exhaustion requirement for this challenge and cannot furnish an agency determination invalidating the governing rule.

**Authority:** Part I of Stevens's Opinion of the Court is joined by all eight other Justices. All nine join the statutory-design and actual-controversy ground; no broader exhaustion-balancing proposition is adopted.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that MRLC need not seek advance official permission for each prospective communication before presenting this facial legal claim. The trial findings establish ongoing speech activity and actual cessation after the regulation became effective, rather than a hypothetical speaker's objection to a dormant rule. McCarthy v. Madigan makes the enacted administrative scheme central; FECA makes this advisory route available without making it mandatory. The remedy sought is a legal decision about the regulation itself, which an advisory opinion cannot invalidate. The association supplies a sufficient controversy for the relief sought, so the Court need not decide Corbett's independent recipient-standing theory. Nor does this conclusion disturb dismissal of the Attorney General, against whom no supported enforcement threat was shown. A materially different administrative scheme or an entirely hypothetical future communication is not decided.

**Precedent treatment:** McCarthy's statutory-primacy principle applies; its broader discretionary balancing is unnecessary here. Its later prison-specific statutory modification does not impose exhaustion under FECA.

##### 2. The rule's cumulative restrictions preserve the statutory express-advocacy boundary

**Holding and operative rule:** Under 2 U.S.C. §441b, 11 C.F.R. §100.22(b) does not exceed the statute merely because it permits limited context rather than an exclusive list of electoral slogans. Its coverage requires a communication considered as a whole, only limited reference to external events, an unmistakable and unambiguous electoral portion suggestive of only one meaning, and no reasonable difference over whether it encourages election or defeat of a clearly identified candidate rather than another kind of action; a reasonable nonelectoral interpretation excludes coverage.

**Authority:** Parts II–III of Stevens's Opinion of the Court are joined by O'Connor, Souter, Ginsburg and Breyer: five direct votes. Stone-Zsela, Scalia, Kennedy and Thomas dissent from the statutory disposition and do not join this construction.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that Buckley v. Valeo and FEC v. Massachusetts Citizens for Life require express electoral advocacy without prescribing a single verbal formula. The lower court itself recognized that MCFL included a marginally less direct explicit appeal; the disagreement is whether paragraph (b)'s contextual route necessarily crosses the narrowed statute's boundary. Its cumulative conditions need not do so. Election proximity, expected electoral effect, policy agreement, hostility toward a candidate, or an agency's preferred reading cannot replace the required unambiguous electoral direction. Limited context may inform the communication's meaning, but cannot displace a reasonable call to issue debate or some other action. The contrary concern that unchanged issue speech will be reclassified whenever an election approaches is answered at this statutory level by enforcing every restriction together, not by deferring to the Commission. A court must independently test the communication against them. This interpretation rejects categorical statutory invalidity; it neither decides the unresolved constitutional vagueness claim nor holds the rule valid in every application. It finds no MRLC publication unlawful and does not order enforcement.

**Precedent treatment:** Buckley and MCFL retain their narrowing construction and protection of genuine issue discussion; the rule is permissible only within that boundary. Akins v. FEC's insistence on actual statutory conditions and independent judicial resolution is applied, without enlarging its distinct coverage holdings.

##### 3. The unresolved constitutional challenge returns for adjudication

**Holding and operative rule:** The categorical declaration that §100.22(b) exceeds statutory authority is vacated, and the case is remanded for the unresolved vagueness challenge and any properly presented application issues under the restrictive construction stated above. Subsection (a), separate contribution rules, distinct membership exclusions, and nonprofit protection based on actual characteristics are not invalidated or generally approved by this disposition.

**Authority:** Part III has the same five-Justice merits coalition. The four dissenters would leave the bounded statutory declaration intact instead.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court removes the statutory premise of the judgment below, not the separate questions that premise made unnecessary to decide. A statutory construction alone cannot establish constitutional clarity in every application. No injunction against an otherwise unsupported enforcement threat, replacement rule, or judicial supervision of rulemaking follows. Day v. Holahan and MCFL require attention to an organization's actual political resources and financing; the acceptance of some corporate contributions does not establish MRLC's ultimate category, and no such determination is needed here. Lawful contribution enforcement and the Attorney General's dismissal remain separate. The remand permits the unresolved claims to be adjudicated without treating today's decision as a directive to prosecute.

**Precedent treatment:** Day and MCFL retain their fact-dependent nonprofit protection. Austin v. Michigan Chamber of Commerce remains within its surviving corporate-treasury scope; nothing here approves every corporate restriction.

#### Precedent Treatment

- McCarthy: applies statutory primacy to the permissive FECA advisory process; no broad exhaustion exception is created.
- Buckley: retains protection of issue discussion through the express-electoral-advocacy construction; no exclusive vocabulary is required.
- MCFL: retains its express-advocacy construction and distinct nonprofit protection based on actual characteristics.
- Akins: applies actual statutory predicates and independent judicial review without extending its membership or political-committee holdings.
- Day: preserves actual nonprofit protection despite some corporate funding; no finding about MRLC's full status is made.
- Austin: neither overruled nor enlarged into general approval of every corporate restriction.

#### Law After Decision

Paragraph (b) is no longer categorically invalid on the statutory ground adopted below. Every cumulative condition remains binding: limited context cannot replace an unmistakable electoral direction or defeat a reasonable nonelectoral meaning. The Court leaves constitutional vagueness and concrete applications open. The decision changes no distinct membership exclusion, contribution predicate or nonprofit-status rule, and decides no separate voter-guide regulation.

#### Separate Writings

Kennedy, joined by Stone-Zsela, Scalia and Thomas, concurs in Part I and dissents from Parts II–III and the vacatur. He would affirm the declaration that paragraph (b) exceeds the statute while leaving standalone vagueness undecided. Buckley and MCFL protect an ascertainable express-terms boundary before publication: the speaker must supply the electoral direction, rather than having the regulator infer it from election proximity and external events. The Commission's treatment of character and qualification discussion permits precisely the shifting classification that the narrowing construction prevents. The no-reasonable-alternative formula does not cure that enlargement of delegated authority.

The dissent does not demand one canonical slogan. An explicit command, including a functional phrase unambiguously instructing a vote for identified candidates, remains different from policy persuasion with an electoral implication. Ordinary context may identify the candidate or explain express words; it may not supply the absent electoral command. Corporate funding cannot itself transform issue speech into a contribution or express exhortation. The dissent preserves subsection (a), actual candidate contributions, genuinely coordinated expenses and corporate-treasury regulation within lawful categories. It proposes no replacement regulation, general ban on context-sensitive interpretation, corporate exemption, or additional injunction.
