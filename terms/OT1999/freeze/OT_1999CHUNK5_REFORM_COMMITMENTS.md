## Common sufficient grounds

**Equal opportunity for timely cross-filing.** When Pennsylvania permits major parties to cross-nominate for the section 2870(f) offices, it may not reject an otherwise qualifying minor party's pre-primary nomination solely because its chosen candidate has filed major-party nomination petitions or later wins those primaries, absent an interest sufficiently connected to the unequal burden. The offices are common-pleas judge, Philadelphia Municipal Court judge, Philadelphia Traffic Court judge, elective school director and justice of the peace. Childress's nomination papers preceded the primary and she lost neither major-party primary. Ordinary primary finality therefore does not explain the distinction in her application.

Burdick's calibrated review governs: examine the character and magnitude of the actual injury, the precise interests asserted and how far those interests require the burden. Severe restrictions require compelling justification and narrow tailoring; reasonable nondiscriminatory restrictions ordinarily rest on important regulatory interests. Discrimination matters to the actual burden and fit, but it does not automatically convert every unequal election procedure into a severe restriction. The application fails without choosing strict scrutiny or announcing a new fixed intermediate tier for every discriminatory ballot law.

Timmons sustains the particular Minnesota minor/major nomination restriction in its complete system; its five-Associate merits coalition expressly did not adopt universal anti-fusion approval, a pure rational-basis rule or incumbent-protection justification. Pennsylvania has itself allowed the same overlapping candidate nomination for major parties at these offices. That feature changes the justification inquiry. Neither the candidate's continuing availability nor the Party's ability to endorse removes the unequal legal nomination opportunity. Norman likewise protects real party organization and independently qualified participation while preserving meaningful support requirements.

The four preserved interests do not justify this timely application. Childress is no sore loser and did not await primary results before filing. Additional party association does not add another individual candidate when major-party cross-nomination already exists. Voluntary nomination does not involuntarily divert the Party's votes, and allowing major-party alliances while forbidding this alliance does not itself encourage new candidates. General preventive election administration is permissible under Storer, Jenness and Munro as retained by Timmons; it still must explain the distinction actually imposed. The Commonwealth's failure to intervene is not default, and the Department's inadequate explanation is not a substitute for constitutional analysis. The late nonpartisanship and voter-identification arguments supply no preserved new factual record; neither establishes why timely minor-party participation must be excluded while major-party cross-filing is permitted.

The narrow common proposition leaves otherwise lawful uniform fusion restrictions, actual nomination deadlines, meaningful support rules, consent requirements, counting and ballot design available. It does not require duplicate votes, a separate party line, party-specific totals, judicial enactment of a uniform prohibition or reopening of a completed election. The independent associational claim remains undecided, and the lower court's deletion of that separate declaration remains appropriate.

**Rule 60(b)(6) and distinct prospective authority.** Timmons did not erase the independent equal-protection premise supporting Eshenbaugh's final judgment. On the motion actually pursued, no extraordinary circumstance justifies using Rule 60 as a substitute for the further review deliberately forgone. All eight affirm denial without conducting a fresh merits appeal of Eshenbaugh's post-primary nomination. Browder's finality boundary, retained in Agostini, reinforces this separation.

Rufo's *Modification of institutional consent decrees*, effective January 15, 1992, and Agostini's *Present legal change and prospective relief from an adjudicated injunction*, effective June 23, 1997, concern Rule 60(b)(5). A qualifying present change can permit prospective adjustment of a continuing adjudicated injunction; completed claims and voluntarily accepted independent obligations remain different. No participant treats finality as an absolute ban on every future prospective adjustment. But the present motion is (b)(6), Timmons did not remove its equality premise, and this decision does not convert the motion into a new (b)(5) proceeding or adjudicate an unpresented modification request.

## The material remedy fork

**Narrowing position — O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Thomas and Breyer.** Childress proves unequal pre-primary treatment. A later nomination, made after the major parties have completed their primary selection, is materially different for primary finality and orderly nomination choices. The existing injunction is not limited to timely cross-filing; it reaches nomination because a candidate files a major-party petition or wins a major-party primary without a timing limit. The Court should not turn Childress's valid equality claim into an unconditional right to add a post-primary nomination. Pennsylvania's severability rule preserves valid provisions and applications unless they are inseparably dependent on the invalid portion or the remainder cannot operate consistently with legislative intent. A confined pre-primary remedy is executable without legislating a new election code.

These five would affirm Childress's entitlement, preserve deletion of the associational declaration, vacate the injunction's unqualified breadth insofar as it lies within the Childress appeal, and remand for identification and adjustment of that relief to the pre-primary holding. They do not uphold every imaginable post-primary restriction, treat the final Eshenbaugh judgment as reversed, or permit a new merits attack on that judgment. Their holding leaves post-primary applications outside Childress's constitutional entitlement rather than announcing a general preclusion of all future equality challenges.

**Breadth position — Stevens, Souter and Ginsburg.** The operative bar turns on party status and prior major-party filing, not a uniform nomination-finality deadline. The Department's stated interests do not sufficiently justify its asymmetry. Eshenbaugh remained a Democratic nominee notwithstanding the Republican loss; the addition of a consenting minor party did not give him a second candidacy. The post-primary possibility therefore does not rescue this party-status bar on the supplied record. These three would retain the injunction as written for the listed offices, without adopting a universal fusion right or preventing an otherwise lawful future neutral timing restriction. They accept severability as a principle but find no demonstrated valid timing-defined application of this unequal bar that requires narrowing this relief.

**Strongest opposing considerations.** The narrowing side must confront the absence of an enacted uniform finality cutoff and the fact that primary winners already remain on the ballot. Its answer is limited: simultaneous selection and a subsequent added party designation need not be treated as identical, and Childress does not justify judicially guaranteeing the latter; it therefore confines the holding and relief rather than inventing a new statutory deadline. The breadth side must confront Burdick's valid primary-finality rules and Timmons' recognition that available endorsement does not require every nomination mechanism. Its answer is that neither case approves a status-based asymmetry lacking adequate connection to those concerns, and Pennsylvania remains able to enact a fitted neutral rule. No side relies on a missing filing or source as proof of default.

**Exact decree limitation.** The published source establishes separate proceedings and relief histories and quotes the December 9, 1996 order whose paragraph 4 the en banc court retained. It does not establish whether that order combined, replaced, duplicated or supplemented the earlier decrees. The original decrees and any consolidation/amendment entries would resolve that operational relationship. The narrowing commitments therefore require an implementation remand limited to relief lawfully within Childress review, with the final Eshenbaugh judgment and obligations not properly before the Court expressly preserved. They do not claim that narrowing Childress automatically restores post-primary enforcement against the Party, dissolves Eshenbaugh's decree or identifies one combined operative decree. This bounded remand resolves the granted appeal without deciding the missing decree relationship by inference.
