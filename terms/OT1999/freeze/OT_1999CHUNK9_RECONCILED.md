## Reconciliation of the disputed initial departures

### Stevens

His initial Path C gave determinative weight to the difference between endorsement and the legal nomination and found the participation interests inadequate. The exact historical dissent, 530 U.S. at 590?603, shows that he accepted meaningful associational protection for private candidate selection while expressly distinguishing the publicly administered primary. It cites his own Timmons dissent in explaining that distinction. He did not simply overlook the formal status of the nominee, assume party consent from a popular vote, or depend entirely on the absence of malicious raiding. His independent grounds included the State's responsibility for its electoral process, the survival of private governing and advocacy functions, the uncertain difference from easily accessible open or registration-based primaries, and the direct link between broader eligibility and electoral participation.

The prior simulated Timmons dissent likewise concerns a willing candidate, consenting parties and an additional nomination designation; it is not a general exclusion entitlement against prospective primary voters. Reform Party concerns unequal denial of an existing pre-primary cross-nomination opportunity and expressly does not decide an independent association claim. Its recognition that endorsement does not restore the withheld legal opportunity establishes a real burden in that setting, not the constitutional character or weight of every primary rule. Hurley concerns private expressive composition, and its government-expression and ordinary-service limits remain material. None of these premises changes the public-primary ground on which Stevens historically distinguished the cases. No changed evidence, grant, remedy or prior public position supports the initial reversal.

Reconciled commitment: affirm as to both state and congressional nominations on the presented associational claim. The State may broaden participation in this state-required and state-financed nominating election without handing party officials a constitutional veto over the electorate. Alternatively, applying actual-burden review, the retained private party functions, trial-supported limited operational injury, and interests in representative government, choice and participation justify this system. He accepts that sincere crossover differs from raiding and that party bylaws remain opposed; neither concession changes his institutional distinction. He need not deny that nominations are politically important to reach that conclusion. No premise that every public regulation is exempt from constitutional review is adopted.

Strongest objection: the official nominee speaks for the party and an outsider-influenced choice may change its expression. His response is that a public election remains a public electoral function even though the winner carries party status, while the party retains constitutionally protected internal choices and advocacy. Protecting voluntary nomination and ballot access against exclusion does not necessarily create a right to exclude qualified voters from the State's primary. He also rejects treating one impermissible asserted purpose as ending review when other adequate interests independently support the law. His contextual alternative does not rest on new numerical proof or on treating the trial court's legal burden label as binding.

Join barrier and writing function: he will not join the severe-burden holding, an insufficient-fit reversal, or a private-association equation that determines the public primary. If the Court reverses, a dissent would explain the public-function distinction and alternative balancing. He reserves, and does not decide, the unpresented Elections Clause issue; this supplies neither an independent ground nor a separate remedy here.

### Ginsburg

Her historical join is Part I of Stevens's dissent, not the Elections Clause discussion. That exact join supplies more specific evidence than a generalized analogy to her Timmons dissent. The initial model gave the legal-nomination principle a reach that her historical Part I expressly rejected for public primary participation. The revised neutral packet preserves the same trial posture, qualified crossover findings, private-party alternatives, and public mechanism. Her new Reform Party join adds an equality holding, preserves contextual review and deletes the independent associational declaration; it does not require her to equate consensual fusion with excluding voters from a public primary. The surviving Hurley distinction supplies no contrary constraint.

Reconciled commitment: affirm the state and congressional applications against the presented associational challenge. She supports both the protected-private-party/public-electoral-function distinction and the alternative conclusion that this nonsevere burden is justified by broader participation, choice and representativeness. The strongest contrary argument is the lost control of the legal standard bearer; her answer is the same institutional boundary she adopted in Part I, combined with the actual trial record. Endorsement need not be a literal substitute for nomination to remain relevant to the extent of the injury in a public electoral system. She preserves party autonomy outside that public function, rejects implied party consent, and does not depend on eliminating every possible crossover effect. She can join a dissent limited to those grounds and its affirmance remedy, but not an independent Elections Clause analysis or a ruling on other primary systems.

### Souter

His initial model supported reversal but made the severity classification an opinion-join barrier. Historical full joinder shows that his own Timmons insistence on meaningful fit below the severe threshold did not cause that barrier in this case. The simulated Timmons reservation about demonstrated governmental stability also survives without fixing the burden level of a different rule. Reform Party's limited equality analysis neither lowers the burden here nor displaces Hurley's private-expression protection or the protection for official candidate selection.

Reconciled commitment: reverse on the common severe-burden ground. The decisive feature is state-compelled participation in the objecting party's official selection, with supported likely effects on nomination and campaign positions. Preserving other party activities does not cure that injury. The State's asserted participation and privacy purposes do not justify this means under strict scrutiny; the nature of the primary still requires acknowledgement of ordinary administration and voting-rights constraints. The strongest objection is that a public electoral system can permissibly regulate party selection and need not await proven injury. His response distinguishes regulating access and finality from compelling the relevant association itself. He can join the common opinion on these grounds, retains no necessary separate concurrence or below-severe alternative, and accepts the confined prospective remedy. No new evidence or changed operative premise supports preserving the initial limitation.
