## Garner v. Jones — March 28, 2000

### Event, facts and the judgment under review

No. 99-137 presents the federal Ex Post Facto Clause challenge to applying Georgia's extended parole-reconsideration interval to Jones. Argument is January 11, 2000. The review is of **Jones v. Garner, 164 F.3d 589 (11th Cir. January 6, 1999)**, which reversed summary judgment for the Board defendants and remanded. It did not order Jones released. The supplied review premise is Jones's challenged 1995 deferral until August 2003, against the three-year reconsideration rule applicable to his 1982 offense.

The published appellate opinion and the Board custodian's affidavit in the joint appendix establish the following source-supported record. Jones received a life sentence for murder in 1974, escaped in 1979, and committed another murder while escaped in 1982, receiving another life sentence. The offense-date rule required initial consideration after seven years and, following denial, reconsideration at three-year intervals. The amended regulation permitted reconsideration at intervals no longer than eight years. Jones was considered in 1989, then in 1992 and 1995 after the intervening Akins litigation. The 1995 denial letter gave the circumstances and nature of the offense and multiple offenses as its main reasons and fixed August 2003 for reconsideration. These facts establish neither suitability for release nor legal ineligibility during the interval.

The Board's 1996 written policy states that eligible life-sentence cases may receive a deferral of up to eight years when the Board determines parole cannot reasonably be expected during the intervening years; an inmate **may** receive expedited review following changed circumstances or new information warranting earlier consideration. It applies to eligible life-sentence cases, including cases involving parole granted and later revoked. It directs members to indicate the interval when voting to deny parole. The regulation and policy are different instruments. The regulation requires notice of denial reasons, with protection for confidential sources and potentially discouraging diagnostic opinions; the policy's possible expedited review must not be converted into a mandatory hearing or a guaranteed response deadline.

The Board custodian's affidavit, joint appendix pp. 47–50, asserts full individual review, an individualized finding of no reasonable expectation of parole in the intervening years, an existing expedited-review procedure for new information or circumstances, and retained authority to alter review frequency. That is record evidence, not merely an argument by counsel. Its assertion of a procedure must not be promoted into an adjudicated finding that the procedure supplies an ordinary, practically available and timely substitute in every case. Conversely, its existence and claimed operation cannot be ignored because the policy uses discretionary language.

The historical Board explanation and district judgment invoked historical Morales as displacing Akins. **That legal premise is absent from this Court's law.** Preserve the supplied challenged scheduling act and lower judgment as review premises; do not invent new Board conduct, defiance, notice, compliance or a replacement explanation. Historical sources establish the act and their stated rationale; the Court adjudicates that act under the actual entering law. The lower court's own descriptions of historical Morales and Lynce are not authority for this timeline.

The Eleventh Circuit reasoned that the Georgia rule covered a broader life-sentence class than the California rule it examined, permitted a longer interval, lacked enforceable safeguards and made earlier review discretionary. It treated the policy as insufficient and found the retrospective extension unconstitutional. The original packet's description of an undeveloped practical-risk record is incomplete: the record contains a sworn operational assertion and a written policy. The legal sufficiency and practical significance of that evidence remain live issues.

### Entering law and live alternatives

**California Department of Corrections v. Morales, April 25, 1995**, O'Connor Parts II–III joined by Stone-Zsela, Stevens, Souter and Ginsburg, controls. A claimant identifies an offense-date legal opportunity, its actual retrospective withdrawal, and a concrete connection to legally available earlier release. Proof that the omitted hearing would actually yield parole is unnecessary. Once that showing is made, the State must substantiate a substitute it invokes by identifying its legal basis and demonstrating practical availability and timely capacity to provide the relevant consideration; the prisoner retains the ultimate constitutional burden. An initial unsuitability prediction and review of that prediction do not alone preserve later consideration of rehabilitation, changed facts or a materially mistaken prediction. Relief restores consideration, not release. The exact standard **Retrospective withdrawal of mandatory parole reconsideration**, with its present-operation and wording authorities, is copied into the law slice.

Morales applies Cavanaugh v. Roller (November 30, 1993), Weaver v. Graham, Miller v. Florida, and Collins v. Youngblood. Weaver protects a legally available avenue to reduced custody without guaranteeing parole; Miller rejects reliance merely on unchanged outer sentencing limits; Collins confines the Clause to punishment rather than every procedural disadvantage. The slice preserves Morales's limits: no universal fixed hearing interval, constitutional presumption transferring the ultimate burden, numerical probability threshold, compelled filing procedure, response deadline or release entitlement. The Georgia framework could be materially different if its actual replacement mechanism meets the governing standard.

The competing paths requiring independent Justice assessment are:

1. **Affirm the reversal of defendants' summary judgment on the actual lost-opportunity ground.** Jones's prior three-year entitlement, actual eight-year deferral and continuing legal parole avenue establish the necessary initial connection. The statement that review may be advanced, and the initial prediction, may fail to substantiate an ordinary timely replacement. The strongest objection is the actual sworn description of an existing expedited mechanism: the Court cannot treat a source gap or the word “may” as proof that the mechanism is unavailable. The scope of an affirmance must track what the record establishes and the lower mandate; it does not automatically produce parole or damages.
2. **Vacate and remand for the governing substitute inquiry while rejecting both categorical theories.** The appellate court may have improperly disregarded discretionary practices solely because they were not formally enforceable, while the district court used the wrong Morales premise. A return may be justified to assess the sworn description under the actual legal-basis, practical-availability and timely-capacity rule. The strongest objection is that another remand should not give the State repeated opportunities to cure an already adjudicated failure on an adequate record. The modeler must address the actual summary-judgment record and ordinary procedure, not order a new study merely to satisfy research needs.
3. **Restore defendants' judgment only if the existing record proves an adequate materially different substitute or defeats Jones's required showing.** The Board's discretion over ultimate release and the absence of a liberty interest in favorable parole, standing alone, cannot do this under Morales. A finding that Jones would probably remain unsuitable is likewise insufficient by itself. Such a result needs a supported account of the operational substitute, not the historical risk formula substituted for current law.

The dates in the received packet remain intact. No invented request by Jones for expedited review, Board rejection of such a request, new rehabilitation evidence, near-uniform statewide practice, release determination or mandatory hearing modality is supplied.

## Free v. Abbott Laboratories — April 3, 2000

### Corrected posture and exact scope

No. 99-391 was argued March 27, 2000. The **Frees are petitioners seeking reversal of federal supplemental jurisdiction**; Abbott, Bristol-Myers Squibb and Mead Johnson are removing defendants and respondents defending it. The original neutral packet reverses those roles. The final Fifth Circuit judgment under review was entered June 3, 1999, following the January 19, 1999 opinion at 164 F.3d 270. The June opinion is reported at **176 F.3d 298**. The January opinion retained the jurisdiction determination as law of the case and certified Louisiana-law questions; Louisiana declined certification, and the Fifth Circuit then affirmed the dismissal under Louisiana antitrust law. The question here challenges the court's jurisdiction to enter that judgment, not the correctness of Louisiana antitrust law.

The Frees filed a Louisiana state-court consumer class action in October 1993 alleging price fixing in infant formula and related state-law theories. They sought treble damages, fees, costs and interest while disclaiming damages exceeding $20,000 for each class member. Defendants removed. The district court rejected federal-question jurisdiction but found the named plaintiffs' claims above the **then-applicable $50,000 diversity threshold** because Louisiana law attributed the class fee recovery to representative parties. It found supplemental jurisdiction over related absent-member claims, initially declining retention; the Fifth Circuit's 1995 decision at 51 F.3d 524 set aside that remand. A later proposed settlement received no final approval, and the district court eventually dismissed the claims. These steps are not an approved settlement or a merits recovery.

Fee attribution was disputed below but **the single granted question assumes a claim of a named plaintiff within original diversity jurisdiction and common Article III relatedness**. Both merits briefs frame the issue as whether §1367 displaces Zahn for smaller class-member claims on that assumption. The Court need not adjudicate the Louisiana fee rule, permit aggregation to manufacture an anchor, decide a complete-diversity defect, redetermine Rule 23 certification, revisit abstention or §1367(c) discretion, or decide state antitrust merits. A general §1332 quotation using $75,000 must not change this 1993 action's applicable amount. The original packet may retain the anchor assumption, but must not falsely state that the Frees originally alleged ordinary damages exceeding the threshold.

Participation is the verified eight-Justice lineup stated above. No reason for O'Connor's absence and no vote split is inferred.

### Governing text and authority

The official 1999 Code text of **28 U.S.C. §1367(a)–(e)** and its December 1, 1990 prospective application are script-copied in the source appendix. Subsection (a) grants jurisdiction, subject to (b), (c) and express contrary federal statutes, over other claims forming part of the same Article III case as claims within original jurisdiction, including joinder or intervention of additional parties. Subsection (b), in diversity-only actions, excludes specified claims by plaintiffs against persons made parties under Rules 14, 19, 20 or 24, and claims by persons proposed as plaintiffs under Rule 19 or intervening as plaintiffs under Rule 24, when inconsistent with §1332. It does not list Rule 23. Subsection (c)'s four distinct grounds remain discretionary: novel or complex state law, substantial predominance, dismissal of every original-jurisdiction claim, or exceptional circumstances furnishing other compelling reasons. The specific wording and distinction between claims against joined defendants and claims by joined plaintiffs must be preserved.

**City of Chicago v. International College of Surgeons, December 15, 1997**, Thomas Part I, joined by Stone-Zsela, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter and Breyer, holds that §1367 reaches related state administrative-review claims in a mixed federal-question complaint; original jurisdiction need not cover every claim in the action. Its holding expressly leaves a diversity-only administrative appeal undecided. It does not resolve Zahn's amount rule. The exact **Supplemental jurisdiction over related state administrative review** standard is copied in the law slice. Steel Co. keeps statutory jurisdiction separate from merits and judicial convenience.

Surviving pre-divergence authorities include **Zahn v. International Paper Co., 414 U.S. 291 (1973)** (separate and distinct class claims each had to meet the amount under the former regime), **Snyder v. Harris, 394 U.S. 332 (1969)** (no aggregation of separate class claims to create the jurisdictional amount), **Finley v. United States, 490 U.S. 545 (1989)** (Scalia's statutory-authorization requirement for pendent parties, joined by Kennedy; Stevens dissented), **Gibbs**, and **Owen Equipment v. Kroger**. The enactment responding to Finley supplies the new statutory text; the degree to which it changes Zahn is the unresolved question. Reading §1367 is not constitutional overruling of Zahn or abandonment of Snyder's aggregation rule.

The competing paths are:

1. **Permit supplemental jurisdiction and affirm the jurisdictional judgment.** One qualifying claim anchors the action, the related claims satisfy (a), and Rule 23 is absent from (b). City of Chicago supports reading “civil action” without requiring original jurisdiction over every constituent claim. The strongest objection is that §1332's preexisting amount requirement and §1367's original-jurisdiction condition may operate before the exceptions are reached; an omission cannot create the anchor. Legislative history expressly seeking to preserve Zahn strengthens that competing construction if the enacted language reasonably allows it.
2. **Retain Zahn and reverse the jurisdictional judgment as to smaller claims.** “Civil action” and original jurisdiction may incorporate the historically independent amount requirement, with (b) preventing circumvention through later joinder and intervention. The strongest objection is that this reading risks turning a supplemental grant into a demand for original jurisdiction over every claim, and gives less effect to (a)'s additional-party sentence and (b)'s deliberate list. The modeler must explain why amount defects are distinct from complete-diversity defects and from the federal-question situation actually held in City of Chicago.

Neither path may silently transform the single question into a universal rule for every Rule 20 plaintiff, nondiverse party, absent anchor or unrelated claim. A reversal would require a remedy matching the absent members' lack of jurisdiction while leaving the assumed named anchor distinct; an affirmance would preserve the lower judgment within the granted question, not independently endorse its state-law analysis. An equal division would produce no controlling rule on §1367 and should not publish individual votes unless lawfully disclosed.

## Powell v. Ridge — April 3, 2000

### Record, preserved questions and relief

This is the supplied merits review of **189 F.3d 387 (3d Cir. August 25, 1999)**, following the simulation's December 13 grant and February 28 submission. No historical Supreme Court disposition supplies its merits or schedule. The two granted questions concern a private action to enforce Title VI disparate-impact regulations, directly or through §1983, and whether relief on that regulatory theory requires proof of §601 intentional discrimination.

The third-circuit opinion supplies a richer pleading posture than the compact packet. Eleven parents sued for themselves and their Philadelphia public-school children, joined by organizations and city/school entities and officials. Four executive state officials were sued, and legislative leaders intervened. Count I invokes DOE's **34 C.F.R. §100.3(b)(2)**; Count II invokes §1983. The complaint seeks declaratory and prospective injunctive relief, **not damages**. It challenges alleged racial inequality in state school funding; it does not establish discrimination, funding liability, a particular amount or a right to a specified statewide expenditure.

The complaint alleges that at comparable poverty levels districts with more nonwhite pupils receive less state funding, that the funding formula produces the disparities, and that limited resources harm the children's educational opportunities. Its 1995–96 regression allegation concerns a $52.88 lower per-pupil state allocation for each percentage-point increase in nonwhite enrollment at matched poverty levels. These are allegations credited for Rule 12 review, not Court findings or an adopted statistical model. The lower court held they gave adequate notice and did not require plaintiffs before discovery to isolate the responsible component of a complex changing formula. Its trial-stage burden discussion is not a Supreme Court holding.

The Third Circuit reversed the dismissal on both counts. It treated §601's established private action, §602's implementing authority, and the regulatory protection against unjustified effects as supporting the direct action. It separately rejected remedial foreclosure of §1983, because funding oversight supplies no sufficiently comprehensive individual remedial system. The district court's exclusion of state officers as nonpersons was wrong for prospective relief under Will and Ex parte Young. The appellate court found the children's alleged comparative treatment injury redressable even if local funding might change, and left the municipal plaintiffs' prudential standing unresolved because individual and organizational plaintiffs could proceed. Those independent threshold distinctions matter; lack of a private regulatory right must not be mislabeled lack of Article III standing.

The lower opinion's footnote 1 records that the complaint did **not** contain a separate statutory §601 count, although it alleged adoption of methods with knowledge of their effects. Counsel suggested that discovery might support intentional discrimination by analogy to Davis. The appellate court did not decide that theory's merits. Therefore rejecting impact-only enforcement would neither establish intentional discrimination nor adjudicate a fully pleaded separate §601 count. Any ordinary amendment or genuinely preserved theory must retain its actual procedural footing; no new claim is invented here.

### Governing instruments and controlling simulated law

The official **42 U.S.C. §§2000d and 2000d-1**, Title VI §§601–602, and the official **1999 34 C.F.R. §100.3** are retained and selected passages script-copied. Section 601 identifies persons protected from exclusion, denial of benefits or discrimination on race, color or national origin in federally assisted programs. Section 602 authorizes and directs federal agencies to effectuate §601 by generally applicable rules consistent with the objectives of the funding statute. It separately provides for recipient/program-confined funding termination after findings, notice and opportunity for hearing, or other means authorized by law; requires advice of noncompliance and an unsuccessful voluntary-compliance determination before action; and requires a congressional report and thirty-day wait before a funding termination/refusal becomes effective. Those agency conditions do not automatically become exhaustion requirements for a private suit or themselves establish one.

Section 100.3(b)(2) addresses a recipient's criteria or methods of administering services, aid, benefits or facilities, forbidding effects that discriminate against individuals because of protected status or defeat or substantially impair program objectives for such individuals. It must be read in its actual recipient/program setting and alongside the general prohibition. Its text does not expressly create a private litigation mechanism. Regulatory validity, statutory right, private enforcement channel, proper defendant, breach and relief are separate questions.

**Blessing v. Freestone, April 21, 1997**, Stevens for a unanimous Court, requires identification of a specific provision intended to benefit the claimant, creating a binding obligation and sufficiently definite individual entitlement. General beneficial programs, administrative duties or systemwide compliance measures alone do not suffice; qualitative standards are not categorically unenforceable. Regulatory elaboration of an existing right is expressly not categorically excluded. An established right is presumptively enforceable under §1983 absent express foreclosure or a sufficiently comprehensive incompatible remedy; agency audits and funding sanctions alone do not prove foreclosure. Scalia and Kennedy's original-meaning reservation withholds no join and is not a controlling limitation. The exact standard **Individual statutory rights and congressional foreclosure under §1983** is copied.

**Suter v. Artist M.** retains provision-specific inquiry, and its rights question is separate from its implied-action question; the law slice preserves the actual holdings. **Gebser, June 22, 1998**, and **Davis, May 24, 1999**, allow specified recipient-attribution routes for proved intentional Title IX discrimination. They establish no new regulatory cause of action or automatic liability from a technical policy violation. Davis's educational deprivation and institutional causation requirements cannot simply be transferred to school funding. Stevens, Souter, Ginsburg and Breyer's actual Gebser attribution ground and O'Connor's Davis joins are available in the slice for independent comparison, but intentional-harassment attribution does not itself decide impact-only rights. NCAA v. Smith keeps funding-recipient status distinct from economic connection.

Surviving pre-divergence authorities include **Cannon v. University of Chicago**, the established private-action context; **Guardians Association v. Civil Service Commission, 463 U.S. 582 (1983)**, whose separate opinions distinguish statutory intentional discrimination, regulatory effects and remedies; **Alexander v. Choate, 469 U.S. 287 (1985)**, recognizing the regulatory disparate-impact account while rejecting unlimited liability for every disparate result; **Wright v. Roanoke** and **Wilder v. Virginia Hospital Association**, definite individual statutory entitlements; and **Pennhurst**, clear funding obligations. An assumption of a regulatory remedy in an earlier case is not automatically an express holding on the cause-of-action question now presented.

The material paths are:

1. **Affirm prospective private enforcement of the particular regulatory prohibition.** The identifiable children assert unequal treatment within an expressly beneficiary-protecting statute; §602 authorizes implementing protection, §100.3(b)(2) specifies the duty, and the requested relief remedies their injury. Cannon, Guardians and Choate support the combined statutory/regulatory account; Wright shows regulations can elaborate a right. Blessing's foreclosure inquiry disfavors treating funding oversight as exclusive. The strongest objection is that §601's intentional-discrimination right and §602's instructions to agencies may not create the broader individual effects entitlement. A remedy cannot supply the missing statutory right, and a rule's valid administrative operation does not necessarily authorize its private enforcement.
2. **Reject both asserted impact-only routes while preserving distinct agency and intentional-discrimination law.** Section 601 does not establish an individual right against every unjustified disparate effect, while §602 addresses agency action rather than granting persons an additional entitlement. A regulation can specify an existing right but cannot independently create the missing privately enforceable statutory right; §1983 needs such a right before foreclosure matters. The strongest objection is that it understates the beneficiary-centered statutory structure and existing regulatory elaboration in Wright and Guardians/Choate. A categorical assertion that regulations can never perform rights-defining work would exceed Blessing and requires its own justification.
3. **Different treatment of the two channels.** The Court could find no directly implied regulatory action but recognize §1983 enforcement if a definite federal right arises from the statutory/regulatory combination, or find the direct prospective action sufficient and leave an unnecessary alternate channel unresolved. An implied-action failure does not mechanically establish §1983 foreclosure; a valid regulation does not mechanically establish either channel. The chosen disposition must address the counts within the granted questions and avoid unnecessary constitutional adjudication.

If the suit proceeds, the remedy is ordinary further adjudication of the pleaded claims, not immediate funding equalization, a statistical presumption, damages, mandated appropriations, a new statewide formula or automatic discovery entitlement beyond ordinary procedure. If the impact-only theories fail, the decree must specify those theories and preserve only genuinely remaining ordinary procedural possibilities. No funding cutoff follows from a private-action ruling. The question does not require invalidation of the regulation, elimination of agency enforcement or narrowing of the established intentional-discrimination action.
