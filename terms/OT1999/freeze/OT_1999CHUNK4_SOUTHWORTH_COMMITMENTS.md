The challenged share is the allocable fee supporting private organizations' political and ideological activities. The nonallocable services, the general power to levy student fees, ordinary student government, nonideological organizations and nonobjectors' funds are outside the claim. The published lower opinion also identifies the student newspaper and Distinguished Lecture Series as unchallenged. The student organizations control their messages, and the University disclaims ownership. Most funding is reimbursement; WISPIRG's lump-sum funding and all of its uses cannot be assumed identical to that arrangement. The two reported WISPIRG dollar figures are not combined or used to calculate relief.

The ordinary SGAF/GSSF allocation and approval processes are stipulated viewpoint neutral, with no University advocacy of a particular view. The binding referendum is expressly excluded from that stipulation. It can allocate, eliminate or veto a group's funding. No finding that every written exclusion was valid, revised, abandoned or enforced neutrally follows from the stipulation. The record describes policies against donations, certain legal services, politically partisan or religious activities, and separate GSSF restrictions on primarily political groups and lobbying. Their precise lawful operation is not settled through an assumed revision.

The live granted dispute concerns speech and association. Earlier free-exercise, RFRA and state claims are not revived. No adjudicated Establishment claim or established payment for worship or devotional instruction is supplied. Religious editorial viewpoint is not automatically a devotional governmental function. Strout is effective, but its integrated-tuition application supplies no fact about this program.

The Seventh Circuit already limited relief to objectors and vacated both the all-students reach and the prescribed detailed administrative scheme. No Justice below supports reinstating those vacated features merely because some liability survives.

## Proposed general-program rule supported by five Associates

Stevens, O'Connor, Kennedy, Ginsburg and Breyer would hold that a public university may require a generally applicable student contribution to a broad program of independently controlled student expression serving its educational function when eligibility, allocation and review preserve viewpoint neutrality and do not attribute agreement or membership to dissenting students or burden their independent expression. Within such a program, the First Amendment does not require an individual ideological veto or refund merely because a recipient espouses a view the payer opposes. This permission concerns the defined educational private-expression program; it does not authorize compelled support for an official orthodoxy, a selected ideological campaign masquerading as a forum, or every privately organized advocacy expense wherever incurred.

Compelled financing implicates expressive interests even without personal attribution. Abood, Keller and Lehnert therefore identify a real burden, not a disposable analogy. Their justification and chargeability inquiry arose from an institution appointed to perform a bounded representative function, with other ideological expenditures outside that function. Here the University's common function is to maintain opportunities for independent and competing student expression, rather than to appoint one ideological representative for all students. Protecting that function through viewpoint neutrality does not require a student to adopt any group's belief. A rule permitting each payer to exclude disfavored views from this common program would change the program's basis from equal access into selective support by ideological preference. No empirical finding that opt-outs are administratively impossible is required or made.

This is an additional application of common-function analysis, not an assertion that Glickman already approved ideological funding. Glickman's actual holding depended on nonideological generic promotion within collective economic regulation. The different justification here is the educational private-expression forum itself, with neutrality securing its breadth. The limiting question is whether the expenditure belongs to that actual program under lawful educational, financial and accountability criteria, not whether the content agrees with the University's teaching or the majority's beliefs. A general educational label, shared benefit, absence of attribution, voluntary university enrollment or democratic authorization alone is insufficient.

Rosenberger informs the distinction between independently edited student speech and official advocacy, and forbids viewpoint exclusion within its actual publication benefit. Its express reservation of compulsory fees means that this case must decide the objection on its own; neither a previously established opt-out nor an automatic answer for every subsidy follows. The five do not require matching dollars for every viewpoint, universal funding of every organization, or expenditure unconstrained by reasonable program scope, financial responsibility, fraud prevention and demonstrated provision of the funded activity. A student's right to argue against recipients and to form or support competing groups remains intact.

On the stipulated ordinary allocation facts, ideological character alone cannot sustain the Seventh Circuit's categorical liability rule. The five would set that rule and the injunction resting on it aside and remand for the distinct referendum and any genuinely preserved issue about the program's actual scope or administration. No categorical approval of all off-campus activity is given: location alone does not make an educational activity ineligible, but a transfer to an external campaign does not become part of a student-expression program merely because the recipient calls it educational. Any necessary characterization must rest on the actual record and preserved claim, not an invented finding that every expenditure is educationally necessary or wholly unrelated.

## Competing subsidy rule supported by three Associates

Scalia, Souter and Thomas would hold that the University cannot compel objecting students to finance private organizations' political or ideological advocacy merely by distributing subsidies among different viewpoints. They accept genuine common institutional expenses and leave unchallenged curricular, library, newspaper, lecture and nonallocable functions outside this decision. Their objection concerns the described compelled transfers maintaining independently directed ideological organizations and their expressive activities. They do not classify every private service delivered by an organization that also speaks as automatically unchargeable.

Abood and Keller protect against compelled support, not only personal attribution or monopolistic representation. For these Justices, the legally required representative function explains the exception permitting specified charges; its absence here weakens the University's justification rather than dissolving the protected interest. Their own Glickman positions reject shared benefit as a sufficient basis for a separate forced expressive expenditure even in a substantially regulated industry. Adding more privately chosen ideological messages does not answer why an objector must finance any of them. A fee specifically supporting these private activities is not disposed of as a general tax or an unavoidable curricular expense.

The broad educational-forum rationale is the strongest alternative: independent organizations give practical experience and expose students to competing ideas, and selective exemptions may affect that environment. The three nevertheless regard those benefits as insufficient to compel the actual private advocacy. A rule excusing compulsion whenever government elects to encourage multiple private messages would remove the limits preserved in Glickman and the reserved compulsory-fee issue in Rosenberger. Private organizations may continue to speak, voluntary contributions remain available and common neutral access infrastructure is not prohibited. No assumption that voluntary funding would preserve the same amount of speech is needed; a desire for more expression is not itself authority to compel its financing.

They would preserve the judgment's protection against compulsory ideological support, while vacating any rule that an organization loses all eligibility for genuinely separable services solely because it also expresses political or religious views. They would remand for a decree tied to the objectors, challenged allocable share and covered uses. An accounting label moving identical compulsory support between accounts does not suffice; genuinely severable work with real controls is a different case. A general continuing objection must receive adequate explanation and protection of the reasonably disputed share before ideological use, with the University bearing its chargeability burden and offering reasonably prompt impartial review. Under Gibson, an adequate advance reduction or interest-bearing escrow can protect the disputed amount; no absolute prohibition on collecting into a genuine protective escrow or universal opt-in regime follows. Under Miller, offering impartial review does not require a nonconsenting student to exhaust private arbitration before suing. The lower court's refusal to prescribe a detailed administrative design remains appropriate, but its language cannot foreclose these lawful alternatives. No sum, refund formula, automatic injunction against all organizations, or award of damages is fixed.

## Referendum, policy and religious-function limits

The five forum-rule Justices require further proceedings concerning the binding referendum's actual terms and operation. Selection of a group's eligibility or subsidy because a majority approves or rejects its viewpoint is incompatible with the proposed rule. The fact that student representatives or voters decide does not remove the University's obligation. But the record does not warrant declaring every ballot or every possible referendum arrangement unconstitutional without examining its decisional role. It could be relevant, for example, whether a vote supplies information subject to genuine neutral final review or actually determines funding by ideological popularity. The supplied program treats votes as binding; the Court may not assume a neutral override or cure. A remand must resolve that issue rather than deem it covered by the ordinary-process stipulation.

Scalia, Souter and Thomas agree that majority preference cannot defeat an objector's rights or justify viewpoint discrimination in an opened private-expression benefit. They would not need a further neutrality determination to establish their independent compulsory-subsidy objection to the ideological expenditures. If the five's forum rule governs, all three support remand rather than an unconditional approval of the referendum; that conditional remedial agreement is not a join in A. No Justice finds an established entitlement to a specific sum for WISPIRG or an adjudicated motive behind each vote.

No Justice approves all written funding exclusions by stipulation. A reasonable activity limit, a viewpoint restriction and a restriction that conceals discrimination are distinct; the speech and association grant does not automatically put every unchallenged clause before the Court. Further proceedings must respect actual preservation, standing and the stipulation's defined reach. The Court cannot invent rescission of the partisan or religious restrictions. Nor can it order payment of every previously disallowed activity.

All eight reserve any properly presented specific devotional-financing question. Agostini, Jackson and Strout require attention to an actual financed function; Rosenberger preserves its independently attributed controlled printing relationship. No established devotional purchase is present to classify, and no current claim supplies a basis for an Establishment holding. The five's forum permission neither displaces those precedents nor turns neutrality alone into authorization of devotional financing. Scalia, Kennedy and Thomas retain their Strout disagreements without relitigating that case. Stevens, Souter, Ginsburg and Breyer's Rosenberger dissents cannot supply a finding absent from this record. Religious views remain views; religious identity alone is not a reason to remove an otherwise eligible private-expression benefit.

## Coalition precision, precedent and remedy

The proposed forum rule has five direct Associate commitments at its stated scope: Stevens, O'Connor, Kennedy, Ginsburg and Breyer. It is not a Marks synthesis of Glickman, Lehnert or competing Southworth rationales. Scalia, Souter and Thomas support a materially incompatible objector-protection rule for the private ideological subsidies. Their alternative cannot be counted as a narrower join in the forum rule. No Justice is assigned an opinion; separate-writing grounds are preserved in the rows and competing-rule section.

The five would vacate the judgment insofar as it establishes categorical liability and an injunction against objectors' support for the ordinary neutral program, and remand for the referendum and any properly preserved unresolved program issues under the new standard. The three would affirm the need for protection from the actual compulsory ideological subsidies, vacate any categorical organization-wide disqualification or remedial language excluding lawful advance protection alternatives, and remand for that narrower relief. Neither path reinstates the vacated all-students injunction or detailed mandatory administration. Neither decides damages, an exact rebate, recipient payment or a universal institutional policy.

Abood and Keller remain controlling in their representative and integrated-bar settings. Lehnert's Court framework and its fractured applications retain their actual coalitions; its statutory-duty separate opinion is a Justice-specific anchor, not Court law. Glickman is neither overruled nor retrospectively enlarged into a holding about ideological student groups. Rosenberger's forum/printing rule and fee reservation retain distinct work. Gibson and Miller govern relevant protective alternatives if a chargeability violation is found; they do not independently establish that every student fee is nongermane. Agostini, Jackson and Strout retain their actual religious-function rules without a new payment finding.

The strongest narrower path for the five would reserve the entire compulsory-forum issue and remand only for examination of the referendum. That leaves the actual categorical judgment under an unresolved legal standard despite sufficient stipulated ordinary-program facts. The strongest broader path would uphold the entire program on the stipulation; the referendum exclusion and unresolved policy scope forbid it. For the three, simply affirming every word below would extend relief beyond covered advocacy uses and conflict with lawful protective alternatives. These limits explain the distinct remands.

No material missing source prevents these provisional commitments. The record is sufficient to decide the general legal disagreement and to identify issues requiring further proceedings. Unverified policy revision, exact WISPIRG uses, individual ballot mechanics and hypothetical devotional payments remain unresolved rather than being assumed for either side.
