## Questions, route and scope

The case concerns the meaning of the two clauses of amended §2254(d)(1) and the rejection of Williams's claim that counsel ineffectively failed to investigate and present mitigation at his capital sentencing. The original petitioner's merits brief identifies three linked granted questions: whether the courts below distorted Strickland through Fretwell's unavailable-benefit principle or by requiring all twelve jurors to favor life; whether the contrary-to clause demands a precedent controlling as to both law and fact; and whether unreasonable application demands that all reasonable jurists agree the state court was unreasonable. The approved compact question encompasses this statutory construction and application, rather than merely asking an already-settled statutory test to be applied.

Williams seeks reversal with directions to vacate the death sentence. The Warden seeks affirmance and retains both lack of prejudice and adequate professional performance as grounds. The state Supreme Court and Fourth Circuit assumed deficiency without deciding it. Their assumptions are not findings, concessions or withdrawal of the Warden's alternative defense.

The Fourth Circuit judgment reversed the federal district court's grant of sentencing relief and affirmed dismissal of other claims. Its separate cross-appeal rulings concerned alleged mental illness of lead counsel and handling of court-appointed psychiatric experts; some were rejected on their merits and others as procedurally defaulted. This grant does not revive those independent claims. Evidence relevant to the presented mitigation claim must be distinguished from a separate expert-access, dual-appointment, privilege or attorney-disability claim. No guilt-phase reversal is requested through the granted sentencing question.

The circuit noted a cursory Article III/Suspension Clause challenge to its statutory standard, with a reservation about adequate briefing. The merits brief develops constitutional-avoidance arguments in support of construction. Do not transform those arguments into an automatically preserved facial challenge requiring invalidation of §2254(d), or assume a holding that independent federal review is constitutionally required in every instance.

## Procedural chronology and applicable statutory version

Williams was tried September 29–30, 1986, convicted of capital murder and robbery, and sentenced to death after the jury found future dangerousness. The judge formally imposed the sentence November 19, 1986. Virginia's Supreme Court affirmed on direct review in 1987, and certiorari was denied January 11, 1988. The state habeas petition was filed August 26, 1988; an amended petition and a two-day evidentiary hearing followed in 1995. Following a state jurisdictional change, the Virginia Supreme Court directed the Danville Circuit Court on May 6, 1996 to report findings and recommended conclusions. On August 15, 1996 the circuit judge who had imposed the death sentence recommended a new sentencing hearing for omitted mitigation, while rejecting the other ineffective-assistance claims. This recommendation did not itself become the final state habeas judgment.

The Virginia Supreme Court rejected sentencing relief June 6, 1997, assuming deficient performance but finding no prejudice. Williams's federal habeas application was filed December 12, 1997. The federal district court on April 7, 1998 granted relief on the mitigation claim, found both deficient performance and prejudice, and dismissed the remaining allegations. The Fourth Circuit reversed that grant and affirmed the other dismissals on December 18, 1998. Rehearing was denied January 15, 1999. The original petitioner's brief reports the certiorari grant on April 5, 1999; argument and decision placement are supplied by the approved neutral packet. The respondent brief's separate execution scheduling does not itself authorize a present execution or a new stay disposition.

The federal filing occurred after AEDPA's April 24, 1996 enactment. Neither the early state habeas filing nor capital status makes this a pre-enactment federal application. Simulated Lindh and Carriger preserve former law for their identified pre-enactment applications; they do not exempt this application from amended §2254(d). No chapter 154 qualification is established merely because this is a death case. There is a state merits adjudication of the presented mitigation claim and no identified exhaustion or procedural-default obstacle to that claim. ## Sentencing evidence, omitted evidence and contested limits

The charged murder involved Harris Thomas Stone, an intoxicated elderly man killed in his home with a mattock after refusing Williams a small loan. Williams took three dollars. The death initially appeared natural or alcohol-related. Williams later initiated communication from jail and confessed, prompting exhumation and an autopsy showing fatal blunt-force injury. The convictions are not disputed on this grant. His voluntary disclosure, cooperation and claimed remorse were part of the defense's mercy argument and must not disappear from a whole-record account merely because aggravation was severe.

The Commonwealth relied on future dangerousness, not a separate finding that the charged crime met Virginia's vileness aggravator. The jury heard Williams's prior armed robbery, burglary and larceny record, his parole release about seven months before the murder, subsequent vehicle thefts, the stabbing and robbery of an elderly man after using a porch fire to draw him out, and a severe assault on an elderly woman that left her permanently incapacitated. It also heard of an arson committed in jail and his statements about urges to choke or strike fellow inmates. Two experts testified to a high likelihood of future violent crimes based on his history. These aggravating circumstances must be weighed with the omitted mitigation; the account cannot reduce the prosecution's case to the charged killing alone.

The defense actually presented Williams's mother and two character witnesses, plus recorded testimony of a psychiatrist recounting Williams's assertion that he had removed bullets from the gun used in a prior robbery and his denial of another offense. The witnesses described him as respectful or nonviolent; cross-examination exposed their limited knowledge of his criminal record. Counsel sought mercy and stressed his confession. A failure to present substantial available mitigation does not mean literally no defense evidence was offered.

The original petitioner's brief, drawing on the state hearing and records, supplies concrete investigation omissions: sentencing preparation began about a week before trial; lead counsel did not seek juvenile/youth records because he believed state law prevented access; he acknowledged he would have used records if he had known their contents. Counsel did not interview the wife and daughter attending trial and did not use Bruce Elliott's offered assistance. The State responds that counsel asked Williams and relatives for helpful information, were told of a stable religious home rather than abuse, consulted mental-health professionals and deliberately curtailed additional character testimony after damaging cross-examination. Some witness-selection decisions were recognized as tactical by the state hearing judge, although that judge still found the mitigation representation deficient. An informed decision to limit cumulative witnesses and an uninformed failure to investigate available records are distinct acts; neither characterization resolves the other automatically.

The omitted material includes several distinct categories:

- **Documented deprivation and abuse:** juvenile records described severe parental alcoholism, intoxicated and neglected children, hunger, filthy living conditions, the parents' imprisonment for neglect and placement of children outside the home. Family testimony and affidavits described beatings by the father, parental violence and childhood fear. The State notes that a later report portrayed the parents as more cooperative and the father as employed and no longer drinking excessively; the records were not uniformly descriptions of continuing neglect. Early childhood deprivation remains different in kind from generic favorable opinions of relatives.
- **Intellectual and educational limitations:** the record included low intellectual functioning, special education and limited school progress. The petitioner stresses the failure to investigate and develop this material. The respondent's original brief quotes trial testimony placing Williams at a borderline intellectual level. Therefore do not state categorically that the jury heard nothing about limited intelligence. Distinguish the fuller childhood, educational and functional history from whatever limited intellectual testimony was already presented.
- **Conduct and relationships in structured settings:** records and proffers described good conduct during earlier confinement and juvenile placement, work or carpentry training, a positive relationship with his daughter, and Elliott's sustained contact through prison ministry and parole. Elliott, an accountant, supplied a potential nonrelative witness. Wife, daughter and Elliott evidence included affidavits, not three live witnesses cross-examined at the state habeas hearing. The proposed good-conduct evidence directly answers part of the future-danger case but does not erase jail arson and reported violent urges.
- **Prison commendations:** the federal district court considered returning a guard's wallet with its contents and helping identify a prison drug operation. The Fourth Circuit's footnote 4 acknowledges these items and states that the state hearing judge attached no significance to them. Lack of significance in that recommendation is not a finding that the commendations never existed; their appropriate legal weight remains a matter for the presented claim.
- **Adverse juvenile history:** opening the juvenile records also exposed numerous additional offenses and commitments that the sentencing jury had not heard. The lower opinions describe fourteen offenses in the 1966–1975 period. These include theft/property offenses and repeated commitments. The State contends that introducing them would reinforce persistent criminality and weaken mitigation; the petitioner contends that the records' explanatory value and favorable evidence nevertheless materially change the sentencing picture. Do not present the records as wholly favorable or assume that every adverse entry would necessarily be admitted without identifying the chosen evidentiary premise.

The original briefs also dispute proposed neurological/fetal-alcohol evidence and further expert opinions about behavior in confinement. The petitioner describes a later fetal-alcohol diagnosis and statements by the Commonwealth's experts predicting nonviolence in a structured environment. The Warden states that the state factfinder did not establish the fetal-alcohol condition and did not credit or rely on those opinions as mitigation. This packet does not resolve those disputed factual premises or turn the grant into reconsideration of the distinct expert claims. The documented childhood conditions, limited functioning, conduct records, family/Elliott material and commendations provide supported paths without treating a fetal-alcohol diagnosis as established. A proposed disposition that depends on establishing the disputed diagnosis or a rejected expert premise must identify and verify that state finding from the relevant appendix passage before relying on it.

The State further disputes the petitioner's claim that future dangerousness meant only dangerousness inside prison. Its brief reports life-sentence parole eligibility and explains that the prosecutor argued danger to society generally. Do not substitute life without parole, guarantee lifetime confinement, or treat evidence of adjustment to prison as the whole legal inquiry. At the same time, the existence of possible parole does not by itself remove good prison conduct from the mitigation assessment.

## What the lower courts decided, and what remains a legal inference

The state hearing judge found deficient investigation/presentation and viewed the omitted evidence as potentially critical given the sparse defense case. His prior role as trial and sentencing judge supplies useful record context; it does not confer authority to decide the federal prejudice question by personal preference or make his recommendation the final state decision.

The Virginia Supreme Court assumed deficiency, invoked Strickland and historical Fretwell, and rejected prejudice. It characterized the omitted case largely as favorable views of relatives and acquaintances, emphasized the crime spree and jail behavior, and observed that juvenile history could harm Williams. It stated both that sentencing was not fundamentally unfair or unreliable and that no reasonable probability sufficient to undermine confidence in the result had been shown. The Warden therefore has an actual alternative argument that the state court applied ordinary probability analysis independently of its Fretwell discussion. That argument cannot be omitted simply because part of the stated framework conflicts with simulated law.

The federal district court found the noncumulative omitted material sufficiently compelling to create a reasonable probability that at least one juror, and perhaps all, would reject death. It rejected the broader Fretwell gloss and considered the state court's description of the mitigation too narrow. The Fourth Circuit adopted its Green v. French standard: either square conflict with controlling law and facts, or an objectively unreasonable derivation/application that all reasonable jurists would regard as unreasonable. It treated historical Fretwell as a general elaboration of the prejudice requirement, rejected the district court's one-juror reasoning as speculation about atypical leniency, and found no prejudice even on its own appraisal. It also rejected the asserted factual misdescription under §2254(d)(2), emphasizing the witnesses actually identified as worthy of consideration by the state hearing judge.

Those are lower-court holdings and arguments, not the current Supreme Court's answer. The state factfinder's findings, the evidentiary categories it expressly credited, the completeness of the legal prejudice inquiry and the weight of the complete mitigation are separate questions. A court may not convert the restriction of the lower court's discussion to credited family witnesses into proof that documentary evidence or prison commendations were absent. Conversely, the larger list of potential witnesses supplied by federal counsel does not establish that the state judge credited all of them or that every proffer is an established fact. The granted §2254(d)(1) construction must not silently become an unrelated ruling under (d)(2) or an unexplained rejection of §2254(e)(1).

## Governing substantive law and statutory choices

Strickland requires professionally unreasonable performance assessed from counsel's perspective at the time, and a reasonable probability of a different result absent the deficiency, sufficient to undermine confidence in the outcome. Reasonable investigation or a reasonable decision making further investigation unnecessary is required; a hindsight assertion of strategy does not itself establish an informed decision. The prejudice inquiry examines the total sentencing picture, including the aggravation, evidence presented, omitted evidence that competent representation would have produced, and relevant adverse consequences. It is neither automatic prejudice whenever evidence was omitted nor a demand to prove a different result more likely than not. It presumes a reasonable, conscientious decisionmaker applying governing law, rather than irrational mercy or an idiosyncratic juror.

Simulated Fretwell, January 25, 1993, expressly preserves that ordinary reasonable-probability test. Its narrow rule denies prejudice from losing a benefit resting on a displaced substantive rule where governing law recognizes no entitlement and no other fair or reliable protection was lost. It expressly adds no separate unfairness requirement in an ordinary ineffective-assistance case. O'Connor's limiting explanation is part of the controlling opinion in this timeline, not merely a separate writing that can be disregarded in favor of the historical majority. That is a concrete changed legal premise relevant to assessing the lower court's broader gloss. It does not predetermine whether the state judgment also rests on an adequate ordinary-prejudice analysis or whether Williams has established performance and prejudice.

The right to present relevant mitigation under Lockett, Eddings and Skipper is legally recognized; omission of such evidence is not automatically the lost unlawful advantage at issue in Fretwell. Those decisions do not require counsel to introduce every item regardless of a reasonable investigation and tactical consequences. The entered simulated holdings control if an original brief quotes a materially different historical post-divergence opinion. In particular, historical descriptions of Jones, Strickler, Fretwell or other post-1991 authorities must be checked against the governing register before being adopted as present law.

Virginia Code §19.2-264.4(E), quoted consistently in both original briefs, directs the court to dismiss a penalty jury that cannot agree and impose life imprisonment. A death sentence thus requires unanimity. The petitioner argues that the reasonable probability of one reasonable juror voting for life is enough to change the legally available sentencing result; he disclaims reliance on unusual personal leniency. The State argues that Strickland assesses a reliable collective decision and cannot rest on speculation about the most sympathetic holdout. Resolve the distinction between lawful individual judgment under the actual unanimity rule and fanciful or irrational disagreement. Do not require twelve votes for life without addressing the statute; do not reduce reasonable probability to a bare possibility that someone might disagree. No general instruction about informing a jury of deadlock consequences is presented.

The statutory excerpt reproduces §2254(a)–(e) from the official 1998 Code. Subsection (a) requires unlawful state custody. For a claim decided on its merits, subsection (d) bars relief unless its (d)(1) legal condition or (d)(2) factual condition is met. Within (d)(1), contrary to and unreasonable application are alternatives joined by an or; both concern clearly established federal law as determined by this Court. Subsection (e)(1) separately presumes state factual determinations correct, with the applicant bearing clear-and-convincing rebuttal. These provisions must not be collapsed into a general requirement that legal error be proved by clear and convincing evidence. Section (e)(2)'s conditional restriction on federal evidentiary hearings has its own trigger and exceptions; no new hearing request or failure-to-develop finding is supplied here.

The entering register expressly leaves the controlling construction of the (d)(1) terms open. The following are competing legal approaches, not preflight holdings:

- Williams argues that where the Court has already prescribed a rule to govern the kind of claim, a state decision inconsistent with that rule is contrary to established law and receives independent assessment, including mixed questions. He treats Strickland as just such a general but specifically applicable rule. In the absence of a prescribed governing rule, he would use objective unreasonable-application review of the rule derived from existing principles. His historical/Teague and avoidance arguments support that construction; they do not themselves invalidate the statute.
- The Warden argues that Congress deliberately restricted federal collateral relief: a state court's failure to identify the governing rule can satisfy contrary-to review, while application of an identified rule to materially different facts receives unreasonable-application review. Materially indistinguishable facts yielding the opposite outcome are another possible contrary-to situation. He supports the circuit's all-reasonable-jurists formulation as an objective legal standard, while expressly denying that the bare existence of an adverse state decision proves reasonableness. He separately argues this claim fails even on independent prejudice review.
- A construction may preserve independent force for both clauses while declining either factual identity as the universal limit on contrary-to review or an actual tally of judges as the test of unreasonable application. If that path is adopted, the Court must state what makes an application unreasonable as distinct from merely incorrect, identify the governing Supreme Court propositions, specify the relevant temporal baseline for clearly established law, and explain the application here. It cannot assume these definitions were already entered law. Generality of Strickland does not itself prove either plenary review of every application or immunity for every state application.

The §2254(d)(1) gateway and ultimate constitutional entitlement remain separate. Correcting the legal framework may permit review without itself proving both Strickland requirements. Fretwell's limitation can support a contrary-to theory if the state court imposed a forbidden additional requirement; the State's contention that its judgment independently applied ordinary prejudice must be assessed. A whole-record unreasonable-application theory is a distinct possible ground. Any independently sufficient grounds and their respective coalitions must later be identified rather than blended.

## Lawful outcomes and remedial limits

Affirmance remains a lawful path if the adopted statutory standard leaves the state rejection within the permissible range, if the Court independently finds no constitutional prejudice where that review is available, or if a properly preserved and supported performance defense independently defeats the claim. It must rest on the actual record and governing simulated law, not the displaced broad Fretwell formulation or an irrebuttable inference from judicial disagreement. An opinion rejecting one lower-court rationale may still affirm on another supported ground.

Reversal on the sentencing component is available if the statutory gateway and both performance/prejudice requirements are established. The remedy concerns the death sentence and a lawful new sentencing opportunity, not acquittal or invalidation of the underlying convictions. A federal conditional writ can require the State to conduct a lawful new penalty proceeding within an appropriate period or otherwise replace the death sentence with a lawful nondeath disposition. Do not invent a fixed deadline, order immediate release, appoint new counsel by unexplained mandate, or dictate the jury's eventual sentence. If restoring the federal district court's grant, identify the operative sentencing relief rather than describing a direct review reversal of the Virginia Supreme Court.

Vacatur or partial reversal with remand is available if resolving the statutory framework leaves a genuine application or the retained performance defense for the lower federal court. Such a remand must identify what remains and why the present record does not resolve it. It must not reopen the other cross-appeal claims, order a new evidentiary hearing without the applicable predicates, or perpetuate an unneeded gap after both requirements have been decided. No missing historical document establishes default, prejudice or a right to relief.
