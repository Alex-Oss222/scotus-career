## Corrected facts and procedural record

Michael Hill was serving a prison sentence in Ohio. New York lodged a detainer, and Hill signed a request for final disposition under Article III. He was returned to New York to face murder and robbery charges. This is the Article III(a) 180-day route. It is not an Article IV(c) 120-day case, and the sending custody was not federal custody.

Several defense motions were pending during parts of the prosecution, and their tolling effect was uncontested in the reported record. That is an accepted record premise in this case. It is not a holding that every defense motion automatically tolls the compact's clock. The dates of receipt by both Article III(a) recipients and a complete motion-by-motion calendar have not been independently reconstructed in this preflight.

On January 9, 1995, the prosecutor and defense counsel appeared in court to select a trial date. The prosecutor proposed May 1, 1995, based on the regular prosecutor's calendar. When the court asked counsel about that date, counsel expressly agreed; the court set trial for May 1. This is affirmative assent to a date, not mere failure to speak. The neutral source does not establish that counsel expressly discussed the IAD, knew the number of nonexcludable days, advised Hill of the deadline or declared a general waiver. It does not establish Hill's presence, personal consent, personal objection or a condition attached to the assent. No one of those facts may be supplied by calling counsel's act a waiver or by calling it merely acquiescence; those competing legal characterizations are what requires decision.

The trial court found that 167 nonexcludable days had elapsed when the date was set on January 9 and that the 180-day limit would expire if the later interval was chargeable to New York. The figure is the trial court's computation, not a fresh arithmetic reconstruction. No different statutory commencement trigger is assumed. The remaining thirteen days do not establish an exact expiration date independent of the accepted exclusions.

On April 17, 1995, before trial, Hill moved to dismiss because the statutory period had expired. The motion was not first made after conviction. A jury thereafter convicted him of second-degree murder and first-degree robbery; the Appellate Division affirmed, 244 App. Div. 2d 927, 668 N.Y.S.2d 126 (1997), before the Court of Appeals ordered dismissal in 1998.

## Statutory law, with distinct triggers and exceptions

The statutory source is Public Law 91-538, section 2, 84 Stat. 1397–1402, the enacted Interstate Agreement on Detainers. The entire seven-page official enactment was downloaded and its text read. Its material provisions are paraphrased here; the saved text is OCR and is not presented as a corrected verbatim quotation. The Court's entering Fex and Reed holdings independently establish the compact's operative federal status and the relevant clock provisions.

1. **Article III(a): prisoner-initiated 180-day period.** The person must have entered a term of imprisonment in a party State's penal or correctional institution. During that term, another party State must have an untried indictment, information or complaint on which a detainer has been lodged against the prisoner. The prisoner must be brought to trial within 180 days after causing delivery to both the prosecuting officer and the appropriate court in that officer's jurisdiction of written notice of the place of imprisonment and a request for final disposition of the charge. Fex makes actual receipt by both recipients the trigger. The request must be accompanied by the custodian's certificate stating the commitment term, time served, time remaining, good time earned, parole eligibility and any relevant parole-agency decision. For good cause shown in open court, with the prisoner **or counsel** present, the court with jurisdiction **may** grant a necessary or reasonable continuance. Counsel's permitted presence is not itself a textual finding of good cause and does not by itself decide the distinct waiver question.

2. **Article III(b), (c), (d), (e) and (f): related limits.** The prisoner gives or sends the notice and request to the custodian, who must promptly forward them and the certificate by registered or certified mail, return receipt requested; the custodian must promptly disclose the source and contents of a detainer and the right to seek final disposition. A request covers all untried charges supporting detainers from the receiving State, and the custodian must notify the appropriate officers and courts and include copies of the notice, request and certificate. Article III(d) separately requires dismissal with prejudice of a covered charge not tried before return to the original place of imprisonment. Article III(e) makes the request a waiver of extradition for covered charges and later service of a receiving-State sentence, and consent to production in court and return under the compact; it does not prevent a concurrent sentence otherwise permitted by law. Escape after executing the request voids it under Article III(f). None of those distinct predicates is established as a disputed ground in Hill.

3. **Article V(c): remedy.** If the appropriate authority refuses or fails to accept temporary custody, **or** if the detainer-based charge is not brought to trial within the applicable Article III or IV period, the appropriate court where the charge is pending must dismiss the indictment, information or complaint with prejudice, and the detainer based on it loses force. This provision does not impose a separate defense-impairment requirement. Whether counsel's agreement validly removes the agreed delay from a timeliness objection is the disputed antecedent question; dismissal's mandatory wording cannot silently answer whether its violation predicate exists.

4. **Article VI(a): inability tolling.** For calculation of the duration and expiration of the Article III and IV periods, time is tolled whenever and for as long as the prisoner is unable to stand trial, as determined by the court with jurisdiction. This is different from a good-cause continuance and from waiver. Article VI(b) separately says that the Agreement's provisions and remedies do not apply to a person adjudged mentally ill. No such adjudication is supplied here, and competence, inability to stand trial and that statutory exclusion must not be collapsed.

5. **Article IV(c), Article I and Article IX.** Article IV's distinct prosecution-initiated temporary-custody route generally requires commencement of trial within 120 days after arrival in the receiving State, with its own good-cause, open-court, prisoner-or-counsel-present continuance proviso. It supplies neither Hill's clock nor his trigger. Article I identifies prompt, orderly disposition of detainers and cooperation as serving prisoner treatment and rehabilitation; Article IX requires liberal construction to effectuate the compact's purposes and supplies severability. Those purposes support interpretation but do not remove the enacted conditions or furnish an unbounded excuse for delay.

New York is the receiving State. The federal receiving-State exceptions described in section 9 of the federal Act do not become New York exceptions merely because the compact is federal law. Nothing here posits a new amendment, a different compact enactment or a newly admitted noncase-law event.

## Surviving pre-divergence counsel/accused authorities

**Taylor v. Illinois, 484 U.S. 400, 417–418 (1988).** Stevens's opinion, joined by O'Connor and Scalia among the present Justices, permits imputation of counsel's trial-management act in the particular willful discovery-violation setting. Counsel must manage the trial without a requirement of client approval of every tactical decision, but the opinion expressly preserves basic rights requiring the client's informed and publicly acknowledged consent and sets ineffective assistance aside. It cites Brookhart for that limit. The strongest analogy for New York concerns binding litigation management; the strongest limit is that Taylor did not construe the IAD's express deadline and continuance mechanism. Its discovery-misconduct finding is not a Hill fact.

**Jones v. Barnes, 463 U.S. 745, 751–754 (1983).** Stevens and O'Connor joined the Court's rejection of a constitutional duty for appointed appellate counsel to present every nonfrivolous issue requested by the client. The opinion preserves the accused's ultimate authority over pleading guilty, waiving a jury, testifying and taking an appeal, while protecting counsel's professional selection of issues. Its appeal-management holding supports a distinction, not an exhaustive classification of every statutory right. Its ethical discussion is not a constitutional requirement of personal approval for every tactical choice.

**Brookhart v. Janis, 384 U.S. 1, 7–8 (1966).** Counsel could not turn a not-guilty defense into the practical equivalent of a guilty plea and surrender confrontation over the accused's expressed insistence that he was not pleading guilty. The case protects the accused's fundamental choice while acknowledging that counsel can bind a client in some procedural circumstances. It neither makes each procedural consent personal nor establishes that Hill personally objected. Harlan's separate opinion distinguishes management of particular cross-examination from the substantial surrender involved there; it is not a Court holding.

## Supported alternative paths

These are analytical possibilities developed from the supported record under Engine section 14; they are not invented quotations from the parties' unrecovered filings. Neither is an established vote or a preferred outcome.

### Path A: enforce the particular agreement through counsel's scheduling authority

The Court could classify selection of a trial date as litigation management entrusted to counsel under Taylor and Jones. Counsel's express affirmative assent supplies a stronger basis than nonobjection. Under that classification, the prisoner can be bound to the specified date without a personal waiver colloquy, because the agreement concerns when the contested trial occurs rather than whether to plead guilty, waive counsel or abandon trial. Mezzanatto supports examining consent within the protection and agreement actually at issue, but it does not supply counsel's authority by itself. The express distinction in Article III(a) between prisoner and counsel at a continuance supports recognition of counsel's scheduling role without automatically fulfilling the good-cause condition.

The principal statutory objection is that agreement alone may evade the safeguards the compact expressly imposes for a continuance, while undermining the prisoner's already invoked right to prompt disposition and the compact's public purposes. A defensible waiver path must explain why the proviso regulates court-granted extensions without making a represented party's affirmative date agreement legally impossible; it cannot simply relabel every calendar delay a good-cause continuance. It must also explain why consent to a particular date is sufficient intentional action under the applicable right-specific rule despite no established deadline-specific advice or personal assent. The distinction between counsel accepting the prosecutor's proposal and requesting that same date must be addressed in its legal significance, not resolved by an invented fact about strategy.

Possible relief is reversal of the state court's conclusion that this express agreement cannot bind Hill, with ordinary remand for genuinely remaining matters. Its scope may be confined to a genuine, unqualified affirmative agreement to a particular trial date. Silence, an actual reserved objection, deception, a personal conflict, ineffectiveness, indefinite postponement, delay beyond the agreed date, anti-shuttling rights and other compact protections remain separate questions unless their predicates are established. No additional factual exception is deemed satisfied here.

### Path B: retain the deadline absent a sufficiently established relinquishment or statutory extension

The Court could read Article III(a)'s prisoner-initiated demand, explicit qualified continuance proviso and Article V(c)'s mandatory remedy as requiring more than the reported agreement to a proposed date before the requested statutory deadline is surrendered. That path can distinguish scheduling cooperation from an intentional relinquishment of an already invoked compact right under Olano, preserve the limited consensual holdings of Mezzanatto and Libretti, and draw on Fex's insistence on the enacted trigger and exceptions. It can affirm on the narrow insufficiency of this agreement rather than announce that the IAD is categorically nonwaivable or that counsel can never waive a timing protection. A broader personal-assent rule would require a separate account of why this particular statutory entitlement belongs with the accused's fundamental choices despite the counsel-management cases.

The strongest counterargument is that counsel did affirmatively agree, that a lawyer normally speaks for the represented defendant on the trial calendar, and that requiring proof of personal assent or an express reference to the IAD for this scheduling act burdens the ordinary allocation Taylor and Jones protect. This path must explain why the compact's text changes that allocation or why agreement to the date falls short of right-specific relinquishment; it cannot equate affirmative assent with silence, turn Mezzanatto into a universal evidentiary requirement, or derive nonwaivability merely from the fact that a rule is mandatory when applicable. Brookhart cannot supply a finding of personal protest absent one.

If the Court finds the statutory period expired without valid waiver or an established exception, the disposition must preserve Article V(c)'s dismissal with prejudice rather than substitute a prejudice test or a new trial. If a material valid-extension issue actually remains unresolved, a bounded remand is available only on that identified issue; the court cannot invent good cause, inability or a personal waiver to avoid the existing record. Prosecutorial calendar convenience alone has not been established here as a lawful good-cause finding.

### Interstate detainers and trial-period commencement

#### Fex v. Michigan, 507 U.S. 43 (1993)

**Docket or dockets:** No. 91-7873.

**Decided:** February 23, 1993.

##### Article III(a): actual receipt starts the trial period

**Holding:** When a prisoner invokes Article III while serving a term of imprisonment and an untried charge in another participating State is the subject of a lodged detainer, the 180-day trial period begins only when the required written notice and request for final disposition have actually reached both the prosecuting officer and the appropriate court in that State. Delivery to the custodian alone does not start it; a necessary or reasonable continuance may be allowed for good cause shown in open court with the prisoner or counsel present, Article VI(a) tolls the period while the prisoner is unable to stand trial as determined by the court having jurisdiction, and Article V(c) requires dismissal with prejudice when the applicable period is violated.

**Proposition-level authority:** Scalia's opinion, joined by White, O’Connor, Kennedy, Souter and Thomas, supplies six votes for this rule and its application.

**Treatment of earlier authority:**

- Carchman v. Nash, 473 U.S. 716 (1985): applied for the compact’s federal-law character and attention to operative language; its exclusion of probation-violation detainers remains intact.
- Houston v. Lack, 487 U.S. 266 (1988): distinguished because a prisoner’s own appeal-filing deadline differs from commencement of the receiving State’s prosecution period; its mailbox rule remains in force within its setting.

**Limits and questions not reached:** The custodian’s prompt forwarding and Article III(c) notice obligations remain; actual receipt does not excuse official delay. Separate notice remedies, constitutional claims and a continuance first sought after expiration are not resolved. There is no automatic grace period or bad-faith prerequisite.

**Operative remedy or transition:** Affirm: trial began 177 days after both required recipients received the papers, so Article III(a) supplies no dismissal with prejudice here.

### Plea-discussion impeachment waivers

#### United States v. Mezzanatto

**Docket or dockets:** No. 93-1340.
**Decided:** January 18, 1995.

##### A knowing and voluntary impeachment waiver is enforceable

**Holding:** Rules 410 and 11(e)(6) permit a defendant knowingly and voluntarily to agree before plea discussions that his statements may be used to impeach his own inconsistent trial testimony; absent an affirmative indication that this agreement was entered unknowingly or involuntarily, the limited waiver is enforceable. Supported challenges based on ignorance, fraud, coercion, involuntariness, or the agreement's scope remain subject to individual review, and inconsistency alone creates no nonconsensual impeachment exception.

**Proposition-level authority:** Opinion of the Court (Thomas), joined by O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Ginsburg and Breyer: six Justices. Stone-Zsela concurs in the judgment only; his different proof requirement is not controlling.

**Treatment of earlier authority:**

- Ricketts v. Adamson, 483 U.S. 1 (1987): draws on enforcement of an agreement's actual lawful terms, without importing consequences that were not agreed.
- Town of Newton v. Rumery, 480 U.S. 386 (1987): applies its rejection of automatic invalidity while preserving individual voluntariness and overreaching review; no new universal public-interest evidentiary burden is imposed.
- Brady v. United States, 397 U.S. 742 (1970): preserves knowing-and-voluntary assent despite the existence of bargaining pressure; pressure alone does not establish coercion.
- Peretz v. United States, 501 U.S. 923 (1991): uses its bounded recognition of consent as an analogy, not a rule that every institutional protection can be waived.
- Brooklyn Savings Bank v. O'Neil, 324 U.S. 697 (1945): distinguishes its prohibition on private defeat of the particular public statutory protection there; the Court does not deny that some protections may be nonwaivable.
- Godinez v. Moran, 509 U.S. 389 (1993): preserves the distinction between mental capacity and an actually informed, voluntary choice; competence or a signature alone does not dispose of a supported contrary record.
- United States v. Olano, 507 U.S. 725 (1993): applies the distinction between intentional relinquishment and forfeiture, preserving right-specific requirements and the actual preserved objection here.
- United States v. Salerno, 505 U.S. 317 (1992): distinguishes express relinquishment from dispensing with enacted evidentiary conditions or treating independent contradiction as disclosure; its holdings remain unchanged.

**Limits and questions not reached:** Then-Rule 410 excludes, against the defendant who made the plea or participated in the discussions in any civil or criminal proceeding, a later-withdrawn guilty plea, a no-contest plea, statements in Rule 11 or comparable state proceedings regarding either such plea, and statements in discussions with the prosecuting attorney that produce no guilty plea or one later withdrawn. Rule 11(e)(6) is substantively parallel. The fairness exception requires another statement from the same plea or discussion to have been introduced and fairness to require contemporaneous consideration. The criminal perjury-or-false-statement exception requires the statement to have been made under oath, on the record, and in counsel's presence; ordinary impeachment is not that exception. This decision validates no case-in-chief, derivative, sentencing, or other-person-testimony use and does not independently adjudicate the agreement's separate perjury-use term. Ordinary constitutional and evidentiary restrictions and the actual agreement's scope remain independent. The Court decides no universal rule for every advance waiver or every fundamental institutional protection.

**Operative remedy or transition:** The Ninth Circuit's categorical nonwaiver ruling and resulting new-trial order are reversed, 7–2, for ordinary implementation of the limited waiver. No mandatory validity hearing, factual coercion finding, acquittal, independent retrial direction or sentencing ruling is ordered. If preserved nonconstitutional error is found, the Government must establish whole-record fair assurance that it did not substantially sway the judgment; grave doubt defeats harmlessness. No constitutional error is newly reached, and Olano's forfeited-error burden is not substituted.

### Statutory habeas claims and collateral thresholds

#### Reed v. Farley, 512 U.S. 339

**Docket or dockets:** No. 93-5418.

**Decided:** 1994-06-20.

##### The compact supplies federal law and a federal habeas question

**Holding:** The congressionally sanctioned Interstate Agreement on Detainers is federal law, and a state prisoner's claim that custody violates its trial-period provisions presents a federal statutory question within 28 U.S.C. §2254(a) as in force at decision. Jurisdiction to consider that question does not itself establish entitlement to collateral relief.

**Proposition-level authority:** Part I of Chief Justice Stone-Zsela's opinion, joined by Justices O'Connor, Scalia, Thomas and Ginsburg. These five Justices directly adopt the jurisdictional conclusion; the dissent also accepts the compact's federal character but does not join the opinion.

**Treatment of earlier authority:**

- Cuyler v. Adams, 449 U.S. 433 (1981): applied to the congressionally sanctioned compact's federal-law character; voluntary state participation does not remove that character.
- Fex v. Michigan, February 23, 1993: its application of the compact as federal law remains intact; its Article III actual-receipt trigger does not replace Article IV's arrival trigger or decide collateral entitlement.
- Stone v. Powell, 428 U.S. 465 (1976): its restriction concerning the Fourth Amendment exclusionary remedy is not extended to decide this statutory claim.
- Withrow v. Williams, April 21, 1993: its refusal to extend Powell merely from a fair state opportunity to a different safeguard is preserved; its Miranda holding does not itself decide the distinct statutory-relief threshold here.

##### The assumed timing violation does not establish the required collateral defect on this record

**Holding:** An ordinary nonconstitutional statutory error asserted under §2254 must satisfy the collateral standard of a fundamental defect inherently resulting in a complete miscarriage of justice, or an omission inconsistent with the rudimentary demands of fair procedure; the §2255 standard applies to this claim despite the different source of the conviction. Assuming Article IV(c) was violated, Reed's presented record—including his predeadline written invocations, the absence of a specific contemporaneous objection to the scheduled dates, and no established defense impairment from the initial overrun—does not establish that defect, so the federal denial of habeas relief is affirmed.

**Proposition-level authority:** Parts II and III of Chief Justice Stone-Zsela's opinion, joined by Justices O'Connor, Scalia, Thomas and Ginsburg. Five Justices directly join the standard and this record's denial. The stronger-demand discussion in Part IV has three votes and supplies no additional Court holding; no combination of separate opinions is required.

**Limits and questions not reached:** The Court assumes rather than decides the Article IV(c) breach and does not adopt automatic motion-pendency tolling, find a valid continuance, or resolve the competing statutory-merits positions. It does not impose a required oral formula or article-number citation, find complete silence or knowing waiver, or decide that an adequately presented demand necessarily produces habeas relief. No independent Sixth Amendment claim is presented. Barker and Doggett retain their complete constitutional inquiry, including Doggett's qualified treatment of presumed defense impairment; neither a 120-day overrun alone nor absence of separately proved prejudice alone answers that inquiry. The transfer-hearing and habitual-offender issues are outside the question decided. No categorical bar to every IAD claim, affirmative entitlement in an aggravated case, or general rule governing every statutory habeas claim is adopted.

**Treatment of earlier authority:**

- Hill v. United States, 368 U.S. 424 (1962): its ordinary nonconstitutional-error threshold is extended from §2255 to the presented §2254 claim; its distinction between formal omission and fundamental defect remains operative.
- United States v. Timmreck, 441 U.S. 780 (1979): its collateral distinction is applied to this timing claim without treating every statutory entitlement as a technicality or deciding every aggravated case.
- Davis v. United States, 417 U.S. 333 (1974): its statutory no-offense holding remains available; this procedural timing claim does not establish that Reed's conduct lay outside the criminal prohibition.
- Brecht v. Abrahamson, April 21, 1993: its retention of Chapman for the preserved constitutional Doyle claim is distinguished, not displaced; this case decides statutory collateral entitlement rather than the harmlessness of an established constitutional trial error.

**Operative remedy or transition:** The Seventh Circuit's denial of habeas relief is affirmed, 5–4, without adopting the lower courts' distinct statutory-merits grounds. No writ, dismissal, remand, new trial or release is ordered; the conviction and sentence remain undisturbed by this claim.




## Supplementary exact excerpts: United States v. Olano

#### United States v. Olano, 507 U.S. 725 (1993)

**Docket or dockets:** No. 91-1306.
**Decided:** April 26, 1993.


##### Forfeiture, waiver and the limits on plain-error authority

**Holding:** Rule 52(b) permits correction of a forfeited legal error only if it is plain and affects substantial rights; valid intentional relinquishment of a known right extinguishes error, while mere failure timely to assert a right does not. Plain means clear or obvious under current law, and the defendant ordinarily must show prejudice affecting the outcome; errors first clarified on appeal, special outcome-independent categories and justified prejudice presumptions are reserved.

**Proposition-level authority:** O'Connor’s Opinion of the Court; Stone-Zsela, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas (6 Justices). Direct majority agreement at this level of generality; no Marks inference.

- Johnson v. Zerbst, 304 U.S. 458: applies intentional relinquishment; right-specific waiver requirements remain.
- United States v. Young, 470 U.S. 1: applies limited plain-error authority and ordinary prejudice requirement.
- Arizona v. Fulminante, 499 U.S. 279: preserves structural/trial-error distinction without deciding every Rule 52(b) category.

## Supplementary exact excerpts: Godinez v. Moran

#### Godinez v. Moran, 509 U.S. 389 (1993)

**Docket or dockets:** No. 92-725.
**Decided:** June 24, 1993.


##### A common capacity standard and a separate actual waiver

**Holding:** Dusky supplies the constitutional mental-capacity standard for standing trial, pleading guilty and choosing to waive counsel: sufficient present ability to consult with counsel with rational understanding, and rational as well as factual understanding of the proceedings. A competent defendant may relinquish the rights only through a knowing, intelligent and voluntary waiver; competence does not establish that the particular choice was informed and uncoerced.

**Proposition-level authority:** Thomas’s Opinion of the Court, this proposition; Stone-Zsela, White, O’Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas (7 Justices). Direct majority support at the stated level of generality; no Marks aggregation.

- Faretta v. California, 422 U.S. 806: legal skill is distinct from competence to choose representation.
- Boykin v. Alabama, 395 U.S. 238, and Johnson v. Zerbst, 304 U.S. 458: actual informed and voluntary waiver remains separately required.
- Westbrook v. Arizona, 384 U.S. 150: additional waiver inquiry preserved; a higher constitutional capacity quantum is not required.



## Supplementary exact excerpts: Libretti v. United States

#### Libretti v. United States, 516 U.S. 29

**Docket or dockets:** No. 94-7427.
**Decided:** November 7, 1995.


##### Consent, factual admission and the statutory bounds of criminal forfeiture

**Holding:** A defendant may knowingly and voluntarily waive the forfeiture jury protection and admit facts supporting forfeiture, but consent cannot enlarge §853's punishment or transfer an interest beyond the defendant's lawful power of disposition. An admission may establish a statutory predicate; an all-assets agreement cannot replace a predicate Congress requires.

**Proposition-level authority:** O'Connor's Part I-B, unanimous on these common propositions.

**Limits and questions not reached:** Lawful acquisition alone does not immunize property: §853(a)(1) reaches directly or indirectly obtained proceeds, (a)(2) the defendant's property used or intended in any manner or part to commit or facilitate the violation, and (a)(3) for a continuing criminal enterprise his interests, claims and property or contractual rights supplying control over it. Under §853(p), the defendant's act or omission must make qualifying property unavailable by inability to locate it despite due diligence, third-party transfer/sale/deposit, location beyond jurisdiction, substantial diminution, or commingling not divisible without difficulty; then other defendant property must be forfeited up to its value. Section 853(d)'s rebuttable presumption at trial requires the Government to prove by a preponderance both acquisition during the violation or within a reasonable time afterward and the absence of a likely source other than the covered violation. An all-assets promise proves neither predicate. No asset-by-asset eligibility or invalidity is found here.

**Treatment of earlier authority:** Godinez distinguishes competence from knowing voluntary choice; Olano intentional relinquishment from inaction; Mezzanatto's limited consent holding does not authorize an unlawful sentence or waiver of every protection.


##### Waiver of the Rule 31(e) special verdict by affirmative agreement and courtroom assent

**Holding:** Libretti's represented agreement to forfeit identified interests, signed jury waiver and express courtroom assent to ending trial and proceeding to sentence waive the Rule 31(e) special verdict on this record. Former Rule 11(c) requires no additional forfeiture-specific advisement, and Rule 23(a) requires no new forfeiture-specific writing for this statutory sentencing verdict.

**Proposition-level authority:** O'Connor's Part II-B, joined by Scalia, Kennedy, Thomas and Breyer; five direct votes. Stone-Zsela, Souter and Ginsburg separately uphold informed relinquishment but do not join this broader sufficient-waiver formulation; Stevens does not independently decide the application.

**Material application:** Affirmative represented assent, the agreement's actual admissions and scope, express explanation that trial would end, and earlier jury-forfeiture references support waiver. Neither competence, a signature nor silence alone establishes knowing surrender of every sentencing issue.

**Limits and questions not reached:** A judge may give an explicit special-verdict explanation and must not affirmatively mislead or leave evident confusion uncorrected. Coercion, voluntariness, understanding and agreement-scope challenges retain their distinct predicates. Private legal advice is not assumed.

**Treatment of earlier authority:** Godinez, Olano and Mezzanatto preserve informed intentional choice within the protection actually surrendered, without automatic waiver from a plea or statutory classification alone.



## Standards and Tests selection: Actual receipt and the Interstate Agreement on Detainers trial period

### Actual receipt and the Interstate Agreement on Detainers trial period

**Governing question:** What instructions govern actual receipt and the Interstate Agreement on Detainers trial period?

**Current rule:** For a prisoner serving a term with an untried charge subject to a detainer in another participating State, Article III(a)'s 180-day period begins when both the prosecuting officer and appropriate court actually receive the required written notice and disposition request, not delivery to the custodian alone. Necessary or reasonable continuance is permitted for good cause shown in open court with prisoner or counsel present. Article VI(a) separately tolls time during inability to stand trial as determined by the court with jurisdiction. Article V(c) requires dismissal with prejudice for violation of the applicable period. Prompt forwarding and Article III(c) notice remain duties; the receipt rule supplies no remedy for their breach or automatic grace period.

**Authority by component:**

- Article III(a): actual receipt starts the trial period: Fex v. Michigan — Article III(a): actual receipt starts the trial period, 1993-02-23. Scalia's opinion, joined by White, O’Connor, Kennedy, Souter and Thomas, supplies six votes for this rule and its application.

**Present operation effective:** 1993-02-23; Fex v. Michigan, for the components identified above.

**Holdings navigation:** Fex v. Michigan, 507 U.S. 43 (1993).
