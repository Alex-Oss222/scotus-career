**Relationships visible in the text:** The paragraph preceding (1) commands disclosure subject to item-specific safety and investigation limitations. Paragraph (3) additionally conditions current-address disclosure on the stated purpose declaration, preserves the listed crime-victim address confidentiality, prohibits direct or indirect sales use of address information obtained under that paragraph, and separately requires a declaration concerning that use. An authorized purpose and the sales-use limitation are both present in the text; the latter is not merely a label for an excluded profession. Paragraph (2)'s victim-name provisions allow withholding or deletion at specified requests; paragraph (3)'s listed victim-address confidentiality is mandatory. The lists are not interchangeable: the multi-crime deletion sentence in (2), as reproduced, does not include §273.5, although the adjacent victim-name list and paragraph (3)'s address list do.

**Incorporated-text limit:** Paragraphs (2) and (3) expressly incorporate Penal Code §841.5. Its separate text is not reproduced in these recovered originals. The introductory cross-references to §§6254.7 and 6254.13, and the referenced Penal Code and Business and Professions Code provisions, are likewise retained as references, not silently expanded from memory. The full within-subdivision restrictions reproduced by the source are included above. This limited task did not recover a separate historical §841.5 text; no qualification from that provision may be assumed away.

## Ninth Circuit's precise third-prong reasoning

**Source:** Full June 25, 1998 opinion reproduced at petition appendix 24a–36a; the relevant discussion is 30a–36a. These are descriptions of that court's reasoning, not independent findings by this reviewer.

1. **First-prong status and scope of interests reviewed.** The district court stated that no party contended that plaintiff's proposed use was misleading or unlawful. Pet. App. 16a. The Ninth Circuit described this as the parties' agreement that the speech at issue was neither illegal nor misleading, rather than a contested evidentiary issue. Id. 30a. This is the accepted premise for plaintiff's proposed activity in those proceedings, not an individualized factual adjudication of every conceivable future communication. Neither passage separately resolves the later merits-brief description of United Reporting's own direct sales use as a distinct factual application. The parties agreed the speech was neither unlawful nor misleading. The defendants below had asserted administrative cost and arrestee privacy. The district court found the cost interest substantial but not materially advanced; LAPD failed to challenge that conclusion in its opening appellate brief. The Ninth Circuit therefore treated the cost challenge as waived and examined privacy only. It expressly accepted arrestee privacy as a substantial interest. Pet. App. 30a–31a & n.3. Respondent's separate contention that the privacy interest was not substantial is consequently not the Ninth Circuit's holding.

2. **Unreliable criminal-history databases: express evidentiary failure.** LAPD argued that commercial access could enable unreliable criminal-history databases harmful to employment prospects of innocent, reformed, pardoned, or young persons. The court found LAPD had provided no evidence supporting that danger. It observed that addresses had been commercially available before the amendment and that, on the record it could discern, no such commercial databases had been maintained. It treated this asserted harm as speculation and did not proceed to whether the restriction would materially alleviate it. Id. 32a. This is a record-based finding about that asserted harm, not a finding that every conceivable commercial use of arrest information is harmless.

3. **Intrusions into homes and private lives: statutory categories and the nature of direct mail.** The court treated this separate harm as more substantial than the database theory. It recounted the district court's observations that the restriction left numerous invasions of privacy possible; that journalists, academics, and other noncommercial users could obtain and report arrest information; and that recipients could discard unwanted mail. It endorsed the district court's distinction, grounded in Shapero, between the solicitor's discovery of legal affairs and the subsequent communication revealing that discovery. It also relied on Bolger's treatment of avoidable mail. Id. 33a–34a. The district court's quoted reference to curiosity seekers is a lower-court description of the permitted-access scheme, not the words of the statutory list copied above or an independently verified qualifying request.

4. **The appellate court's express dispositive application.** The Ninth Circuit then independently reasoned that the statutory exceptions precluded direct and material advancement of the asserted privacy objective. Applying Coors Brewing and its own Valley Broadcasting decision, it regarded the journalistic, scholarly, political, governmental, and investigative categories as provisions of the same scheme that undermined and counteracted the privacy restriction. It emphasized that the information could be published in newspapers, articles, and magazines when not used for commercial purposes and judged publication of a person's name, crime, and address more intrusive than receiving a private service solicitation. Id. 34a–36a. This is an express legal and comparative judgment about the categories and their effects. It is not a quantified empirical finding that every newspaper routinely publishes every arrestee's address, nor a finding that every direct solicitation causes no privacy injury. The opinion's database evidentiary failure and its category-based counteraction rationale are distinct and must not be merged into a single finding that the government offered no evidence of any privacy concern.

5. **Lanphere and the unaddressed fourth prong.** In footnote 4, the court rejected LAPD's reliance on Lanphere. It criticized that decision for asserting advancement without sufficient analysis and for accepting direct or reasonably direct advancement without adequately addressing material advancement under Edenfield, Coors Brewing, and Valley Broadcasting. Id. 33a–34a n.4. The opinion's footnote refers to Edenfield's requirement as the second prong, although the body expressly places the advancement inquiry at the third; that textual label should not be converted into a different substantive test. The Ninth Circuit expressly declined to reach Central Hudson's final prong and the equal-protection, due-process, and overbreadth arguments. Id. 36a nn.5–6.

**What the rationale does and does not establish:** The appellate holding expressly relies on same-statute permitted categories undermining the privacy objective. Its reasoning is not limited to a requirement that the government regulate every independent source of information at once. At the same time, its category analysis is not an independently measured finding of the relative volume or actual dissemination effects of each permitted and prohibited use. Whether that legal comparison suffices under the governing law is reserved to the authorized adjudicative context.

## The parties' competing factual and fit arguments

**LAPD/petitioners:** The merits brief contests the appellate court's comparative premise. It argues that journalists and academics ordinarily use addresses to locate interviewees or distinguish identities, seldom print exact addresses, and reach a narrower group than bulk commercial resellers. It cites a First Amendment Coalition letter (Court of Appeals Supplemental Excerpts 382), newspaper exhibits (id. 496–547), and the Register itself (id. 11), and says the record contains only one California newspaper printing such addresses, apparently for serious crimes. Pet. Br. 34–36 & nn.20–22. Those are petitioner arguments and record recitations; this reviewer has not independently recovered those excerpt volumes. Petitioners also contend that solicitation itself confronts arrestees with widespread disclosure of their status, that restricted dissemination prevents discriminatory uses, and that permitted categories advance the separate public-information objective. Id. 36–39. Their fourth-prong argument asserts a reasonable balance between public knowledge and privacy rather than an all-or-nothing disclosure choice. Id. 39–42. None of these assertions is a new Ninth Circuit finding.

**United Reporting/respondent:** Respondent argues that there was no evidence of a solicitation-invasion problem, invokes favorable arrestee declarations, disputes the substantiality and consistency of the asserted interests, and attacks both overinclusion and underinclusion. Resp. Br. 20–25. It argues that permitted dissemination defeats privacy and discrimination rationales and that the sales restriction can also reach useful mixed editorial and commercial material. Id. 22–24. Its characterizations of private-investigator access as unrestricted and of permissive categories as favored commercial speakers remain party constructions; the copied statute still carries its express sales-use prohibition. Its separate fourth-prong argument proposes recipient opt-outs, antidiscrimination rules, and counterspeech as alternatives. Id. 25–26. Those alternatives were presented to the reviewing Court but were not adopted by the Ninth Circuit, which did not reach that prong.

## Relief/source limit preserved

The Ninth Circuit affirmed the judgment invalidating the challenged restriction as an infringement of United Reporting's rights. Pet. App. 36a. The district court's reproduced opinion orders preparation of a declaratory and injunctive judgment, rather than reproducing the final signed injunction. Id. 23a. JA 1 and the briefs describe a permanent enforcement injunction, but its precise final decretal language remains unrecovered. The full-reading and pleading/own-injury findings in the original-record validation remain applicable.
