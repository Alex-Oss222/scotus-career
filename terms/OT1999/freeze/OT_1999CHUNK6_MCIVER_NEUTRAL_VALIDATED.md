## Governing law and supported alternatives

Knotts concerns observed public travel; Karo separates installation with the then-owner's consent, transfer, and later acquisition of concealed interior information. Neither automatically resolves nonconsensual installation on an existing owner's vehicle. Katz's privacy principle, Jacobsen's meaningful possessory-interference standard and the actual Class/Cardwell scopes must be kept distinct. Soldal protects possession independently of privacy, but its actual home-removal holding does not predetermine whether this attachment is meaningful interference. Bond distinguishes incidental contact with an effect from added physical examination of concealed information; this device's acquisition of future location information rather than concealed contents is a material comparison, not an assumed answer. Pinson protects concealed domestic activity and supplies no universal device-use rule.

**No-search/no-seizure path:** The government can argue that attaching small magnets to an exposed undercarriage neither obtained concealed information at installation nor affected driving, dominion or mechanical operation. Karo rejects technical trespass alone as decisive, and Knotts permits assistance in observing public movement. The strongest contrary argument is that an owner need not hide an automobile's exterior to retain control over its physical alteration, and that government use of the effect as its own information-gathering instrument is different from looking at an exposed surface. Karo's consensual original installation and Class's regulated VIN setting limit the analogy.

**Search path:** McIver can distinguish mere observation from purposeful physical manipulation of a protected effect to obtain information. Katz does not erase the constitutional protection of effects, and Hicks/Bond show that lawful access or exposure to some observation does not necessarily authorize an added physical investigation. The strongest contrary argument is that those cases involved acquisition of otherwise concealed information, whereas this attachment revealed nothing private and its later monitoring concerned public routes. A general installation-as-search rule would therefore require an express legal development from date-eligible sources; it must not be presented as already settled by a later tracking case.

**Independent seizure path:** Unauthorized attachment, including installation work and the antenna, may meaningfully interfere with the owner's exclusion and control interests even without loss of driving ability. The contrary path reads Karo's meaningful-interference threshold as requiring more than unwanted occupation of space or speculative damage, and relies on the lack of actual damage or dispossession. Treat search and seizure separately rather than assuming one follows from the other.

If constitutional coverage is found, reasonableness and remedy remain independent. Labron/Kilgore rejects a separate exigency requirement where the established automobile exception actually applies, but does not itself authorize any investigative device installation or protected-premises entry. Kleinfeld's absence-of-special-exigency discussion cannot override that current rule. The supported lower record identifies no argued exception for installation and no consent; a new warrant exception or waived justification cannot be invented. If a preserved exception remains genuinely open, its actual facts and scope require review rather than automatic validation.

A constitutional error could lead to a bounded remand on causation, surviving lawful evidence and harmlessness, or an existing-record harmlessness conclusion if the proper burden is satisfied. Kleinfeld supplies a supported harmlessness argument, not a binding finding. The government's strongest position is the extensive earlier cameras and visual following; McIver's strongest answer is that tracking may have directed particular observations and the truck-warrant ruling excised a different illegality. Neither an automatic acquittal nor unrestricted new evidentiary opportunity follows from rejection of the no-search/no-seizure ground. Any further adjudication must stay within actual preservation and the grant.

## Standards and Tests selection: Possessory seizures and the claimant's own Fourth Amendment interest

### Possessory seizures and the claimant's own Fourth Amendment interest

**Governing question:** What instructions govern possessory seizures and the claimant's own Fourth Amendment interest?

**Current rule:** Meaningful government-attributable interference with possession of a home is a Fourth Amendment seizure without a search, privacy or liberty invasion, or criminal investigative purpose. A competing due-process characterization does not displace the specific seizure claim; coverage does not establish unreasonableness or §1983 liability. A suppression claimant must show interference with his or her own protected property interest in a seizure or reasonable expectation of privacy in a search. Conspiracy membership neither creates an exception to that personal-interest requirement nor destroys an actual protected interest. Attribution, immunity and the remaining merits must be separately established.

**Authority by component:**

White’s opinion, joined by Stone-Zsela, Blackmun, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas; nine Justices adopt the same rule.
- A due-process characterization does not displace the specific seizure claim: Soldal v. Cook County — A due-process characterization does not displace the specific seizure claim, 1992-12-08. White’s opinion, joined by Stone-Zsela, Blackmun, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas; unanimous holding.
- Suppression depends on the defendant’s own protected interest: United States v. Padilla — Suppression depends on the defendant’s own protected interest, 1993-05-03. Per curiam Opinion of the Court; Stone-Zsela, White, Blackmun, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas (9 Justices). Direct majority agreement at this level of generality; no Marks inference.

## Standards and Tests selection: Targeted thermal examination of concealed home activity

### Targeted thermal examination of concealed home activity

**Governing question:** When technological reception of a closed home’s heat patterns is a search.

**Current rule:** A targeted technological examination is a Fourth Amendment search when its reported heat-pattern information reveals otherwise unobservable information about concealed domestic operation. External reception and lack of an image identifying a person or object do not remove that protection. Such a search requires a warrant absent a recognized exception; a later physical-entry warrant does not retroactively authorize it. Investigative purpose or equipment use alone is insufficient.

**Authority by component:**

- I. The targeted examination of this closed home's heat patterns is a search: I. The targeted examination of this closed home's heat patterns is a search, 1995-05-08. Part I of Scalia's opinion, joined by Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O’Connor, Souter, Thomas, and Ginsburg: seven Justices. This directly controlling application concerns the covered dwelling and demonstrated differential-heat output. Stone-Zsela also states a broader formulation in his separate writing; the other six do not join that formulation merely by joining this application.
- II. No warrant exception justifies this scan on the established record: II. No warrant exception justifies this scan on the established record, 1995-05-08. Part II of Scalia's opinion, joined by Stevens, O’Connor, Souter, Thomas, and Ginsburg: six Justices. Stone-Zsela does not join Part II and expresses no view on its additional proposition. Kennedy and Breyer do not reach justification because they find no search.

**Present operation effective:** 1995-05-08; the component authorities identified above.

**Exceptions and limits:** The holding adopts no general rule for every sensor or exterior emission and depends on no finding about public availability, flight altitude or frequency. Search and warrant justification remain distinct from exclusion, subsequent warrant validity and preservation; correction of a no-search ruling revives no forfeited affidavit challenge or automatic suppression remedy.

## Standards and Tests selection: Privacy in a rented vehicle and personal luggage

### Privacy in a rented vehicle and personal luggage

**Governing question:** Fourth Amendment interests of an unlisted driver and in separately protected closed luggage.

**Current rule:** Absence from the rental agreement does not alone defeat privacy where the driver possesses and controls the vehicle with the authorized renter’s permission and without theft, fraud or comparable wrongful possession. The actual legitimate relationship, right to exclude, owner’s retained rights and effective termination of possession must be assessed; permission alone does not establish privacy. Lack of vehicle privacy does not extinguish an independently established legitimate interest in the driver’s closed luggage, supported by ownership or lawful control and measures preserving privacy.

### Automobile searches

#### Pennsylvania v. Labron; Pennsylvania v. Kilgore, 518 U.S. 938 (1996)

**Docket or dockets:** Nos. 95-1691 and 95-1738.
**Decided:** July 1, 1996.

##### Whether probable cause and ready mobility require an additional federal exigency

**Holding:** Police may search a readily mobile automobile without a warrant when probable cause supports belief that it contains evidence or contraband; the Fourth Amendment requires no additional showing of unforeseen circumstances, imminent departure or insufficient time to obtain a warrant. Police custody, the ability to guard the vehicle or an earlier opportunity to seek a warrant does not itself impose that extra condition. The object sought limits the search to places and containers capable of containing it.

**Proposition-level authority:** O’Connor’s opinion, joined by Stone-Zsela, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas and Breyer; seven Justices. Stevens and Ginsburg oppose review on jurisdictional/discretionary grounds, not an additional-exigency merits rule.

**Material application:** Carroll/Chambers supply automobile mobility; Carney adds reduced regulated-vehicle privacy; Johns preserves search authority during custody, with its separate unreasonable-delay limitation. Ross ties the permissible search to its object.

**Limits and questions not reached:** No independent authority to enter a home or protected curtilage, search an immobile structure as an automobile, omit probable cause or exceed object-based scope. Different residential uses and protected-premises access retain their own rules.

##### How that rule applies to the two reviewed searches

**Holding:** The accepted probable cause and adjudicated automobile circumstances bring these searches within the exception for purposes of the federal additional-exigency ground on which the judgments rest. The Court reverses that ground in each case and remands; its application is neither an unconditional approval of every entry and search act nor an instruction merely to reconsider whether the rejected urgency requirement should remain.

**Proposition-level authority:** O’Connor’s opinion, joined by Stone-Zsela, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas and Breyer; seven Justices. Stevens and Ginsburg oppose review on jurisdictional/discretionary grounds, not an additional-exigency merits rule.

**Material application:** Labron’s accepted probable cause arose from drug transactions and retrievals from an ordinary street-parked Lincoln. Its failed start on attempted impoundment and highway-legal exterior establish neither a running engine nor known pre-search incapacity. Kilgore’s accepted probable cause arose from the controlled purchase and truck connection; private parking, police custody and available guards do not add a federal urgency requirement. Lack of consent or visible contraband does not defeat an otherwise applicable automobile exception.

**Limits and questions not reached:** No general known-mechanical-incapacity rule, lawful boundary-crossing finding, curtilage classification, conversion of house consent into truck consent, new consent/access hearing or revived Labron privacy-interest objection.

##### What the reversal and remand permit

**Holding:** Each judgment is reversed, and each case is remanded for proceedings consistent with the Court's automobile and jurisdictional holdings. Pennsylvania may apply an actually adequate and independent state ground if properly available, and may address genuinely remaining scope or other distinct issues under its procedures; this mandate establishes neither their availability nor their merits and directs no automatic admission or prosecution outcome.

**Proposition-level authority:** O’Connor’s opinion, joined by Stone-Zsela, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas and Breyer; seven Justices. Stevens and Ginsburg oppose review on jurisdictional/discretionary grounds, not an additional-exigency merits rule.

**Material application:** The exception is applied to the actual reviewed additional-exigency issue, not left wholly undecided. Distinct genuinely remaining scope, access and independent state-law issues are subject to ordinary procedural predicates.

**Limits and questions not reached:** No new preservation, waiver, admission of evidence, conviction, retrial or automatic hearing is ordered.

**Operative remedy or transition:** Certiorari granted in each case; both suppression judgments summarily reversed and remanded, 7–2, in a reasoned decision without plenary argument. The undisclosed petition poll is not a merits tally. For jurisdictional holdings, see Federal Courts — Finality and adequate independent state grounds, these cases, July 1, 1996.

**Treatment of earlier authority:**

- **Cox Broadcasting v. Cohn:** The Court applies the third practical-finality category to these binding suppression determinations and the State's inability to obtain later effective review. No general interlocutory-review rule is added.
- **Michigan v. Long:** Its federal/interwoven-ground inquiry is applied separately to Labron and Kilgore. A State's power to afford greater protection remains, but possible state authority is not proof of an actual independent judgment.
- **Arizona v. Evans:** Its Long holding remains controlling. Its separate exclusionary-rule exception and unresolved clerical-error attribution do not authorize these searches or require the same factual remand in a different case.
- **Sochor and Middlebrooks:** Their actually sufficient independent preservation and state constitutional grounds remain respected; the Court distinguishes them from the grounds under review here.
- **Carroll v. United States and Chambers v. Maroney:** The established automobile exception is applied without a new case-specific warrant-impracticability requirement. Police control does not by itself require a different federal rule.
- **California v. Carney:** Mobility and reduced automobile privacy support the rule; its actual setting and reservation concerning differently situated residential uses remain. It creates no general authority to enter protected property.
- **United States v. Johns:** Custody alone does not eliminate vehicle-search authority. The Court does not remove Johns's separate protection against unreasonable delay or decide a delay claim here.
- **United States v. Ross:** Probable cause's object determines the permitted places and containers to search; it is not a license for a search beyond that object.
- **Ornelas v. United States:** Independent review of ultimate constitutional probability questions, clear-error protection for historical facts and due weight to grounded inferences remain. The case supplies no new fact, waiver or probable-cause presumption.
- **Whren v. United States:** Objective authority for the initial stop remains separate from search, manner, scope and duration. Its unanimity does not supply an additional vote or factual premise here.
- **Wilson v. Arkansas and Pinson:** The distinct protections governing dwelling entry and the particular technological examination of domestic information remain. Neither is a general automobile rule or establishes Kilgore's property boundary or lawful access.

No prior decision is overruled. The Court applies the inherited automobile exception to reject the added federal condition; it does not adopt a new general mechanical-defect, curtilage or premises-consent rule.

### Bond v. United States — 2000-04-17

#### Judgment & Remedy

The Fifth Circuit judgment is reversed, 7–2. Supporting: Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas and Ginsburg. Opposing: Scalia and Breyer.

The case returns for implementation of the suppression ruling in Bond's criminal case. The actual motion also concerns later confessions; the Court does not decide every unreviewed causal or statement-specific issue. It finds no independent admission ground preserved and grants no new opportunity to invent one. No automatic acquittal, release, new trial or additional hearing is ordered.

#### Opinion Topology

Justice Souter delivers the opinion of the Court, joined in full by Chief Justice Stone-Zsela and Justices Stevens, O'Connor, Kennedy, Thomas and Ginsburg. Justice Breyer dissents, joined by Justice Scalia.

#### Holdings

#### Exploratory touch can search closed luggage without opening it

**Holding and operative rule:** Placing closed personal luggage in a shared overhead area exposes it to ordinary movement, incidental contact and associated compression, but does not expose concealed contents to an added physical examination of their shape and density. Exploratory squeezing that acquires that concealed information is a search; the inquiry concerns the objective character of the examination, not an officer's undisclosed motive or the mere fact of touching.

**Authority:** Justice Souter's opinion, joined by Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Kennedy, Thomas and Ginsburg, supplies seven votes for the rule, its application and the bounded remedy.

**Controlling explanation:** Katz protects privacy maintained in a publicly accessible setting, and Place recognizes luggage as a repository of private effects. The independent-luggage rule keeps that interest distinct from privacy in the surrounding vehicle. Bond zipped his bag and kept it above his seat. Other passengers' ability to move it did not expose everything an examination could discover.

The agent felt and squeezed overhead bags and discerned a hard package's edges and different density in Bond's bag. Even respecting the lower court's limited-contact finding, that act examined contents beyond ordinary handling. No particular squeeze count, bin configuration or disputed packaging inference is needed. Dickerson distinguishes authorized touching from additional exploration to obtain concealed information; Hicks likewise distinguishes observation from added physical manipulation. Neither requires entry into the container, and neither supplies an initial protective authority here.

Ciraolo and Riley permit deliberate observation of exposed information. They do not equate visual observation with every tactile means of examining closed contents. The large package and soft fabric do not establish unlimited exposure to such examination. The rule leaves routine repositioning, bumps and ordinary handling outside its trigger and decides no general question about canine sniffs or supported safety searches.

The agent had no individualized suspicion before this examination, and lawful immigration questioning supplied no separate search authority. Later discovery of drugs cannot supply retrospective justification. The Government does not rely independently on later consent. Robinette preserves the distinction between permission and independence from prior illegality, but provides no basis to reopen that surrendered argument. The resulting remand addresses the actual drugs-and-confessions motion without deciding unreviewed statement-specific consequences.

#### Precedent Treatment

- Katz and Place are applied to privacy retained in closed luggage despite travel in a public conveyance.
- The independent-luggage rule is applied: a bag's separately established privacy does not disappear with the surrounding vehicle's public accessibility.
- Dickerson is applied for the boundary between authorized contact and added exploration; its protective-frisk setting is not imported as search authority.
- Hicks is applied to additional physical acquisition of concealed information rather than mere observation.
- Ciraolo and Riley are distinguished because they concern information exposed to public observation; their objective approach remains intact.
- Flippo preserves the distinction between lawful presence, a visible exterior and concealed contents; no general presence-based exception is created.
- Wardlow preserves the sequence of objective justification and forbids reliance on later discoveries to justify the earlier intrusion.
- Robinette retains the separation between consent voluntariness and independence from prior illegality; no consent ground is adjudicated here after the Government's nonreliance.

#### Law After Decision

The Fourth Amendment protects closed overhead luggage against an objectively exploratory tactile examination beyond the contact ordinary shared storage exposes. The decision creates no universal touching prohibition, subjective-purpose test or new search exception. The separate detention-duration ruling and unreviewed statement-specific questions are not resolved by this holding.

### Bond v. United States — 2000-04-17

Justice Breyer, joined by Justice Scalia, would affirm. Closed luggage retains privacy, but this soft bag and its sizeable hard package exposed the relevant contours to foreseeable gripping and compression in crowded overhead storage. Ciraolo and Riley permit an officer to attend deliberately to information ordinary public contact reveals. The lower court's limited-contact finding does not establish an additional intrusion comparable to the further manipulation in Dickerson or Hicks. The dissent would distinguish opening the bag, rearranging its contents or materially more intrusive handling; it does not authorize unrestricted investigative touching. Its disagreement concerns this objective exposure boundary, not the officer's private purpose, and it therefore reaches no suppression consequence of an unlawful search.
