## Actual challenged scope and evidence

The challenged 1998 rule permits use within subscribed local, interexchange and commercial-mobile categories and permits sharing between affiliates serving those subscribed categories. It requires approval to cross into unsubscribed categories. The statutory baseline separately permits service-related use, legally required use and the specified operational, protection and inbound-call uses. The rule's exceptions for inside wiring, mobile-service health research and specified adjunct-to-basic services must be considered in their actual scope. Its original restrictions concerning equipment/information services, competitor tracking and winback are separate provisions, not interchangeable descriptions of the approval rule.

Approval can be written, oral or electronic; it is not written-consent-only. Oral approval carries a demonstration burden. Approval continues until revoked or limited, and notification/approval records must be kept at least a year. Prior notice must identify the information, recipients, purposes, rights and steps, make clear that refusal does not affect subscribed services, and be comprehensible and nonmisleading. The original rule's notice and solicitation conditions and all qualifications are mechanically supplied in `preparation/OT_1999CHUNK2_USWEST_OFFICIAL_OPERATIVE_TEXT.md`; they must not be replaced by an unqualified description that all data use or all targeted advertising is prohibited.

The carrier's outbound-call study reported 33 percent refusing approval, 28 percent approving, and 39 percent hanging up or requesting no further calls. The last category cannot be silently counted as an express refusal. Among customers approached after initiating contact, 72 percent approved. Direct-mail response rates were 6–11 percent for residential customers and 5–9 percent for business customers; these are response rates, not necessarily refusal rates. The FCC and the carrier disagreed about what these results show. Neither privacy preference nor indifference nor dislike of marketing is conclusively established for every nonrespondent.

The majority regarded the agency's harm and alternative-mechanism evidence as insufficient and independently rejected fit. The dissent quoted the agency's actual consideration of written-only consent, express oral/written/electronic consent, and notice-and-opt-out. The FCC selected express approval because unread notices may yield uninformed silence and rejected written-only consent as unduly restrictive. Thus the accurate dispute is the adequacy and legal significance of the agency's consideration and support, not a historical fact that opt-out was never considered. The majority did not order adoption of opt-out.

The government's stated interests include confidentiality and competition; the latter remains a disputed supplemental justification, not an invented concession or automatically sufficient ground. The majority reported the agency's statement concerning limited privacy concerns from sharing within an integrated firm. That must be considered alongside the agency's service-category theory and the distinction between an integrated firm and separately treated affiliates. The challenged harm is not reducible to receipt of unwanted mail; the agency also identifies use and disclosure of confidential calling information beyond the existing service relationship.

## Governing legal alternatives

Lanphere and United Reporting require independent commercial-speech review of a use restriction selected by the contemplated truthful, lawful marketing, notwithstanding control over the source information. The lower dissent's nonexpressive-method characterization is an available argument, but it cannot silently displace that governing trigger. Central Hudson and Fox require substantial interest, real harm, material advancement and reasonable proportional fit. Edenfield permits supported prediction and imposes no universal empirical-study requirement. The actual Went For It decision reserves confidential-record misuse; it does not decide this record. Discovery Network, Rubin and Greater New Orleans require comparison of relevant permissions and exceptions without requiring regulation of all information or the least restrictive imaginable method.

The statutory question remains distinct. Section 222(c)(1) requires customer approval in its stated circumstances but does not specify the general approval method. Chevron does not control the First Amendment question; serious constitutional doubt must be analyzed rather than presumed from the fact that a challenge was made. Neither the corporate form nor common-carrier status removes commercial-speech protection. Nor must the FCC establish a freestanding constitutional privacy right in order to identify a substantial confidentiality interest. Paul P.'s particular constitutional holding supplies neither a general ownership right in CPNI nor a judgment resolving this statutory confidentiality case.

The Commission's strongest supported position is that opt-in directly prevents unknowing use of confidential service records, reflects the existing chosen service relationship, and leaves practical consent methods and other marketing open. The carrier's strongest supported position is that the actual internal-use mechanism, exceptions and study do not adequately justify loss of effective targeted communication when informed opt-out may suffice. Those alternatives must be assessed on the recorded evidence, without treating either silence as approval or nonresponse as refusal by definition.
