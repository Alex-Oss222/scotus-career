# United States v. Playboy Entertainment Group, Inc. — fresh neutral validation

Engine stage 3A only. No. 98-1682; argued November 30, 1999; authorized merits event May 22, 2000. Direct appeal from the three-judge District of Delaware judgment enjoining enforcement of §505 of the Telecommunications Act of 1996. The Act's direct-review provision is distinct from the codified cable section numbered 561. No current-case vote or proposed disposition is supplied. No nonparticipation is identified.

## Challenged text and evidentiary distinctions

Section 505, codified at 47 U.S.C. §561, applies to a multichannel video programming distributor providing sexually explicit adult or other indecent programming on a channel primarily dedicated to sexually oriented programming. It requires full scrambling or blocking of that channel's video and audio so a nonsubscriber to the channel or programming does not receive it. Until compliance, the distributor must not provide the programming during hours when a significant number of children are likely to view it, as determined by the FCC. The FCC chose 10 p.m. to 6 a.m. as the permissible period. Subsection (c) defines scrambling as rearranging signal content so the programming cannot be viewed or heard understandably; it does not create an additional FCC determination-of-compliance procedure. The full text, including each subsection, is copied below.

Section 504, codified at §560, separately requires a cable operator, on a cable subscriber's request and without charge, to fully scramble or block the audio and video of each channel carrying such programming so a nonsubscriber does not receive it. Subsection (b) supplies the same definition of scrambling. This is a household-request remedy distinct from §505's systemwide rule. Section 504's own text does not command a publicity program or interim notice of a blocking device; the district court's notice findings and remedy must be identified separately.

The challenged speech is nonobscene adult programming protected for adults. Analog scrambling could leave discernible images or audio, called signal bleed, in nonsubscriber households. Digital transmission could prevent that problem but was not yet widely available. The government's stated concern is children's exposure without parental consent, not a general power to suppress adult access or a demonstrated decision by every parent to reject the programming.

The district court's proposed less restrictive arrangement combines §504 household blocking with adequate recurring notice about the channels, signal bleed, children's possible exposure, the free blocking remedy and how to request it. It required Playboy to insist on the notification arrangement in its cable contracts. Those are lower-court remedial findings and requirements; they are not all commands Congress wrote into §504. Effectiveness must be evaluated for this actual arrangement, including whether notice reaches affected households and requested devices work.

The record includes a survey in which roughly 69 percent of operators chose time channeling and evidence that 30–50 percent of adult viewing occurred before 10 p.m. Fewer than one-half of one percent of subscribers requested blocking during the period when §504 alone operated. A low request rate may reflect lack of notice, preference, perceived risk or other circumstances; it does not by itself prove that parents are indifferent, that effective notice is impossible, or that the remedy is effective. The extent, duration and frequency of intelligible bleed and children's exposure remain matters to evaluate on the record, not assumptions supplied from the word pornography.

## Entering law and opposing paths

The law targets speech by sexual content and burdens adult access to protected programming. The model must select and justify the scrutiny that actually follows from the supplied precedents, including differences among broadcasting, cable and the Internet. It cannot import Turner's content-neutral carriage deference without addressing the content distinction, nor assume Pacifica gives cable the same pervasive-access treatment as broadcasting.

The simulated ACT/Pacifica rule permits appropriately confined broadcast indecency regulation while refusing an inadequately justified sixteen-hour adult burden. Denver's controlling rule invalidated compelled segregated blocking of indecent leased-access channels but fractured on voluntary editorial permission; those propositions have different coalitions and reasons. Reno invalidated broad restrictions on adult Internet speech and distinguished users' affirmative access and the lack of broadcasting's scarcity/presence rationale. O'Connor's separate willingness to preserve transmissions known to involve only minors does not approve a restriction reaching mixed adult audiences. Erie concerns a different conduct regulation and does not convert this content-directed cable rule into a generally applicable conduct rule.

NCRL, effective May 1, invalidates the particular political-committee and issue-expenditure coverage that lacked a determinate electoral-financing boundary. It preserves the distinction between protected expression and genuinely covered financing, refuses to replace missing statutory limits with nonbinding litigation assurances, and leaves the underlying campaign rules within their actual scope. That is a coverage-defect holding, not a decision about children, signal bleed, cable scrutiny or relative effectiveness of blocking. Its unanimous common ground and separate reservations cannot establish current votes. Its insistence on the actual restraint and lawful, concrete boundary is relevant by analogy only; the independent cable analysis remains necessary.

**Path sustaining §505:** Protecting children from unwanted, sexually explicit signal bleed can support a strong governmental interest. Nonsubscribing households receive residual pictures or sound without choosing the channel, and household request systems may fail to protect children whose parents do not know the risk or cannot reliably secure blocking. An operator can avoid time channeling by fully blocking; the government can argue that technology and scheduling options target the technical problem while preserving adult late-night access. Denver's separate views favoring targeted protection and concern about mixed/unpredictable programming supply relevant arguments at their actual scope.

The strongest objection is that this optional compliance structure still produces a substantial burden on willing adult speakers and viewers, as the actual use of time channeling shows. A significant protective interest does not prove necessity of a systemwide daytime ban where household blocking plus meaningful notice could serve it. The government's failure to show the comparative inadequacy of that specific alternative cannot be filled by an assumption that any sexual image inevitably defeats parental control. Any asserted deference must be tied to the correct level of scrutiny and actual record, not transferred wholesale from Turner.

**Path invalidating §505:** The government must justify the substantial adult burden and demonstrate why an available, less speech-restrictive arrangement will not adequately serve the child-protection interest. Effective notice and free household blocking can empower parents to prevent unwanted exposure while preserving daytime adult access. The record's low requests are not proof that this adequately publicized system would fail. Where the evidence does not establish that the specific alternative is insufficient, the broader content-based restriction may fail without denying the legitimacy of protecting children.

The strongest objection is that individual requests leave unprotected homes whose parents never receive or act on notice, while signal bleed remains unsolicited. A parental-choice system may provide materially less protection than complete blocking, and voluntary industry notice may be unstable. The model must assess the actual notice obligation and implementation evidence instead of treating the existence of §504 on paper as proof of comparable protection. It must also address whether narrower digital/analog, technological or evidentiary relief is supported, without writing a different statute or declaring every household-blocking regime sufficient.

The earlier Denver separate positions require careful issue comparison. Scalia and Thomas emphasized channel context, ordinary parental tools and the limited maximum delay under that former regime; a maximum delay is not a mandatory waiting period. Their reasoning does not establish approval of this actual time restriction. Kennedy and Ginsburg's content-scrutiny concerns, and O'Connor's distinction between voluntary editorial choice and compulsory restrictions, likewise provide arguments rather than current votes. No modeled vote is inferred here.

## Standards and Tests selection: Content-selected restrictions on protected broadcast indecency

### Content-selected restrictions on protected broadcast indecency

**Governing question:** Restrictions on nonobscene indecent broadcasting justified by protecting children and assisting parents.

**Current rule:** Nonobscene indecent broadcasting is protected expression. A restriction selecting that content must be carefully tailored to a compelling interest, with independent review of necessity and means in light of broadcasting's actual characteristics. Child protection and parental assistance are compelling but do not establish the necessity of the selected schedule. Legislative predictions receive substantial consideration without displacing independent review; neither scientific certainty nor a particular report is indispensable. Adult access must remain protected, and Pacifica's contextual permission does not validate every general daily restriction.

**Authority by component:**

- Governing review and media-specific limits: ACT/Pacifica — Protected broadcasting and the applicable review, 1996-03-04; Ginsburg Part I, joined by Stone-Zsela, O’Connor, Kennedy, Souter and Thomas (six). Parts II–III, joined by Stone-Zsela, O’Connor, Kennedy and Thomas (five), invalidate and remove the particular mandatory sixteen-hour schedule.

**Present operation effective:** ACT/Pacifica, 1996-03-04.

**Exceptions and limits:** The mandatory 6 a.m.–10 p.m. schedule cannot be enforced; the existing 10 p.m.–6 a.m. protection remains. No replacement national hour, automatic serious-merit or low-rating exemption, universal program-harm showing, station-specific permission hearing or new definition is created. Pacifica, §1464 and lawful obscenity enforcement retain their actual scopes; content-neutral cable carriage has a distinct standard.

**Holdings navigation:** Action for Children's Television v. FCC; Pacifica Foundation v. FCC, judgment below 58 F.3d 654.

## Standards and Tests selection: Cable access permissions and the invalid compulsory segregation system

### Cable access permissions and the invalid compulsory segregation system

**Governing question:** The surviving 1992 §10 access provisions, their constitutional limits, and the distinct 1996 §506 permissions.

**Current rule:** 1992 §10(a) remains operative independently of §10(b): on leased-access capacity, an operator may prospectively enforce a written and published policy barring programming reasonably believed to describe or depict sexual or excretory activities or organs in a patently offensive manner under contemporary community standards, with FCC standards for the cable medium. It permits rather than commands exclusion. For covered leased-access programming not voluntarily prohibited under (a), §10(b)'s integral compulsory identification/notification, channel-segregation, default-blocking and advance written-election arrangement may not be imposed on willing speakers and adult recipients; its cumulative burdens lack adequate justification. Section 10(c) and its rules may not authorize suppression of protected nonobscene indecency on public, educational and governmental access capacity. The decision does not invalidate lawful obscenity controls or independently adjudicate §10(c)'s category for material soliciting or promoting unlawful conduct, construed by the FCC as otherwise proscribed material.

Separately, 1996 §506(a) permits refusal of public-access programming or portions containing obscenity, indecency or nudity; §506(b) permits refusal of leased-access programming or portions containing those categories. These permissions neither command refusal nor incorporate the 1992 written-policy, reasonable-belief or compulsory subscriber mechanisms. Public-access wording does not itself include all educational and governmental capacity.

**Authority by component:**

- Section 10(a) permission and severability: 1. Section 10(a): particular permission upheld; divided sufficient grounds and 4. Severability and the scope of relief, 1996-06-28; six support retention (Stevens, O’Connor, Scalia, Souter, Thomas, Breyer), but only four support its balancing rationale.
- Invalid integral §10(b) arrangement: 2. Section 10(b): the integral compulsory design violates the First Amendment, 1996-06-28; Souter Part III, joined by Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O’Connor, Kennedy, Ginsburg and Breyer (seven).
- Limited §10(c) invalidity: 3. Section 10(c): protected-indecency authority invalidated; reasons remain divided, 1996-06-28; Stone-Zsela, Stevens, Kennedy, Souter, Ginsburg and Breyer (six judgment supporters), divided between two three-Justice sufficient grounds.
- Independent 1996 permissions: Public Law 104-104 §506(a)–(b), amending 47 U.S.C. §§531(e), 532(c)(2), effective 1996-02-08; the same distinct source boundary is preserved by Denver, all nine, on 1996-06-28.

**Present operation effective:** Denver Area/Alliance, 1996-06-28, for the 1992 provisions; the separate §506 text operates from 1996-02-08.

**Open question:** No majority adopts a complete general framework for access permissions, a universal public-forum classification, or a rule making every operator choice governmental. Section 506’s constitutionality and particular exercises remain unadjudicated; §10(c)’s invalidation does not extinguish that independent authority or establish unconditional carriage rights.

**Exceptions and limits:** The §10(b) holding addresses its integral machinery, not every notification, age screen, voluntary request or blocking device. The statutory thirty days was an outside fulfillment period, not a mandatory wait. No categorical serious-value exemption or universal validation of indecency definitions follows.

**Holdings navigation:** Denver Area Educational Telecommunications Consortium, Inc. v. FCC; Alliance for Community Media v. FCC, 518 U.S. 727 (1996).

## Standards and Tests selection: Cable-channel blocking duties under the 1996 Act

### Cable-channel blocking duties under the 1996 Act

**Governing question:** The separate triggers for requested subscriber blocking and sexually oriented channel blocking.

**Current rule:** Under 47 U.S.C. §560, upon the request of a cable-service subscriber, an operator must, without charge, fully scramble or otherwise fully block the audio and video programming of each channel carrying such programming so that one not a subscriber does not receive it. The enacted channel scope has no independent indecency or sexually oriented channel trigger. Under the distinct 47 U.S.C. §561, a multichannel distributor providing sexually explicit adult or other indecent programming on a channel primarily dedicated to sexually oriented programming must fully scramble or block that channel's audio and video so a nonsubscriber to that channel or programming does not receive it. Until compliance, it must withhold that programming during FCC-determined hours when a significant number of children are likely to view it. Both define scrambling as rearranging the signal so the programming cannot be viewed or heard understandably.

**Authority by component:**

- Independent request and program-category commands, definitions and transitions: Public Law 104-104 §§504–505, adding Communications Act §§640–641; §504 effective 1996-02-08, §505 amendment effective 1996-03-09. Denver — independent 1996 statutory boundaries, 1996-06-28, preserves their separate status.

**Present operation effective:** Section 504, 1996-02-08; section 505, 1996-03-09.

**Exceptions and limits:** No FCC-selected hour, compliance fact, technical-feasibility finding, or general construction of subscription scope is established. Act §561’s facial-review provision is a different enactment from codified 47 U.S.C. §561.

**Holdings navigation:** Denver Area Educational Telecommunications Consortium, Inc. v. FCC; Alliance for Community Media v. FCC, 518 U.S. 727 (1996).

## Standards and Tests selection: Adult access and content-based criminal network-speech restrictions

### Adult access and content-based criminal network-speech restrictions

**Governing question:** Facial review of indecency restrictions on a general-purpose communications network.

**Current rule:** A content-based criminal restriction fails the First Amendment when uncertain, substantially overbroad categories suppress substantial protected adult speech and the government does not establish narrow tailoring in light of practical, materially less restrictive means of limiting minors' access. Knowledge that a minor is present, other available channels and scienter do not by themselves cure uncertain content categories or adult-access burdens. Age-verification defenses must afford workable protection on the actual record; cost, loss of anonymity and lawful adult exclusion remain material. A severability clause permits removal of a discrete unlawful textual branch but does not authorize judicial creation of a minor-only offense or an absent value exception.

**Authority by component:**
- Overbreadth, uncertainty and narrow fit: Reno v. ACLU, Reno v. American Civil Liberties Union — I. The challenged content restrictions violate the First Amendment; textual severance boundary: Reno v. American Civil Liberties Union — II. Textual severance preserves obscenity law; judicial rewriting cannot save the remaining restrictions, Ginsburg with Stone-Zsela, Stevens, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas and Breyer (eight), 1997-06-26.

**Present operation effective:** Reno v. ACLU, 1997-06-26.

**Open question:** Precisely drawn future controls with effective nonexclusionary age assurance, parental-approval questions, every Internet service's classification and extraterritorial scope remain open. The holding creates no independent Fifth Amendment rule and leaves obscenity, child sexual-abuse material, threats, fraud, unlawful solicitation and direct harmful contact to their own predicates.

**Holdings navigation:** Reno v. American Civil Liberties Union (1997-06-26) — First Amendment / Internet content restrictions.

### Indecent broadcasting and adult access

#### Action for Children's Television v. FCC; Pacifica Foundation v. FCC, judgment below 58 F.3d 654

**Docket or dockets:** D.C. Circuit Nos. 93-1092 and 93-1100; no separate Supreme Court docket assigned.

**Decided:** March 4, 1996.

##### The controlling review of protected nonobscene indecent broadcasting

**Holding:** Nonobscene indecent expression is protected. Content-selected restrictions must be carefully tailored to a compelling interest, with independent review of necessity and means in light of broadcasting's actual characteristics. Protecting children and assisting parental supervision are compelling interests but do not themselves establish this schedule's necessity.

**Proposition-level authority:** Ginsburg's Part I, joined by Stone-Zsela, O'Connor, Kennedy, Souter and Thomas; six Justices.

**Limits and questions not reached:** Predictive legislative judgments receive substantial consideration but not conclusive effect. Scientific certainty and a particular legislative report are not indispensable; material differences between broadcast and telephone services remain.

**Treatment of earlier authority:** Pacifica's contextual holding supplies no general timetable approval. Sable protects adult access and careful tailoring; Turner preserves deferential but independent review without transferring its content-neutral cable intermediate test or four-Justice insufficiency application.

##### Whether the mandatory 6 a.m.–10 p.m. schedule is sufficiently tailored

**Holding:** The mandatory sixteen-hour exclusion insufficiently fits the record justification for its broad burden on protected adult broadcasting and is unconstitutional. Substantial child access, late audiences and supervisory difficulties do not establish the necessary connection to this general exclusion across media and contexts.

**Proposition-level authority:** Ginsburg's Part II, joined by Stone-Zsela, O'Connor, Kennedy and Thomas; five Justices. Souter's separate renewed-review vacatur is not a sixth vote for invalidity.

No automatic low-ratings exemption or individual defense is established. The decision requires neither proof of harm from every program nor flawless alternatives and does not make all media interchangeable. Facial vagueness and particular sanctions remain undecided.

**Treatment of earlier authority:** Butler protects adults against confinement to children's material without prescribing hours. Pacifica and Red Lion are not overruled; Pacifica's three-Justice speech-value discussion is not made controlling.

##### The mandatory remedy and continuing nighttime protection

**Holding:** The appellate approval must be set aside, the corresponding FCC rule removed and §16(a)'s dependent command barred insofar as it requires the invalid 6 a.m.–10 p.m. schedule. **Proposition-level authority:** Ginsburg's Part III, joined by Stone-Zsela, O'Connor, Kennedy and Thomas, supplies five direct votes for final invalidity and mandatory removal; all nine preserve nighttime protection. Stone-Zsela calls the action on appellate approval vacatur and the other four reversal; their operative removal direction is identical. Souter does not join it.

**Limits and questions not reached:** The original §16(a) distinction covered public stations signing off at or before midnight from 6 a.m.–10 p.m., and other stations from 6 a.m.–midnight. Section 551's conditional parental-choice provisions establish no presently effective technological alternative, repeal, mootness or automatic remand; no device-system factual premise is adopted. No individual sanction, damages or later technology implementation is decided.

### Cable access and protected indecency

#### Denver Area Educational Telecommunications Consortium, Inc. v. FCC; Alliance for Community Media v. FCC, 518 U.S. 727 (1996)

**Docket or dockets:** Nos. 95-124 and 95-227.
**Decided:** June 28, 1996.

##### Whether 1992 §10(a)’s leased-access permission is facially invalid

**Authority of the judgment:** The facial challenge fails, 6–3: Stevens, O’Connor, Scalia, Souter, Thomas and Breyer sustain §10(a); Stone-Zsela, Kennedy and Ginsburg would invalidate it. No controlling sufficient rationale exists under Marks. Souter’s contextual accommodation rationale has Stevens, O’Connor and Breyer; Scalia and Thomas instead rely on the absence of a freestanding right to compel an unwilling private editor. Neither rationale is established as a logical subset of the other. The component judgment does not establish a general permission-review framework.

**Limits and questions not reached:** The retained permission allows, but does not require, prospective enforcement of a written and published policy prohibiting programming reasonably believed to depict or describe sexual/excretory activities or organs in a patently offensive manner under contemporary community standards, specified by the FCC for cable. Protected nonobscene indecency is included; this is not Miller’s full obscenity test and has no categorical serious literary, artistic, political or scientific value exemption, although context matters. No universal validation of indecency definitions, certification, actual policies or genuinely governmental facilities follows.

##### Whether §10(b)’s compulsory identification, segregation and opt-in arrangement violates the First Amendment

**Holding:** When covered leased-access programming is not voluntarily prohibited under §10(a), the government may not impose this integral identification/notification, channel-segregation, default-blocking and advance written-election system. Its cumulative burden on willing speakers and adult recipients fails independent necessity and means review despite compelling child-protection and parental-assistance interests.

**Proposition-level authority:** Souter’s Part III and V-B, joined by Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O’Connor, Kennedy, Ginsburg and Breyer; seven Justices.

**Material application:** Programmers identify and notify; operators concentrate covered speech on blocked capacity. Access requires a written request and FCC-required statement of adult age, fulfilled within thirty days, not a mandatory thirty-day wait; another blocked channel may be used if capacity is insufficient. The operator-installed lockbox accommodation described for systems without premium/pay-per-view channels leaves the operator holding the key/code pending a request and retains default denial and written opt-in. It differs from a household-controlled device. Mixed unpredictable programming creates real parental difficulty but does not justify the integral design’s delay, deterrence and channel-wide burdens. Household controls remain usable after opt-in.

**Limits and questions not reached:** No finding supplies a public registry, universal affordability, perfect technical alternative, implementation of 1996 blocking laws or absence of all supporting evidence. Ordinary age checks, notices, voluntary requests, subscription mechanisms and blocking devices are not individually held unconstitutional. No additional vagueness, prior-restraint or certification ruling is necessary.

**Operative remedy or transition:** Approval reversed, 7–2; the integral compulsory scheme and corresponding rules must be set aside, without a judicial replacement design.

##### Whether §10(c) may authorize suppression of protected indecency on PEG access capacity

**Authority of the judgment:** Approval is reversed within the protected nonobscene-indecency scope, 6–3: Stone-Zsela, Stevens, Kennedy, Souter, Ginsburg and Breyer support relief; O’Connor, Scalia and Thomas oppose it. Souter/Stevens/Breyer’s contextual means/attribution ground and Kennedy/Stone-Zsela/Ginsburg’s strict legislative-content ground each have three votes. Neither is a demonstrated logical subset of the other; no controlling sufficient rationale exists under Marks, and no universal PEG public-forum or state-action rule is adopted.

**Limits and questions not reached:** Section 10(c) directs enabling rules concerning obscenity, sexually explicit conduct and unlawful-conduct solicitation/promotion; it does not compel operator censorship. The FCC interprets sexual explicitness through indecency and the unlawful-conduct category as otherwise proscribed material, permitting operator content certifications. Section (c) lacks (a)’s written/published-policy and reasonable-belief limits and (b)’s subscriber machinery. Lawful obscenity controls remain; unlawful solicitation and freestanding certification are not independently adjudicated, and protected advocacy is not automatically unlawful solicitation.

**Operative remedy or transition:** The corresponding rule must be set aside to the extent it authorizes protected-indecency suppression. This is final relief implementing the component judgment, not a remand to reconsider constitutionality after a universal franchise inquiry.

##### Whether §10(a) remains operative and what the judgment reaches

**Holding:** Section 10(a) remains independently operative after §10(b)’s removal. The permission’s independent operation and Congress’s purposes permit severance; the invalid compulsory scheme is removed as an integral whole.

**Proposition-level authority:** Souter’s V-A, joined by Stevens, O’Connor, Scalia, Thomas and Breyer; six Justices. The seven-Justice V-B coalition controls removal of (b). All nine accept V-D’s boundaries of the adjudicated issues without opponents thereby joining invalidity of a disputed component.

**Limits and questions not reached:** Section 10(d)’s obscenity exception to access-programming immunity remains and is not general liability for protected indecency. Independent 1996 §506(a) permits refusal on public-access capacity for obscenity, indecency or nudity; its public-access language does not automatically cover all educational/governmental capacity. Section 506(b) separately covers leased access. Neither commands refusal nor imports the 1992 policy/request conditions; their constitutionality and application are not decided or extinguished. Sections 504 and 505 retain their distinct subscriber-request/no-charge and sexually-oriented-channel triggers and transitions; their enactment proves no technical efficacy or actual implementation. Act §561’s three-judge facial-review route is not a new proceeding here. No unconditional carriage right, general private-editor immunity, universal forum designation, damages, new FCC hearing or automatic interim relief follows.

**Operative remedy or transition:** Remand to implement the three distinct component results within the challenged 1992 authorities.

**Treatment of earlier authority:**

- **ACT/Pacifica (March 4, 1996):** The Court applies its protection of nonobscene indecency and independent necessity/means review to the compulsory (b) arrangement. Its broadcast timetable disposition is not a cable rule. The divided permission opinions preserve the governing protection while disagreeing on application.
- **FCC v. Pacifica Foundation:** Its narrow, contextual broadcast holding remains; it does not automatically sustain every cable indecency restriction or establish that the present protected speech is of lower constitutional value.
- **Sable Communications v. FCC:** The (b) majority applies the distinction between compelling protection of children and adequately justified restrictions on adults. Scalia's separate willing-outlet analysis concerns the permissions and is not the Court's sufficient (b) ground.
- **Turner Broadcasting:** Cable editorial interests retain ordinary speech protection. The prior content-neutral full-power must-carry classification and intermediate standard do not govern an expressly content-selected indecency scheme. Neither the earlier limited shared predictive-review principle nor its fractured record-insufficiency discussion requires remand in every cable case.
- **Hurley:** The protection of an actual private expressive presentation remains intact. The component opinions disagree about the legal consequences of access duties; none establishes that ownership alone makes every independent access program the operator's message.
- **Perry and Cornelius:** Their attention to the actual forum and government's arrangement remains. The Court creates no universal cable-access forum; Kennedy's conditional forum ground does not become such a holding.
- **Rosenberger, Pinette and Chabad:** Their program-specific viewpoint, attribution and contextual rules are not converted into a presumption that every regulated channel is a public forum or governmental speaker. The divided opinions' use of those limits creates no new general forum or attribution test.
- **Blum, Jackson and Lugar; Reitman and Crawford:** The distinction between a private act and governmental alteration or encouragement remains relevant. No categorical operator immunity or rule making every private refusal government action is adopted.
- **Alaska Airlines:** The Court applies independent operation and congressional intent in retaining (a) after invalidating (b).

No earlier decision is overruled. The separate opinions' broader grounds do not independently alter controlling law.

### Internet content restrictions

#### Reno v. American Civil Liberties Union

**Docket or dockets:** No. 96-511.

**Decided:** June 26, 1997.

##### Whether the CDA's indecency and patently-offensive restrictions satisfy the First Amendment

**Holding:** A content-based criminal restriction on expression through a general-purpose communications network is facially invalid when uncertain and substantially overbroad categories suppress substantial protected adult speech and the Government fails to establish narrow tailoring in light of practical, materially less restrictive means of limiting minors' access. On this Internet record, §223(a)(1)(B)'s indecency prohibition and §223(d)(1)'s patently-offensive transmission and display prohibitions, together with their corresponding facility-permitting offenses, fail that requirement.

**Proposition-level authority:** Ginsburg's opinion of the Court, joined by Stone-Zsela, Stevens, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas and Breyer; eight direct votes. O'Connor joins parts of the judgment only.

**Material application:** Uncertain criminal categories reach substantial lawful adult discussion; scienter does not supply a clear content boundary. The record's age-verification costs, anonymity loss and exclusion of lawful adults leave the statutory defenses inadequate, while user filtering and precise treatment of unprotected material provide materially less restrictive approaches.

**Limits and questions not reached:** Vagueness contributes to the First Amendment holding; no separate Fifth Amendment ruling is made. The decision establishes no uniform classification of all Internet services and does not invalidate every age screen or every future control.

**Treatment of earlier authority:** Sable, Butler and Bolger preserve adult speech despite child-protection interests. Ginsberg's confined commercial rule for material obscene for minors is distinguished. Pacifica retains its broadcast setting; ACT/Pacifica preserves independent protection of adult access. Turner's content-neutral carriage setting is distinguished. Denver Area's compulsory-segregation holding supplies an adult-access analogy, without generalizing its fractured permission judgments. Broadrick supplies substantial overbreadth.

##### What textual severance and clarified preliminary relief remain permissible

**Holding:** The discrete words imposing indecency liability in §223(a)(1)(B) are severable from its obscenity prohibition. The Court affirms the clarified preliminary injunction against that indecency branch and its corresponding §223(a)(2) liability, and against §223(d)(1)–(2); the general severability provision does not authorize creating a new minor-only offense or inserting a value exception absent from the enacted text.

**Proposition-level authority:** Ginsburg's opinion of the Court, joined by Stone-Zsela, Stevens, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas and Breyer; eight direct votes. O'Connor joins parts of the judgment only.

**Limits and questions not reached:** No minor-only offense or serious-value exception is judicially added. The Court reserves a blanket prohibition for seventeen-year-olds, parental approval, extraterritorial reach and future effective nonexclusionary age assurance. Independently lawful obscenity, child sexual-abuse material, threats, fraud, unlawful solicitation and direct harmful-contact laws retain their own predicates.

**Operative remedy or transition:** The clarified preliminary injunction is affirmed against indecency under §223(a)(1)(B) and corresponding §223(a)(2) liability, and against §223(d)(1)–(2). Section (a)(2) remains enforceable for (a)(1)(A), (C), (D), (E), and obscenity or child pornography under (a)(1)(B). Section (d) has no separate textual obscenity branch and receives no such carve-out. The relief remains preliminary; no permanent decree or new monetary award is entered.

**Material judgment:** Transmission and corresponding facility relief: 8–1, O'Connor opposing only the minor-only transmission applications. Display relief and preservation of severable independent law: unanimous.

### City of Erie v. Pap's A.M. — 2000-03-29

[Record](../records/city-of-erie-v-paps-am_merits_2000-03-29.md)

#### Event and question

City of Erie v. Pap's A.M., No. 98-1161. Merits decision, March 29, 2000. Argued November 10, 1999. On writ of certiorari to the Supreme Court of Pennsylvania, reviewing its October 21, 1998 judgment, 719 A.2d 273. The grant concerns the application of Erie's minimal-clothing requirement to adult-entertainment dancers and the threshold question whether the Court can reach that federal dispute after Pap's ceased operating Kandyland.

Erie enacted Ordinance 75-1994 on September 28, 1994. Section 1(c) prohibits knowingly or intentionally appearing nude in a public place. Section 2 defines nudity through specified insufficiently covered anatomy and devices or coverings that simulate specified anatomy; those distinct terms are reproduced below. Section 3 extends public place to outdoor and enclosed venues, including adult or membership clubs, theaters and invited events with or without admission charges. Section 4 excepts children under ten and an individual exposing a breast while breastfeeding an infant under two. Section 6 directs the least constitutional infringement consistent with the ordinance's purposes and requires severance when a court finds a portion, application or enforcement excessively restrictive of the specified rights. It contains no enacted exception for expression judged artistically superior.

Pap's operated Kandyland, a commercial establishment with nude female dancing and no alcohol sales, though patrons could bring alcohol. The Pennsylvania majority invalidated the nudity restriction under the First Amendment and severed sections 1(c) and 2, leaving the separate prohibitions on sexual intercourse, deviate sexual intercourse and genital fondling intact. The majority did not decide the Pennsylvania Constitution, substantial overbreadth or the objection to standing to assert overbreadth. A two-Justice state constitutional concurrence did not provide the majority judgment's independent ground.

The submitted record includes the Council video, the parties' agreed facts and the City's repeated litigation interpretation that theatrical expression such as Equus, Hair and Oh! Calcutta! could receive different treatment as higher protected expression through section 6 and the preamble. Counsel attributed that interpretation to the City and Council, acknowledged its subjectivity, and sought judicial narrowing; the trial court did not adopt a binding theatrical exemption. The parties agreed that Equus featured frontal nudity for several weeks in October and November 1994 without enforcement efforts during a run in which the prohibition became effective. They deleted the proposed characterization of the production's artistic and social merit. The admitted episode does not establish the sole reason for nonenforcement, a complete enforcement policy or official awareness of every performance. The early temporary restraint and differing effective-date descriptions prevent treating every day of the run as an available enforcement opportunity.

Pap's closed Kandyland and sold the property in 1998. Its June 1999 representation disclaimed an interest in the premises or another nude-dancing business; a September affidavit confirmed cessation and the owner's retirement. Continued incorporation establishes no plan to reopen. No surviving damages claim, citation, penalty, substitute claimant or fee-based merits controversy is established.

#### 1. The existing state judgment presents a live federal controversy

**Holding and operative rule:** In this state-review posture, the judgment's continuing withdrawal of the City's enforcement authority, coupled with the respondent's continuing adverse defense of that judgment, supplies a live federal controversy despite Pap's cessation of the regulated business. This rule depends on the actual legal disability and adversity; abstract governmental interest, incorporation, fees or an imagined return to business does not suffice.

**Authority:** Stevens's Part I, joined by O'Connor, Kennedy, Souter, Ginsburg and Breyer: six direct rationale votes. Stone-Zsela separately votes to reach the merits without joining this rationale; Scalia and Thomas dissent.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that this state-review controversy remains live because the judgment presently withdraws the City's enforcement authority and the respondent continues to defend that concrete alteration of legal rights. ASARCO permits the party first invoking federal jurisdiction to establish its own injury from the state judgment; it does not dispense with a case or controversy. Here reversal would change the City's actual legal disability, rather than answer an abstract question about desirable legislation.

Pap's closure, sale and retirement are credited. Continued incorporation is not proof of future dancing, and no manipulation, prospective reopening or surviving monetary claim supplies jurisdiction. The objection that only one party retains a business interest is substantial, but the operative state judgment and continued adverse defense preserve this particular dispute over municipal authority. Arizonans for Official English involved an expired individual federal claim and supplies no automatic answer erasing this distinct state-review posture. Laidlaw's defendant-cessation burden is not transferred to a plaintiff leaving its business. The Court recognizes no general exception for public-law litigation or fee disputes; without the actual legal disability and continuing adversity, abstract importance would be insufficient.

**Precedent treatment:** ASARCO supplies the state-review injury principle while retaining the case-or-controversy requirement. Arizonans's actual-expiration rule and Laidlaw's defendant-cessation doctrine retain their distinct predicates.

#### 2. Communicative targeting cannot receive incidental-burden treatment

**Holding and operative rule:** A public-nudity ordinance cannot receive incidental-burden treatment when its justification and operation principally target the communicative adult-entertainment setting; Erie's record does not satisfy the scrutiny that follows. The granted application therefore cannot be sustained, while genuinely general nudity rules and evidence-based regulation of nonexpressive harms remain available.

**Authority:** Stevens, Scalia, Thomas and Ginsburg in Part II, together with Stone-Zsela's express agreement in his concurrence, directly support this precise rule, its application and its stated boundary: five Justices across writings. Stone joins no portion of the lead. This is direct proposition-level agreement, not a Marks inference or five votes for every Part II sentence.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that a general public-nudity label cannot give incidental-burden treatment to an application whose justification and operation principally target the communicative adult-entertainment setting. O'Brien requires an interest unrelated to suppressing expression before its incidental-burden analysis can sustain the restriction. The content-neutrality inquiry concerns what the government actually regulates and why, not only the breadth of its words.

Erie's defense of this application through harms attributed to the message-bearing setting does not satisfy that requirement or the scrutiny that follows. A modest clothing requirement does not by itself cure this defect. The rule leaves genuinely general nudity laws and supported regulation of crime, obstruction, sanitation, zoning or other nonexpressive harms available. It neither establishes that every adult-business effect is communicative nor resolves a different properly justified regulation. The more detailed evidentiary account and strict-scrutiny analysis in Part II have four complete joins; the independent intermediate-scrutiny alternative has three. Neither is attributed in full to the five supporters of this common rule.

**Precedent treatment:** O'Brien's nonsuppression requirement is applied rather than displaced. Existing content-neutrality doctrine continues to test operation and justification. No categorical invalidity of nudity rules, new theater exemption or independently controlling O'Brien record-insufficiency holding follows.

#### Scope of the affirmance

Affirmance leaves the Pennsylvania judgment, including its existing severance of sections 1(c) and 2, intact. The common as-applied reason does not replace that state remedy with a newly narrowed federal decree or restore other applications of the severed provisions. The Court does not decide the Pennsylvania constitutional or preserved overbreadth grounds, every simulated-anatomy application or unpresented theatrical claims.

#### Precedent treatment

- **ASARCO Inc. v. Kadish, 490 U.S. 605 (1989):** Applied in Part I to concrete injury from a state judgment when the injured party first invokes federal review; the continuing case-or-controversy requirement remains. The threshold dissent distinguishes original standing from subsequent mootness.
- **Arizonans for Official English v. Arizona, March 3, 1997:** Its requirement of a continuing personal controversy remains; Part I distinguishes its expired federal claim from this existing state judgment. Its corrective treatment of a federal judgment does not authorize automatic vacatur of a state judgment.
- **Friends of the Earth, Inc. v. Laidlaw Environmental Services (TOC), Inc., January 12, 2000:** Its injury, redressability and defendant-cessation predicates remain; plaintiff closure does not automatically invoke that cessation burden or create damages here.
- **U.S. Bancorp Mortgage Co. v. Bonner Mall Partnership, 513 U.S. 18 (1994):** Its equitable approach supplies no automatic right or power to erase a state judgment upon dismissal of federal review.
- **United States v. O'Brien, 391 U.S. 367 (1968):** Its requirement that the asserted interest be unrelated to expressive suppression governs the common merits holding. Its distinct advancement-and-fit inquiry remains available for genuinely incidental regulation; Part III's adverse application has three votes only.
- **Turner Broadcasting System, Inc. v. FCC, June 27, 1994:** The existing distinction between actual content selection and benign purpose remains. Part II applies that principle in its fuller account; the congressional predictive-deference rule and four-Justice present-record application are not transplanted wholesale.
- **Ino Ino, Inc. v. City of Bellevue, September 28, 1998:** Its supported physical-control holding and its separate protection of visible nonobscene expression retain their actual coalitions and limits. Part II's four Associates apply the distinction between operational controls and suppression of communication; Stone's Erie concurrence does not acquire a join in Ino Ino's separate visibility holding.
- **Barnes v. Glen Theatre, Inc., 501 U.S. 560 (1991):** Its judgment and distinct rationales remain; no single rationale is made binding through unshown logical nesting. The writings here disagree over its application to this record, and no general prohibition on public nudity is invalidated.
- **City of Renton v. Playtime Theatres, Inc., 475 U.S. 41 (1986):** Relevant noncommunicative effects and reasonably relevant outside evidence remain permissible; a crime label does not itself establish fit or convert objection to a message into a noncommunicative harm.
- **Ward v. Rock Against Racism, 491 U.S. 781 (1989):** Advancement and adequate fit remain distinct from a least-restrictive-means requirement. The several intermediate-scrutiny writings preserve that distinction, without a majority adopting Part III's present insufficiency ground.
- **Edenfield v. Fane, April 26, 1993; Turner Broadcasting System, Inc. v. FCC, March 31, 1997; Nixon v. Shrink Missouri Government PAC, January 24, 2000:** Their actual justification and evidence principles inform the separate evidentiary positions. None establishes a universal local-study requirement, shifts the government's burden to the challenger or decides this clothing requirement's efficacy in advance.
- **Forsyth County v. Nationalist Movement, June 19, 1992:** Its audience-hostility principle remains; it does not equate every adult-business consequence with persuasion. No precedent is overruled.

#### Resulting law and limits

Effective March 29, 2000, the state-review threshold rule rests on six rationale votes. The merits establish the five-Justice rule against incidental-burden treatment when a public-nudity ordinance's justification and operation principally target the communicative adult-entertainment setting, with Erie failing the resulting scrutiny. The judgment leaves Pennsylvania's existing severance of sections 1(c) and 2 intact and the three other public-indecency prohibitions undisturbed.

There is no controlling independent finding that this record fails intermediate scrutiny, no blanket invalidation of general public-nudity laws, no constitutional entitlement to every form of adult entertainment and no new evidentiary or hearing duty. The full Part II account has four joins; Part III has three. Existing rules for genuinely general conduct regulation and supported control of nonexpressive harms remain. State constitutional law, substantial overbreadth, other simulated-anatomy applications and the claims of absent theatrical producers are not adjudicated.

Refusal of an erotic-message stipulation is not waiver of expression. Anticipated theatrical chill does not establish a canceled production or a claim by a theater party. There is no majority complete rationale for (a) or (c), and neither plurality is adopted as a controlling narrowest opinion. Agreement on those component judgments does not combine the distinct grounds into a single framework.

#### Controlling Propositions and Authority

##### 1. Section 10(a): particular permission upheld; divided sufficient grounds

**Judgment without controlling rationale:** By six votes, the Court rejects the facial challenge to §10(a) and retains its permission after (b)'s removal. It permits an operator to enforce prospectively a written and published policy prohibiting programming the operator reasonably believes describes or depicts sexual or excretory activities or organs in a patently offensive manner measured by contemporary community standards; the FCC specifies standards for the cable medium. It permits, rather than requires, prohibition. The judgment has the six supporters identified above, but Part II's sufficient rationale has only Stevens, O'Connor, Souter and Breyer; Scalia and Thomas supply the distinct ground stated below.

**Plurality explanation:** The four regard this confined permission as a permissible accommodation of child protection and genuine private editorial choice. ACT requires review of necessity and means despite the importance of protecting children; Turner and Hurley require taking actual editorial interests seriously. Unlike a command barring a willing operator's transmission, (a) leaves that operator free to carry protected programming. Prospectivity, publication, a written policy and reasonable belief constrain the permission. Removing (b) removes the compulsory alternative challenged as pressure to ban, without deciding that every cost incentive disappears. Congress need not freeze every prior statutory access allowance, but cannot avoid First Amendment review merely by calling a content-selected change private choice. The operative judgment does not create general immunity for a particular policy or an actual governmental facility.

Scalia and Thomas instead find no freestanding constitutional entitlement to compel an unwilling private editor to carry another's expression. They reject contextual balancing as the sufficient ground; their opinion is summarized below. The (a) dissenters find the statutory content-defined veto insufficiently tailored even as a permission.

The definition covers protected nonobscene indecency; it is not Miller's complete obscenity test and contains no categorical exemption for serious literary, artistic, political or scientific value. Context remains relevant. The judgment grants no separate relief on the definition-based theories, but supplies no additional majority rule universally validating indecency definitions, certification requirements or private policies.

##### 2. Section 10(b): the integral compulsory design violates the First Amendment

**Holding and authority:** When covered leased-access programming is not voluntarily prohibited under (a), government may not impose this combined identification/notification, channel-segregation, default-blocking and advance written-election system. Its cumulative burdens on willing speakers and adult recipients are not adequately justified by the child-protection and parental-assistance interests offered for this design. Souter's Part III commands seven votes: Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Kennedy, Souter, Ginsburg and Breyer.

The statute directs FCC rules within 120 days of enactment and requires operators to comply. Programmers identify and notify operators of covered indecency; the operator concentrates it on a designated blocked channel. Access requires a subscriber's written request, with the FCC's statement that the subscriber is eighteen or older. The operator must fulfill the request within thirty days; the rule does not require a thirty-day wait. An additional blocked channel may be used if the first lacks capacity. The government's description of the FCC accommodation for systems without premium or pay-per-view channels permits an operator-installed lockbox, with the operator retaining its key or code until the subscriber requests unblocking. That mechanism is distinct from a parent-controlled household device and supplies no numerical eligibility rule here.

**Controlling explanation:** Protecting children and assisting parents are compelling interests. ACT nevertheless requires independent examination of necessity and means; Sable preserves adults' access to protected expression, and Turner prevents automatic transfer of broadcast scarcity rules to cable. Here willing speakers and willing operators must identify and regroup covered programming, while every adult begins without access and must make a written election for the segregated channel. This combination creates substantial delay, deterrence and channel-wide burdens even though actual fulfillment may occur before thirty days. It is not merely an age check or an ordinary subscription request. Mixed, unpredictable programming makes parental supervision difficult, but that difficulty does not justify this compulsory system's full burden. Existing requested household controls provide a materially different way to assist parents; their relevance does not require a finding of perfect protection, universal affordability or actual uptake. The operator-retained lockbox accommodation preserves default denial and written opt-in and thus does not cure the constitutional defect. Parents can still employ household controls after requesting access; the holding does not assume otherwise. The enacted 1996 provisions provide separate legal comparisons, not proof that particular equipment works everywhere. The required fit is lacking even accepting the practical concerns advanced for the law; no absence of all supporting evidence, actual public registry or universal technical alternative is found.

The integral arrangement and its corresponding rules must be removed. The Court does not rewrite it into a different channel system or separately hold every notification, age screen, voluntary request or blocking device unconstitutional. Its sufficient ground makes a further general ruling on vagueness, prior restraint or certification unnecessary.

##### 3. Section 10(c): protected-indecency authority invalidated; reasons remain divided

**Judgment without controlling rationale:** Six Justices reverse approval of §10(c) and its implementing rule insofar as they authorize suppression of protected nonobscene indecency on PEG access capacity. The statute directed the FCC within 180 days to enable operators to prohibit obscenity, sexually explicit conduct and material soliciting or promoting unlawful conduct. The FCC reads sexually explicit conduct through its indecency definition and the unlawful-conduct category as otherwise proscribed material; its rules permit an operator to obtain certification that programming lacks prohibited content. The provision directs enabling regulations, not a command that operators censor. It contains neither (a)'s written/published-policy and reasonable-belief limits nor (b)'s compulsory subscriber mechanism.

Souter's Part IV, joined by Stevens and Breyer, and Kennedy's separate opinion, joined by Stone-Zsela and Ginsburg, each support final protected-indecency relief. No majority adopts either sufficient framework. The decision does not independently adjudicate the unlawful-solicitation category, invalidate lawful obscenity controls or decide a freestanding certification claim.

**Plurality explanation:** Stevens, Souter and Breyer find the additional federal veto insufficiently fitted to its asserted purposes in these access arrangements. Locally arranged access expression is not established as the operator's own presentation merely because the operator owns the facilities. Hurley protects an actual private expressive presentation, and the contextual attribution authorities do not supply one by title alone. The federal permission lacks (a)'s confined policy architecture and can exclude protected speech from adults altogether. Child protection remains compelling, but independent means review and the distinct local/parental arrangements do not support this additional unqualified protected-indecency authority. The statutory defect can be decided without classifying every local institution as a public forum, finding all operators governmental or ordering a further investigation of every franchise. A concrete private-editor claim remains possible on its own facts.

**Separate sufficient ground:** Stone-Zsela, Kennedy and Ginsburg apply strict scrutiny to Congress's content-defined withdrawal of access protection. The challenged governmental act is the enactment itself, which permits exclusion of protected access expression; the claim does not require attributing every later operator choice to the State. Turner supplies the content-selection inquiry and ACT requires justification of necessity and means. Child protection is compelling, but total discretionary exclusion of adults' protected expression is not narrowly tailored merely because the operator could choose to carry it. Household choice and narrower safeguards matter; an assumed compelled-message injury does not establish that all independent access programming is the operator's own speech. Actual government dedication may support a forum rationale in a concrete case, but no universal PEG classification is necessary. This is the same sufficient ground on which these three would also invalidate (a), with its own terms considered.

##### 4. Severability and the scope of relief

**Holding and authority:** Section 10(a) remains independently operative after (b)'s removal. Souter's V-A commands six votes: Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Souter, Thomas and Breyer. The seven invalidating (b) remove its integral compulsory machinery; the six invalidating protected-indecency authority under (c) require corresponding implementing relief. All nine preserve the decision's boundaries as to other enactments and unpresented claims.

**Controlling explanation:** Alaska Airlines requires attention to independent operation and congressional intent. Section 10(a)'s prospective permission can operate without compulsory segregation and default blocking; retaining that choice still serves Congress's identified accommodation and child-protection purposes. The defect in (b) therefore does not itself require destruction of (a). The seven rejecting (b), however, do not choose a new combination of its interdependent mandatory steps for Congress. The limited (c) disposition reaches the protected-indecency authorization, leaving lawful obscenity and otherwise-unprotected or unlawful-material controls outside that invalidation. Independent 1996 permissions are different enactments and are not decided through review of the 1992 rules. Setting aside an invalid 1992 authority alone therefore neither establishes an unconditional carriage right nor decides a future claim involving a genuinely private presentation, governmental facility or enforceable local obligation.

#### Precedent Treatment

- **ACT/Pacifica (March 4, 1996):** The Court applies its protection of nonobscene indecency and independent necessity/means review to the compulsory (b) arrangement. Its broadcast timetable disposition is not a cable rule. The divided permission opinions preserve the governing protection while disagreeing on application.
- **FCC v. Pacifica Foundation:** Its narrow, contextual broadcast holding remains; it does not automatically sustain every cable indecency restriction or establish that the present protected speech is of lower constitutional value.
- **Sable Communications v. FCC:** The (b) majority applies the distinction between compelling protection of children and adequately justified restrictions on adults. Scalia's separate willing-outlet analysis concerns the permissions and is not the Court's sufficient (b) ground.
- **Turner Broadcasting:** Cable editorial interests retain ordinary speech protection. The prior content-neutral full-power must-carry classification and intermediate standard do not govern an expressly content-selected indecency scheme. Neither the earlier limited shared predictive-review principle nor its fractured record-insufficiency discussion requires remand in every cable case.
- **Hurley:** The protection of an actual private expressive presentation remains intact. The component opinions disagree about the legal consequences of access duties; none establishes that ownership alone makes every independent access program the operator's message.
- **Perry and Cornelius:** Their attention to the actual forum and government's arrangement remains. The Court creates no universal cable-access forum; Kennedy's conditional forum ground does not become such a holding.
- **Rosenberger, Pinette and Chabad:** Their program-specific viewpoint, attribution and contextual rules are not converted into a presumption that every regulated channel is a public forum or governmental speaker. The divided opinions' use of those limits creates no new general forum or attribution test.
- **Blum, Jackson and Lugar; Reitman and Crawford:** The distinction between a private act and governmental alteration or encouragement remains relevant. No categorical operator immunity or rule making every private refusal government action is adopted.
- **Alaska Airlines:** The Court applies independent operation and congressional intent in retaining (a) after invalidating (b).

No earlier decision is overruled. The separate opinions' broader grounds do not independently alter controlling law.

#### Current Law

The challenged 1992 §10(a) permission remains available with its own prospective, written/published-policy, reasonable-belief and content terms. Its retention does not establish a majority complete theory governing all statutory carriage permissions. Section 10(b)'s integral mandatory arrangement cannot stand. Section 10(c)'s authority to suppress protected nonobscene indecency and the corresponding implementing rule cannot stand, although no unified sufficient constitutional rationale commands five votes. Lower courts must implement these provision-specific dispositions without treating either plurality or a separate opinion as a complete majority framework for other disputes.

Independent 1996 law remains distinct:

- **Section 504, 47 U.S.C. §560:** On a cable-service subscriber's request, the operator must, without charge, fully scramble or otherwise fully block the audio and video programming of each covered channel so a nonsubscriber does not receive it. The provision has no independent sexual-content or indecency trigger. Scrambling rearranges the signal so the programming cannot be viewed or heard understandably.
- **Section 505, 47 U.S.C. §561:** For channels primarily dedicated to sexually oriented programming, a multichannel video programming distributor must fully scramble or block sexually explicit adult programming or other indecent programming, in audio and video, to prevent nonsubscriber receipt. Until compliance, it must withhold that programming during hours the FCC determines a significant number of children are likely to view it. The same understandable-signal definition applies. Its amendment took effect March 9, 1996; no actual hours, compliance or technical capability is established by this decision.
- **Section 506(a):** Independently permits refusal of public-access programs or portions containing obscenity, indecency or nudity. Its public-access language does not automatically encompass all educational and governmental capacity.
- **Section 506(b):** Supplies the separate leased-access permission for those categories. Neither subsection commands refusal or imports the 1992 written-policy, reasonable-belief or subscriber-request conditions. Both operate from February 8, 1996. Their constitutionality and a particular exercise are not decided or approved here; their independent authority is not automatically eliminated.
- **Act §561, a different provision from 47 U.S.C. §561:** A civil facial challenge to Title V, its amendments or provisions requires a three-judge district court under 28 U.S.C. §2284. An interlocutory or final judgment, decree or order holding a covered provision unconstitutional is appealable as of right directly to this Court within twenty days of entry. That route is not a general rule for all communications litigation and does not enlarge this review of the 1992 provisions.

Congress may enact constitutionally tailored child protections, including parental-control and subscriber-choice measures. Operators retain genuine editorial rights; lawful obscenity prohibitions remain. No unconditional right to carriage, automatic repeal, mootness ruling, universal public forum or general permission to suppress protected advocacy follows.

#### Separate Positions Relevant to the Decision

**Kennedy, joined by Stone-Zsela and Ginsburg, concurring in the judgment on (c) and dissenting on (a):** Congress itself selects protected content for loss of access protection. Legislative action is reviewable even when every resulting private refusal is not governmental. Turner supplies ordinary cable protection and the content inquiry; ACT and Sable require adequate means as well as a compelling purpose. A permission can deny willing adults access altogether and does not become tailored merely because a private operator chooses whether to use it. The policy safeguards in (a) do not cure that underlying selective veto; (c) does not even contain those same limits. Neither popular offensiveness nor moral disapproval alone supplies a sufficient governmental justification, and the protection extends to unpopular expression without ideological preference.

These Justices recognize Hurley's genuine private-presentation right, but reject assuming that every independently programmed access channel carries the operator's own message. A locally dedicated channel open to private speakers may support a forum analysis under Perry and Cornelius; their opinion does not find that dedication in every franchise, declare all PEG governmental or authorize local managers to censor protected expression at will. They join the Court's (b) explanation because the combined compulsory burdens fail the required means review. They reserve §506(a) and (b), lawful obscenity prohibitions, distinct unlawful-conduct questions and concrete editorial claims. They seek no unconditional carriage order. Their strict sufficient framework for (a)/(c) has three votes.

**O'Connor, dissenting on (c):** She accepts constitutional scrutiny of Congress's permissive law, but finds its accommodation of genuine independent editorial choice and protection of children sufficient against this facial challenge. Turner and Hurley give that editorial interest substantial weight. The permission does not itself order an unwilling operator to exclude speech, and the different origins of leased and locally arranged access do not, for her, justify the different constitutional result. She recognizes that (c) lacks (a)'s written/published-policy and reasonable-belief conditions; she does not silently supply them. Their absence does not persuade her that all applications of this uncoerced permission must fail. Actual governmental institutions, an established local obligation or coercive administration may present different claims.

Her position differs from Scalia and Thomas's independent private-editor ground: private choice does not end review of Congress's content selection. It also differs from her vote on (b), where willing communication encounters the government's integral compulsory structure. An ordinary age screen or subscription request alone is not condemned, but the operator-retained lockbox accommodation leaves that structure intact. She joins the Court's (b) invalidity and the four-Justice contextual (a) rationale, without joining a universal cable forum or a categorical absence of governmental action.

**Scalia, joined by Thomas, concurring in the judgment on (a) and dissenting on (b)/(c):** Their permission analysis starts with the difference between a protected speaker's ability to seek a willing outlet and a constitutional right to force an unwilling private editor to carry the message. Scalia's Sable concurrence, their Turner positions and Hurley support that distinction. Congress's partial relaxation of imposed access duties is not itself a direct governmental ban on the programmer's speech. Statutory designation alone does not constitutionalize a forum or every former access entitlement. They reserve an actually governmental institution and independently established obligations without finding that any particular franchise lacks them; they decide neither the entire access regime's constitutionality nor a taking. This is a merits ground, not a denial of Article III standing. They do not join the plurality's contextual balancing as the sufficient reason to sustain (a) or (c).

They separately apply strict scrutiny to (b), which burdens transmission by a willing operator. Mixed and unpredictable schedules make ordinary subscriber-initiated blocking a less effective default protection; segregation leaves the other basic channels available while preserving a real adult election. A written request can reduce requests made by children without authorization, and thirty days is a compliance maximum, not a compulsory waiting period. Household controls remain usable after opt-in. They find that design sufficiently fitted despite its adult-access burdens, rather than requiring government to accept a materially weaker parental-assistance mechanism. They neither require a flawless alternative nor presume an official list, universal affordability, actual disclosure or implementation of the 1996 blocking laws. Thomas distinguishes ACT's mandatory broadcast-hours exclusion from the present adult election; Scalia's practical ACT concerns do not lower the governing standard. Their strict-(b) analysis has two votes.

### Reno v. American Civil Liberties Union, No. 96-511

Merits decision, June 26, 1997

#### Chronology and Posture

Argued March 19, 1997; decided June 26, 1997. On direct appeal from a three-judge District Court for the Eastern District of Pennsylvania under §561 of the Telecommunications Act of 1996. The question is whether the CDA's indecency and patently-offensive restrictions are facially invalid under the First Amendment because they are content based, vague, substantially overbroad and insufficiently tailored to protecting minors.

The Act was enacted February 8, 1996. The district court's preliminary-injunction order was dated June 11 and entered June 12, 1996; its June 28 clarification preserved June 12 as the appeal-clock entry date. The plaintiffs demonstrated present speech restrictions and practical compliance burdens. The record describes diverse lawful Internet expression and age-verification costs and barriers, especially for noncommercial and small speakers.

Section 223(a)(1)(B) reaches knowing creation or solicitation and initiation of transmission by a telecommunications device of an obscene or indecent communication, knowing its recipient is under eighteen, regardless of who placed the call or initiated the communication. The enacted definition of telecommunications device excludes an interactive computer service. Section (d)(1)(A) separately reaches knowing use of an interactive computer service to send to specified persons under eighteen; (d)(1)(B) reaches display available to a person under eighteen. Their content category covers contextual depictions or descriptions of sexual or excretory activities or organs in terms patently offensive under contemporary community standards. The corresponding facility offenses require knowing permission and intent that the facility be used for the prohibited activity. Violations carry fines, imprisonment of up to two years, or both.

Section (e)(1) protects mere access or connection to a facility, system or network outside the provider's control, including incidental functions that do not create content. Section (e)(2) denies that defense to a conspirator with an entity actively creating or knowingly distributing prohibited communications, or one knowingly advertising them; the conspirator need not personally create content. Section (e)(3) separately denies it for access to an offending network the provider owns or controls. Employer liability under (e)(4) requires within-scope employee or agent conduct plus knowing authorization or ratification, or reckless disregard. Section (e)(5) provides alternative defenses: good-faith reasonable, effective and appropriate measures under the circumstances to restrict minors' access, or restricted access requiring a verified credit card, debit account, adult access code or adult personal identification number. Under (e)(6), the FCC may describe suitable measures; it may not approve, sanction, compel or enforce their use or endorse particular products. Their use is evidence of good faith. Interactive services do not thereby become common or telecommunications carriers.

Section (f)(1) protects lawful good-faith implementation of defenses or access restrictions against suit. Section (f)(2) bars inconsistent state or local liability for the identified commercial, library and higher-education activities, while allowing complementary regulation confined to intrastate services without inconsistent interstate duties and preserving regulation of conduct outside this section. Section (g) preserves other federal laws. Section (h)'s device definition adds no broadcast-licensee or cable-operator obligations under other indecency provisions; its access-software definition covers content-neutral filtering, selection and transmission tools that do not create or provide the communication's content. These distinct savings provisions supply no absent narrow content definition.

All nine Justices participated at argument and decision.

#### Controlling Propositions and Authority

**Authority for both holdings:** The eight-Justice opinion of the Court identified above directly adopts the following propositions, explanations, limits and remedy. O'Connor's agreement with part of the judgment does not join this opinion.

##### I. The challenged content restrictions violate the First Amendment

**Holding and operative rule:** A content-based criminal restriction on expression through a general-purpose communications network is facially invalid when uncertain and substantially overbroad categories suppress substantial protected adult speech and the Government fails to establish narrow tailoring in light of practical, materially less restrictive means of limiting minors' access. On this Internet record, §223(a)(1)(B)'s indecency prohibition and §223(d)(1)'s patently-offensive transmission and display prohibitions, together with their corresponding facility-permitting offenses, fail that requirement.

**Controlling explanation:** Sable recognizes the compelling interest in protecting children but forbids reducing adults to children's speech when less restrictive means are available. Ginsberg sustained a confined commercial restriction on material obscene for minors, including a value limitation absent here. Pacifica's scarcity, intrusiveness and broadcast setting do not describe this medium; ACT/Pacifica requires independent protection of adult access even there. Turner concerns content-neutral carriage, not this direct selection of prohibited content. Denver Area's compulsory-blocking holding supplies the practical adult-access analogy, without extending its fractured permission judgments into a universal rule.

The criminal categories reach serious discussion of health, sexuality, art, politics and personal experience. Uncertainty about content and community standards chills speech; scienter cannot tell a speaker which protected discussion is criminal. Knowledge of a minor or reference to specified recipients does not prevent the prohibition from reaching conversations also intended for adults. Other communication channels do not cure exclusion from this one.

The defenses do not remove those burdens. Many small and noncommercial speakers lack feasible age verification; costs, loss of anonymity and exclusion of lawful adults persist. Proposed tagging does not establish the effective protection the defense requires on the present record. User-controlled filtering and precise regulation of unprotected material offer materially less restrictive approaches. The Government has not justified this broad criminal suppression in light of those alternatives. The Court does not find every age screen invalid or all future controls ineffective. Vagueness contributes to the First Amendment holding; no independent Fifth Amendment holding is needed.

##### II. Textual severance preserves obscenity law; judicial rewriting cannot save the remaining restrictions

**Holding and operative rule:** The discrete words imposing indecency liability in §223(a)(1)(B) are severable from its obscenity prohibition. The Court affirms the clarified preliminary injunction against that indecency branch and its corresponding §223(a)(2) liability, and against §223(d)(1)–(2); the general severability provision does not authorize creating a new minor-only offense or inserting a value exception absent from the enacted text.

**Controlling explanation:** The word choice permits removal of the indecency branch without disabling the distinct obscenity rule. The proposed further construction would instead change the offenses' substantive coverage. Knowledge and audience limits do not provide the missing narrow content definition, and a serious-value or harmful-to-minors exception cannot be supplied by judicial legislation. The statutory defenses preserve their own terms but do not establish workable protection for the record's diverse speakers. The challenge's varied activities and substantial protected reach make a selective judicial reconstruction inappropriate. O'Connor would retain knowing transmissions solely to minors; the Court does not adopt her conclusion that minors' protected-speech overbreadth is insubstantial or her proposed application severance.

The ruling does not hold that Congress can never regulate protected Internet indecency, decide the proposed blanket ban on such messages to seventeen-year-olds or the role of parental approval, or resolve the separate question of extraterritorial efficacy and scope. It does not require one constitutional classification for every Internet service. Independently lawful obscenity, child sexual-abuse material, true-threat, fraud, unlawful-solicitation and direct-harmful-contact provisions remain governed by their own predicates; they do not save these overbroad restrictions. A future precisely drawn rule using effective, nonexclusionary age assurance remains open.

#### Precedent Treatment

- Sable, Butler and Bolger preserve adult access despite the compelling interest in protecting minors; child protection does not alone justify suppressing adult expression.
- Ginsberg's commercial restriction on material obscene for minors is distinguished because the CDA reaches broader protected categories without its value limitation.
- Pacifica retains its contextual broadcast force and supplies no reduced Internet protection.
- ACT/Pacifica's independent review of content-selected restrictions and adult access remains intact; the Internet record is separately examined.
- Turner retains the distinction between content-neutral carriage and content discrimination, with independent scrutiny of the evidentiary fit.
- Denver Area's compulsory-segregation holding is applied by analogy to practical adult-access burdens; its separate permission judgments create no universal Internet rule.
- Broadrick supplies the substantial-overbreadth framework; the enacted breadth cannot be repaired by an unavailable narrowing construction. No precedent is overruled.

#### Law After Decision

The challenged indecency and patently-offensive restrictions cannot be enforced at the clarified scope. Content-based criminal regulation of this medium must protect lawful adult expression and satisfy narrow tailoring on the actual record. Obscenity and other independently unprotected conduct retain their own legal treatment. Future, precisely drawn regulation and genuinely effective nonexclusionary age controls remain open; no general Internet-service classification or freestanding Fifth Amendment rule is adopted.

#### Separate Positions Relevant to the Decision

**O'Connor, concurring in the judgment in part and dissenting in part.** Butler, Sable and Bolger protect adult access; Ginsberg permits genuine restrictions confined to minors. The Internet presently lacks generally usable identity gates, so the display offense and mixed adult/minor conversations cannot be saved by treating the network as divided into adult and child zones. She agrees with the injunction to that extent and with preservation of independent obscenity enforcement.

She would retain the transmission provisions only where the initiating adult knows all recipients are minors, with corresponding facility liability confined to those underlying communications. She construes §223(d)(1)(A) to require knowledge of minority, relying on its wording and DeBartolo's constitutional-avoidance principle. Such a communication has no other adult audience whose access is lost. Brockett's application-severability principle and the statutory severability provision support preserving that application. Although the CDA's content categories are broader than Ginsberg's obscenity-for-minors rule, she finds no showing under Broadrick that the additional protected minor speech is substantial relative to the provisions' legitimate sweep. She does not deny minors all First Amendment protection or foreclose individual vagueness challenges. Her view relies on current technology, not predicted improvements. No other Justice joins her separate writing.

## Event

North Carolina Right to Life, Inc. v. Bartlett, review of 168 F.3d 705. Plenary merits decision, May 1, 2000. Certiorari granted February 22, 2000; argued April 3, 2000. On review of the Fourth Circuit's February 17, 1999 judgment. The questions are whether political-committee registration, reporting and disclosure may cover an issue organization whose major purpose is not nominating or electing candidates, and whether regulated campaign expenditures may extend beyond express electoral advocacy without unconstitutional vagueness or overbreadth.

NCRL is an independent nonprofit issue organization without a major purpose of electing candidates. Its separate political action committee, NCRLPAC, contributes to and independently supports candidates. Barbara Holt supervises both and is a registered lobbyist. NCRL submitted two sample voter guides to the Elections Board. An endorsement sheet was accidentally enclosed with the first; the second omitted it. An official nevertheless advised that distributing the second guide would violate the corporate political-purpose expenditure prohibition. NCRL refrained from distribution. The State's litigation assurance of a narrower electoral reading was not a binding construction or established prosecutorial undertaking.

The Fourth Circuit affirmed facial invalidation of the political-committee definition and invalidation of the corporate political-purpose provisions. It separately upheld session-time lobbyist and employing-PAC contribution/solicitation restrictions and left a donor-identification declaration standing whose merits the State did not appeal. The two granted questions delimit this Court's review; the separate direct-corporate-gift, session and donor components are not independently adjudicated.

## Public Action

The Court affirms the judgment within the two granted questions, preserving relief against the political-committee definition and the challenged expenditure coverage of genuine independent issue advocacy.

## Holdings

#### The actual threat and cessation of speech permit this pre-enforcement challenge

**Holding and operative rule:** NCRL may obtain review of the challenged coverage before prosecution where its intended continuing speech encounters penal statutory language, an official application to its proposed guide and actual cessation, without a binding construction removing the threat. This record supplies no basis to require referral of the statutory question to the Elections Board or to abstain from the two granted questions.

**Authority:** Souter's Opinion of the Court, joined throughout by all eight other Justices. All nine adopt the three holdings at their stated scopes.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that this is an actual restraint on planned expression, not an abstract request for advice. The official's response to the endorsement-free guide and NCRL's resulting cessation connect the criminal provisions to its continuing activity. A litigation assurance supplies neither a binding limiting rule nor an established undertaking that removes that application. Maine Right to Life permits review of an actual recurring-speech controversy without compulsory resort to a merely optional advisory process. Here no supported state supreme court certification procedure or authority to certify the construction to the Board supplies the proposed alternative. The Court rejects abstention on these facts without announcing a rule against abstention in every speech dispute. Akins's separate informational-standing question does not govern speakers challenging the restrictions applied to their own expression.

#### Incidental influence on elections cannot trigger this full political-committee regime

**Holding and operative rule:** North Carolina may not impose its full political-committee registration, recordkeeping and continuing reporting regime on an issue organization lacking a major electoral purpose merely because some communications discuss candidates or incidentally influence an election. Section 163-278.6(14)'s primary-or-incidental-purpose and election-influence coverage is facially invalid for its substantial reach into genuine independent issue discussion without a determinate electoral-financing boundary.

**Authority:** The unanimous Court opinion.

**Controlling explanation:** The definition covers support or opposition to any candidate or party as either a primary or incidental purpose and separately reaches efforts to influence an election. Those terms expose ordinary policy discussion to comprehensive organizational duties backed by criminal sanctions. Buckley v. Valeo distinguishes protected independent discussion from the financing activity that may justify campaign regulation. The legitimate interest in identifying electoral financing does not itself identify which organizations or transactions this definition covers. Nor does the lesser burden of a particular report answer the continuing committee obligations imposed here.

The Court cannot cure the defect by deleting incidental-purpose coverage or the separate election-influence limb. No binding construction supplies that confinement. NCRLPAC's candidate activity cannot establish that NCRL's own issue speech is candidate financing. Clifton protects genuine candidate inquiry without immunizing proved campaign services; it reinforces the need to distinguish the activities actually regulated. Akins remains controlling on its separately defined actual-financing subject; today's decision creates no categorical immunity from genuinely covered candidate financing simply because an organization principally discusses issues. It likewise leaves sponsor disclosure, candidate and party disclosure, lawful coordinated-spending rules and committee treatment of organizations whose major purpose is electoral advocacy available on their own predicates. The present facial defect can be resolved without rewriting the State's law or ordering a new factual study.

#### The expenditure provisions lack a sufficiently definite electoral-advocacy boundary

**Holding and operative rule:** North Carolina may not prohibit genuine independent issue advocacy under §163-278.6(16)'s political-purpose language, as used by §§163-269 and 163-278.19, merely because the speech seeks to influence an election. Regulated expenditure coverage must supply a sufficiently definite electoral-advocacy boundary; the present general influence formula and nonbinding narrowing assurance do not do so.

**Authority:** The unanimous Court opinion.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that discussing candidates and affecting voters do not, without more, transform independent issue speech into a prohibited campaign expenditure. Buckley and FEC v. Massachusetts Citizens for Life preserve that boundary while recognizing materially distinct financing rules. The official's response to NCRL's second guide demonstrates the practical threat; it does not establish the guide's exact language or resolve its classification under a precise alternative test.

The State's interest in preventing evasion cannot supply restrictions absent from its broad political-purpose formula. Maine Right to Life's existing statutory holding concerns a communication assessed as a whole, only limited reference to external events, an unmistakable and unambiguous electoral portion with only one meaning, and no reasonable difference over whether it encourages election or defeat of a clearly identified candidate rather than another action. A reasonable nonelectoral interpretation excludes coverage under that construction. North Carolina's general influence formula supplies no comparable confinement. Describing that existing holding does not decide Maine's reserved constitutional vagueness question or approve every contextual rule. Today's Court neither requires a canonical list of electoral words nor decides whether the second guide satisfies a different, sufficiently definite rule. The judgment preserves protection against this coverage defect while leaving distinct contribution, coordination, sponsor-disclosure and nonprofit-status questions to their own lawful rules.

**Limits and questions not reached:** The Court does not decide the exact second guide's lawfulness under a different, sufficiently definite rule; create an organization-wide major-purpose exemption for actual candidate financing; rule on every contextual definition; import federal dollar thresholds or membership exclusions into state law; decide NCRL's direct corporate candidate-giving rights; or reopen the session restrictions and donor-identification declaration outside the grant.

## Precedent Treatment

- Buckley v. Valeo, 424 U.S. 1, supplies the distinction between genuine issue discussion and electoral financing; its protection does not require a single mandatory vocabulary, and its distinct campaign-finance disclosure holdings remain in force.
- FEC v. Massachusetts Citizens for Life, Inc., 479 U.S. 238, preserves the independent-expression boundary and its separate protection based on an organization's actual characteristics. It is not enlarged into a categorical direct-candidate-gift exemption.
- Akins v. FEC, June 25, 1997, remains governing for genuinely covered candidate financing and actual transaction predicates. The present relief does not replace that holding with the lower court's categorical organizational exemption or determine a covered NCRL transaction.
- FEC v. Maine Right to Life Committee, Inc., September 14, 1998, retains its actual-controversy holding, cumulative limited-context statutory construction and unresolved constitutional questions. No new endorsement of its disputed contextual rationale or judgment about the present guide is necessary to today's common ground.
- Clifton v. FEC, September 14, 1998, applies at its independent-inquiry boundary; real campaign services retain their own requirements, and the decision is not converted into a universal bar on fitted contact or reporting safeguards.
- Day v. Holahan and Kentucky Right to Life, Inc. v. Stengel preserve the distinction between fact-dependent nonprofit independent-spending protection and direct candidate contributions. Their different subjects are not resolved by the present issue-speech holding.
- McIntyre v. Ohio Elections Commission preserves individual anonymous leaflets without determining every organizational disclosure duty. Russell v. Burris and Nixon v. Shrink Missouri Government PAC preserve their contribution-limit rules without supplying the missing expenditure-coverage boundary here.

## Law After Decision

North Carolina's challenged political-committee definition remains facially invalid. The expenditure provisions cannot reach genuine independent issue discussion merely for its electoral influence. The Court leaves tailored financing and disclosure rules within their distinct lawful domains, recognizes no blanket exemption based on an organization's primary purpose and supplies no replacement statutory text. Akins, Maine Right to Life and the other existing coverage rules retain their stated force and limits. No precedent is overruled.

## Separate Writings

Kennedy, joined by Scalia and Thomas, concurs in the Court's opinion and judgment. North Carolina's coverage fails even under their more definite express-terms approach. Under their reading of Buckley and MCFL, the speaker must supply an electoral command. Ordinary context may identify a candidate or explain explicit words, but election proximity and other external events may not generate an electoral direction absent from the communication. They require no canonical slogan and do not forbid ordinary contextual interpretation. They continue to withhold affirmative approval of Maine Right to Life's broader contextual rationale, which the Court's common opinion accurately describes as existing law without adopting anew.

They also distinguish actual candidate cash transfers and genuine conduits from an association's own expression. An issue-oriented purpose supplies no automatic immunity for the former, but cooperation alone does not resolve the constitutional treatment of the latter. Kennedy's Akins distinction therefore remains; Scalia and Thomas's Akins standing dissent supplied no separate statutory-merits holding. None of these reservations saves the present incidental-influence definition or requires a new factual inquiry into the second guide. The concurrence creates no universal organizational-disclosure immunity and leaves the bounded affirmance intact.

## Procedure After Action

The existing declaratory and injunctive relief stands within the two reviewed questions. The other portions of the Fourth Circuit's judgment remain undisturbed at their existing scope. Ordinary mandate procedure applies. No registration, donor list, penalty, damages, new administrative proceeding, compulsory rulemaking, factual study or further return to this Court is ordered.
