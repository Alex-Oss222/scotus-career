## Express corrections to the base packet

1. **Distribution is not a measured request rate.** In the paragraph beginning “The record includes a survey,” replace for substantive use the assertion that fewer than one-half of one percent of subscribers requested blocking. Finding 21 reports that surveyed operators **distributed** adult-channel blocking devices to fewer than 0.5% of their subscribers. The survey covered 79 operators out of roughly 6,000, and 62 answered the distribution question. It did not directly measure requests received, requests refused, requests awaiting fulfillment, installation success, parents notified, or children protected. The base's later “low requests” shorthand in its invalidation-path paragraph must likewise be read as **low observed device distribution**. Note 20 separately quotes a Jones Intercable memorandum describing a minimal number of requests; that narrower company account cannot convert the survey's distribution measure into a nationwide request measure.

2. **Notice was an unresolved predicate, not a proved condition of the observation period.** The approximately fourteen-month observation described in finding 21 did not establish the success or failure of an adequately publicized request system. The conclusions expressly say that the record did not clearly establish adequate notice. Low distribution was consistent both with ineffective implementation and with little parental concern; the court did not adopt Playboy's general premise that informed parents had knowingly decided against blocking. Any comparative-effectiveness claim must retain the lower court's adequate-notice condition and its limits concerning concerned parents.

3. **Reception without subscription is different from reception without tuning.** The base's sustaining-path sentence saying nonsubscribing households receive residual pictures or sound “without choosing the channel” requires clarification. Finding 9 describes a cable subscriber who does not subscribe to the premium channel **tuning to that scrambled channel** and receiving discernible pictures or sound. The child may do so without parental knowledge or permission. The source does not find that the material appears regardless of the viewer's channel selection.

4. **The survey percentage is bounded and internally inconsistent.** The base's roughly 69% statement corresponds to finding 18, but note 14 gives 68% of surveyed operators carrying adult programming. Preserve both source figures and the survey scope; do not present a precise reconciled national proportion. Note 23's statement of unanimous choice concerns operators with incomplete scrambling technology faced with the specified economically prohibitive systemwide alternatives, not all cable operators.

5. **The notice obligation reaches operators through Playboy's contracts.** The base correctly distinguishes the notice arrangement from §504's text, but its abbreviated remedy description must retain the court's express lack of jurisdiction over operators to command notice directly. The court states that it will require Playboy, through its contractual arrangements, to ensure adequate notice. This is neither a statutory notice command nor a finding that every relevant contract or household had already received the required notice. It does not independently bind nonparty programmers.

The remaining factual framing in the base is usable only with the fuller qualifications below. Its original readiness statement does not erase these corrections or establish notice, comparative efficacy, technical performance, or economic feasibility as uncontested facts.

## Statutory duties, technology, and implementation

Findings 1, 10, and 20 and notes 2, 8, and 18 distinguish two duties. Section 505 requires full audio-and-video scrambling or blocking for the covered sexually oriented channels; until compliance, the FCC-selected time restriction applies. Section 504 requires complete blocking upon a cable subscriber's request, without charge to that subscriber, and is not confined to sexually explicit programming. The court treats the latter subscriber-selected mechanism as content neutral. Section 504 compliance does not itself satisfy §505 when both duties apply. The notice program considered by the lower court is separate from either provision's enacted commands. Statutory text in the authorized entering law controls its own exact terms and effective dates.

Findings 5–10 describe particular technologies in the record. Analog RF or baseband scrambling may leave discernible pictures or sound. In the identified arrangements, adding positive or negative traps to that scrambling prevents bleed; so do converter boxes with channel mapping and digital transmission. Channel mapping diverts a scrambled-channel selection to a promotional message or blue screen. The findings do not declare every device called a lockbox reliable in every installation. Modern television and VCR lockout or V-chip features are mentioned, but do not replace the operator's statutory obligation.

Finding 24 recites the **Government's contentions** that fulfillment may take weeks, a device may fail, and moving an adult network to another channel may defeat an existing block. These propositions are supported in the opinion by deposition citations, but are not separately resolved as universal factual findings. The opinion provides no quantified failure rate, maximum fulfillment period, automatic reconfiguration requirement, testing protocol, or replacement deadline. Its final acceptance of the notice-dependent alternative does not silently find each implementation concern nonexistent.

Note 21 rejects the proposal that mailing a negative trap eliminates installation labor: the experts agreed that those traps are installed on the outside cable or pole. The preparation summary's reference to an installed-cost assumption should be read precisely: the note says **$37 per blocking mechanism plus installation**. Finding 7(c)'s $12–$15 device price is a different amount and does not establish total installed cost. The source does not support a universally effective household self-installation remedy consisting of a mailed inexpensive device.

## Exposure and harm: findings and limits

Findings 11–12 distinguish estimates of **potential** exposure from demonstrated actual exposure. The court found that the Government had not quantified the actual number of exposed households and had presented only limited anecdotal incidents. The source neither establishes universal exposure nor finds that no children were exposed. The potential-exposure estimates did not establish how many households subscribed to adult programming, had effective system blocking, or possessed effective household controls; the court did not quantify the adjustment warranted by those objections.

Finding 11(b) says that in the listed complaint instances, except one, operators offered to correct or actually corrected the problem without charge, at varying speeds. It does not say all requests were promptly fulfilled or all reported exposure was successfully eliminated. The source also recounts a child encountering signal bleed at a friend's house despite a lockout at the parent's own home. Notes 10–11 identify the exceptional charge report and the limited anecdotal harm evidence; they do not prove a general completion or failure rate.

Findings 16–17 and the conclusions describe substantial weaknesses in the clinical and inferential evidence. Dr. Benedek's asserted harms included unpleasant feelings, possible modeling, and changes in attitudes; she linked the modeling of unblocked audio to signal bleed, while describing the postulated effects as transient in the vast majority of cases. The lower court discounted aspects of her expertise and the analogy from television violence to sexually explicit signal bleed. It also considered Dr. Green's published statement about the possible hazard of young children visually experiencing adult sexuality. Its ultimate conclusion nevertheless was that susceptible minors faced a sufficient risk of harm to warrant protection. That conclusion must accompany any description of the evidence's weakness. No current appellate legal conclusion follows from this source validation.

## The lower court's exact notice-dependent alternative

The court suggests notice that identifies the channels with sexually oriented programming; explains that partially discernible video and full audio can appear through signal bleed; warns that children can encounter it without parental knowledge or permission; states that blocking devices are free; and explains that a subscriber may telephone the operator for a device to block the offending channel.

Its examples of suitable media are billing inserts, barker or preview channels, and on-air advertisements on channels other than the sexually explicit channel. These are examples, not an instruction to use all media simultaneously. It calls for regular notice at reasonable intervals and a special mailed notice to subscribers who requested a lockbox when the operator changes the channel carrying sexually explicit programming. It specifies no number of days between notices and no automatic device replacement, installation deadline, or repair protocol.

The comparison is **§504 with adequate notice** against §505. The court says that the arrangement is effective for a parent concerned about signal bleed and would appear as effective as §505 for those concerned, while burdening protected speech less. It acknowledges reliance on parental vigilance, access by determined minors, and imperfection under either arrangement. This is a conditional judicial assessment, not empirical proof that a well-publicized fourteen-month trial had already achieved equivalent protection for all children.

The lower court's sentence about resourceful minors watching bleed “after the safe-harbour hours” must not be used to establish daytime bleed despite compliance: the same paragraph states that the time-channeling requirement prevents bleed during the hours children are likely to watch, and the source identifies the permissible transmission period as 10 p.m.–6 a.m. The text supports a limitation on perfect protection, not a new exception to the stated schedule. Likewise, the earlier categorical sentence that only subscribers are exposed must be read with the opinion's express discussion of residual bleed and its time limits; it is not a finding that all nonsubscriber exposure is technologically impossible throughout the day.

The court states that without adequate notice §504 would cease to be a viable alternative, but does not specify an automatic restoration of §505 enforcement or an enforcement procedure for a later notice failure. It preserves operators' choice to stop carrying adult programming if uneconomic and relies on programmers' incentive to make distribution feasible. That incentive is not a finding of guaranteed profitability or completed implementation.

## Lower judgment, burden statements, and remaining limits

The court treats §505 as content based, rejects the Government's secondary-effects characterization, and accepts three interests in combination as compelling: protecting susceptible children, supporting parental authority, and protecting privacy at home. It nevertheless declares §505 unconstitutional and enjoins enforcement because §504 **with adequate notice** is a less restrictive alternative. Equal protection and vagueness are expressly not reached. This describes the judgment under review only.

Finding 22 says Playboy must demonstrate efficacy to establish its alternative claim. The conclusions place the constitutional burden on the Government to establish compelling interests and the absence of an adequate less restrictive measure. Both source statements remain visible; the validation does not silently rewrite one into the other or decide the controlling appellate allocation. The lower court's legal conclusions, economic findings, and factual uncertainties must be kept distinct.
