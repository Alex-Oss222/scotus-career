## Actual allegations and disposition

The decedents were Geraldine Downen and her eighteen-year-old daughter Valerie, killed by Geraldine's estranged husband, Bud Downen. A restraining order protected Geraldine. The operative complaint alleged that police repeatedly ignored pleas for protection and failed to enforce the order. The court agreed that those general allegations failed to state a federal due-process claim under DeShaney.

During appellate oral argument, counsel offered a more specific potential allegation: Bud was the police chief's close friend, and officers who attempted to stop his conduct were directed by the chief not to do so because he was Bud. The opinion does not treat that assertion as a proved event. It was more specific than the complaint and supplied the basis for permitting an attempted amendment, not a finding of liability.

The majority reversed dismissal and remanded to allow amendment in light of DeShaney, which had been issued immediately before dismissal below. It also permitted more specific equal-protection allegations to be pleaded, without deciding them. It expressly stated that an amended complaint might still fail against some or all defendants and did not resolve the ultimate constitutional claim. It decided no individual's qualified-immunity defense, no damages and no municipal-policy liability.

## Affirmative interference with public protection

The majority treats the proposed command to other officers as materially different from merely failing to provide aid. It describes an official's affirmative interference with protective services that otherwise would have been available, increasing vulnerability and potentially condoning the assailant's violence. The protective services in this account are other police officers' services. Freeman therefore cannot be accurately confined to obstruction of private rescuers or to private self-help.

The court reasons that DeShaney leaves a possible noncustodial claim when affirmative state action raises the danger or vulnerability beyond what would have existed absent that action. It states that a duty arises at some point along that line, while expressly acknowledging that the necessary degree of government participation and increased danger was unsettled. It does not hold that every supervisory instruction, failure to enforce an order, increased risk or imperfect protection crosses that line. The majority's actual judgment permits the specific theory to be pleaded and evaluated; it does not announce that the offered facts inevitably establish the claim.

The alleged favoritism toward a known assailant, specific attempts by officers to intervene, the command stopping them and resulting increased vulnerability distinguish this account from ordinary resource allocation or an unspecified failure to investigate. Those distinctions are relevant; the opinion does not turn them into an exhaustive constitutional checklist or expressly hold that personal friendship is an indispensable element.

## Dissent and source qualifications

Magill agrees that the existing pleading is insufficient. His dissent challenges the procedural basis for permitting another amendment: the plaintiff had already amended, never requested a further amendment or reconsideration on this ground, failed to seek that relief on appeal, and introduced the more specific facts in appellate rebuttal. His notes distinguish the existing omission allegation from the newly proposed affirmative-interference allegation and invoke then-existing pleading and preservation rules.

The dissent does not supply a contrary merits holding that a proved chief's order obstructing other officers' available protection can never violate due process. Nor can its discussion be treated as an immunity adjudication simply because one cited pleading rule mentions preparation of an immunity motion. Its procedural disagreement does not alter the majority's permission to amend. No historical pleading formulation from this opinion is silently substituted for the simulated Court's current procedural law.

## Relation to Pinder and the Saenz conduct-date inquiry

Pinder's 1995 en banc opinion identifies Freeman as too late to supply notice for Johnson's March 1989 conduct. That is not a temporal reason to exclude Freeman from a June 1995 review. Pinder also disputes characterizing Freeman and the other later cases as simple official-promise cases; it does not say that Freeman adjudicated promises alone or expressly overrule its interference analysis. The pertinent sources therefore must be compared at their real factual and temporal scopes.

Freeman materially strengthens the need to consider whether a supervisory command affirmatively disabled otherwise available public protection, rather than assuming that the public character of the aid or a chain-of-command setting necessarily makes the claim mere omission. Alongside Ross's private-rescue obstruction, it supplies a distinct public-officer analogue. The actual command, known danger, available protection, causal displacement and resulting increase in vulnerability must be identified from the Saenz record, not supplied by borrowing Freeman's allegations.

The limiting counterpoint is that Freeman resolved a pleading opportunity, explicitly reserved whether the proposed allegations would suffice, acknowledged uncertainty about the degree of danger creation required, and did not determine personal immunity. Its authority cannot alone be described as a holding that a reasonable officer in Saenz's particular circumstances necessarily lacked immunity. Conversely, those procedural and doctrinal reservations do not make its affirmative-interference reasoning irrelevant to notice; the published distinction was available well before June 1995. Its force must be evaluated with governing circuit law, the other pertinent preconduct sources and the sufficiently specific right actually asserted.

The inquiry must continue to distinguish 2000 merits, June 1995 personal notice, and municipal responsibility. Later simulated Pinder law can govern its proper merits subject without retroactively supplying notice. This supplement adds a verified source and preserves both its concrete analogue and limits; it supplies no adjudicative conclusion.
