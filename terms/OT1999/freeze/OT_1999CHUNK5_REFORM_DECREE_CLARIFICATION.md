# Reform Party — neutral clarification of injunctions and appellate scope

Source reviewed: full relevant procedural paragraphs 2-9 and 19-24, conclusion paragraph 48, footnote 13, and Greenberg paragraphs 56-65 in `../sources/chunk5-neutral-early/reform.txt`, the primary text of 174 F.3d 305. No current Supreme Court source, private position or comparator was used. This is a neutral source clarification, not a judgment recommendation.

## What the published source establishes

The en banc court expressly describes two separate but related cases and two appellate components. No. 97-3359 concerns the denial of Rule 60(b)(6) relief in Eshenbaugh's case; No. 96-3677 concerns the Childress summary judgment. Eshenbaugh's initial adverse district judgment was reversed in September 1996, rehearing was denied in November, no further review was sought, and the District Court then entered declaratory and injunctive relief on remand. The source separately reports that the District Court granted Childress summary judgment and entered declaratory and injunctive relief in October 1996. It therefore establishes distinct case proceedings and distinct grants of relief in their procedural histories.

The en banc conclusion affirms both the Rule 60 denial and Childress summary judgment. It then expressly affirms paragraph 4 of a District Court order dated December 9, 1996 as written, and directs a remand of the Childress case to delete only the separate free-association phrase from paragraph 3 of that order. Footnote 13 reproduces paragraphs 3 and 4. Paragraph 3 declares an equal-protection and associational burden insofar as the specified provisions bar the plaintiff minor party's nomination of someone who is also a major-party candidate for a section 2870(f) office. Paragraph 4 restrains defendants from invoking the provisions to prevent a minor-party nomination for such an office because the candidate files a major-party petition or wins a major-party primary. The paragraph contains no before-primary limit. The en banc court retained that injunction and deleted only the specified associational declaration on the Childress remand.

## What the source does not settle

The published opinion does not provide complete separately captioned decree texts and docket entries for both district actions. Its narrative reports Childress relief in October, Eshenbaugh relief after November rehearing, and then quotes a December 9 order while directing modification in the Childress case. It does not explain whether that December order memorialized, superseded, duplicated or combined earlier orders in both actions. A claim that there was one combined operative decree, or a claim that there were two currently operative injunctions with independently verified identical wording, exceeds this source. The precise unresolved proposition is the operative decree relationship and reach in the two district dockets; the original decrees and any consolidation/amendment entries would resolve it.

## Supported narrower path and its limits

Greenberg expressly joins denial of Rule 60 relief and then agrees with Childress's result while rejecting the unrestricted breadth of the merits reasoning and injunction. He would confine the equal-protection conclusion to cross-filing before the primary and preserve after-primary applications under Pennsylvania severability law. His separate opinion therefore supports a distinction between preserving the final Eshenbaugh disposition and narrowing the prospective merits holding in the Childress appeal. It does not establish that narrowing Childress alone dissolves, modifies or renders unenforceable every obligation arising from the final Eshenbaugh judgment.

A disposition may identify the two appellate components, preserve the denial of the Eshenbaugh Rule 60(b)(6) motion, and limit the Childress holding/remedial remand to pre-primary cross-filing. If original decree scope is not recovered, an implementation remand can require the lower court to identify and adjust the prospective relief actually within the Childress appeal consistently with that limitation, while expressly preserving the separate final Eshenbaugh judgment and any obligation not lawfully before the reviewing court. Do not claim that this restores unrestricted post-primary enforcement against the Party, changes the final Eshenbaugh decree, or converts the pending motion into a new Rule 60(b)(5) motion. Any requested alteration of the continuing Eshenbaugh obligation needs its own lawful procedural basis and express treatment; finality does not itself answer every prospective-relief question.

Validation: the published lower opinion supports the two appellate components and the narrow Childress path; the exact decree relationship remains specifically unresolved. This limitation should constrain operative wording, not be filled by an invented decree or assumption.
