## Reach, participation and decisive record premises

All eight Associates reach the granted Feres application question through the supplied merits grant and 28 U.S.C. §1254(1). All nine incumbent Justices are seated and participating at both relevant milestones; five votes are required for a Court judgment component or controlling proposition. Stone is not modeled or counted here. No threshold obstacle, DIG, jurisdictional enlargement or broader grant is invented. The petition's request to overrule Feres does not enlarge the granted application question. None of these commitments overrules Feres.

Each commitment accepts the facts on which the dismissal was sustained: Richards had permission to leave his duty station early, was driving his own car home for the personal purpose of attending to his pregnant wife, and was not then performing an assigned military task. He nevertheless remained an active-duty soldier, in uniform, with no pass or furlough; the collision occurred within Fort Knox. Highway 1638 was a public road on an Army easement, maintained and patrolled by Kentucky and used by civilians. The competing Education Center destination was not found and cannot support affirmance as an established military errand. The legal effect of these supplied circumstances is disputed; their existence is not a research gap.

The complaint presents five conduct theories: negligent maintenance; knowingly permitting an unsafe truck on public roads; allowing an allegedly unlicensed soldier to drive that class of truck; negligently ordering him to drive despite insufficient skill or experience; and negligent operation. Alleged brake, gauge, lamp, horn, battery, range, brake-fluid and master-cylinder defects remain allegations denied by the answer. The petition's red-light narrative identifies a police-report source but is not an adjudicated finding. Potts's federal scope of employment is expressly admitted; that admission supplies neither negligence nor a service-incident finding concerning Richards.

The widow, estate and children seek losses from Richards's death. There is no separately injured civilian claimant. Their derivative status follows the treatment of the underlying injury and theory; changing the claimant does not defeat a valid Feres bar. The reported insurance, gratuity and survivor payments do not by themselves establish an election, a universal offset or exclusive remedy for an injury outside Feres.

Both lower rationales are addressed. The panel treated active service, travel immediately from the duty station and remaining on the installation as sufficient service connection despite the accepted personal destination and public road. Its discipline reservation does not negate its judgment. The district court additionally relied on federal uniformity, compensation and the concrete challenge to military maintenance and personnel decisions. Its alternative Rule 12(c)/56 motions were not decided. Neither opinion decided liability or an independent §2680(a) ground.

## Competing rules and component conclusions

**Path A — affirm the entire Feres dismissal.** The accepted personal purpose does not take this immediate departure from the military workplace, still within the installation and without pass or furlough, outside the service relationship. Feres includes service-related circumstances beyond performance of a particular order; Johnson does not require a separate showing of actual discipline interference once service-incident injury is established. On this application all five theories and the derivative claims are barred. The strongest objection is that permission to end work, private homeward travel and a public highway make the operation claim materially like Brooks, while the panel itself doubted a special discipline concern. This path must explain the remaining service connection instead of treating enlistment, uniform, road ownership or benefits alone as dispositive.

**Path B — affirm the command-based dismissal, reverse the dismissal of separable road-operation and equipment-safety theories, and remand.** Authorized release from the day's work followed by private homeward travel on this publicly used, state-maintained road is outside the relevant service relationship for ordinary vehicle operation and equipment safety. The route's origin at the duty station, uniform, lack of a formal furlough and location within the installation do not convert those ordinary public-road duties into duties owed through the military relationship. This is an application of Brooks to the combined accepted circumstances, not an exception for every public road, every personal purpose or every unassigned activity. Shearer nevertheless bars the separate theories challenging the Army's permission for an allegedly unqualified soldier to perform the driving assignment and its order that he do so despite inadequate skill or experience. The strongest objection is that all theories concern one death during an immediate on-base departure from duty and that Shearer warns against making off-duty character or ordinary-negligence terminology decisive. This path answers through the actual duty challenged and the preserved distinction in Shearer between vehicle negligence and command supervision, while leaving mission, barracks, service medical and comparable service-incident injuries within Feres.

For Path B, negligent maintenance and permitting a knowingly unsafe truck on public roads are treated as the alleged failure to maintain and use roadworthy equipment, not as a proved challenge to procurement, deployment, combat readiness or allocation policy. The complaint does not establish such a policy premise. By contrast, the unlicensed-driver and negligent-order theories directly place the Army's selection and supervision of a soldier for a military driving assignment in issue. That distinction does not depend on proving a discretionary-function defense or inventing a mandatory licensing regulation. If an actually presented independent defense applies to a surviving theory, it remains available below.

## Justice-specific enhanced commitments

### Stevens

**Commitment and own authorities.** Path B. Stevens joined the controlling Feres discussion in Shearer Part II-B, 473 U.S. 52, 57–59, while withholding Part II-A; joined the Court in Stencel, 431 U.S. 666; joined Scalia's Johnson dissent, 481 U.S. 681, 692–703; and joined Ginsburg's Minns opinion and Thomas's Part I. Together those positions preserve existing command-related bars while opposing enlargement of a judicial exception merely because a military event appears in a causal chain. The present operation and equipment claims concern duties to users of a public road after authorized personal departure. The actual driver-selection and negligent-order allegations fall on Shearer's command side.

**Adversarial conclusions.** Path A has substantial support in Feres's nonfurlough facts, Johnson's broad service-incident rule and this journey's immediate origin at the base workplace. Stevens rejects its application to the road-safety theories because that makes the formal absence of furlough and internal base geography do the work that the service relationship must do, despite the accepted release and personal journey. The strongest objection to Path B is his own Shearer join: an off-duty victim and an apparently ordinary negligence count can still implicate military command. He answers that objection by sustaining the two personnel/assignment theories, not by requiring a showing that broad military policy was challenged.

**Scope, remedy and joins.** He supports the complete Path B rule and partial disposition. His Stencel join supplies no independent reason to bar an injury not first classified as service-incident. He would not join an opinion allowing all pleaded theories merely because the same truck could have struck a civilian, treating family losses as independent bodily injuries, or making an assigned task indispensable to Feres. He accepts the common remand limits below and needs no separate writing if the opinion preserves them.

### O'Connor

**Commitment and own authorities.** Path A. Her joins in Shearer and Powell's Johnson majority, 481 U.S. 681, 686–692, and her own Minns concurrence preserve a service-relationship bar independent of §2680(a). Minns expressly reserved genuinely distinct civilian duties and nonservice negligence; it did not make every ordinary-safety allegation distinct from service. She regards the immediate journey out of the duty station, while still within Fort Knox and without pass or furlough, as part of Richards's service circumstances even on the accepted personal destination. No finding that he was performing a military errand is needed.

**Adversarial conclusions.** Brooks, authorized release, the private car and the public road support Path B, and she accepts that neither uniform nor active enlistment alone would suffice. She rejects the proposed separation here because the underlying injury remains incident to service on the full circumstances; ordinary driving or maintenance language cannot remove that predicate. The strongest objection to Path A is the panel's concession that a civilian could bring an analogous suit without threatening a special superior–subordinate relationship. Her Johnson join answers it: actual direct discipline review is not an indispensable additional element for each injury already incident to service. The federal relationship and compensation system support that conclusion, but benefits alone create no universal election rule.

**Scope, remedy and joins.** She supports affirmance of all claims and independently agrees that the pleaded personnel-selection and negligent-order theories implicate Shearer. She would not join a rule making every on-base injury barred, every road accident actionable, or formal furlough an invariably necessary condition of Brooks. She does not adopt §2680(a) as a substitute unadjudicated ground. If the Court adopts Path B, her disagreement concerns its removal of the service-incident bar from the operation and equipment theories, not the admitted scope fact or an invented liability finding.

### Scalia

**Commitment and own authorities.** Path B. His Johnson dissent attacked the addition of military exclusions not supplied by the FTCA while reserving whether stare decisis required retaining Feres. His published Minns position joined the statutory policy-discretion rule and Ginsburg's partial dissent; his additional opinion insisted that policy discretion be tied to the responsibilities actually entrusted to the actor. These grounds oppose extending a surviving judicial exclusion to ordinary road operation and equipment safety solely because the claimant remained enlisted and had not crossed the base boundary.

**Adversarial conclusions.** Path A's strongest point is that Feres and Johnson remain law and do not confine service injury to a presently assigned task. He accepts that point but finds the accepted authorized personal trip sufficiently outside the military relationship under Brooks; he does not adopt the rejected civilian-tortfeasor distinction from his Johnson dissent as current law. The strongest objection to Path B is that preserving Brooks cannot erase Shearer's holding merely by calling an Army driving assignment ordinary negligent entrustment. He therefore sustains the unlicensed-driver permission and negligent-order theories as actually pleaded. Those claims demand review of the Army's suitability judgment and direction of a servicemember; this conclusion does not require them also to satisfy §2680(a).

**Scope, remedy and joins.** He joins Path B without requiring an opinion to endorse his criticism of Feres's statutory foundation. He would not join a declaration that all military maintenance involves policy, an automatic planning/operations rule, or an affirmative holding that the government violated a nonexistent supplied directive. He would also refuse a benefit-election rule unsupported by Brooks and Brown. Wholesale overruling is not part of his commitment within this grant. A separate concurrence is available only to preserve his statutory reservation if a lead opinion suggests approval of Feres beyond the force of existing precedent; it is not needed to alter the judgment or add a new rule.

### Kennedy

**Commitment and own authorities.** Path A. Kennedy joined Thomas's Minns Parts I and II: actual policy discretion was protected; the remaining pleaded safety duties there were inseparable from military-preparation choices. He reserved Feres rather than endorsing O'Connor's separate rule. His present Feres conclusion is a new application of surviving precedent, not a vote imported from that earlier judgment. Richards presents an injury to the active-duty member himself during an immediate departure from the duty station on the installation, rather than the unresolved reach of Feres to a child's separately manifested injury. The direct service setting supplies the predicate absent from his Minns reservation.

**Adversarial conclusions.** Path B is strongest where operation can be tried without reviewing command and where the accepted personal destination and public road resemble Brooks. Kennedy rejects a conclusion that those facts alone sever this departure from its duty-station setting: Feres's barracks and service-treatment cases already show that a service-related injury need not occur while carrying out an immediate command, and Johnson prevents making the character of the alleged negligence the exclusive criterion. The strongest objection to Path A is that it could collapse into an all-on-base rule. His commitment therefore rests on the actual conjunction of immediate departure from work, continuing active service without pass or furlough, and the installation setting; it does not declare the boundary fence or leave paperwork universally controlling.

**Scope, remedy and joins.** He joins the Path A application and the separate Shearer command proposition. He accepts that a genuinely independent private trip could be within Brooks even though the tortfeasor is military. He would withhold a join from language treating Minns as a majority Feres extension, denying that ordinary operation may be separable in an appropriate nonservice case, or converting any maintenance allegation into protected policy discretion. No remand is warranted merely to establish permission, personal-purpose assumption or road character already supplied. Negligence and benefits need no merits adjudication on his affirmance.

### Souter

**Commitment and own authorities.** Path B. Souter joined both Thomas's controlling Minns Part I and Ginsburg's separate opinion rejecting categorical dismissal of separable execution allegations and automatic immunity from military causal origin. That published position supplies the relevant distinction, without converting the children's separate injuries in Minns into the derivative losses presented here. He applies Brooks to the accepted personal journey and ordinary public-road duties, while preserving the existing Shearer rule for the pleaded exercise of Army authority over the driver.

**Adversarial conclusions.** Path A is supported by the no-furlough circumstance and the immediate on-base connection to work. Souter rejects its use to bar the operation and equipment theories because the complete record also supplies affirmative release from work and personal travel; those circumstances cannot be treated as merely absent evidence of service. Brown supports rejection of causal history alone, although its discharged-veteran facts do not make Richards a civilian. The strongest objection to Path B is that Shearer does not require a broad policy choice and the complaint affirmatively challenges military permission and orders. He meets that objection through partial affirmance of those theories, rather than assuming that every allegation denominated execution may proceed.

**Scope, remedy and joins.** He supports all Path B propositions. The distinction is between the actual duties litigated in these accepted circumstances, not a new checklist, presumption, burden shift or mandatory evidentiary procedure. He would not join full reversal that left the driver-selection and order allegations untreated; nor would he join a remand premised on wholly unknown travel or employment-scope facts. On surviving theories he leaves any legally material destination dispute and properly presented defenses below without directing new factfinding as an end in itself.

### Thomas

**Commitment and own authorities.** Path A. His own Minns Parts I and II distinguish the precise statutory test from its application and reject relabeling necessarily policy-dependent allegations as automatically actionable execution. Minns reserved Feres. Here he applies the surviving Feres–Johnson rule to the direct servicemember injury rather than resolving that reserved question about separately injured children. The immediate on-base journey away from the duty station remains sufficiently incident to service despite permission to depart for a personal reason and the absence of a presently assigned task.

**Adversarial conclusions.** Brooks and the statutory inclusion of military employees give Path B real support; §2671's scope provision nevertheless concerns Potts, not whether Richards's injury falls within Feres. Thomas rejects an inference that admitted federal scope supplies the full waiver irrespective of that surviving exclusion. The strongest objection to Path A is its potential to add a categorical military exception contrary to his Minns Part I. He does not do so: Feres supplies the existing rule, and this combination of work departure, nonfurlough active service and on-base location supplies its present application. Johnson permits that conclusion without separately demonstrating protected policy discretion in ordinary vehicle operation.

**Scope, remedy and joins.** He supports the Path A application and the Shearer command ground; no broader holding on all personal military travel is required. He will not join either a newly invented military-policy immunity or a finding of an actual §2680(a) defense from the absence of a reproduced maintenance directive. He does not treat his Minns authorship as authority to decide Feres there or here by head count. The lower judgment is affirmed on its actual Feres ground, not on an alternative Rule 12(c), Rule 56, contributory-negligence or damages determination.

### Ginsburg

**Commitment and own authorities.** Path B. Her Minns separate opinion, joined by Stevens, Scalia and Souter, insists on identifying a particular service or policy relationship and preserving separable ordinary execution claims. Her direct join to Thomas's Part I accepts immunity for actual protected discretion, without an exception for every allegation of negligence. Here the death and family losses are derivative and receive no independent civilian-injury route. The operation and equipment-safety theories instead survive because Richards's accepted authorized personal journey falls on Brooks's nonservice side for those duties.

**Adversarial conclusions.** Path A correctly stresses continuing active service, no furlough and the location and origin of the journey. Ginsburg finds those facts insufficient to erase the affirmative release and private destination where the duty litigated is road safety applicable to ordinary travelers. The strongest objection to Path B is the complaint's own challenge to selection and orders, not merely the government's characterization of the case as military. She accepts that objection as to permission for an allegedly unqualified soldier to undertake the assignment and the order that he drive despite inadequate skill. Shearer's command rule remains applicable even to an off-duty victim and even when a plaintiff identifies negligent performance rather than attacking general policy.

**Scope, remedy and joins.** She joins Path B in full. She would refuse either an all-military-origin bar or an all-public-road exemption, and would not treat the same civil duty as proof that §2680(a) can never apply. The partial remand restores the surviving claims only to ordinary adjudication with all unresolved tort requirements and properly presented defenses. Her Minns remand did not confer discovery or establish a mandatory directive; neither would this one. No separate opinion is needed if those distinctions appear in the controlling formulation.

### Breyer

**Commitment and own authorities.** Path A. Breyer joined Thomas's Minns Parts I and II and reserved Feres. Those joins recognize the statutory distinction but examine the practical dependence of the pleaded claims on the government activity challenged. In this direct servicemember case he reads Feres and Johnson to include Richards's immediate departure from the military workplace within the installation, despite the accepted personal family purpose. He treats the concrete personnel and order allegations as additional Shearer support, without assuming that every vehicle-maintenance act is policy discretion.

**Adversarial conclusions.** Path B is strongest for ordinary driving: civilians using the same road face the same danger, and the panel doubted an unusual discipline cost. Breyer nonetheless regards that comparison as insufficient once the particular injury remains incident to service; Johnson sustains the federal relationship and compensation rationales without requiring the trial to question a command in every count. The strongest objection to Path A is that public-road location and permission to leave make this an ordinary private journey, not military activity. He distinguishes permission to depart early from the more complete separation reflected in Brooks's furlough and Brown's discharge, while expressly declining to make formal leave indispensable in every future case. The present journey was still an immediate movement from the duty station within the service setting.

**Scope, remedy and joins.** He joins the Path A application and the Shearer command proposition. He would not support an affirmance dependent on the disputed tuition-assistance destination, a quantitative comparison between benefits and tort damages, or a presumed negligence defense. His prior Minns vote does not establish a Feres extension and does not make this a §2680(a) case. He needs no extra factual hearing to affirm on the accepted circumstances; if a majority chooses Path B, he can agree with its remand's preservation of unresolved tort issues without joining its conclusion that the operation and equipment claims fall outside Feres.

## Shared propositions, independent grounds and unresolved Court coalition

All eight Associates support preserving the Feres–Brooks boundary, Johnson's rejection of a tortfeasor-status exception, and Shearer's rule for actual command supervision; no one treats Minns's fractured whole judgment as a controlling Feres extension. All eight support affirmance of the driver-permission and negligent-order dismissal under Shearer. The four Path A Justices additionally support those dispositions through their injury-wide service-incident ground. The latter ground does not become a common rationale for the other theories.

For operation, maintenance and unsafe-truck road use, O'Connor, Kennedy, Thomas and Breyer support affirmance; Stevens, Scalia, Souter and Ginsburg support reversal and remand. Neither four-Justice position alone controls a nine-member Court. No final whole-case tally, tie disposition, opinion of the Court, author or assignment is supplied here. A final statement of what controls must await reconciliation and Stone assembly, counting judgments separately from propositions. No Marks synthesis combines contrary treatment of these claims or the independent Feres and discretionary-function grounds.

The support for the two command theories does not mean a mere reference to supervision always establishes Feres. The operative proposition is confined to the pleaded challenge to the Army's permission and order for a soldier to perform a military driving assignment despite alleged inability or disqualification. It does not adjudicate all supervision claims involving civilian claimants or every ordinary road-maintenance act.

## Remedy, source limits and freeze conclusion

On Path A, the Third Circuit's judgment and the district court's Feres dismissal stand. No fault determination, damages award, election or benefit offset follows. On Path B, the dismissal of operation, maintenance and knowingly unsafe road-use theories is reversed; the driver-permission and negligent-order dismissal is affirmed; the case returns through the Third Circuit for proceedings consistent with those limits. The personal homeward destination remains the accepted premise for reviewing this dismissal, not a final finding binding the parties on every later issue. A genuinely disputed destination may be addressed below only if legally material under the adopted rule. The admitted scope-of-employment fact remains in the record.

Every surviving theory remains subject to applicable local tort law, negligence, causation, damages, contributory negligence, and any properly presented independent FTCA defense. Minns requires actual identification of protected policy choice for §2680(a); it neither supplies that defense here nor rules it out in advance. No binding maintenance or licensing directive is inferred. No compulsory discovery, special hearing, production order, filing deadline, automatic benefit deduction or unconditional right to compensation is created.
