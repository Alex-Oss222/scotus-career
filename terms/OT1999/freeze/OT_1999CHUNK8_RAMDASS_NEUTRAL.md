## Supported record and legal power

Virginia section 53.1-151(B1) requires three separate qualifying murder, rape, or robbery-by-deadly-weapon offenses, or their combination, not part of a common act, transaction, or scheme. Subsection (B2)'s distinct drug-offense and intervening-liberty conditions are not conditions in (B1). Subsection (C)'s first-life-sentence rule ordinarily permits parole after fifteen years, with twenty-five years for its specified Class 1 felony or child-murder category, unless (B1) or (B2) makes the person ineligible. The separate multiple-life and prior-life-parole provisions have their own predicates; none is established as an independent ineligibility ground here. Minimum service periods must not be restated as permanent ineligibility.

Rule 3A:15(a) allows an accused's insufficiency motion after the Commonwealth rests or all evidence concludes and separately addresses renewal after a hung jury. Subsection (b) permits a motion by the accused, made no later than twenty-one days after final order, to set aside a guilty verdict for trial error or legally insufficient evidence. Subsection (c) requires acquittal for insufficiency and a new trial for another ground on which the verdict is set aside. The hung-jury provision does not displace subsection (b), and subsection (b)'s deadline does not say that a previously rejected subsection (a) motion extinguishes all later authority.

## Governing law and supported alternatives

The Standards and Tests entry is **Parole ineligibility as an answer to future-danger evidence**. Its operative disclosure protection dates from Simmons, June 17, 1994; its present retroactivity operation dates from O'Dell, June 19, 1997. Simmons requires an effective opportunity, through accurate defense presentation or an adequate instruction, to explain the exclusion of ordinary parole when the State raises future dangerousness and life without ordinary parole is the only lawful noncapital alternative. It prescribes no single script and no guarantee against clemency or future legislation. The State may still argue danger within prison. Its actual harmlessness holding was on direct review and on its record.

Allridge, January 15, 1996, rejects an entitlement to a favorable prediction of release where ordinary parole remains legally available, without authorizing misleading information or categorically excluding truthful parole evidence. O'Dell makes the confined Simmons protection available against Teague, on its distinct old-rule and alternative watershed grounds, while preserving actual eligibility, effective opportunity, error consequences, and genuine habeas conditions. Neither decision already settles the constitutional consequence of a separate guilty verdict awaiting entry of judgment.

The supported alternatives are:

1. **State-law eligibility as the constitutional trigger.** Accept Virginia's judgment-entry rule and assess legal eligibility at the capital jury stage. The Commonwealth can argue that ordinary parole remained legally available then, and that anticipated entry of a separate judgment is outside Simmons's fixed-law condition. Ramdass's strongest answer is that formal entry did not reflect an ordinary release uncertainty and that the capital judge ultimately sentenced after entry. Selecting the jury-stage legal status must be justified, not merely assumed from the word conviction.

2. **A federal duty concerning a legally fixed sentencing alternative despite timing of entry.** Ramdass can argue that the completed verdict and recommended sentence, later entry before capital judicial sentencing, and State use of the same robbery make this a truthful explanation of the available sentence rather than a forecast of parole-board discretion. Gardner and Skipper supply the opportunity-to-answer foundation. The Commonwealth's strongest objection is that this approach adds an entitlement concerning an anticipated conviction and requires an objective boundary between fixed legal consequences and predicted adjudications. A court adopting a practical-certainty trigger must identify what supported facts meet it, or reserve its application; a party's assertion that no basis for relief existed cannot itself establish the condition.

3. **Adequacy of the actual opportunity, if the trigger is met.** The defense presentation, exact question and answer, prosecution argument, and whole relevant proceeding govern whether Ramdass had an effective means of explaining the legal alternative. Neither a refused instruction alone nor counsel's prediction alone decides that inquiry. This alternative does not invent an independently preserved claim about every possible release qualification.

4. **Independent statutory denial or limited return.** Even if a federal constitutional rule would support relief on direct review, habeas relief requires its own statutory ground. A supported denial under section 2254(d)(1) is distinct from a no-error conclusion. If the appellate standard was legally defective but application or effect remains unresolved, a bounded return differs from reinstatement of the District Court's relief.

Terry Williams, April 18, 2000, supplies the current construction of section 2254(d)(1): the clauses do independent work; conflict with the Court's governing legal rule may satisfy contrary-to review without identical facts; an opposite result on materially indistinguishable facts also qualifies; unreasonable application is objective and cannot be replaced by a poll of reasonable jurists. A general holding is not excluded merely because it is general. The decision does not adopt either competing exclusive general allocation of the clauses or a universal extension/refusal-to-extend rule.

**The November 4, 1994 cutoff matters.** Simmons and the earlier Gardner, Skipper, Ramos, and relevant other preexisting Supreme Court holdings can supply the constitutional law against which that state merits decision is assessed. O'Dell and Allridge are later present-law authorities and cannot be backdated as newly established 1994 constitutional holdings. Terry Williams construes the now-applicable review statute; it does not shift the constitutional-law cutoff to 2000. Weeks confirms that error and statutory eligibility are independent. Section 2254(d)(2), the factual presumption under (e)(1), and the evidentiary-hearing conditions under (e)(2) remain separate; a legal dispute about the significance of an undisputed verdict date does not itself establish factual unreasonableness or hearing entitlement.
