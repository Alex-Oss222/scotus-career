## Material facts replacing the incomplete summary

Private Charles Richards was stationed at Fort Knox. On June 26, 1995 he received permission to leave work early. The Army issued neither a pass nor a furlough. He drove his own vehicle from his duty station. The collision with a five-ton military fuel truck occurred inside the base at Kentucky Highway 1638 and United States Highway 31W. Kentucky maintained and patrolled Highway 1638 under an easement from the Army; civilians and service members used the road.

The accepted procedural premise is personal travel home to attend to his pregnant wife. The government disputed that destination and asserted travel to the Fort Knox Education Center for Army tuition-assistance paperwork. Both lower courts accepted the claimant's homeward-bound account for the dismissal without deciding the dispute. The government version is not an established fact and cannot supply a service-connected errand against the claimant. Conversely the accepted personal purpose does not establish formal furlough, discharge from active-duty status, an off-base accident or absence of every service relationship.

The claims allege failure properly to maintain the Army vehicle and negligent supervision of its driver. The packet's emphasis on ordinary driving must not erase those actual allegations. The lower courts dismissed under Rule 12(b)(1) on Feres grounds; the Third Circuit reviewed application of the bar de novo. No negligence, causation, scope-of-employment, damages or independent statutory-exception question was adjudicated in the supplied record.

The Third Circuit treated departure from the duty station, active-duty status and the on-base accident as an incident-to-service relationship, despite the assumed personal purpose and public highway. Part II-B separately questioned whether that result advances military-discipline concerns, noting that the same negligent conduct could injure a civilian and that the resulting suit could raise similar questions. Those are the lower court's grounds and stated reservations, not this Court's holdings.

## Current law and balanced paths

Feres and the surviving Stencel Aero, Shearer and Johnson authorities remain in force. Brooks supplies a genuine boundary for an accident outside the relevant service relationship, rather than a universal public-road or no-assigned-task exception. The record permits a direct legal comparison: the claimant invokes released work duties, assumed personal travel, public access and the ordinary character of the asserted negligence; the government invokes continuing military status without a pass or furlough, departure from the duty station, location within the base and the broader incident-to-service doctrine. Neither status nor geography alone may be silently substituted for the whole inquiry, and a requirement of a particular assigned task is not already established law.

Minns adds no majority Feres rule for every service-linked injury. Its whole-dismissal grounds are nonnested, and only two judgment supporters used Feres. Its distinct eight-Justice section 2680(a) rule protects actual choice susceptible to protected policy judgment, not every military act. Vehicle ownership and a supervision allegation alone do not establish that exception; it requires identification of the conduct and protected discretion. Nor does the existence of an operational allegation conclusively defeat it. The FTCA's other requirements remain independent.
