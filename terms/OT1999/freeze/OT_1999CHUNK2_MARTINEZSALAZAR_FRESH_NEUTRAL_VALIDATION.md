## Actual selection record and rule

The defendants received ten joint petit-jury peremptories and a separate alternate-juror challenge. Gilbert expressed a predisposition toward the prosecution. After their cause challenge and two objections failed, defendants used a peremptory to remove him and eventually exhausted their allotment. They did not request another challenge, identify another juror they would have removed, or challenge the seated jury's impartiality. Counsel stated no objection when the final names were read. Those facts do not erase the preserved cause objections or establish a finding of deliberate judicial manipulation.

The Ninth Circuit's finding that refusing the cause challenge was an abuse of discretion is uncontested on review. Its conclusion that the seated impartial jury caused no Sixth Amendment violation is distinct from its Fifth Amendment/full-allotment theory of impairment and automatic reversal. Judge Rymer's competing reading treats the rule as granting the stated opportunities to strike and separately contests the asserted automatic constitutional remedy. Each is a lower-court position to be assessed, not this Court's law.

Rule 24(b) supplies the joint numerical allotment and permits, rather than requires, additional challenges in multiple-defendant cases. The separate alternate challenge cannot be treated as an unused substitute for an ordinary challenge. The exact subdivision (b) text was mechanically copied into `preparation/OT_1999CHUNK2_RULE24_OPERATIVE_TEXT.md`. Its official later compilation is used solely to document unchanged earlier text: the listed 1999 amendment concerns subdivision (c), alternate retention and reorganization, not a change in the ordinary allotment or multiple-defendant discretion. No post-event substantive amendment or alternate-retention issue is imported.

The statutory dispute is whether receiving and exercising the full numerical allotment satisfies the rule when one strike cures a cause error, or whether the promised allotment means that many choices after all jurors legally removable for cause have been removed. The absence of a request for a discretionary extra strike is relevant context, not automatic proof that the trial court was required to replenish the allotment or that every objection was waived.

## What Ross does and leaves open

Ross distinguishes the constitutional right to an impartial jury from statutory peremptories and measures a state-created peremptory right by the state rules governing it. Its Oklahoma result depends on the state's requirement of curative use and its conditions for obtaining reversal. Footnote 4 expressly leaves open the impairment question without that state-law limitation. Ross therefore does not independently create a federal duty to cure every cause error with a peremptory or conclusively decide Rule 24's meaning.

The Ross dissent, joined by Stevens, preserves a competing account of the strategic and compositional loss caused by forced curative use and its relation to the right to an unbiased jury. That account must be confronted on its terms; it is not the controlling Ross rule. The majority's distinction of Gray and the dissent's contrary treatment concern their specific settings and cannot silently become an answer to every federal cause error.

The government's proposed curative-use requirement is broader than its argument that this defendant received all Rule 24 provides. They may be accepted or rejected separately. An erroneous cause ruling, denial of the numerical allotment, deliberate manipulation and the seating of a biased juror are different possible cases. This record establishes the first and supplies no finding of the others.

Only after deciding whether a legally protected entitlement was impaired should the model resolve the constitutional consequence and the applicable remedy. Swain's impairment language does not eliminate that antecedent question. Likewise, the impartiality of the seated jury does not by itself settle whether a separate rule-based interest existed. If an impairment is found, automatic-reversal and ordinary federal harmless-error arguments require their own grounds rather than conversion of every rule error into a due-process violation.
