## Carmell v. Texas — 529 U.S. 513 (April 25, 2000), No. 98-7540

**Historical judgment:** Reversed and remanded, 5–4. Supporting: Stevens, Scalia, Souter, Thomas, Breyer. Opposing: Rehnquist, O'Connor, Kennedy, Ginsburg.

| Writing | Author and joins | Scope |
|---|---|---|
| Court | Stevens; Scalia, Souter, Thomas, Breyer | Entire opinion and judgment. |
| Dissent | Ginsburg; Rehnquist, O'Connor, Kennedy | Entire dissent. |

The Court applied Calder's fourth category to a retrospective reduction in the quantum of evidence legally sufficient to convict. The pre-amendment corroboration/outcry condition was a sufficiency rule, not merely a rule admitting a witness's testimony. The Court distinguished Hopt's witness competency change and Thompson's admissibility change; Collins did not extinguish the fourth category. Ordinary evidentiary changes, rules that may benefit either side, and competency rules are not swept into the holding merely because they influence trial outcomes.

Counts 7–10, charged between June 1992 and July 1993, involved a victim aged 14 or 15. Before September 1, 1993, the relevant exception to the corroboration/outcry condition covered victims under 14; afterward it covered victims under 18. The outcry period also changed from six months to one year. Her March 1995 report was too late under either period. The historical court of appeals assumed lack of corroboration without resolving the State's contrary contention. The Supreme Court therefore did not itself determine that the record lacked legally sufficient corroboration. Convictions on those counts could not stand insofar as uncorroborated. The disposition was a remand applying the former requirement, with acquittal required for counts whose trial proof was insufficient under that rule; not an unconditional retrial authorization.

Ginsburg treated Article 38.07 as a witness-credibility rule equivalent to removing a testimonial disability, not a reduction of the prosecution's substantive burden. The dissent did not need to abolish every application of Calder's fourth category: its distinction between credibility regulation and an across-the-board quantum rule was central. Its footnote 2 describes possible corroborating testimony about Carmell's letter; it does not resolve sufficiency. The separate concern about the certiorari question's count coverage does not change the Court's actual four-count treatment.

Sources: [Court](https://www.law.cornell.edu/supct/html/98-7540.ZO.html), [Ginsburg](https://www.law.cornell.edu/supct/html/98-7540.ZD.html), [official U.S. Reports PDF](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/USREPORTS-529/pdf/USREPORTS-529-513.pdf).

## North Carolina Right to Life, Inc. v. Bartlett — 168 F.3d 705 (February 17, 1999), No. 98-1636

**Historical anchor:** Fourth Circuit merits judgment, affirmed in part and reversed in part. Wilkinson wrote for Wilkinson, Ervin, and Wilkins; no separate writing. No corresponding Supreme Court merits writing or lineup is supplied or inferred.

The complete lower judgment is broader than the two issues selected for this Run. It invalidated the political-committee definition in §163-278.6(14), whose primary-or-incidental-purpose and influence-election language exposed issue advocacy to committee burdens; it declined the State's narrowing construction. It also invalidated corporate expenditure/contribution restrictions as applied to the nonprofit MCFL-type organization, despite limited corporate-origin receipts through the described telephone refund program. ## National Mining Association v. Department of the Interior — 177 F.3d 1 (May 28, 1999)

**Historical anchor:** D.C. Circuit appeals Nos. 97-5202, 97-5203, 97-5204 and 98-5248. Henderson wrote for Wald, Silberman, and Henderson; no separate writing. The court affirmed in part, reversed in part, and remanded. No Supreme Court merits lineup exists for this comparator.

The case reviewed the 1997 interim final ownership/control rule following the earlier invalidation of upstream attribution. The current rule permitted downstream indirect control. The court upheld that interpretation; invalidated blocking based solely on formerly controlled entities, while preserving the separate statutory provision for a demonstrated pattern of willful violations of the requisite nature and duration resulting in irreparable environmental damage and indicating intent not to comply; invalidated the officer/director and 10–50% ownership presumptions; and upheld the general-partner and asset-control presumptions. Other presumptions were not presented.

It rejected the §2462 five-year limitation argument for permitting consequences tied to continuing violations without deciding whether the rule was penal. It invalidated retroactive application where both control and the violation preceded November 2, 1988, but distinguished control acquired after that date even when the violation was older. It upheld the additional information demand and allowed inability to obtain information to rebut attribution. It sustained express and implied authority to suspend or rescind erroneously issued permits. Federal revocation of a state permit still had to follow §521(a)(3)'s specified procedures, including the separate imminent-harm route where applicable. It did not hold that every correction or reopening was forbidden. Separate procedural due-process challenges were not decided in this appeal.

**Correction to supplied comparator:** The broad phrase about consequences after control ended must not erase present indirect control, the statutory willful-pattern exception, the specific retroactivity boundary, or corrective permit authority. The later Rule's challenge is distinct from the earlier upstream case.

Source: [full published lower opinion](https://law.resource.org/pub/us/case/reporter/F3/177/177.F3d.1.97-5202.97-5204.98-5248.html). The opinion's closing reference to 97-5248 differs from the header's 98-5248; use the header docket.

## Saenz v. Heldenfels Brothers, Inc. — 183 F.3d 389 (July 30, 1999), No. 98-40902

**Historical anchor:** Fifth Circuit affirmance of summary judgment for Officer Gonzalez and Brooks County. Edith H. Jones wrote for Duhe, Barksdale, and Jones; no separate writing. No Supreme Court merits vote or rationale is inferred.

The incident occurred June 19, 1995. The opinion accepted the plaintiffs' summary-judgment version of the alleged conduct: after the stop of a suspected intoxicated driver, a senior officer told the junior officer not to investigate; a collision followed minutes later. The Fifth Circuit rejected the substantive-due-process theory because the alleged failure to arrest did not create the constitutional relationship or affirmative state-created danger claimed. It had not adopted a general state-created-danger doctrine; it also distinguished the affirmative obstruction of an identified rescue in Ross. It resolved absence of a constitutional violation without a separate clearly-established-law analysis. The remaining state claims had been severed and remanded. The private company's presence in the caption does not place a federal due-process claim against it within this appeal.

## Fischer v. United States — 529 U.S. 667 (May 15, 2000), No. 99-116

**Historical judgment:** Affirmed, 7–2. Supporting: Rehnquist, Stevens, O'Connor, Kennedy, Souter, Ginsburg, Breyer. Opposing: Scalia, Thomas.

| Writing | Author and joins | Scope |
|---|---|---|
| Court | Kennedy; Rehnquist, Stevens, O'Connor, Souter, Ginsburg, Breyer | Entire opinion. |
| Dissent | Thomas; Scalia | Entire dissent. |

The provider received benefits under §666(b) because Medicare's structure, operation and purpose support hospitals' continuing capacity to provide quality care, beyond a point-of-sale exchange for particular patients. Patients' status as beneficiaries did not exclude providers from also benefiting. The Court expressly declined to treat every federal payment as a benefit: program design and the recipient's own role matter. Ordinary commercial payments and §666(c)'s protection for bona fide compensation and usual-course business reimbursement remain distinct analytical questions. Section 666(c) was not read out of the statute.

Thomas treated Medicare payments here as reimbursement for the market services supplied to individual beneficiaries. Ancillary regulatory requirements and reimbursement allowances did not transform compensation into a federal subsidy of the hospital. The dissent emphasized federalism/Spending Clause concerns and lenity. Its notes record that the trial proof did not identify a Part A/Part B breakdown or show that WVHA received the exceptional targeted subsidies discussed in the Court's program description. The majority did not make such a special-payment finding.

## United States v. Morrison / Brzonkala v. Morrison — 529 U.S. 598 (May 15, 2000), Nos. 99-5, 99-29

**Historical judgment:** Affirmed, 5–4, invalidating §13981 under both asserted grants of congressional power. Supporting: Rehnquist, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Thomas. Opposing: Stevens, Souter, Ginsburg, Breyer.

| Writing | Author and joins | Scope |
|---|---|---|
| Court | Rehnquist; O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Thomas | Entire opinion, Commerce Clause and §5 grounds. |
| Concurrence | Thomas alone | Entire concurrence; also joins Court in full. |
| Dissent | Souter; Stevens, Ginsburg, Breyer | Entire dissent; would sustain Commerce Clause authority and does not reach §5. |
| Dissent | Breyer; Stevens throughout; Souter and Ginsburg only Part I-A | Part I-A critiques the economic/noneconomic distinction and its operation; Part I-B discusses legislative procedures; Part II raises doubts about §5 reasoning but expressly does not decide that alternative ground. |

The Court refused to sustain a civil remedy for noneconomic gender-motivated violence solely through aggregated downstream economic effects. It relied on the absence of a jurisdictional element and the attenuated reasoning despite extensive congressional findings. It did not announce that every noneconomic regulation can never fall within the commerce power. On §5 it retained the state-action boundary, rejected reliance on separate-opinion dicta in Guest and Carter, and found the private-assailant remedy unlike a congruent corrective measure aimed at state discrimination. Its additional nationwide-remedy concern must not be mistaken for a finding that every State had been investigated and exonerated.

Thomas would reconsider the substantial-effects doctrine itself as inconsistent with original understanding. Souter would apply rational-basis review to Congress's substantial-effects determination, emphasizing the extensive record, integrated economy, precedent and political safeguards rather than categorical subject-matter exclusions. Breyer's Part I-A agrees with that position and develops practical objections to the categories; his Part I-B suggests that thorough congressional consideration of federalism might become relevant without adopting a new definitive test. Only Breyer and Stevens join Part II, and even they reserve ultimate resolution of §5. Thus four dissenting votes for the judgment do not establish four votes endorsing §5 authority.

The Title IX claims against Virginia Tech were outside the grant; dismissal of one and the separate hostile-environment remand are not merged into the §13981 disposition.

## Johnson v. United States — 529 U.S. 694 (May 15, 2000), No. 99-5153

**Historical judgment:** Affirmed, 8–1. Supporting: Rehnquist, Stevens, O'Connor, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg, Breyer. Opposing: Scalia.

| Writing | Author and joins | Scope |
|---|---|---|
| Court | Souter; Rehnquist, Stevens, O'Connor, Ginsburg, Breyer in full; Kennedy in part | Six complete joins. Kennedy does not join the §3583(a) and §3583(e)(2) discussions identified in his concurrence. |
| Partial concurrence | Kennedy alone | Agrees former §3583(e)(3) permits the residual supervised-release term after reincarceration. Rejects combining disjunctive statutory alternatives; avoids unnecessary §3583(a) discussion and pre-revocation extension under (e)(2). |

**Temporal component:** The consequences of violating supervised release are attributed to the original offense. New subsection (h), enacted September 13, 1994, did not apply to this earlier offense absent congressional direction for retroactive application. The Court therefore avoided resolving the constitutional Ex Post Facto challenge. Souter's six complete joins, Kennedy's partial join and Scalia's express agreement support that treatment; Thomas's judgment concurrence should not be silently converted into assent to Part II.

**Former (e)(3) component:** The Court read revocation as leaving a residual term capable of being served partly in prison and partly under supervision. It relied on text, parole practice and congressional purpose; Kennedy agreed on the narrower textual ground, while Thomas accepted the textual result without purpose reasoning. Kennedy's emphasis that the combined new imprisonment and supervision cannot exceed the original term is material. The supplemental (e)(2) suggestion about extending a term before revocation and the discussion of (a) do not acquire his join. Scalia read revocation as cancellation: Congress's prior authorization to require service of all or part of a term in prison did not authorize recreating supervision afterward. He did not dissent from the original-offense temporal anchor or statutory nonretroactivity.

**Correction to supplied comparator:** Its one-line opinion structure omits two separate writings and obscures partial and judgment-only joins. An 8–1 judgment is not an eight-Justice Court opinion. Cornell's preliminary text misnames/dates the enactment as a 1996 Act; the official opinion at 698 identifies the Violent Crime Control and Law Enforcement Act of 1994. The certiorari citation is 528 U.S. 950, not Cornell's 590. Use the official version.

## Public Lands Council v. Babbitt — 529 U.S. 728 (May 15, 2000), No. 98-1991

**Historical judgment:** Affirmed, 9–0. Breyer wrote for all nine Justices. O'Connor concurred, joined by Thomas; both also joined the Court in full.

Three facial statutory challenges were decided: the revised grazing-preference definition; deletion of the requirement that an applicant be engaged in the livestock business; and federal title to future permanent improvements made under cooperative agreements. The statutory priority did not guarantee a fixed forage quantity, but the continuing duty adequately to safeguard grazing privileges remained, as did review of particular administrative decisions. Removing the business requirement did not repeal the statutory stock-owner qualification. The statute and regulations operated together; applicants engaged in the livestock business retained the specified preference. The rule did not authorize conservation-only permits; the adverse lower ruling on those permits was not appealed by the Secretary. Failure to make substantial use for two consecutive fee years, absent authorization, remained subject to cancellation.

The title rule was prospective and applied to cooperative agreements, not all improvements under every instrument. Removable improvements made under a permit could remain privately owned. The statute's provision for compensating a prior owner did not require the Secretary to vest private title in future permanent improvements. Negotiated terms and the statutory compensation provision remained relevant.

O'Connor emphasized the agency's representation that active use plus suspended use would remain equivalent to the former preference, and the resulting narrowness of the facial challenge. Petitioners did not present an APA arbitrary-and-capricious challenge; particular future violations could be challenged later. She did not provide an alternative nonjoinder rationale.

**Correction/qualification:** 'Challenged grazing regulations' means these three provisions. It does not include reinstating the invalidated conservation-permit rule, erasing stock ownership, deciding the APA merits, or transferring existing title.

Sources: [Court](https://www.law.cornell.edu/supct/html/98-1991.ZO.html), [O'Connor](https://www.law.cornell.edu/supct/html/98-1991.ZC.html), [official PDF](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/USREPORTS-529/pdf/USREPORTS-529-728.pdf).

## United States v. McIver — 186 F.3d 1119 (August 6, 1999), Nos. 98-30145, 98-30146

**Historical anchor:** Ninth Circuit affirmance of convictions. Alarcon wrote; Rymer joined all. Kleinfeld joined everything except Part II-B and concurred in the result. Thus the judgment was unanimous but the attachment/seizure analysis had two joins, not three. No Supreme Court merits vote is supplied.

Part II-B held that attaching the devices to the underside of the Toyota 4Runner was neither a search nor a seizure on this record. The driveway was conceded to be outside the curtilage; the majority found no protected privacy interest in the exterior and no meaningful possessory interference. McIver expressly conceded that monitoring public-road movement itself was not a Fourth Amendment search; his claim challenged installation and its fruits. The GPS stopped working after three days, and the beeper was used during the limited September 23–October 2, 1997 period. This was two devices on one vehicle, not one device on each of two vehicles.

Kleinfeld treated attachment as a seizure because the right to exclude others from one's effect is a possessory interest; lack of visible damage did not answer it. He distinguished the consensual installation before delivery in Karo and relied on Soldal's separation of property from privacy. He found the error harmless because independent surveillance was overwhelming and the tracking devices were superfluous. He did not concur in the no-seizure rule.

The other appellate issues included open-field cameras, the separate vehicle-search ground, photographic authentication, conspiracy sufficiency, a supplemental instruction, and sentencing use of suppressed evidence. Those unselected components are not automatically reopened by review of Part II-B.

**Correction to supplied comparator:** It omits Kleinfeld's concurrence and the actual monitoring concession. Stating a unanimous no-search/no-seizure holding would be wrong. The primary narrative dates attachment September 23; a quoted affidavit uses September 22. That minor inconsistency is preserved rather than converted into a new material fact.

## Geier v. American Honda Motor Co. — 529 U.S. 861 (May 22, 2000), No. 98-1811

**Historical judgment:** Affirmed, 5–4. Supporting: Rehnquist, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Breyer. Opposing: Stevens, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg.

| Writing | Author and joins | Scope |
|---|---|---|
| Court | Breyer; Rehnquist, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy | Entire opinion. |
| Dissent | Stevens; Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg | Entire dissent. |

The Court held the express preemption provision did not displace this common-law claim, but the saving clause did not foreclose ordinary conflict preemption. The 1984 version of FMVSS 208 deliberately sought a gradual mix of passive restraints, including safety, development, cost and public-acceptance considerations. A general tort duty to install airbags in all comparable 1987 cars would obstruct those objectives. Agency explanations had weight but were not the sole basis; the Court did not require an express preemption pronouncement in the regulation to establish actual conflict.

The Court left open a materially different claim based on a particular vehicle's design features, whose narrower demand might not obstruct the fleet mix and phase-in. Regulatory compliance alone was not a universal immunity. Manual belts, airbag alternatives, fleet percentages and the conditional rescission mechanism matter to the historical objective; a loose assertion that federal law allowed a choice is insufficient to restate the reasoning.

Stevens read the express provision and saving clause as strong protection for common-law remedies and applied the presumption against displacement of state tort law. He found no clear conflicting duty: the standard set floors, not ceilings; liability could encourage alternatives and would not necessarily impose an all-airbag fleet. Regulatory history and a later litigating position did not supply the specificity and accountable process needed to preempt. He would allow the claim to proceed without deciding its tort merits. The dissent also disputed the Court's reading of this complaint as solely a generic no-airbag claim.

The lower disposition was dismissal affirmed by the D.C. Circuit, not a factfinding verdict or an award of summary judgment after resolving tort merits. The official text distinguishes the agency's statutory authority, the objective embodied in the rule, and the agency's later account of that objective.

## United States v. Playboy Entertainment Group — 529 U.S. 803 (May 22, 2000), No. 98-1682

**Historical judgment:** Affirmed, 5–4. Supporting: Stevens, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg. Opposing: Rehnquist, O'Connor, Scalia, Breyer.

| Writing | Author and joins | Scope |
|---|---|---|
| Court | Kennedy; Stevens, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg | Entire opinion. |
| Concurrence | Stevens alone | Rejects Scalia's pandering/obscenity theory; truthful advertising does not strip the advertised protected content of protection. |
| Concurrence | Thomas alone | Obscenity can be prohibited, but the case was litigated on the protected, merely indecent premise without findings or advocacy to the contrary; strict scrutiny cannot be diluted to accommodate that choice. |
| Dissent | Scalia alone | Also joins Breyer; separately would sustain the provision as regulation of the business of obscenity/pandering under his Ginzburg theory. |
| Dissent | Breyer; Rehnquist, O'Connor, Scalia | Accepts the governing compelling-interest/least-restrictive-effective-alternative framework, but finds sufficient proof of the problem and of §504's comparative inadequacy. |

Section 505's content-based full-blocking or time-channeling regime required strict scrutiny. In practice it removed the affected programming for 16 hours daily where full blocking was unavailable. The Government did not prove that a well-publicized, household-specific §504 blocking option would fail to protect children as effectively through less restrictive means. Low take-up alone did not resolve whether the problem was limited, notice inadequate, or blocking ineffective; the risk of nonpersuasion remained with the Government. The Court did not require a perfect alternative or assume that every parent would act. Nor did it permit substituting the Government's preferences for informed parents merely on an unproved assumption of inertia.

Breyer distinguished opt-out §504 from §505's default protection and emphasized children whose parents were unaware, absent or unable to complete the requests; he viewed the evidence, delay problems and cost figures differently. His disagreement was not a rejection of strict scrutiny or a reversal of the Government's burden. Scalia's separate obscenity rationale had no additional join.

The proper remedy was enjoining §505, not judicially rewriting §504 through compulsory contract terms. The Court affirmed without deciding the separate postjudgment-motion jurisdiction question. Section 504's free-on-request obligation and §505's different default/time restriction are distinct. The statutory appendix, rather than an incidental citation in a separate writing, controls the subsection labels. The official Breyer text at 838 cites §504(c) where Cornell gives §505(c); the full appendix identifies the definitions in §505(c) and §504(b).

## Limits of this verification

This artifact verifies reported opinions, their reasoning and writing topology. It does not certify independent full review of every petition, merits brief and appendix. That case-record work proceeds separately under Engine §14. In particular, Carmell's assumed lack of corroboration, Fischer's specific payment composition, Geier's complaint characterization, and McIver's source date discrepancy cannot be converted into additional factual findings by comparator research. Published source recitations have been separately sanitized for a fresh neutral validator. Any use of later descriptions as the then-governing law must be prevented by the entering-law and event-date controls.
