## Operative scope common to the comparison

Review concerns the facial preenforcement challenge to section 18-9-122(3): a knowing speaker-initiated approach within eight feet of another person, without that person's consent, for passing a leaflet or handbill, displaying a sign, or oral protest, education or counseling, on the public way or sidewalk within 100 feet of an entrance door to a legally authorized health care facility. The authoritative no-retreat construction protects stationary speakers when another person approaches. There is no total speech-free zone, no prohibition on all words, no ban on mere presence or stationary signs, and no new duty to move away.

The content theory appeared in the complaint and the state courts passed on neutrality. Historical discussion of possible waiver does not establish a current concession or forfeiture. All eight reach the presented classification and practical-fit issues.

Subsection (2)'s obstruction offense is distinct. Subsection (5)'s permission for local regulation no less restrictive does not validate every future ordinance. Subsection (6)'s civil-liability cross-reference is not the entire separate cause of action. No new civil remedy, criminal conviction or petitioner misconduct is adjudicated. ## Affirming grounds, barriers and individual comparison

The five affirmers adopt the following bounded account. Protected leafleting, signs and conversation on traditional public sidewalks bear a real burden, especially the loss of a few steps toward a potential leaflet recipient. Neutrality rests on the actual close-approach mechanism, its operation regardless of topic or viewpoint, and access/treatment protection independent of disagreement with ideas. A place label or benign stated purpose alone is insufficient. Examining words to distinguish the listed communicative activity from casual conversation does not, in this setting, select a prohibited subject or favored position. The State cannot convert emotional offense at abortion advocacy into a noncommunicative harm.

Ward requires a significant legitimate interest, narrow tailoring and ample alternative channels, but not the least restrictive imaginable means. The supported crowding/confrontation problem and the preventive administration of access protection justify this limited approach rule despite some harmless contacts within its reach. Subsection (2) and other conduct laws are important alternatives, but do not conclusively establish that every prophylactic approach rule is unnecessary. Eight feet, the 100-foot location limit, knowing approach, consent and the no-retreat construction operate together; none is sufficient by itself. Signs and audible speech remain available, stationary speakers can offer literature to passing persons, and willing recipients may approach or consent. These channels need not be identical to private low-voiced counseling, but must be practically usable. The five do not hold that amplification invariably replaces intimate conversation or that a restriction assists every speaker.

Madsen's 300-foot advance-invitation restraint and Schenck's moving fifteen-foot restraints remain invalid under their actual holdings. They involved injunction-specific scrutiny and materially different burdens; neither creates a universal prohibition or permission for a personal-distance rule. The five preserve peaceful initial advocacy as protected activity while finding this legislation's narrower burden justified. The valid fixed-zone refusal provision in Schenck is not a general listener veto on public streets.

The five reject facial vagueness because the prohibited approach, ordinary communicative-purpose terms, measurable boundaries, scienter and binding construction give adequate notice and constrain enforcement. This does not decide every ambiguous encounter. Souter, O'Connor, Ginsburg and Breyer may additionally rely on the historical concurrence's section 18-1-503(4) point: absent a contrary indication, culpability applies to each offense element, rather than treating an unknowable facility location as strict liability. The width of 'education' covers much ordinary purposeful advocacy; it does not silently rewrite the law to prohibit every social greeting or question. No new speech exemption or statutory amendment is supplied.

The facial overbreadth claim fails for these five because the general facility/recipient coverage does not show a substantial impermissible sweep in relation to valid applications. They do not establish that time-place-manner laws are immune from overbreadth review. Particular wide entrances, multipurpose buildings and discriminatory applications remain available for appropriate later examination. The consent condition is permission for a close approach, not official advance licensing or authority for one listener to silence speech to others. No general right to avoid unpopular ideas on a sidewalk is adopted; the historical Court's own footnotes 24-25 also distinguish the protected access/physical-encounter interest from such a right.

Stevens's own Madsen separate position favored individualized complete relief for bound actors, and expressly left a similar general rule as a different question. It therefore is not an automatic historical bridge to affirmance. Here Ward's statutory inquiry and the narrower approach operation supply the bridge. His Erie content-selection opinion examines actual operation; unlike Erie's communicative hierarchy and municipal account, this statute leaves the same message available from stationary speakers and beyond the approach limit. His actual-facts comparison supports, rather than assumes, neutrality.

O'Connor's own Madsen opinion protects initial approach and rejects broad visible-image suppression; those remain substantial counterarguments. Her present conclusion depends on the narrower statutory operation and usable channels. Her Erie position required affirmative justification and a supported noncommunicative relation, not mere lack of contrary proof. The direct crowding/access mechanism meets that requirement for her here.

Souter's Erie separate writing demanded a germane basis for both harm and alleviation, while allowing common experience when the connection is apparent. Here physical close approaches bear that direct relation. His additional explanation may stress that the same offensive words remain lawful from a stationary position; it may not declare facial content discrimination harmless whenever officials invoke a neutral motive. His assessment of enforcement clarity and absence of demonstrated substantial overbreadth remains facial, not an advance endorsement of every prosecution.

Ginsburg's Erie targeting and independent-fit positions require both inquiries, and she reaches both here. Her Madsen and Schenck joins preserve the line between coercive physical effects and offensive ideas and explain why no broad captive-audience rule is necessary. Breyer's distinct remand preferences in Schenck and Erie do not require reopening settled statutory scope here. Both may join the additional delivery-based explanation on its stated limits.

All five can join a full affirming opinion with these grounds. Souter is willing to write or join an additional explanation; O'Connor, Ginsburg and Breyer are willing to join its bounded analysis. Historical authorship creates no obligation to publish it. Stevens requires no separate writing. None of the five joins an opinion resting only on audience dislike, presumed misconduct, a blanket medical-vicinity exception, or automatic constitutionality of any eight-foot rule.

## Dissenting grounds, corrections and writing willingness

Scalia, Kennedy and Thomas find selection of protest, education and counseling to be selection of protected communication, even if no particular viewpoint is named. Identical physical approaches for other speech remain permitted. A neutral justification does not cure that operative distinction; threats and blackmail are not useful analogues for peaceful protected persuasion. The available access-protection interests do not supply the strict justification required for this sweep. The three also see the statutory setting and purpose as reinforcing the concern that the law targets opposition to abortion; Kennedy is specifically willing to state his stronger predictable-operation and viewpoint objection. They do not invent an authoritative exemption for pro-abortion advocacy or deny the State's power over obstruction and intimidation.

Independently, all three reject the law under Ward. The rule burdens peaceful initial approaches and ordinary handbilling to every person in the designated vicinity of every covered facility, including contacts with no access obstruction. Stationary proffers, distant speech and amplification do not adequately preserve the practical opportunity to tender literature and initiate the quiet exchange sought here. Narrow tailoring and alternative channels are independent requirements; the existence of some channels does not relax fit. Subsection (2) and ordinary conduct remedies sharpen the deficiency without establishing a universal constitutional duty to exhaust every alternative. They preserve lawful conduct controls and do not claim that Ward demands the least restrictive conceivable rule.

Scalia's historical Part II states this intermediate ground expressly, including the practical handbilling burden and the mismatch between all covered approaches and obstructive conduct (530 U.S. 749-761). His refusal to join Erie's intermediate alternative concerned the proof and rationale there, not a rule forbidding alternative grounds in other cases. His own Schenck join already found mobile restraints invalid under Madsen in addition to his stricter theory. No present changed premise supports the initial omission; it is corrected. Thomas's full historical Scalia join and his own Erie alternative confirm his initially committed independent ground.

Kennedy's historical Part II additionally finds the interaction of undefined communicative categories, mixed consent signals, moving encounters and uncertain facility boundaries constitutionally vague and overbroad (530 U.S. 772-775). Scienter does not, for him, make an undefined legal standard ascertainable. The no-retreat construction answers one difficulty but does not settle what counts as education/counseling, consent or a knowing prohibited approach amid several moving pedestrians. Neither current Erie nor the supplied earlier precedents supplies a materially new narrowing construction. The initial decision not to reach vagueness lacked a supported changed premise and is corrected. His historical independent Ward ground remains; his Erie request for a classification remand does not require one where this statute's operative terms and authoritative interpretation are already before the Court.

The three reject a general power to suppress unpopular ideas for unwilling sidewalk listeners, while preserving genuinely captive settings, privacy of the home and independently valid physical-interference restraints. They accept the no-retreat construction and may not portray the statute as compelling continuous withdrawal. Their speech grounds do not depend on importing historical Casey authorship or the outcome of the separate same-day Stenberg case. Neither those historical cross-references nor rhetorical disagreement adds a current abortion holding.

All three are willing to write or join a dissent on the common classification, independent Ward and protected-sweep grounds. Kennedy is willing to state his additional vagueness and more explicit viewpoint analysis separately. Scalia and Thomas are not assigned a join in that additional vagueness ground.
