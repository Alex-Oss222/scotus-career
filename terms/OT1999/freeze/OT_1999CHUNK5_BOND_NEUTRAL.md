## Question and procedural boundaries

The granted question is whether the agent's tactile examination of Bond's soft carry-on luggage, stored in the overhead area of a passenger bus, constituted a Fourth Amendment search. The question concerns the objective character of this contact and the privacy interest retained in the bag. It does not turn on the officer's private desire to find drugs, and it does not ask whether every touching of luggage is a search.

Bond was convicted after a bench trial on stipulated facts of possessing methamphetamine with intent to distribute and conspiring to commit that offense, under 21 U.S.C. §§841(a)(1) and 846. He received 57 months' imprisonment and three years' supervised release. His suppression motion addressed both the drugs and subsequent confessions. The district court denied suppression and reconsideration because it regarded the exterior touching as minimally intrusive and outside the Fourth Amendment's search and seizure categories. The Fifth Circuit affirmed, 167 F.3d 225 (February 8, 1999).

The circuit also rejected a distinct prolonged-checkpoint-detention argument, describing one or two added minutes and five to ten minutes for the inspection overall. The certiorari grant does not separately reopen that ruling. Lawful presence on the bus and a lawful immigration inspection do not themselves establish authority for any additional search of luggage. Conversely, the dispute does not require a ruling that boarding or the immigration questions were unlawful. A fact-specific search holding must stay within the granted question. There is no identified mootness, ownership, forfeiture or participation obstacle to deciding it.

## Material facts and disputed inferences

On July 17, 1997, Bond traveled on a Greyhound bus from California toward Arkansas. The bus stopped at the permanent Border Patrol checkpoint near Sierra Blanca, Texas, on Interstate 10 east of El Paso; this was an interior immigration checkpoint, not the international border. Border Patrol Agent Cesar Cantu boarded and moved from front to back checking immigration status. He was satisfied by the time he reached the rear that all passengers were lawfully present. The bus had no rear exit, so he walked forward again.

Bond's green canvas bag was zipped shut and placed in the luggage area directly above his seat. It was soft-sided, approximately a foot and a half long and about a foot wide/high. It contained clothing, food and a duct-taped package of methamphetamine inside a pants leg. The drug package weighed about 1.3 pounds and measured roughly six or seven inches by four or five inches. The petitioner's brief describes the package as wrapped in pants; the Government emphasizes that the district court found it inside a pants leg and disputes the inference that this concealed its shape from routine contact. Neither description proves the legal conclusion about exposure.

The bus was nearly full, and the overhead area contained considerable luggage. The record did not establish whether this bus used open racks or bins with doors; the agent could not recall. The circuit treated either form as accessible to other passengers. Do not invent a closed, locked compartment or find an independent violation from opening a bin. Bond acknowledged that other passengers could handle or move his bag while stowing or retrieving their luggage. He did not thereby concede that the particular tactile examination was ordinary handling, or that the bag's contents had been exposed without limit.

On the return walk Cantu felt and squeezed overhead luggage. He felt Bond's bag and discerned a relatively hard brick-like object with defined edges and a different density. His testimony characterized the pressure as hard, while adding that he did not squeeze hard enough to break anything. His experience led him to suspect packaged narcotics. The petitioner's brief quotes the transcript as a very hard squeeze and stresses the probing needed to identify a concealed object. The Government relies on the district court's characterization of minimally intrusive touching and argues that one squeeze readily revealed a large object through the soft fabric. It disputes the inference of extensive repeated manipulation. Bond testified that Cantu reached for the bag, shook it a little, squeezed it and sniffed it. The shared minimum is an intentional tactile examination of the bag, not merely moving it out of the way. There is no need to invent a number of squeezes or duration to resolve the parties' competing legal characterizations.

Cantu's routine luggage inspection lacked individualized suspicion before the tactile contact. After sensing the object he asked who owned the bag; Bond acknowledged ownership and permitted him to open it. Cantu found the methamphetamine. Following Miranda warnings, Bond admitted delivering the drugs; he later gave another warned confession to a different officer. The package and statements therefore followed the disputed examination rather than supplying its original justification.

## Entering law and the unresolved choice

Katz distinguishes what a person exposes to public observation from what the person seeks to preserve as private, including in a publicly accessible location. Place recognizes privacy in personal luggage; its treatment of a limited canine sniff does not establish that every investigative method short of opening luggage is outside the Amendment. Hicks distinguishes lawful observation from additional manipulation that reveals concealed information. Ciraolo and Riley provide the Government's public-exposure analogy and require attention to the actual claimed privacy interest rather than police motive. These earlier authorities are used only to the extent consistent with the simulated register.

Simulated Dickerson, June 7, 1993, permits contraband seizure when authorized protective touching itself supplies probable cause and lawful access exists; immediate recognition requires probable cause, not certainty. It forbids squeezing or further exploration of a nonthreatening object to obtain that cause after the protective justification ends. Its actual suppression disposition rests on the accepted manipulation findings. It neither creates an exploratory-touch exception nor automatically answers whether handling overhead luggage is initially authorized by its exposure to public contact. The analogy depends on deciding that exposure question, not importing a weapons frisk or personal-search finding into this case.

The simulated independent-luggage rule preserves a separately established privacy interest in closed personal luggage even if the traveler lacks a privacy interest in the surrounding vehicle. Ownership/control and precautions preserving privacy matter. A vehicle's public accessibility is therefore not a complete answer about a bag's concealed contents. Recognizing privacy does not eliminate supported warrant exceptions, but no unasserted exception is supplied merely to avoid deciding this case.

Current-term Flippo rejects a categorical crime-scene search exception and preserves the difference between an item's visible exterior and closed contents; its remand for actually preserved alternatives is not a mandate to invent alternatives here. Wardlow preserves objective review, respect for historical facts and the prohibition against using later-discovered contraband as retrospective justification. Neither decision already holds that all contact with luggage is a search.

Robinette keeps voluntariness and causal independence from earlier illegality distinct. That background prevents the shortcut that voluntary consent automatically cures an earlier unlawful search. Because the Government does not rely independently on the consent here, Robinette is not a reason to reopen a withdrawn or unasserted defense. The raw packet's generic direction to consider later consent as an alternative admission ground is superseded.

The open issue is the constitutional extent of exposure created by placing a soft, closed bag in a shared overhead area: whether this contact examined concealed contents beyond ordinary handling, or instead perceived only information already exposed to reasonably foreseeable contact. The raw packet's assertion that no doctrinal issue remains beyond application is too categorical. A new rule must specify its objective trigger and limits and explain how Dickerson and public-observation cases fit it.

## Strongest supported opposing positions

Bond argues that luggage is used to preserve the privacy of personal effects during public travel. Zipping the bag and keeping it above his seat manifested that interest. Accepting ordinary bumps, repositioning and compression to make space does not expose its interior to a probing examination. A hard squeeze capable of identifying the edges and density of an object through the fabric, duct tape and pants is objectively different from incidental handling. Physical entry into the bag is unnecessary for a search, as tactile examinations and Hicks illustrate. The argument concerns how the officer obtained the information, not his undisclosed motive. Bond seeks reversal and suppression of the drugs and confessions.

The Government argues that the particular bag and large solid package exposed the relevant contours to any passenger or carrier employee foreseeably grabbing, moving or compressing the bag. It stresses the district court's limited-contact characterization and the crowded luggage area. An officer may deliberately and carefully observe what the public can perceive; Ciraolo, Brown and Knotts do not confine police to accidental, fleeting observations. A rule keyed to the officer's interest in drugs would contradict the shared objective approach, while a rule turning on subtle differences between squeezing and manipulation could be difficult to apply. Its strongest narrower position is that this actual contact did not materially differ from ordinary foreseeable handling, even if materially more intrusive tactile examinations could be searches. It distinguishes the bodily privacy and limited frisk authorization in Terry/Dickerson and the concealed home information in Hicks. It requests affirmance.

Both positions must confront the opposing premise: Bond must explain an administrable objective boundary and address the actual limited-contact finding; the Government must explain why physical access to an exterior entitles the officer to obtain information about enclosed contents, without converting possible unwanted interference into unlimited public exposure. Neither side may replace that issue with the bare words deliberate, public, tactile or minimal.

## Lawful dispositions and remedy

An affirmance is available if the Court concludes that the agent obtained only information exposed to the contact reasonably foreseeable here, under a rule consistent with the entering law. It would leave the convictions and suppression denial intact. That path need not authorize opening closed bags, manipulating their contents without limit, or conducting bodily frisks without justification.

A reversal is available if the Court concludes that this objective examination invaded a retained privacy interest and lacked legal justification. The mandate should return the criminal case for proceedings consistent with that search holding and the actual suppression issue. Bond's request includes drugs and confessions; do not silently reduce it to the drugs. At the same time, a search holding alone is not an acquittal, an unconditional release order or a finding that every unreviewed issue concerning every later statement has been decided. Specify the reach of any suppression direction and any genuinely remaining consequence under the adopted rule. The Government's nonreliance on subsequent consent prevents a remand designed merely to offer that ground anew.

A narrower vacatur/remand is possible if a material factual or legal application genuinely remains unresolved under the rule selected. It requires identifying that actual uncertainty and the proceeding to which it returns. The disagreement over legal characterization is not itself a missing-evidence finding. Do not reconstruct an additional evidentiary hearing simply because the Court rejects the circuit's rule. Ordinary observation, moving or repositioning a bag, accidental contact and lawful safety handling are not categorically adjudged searches by a holding about this exploratory examination; any purported exception retains its own factual predicates. No automatic immunity for every visual technique, sniff or claimed safety search follows either.
