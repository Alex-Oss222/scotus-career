**Question granted:** Does RFRA require the IRS to use a religious objector's proposed alternative collection procedure, thereby avoiding ordinary penalties or interest, when the taxpayer declines voluntary payment?

## Posture, scope and dates

The Second Circuit affirmed judgment on the pleadings dismissing Gordon and Edith Browne's refund action concerning interest and additions for tax years 1993, 1994 and 1995 and declining to compel collection by levy without those charges. The reported opinion is 176 F.3d 25, No. 98-6124, decided March 24, 1999. The District Court ruling is 22 F. Supp. 2d 309, decided May 13, 1998, filed May 14, with final judgment entered May 18, 1998. The petition appendix reproduces both opinions. The Second Circuit's July 12, 1999 rehearing order is also reproduced.

The United States' opposition reports appellate judgment entry May 24, 1999 and petition filing October 12, 1999, the Tuesday after a federal holiday. The petition's jurisdiction paragraph instead says filing October 8, while its signature is October 11 and its cover bears an October 12 filed stamp. The distinction between the March opinion and reported May judgment dates remains a source discrepancy, not an invented jurisdictional defect. Both sides describe the petition as timely after the July 12 rehearing denial. No extension matter is added.

The granted question is RFRA. The original complaint separately pleaded constitutional penalty and interest claims and RFRA penalty and interest claims, invoking Code provisions as well. The broad merits grant does not itself establish jurisdiction over every requested form of relief or grant every original constitutional or independent Code issue. A favorable ruling on one RFRA component cannot silently enlarge review of the others. No historical denial of review or other historical Supreme Court action is imported.

## Record and proposal, with source status

The Brownes are Quakers whose sincere religious objection distinguishes voluntary payment supporting military activity from involuntary collection. They do not dispute the underlying income-tax liability or the Government's authority to collect it. They filed accurate, timely returns for 1993–95 and paid roughly 72 percent of the amounts due, withholding the portion they calculated as military-related. They gave equivalent sums to charitable organizations and expected the IRS to collect the remaining taxes involuntarily. The IRS eventually collected the principal through levies and collected interest and additions for failure to pay and estimated-tax underpayment. These core circumstances are common to the lower opinions and the parties' accounts.

The District Court applied Rule 12(c): well-pleaded material allegations and reasonable inferences favor the nonmovant. The complaint is not a set of adjudicated trial findings. It alleges prompt replies, disclosed assets and accessible accounts, little actual collection work, and delay that increased the charges unnecessarily. The petition further asserts that sufficient assets were kept available and that the Government conceded at appellate argument that collection could have occurred in two months. No argument transcript was recovered, so that last assertion remains counsel's account, not an authenticated concession or judicial finding. The opposition establishes that the Government contests the legal and operational equivalence of compelled levy and voluntary timely compliance; it does not supply adjudicated findings of a particular administrative cost.

The complaint alleges the following chronology for 1993: return mailed April 11, 1994; IRS contact June 27; response July 4; levy collection began April 1995 and ended June 1996. For 1994 it alleges return mailed April 14, 1995; IRS contact July 3; response July 11; collection on February 13, 1996. These are allegations accepted as such for the pleadings posture, not findings after evidence. The original scanned appendix itself breaks off in the 1995 return paragraph and resumes midtext before paragraph K; the discontinuity was visually checked on PDF page 131, printed appendix page 100a. No missing 1995 interval is fabricated.

The actual proposed relief is more concrete than the runtime summary suggested: once the IRS knows it is dealing with religious hardship and has identified assets, use ordinary compulsory levy promptly instead of repeated demands that permit charges to accrue, and refund or abate the resulting interest and additions. The complaint says one levy notice would have met due process; that is the taxpayers' legal position, not a verified conclusion about all statutory notice, timing, review, ownership, or bank-response conditions. No escrow, assigned security, automatic withdrawal authorization, guaranteed payment date, waiver of every procedural protection, or equal-cost mechanism is established. A power to levy does not itself establish a taxpayer's right to demand its exercise at a chosen time. Conversely, a legal or operational objection to that demand must not be converted into an invented factual finding of actual costs.

The requested common-revenue payment is not conditioned on a government nonmilitary earmark as in Adams. The Brownes accept involuntary collection of the full ordinary liability; the distinction concerns who initiates payment, when collection occurs, and which consequences follow. The use of religious language in their return letters does not convert this distinct proposal into Adams's proposed spending restriction.

## Actual charges and original Code grounds

| Component | Source and operative distinction | Status in this record |
|---|---|---|
| Failure to pay tax shown on a return | Section 6651(a)(2), not section 6651(a)(1). The addition ordinarily accrues at 0.5 percent per month or fraction, capped at 25 percent, subject to its amount rules and the conjunctive reasonable-cause/no-willful-neglect exception. | Actual component identified by both parties and the appellate opinion. Returns were timely; no failure-to-file addition is supplied. No amount is calculated here. |
| Estimated-tax underpayment | Section 6654, including the distinct section 6654(e)(3)(A) unusual-circumstances qualification. It is not section 6651's reasonable-cause exception. | Actual addition identified by the United States' statement and the original complaint. Its timing function and the requested religious waiver require separate treatment. |
| Interest on unpaid principal | Section 6601 supplies interest from the prescribed payment date to payment, subject to its actual qualifications. Section 6601(h) excludes a failure to pay estimated tax under sections 6654/6655 from that interest provision. | Interest is a distinct collected charge; no compound calculation or blanket identity with the additions is assumed. |
| Original interest-abatement discretion | Section 6404(e)(1), with the original ministerial-act, taxpayer-contribution and written-contact qualifications for these tax years. | Invoked as a Code source and as part of the taxpayers' discrimination/least-restrictive-means theory. Not a general hardship waiver of all interest. |
| Alleged unequal access to hardship consideration | Treasury Regulation 301.6651-1(c), the actual Code exceptions, and identified IRS procedures. | Comparability and religious discrimination are disputed legal/application questions, not established merely by the existence of relief for illness, disaster, inability to pay or IRS error. |

The regulation's payment rule requires ordinary business care and prudence in providing for liability and nevertheless inability to pay or undue hardship, with the latter expressly cross-referenced to section 1.6161-1(b). The reproduced manual describes that hardship as substantial financial loss, beyond inconvenience. The petition reply's objection that the Government improperly added a financial limitation does not erase the incorporated regulation. Illness, absence or disaster are circumstances considered under the actual applicable provision, not automatic waivers for every charge.

Section 6654(e)(3)(A) requires the Secretary's determination that casualty, disaster or other unusual circumstances would make the addition against equity and good conscience, to the extent of the qualifying underpayment. Section 6654(e)(3)(B) is separate: retirement after age 62 or becoming disabled in the relevant taxable year or its predecessor, together with underpayment due to reasonable cause and not willful neglect. The complaint cites paragraph (B)(ii) but supplies no qualifying retirement/disability finding. Its recitation that Gordon retired in August 1988 does not itself satisfy the timing qualification for 1993–95. No additional statutory entitlement is inferred.

The complaint also cites section 6404(f), whose actual subject is penalty/addition abatement attributable to erroneous written IRS advice, subject to reasonable reliance, a specific written request and adequate accurate taxpayer information. It is not an interchangeable interest-hardship provision. No qualifying erroneous-advice fact is established here.

The August 20, 1998 manual excerpts are dated later than the liabilities and the May 1997 denial. They may support the litigants' asserted description of an existing practice but do not by themselves prove the wording operative during every earlier event. In particular, reproduced IRM 120.1 §6.1.3 discusses administrative appeal rights for specified preparer/promoter penalties under sections 6700/6701 and then sections 6694/6695. Its introductory scope is not a general merits rule for all income-tax penalty waivers. The actual May 2, 1997 letter independently invokes Regulation 601.106(b)'s exclusion from administrative appeals of disputes involving solely refusal to comply on moral, religious, political, constitutional, conscientious or similar grounds. The existence and scope of that administrative restriction do not prove that every statutory waiver uses a religion-specific standard.

The lower courts did not write separate, fully reasoned Code holdings on each waiver or calculate any charge. The District Court concluded that, having found no lawful basis for noncompliance, the taxpayers were necessarily not entitled to refund interest and penalties otherwise properly assessed. The appellate court affirmed using the constitutional and RFRA rationales. The Government's opposition presents additional independent Code defenses—no qualifying inability/hardship and deliberate nonpayment—but those arguments are not transformed into separate lower-court findings or Supreme Court holdings by this packet. Independent Code questions outside the grant remain at their actual procedural status; do not mechanically import Adams's express preservation of its own independently decided Code determinations as a finding that Browne has identical rulings.

## Interest statute and forum timing

The official 1994 Code text, copied in the statutory slice, provides that section 6404(e)(1) may abate interest attributable to IRS error or delay in a ministerial act; no significant aspect of the error or delay may be attributable to the taxpayer, and it must follow written IRS contact about the deficiency or payment. The temporary regulation reproduced in the appendix defines a ministerial act as procedural/mechanical, without judgment or discretion, after the prerequisites have occurred; a legal determination is not ministerial. The source's examples distinguish a completed approval followed by mechanical delay from discretionary investigative prioritization.

Public Law 104-168 §301 added managerial acts and the unreasonable-error qualification, but §301(c) applies that expansion only to taxable years beginning after July 30, 1996. The petition appendix prints that amended version; it must not be applied retroactively to 1993–95 merely because it appears there. The complaint itself quotes the earlier ministerial-act language. No decision here is supplied about whether any particular collection step was ministerial or discretionary, whether the taxpayers materially caused a relevant delay, or which period could qualify.

Public Law 104-168 §302 separately added Tax Court review of a refusal to abate interest. It applies to requests after July 30, 1996; it states claimant-eligibility requirements by cross-reference, a filing period of 180 days after mailing of the final refusal, abuse-of-discretion review, and power to order abatement. It does not textually require a deficiency petition under section 6213. That is a separate source from ordinary deficiency jurisdiction. The provision was initially section 6404(g), renumbered by Public Law 105-206 §§3305 and 3309 to section 6404(i). The required presidential emergency designation for §3309 was sent July 22, 1998 and is saved from the official Public Papers. No later renumbering or later Supreme Court construction is used.

The complaint and the IRS denial header give claim receipt March 24, 1997 and final disallowance May 2, 1997. The attachment refers to correspondence counsel said was mailed January 27, 1997 and Form 843 signatures dated May 21, 1996. The signatures do not establish the date a request was filed. The supplied received/mailed dates are after enactment; any contention that the operative original request actually predates enactment would require its filing evidence. The District Court complaint was filed October 28, 1997. This is not proof that a Tax Court action was timely brought or that all claimant-eligibility conditions were met. Do not invent a Tax Court petition, tolling, transfer, new deadline, or exhausted judicial route.

## Preserved threshold forks

The United States expressly argued in its opposition that the District Court lacked jurisdiction over interest abatement because section 6404(i) provides exclusive Tax Court review. It separately invoked section 7421 and 28 U.S.C. §2201 against the requested declaration and injunction. It stated that neither lower court resolved those questions. The taxpayers' reply disputed jurisdictional failure, invoked jurisdiction-before-merits principles and asserted that their self-reported liability was not a deficiency subject to Tax Court review. Their assertion that the objection was summarily rejected is not an express jurisdictional ruling in the reproduced opinions. Silence and a merits judgment do not resolve the preserved dispute by inference.

The original complaint invokes 28 U.S.C. §§1331, 1340 and 1346(a)(1), with section 7422, as jurisdiction for the refund suit. Section 7422(a)'s actual refund-claim prerequisite applies to penalties and wrongfully collected sums as well as tax principal; relabeling the action as not a tax-refund case does not itself avoid it. The refund of already collected penalties, the original discretionary section 6404 interest claim, an independent RFRA interest-refund theory, and prospective orders controlling collection must be classified separately. The Government's objections do not necessarily remove the ordinary paid-penalty refund component.

**Government's threshold path:** The specific Tax Court route displaces District Court review of section 6404 refusal, and the tax-remedy statutes bar prospective declarations or injunctions. A merits affirmance cannot serve as a substitute for dismissing a component over which jurisdiction is absent.

**Taxpayers' threshold path:** They seek recovery of collected charges for a distinct RFRA violation, within the refund action's jurisdiction, and invoke RFRA's application to federal statutes and its authorization of appropriate relief. They distinguish their self-assessed liability and claim denial of access to review. Those arguments require a construction of RFRA together with the actual specific review/remedy statutes; neither RFRA's broad substantive coverage nor its appropriate-relief phrase automatically establishes jurisdiction, an immunity waiver of unlimited scope, or entitlement to an injunction.

**Narrow procedural path:** Because the judgment indiscriminately rejects the claims on the merits while the interest and prospective-relief forum objections remain unaddressed, the Court can consider vacatur/remand for the required component-specific threshold determinations, without deciding those components' merits, while independently addressing any established refund jurisdiction. A remand is not a conclusion that a statutory bar is inapplicable, nor a command to grant relief or start a new limitations period. Current Steel Co. law, copied in the slice, prohibits assuming disputed Article III power to decide contested merits; it does not convert every statutory question into Article III or establish a universal order for every nonmerits issue.

This preparation supplies the official original section 7421 text, the original refund prerequisite and the section 6404 review enactment/transition. Exact full title 28 text was not recovered from the attempted official 1994 endpoints. The preserved section 2201/1346 positions are established by the original filings, not by a representation that those Code pages were read. A proposed definitive new construction of those provisions or an exception to their limits requires narrow primary-source completion before that path is finalized. That source limit does not require an invented factual premise, litigant default, or omission of the preserved threshold path.

## Effective Adams law and supported RFRA alternatives

Adams holds that the claimant must establish substantial burden; the Government then bears production and persuasion on a compelling interest and least restrictive means in the actual application. A general uniform-tax label, or failure to satisfy a Code waiver, does not complete RFRA. Adequate facts and legal reasons can suffice without a new study or automatic hearing. The Court separately vacated the collection, section 6651(a)(1) filing-addition and section 6654 RFRA determinations, awarding no exemption, earmark, waiver, refund or collection restraint. Federal RFRA's constitutional validity remained undecided. These holdings apply at their stated scope, not as an automatic disposition of Browne's different levy proposal and interest claims.

The Browne District Court assumed substantial burden while deciding legal sufficiency; the taxpayers attribute a concession for the pleadings motion to the Government. No completed trial finding of substantial burden is supplied. The Government's opposition acknowledged federal RFRA's continuing application after Boerne. The District Court's expressed constitutional doubt was followed by an assumed-validity merits decision; it is not an independent constitutional invalidation. The valid-field RFRA reasoning from Clearwater, Swanner and American Life League remains applicable as preserved in Adams, and Smith's constitutional rule is distinct.

**Path supporting the refund judgment:** Even applying Adams's burdens, the accepted proposal transfers initiation and collection work to the IRS and permits payment after the ordinary due dates. The Government may argue from the actual duties and the proposal's legal operation that on-demand compulsory collection does not preserve timely uniform contribution. Section 6651(a)(2) addresses timely payment; section 6654 addresses timely installments; interest compensates for the unpaid period. The Government may separately justify each against the actual request. A conclusion based on those legal characteristics does not require finding an invented dollar cost, but cannot rest solely on the word taxation or the existence of the principal liability. A broad failure-to-file rationale is unavailable because timely filing is conceded.

**Path supporting vacatur or limited RFRA relief:** The lower courts treated voluntary compliance and uniform participation as sufficient, and the District Court treated lawful liability as necessarily defeating all charge relief. The taxpayers allege ready assets, prompt disclosure and avoidable collection delay. Under Adams, a court must consider the claimed burden, the actual collection alternative, the different charges and genuinely comparable waivers under the proper burdens. On a judgment-on-the-pleadings record, disputed operational facts cannot be resolved against the taxpayers merely by calling ordinary collection burdens self-evident. If existing legal reasons do not suffice, further adjudication may be required. A direct refund or compulsory-levy order additionally requires jurisdiction, established RFRA entitlement and lawful remedial scope; no entitlement is supplied merely by the allegations or Adams's remand.

The distinction between the requested waiver of all ordinary consequences and a narrower claim concerning avoidable post-contact delay matters. Neither is silently substituted for the other. A partially justified charge or limited wrongful period would require its own analysis and lawful remedy; no blanket refund follows. Retaining a threshold or computation issue for ordinary proceedings does not certify a specific amount, that no costs were shifted, or that a levy by a particular date was legally possible.
