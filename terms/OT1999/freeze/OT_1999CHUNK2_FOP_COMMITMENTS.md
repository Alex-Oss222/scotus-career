### Commerce

Adopt Hiley's October 9, 1998 holding: the qualifying-recipient possession prohibition is a fitted anti-evasion and anti-impairment component of the valid federal prohibition on supplying firearms and ammunition to that recipient class. Excluding possession would materially impair that supply restriction by leaving acquisition and retention by the prohibited recipients beyond the complementary rule. The Commerce and Necessary and Proper Clauses permit the inclusion. Hiley already decided this statutory relationship; Hale and Kirk supply the necessary-inclusion method, not a universal possession power.

The strongest opposing path identifies local possession as noneconomic conduct and rejects reliance on a firearm's remote interstate journey or on generalized domestic-violence costs. That objection defeats the lower court's shortcut but not Hiley's distinct supply-and-recipient mechanism. Lopez requires connection before magnitude, judicially identifiable fit and a remaining constitutional boundary; this rule supplies the identified regime and relationship rather than aggregating crime costs. No new individual substantial-effects element or judicially inserted offense language follows.

Section 922(g) separately addresses interstate or foreign shipment or transportation, possession in or affecting commerce, and receipt of an item previously shipped or transported in interstate or foreign commerce. The government must establish the applicable enacted elements in an actual prosecution. The possession provision does not acquire section 922(d)(9)'s distinct knowledge-or-reasonable-cause supply element by implication. This facial holding establishes no prosecution-specific nexus or complete culpability rule. The section 925(a)(1) governmental-use exclusion remains as previously decided, without turning every public employee's private firearm into an official-use firearm.

### State administration

Adopt Hiley's no-command ground: the substantive disability does not direct a State or its officers to legislate, terminate an employee, prosecute federal offenses, conduct background checks, seize a firearm or appropriate funds. The supported record identifies federal enforcement, especially ATF. The reported November 26, 1996 letter advises an agency lacking seizure authority to contact ATF; it does not itself assign the State a compulsory enforcement task. A reduction in lawful armed-duty staffing options is a consequence of substantive federal regulation, not itself a duty to operate a federal program.

The strongest opposing path invokes the States' authority to select and equip officers and argues that the absence of an official-use exception practically controls that selection. Hiley rejects precisely that inference, while Garcia and Baker preserve valid direct regulation of state activity. The existing statutory exemption is not a constitutional baseline. New York's prohibition on compulsory legislation or compulsory ownership/liability remains intact.

Printz/Mack does not establish that every affirmative state-executive duty is categorically unconstitutional. Its controlling rule permits appropriate temporary records-based assistance within proper executive function under affirmative statutory direction while reserving the actual fiscal objections and broader administration. No such direction is necessary to uphold this statute: the present absence of an administrative command is sufficient. Neither the narrower permission nor the unresolved fiscal issues decide an absent investigation, seizure or permanent-administration order. If an actual agency instruction exceeds its statutory authority, its preserved application would require review on that ground before unnecessary constitutional decision; this record establishes no such command or liability.
