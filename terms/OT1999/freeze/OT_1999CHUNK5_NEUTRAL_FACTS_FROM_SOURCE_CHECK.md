## Shanklin

Argument was March 1, 2000. Federal funds paid 99 percent of the 1987 project installing warning signs at 196 crossings in 11 Tennessee counties. The collision occurred October 3, 1993. Advance warning signs and reflectorized crossbucks at this crossing were installed and complied with federal standards for those devices. The wrongful-death trial included the warning-device allegation and three other negligence allegations. The jury allocated 70 percent responsibility to Norfolk Southern and 30 percent to the decedent, assessed total damages of $615,379, and the district court entered $430,765.30. Respondent argued that (b)(3) conditions were present and that required pavement markings were absent; the neutral statement that no separate installed-device noncompliance claim is identified must not erase those arguments.

The full regulatory text includes 23 CFR 646.214(b)(3)(ii): in individual cases where a diagnostic team justifies that gates are not appropriate, FHWA **may** find the (b)(3)(i) requirements inapplicable. Paragraph (b)(4) separately subjects the warning-device choice to FHWA approval where the requirements of (b)(3) are not applicable. A legal-duty analysis must state these provisions separately. The FRSA local-hazard exception is not asserted as applicable here.

Support: published record recitation, 529 U.S. 344, 347–351 and regulatory note; App. 47–48, 128–134; petition appendix 3a, 28a–37a. No Supreme Court resolution of the preemption question is included here.

## Michael Wayne Williams

The compact neutral packet omits distinct claims and procedural steps material to disposition. Three hearing requests are presented: (1) a Brady claim concerning Cruse's pretrial psychiatric report; (2) juror Stinnett's nondisclosure of her prior marriage to prosecution witness Deputy Meinhard and prior representation by prosecutor Woodson in their divorce; and (3) prosecutorial nondisclosure concerning those relationships. A fourth claim alleged an undisclosed informal plea agreement with Cruse; the Fourth Circuit rejected it on the merits under 2254(d)(1), without reaching the hearing bar for that claim.

The district court initially granted a hearing on the juror, prosecutorial, and plea-agreement claims, but denied a hearing on the psychiatric-report claim. The Fourth Circuit intervened before a hearing and remanded for application of 2254(e)(2); the district court then vacated the hearing order and dismissed. On appeal the Fourth Circuit recognized a diligence threshold but found inadequate diligence; it also relied alternatively on pre-AEDPA cause and prejudice. Petitioner concedes he cannot satisfy 2254(e)(2)(B)'s clear-and-convincing innocence condition. Do not collapse this conceded condition into an omission in the research file or assume that the three hearing requests share identical diligence facts.

State counsel's own submission included Cruse's sentencing transcript referring repeatedly to the psychiatric report; a broad request for psychological examinations was denied absent a court order, after which no specific further effort is identified. In contrast, counsel sought funding for a jury investigation without information revealing Stinnett's concealed relationships; the motion was opposed and denied. Stinnett and Woodson supplied affidavits explaining their nondisclosures. Whether those explanations establish bias or misconduct remains a merits question. Further Virginia postconviction relief was unavailable when these relationships were discovered during federal habeas investigation.

Support: underlying-record and procedural recitations, 529 U.S. 420, 424–429, 437–445; App. 346–358, 388–389, 424, 483–499, 625–630. No Supreme Court disposition or diligence conclusion is imported here.

## Terry Williams

The state habeas trial judge was the same judge who imposed the death sentence; after hearings he recommended a new sentencing hearing on ineffective-assistance grounds. Virginia's Supreme Court had assumed habeas jurisdiction after a statutory amendment and required findings and recommendations from the circuit court. It rejected prejudice while assuming deficient performance. The federal district court granted relief; the Fourth Circuit reversed under its all-reasonable-jurists standard. The federal petition was filed in December 1997. Undiscovered or unpresented mitigation included severe childhood neglect and abuse, limited intellectual capacity, favorable conduct in prison and structured settings, and available character witnesses. Some juvenile records also contained unfavorable offenses; the omitted investigation cannot be described as uniformly favorable.
