## Bond v. United States — complete

**Historical event:** April 17, 2000; argued February 29, 2000; No. 98-9349; 529 U.S. 334. Fifth Circuit, 167 F.3d 225, reversed, 7–2.

**Judgment and proposition joins:** Rehnquist wrote the whole Court opinion, joined by Stevens, O'Connor, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, and Ginsburg. Breyer dissented, joined by Scalia. No partial joins or other writings.

**Controlling grounds and limits:** Probing tactile examination of Bond's opaque soft carry-on bag was a search violating his reasonable expectation of privacy. The fact that passengers or employees may move and handle an overhead bag does not expose it to exploratory manipulation as a matter of course. Place identifies luggage as a protected effect; Smith supplies subjective expectation and objective reasonableness; Ciraolo and Riley involved visual observation and do not decide this physical probing. The majority distinguishes ordinary handling by objective effect; the agent's subjective purpose is expressly irrelevant, including under Whren. The Government did not invoke Bond's later consent to opening the bag as an independent basis for admitting the evidence (Court footnote 1). The opinion therefore does not decide a consent/attenuation justification.

**Dissent:** Breyer and Scalia accept the reasonable-expectation framework but consider this squeeze ordinary, foreseeable manipulation in crowded luggage storage. They rely on the actual testimony, the trial court's finding of minimally intrusive touching, and public-exposure precedents. They reject an inference of greater intrusion solely from investigative purpose and dispute a categorical tactile/visual distinction. This is a disagreement over objective intrusion on the record, not an approval of suspicionless physical searches generally.

## Norfolk Southern Railway Co. v. Shanklin — complete

**Historical event:** April 17, 2000; argued March 1, 2000; No. 99-312; 529 U.S. 344. Sixth Circuit, 173 F.3d 386, reversed and remanded, 7–2.

**Judgment and proposition joins:** O'Connor wrote the whole Court opinion, joined by Rehnquist, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, and Breyer. Breyer also concurred separately. Ginsburg dissented, joined by Stevens. The supplied comparator omits Breyer's separate writing; it must be included in reconciliation.

**Controlling grounds and limits:** FRSA plus 23 CFR 646.214(b)(3)–(4) displaces state duties concerning warning-device adequacy when FHWA approves and funds the crossing improvement and the devices are installed and operating. The Court reads Easterwood as establishing a mandatory federal standard for federally funded devices. No separate diagnostic-team or individualized adequacy determination is necessary to trigger that displacement. Whether the State should have installed additional devices or conditions later changed does not revive the state adequacy claim. Other state duties are not disposed of simply by this warning-device holding. The regulatory text retains the (b)(3)(ii) exception: in an individual case where a diagnostic team justifies that gates are inappropriate, FHWA may find the automatic-gate requirements inapplicable; otherwise (b)(4) subjects the choice to FHWA approval where (b)(3) does not apply. The FRSA statutory local-hazard exception was stated to be inapplicable in this case. Federal funding does not prevent a State from installing more protective devices with its own funds or additional FHWA funds.

**Separate writings:** Breyer fully joins the Court while agreeing that policy ordinarily favors treating federal minimum standards as a floor. He treats Easterwood and the agency's earlier interpretation as decisive and emphasizes the agency's power to amend its regulation to distinguish minimum-protection programs. Ginsburg and Stevens read Easterwood as establishing only necessity, not sufficiency, of federal funding; they reject displacement without an actual determination that devices are adequate for the particular crossing and favor the reasoning of Shots. They do not regard the Government as estopped by its earlier amicus position.

**Sources:** [Cornell syllabus](https://www.law.cornell.edu/supct/html/99-312.ZS.html), [Court opinion](https://www.law.cornell.edu/supct/html/99-312.ZO.html), [Breyer concurrence](https://www.law.cornell.edu/supct/html/99-312.ZC.html), [Ginsburg dissent](https://www.law.cornell.edu/supct/html/99-312.ZD.html), [official Reports](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/USREPORTS-529/pdf/USREPORTS-529-344.pdf). Local prefix `shanklin-`. Historical cutoff: 2000-04-17.

## Williams v. Taylor (Michael Wayne Williams) — complete

**Historical event:** April 18, 2000; argued February 28, 2000; No. 99-6615; 529 U.S. 420. Fourth Circuit, 189 F.3d 421, **affirmed in part, reversed in part, and remanded**, unanimously. The supplied comparator's disposition omits the affirmance.

**Judgment and proposition joins:** Kennedy wrote for all nine Justices: Rehnquist, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg, Breyer. No separate writing or qualification of a join.

**Component disposition and limits:** Under the opening clause of 2254(e)(2), a prisoner has failed to develop the facts only if lack of diligence or greater fault is attributable to him or his counsel. Diligence concerns reasonable effort given information then available, not hindsight proof that investigation would succeed. Ordinarily it includes seeking a state evidentiary hearing in the manner state law requires. A diligent prisoner need not satisfy the subsection's further exceptions; a prisoner at fault must meet its remaining stringent conditions. The Court identifies Keeney as the source of the threshold distinction while recognizing that AEDPA raises the excusal bar and eliminates a freestanding miscarriage-of-justice hearing exception.

The denial of a hearing on the psychiatric-report Brady claim is affirmed: state counsel had notice of the report in Cruse's sentencing transcript, made only a general request, and did not pursue it after the prosecutor demanded a court order; petitioner concedes he cannot satisfy 2254(e)(2)(B). Denial of a hearing on juror bias and prosecutorial misconduct is reversed: Stinnett's and Woodson's nondisclosures concealed facts, and counsel reasonably requested investigatory resources without a factual reason to inspect this juror's divorce history. This does not impose a state duty to finance undeveloped claims. Further state relief was unavailable when the facts emerged. The same analysis establishes cause for any procedural default, but the appropriate prejudice standard and ultimate merits remain for further proceedings. The separate alleged undisclosed informal plea-agreement claim was rejected below under 2254(d)(1); Part IV expressly does not reach whether 2254(e)(2) permits a hearing on it.

Local prefix `williams-michael-`. Historical cutoff: 2000-04-18.

## Williams v. Taylor (Terry Williams) — complete

**Historical event:** April 18, 2000; argued October 4, 1999; No. 98-8384; 529 U.S. 362. Fourth Circuit, 163 F.3d 860, reversed and remanded, 6–3. Reversal: Stevens, O'Connor, Kennedy, Souter, Ginsburg, Breyer. Rehnquist, Scalia, Thomas would deny habeas relief.

**Exact writing topology:**

| Writing/portion | Author and joins | Historical force |
|---|---|---|
| Stevens I, III, IV | Stevens; O'Connor, Kennedy, Souter, Ginsburg, Breyer | Six-Justice Court opinion: posture, clearly established Strickland rule, deficiency and prejudice, erroneous use of Fretwell, entitlement to relief |
| Stevens II, V | Stevens; Souter, Ginsburg, Breyer | Four-Justice opinion; not the Court's controlling general interpretation of 2254(d)(1) |
| O'Connor II except legislative-history footnote | O'Connor; Rehnquist, Scalia, Kennedy, Thomas | Five-Justice Court opinion interpreting 2254(d)(1) |
| O'Connor II legislative-history footnote | O'Connor; Rehnquist, Kennedy, Thomas | Four Justices; Scalia expressly withholds join |
| O'Connor remaining portions | O'Connor; Kennedy | Concurrence in part and in judgment; Kennedy joins her entire writing |
| Rehnquist concurrence/dissent | Rehnquist; Scalia, Thomas | Agrees with O'Connor's statutory standard; rejects habeas relief on its application |

**Controlling propositions and reservations:** O'Connor's five-Justice standard gives independent work to contrary-to and unreasonable-application clauses. Contrary-to covers a contrary legal rule or opposite result on materially indistinguishable facts. Unreasonable application covers objectively unreasonable application of the correct governing Supreme Court principle to the facts; error alone is insufficient. The Fourth Circuit's all-reasonable-jurists formulation cannot become a subjective vote count. Clearly established law means Supreme Court holdings rather than dicta as of the relevant state-court decision. The Court expressly reserves the treatment of cases characterized as unreasonable extension or refusal to extend a legal principle to a new context. The two statutory clauses are not rendered mechanically exclusive by this holding.

Stevens's six-Justice merits portions hold Strickland clearly established and find both deficient investigation and a reasonable probability of a different sentencing result. Fretwell did not supplant Strickland: it denies reliance on an outcome advantage to which substantive or procedural law gives no entitlement; Nix similarly excludes prejudice based on foregone perjury. Those unusual situations do not add a separate fundamental-unfairness prerequisite where counsel withheld lawful mitigation. The state court also unreasonably failed to assess the totality of mitigation against aggravation, including moral culpability apart from future dangerousness. Rehnquist, Scalia, and Thomas believe the state court actually applied Strickland and reasonably found the strong dangerousness evidence outweighed the omitted mitigation. Stevens's four-Justice statutory discussion would retain independent federal judgment after careful attention to the state decision; it cannot be presented as the Court's adopted general standard.
