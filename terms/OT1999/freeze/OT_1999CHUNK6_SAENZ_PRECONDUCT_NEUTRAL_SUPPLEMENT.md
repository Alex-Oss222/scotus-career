## Ross v. United States, 910 F.2d 1422 (7th Cir. 1990)

Ross reviewed pleading dismissals and an individual deputy's immunity summary judgment, taking the favorable allegations as true rather than finding them proved. A drowning twelve-year-old could allegedly have been rescued by equipped, qualified people already present. A deputy used threats of arrest and his patrol boat to prevent their intervention while waiting twenty minutes for the officially designated divers. The court assumed for the appeal that timely intervention would have saved the child. The county's allegedly categorical policy barred that available rescue without providing a meaningful alternative. The court reinstated the county/sheriff and deputy claims, but upheld the dismissal of the city whose personnel merely failed to provide public rescue services and the separate federal landowner claim.

The deputy's individual immunity was rejected under Anderson's particularized-notice inquiry. The court held that by August 11, 1985 a reasonable officer would understand that arbitrary use of police authority to cut off available lifesaving rescue, with knowledge of an immediate substantial risk of death and no meaningful alternative, violated due process. It relied on the affirmative-interference discussions in Jackson v. City of Joliet and Jackson v. Byrne. It did not demand malice: on the alleged facts, knowing disregard of the substantial risk could establish constitutional recklessness. It did not turn every negligent rescue decision or private danger into a constitutional injury. Qualified rescuers, known imminent peril, effective obstruction and actual causal deprivation all mattered. The plaintiff still had to prove the allegations, causation, culpability and policy attribution.

For Saenz, Ross supports the proposition that lack of custody does not categorically immunize an official's own coercive destruction of available protection. Its force is limited by the different protection allegedly removed: existing qualified rescue of an identified drowning child, rather than a junior officer's proposed discretionary investigation of a driver before contact with unidentified road users. That distinction must be evaluated; it is neither an automatic factual-twin requirement nor proof that all affirmative police commands are constitutional deprivations. Ross's municipal analysis also confirms that an officer's individual immunity and the existence of a municipal constitutional wrong are separate questions.

## Salas v. Carpenter, 980 F.2d 299 (5th Cir. 1992)

Salas reviewed an interlocutory denial of the sheriff's dismissal and immunity summary-judgment motions. The sheriff dismissed better equipped city SWAT and hostage negotiators but his own officers simultaneously secured the scene and negotiated for the hostage's release. His team was inexperienced and poorly equipped; the hostage was killed by her private captor. The Fifth Circuit held that replacing one attempted rescue with another was materially different from Ross's blocking rescue without a meaningful alternative. Failed or inferior protection did not become no protection simply because the attempt failed. It rejected both the constitutional claim and, independently, the argument that the sheriff's conduct violated sufficiently clear law as of August 1989. The county remained a separate defendant below.

Salas accepted the possibility that arbitrary prevention of rescue with the requisite culpability could violate due process. It did not establish a general state-created-danger cause of action. Its separate immunity analysis considered both uncertain law and the facts reasonably known to the official. Anderson required apparent unlawfulness without an identical prior decision; reasonably disputed legality could support immunity. Its statement about uncertainty in August 1989 cannot simply be reused as a finding about June 1995. Its evidentiary discussion excluded an expert's conclusory mental-state labels while permitting expertise about proper rescue methods; it did not make culpability immune from circumstantial proof.

For Saenz, the strongest plaintiff distinction is that Gonzalez allegedly prevented Martinez's intervention and supplied no substitute protective action, whereas Salas involved ongoing negotiations. The strongest defense distinction is that stopping a subordinate's proposed public investigation is not necessarily destruction of private self-help or rescue of a person already in known mortal peril. Salas treated the availability and character of replacement protection, the official's actual conduct, and what he could reasonably understand as material. Neither inferior public service nor a semantic distinction between action and inaction answers the constitutional question.
