## Common scope and the disputed rule

All eight Associates participate and reach the presented federal question. Counts 7–10 concern the fourteen- or fifteen-year-old complainant and offenses from June 1992 through July 1993. Under the offense-date rule, uncorroborated testimony was sufficient only following a report to another person within six months; the exception for complainants younger than fourteen did not apply. The amendment effective September 1, 1993 extended the age exception to those younger than eighteen and the report period to one year. The March 1995 report satisfies neither period for these counts. Thus the age-exception change, rather than selection between the two report periods, presents the operative retrospective issue. The six younger-age counts and five post-amendment counts, including the life sentences, remain outside this challenge.

The lack of corroboration is assumed for the legal question; it is not finally established. Texas preserves its contention that actual corroboration supported the convictions. Every Associate declining the amendment's retrospective use therefore supports a remand for that existing-record issue under the older law, not an immediate acquittal based on an invented evidentiary finding. If the record fails the older sufficiency requirement, the stated state-law consequence is acquittal on the affected counts, not a new opportunity to add prosecution proof. No blanket new-trial order or release from unaffected sentences follows.

Two supported paths remain. The first treats removal of the corroboration condition as a reduction in legally sufficient proof, within Calder's fourth category as preserved and bounded by Collins, Hopt and Thompson. The second treats the amendment as removal of a witness-specific credibility restriction comparable to the permissible competence and admissibility changes in Hopt and Thompson, leaving ultimate facts and proof beyond reasonable doubt unchanged. Neither path may rest simply on procedural labels, disadvantage, the worth of the former policy, or the continuing legality of the underlying conduct.

## Individual commitments and enhanced comparison

### Stevens

**Result:** Reverse the constitutional ruling sustaining retrospective application to counts 7–10 and remand for the preserved corroboration issue under offense-date Article 38.07.

**Ground:** The legislature may change ordinary trial methods, but may not retrospectively make the complainant's testimony alone sufficient when the former law made additional proof indispensable absent its reporting or age conditions. The witness was already competent and the testimony admissible; what changes is the legal sufficiency of the prosecution's existing proof.

**Authority and individual support:** His Collins concurrence, 497 U.S. 37, 52–61, distinguishes a new corrective remedy for an already valid conviction from a change in safeguards used to obtain a valid conviction. His Morales join and Garner separate position protect the actual operation of an existing legal safeguard rather than the mere hope of a favorable outcome. Those parole positions do not independently decide trial proof; Calder's fourth category and Hopt's sufficiency reservation supply the necessary trial-law ground here.

**Adversarial comparison:** The competence analogy is strongest because the corroboration rule expresses distrust of a particular class of testimony. It fails for him at the legal operation: the same admitted testimony that previously required acquittal can now support conviction without the formerly indispensable additional evidence. Thompson adds an admissible comparison source and leaves evaluation to the jury; it does not remove an independently prescribed minimum basis for conviction. The contrary concern that any useful evidentiary reform could be frozen is answered by this distinction, not by denying the validity of Hopt or Thompson.

**Join barriers and alternatives:** He can join a bounded Calder/Hopt sufficiency rule without reviving the substantial-protection formulation rejected by the Collins majority. His broader published concern may explain a concurrence, but is not necessary to the judgment. He rejects both automatic acquittal before the corroboration issue is resolved and a prosecutorial repair trial.

### O'Connor

**Result:** Affirm the rejection of this Ex Post Facto challenge. No remand to test offense-date corroboration is required by that federal ruling.

**Ground:** The amendment removes a special credibility disability associated with the complainant's age and delayed report; it does not alter the offense's ultimate facts, the jury's reasonable-doubt burden or the lawful punishment. Hopt and Thompson permit retrospective changes in the evidentiary means by which the unchanged facts may be proved.

**Authority and individual support:** She joined Collins's Court opinion, including its refusal to treat every substantial procedural disadvantage as forbidden and its express retention of ordinary evidentiary changes. Her Morales Court opinion and Garner Court opinion require a concrete effect within the Clause's punishment protection; they do not constitutionalize every former procedural advantage. Her trial-proof conclusion is an application of Collins and Hopt, not a transfer of a parole vote.

**Adversarial comparison:** The defendant's strongest point is that Texas labels failure to corroborate legal insufficiency and requires acquittal. She treats that remedy as evidence of the rule's force but not as controlling its federal constitutional character: restrictions on testimony can be outcome-determinative without defining the offense-date amount of proof in the constitutional sense. The age and report alternatives regulate a source's reliability, while guilt still requires persuasion beyond reasonable doubt. An actual reduction in that burden or a change to ultimate facts would present a different case.

**Join barriers and alternatives:** She will not join a categorical procedural exemption or deny that some laws described as evidence rules fall within Calder. She also will not infer actual corroboration or decide an unpresented state-law claim. A contrary majority's remand must preserve Texas's contention and confine any acquittal consequence to the challenged counts.

### Scalia

**Result:** Reverse the adverse federal ruling on counts 7–10 and remand for the preserved existing-record corroboration question.

**Ground:** Calder's fourth category, expressly recounted in Collins, remains distinct from a generic prohibition on disadvantage. A law that removes a required evidentiary minimum permits conviction with less proof; the exception for ordinary competence or admissibility changes does not erase that category.

**Authority and individual support:** His Collins majority join includes both its limited account of the Clause and its Calder/Hopt qualifications. His Garner join demonstrates adherence to the operative controlling boundary without manufacturing an actual-outcome requirement; it supplies no separate trial-proof rule. Hopt, 110 U.S. 574, 588–590, expressly leaves the required amount or degree of proof untouched when permitting the new witness. Thompson, 171 U.S. 380, 386–388, likewise distinguishes admitting new comparison evidence from reducing required proof.

**Adversarial comparison:** Texas's strongest argument is that its rule regulates only credibility and the formal reasonable-doubt standard survives. For him, the statute's legal command is decisive: without the timely report or age exception, testimony alone cannot establish guilt even if the jury believes it. Removing that command changes legal sufficiency, not merely admissible sources. Limiting the fourth category to a changed verbal burden would leave its evidence language without independent work.

**Join barriers and alternatives:** No general substantial-rights test, no restoration of Kring or Thompson v. Utah, and no constitutional rule preserving every witness restriction. He supports the existing-record remand; a rule requiring proof that corroboration would have changed the jury's mind would mistake the statutory sufficiency requirement.

### Kennedy

**Result:** Affirm the rejection of the presented federal challenge.

**Ground:** Article 38.07's age/report conditions are witness-specific reliability restrictions. Their removal operates like the competence restriction in Hopt and the exclusion in Thompson rather than a lowered element or burden of persuasion. The Ex Post Facto Clause protects defined crime, punishment and constitutionally relevant proof requirements, not every evidentiary advantage existing at the offense date.

**Authority and individual support:** His Collins majority join rejects a free-standing substantial-protection category while retaining Calder's finite categories. His Garner join preserves the binding Morales opportunity rule on its concrete punishment premise, illustrating why a procedural rule's function matters. Hopt and Thompson provide the decisive trial-method analogy; no own earlier published ruling on this precise Texas corroboration provision is attributed to him.

**Adversarial comparison:** The claimant's strongest response is the acquittal consequence under Texas law. Kennedy does not dismiss it as harmless, but concludes that an evidentiary exclusion can equally foreclose a conviction and still be changed under Thompson. Treating the State's label or chosen appellate remedy as dispositive would make equivalent credibility rules constitutionally different according to drafting form. He reserves a true reduction in proof of the charged facts or in the degree of persuasion.

**Join barriers and alternatives:** No claim that every procedural amendment escapes scrutiny; no finding the preserved corroboration contention has been proved; no use of unaffected sentences to dismiss the challenge as moot. He could accept the limited remand mechanics if the Court rejects his merits view without joining the invalidation rationale.

### Souter

**Result:** Reverse the constitutional ruling for counts 7–10 and remand under the offense-date requirement.

**Ground:** Removing the need for corroboration under these age and report predicates withdraws a legally required minimum evidentiary basis for guilt. The distinction is between admitting a source that a jury may evaluate and declaring already admissible proof legally sufficient without the additional evidence previously required.

**Authority and individual support:** His Morales join and Garner Court-opinion join insist on identifying what the earlier law actually guaranteed and what the later law withdrew. They also preserve Collins's refusal to constitutionalize disadvantage alone. Here the guarantee is not parole procedure but a conviction prerequisite independently recognized by Calder, Hopt and Thompson. Those authorities supply the trial-specific basis.

**Adversarial comparison:** The credibility characterization is plausible and the continuing reasonable-doubt standard significant. But if the amendment applies, the formerly conclusive legal deficiency disappears without any new evidence. Hopt's newly competent witness and Thompson's newly available exemplar do not establish that result. Neither the reporting exception nor the age exception makes the former condition less binding when both are absent.

**Join barriers and alternatives:** Do not require that the witness be disbelieved or characterize her testimony as inherently unreliable. The rule concerns legislative sufficiency, not the truth of her accusation. Do not import Morales's burden allocation or a parole-risk formula into this trial inquiry. Preserve the State's actual-record argument on remand.

### Thomas

**Result:** Reverse the adverse federal ruling for counts 7–10 and remand on the existing record under the earlier rule.

**Ground:** The fourth Calder category retains an operative evidentiary minimum distinct from the other categories. The later statute authorizes conviction without additional evidence the offense-date law required when neither exception applied. Ordinary witness-competence reform remains permissible.

**Authority and individual support:** Calder's historical formulation, as adopted and confined in Collins, and Hopt's express distinction supply the ground. His Garner join is compatible with applying an established legal protection at its exact scope but does not establish the trial result. No participation in Collins or own preexisting trial-corroboration holding is invented.

**Adversarial comparison:** The State's strongest position is that no offense element or reasonable-doubt standard changed. That does not answer the separately recognized category governing a reduction in required proof. Hopt cannot be read to abolish the very distinction it uses to uphold witness competence. Conversely, treating all new evidence as forbidden would contradict both Hopt and Thompson and is rejected.

**Join barriers and alternatives:** The adopted rule must rest on the identified historical category, not an open-ended substantial-rights balancing test. No fresh test, numerical proof threshold or universal constitutional corroboration requirement follows. The State may establish compliance with the older law from the trial record; it receives no automatic chance to supplement deficient proof.

### Ginsburg

**Result:** Affirm the rejection of this Ex Post Facto challenge.

**Ground:** The amendment removes a special restriction upon the use of a particular witness's testimony, analogous in constitutional function to Hopt's removal of a witness disability. The jury must still find the same criminal acts beyond reasonable doubt. The legislature's alteration of the age/report categories does not necessarily prescribe a smaller quantum of persuasion or substitute a new offense fact.

**Authority and individual support:** Her Morales join and Garner separate position protect an actually withdrawn avenue to release and reject a purely formal assurance of an adequate replacement. Those decisions require examination of operation rather than labels; they do not decide corroboration. Here Hopt and Thompson supply a permissible evidentiary-reform path, and Collins supplies the restriction on general procedural-disadvantage reasoning. The supplied sources contain no Ginsburg preexisting holding specifically classifying this kind of corroboration rule; this commitment is a stated inference from the actual alternatives, not an invented prior vote or a claimed quotation.

**Adversarial comparison:** The defendant's best objection is that the same record changes from legally insufficient to potentially sufficient. That consequence also attends permissible removal of categorical witness disabilities, and the Texas remedy alone does not determine constitutional classification. The special age and reporting triggers indicate a credibility rule rather than a uniform requirement to establish guilt through a specified quantity of independently sufficient evidence. She preserves the fourth category for an actual reduction in constitutionally relevant proof requirements and does not erase it through a blanket procedural label.

**Join barriers and alternatives:** No assumption that the State actually corroborated the testimony, no criticism of a victim or invention of a legislative motive, and no displacement of Morales or Garner. If the contrary sufficiency distinction controls, the unresolved corroboration contention forecloses an immediate acquittal directive.

### Breyer

**Result:** Reverse the constitutional ruling on counts 7–10 and remand for actual-record application of the earlier rule.

**Ground:** The practical and legal change is that a conviction becomes available with an evidentiary basis formerly insufficient as a matter of law. That is distinct from improving the jury's information through new admissible evidence. The proof requirement can be protected without freezing ordinary trial administration.

**Authority and individual support:** His Garner join applies an existing guarantee according to its demonstrated operation while leaving ordinary procedure and ultimate constitutional proof intact. That analogy is limited; Collins, Hopt and Thompson supply the trial-proof distinction. He was not a member of the Collins Court and no such join is claimed.

**Adversarial comparison:** The State's strongest argument is that allowing a new witness and removing corroboration can have comparable effects on trial outcomes. The relevant difference is legal sufficiency, not a prediction about those outcomes: Hopt and Thompson permit additional material to be evaluated, while the amendment makes the old material enough without the extra evidentiary support. Preserving that line gives ordinary reform room to operate and prevents retrospective legislative relaxation of an essential conviction condition.

**Join barriers and alternatives:** No statistical probability inquiry, no requirement to show actual innocence or a likely different verdict, and no automatic acquittal on an assumed fact. He joins the bounded existing-record remand and preservation of the eleven unaffected counts.
