## Event, questions and participation

Post-grant merits review of the Second Circuit's January 29, 1999 judgment, 166 F.3d 507, affirming summary judgment in S.D.N.Y. No. 97 CIV. 3205(RWS), 996 F.Supp.343 (May 7, 1998). The supplied authorized calendar gives grant January 31, 2000, argument March 27, 2000, decision event April 24, 2000. These Supreme Court dates are scheduling premises, not newly imported historical orders. Ordinary review is available under 28 U.S.C. §1254(1). All nine roster members were seated at argument and event; no case-specific nonparticipation is established; six is quorum and five of nine control judgment.

Granted: (1) whether private physicians and hospitals act under color of state law when invoking a state statute authorizing involuntary psychiatric commitment; (2) whether delegated confinement authority, regulation and the State's enforcement role make this deprivation fairly attributable to the State. The second question does not establish that a state officer actually enforced this commitment. The underlying Fourth/Fourteenth Amendment merits, damages and state tort claims are not themselves resolved by deciding attribution.

## Supported record and posture

Doe's private insurer referred her to Rosenberg, a private physician with hospital privileges. After an initial examination and a missed privately arranged psychiatric appointment, she returned for blood-test results. Rosenberg described psychotic behavior and danger to herself; Doe denied agitation or dangerousness and explained that she left to preserve privacy and reach another appointment. These are conflicting accounts, not adjudicated findings that she was dangerous.

Rosenberg wrote a medical order directing hospital security to take Doe to Columbia Presbyterian Medical Center for evaluation. He also signed conditional authorization for four-point restraints, but the published record states those restraints were not used. The actors identified as transporting her were hospital security; the record does not identify police, a sheriff or another state official carrying out this transport. Rosenberg was not a hospital employee and did not supply the commitment certificates.

At the private hospital, Skomorossky and Innes certified involuntary admission, finding dangerousness and inadequate alternatives; staff physician Arcow confirmed their evaluations. Commitment occurred May 5, 1996 under New York Mental Hygiene Law §9.27. Doe obtained review under §9.31 and was ordered released May 16; the hospital released her that day. The later court-ordered release is not prior governmental approval of the physicians' commitment choice. All named physicians were private; the hospital held Office of Mental Health licenses for its psychiatric services and comprehensive psychiatric emergency program. The district opinion additionally describes a contractual relationship permitting its psychiatric wing; it identifies no state command to commit Doe.

Doe sued under §1983 for Fourth/Fourteenth Amendment violations and asserted pendent claims including false imprisonment, informed-consent, treatment-choice and confidentiality claims. The district court granted defendants summary judgment because state action was absent, then declined supplemental jurisdiction under §1367(c)(3). The Second Circuit affirmed for substantially those reasons and rejected a challenge to discovery rulings under abuse-of-discretion review. The discovery issue is not independently listed in the supplied grant; no fresh discovery entitlement should be inferred.

## Lower-court reasons, distinguished from facts

The district court applied compulsion/significant encouragement, close nexus/joint participation and traditionally exclusive public function. It understood §9.27 as permissive authority with medical discretion, licensing and regulation as insufficient without responsibility for the particular decision, and New York's history as including private confinement before and alongside public institutions. It distinguished Edmonson's government-administered jury mechanism and West's contracted care of persons already in state custody. It separately treated Rosenberg's referral/transport as one step removed from commitment.

The court's statements that the physicians exercised legally independent judgment, that dangerousness is a medical rather than social question, that historical private confinement defeats exclusivity, and that transport is not a significant liberty deprivation are legal reasoning, not facts binding this Court. The granted attribution question permits scrutiny of that reasoning; affirmance need not endorse the transport-liberty statement or a blanket rule for all private commitments. Doe's expert described dangerousness as a social assessment; the district court rejected that submission as bearing on statutory interpretation. No general exemption for medical judgment follows from that evidentiary treatment.

## Entering law and competing supported paths

The slice supplies American Manufacturers v. Sullivan (March 3, 1999) and Standards and Tests, **Private payment choices and governmental attribution** (effective March 3, 1999): statutory permission, regulation and public review alone do not attribute an initial private choice, while coercion, significant encouragement, joint participation and exclusive public function retain independent predicates. Sullivan concerns payment withholding and does not itself decide private detention. Blum, Rendell-Baker, Jackson, Flagg Brothers and Lugar remain date-eligible; Edmonson and West supply materially different governmental participation and duty-delegation contexts. Sullivan's published proposition-level joins are in the slice and may inform modeling without importing any position in this case.

Affirmance path: identify the challenged private decisions separately, apply Sullivan to the actual absence of state command or joint decision, and evaluate the specific public-function history. Statutory limits and judicial review can protect against unlawful confinement without converting every private invocation into governmental action. The strongest objection is that civil commitment entails deprivation of liberty legally authorized by the State, not merely selection of medical treatment; calling the precipitating judgment medical cannot answer whether the resulting confinement implements delegated coercive authority.

Reversal/vacatur path: distinguish detention from Sullivan's initial payment choice; evaluate whether New York delegates a governmental power to make immediately operative confinement decisions, or whether the statutory machinery and licensing relationship make that specific deprivation governmental. West establishes that professional discretion does not always defeat attribution when a governmental duty is delegated; Edmonson distinguishes ordinary private action from privately exercised government-created authority embedded in public machinery. The strongest objections are the absence of actual state officers in this confinement, historical private commitment, and Lugar's separation of state-created authority from sufficient attribution. Statutory authorization alone cannot simply be renamed joint participation. A legally adequate delegation theory must state why this function satisfies the governing rule.

Narrower paths include separate analysis of Rosenberg's preparatory transport and the hospital's commitment; an attribution remand on a supported legal error cannot automatically establish dangerousness, unconstitutional confinement, damages or tort liability. A hypothetical official command, joint decision, state-officer enforcement, legally final delegated adjudication or public hospital remains outside the established facts. Neither ordinary medical discretion nor statutory permission is an absolute immunity rule.

## Statutory precision, remedies and limits

The lower opinion quotes §9.27(a)'s permission to receive and retain a person alleged mentally ill and in need of involuntary care upon two examining physicians' separate certificates and an application, even where examinations occur jointly. It describes §9.27(d)'s requirement to consider alternatives. It distinguishes §9.27's facial text from New York judicial decisions requiring dangerousness; do not describe dangerousness as express language in §9.27(a). The definition of need for involuntary care in §9.01 requires illness making hospital care essential to welfare and impairment preventing understanding of that need. The distinct serious-harm definition addresses substantial physical danger to self or others through the described conduct; it is not permission to commit any person merely judged difficult or unusual. Sections 9.39 (emergency admission), 31.02 and 31.27 (licensing) are separate provisions, not interchangeable authorizations for Doe's §9.27 commitment.

Affirmance preserves federal summary judgment and discretionary dismissal of pendent claims, without deciding their merits. Reversal of attribution removes that ground for federal dismissal and permits ordinary proceedings on remaining elements and defenses; dependent treatment of pendent claims must be reconsidered to the extent necessary. No present damages, new commitment, release order, mandatory discovery, evidentiary hearing or generalized statutory invalidation follows automatically. The supplied grant does not independently reopen the discovery ruling.
