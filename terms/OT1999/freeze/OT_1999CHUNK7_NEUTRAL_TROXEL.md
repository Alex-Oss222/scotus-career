## 3. Judgment below and competing reasoning

The state majority treated parental care and upbringing as a fundamental liberty, required a compelling justification tailored to the intrusion, and held these statutes deficient because they permitted any person to seek visitation at any time without a harm threshold, a substantial-relationship requirement or prescribed consideration of parental reasons. It recognized that severing an established substantial third-party relationship could cause severe psychological harm, while finding that the statutes did not require such a showing. It rejected the proposition that a judge's view of a better arrangement alone supplies the required justification. Its invalidation was broader than a conclusion limited to this trial court's treatment of Granville.

The state dissent agreed that the statutes authorized the petitions. It distinguished temporary, limited visitation from termination or transfer of custody, regarded the child's welfare and existing ties as independent interests, and argued that a contextual best-interests inquiry could protect them without a universal prior finding of parental unfitness or harm. It treated parental objections, relationship strength and other relevant circumstances as matters available within that inquiry, rather than omitted mandatory statutory elements. It would reinstate the Troxel visitation order. That state dissent's speculation that a denial of certiorari elsewhere signaled constitutional approval supplies no legal authority here.

Neither state opinion supplies the Supreme Court's answer. The statutes' enactment commands, a judicial construction, a lower factual finding and an advocate's proposed safeguard must remain distinct. The lower opinion also remanded fee matters for financial-need and ability-to-pay determinations; this supplies no independent federal fee disposition or entitlement in the present granted question.

Granville and Brad Troxel were unmarried parents of two daughters. After their separation, the children visited their paternal grandparents through their father. He died in May 1993. The mother continued some visitation and then sought to limit it. The grandparents petitioned in December 1993 for two overnight weekends each month and two summer weeks. Granville proposed one day per month without overnight stays, and family holiday participation. The trial court ordered one weekend monthly, one summer week, and four hours on each grandparent's birthday. The dispute was over amount and conditions, not a complete refusal of contact. The mother later married and her husband adopted the children. No allegation or finding of parental unfitness is identified in the published recitation. This absence is not an independent present adjudication of every parental-capacity question.

The Court of Appeals reversed and dismissed on its construction requiring a pending custody action. The Washington Supreme Court rejected that construction but affirmed dismissal on federal constitutional grounds in the consolidated lower proceeding. Only the Troxel/Granville visitation dispute is presented here. The state court's facial construction and reasoning remain distinct from the concrete trial application; no federal disposition is supplied.

The trial court's written findings, reproduced in the published recitation from Appendix page 70a, described the grandparents' large local loving family and opportunities involving cousins and music; they identified benefit from quality time balanced with the children's nuclear family, including the mother's and stepfather's other children. These findings did not identify parental unfitness or an actual harm from the mother's proposed schedule. The oral ruling invoked ordinarily beneficial grandparent contact unless the grandparents' lifestyle would adversely affect the children; the court said it had not been shown that visitation was contrary to their interests. A further oral explanation relied on the judge's favorable childhood summer experiences with grandparents. These are descriptions of the trial judge's stated reasons, not this packet's constitutional evaluation. The competing legal implications of that treatment of the parent's choice remain for independent modeling.
