## Operative structure, with the two versions kept distinct

**Scope and purpose.** The 1984 automatic-protection amendment concerns passenger cars and front outboard seats; it does not impose that amendment's automatic-protection requirement on front-center or rear seats, or on trucks, tractors, or multipurpose vehicles (28963, 28996). That is distinct from Standard 208's general application to passenger cars, multipurpose passenger vehicles, trucks, and buses, and its additional pressure-vessel/explosive-device coverage reproduced in the 1986 appendix. The standard's stated purpose is to reduce occupant deaths and injury severity through vehicle-crashworthiness requirements measured by dummy forces and accelerations in test crashes and equipment requirements for active and passive restraint systems (appendix 3–4). The exempted passenger-car seats retain the belt requirements stated in the applicable option; exemption from automatic protection does not mean exemption from every restraint requirement.

**Three compliance options.** S4.1.2.1 requires automatic frontal/angular protection at each front outboard position under S5.1; front-center and rear positions have conforming Type 1 or Type 2 belts. Lateral and rollover protection is furnished either automatically under S5.2/S5.3 or through the specified conforming front-outboard belt assemblies, with the specified S5.1 testing using those belts in addition to automatic protection. S4.1.2.2 permits the narrower head-on automatic system, with the prescribed belts at each seating position, perpendicular-impact testing both with automatic protection and with the Type 1 belt, and the front-outboard warning system. S4.1.2.3 permits the lap-and-shoulder-belt/warning system: except for convertibles and open-body vehicles, front outboard belts must be either the conforming Type 2 assembly with nondetachable shoulder belt or a Type 1 assembly meeting the specified perpendicular-impact requirement; center-front and other seats retain their separately stated belt/warning requirements. Convertibles and open-body vehicles have the distinct all-position Type 1/Type 2 belt and front-position warning requirements reproduced at appendix 7. The appendix retains a numbering defect in the latter heading; its operative words, not a silently repaired heading, supply this description. The original 1984 amendments reproduce the first two options but leave the third option incorporated by reference; the 1986 appendix reproduces it. None of these options is simply an airbag mandate.

**Original 1984 phase-in.** For cars manufactured September 1, 1986 through August 31, 1987, S4.1.3.1.1 permits the three options subject to the phase-in and credit provisions. S4.1.3.1.2 requires S4.1.2.1 compliance for an amount not less than 10 percent of each manufacturer's average annual passenger-car production during September 1, 1983 through August 31, 1986, subject to the mandatory-use-law condition. The next periods require 25 percent using the September 1984–August 1987 production average and 40 percent using the September 1985–August 1988 average. S4.1.3.4 counts a car whose driver's position meets the automatic requirements by a nonbelt means as 1.5 vehicles. The contemporaneous explanation expressly requires automatic protection at the right-front passenger position for that extra credit, although the same nonbelt technology need not serve both positions (29000, 29009–29010).

**1986 manufacture-date calculation.** The reproduced S4.1.3.1.2 allows the 10-percent threshold to use either the preceding three-year annual-production average just described or the manufacturer's annual production during September 1, 1986 through August 31, 1987. The 25- and 40-percent provisions likewise permit their stated prior-average or current-production alternatives. The 1986 credit provision distinguishes (a)(1), which counts as 1.5 a car with driver automatic frontal protection by means not including a belt and right-front automatic frontal protection by any means, from (a)(2), which counts as one a car with that nonbelt driver protection and a manual Type 2 belt at the right-front seat. The original 1984 explanation's right-front automatic condition therefore must not be recast as a prohibition on the later one-vehicle category.

For the first phase-in year, a car manufactured September 1, 1985 through August 31, 1986 may also count if it complies with S4.1.2.1. For the second year, a complying car manufactured September 1, 1985 through August 31, 1987 may count only if not already counted toward the first year. For the third, a complying car manufactured September 1, 1985 through August 31, 1988 may count only if not counted toward either earlier year. These are the separate S4.1.3.4(b)–(d) conditions, not an unrestricted right to reuse credits. Passenger cars produced by more than one manufacturer are attributed to a single manufacturer: imported cars to the importer, domestically manufactured cars to a participating manufacturer that markets them, subject to an express written contract assigning the car to one manufacturer and reported under Part 585. The reproduction's truncated numbering does not eliminate that contractual qualification (appendix 8–12).

The 1986 reproduction also says a vehicle shall not be deemed noncompliant if its manufacturer establishes that it had no reason to know, in the exercise of due care, of the nonconformity. This is a condition concerning regulatory noncompliance; it does not state a general common-law immunity or establish Honda's diligence. The 1984 preamble had described a contemplated later notice on due care and testing (29006); that proposal must not be treated as identical to already adopted 1984 operative language.

**Full implementation and mandatory belt-use law condition.** Both versions set September 1, 1989 for S4.1.2.1 compliance by all newly manufactured passenger cars, subject to S4.1.5. That condition requires the Secretary, no later than April 1, 1989, to determine that qualifying state mandatory belt-use laws have been enacted covering at least two-thirds of the combined population of the 50 States and District of Columbia, using the specified most recent Census population estimates. Cars manufactured under S4.1.3 or S4.1.4 on or after the determination may then use any of the three options. It is a determination-triggered change, not an automatic exemption inferred from enactment in some States.

Qualifying laws must require each front-seat occupant of a passenger car equipped with belts under Standard 208 to have a belt properly fastened while the car moves forward; allow waivers, if any, only for medical reasons; impose at least a $25 penalty, potentially including costs, for each violating occupant; permit a belt violation by a person injured in a passenger-car accident while violating the requirement to mitigate damages in that person's later injury litigation, with an existing state rule of law allowing mitigation sufficient; provide a program encouraging compliance; and become effective no later than September 1, 1989. Mitigation is permitted, not mandated in every case. The scan supplies the words omitted by OCR at appendix 13. The 1984 preamble separately discusses State evaluation reports and possible waivers for substantially complying laws passed before August 1, 1984 (28997, 28999); neither is silently inserted into the reproduced operative S4.1.5.2 list. No finding that the rescission trigger occurred is supplied here.

## What the contemporaneous agency explanation establishes

**Adopted reasons.** The Department chose a performance standard allowing several technologies because that latitude could improve effectiveness, permit some consumer choice, meet differing concerns about cost or intrusive belts, and encourage other systems such as passive interiors (28996–28997). It expected a plain reinstatement to produce almost exclusively detachable automatic belts, whereas the adopted incentive sought greater use and development of nonbelt systems (29000). It did not require a fixed percentage of airbags, a fixed mix of technologies, or a minimum number of cars lacking airbags. The text supports a deliberate policy of flexibility and technological development; it does not itself establish a numerical mix mandate.

The Department identified phase-in purposes beyond administrative convenience: earlier availability on some cars, time to develop alternatives to belts, improved information for consumers and the Department, gradual public acceptance, management of production and reliability problems, and avoidance of a rushed all-belt response to a universal earlier deadline (28999–29000, 29009). It described the chosen percentages as balancing technical feasibility and innovation. It rejected an airbags-only requirement based on costs, remaining engineering and experience problems, possible public backlash, need to gather comparative effectiveness data, and the risk that eliminating technological alternatives would stifle innovation (29000–29002). These are the agency's contemporaneous stated reasons, distinct from industry submissions and from any later litigation argument.

**Limits of those reasons.** The same rule treats traffic safety as the governing statutory objective, acknowledges important benefits of airbags, and finds the available automatic technologies feasible and practicable (28996). It regards many airbag fears as unfounded or manageable, not as established reasons that airbags should never be installed (28991–28992, 29001). It encourages broader nonbelt deployment and eventual evaluation of improved systems. Its discussion of public acceptance does not adopt every industry prediction; its data analysis repeatedly states limits of the underlying surveys and effectiveness estimates. An agency estimate concerning a class of vehicles is not proof about this Accord, the plaintiffs' injuries, an alternative design, or causation.

**Liability remains part of the actual explanation.** At 29000 the Department answers the concern that manufacturers would choose the cheapest systems and lose little investment if enough States adopted belt-use laws. Its answer relies on competition, potential liability for deficient systems, and product pride, together with adequate phase-in time. The scan confirms that passage. Earlier discussions at 28971–28972 report commenters' liability positions; those submissions are not the agency's holding. The Department's own responses at 28991–28992 concern repair-shop and product-liability risks of new restraint systems. The promulgation does not expressly decide the preemption of this no-airbag tort claim, and no broader common-law saving conclusion is extracted from those passages.

## Neutral legal paths and limits preserved

The original packet's two supported paths remain open. One path treats the combination of performance alternatives, staged timing, innovation, and public acceptance as a demonstrated federal objective that a legally obligatory airbag rule for the covered vehicles might frustrate. It must identify the actual asserted tort duty, the valid regulatory objective, and the concrete inconsistency; federal compliance alone, or the bare availability of a nonairbag option, does not complete that showing.

The opposing path treats these options as the permitted means of satisfying national regulation while former 15 U.S.C. §1397(k) preserves common-law liability despite compliance. It can rely on the absence of a command to omit airbags, the standard's safety purpose, and the contemporaneous acknowledgment of deficient-system liability. It must still address whether the particular asserted duty would defeat a supported substantive regulatory objective; physical ability to install an airbag does not alone answer every conflict question under the packet's entering law.

The express restriction in former §1392(d), its allowance for identical state standards and higher government-procurement standards, and the distinct common-law compliance saving provision retain the complete text in the base packet. No regulatory passage is treated as silently repealing either provision. Whether a regulation authoritatively protects an option against this tort duty, rather than merely allowing it for federal compliance, is a legal conclusion for the fresh model. Neither a source-preparation claim nor this validation determines it.
