## Wood: supported propositions and limits

**Concrete intervention and evidentiary posture.** The officer arrested an intoxicated driver, arranged to impound the car, removed its keys, and required Wood, the passenger, to get out. The stop occurred at 2:30 a.m. The majority treated the resulting abandonment in a high-crime area, cold weather, and the danger facing a woman left there as the legally material combination. Wood alleged that she later accepted a stranger's ride and was raped. Whether the officer offered assistance, whether he saw her leave with someone, what practical help nearby open businesses offered, and the reasonableness of her accepting the ride remained disputed. These were summary-judgment propositions viewed favorably to Wood, not trial findings that all asserted events happened.

**Affirmative action, custody, and fault.** The majority distinguished an official's creation or aggravation of a particular person's danger from a failure to rescue someone already exposed to danger. Removing the driver and transportation and then stranding Wood supplied the asserted affirmative state intervention. The reasoning did not require that Wood herself had been arrested or formally placed in custody. It did not announce a constitutional obligation to protect every member of the public in a dangerous neighborhood.

The amended majority found evidence from which a factfinder could find deliberate indifference to personal security. Mere negligence remained inadequate. It questioned its earlier reliance on gross negligence after *City of Canton* and did not settle that gross negligence alone suffices for the individual's underlying constitutional violation. Majority note 4 recognizes that a jury could characterize the conduct differently and leaves the trial court the task of appropriate definitions. The source therefore cannot be reduced to a holding imposing liability for any negligent removal of transportation.

**Causation and alternatives expressly reserved.** Majority note 2 states that the district court had not ruled on proximate cause, the defendants had not pressed that ground on appeal, and the appellate court did not examine its merits. The decision permits the claim to proceed; it does not conclusively attribute the rape to the officer. Majority note 6 says the record did not establish how much Wood told the officer about her lack of money or unavailable family help. Majority note 8 does not decide her alternative shocks-the-conscience theory and leaves her free to attempt to prove it at trial. The availability of state tort relief did not defeat the substantive due-process theory under the majority's distinction between substantive and procedural rights.

**Particularized immunity notice.** Section II separately asks whether an objectively reasonable officer on September 23, 1984 could have believed the alleged conduct constitutional. The majority acknowledges the absence of binding Supreme Court or Ninth Circuit precedent at the time. It relies on the prior Seventh Circuit decisions *White v. Rochford*, *Bowers v. DeVito*, and *Jackson v. City of Joliet*: affirmative endangerment through removal of protection differed from failure to avert an existing danger. It rejects a demand for the identical fact pattern but evaluates whether White's protected children and Wood faced legally distinguishable unreasonable dangers after police intervention. It also analyzes whether its circuit likely would have adopted those out-of-circuit authorities, emphasizing the absence of conflicting authority on the stated claim.

The majority expressly says it does not use the post-incident *Escamilla* decision to establish 1984 notice; it uses that later decision only to explain its assessment of how the circuit would have treated existing authorities. Its conclusion is conditional: if Wood proves her version, the officer is not entitled to qualified immunity. Majority note 7 leaves undecided whether a deliberate-indifference finding would independently defeat immunity. Neither that reserved suggestion nor the general existence of a personal-security liberty interest is a substitute for the section II notice analysis.

**Judgment and writings.** Thompson wrote the majority opinion on the panel with Fletcher and District Judge Carroll. Carroll dissented. The judgment reverses summary judgment for Ostrander and his wife and remands; it affirms dismissal for Maloney and his wife because that appellate challenge had been abandoned. The wife's claim was retained only because it appeared directed to the marital community and depended on the claim against her husband; the majority expressed no view on its merits.

Carroll's dissent contests the constitutional duty, the adequacy of deliberate-indifference evidence, and qualified immunity. He reads DeShaney as foreclosing the claimed duty absent relevant restraint, considers Wood's remaining choices and the source of private danger material, and views the child-protection features and divided writings in White as insufficient notice for this adult-passenger claim. His argument that state tort law can govern assistance is a dissenting position. It does not erase the majority's affirmative-danger holding. Conversely, the existence of the dissent and the majority's detailed analogy do not make Wood a universal notice rule for every setting involving loss of private aid.

## Gregory: supported propositions and limits

**Concrete events and control.** A sober designated driver, Turner, was stopped for running a red light; a warrant check disclosed an outstanding warrant. At Turner's request, the officer allowed him to drive Gregory and Fields to the police station. Turner parked in front, left the passengers in the car, and entered the station. Roughly thirty minutes later Gregory drove away and crashed, killing himself and injuring Fields. The passengers were intoxicated, but were never arrested, taken into custody, or placed under the officer's control. The plaintiffs did not challenge Turner's arrest or legitimate detention and did not claim the officer had to become their designated driver.

**Two grounds and the state/private-action distinction.** The en banc majority first held that the evidence did not permit a reasonable finding that the officer knew or should have known that the passengers were intoxicated and unfit to drive. Drinking and visiting nightclubs did not, on the majority's assessment of this record, establish detectable intoxication. It also supplied an independent ground: even assuming knowledge, the officer did not affirmatively place them in danger. He allowed Turner to take them to the station and left Turner able to arrange help, bring them inside, or keep the keys. Turner was seated in the station, not placed in a cell, and had no need to rush inside. The majority attributed the dangerous access to the car to Turner's leaving its keys with the passengers, rather than the officer's lawful execution of the arrest.

The majority expressly states two recognized duty settings: state limitation of the person's capacity to care for himself, and affirmative placement of a particular person in a danger he otherwise would not face. It applies neither here. It distinguishes Wood and White as abandonment in dangerous surroundings, rather than rejecting their affirmative-intervention principle. The source thus supports both the existence of a danger-creation route and the need to establish an affirmative, sufficiently connected state act on the particular record. Its handling of Turner's opportunity and keys cannot be carried over where those factual opportunities or a comparable intervening private choice are absent.

**Fault, immunity, and remedy.** Having found no deprivation of a protected right, the majority expressly declines to decide whether the district court properly treated the claim as merely negligent or what higher culpability standard would be required. It does not decide qualified immunity or hold that a specified right was unclear in March 1986. Summary judgment for the officer is affirmed on constitutional merits. The inadequate-training and policy/custom claims against the city also fail because the officer's conduct did not violate the passengers' constitutional rights. The decision does not adjudicate the city's liability for a separate established violation.

**Separate writing and record limits.** Fagg wrote the majority. Heaney dissented, joined by Lay, McMillian, Richard S. Arnold, and John R. Gibson. The dissent agrees with much of the legal framework but regards the summary-judgment evidence and permissible inferences differently. It treats the nightclub discussion, the passengers' noise and balloon play near the officers, Gregory's ownership of the car, and the dispatch warning that the departed driver was probably intoxicated as evidence of actual awareness or reckless disregard. From those facts it would permit a factfinder to find active intervention that removed their designated driver and left them exposed to driving while intoxicated.

The dissent disputes accepting the officer's account that he learned of the departure only after the accident. It does not present its contrary inference as an established fact. Its notes explain why the majority's cited alcohol-consumption decisions do not exclude the requested inferences in this record, and its conclusion acknowledges the missing depositions of Turner and the dispatcher. Those are historical record limits recited by the opinion; they are not grounds to infer a missing filing or litigant default in the pending matter. The majority's reasoning and the dissent's competing inferences must remain distinct.
