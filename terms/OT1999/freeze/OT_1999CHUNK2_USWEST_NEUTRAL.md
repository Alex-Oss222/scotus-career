### Issue 1: Commercial speech, confidentiality and fit

* **Controlling Simulated Law:** Lanphere & Urbaniak v. Colorado (April 18, 1995) subjects a marketing-purpose restriction on information use to commercial-speech scrutiny even where no general access entitlement exists. Edenfield requires real harms and material alleviation without an invariable empirical-study requirement. Simulated Florida Bar v. Went For It (June 21, 1995) invalidates the public-record targeted-mail waiting period for deficient advancement and fit while recognizing substantial privacy interests and reserving confidential-record misuse. Greater New Orleans (June 14, 1999) independently applies advancement and reasonable fit to an advertising scheme undermined by its own comparable permissions. These holdings require examination of the confidentiality harm, actual exceptions and consent mechanism here; none automatically resolves them.
* **Current Reusable Doctrine:** Central Hudson and Fox govern truthful, nonmisleading commercial speech about lawful services: substantial interest, direct and material advancement, and reasonable fit. Supported prediction is permissible; neither unsupported assertion nor an automatic demand for studies is the rule. Alternatives matter without a least-restrictive-means requirement.
* **Other Governing Law:** First Amendment; 47 U.S.C. § 222(a) and (c)(1); the challenged FCC CPNI order. Privacy in confidential service records is distinct from preventing receipt of discardable mail derived from public records.
* **Material Other Authority:** Discovery Network requires justification tied to the selected commercial category. Rubin v. Coors and Greater New Orleans require scrutiny of contradictions between the claimed harm and comparable permitted uses; service-related permissions must be compared with the challenged new marketing uses.
* **Open Point:** Whether unconsented use of confidential calling records establishes the claimed concrete harm; whether opt-in directly addresses that harm and fits reasonably despite opt-out and the carrier’s opt-in evidence.

## C. DECISION-MATERIAL LITIGATION POSITIONS

* The Commission identifies the harm as nonconsensual use of confidential calling information, rather than simply unwanted advertising. It contends opt-in prevents that use, that silence after an unread notice is not informed approval, and that service-related permissions reflect the customer’s existing choice.
* The carrier invokes Edenfield, Lanphere and Went For It, arguing that the FCC inadequately proved harm or the inadequacy of opt-out, suppressed truthful marketing to willing customers, and treated low opt-in responses as consent to a restriction customers may not understand.
* Statutorily, the Commission treats affirmative approval as a permissible reading of § 222. The carrier relies on constitutional doubt to defeat deference. Neither side’s statutory argument replaces independent First Amendment analysis.
