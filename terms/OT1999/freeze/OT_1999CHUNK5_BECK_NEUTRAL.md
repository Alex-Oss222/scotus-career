## Event and scope

No. 98-1480; review under §1254(1) of 162 F.3d 1090 (11th Cir. December 15, 1998). Scheduled merits event April 26, 2000. The supplied grant concerns civil injury under §§1962(d), 1964(c): whether injury from any overt act in furtherance of a RICO conspiracy suffices or whether the injurious act must itself be wrongful under RICO. Beck seeks reversal of conspiracy summary judgment. The question does not independently ask whether criminal conspiracy requires an overt act, each conspirator must personally commit predicates, the enterprise agreement was proved, or all of Beck's other claims were correctly rejected. No recusal is established; all nine were seated; quorum six, judgment majority five.

## Supported facts and complete lower judgment

Beck was president/director and sometimes CEO of Southeastern Insurance Group. The favorable-to-Beck summary-judgment account describes directors using illegal contractor fees, diverting funds and falsely reporting liabilities as assets. After discovering misconduct, Beck sought internal correction and informed insurance regulators. He alleges directors arranged a false adverse performance report and fired him in May 1988 to protect their scheme and avoid contractual stock repurchase. His employment contract prescribed reasons for justifiable termination and a stock-repurchase consequence for other termination; the existence of that possible contract claim does not establish a RICO predicate.

Separate claims concerned a debenture and stock purchase, retention of shares, a loan guarantee and related attorney fees. Beck alleged mail/wire-fraud inducements and a substantive §1962(c) offense, as well as conspiracy injury from retaliatory firing for refusing to participate and reporting misconduct. The action began as a crossclaim May 28, 1990 in derivative litigation, was severed and transferred. The district court granted summary judgment on federal RICO theories and declined state claims, denied sanctions, limited taxable costs and dismissed Sparber for inadequate service.

The Eleventh Circuit rejected a blanket proposition that shareholder/creditor status always defeats standing: a directly targeted personal injury could qualify. It nevertheless affirmed federal summary judgment on lack of supporting evidence of fraud/causation, and separately held that a civil conspiracy plaintiff's injury must arise from a racketeering act. It rejected the termination-report theory under the substantive count because Beck did not rely on the report and evidence did not establish intent to defraud him; it treated the termination as nonracketeering in the conspiracy analysis. These narrower substantive rulings are not newly granted questions, and their correctness should not be silently decided here.

The circuit VACATED the dismissal of pendent state claims and remanded because §1367 did not apply to the May 1990 action and its automatic tolling provision could not protect those claims. It required reconsideration of common-law pendent jurisdiction and potential limitations prejudice, suggesting a limitations-defense waiver as one permissible protective condition if dismissal was chosen. It affirmed denial of sanctions, the costs ruling and Sparber's service dismissal. Those components do not disappear if the conspiracy judgment is affirmed. A Supreme mandate limited to the granted federal question must preserve their existing scope and the existing state-claim remand.

## Entering law and live statutory question

The slice supplies Holmes's contextual requirement of factual causation and a sufficiently direct relation between the injury and §1962 violation (March 24, 1992); Reves's some-role-in-enterprise-direction requirement for §1962(c) (March 3, 1993); Salinas's knowing agreement to further an undertaking satisfying the substantive enterprise offense without each conspirator personally promising two predicates (December 2, 1997). Salinas expressly reserved a general overt-act rule in this timeline. Do not import the historical Salinas broader description quoted in the Eleventh Circuit opinion as a controlling simulated holding.

Relevant Standards are **Direct relation between a civil RICO violation and injury** (Holmes March 24, 1992), **Direction of enterprise affairs under §1962(c)** (Reves March 3, 1993), and **Knowing agreement to further a RICO enterprise offense** (Salinas December 2, 1997). Steamfitters (January 10, 2000) applies contextual directness to the intervening smoker/illness/treatment chain and preserves direct property injury without importing antitrust injury into RICO. A claim that conspirators directly fired the plaintiff is not the same causal chain; the type of legally cognizable injury remains a separate question.

Section 1962(d) makes conspiracy to violate (a), (b) or (c) unlawful. Section 1964(c) links private business/property recovery to injury by reason of a violation of §1962, with treble damages, costs and reasonable attorney fees when its conditions are met. Sections (a), (b) and (c) state distinct substantive prohibitions; no rule should require every civil conspiracy injury to be a violation of (c) alone or every defendant personally to perform the injurious predicate. Sedima's rejection of special racketeering injury/prior conviction and its analysis of substantive violations remain relevant pre-divergence law; the conspiracy subsection's distinct reach is the unresolved issue.

## Supported competing paths and objections

Beck's path: §1964(c) refers to all §1962 violations, including conspiracy; an intended retaliatory injury directly caused by an act carrying out that agreement could be injury by reason of the conspiracy even if the overt act does not separately qualify as a predicate. A narrower rule might require a direct, intended act in advancement of the racketeering object rather than any remote consequence. Salinas distinguishes agreement from personal predicate commission. The strongest objection is that civil conspiracy historically imputes liability for an independently actionable wrong and does not make every associated private wrong a RICO injury; a broad reading could evade limits applying to substantive RICO claims.

Respondents' path: distinguish unlawful agreement from a private compensable injury, require an injury-causing act wrongful under RICO, and preserve conspiracy's work of attributing that act to agreeing participants who did not personally commit it. Holmes supplies causation but does not itself define this additional civil-conspiracy injury boundary. The strongest objection is that Congress separately prohibited agreement and expressly extended §1964(c) to all of §1962; importing a tort-conspiracy premise must be justified by statutory context and cannot erase subsection (d). Common-law categories are interpretive evidence, not enacted text.

A decision may affirm on the confined premise that termination in this record is not a predicate or otherwise independently wrongful under RICO while reserving the precise coverage of other nonpredicate substantive RICO wrongs. Alternatively, reversal of the categorical exclusion would require remand for ordinary treatment of the conspiracy, causation and other preserved requirements, not judgment awarding Beck treble damages. No lawful path requires altering criminal conspiracy elements, Reves's substantive test, every conspirator's personal conduct requirement or unrelated state rights.

## Remedy, source limits and validation

Affirmance of conspiracy summary judgment must not reinstate the district court's vacated state-claim dismissal, newly approve its derivative-injury rationale, reverse the independent service ruling or award sanctions. Reversal removes the granted federal ground and remands only as necessary; it does not deem a racketeering agreement or any amount of injury proved. The separate federal substantive dispositions remain outside this grant unless an actual dependence is shown; the Court need not adjudicate their reliance rules to answer the presented statutory question.

The 1994 and 1999 official §1964 texts are both saved. The 1995 securities-fraud restriction and criminal-conviction exception are visible in the later edition; this question concerns retaliatory termination, not a new ruling on that amendment's temporal reach or securities exception. No securities-fraud theory may be silently reinstated. A holding stating §1964(c) generally should preserve that distinct qualification without deciding its application here.
