## What the lower court actually decided

The extract establishes separate lower-court grounds that must be assessed independently:

1. **Constitutional trigger and no error.** The Fourth Circuit understood Simmons to require legal parole ineligibility under state law at the relevant sentencing stage. It accepted the Virginia Supreme Court's determination that Ramdass did not meet the three-strikes condition. That combination defeated the Simmons claim on the lower court's legal theory. The supplied excerpt uses sentencing generally; the established neutral chronology locates the disputed eligibility determination at the penalty jury's deliberations. It must not be silently restated as a finding that Ramdass remained eligible after the February 18 Domino's judgment or at the April 2 judicial sentencing.

2. **Independent statutory and retroactivity grounds.** The court expressly reasoned that even acceptance of practical rather than legal ineligibility would not yield relief because that extension was not clearly established under section 2254(d)(1). It also treated Teague as independently defeating the proposed extension. Its discussion compared the two restrictions while acknowledging that section 2254(d)(1) did not simply codify Teague. The source therefore establishes an alternative legal ground, not merely a description of the merits holding.

3. **Legal classification and state-law limits.** The court rejected the District Court's treatment of the Virginia eligibility determination as an unreasonable determination of fact. It characterized eligibility, including the meaning and application of conviction in the three-strikes statute, as state law. Under section 2254(a), disagreement with that state-law conclusion alone did not supply a federal custody violation. This is a supported account of the District Court rationale as reported and rejected by the appellate opinion; it is not certification of every independent District Court ground or the complete District Court analysis.

These grounds cannot be reduced to an unspecified demand for deference or to a claim that reversal rested exclusively on an incorrect reasonableness formula.
