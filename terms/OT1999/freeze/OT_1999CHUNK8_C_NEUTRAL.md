## Apprendi — record and posture

**Route.** Review of the New Jersey Supreme Court's June 24, 1999 judgment, 159 N.J. 7, 731 A.2d 485, affirming the Appellate Division, 304 N.J. Super. 147, 698 A.2d 1265. Jurisdiction is the supplied direct state-judgment route under 28 U.S.C. §1257(a). The question is allocation and burden for the nonrecidivist biased-purpose fact used to raise the authorized maximum on the convicted firearm count. The merits papers dispute that constitutional classification, not New Jersey's power to punish bias-motivated crimes as such.

**Charged and admitted offenses.** The actual January 19, 1995 indictment, JA 2–12, contains 23 counts. Count 3 alleges possession of a firearm for an unlawful purpose on September 24, 1994; Count 18 alleges the same offense on December 22, 1994, under §2C:39-4(a). Count 22 alleges possession of an anti-personnel bomb under §2C:39-3(a). Apprendi pleaded guilty to these three counts on July 24, 1995, and the agreement called for dismissal of the other twenty. The indictment itself resolves a conflicting lower-opinion reference to 22 counts; the plea colloquy's occasional November description should not be used to change Count 3's charged date.

**Indictment qualification.** Count 18 contains the firearm and unlawful-purpose allegations, not the purpose-to-intimidate-because-of-race allegation. The indictment nevertheless expressly alleges racial or ethnic intimidation in harassment Counts 5, 10, 15 and 20. Those were among the dismissed charges. It would be inaccurate to write that the indictment contained no racial-bias allegation anywhere, or to treat an allegation in a dismissed harassment count as a finding admitted in the firearm conviction.

**Plea and reserved issue.** JA 19–24 establishes a jury waiver for the pleaded offenses and express discussion of the pending attack on the enhancement statute. The State reserved an application for the enhanced term on Count 18; the defense reserved its constitutional attack. Apprendi acknowledged possible consecutive ordinary terms of ten years on each firearm count, thus twenty years total, and a possible twenty-year enhanced Count 18 term plus ten years on Count 3, thus thirty years under the agreement. Count 22 was to run concurrently. Awareness of potential exposure is not an admission of biased purpose; the reserved challenge must not be erased by the guilty plea.

**Contested fact.** Apprendi admitted firing at the Fowlkes home and the unlawful purpose to frighten or harass its occupants. The State presented the interrogating officer's account of Apprendi saying he acted because the residents were Black and to communicate that they were unwelcome. Apprendi denied racial purpose, disputed that account's reliability, and presented character and psychological evidence. The psychologist did not establish an insanity or diminished-capacity defense; his evidence concerned judgment, impulse control and an alternative explanation. The judge credited the State's racial-purpose showing by a preponderance. These are distinct categories: admissions about the shooting, the officer's testimony, defense counterevidence, and the judge's finding. Do not convert them into a jury finding or admitted racial purpose. Petition appendix 139a–145a, 160a–161a, 164a–301a; the parties' merits statements identify the precise witnesses and disputes.

**Punishment.** The ordinary second-degree range was five to ten years under §2C:43-6(a)(2). The challenged finding authorized the extended second-degree range of ten to twenty years under §2C:43-7(a)(3). Count 18 received twelve years with four years of parole ineligibility. Count 3 received seven years with three years of parole ineligibility; Count 22 received three years; all actual terms were concurrent. The four-year parole bar on Count 18 arose from the separate Graves Act provision, §2C:43-6(c), not from a rule that §2C:44-3(e) itself fixes a four-year minimum. A possible aggregate sentence on other counts is a distinct argument from authority for the actual twelve-year sentence on Count 18.

**Applied statutory scheme and qualifications.** The parties quote the 1995 version of §2C:44-3: on the prosecutor's application the court shall impose an extended term for the subsection (e) finding proved by a preponderance. The stated exclusions concern §2C:12-1(a), §2C:33-4, and violations of §2C:14-2 or §2C:14-3 when the application rests on purpose to intimidate because of gender. Subsection (e) concerns purpose to intimidate an individual or group because of race, color, gender, handicap, religion, sexual orientation or ethnicity. None of those listed offense exclusions applies to the pleaded firearm offense. Keep the introductory mandatory subsection-(e) command separate from discretion governing other extended-term grounds.

The original 1990 enactment, reproduced at JA 53–60, also used ill will, hatred or bias language and had its own exclusions. The 1995 amendment removed that wording following State v. Mortimer and added gender and handicap. Mortimer supplied the narrowing construction used in addressing the vagueness objection. The New Jersey Supreme Court later noted that the sentencing judge had not articulated the amended statutory standard and that court and counsel appeared unaware of the amendment. It regarded the record as establishing the required racial purpose nevertheless. That is the state appellate court's assessment, not an admission by Apprendi, a jury finding, or an assertion that the trial judge expressly applied the amended words. The current granted allocation/proof question does not require a new facial vagueness adjudication or a finding that the legislature may punish thought without conduct. The original session-law text and the parties' quoted applied text are preserved separately; do not combine their exclusion lists into a synthetic statute.

### Apprendi — supported rival positions and scope

The petitioner's merits theory is that a present-offense mental-state fact legally necessary to exceed the offense's ordinary maximum performs the work of an element and therefore requires jury determination and proof beyond reasonable doubt, subject to a valid admission or waiver. The contested racial-purpose hearing, ten-to-twenty-year change and actual twelve-year Count 18 sentence supply the concrete application. Calling the fact motive rather than intent, or placing it in a sentencing chapter, does not answer that functional contention. The State has authority to define and punish bias crimes; the procedural question remains.

The State's merits theory is that the pleaded firearm offense was complete before the sentencing inquiry, motive traditionally informs punishment, the legislature expressly made this a broadly applicable sentencing provision, and an adversarial hearing plus notice safeguards the defendant while avoiding introduction of inflammatory racial evidence at the guilt stage. Its McMillan argument relies on several constraints, not unlimited authority to relabel any element: no presumption displacing guilt proof; no change from a nominal offense to mandatory life punishment; no apparent evasion in the statute's design; and the asserted sentencing tradition. The response is that McMillan did not increase the maximum, that proof protections and prejudice-management measures can coexist, and that the present fact concerns commission of the charged offense rather than a prior judicial conviction.

Historical merits briefs cite versions of Almendarez-Torres and Jones that are not the operative simulated dispositions. Retain the arguments only to the extent supportable under the actual law above. The lower court's historical reliance on a recidivism decision is a description of its reasons, not controlling in-world Supreme Court law.

The petition requests overturning the twelve-year Count 18 term and remand. Any disposition must distinguish that conviction's authorization from possible aggregate restructuring, the separate parole statute, unchallenged convictions and discretionary resentencing. Do not decree a new prosecution, new jury hearing, acquittal or particular sentence without deciding its authority. An affirmance path requires an actual constitutional defense of the judicial allocation; a reversal path requires an actual constitutional holding, because federal statutory avoidance does not rewrite New Jersey's adopted construction. Minimum-only rules, capital eligibility/selection, every Guidelines system, general consecutive-sentencing rules and a constitutional prior-conviction exception can remain outside this nonrecidivist case.

## Dickerson — record and posture

**Route and scope.** Review of the Fourth Circuit's interlocutory suppression appeal, 166 F.3d 667 (1999), in a federal bank-robbery/firearm prosecution. The indictment charges conspiracy under §371, three bank-robbery counts under §2113(a), (d), and three §924(c) counts. There is no conviction or sentencing judgment to vacate. The granted question concerns whether §3501 permits case-in-chief admission of the statement notwithstanding the Miranda ruling. The separate challenge to the Fourth Circuit's authority to take up the statute, and its physical-evidence ruling, are outside the supplied grant; do not silently expand review to them.

**Suppression findings.** Agents interviewed Dickerson at the Washington FBI field office on January 27, 1997 following investigation of a January 24 bank robbery. At the suppression hearing Agent Lawlor and Dickerson disagreed about when warnings and a written waiver occurred. The district judge found custody and credited Dickerson's testimony that all disputed statements preceded the warnings and waiver. The Government later supplied Detective Durkin's affidavit and Dickerson's handwritten statement with its reconsideration motion; the district court declined to reopen because it had not shown that the material was previously unavailable. The Fourth Circuit affirmed that refusal as within the trial court's discretion. Later evidence proffer is not an accepted new finding that the original suppression ruling was false.

**Voluntariness qualification.** The Fourth Circuit treated the statements as voluntary, reasoning that the district court's refusal to suppress derivative evidence necessarily reflected voluntariness. Attribute that conclusion to the appellate court and its reasoning. Do not recast it as a fresh Supreme Court credibility finding or a finding that warnings in fact preceded the statement. The constitutional issue can address §3501's claimed authority to admit a voluntary statement excluded by Miranda without resolving all fact disputes anew.

**Separate evidence.** The district court suppressed physical evidence found in the apartment; the Fourth Circuit reversed that ruling. It was not within the granted review. A separate request to suppress a backpack and solvent recovered from the car was denied. Neither the pistol/cash search nor a new fruit-of-the-statement theory is the judgment component now presented.

**Who argues what.** The United States does **not** defend §3501's displacement of Miranda. Its merits brief supports Dickerson on that question and argues against overruling Miranda, while preserving its ability to prosecute with other evidence and discussing the earlier evidentiary dispute. The judgment's principal defense is Cassell's amicus merits brief, filed March 9, 2000. Do not attribute that defense to the Solicitor General. The filing is an available litigation source; its historical appointment label does not itself enter a separate simulated Court order.

### Dickerson — statute in full scope

The exact statutory body was mechanically copied from the [1994 official Code](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/USCODE-1994-title18/html/USCODE-1994-title18-partII-chap223-sec3501.htm) into [3501_1994_statute.txt](../sources/chunk8/c/3501_1994_statute.txt).

- **(a):** In federal or District of Columbia criminal prosecutions a voluntary confession is admissible. The judge resolves voluntariness outside the jury's presence. If voluntary, the jury hears relevant voluntariness evidence and receives an instruction to assign the statement the weight deserved under all circumstances.
- **(b):** The judge considers all circumstances, including arrest-to-arraignment time when applicable, knowledge of the offense, advice or knowledge of the right not to speak and use of the statement, prior advice of counsel rights, and whether counsel was absent during questioning and confession. Presence or absence of a listed factor **need not** be conclusive. The statute does not command the judge to ignore warnings or declare them irrelevant, and does not make absence of a warning invariably dispositive.
- **(c):** Separate presentment-delay protection: voluntary statements within six hours of the relevant arrest/detention are not excluded solely for presentment delay if weight is left to the jury. The six-hour limit does not apply where the trial judge finds longer transportation/distance-related delay reasonable to the nearest available qualifying officer. Alvarez-Sanchez controls the separate duty and timing inquiry; this case need not decide all post-six-hour or McNabb-Mallory questions.
- **(d):** The section does not bar voluntary confessions given without interrogation or when the speaker is not under arrest or detention. These alternatives and their voluntariness limit remain distinct.
- **(e):** Confession includes both an admission of guilt and any oral or written self-incriminating statement. It is not limited to a complete confession to every charge.

A conclusion concerning voluntariness-only displacement of Miranda should not be described as wholesale invalidation of every subsection or destruction of the independent coercion inquiry.

### Dickerson — entering law and rival source arguments

**Miranda** supplies the predivergence custodial-interrogation safeguards and its allowance for equally effective alternatives. **Harris/Hass** distinguish impeachment use; **Quarles** addresses public safety; **Tucker** and **Elstad** distinguish derivative evidence and later voluntary warned statements from automatic suppression of all fruits. **Connelly** preserves the coercive-police-activity condition in the due-process voluntariness inquiry. These decisions must be considered for their actual settings, rather than flattened into either an equation of every warning violation with actual compulsion or an answer that Miranda has no constitutional authority at all.

The simulated authorities preserve warning safeguards while leaving this statutory question open: **Withrow** refuses to extend Powell's Fourth Amendment habeas restriction to properly presented Miranda claims; **Stansbury** gives the objective custody inquiry; **Davis** adds the specified postwaiver clarification duty and expressly reserves §3501; **Thompson** treats ultimate custody as a constitutional mixed question while keeping supported historical facts distinct; **Alvarez-Sanchez** construes subsection (c) narrowly and expressly reserves the statute's overall relationship to Miranda. Their decisions are not a prior adjudication that Congress may or may not enact this replacement.

**Smith v. Robbins** is a material current-term analogy. All nine permit genuinely equivalent alternatives to Anders's exact form. Stevens, Stone-Zsela, Souter, Ginsburg and Breyer find Wende inadequate because retained counsel, a factual narrative, independent judicial review and possible later briefing do not supply the missing advocate-assisted legal screening. O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy and Thomas accept the alternatives rule but regard Wende as sufficient. Cassell's historical brief cites a different outcome for Wende; that result cannot be imported. The operative lesson and each Justice's application are available without deciding that an appellate-counsel case automatically determines confession safeguards.

The petitioner's and United States' briefs argue that Miranda is a constitutional implementation, demonstrated by its application to state courts and habeas review; Congress cannot simply restore the voluntariness test that Miranda found insufficient. They invoke workable administration, settled law-enforcement practice, reliance and stare decisis. Their argument permits effective alternatives and recognized exceptions; it need not establish a verbatim warning requirement, a civil cause of action for every violation, or actual compulsion in every unwarned statement.

Cassell's defense argues that the Self-Incrimination Clause bars actual compelled evidence, while Miranda supplies prophylactic safeguards broader than that prohibition; Congress may replace that remedial system without redefining the right. His proposed alternative reads §3501 together with judicial screening, jury weighting, warning-related incentives, civil remedies and police training. He invokes Bivens/Bush/Chilicky replacement-remedy reasoning, the Robbins principle and the contrast between unwarned and actually compelled statements. He alternatively asks the Court itself to modify Miranda's categorical rule to permit proof of actual voluntariness, even if Congress cannot accomplish the change alone. Do not collapse the legislative-authority argument into that distinct judicial-reconsideration alternative.

The competing response is that the asserted substitutes must protect the constitutional function, not merely provide a later remedy for some proven coercion; their practical adequacy is disputed. Reliance on civil-remedy availability must preserve actual cause-of-action and immunity limits rather than promise universal recovery. Boerne's legislative-enforcement principles must be taken from the simulated holding, not the historical brief's unqualified formulation. Empirical claims of interrogation costs, training improvements and warning persistence are advocacy, not judicially established facts.

Remedy remains tied to an interlocutory evidentiary ruling. Rejection of §3501 as a sufficient replacement removes that basis for reversing suppression and permits a bounded remand; it does not dismiss the indictment, acquit Dickerson, decide the independent physical-evidence ruling, or decide ungranted reconsideration issues. Acceptance of the statute or a judicial revision requires specifying the governing admissibility rule and its application/remand limits. A claim that every constitutional safeguard is legislatively irrevisable exceeds the presented question just as much as a claim that Congress may always replace one by voluntariness alone.

## Independent neutral validation: supplementary posture and lawful paths

### Apprendi: lower judgments and material disagreements

The New Jersey Supreme Court affirmed the Appellate Division. Its majority recognized that the enhancement increased the maximum, but treated the remaining McMillan considerations, the legislature's explicit allocation, and the tradition of considering motive at sentencing as sufficient. It denied that legislatures may remove any ordinary culpability or grading element at will. It also considered the possible prejudice from racial-purpose evidence at a guilt trial, the State's interest in punishing discriminatory criminal conduct, and possible aggregate punishment on the pleaded charges. Those are the lower court's legal reasons, not independent findings that the federal jury objection is satisfied. Its reliance on the historical Almendarez-Torres and Jones must be evaluated under the different operative Supreme Court holdings in this timeline.

The majority acknowledged that the sentencing court did not articulate the amended standard, but concluded from its view of the evidence that the required purpose existed. It described that articulation issue as outside the appeal taken on the dissent below. Preserve that distinction between the current federal allocation/proof issue and an independent ungranted state sentencing complaint. Its reference to possible thirty-five-year exposure does not displace the plea transcript's specific concurrent Count 22 and thirty-year agreement limit. The actual sentence on Count 18 remains twelve years, regardless of what consecutive ordinary terms could have produced on other counts. The opinion's assessment of compelling racial-purpose evidence does not erase the trial dispute or supply an admission.

Justice Stein's dissent, joined by Justice Handler, offered separate state statutory, state constitutional and federal grounds. It regarded biased purpose as the additional culpability needed for aggravated punishment, invoked the Code's definitions and proof requirements, and distinguished a present mental-state inquiry from record proof of a prior conviction. It argued that a separate jury phase could manage prejudice from bias evidence, using the State's existing procedure for firearm possession and prior-felon status as an analogy. It sought reversal and a new trial. The Appellate Division dissent likewise treated biased purpose as an element. Those state-law alternatives explain the litigation; this Court must accept the state high court's authoritative construction rather than rewrite state law through federal statutory avoidance. The federal question can support its own bounded remedy without adopting the lower dissent's complete requested new-trial disposition.

Source: petition appendix, New Jersey Supreme Court majority at 20a–28a and Stein dissent at 61a–66a; the exact OCR is retained in `../sources/chunk8/c/apprendi_02._Petition_for_Writ_of_Certiorari.txt`. The prior-opinion descriptions inside that historical filing do not replace operative simulated law.

Three materially supported constitutional paths remain open for independent assessment. First, the Court could require jury proof beyond reasonable doubt of a nonrecidivist fact necessary to exceed the maximum otherwise authorized by the conviction. The increased Count 18 ceiling and contested present-offense purpose support that theory; its principal challenge is the legislature's role in defining crimes and the existing distinction between elements and punishment. Second, a Court could decide only the narrower present-offense culpability question: racial purpose here performs the same work as an additional mental-state element, while other aggravators remain unclassified. That narrower path must explain why its culpability boundary is legally supported and workable, and cannot assume all sentencing motive inquiries are elements. Third, the Court could sustain this legislative treatment as sentencing, based on the completed firearm offense, traditional consideration of motive, and the scheme's limiting features. That path must answer the maximum increase and the danger that labels circumvent Winship and the jury guarantee; it may not rely on a supposed controlling recidivism exception or unlimited legislative power. These are rival legal possibilities, not proposed commitments.

The proof burden and the jury allocation are related but distinct constitutional questions. A decision relying on a narrower proof-ground must state whether the jury question remains open and what process follows; it cannot silently turn a reasonable-doubt obligation into a jury holding. Likewise a constitutional charging conclusion must identify its source. Federal grand-jury indictment rules from Almendarez-Torres and Jones do not themselves govern this state prosecution. The plea waived jury adjudication of the pleaded offenses while reserving the enhancement attack; neither notice of possible exposure nor that limited waiver establishes waiver of the reserved claim.

### Dickerson: lower judgment and its dissent

The Fourth Circuit majority treated Miranda as a prophylactic rule beyond actual compulsion and therefore regarded section 3501 as Congress's governing federal admissibility rule. It read the record as supporting voluntariness, used the statute to reverse statement suppression, and separately sustained admission of the apartment evidence. Judge Michael disagreed with invoking the statute when the United States expressly declined to rely on it and the court had received only amicus advocacy for its application. His dissent was principally about judicial restraint and fair adversarial presentation; it questioned how a wholly nonconstitutional rule could continue governing state prosecutions but did not decide Miranda's constitutional status. He would sustain the reconsideration denial and statement suppression. He agreed that the apartment evidence was admissible under Leon's good-faith exception, while rejecting the majority's separate particularity ground. These physical-evidence and lower-court issue-raising disagreements do not enlarge the present grant.

Source: petition appendix, Fourth Circuit majority and Michael writing at 25a–27a; exact OCR in `../sources/chunk8/c/dickerson_02._Petition_for_Writ_of_Certiorari.txt`. Historical descriptions of Davis or other postdivergence precedent inside that filing are descriptions of the lower court's reasoning only; the actual simulated versions govern.

The supported paths are distinct. A constitutional-implementation path may retain Miranda's case-in-chief protection while allowing genuinely effective alternatives and decide that this voluntariness regime does not supply one. Its strongest challenge is the body of decisions allowing uses of unwarned voluntary evidence and describing prophylaxis. A legislative-replacement path may distinguish the right against actual compelled evidence from judicial safeguards and evaluate section 3501 together with its proposed supporting protections. Its strongest challenge is the state-court and habeas operation of Miranda, the adequacy of a retrospective voluntariness inquiry, and the Court's authority to define enforceable constitutional protection. A third path considers the separate request for judicial reconsideration of Miranda even if Congress cannot itself displace it. It must address stare decisis, workability, reliance and the substantive adequacy of the substitute; congressional enactment is not by itself a sufficient stare-decisis reason. None of those paths assumes the result of Smith v. Robbins controls another safeguard's adequacy.

A construction preserving section 3501 subject to constitutional requirements and a holding that Congress cannot authorize this particular unwarned case-in-chief use are compatible in principle. If adopted, their scope must be stated expressly; neither means that all subsections are void, warnings can never change, or actual compulsion has become irrelevant. A ruling concerning Miranda's constitutional authority need not decide civil liability for every violation, every fruit doctrine, presentment delays, ungranted search questions or the validity of a particular new warning formula.
