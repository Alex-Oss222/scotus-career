## Common procedural ground of six Associates

**Joining:** Stevens, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Ginsburg and Breyer, throughout this bounded ground and its Fort Yuma remand consequence.

**Operative rule and application:** Although technical preclusion rules do not apply mechanically within a continuing original action, their ordinary requirement that an affirmative defense be timely presented informs the Court's treatment of that action. The State parties' Arizona I defense is unavailable here because they omitted it when the enlarged Fort Yuma demand was presented in 1979 and again during the 1982 proceedings, despite the opportunity to assert it, and first raised it in 1989 after the litigation had proceeded on the different grounds and boundary-adjudication framework described in the neutral record.

Arizona II's recognition of finality principles in an ongoing original action supplies the framework, not a universal application of ordinary pleading deadlines. Rules 8(c) and 15(d) illustrate the opportunity and responsibility to present an affirmative defense as the controversy develops; the fact that the original pleadings preceded the first decree does not eliminate later opportunities to present the defense. Counsel's later recognition of the argument does not by itself excuse those missed opportunities. The defense could have been presented even though the earlier stage ultimately resolved other questions.

The all-five-reservations language in the 1979 and 1984 decrees, the States' stipulation to the 1979 terms and their requests for judicial boundary determination reinforce this particular procedural setting. They are not an express adjudication of the merits of preclusion or automatic contractual abandonment of every defense. Arizona II's reservation of a possible original-action route after failure of the district-court route likewise does not itself adjudicate the present defense. The six do not treat retained jurisdiction as unlimited reopening authority.

This ground is forfeiture from the supported litigation course, not a finding of intentional waiver or estoppel. It does not infer a concession, absence of an objection, preserved objection or agreement from the unrecovered 1989 responses. Granting the reopening motion and the Master's subsequent merits consideration do not compel these six to disregard earlier forfeiture. The missing responses therefore are not indispensable to this ground, and their absence is never a party default.

There is also no mandatory judicial-resuscitation route. The neutral history establishes that the disputed title predicate was not actually decided; the asserted claim bar rests on what could have been litigated. A court's limited ability to raise preclusion to avoid repeating its own prior adjudication does not require revival here. This is not a general prohibition on appropriate judicial initiative and not a holding that claim preclusion can never reach an unlitigated theory.

**Express limits:** The six reject the Master's suggestion that Interior's changed legal view is a newly discovered factual circumstance defeating otherwise applicable preclusion. No one acquires water merely because the Government changed its interpretation of the 1893 agreement. Rejecting this defense does not determine Fort Yuma title, the validity or effect of the cession, irrigable acreage, reserved-water quantity or the binding effect of the Secretary's determination on the States. Those unresolved predicates remain for actual adjudication before any additional water is awarded.

## O'Connor and Thomas: separate original-allocation ground

O'Connor and Thomas retain the revised model's position that the Court should entertain the original-decree defense and sustain it. Their historical join expressly confronted both the timing disagreement and the conditional language relied upon by the six. It is stronger evidence than a general inference from their treatment of unrelated consent judgments.

They give weight to the continuing original-action posture, the limited matters previously resolved, the reservation of later litigation possibilities, express presentation in the 1989 reopening motion and actual referral and adjudication by the Master. On that supported procedural course they would exercise the Court's authority to decide the defense rather than treat the earlier omissions as conclusive forfeiture. They do not assert that the unrecovered 1989 responses contained no objection: that historical separate-writing characterization has not been admitted as a neutral factual premise and is unnecessary to their narrower current position. They likewise do not find that any missing filing conclusively preserved the defense.

On the merits, they would hold that a completed reserved-water allocation cannot be enlarged merely because the United States later changes its legal understanding of a land transaction whose relevant factual materials were available when it litigated the reservation's original demand. The later conditional adjustment provisions allow appropriate quantity changes after a lawful final boundary determination; they do not themselves remove a preclusion defense to reopening the omitted predicate. Arizona II's omitted-acreage reasoning and the claim-finality principle illustrated by Nevada v. United States support their conclusion, with the original maximum-claim representation and warning informing the scope of the controversy.

The reported private-claimant obstacle for Fort Mojave and Colorado River cannot simply be attributed to Fort Yuma. These two rely on the supported distinction at its reported scope, not on a newly found absence of every possible affected interest. Their result concerns the already litigated water demand; it does not transfer land, decide the 1893 agreement on the merits or hold that the United States and Tribe have identical interests in every proceeding. The original statements are not treated as a perpetual contractual surrender of all future rights. A genuinely different claim, physical event or independent lawful agreement retains its own analysis.

O'Connor's Matsushita opinion and narrow Baker rationale preserve an instrument's actual lawful effect; Thomas's Matsushita joins and distinct Baker rationale preserve the same distinction. Those positions support their consideration of actual decree scope, but do not turn an adjustment clause into express reopening permission. Their Plaut dissents and Agostini joins distinguish a completed adjudication from lawfully changed prospective operation. No new statute, controlling overruling or final boundary determination here supplies the changed premise those cases require. They would sustain the State parties' exception and reject the enlarged Fort Yuma water demand in this original proceeding, without a new Fort Yuma merits remand.

## Distinct instrument questions and settlements

**Docket 320 — all eight.** The $15 million consent judgment finally settles the United States' and Tribe's claims against one another within its reciprocal scope and appeal waiver. That claim finality remains. But the alternative taking and trespass theories were compromised without an actual or clearly intended determination of the disputed title predicate; the separate reserved-water demand against competing users is not shown to fall within a release of claims against the United States. The no-admission provision does not invalidate the judgment. International Building supplies the compromise/issue distinction; actual Matsushita, Baker and Richards preserve lawful judgment effect and binding prerequisites without deciding this federal instrument by section 1738. No independent finding adopts the United States' full account of the consideration.

The historical six directly adopted the bounded rejection of the consent-judgment bar. O'Connor and Thomas's historical separate writing found the earlier original-allocation bar sufficient and did not independently adopt the other analysis; silence is not an opposing vote. Their fresh, expressly component-specific modeled agreement is retained because it preserves the very finality their own actual Matsushita/Baker positions demand and adds no title finding or general settlement exception. No concrete historical contrary objection has been identified. Their agreement with this separate instrument conclusion cannot be counted as support for a Fort Yuma remand they reject on another ground.

**Section 22 — all eight.** Subsection (a)'s payment discharge and subsection (b)'s adverse-determination bar have distinct triggers and expressly concern claims against the United States. The payment of this alternative-theory compromise does not by itself establish a taking, extinguishment of title or an all-persons release of dependent water claims. The broader possible consequences of a judgment that actually determined the relevant title or extinguishment predicate remain open. This ground is distinct from ordinary preclusion and from the earlier original allocation; rejection of it gives no one unproved title or water. The historical discussion leaves the same differently supported statutory judgments open.

**Fort Mojave — all eight.** Approve the recommended settlement fixing the specified Hay and Wood Reserve boundary and allowing the lesser of 3,022 acre-feet or water sufficient for 468 acres.
