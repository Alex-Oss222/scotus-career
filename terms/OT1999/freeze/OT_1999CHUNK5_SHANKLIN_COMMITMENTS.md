## Governing law retained by all eight

Easterwood and the register *Federal railroad coverage and separate warning and speed duties*, effective April 21, 1993, require substantial subsumption of the actual state-law duty. Merely touching railroad safety, general crossing planning or MUTCD compliance does not suffice. Common-law duties can be state requirements. A covered additional or more stringent requirement survives only if necessary to eliminate or reduce an essentially local safety hazard, compatible with federal law and not an unreasonable interstate-commerce burden. The conditions are cumulative; none is established merely by crossing-specific hazards, and this case asserts no such exception.

Section 646.214(b)(3)(i) requires gates with flashing lights for the specified conditions: multiple main tracks; nearby tracks capable of concealing another approaching train; high train speed with limited sight distance; high speed with moderately high highway and rail traffic; high highway or train volumes, substantial schoolbus or hazardous-material truck traffic, unusually limited visibility, continuing accidents or combinations; or a diagnostic-team recommendation. Under (b)(3)(ii), a diagnostic team's justification that gates are inappropriate permits FHWA to determine those requirements inapplicable; it neither requires omission nor establishes that a justification occurred here. When (b)(3) does not apply, (b)(4) requires FHWA approval of the device choice. The distinct (b)(2) route for crossings within or near the terminus of a federal-aid highway construction or improvement project is not silently treated as the source for this stand-alone warning project.

The high speed, sight-distance, traffic, hazardous-material and prior-collision evidence cannot be silently converted into findings that all regulatory conditions existed at installation or that FHWA granted a particular exception. No holding should state that crossbucks themselves prove federal compliance with the gate or pavement-marking requirements.

## Preemption position: O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas and Breyer

**Operative rule.** When federal funds participate in an approved project installing warning devices at the crossing and those devices have been installed and are operating, sections 646.214(b)(3)–(4) govern the selection and adequacy of the protective devices, displacing an independent state-law duty to select different or additional devices. Coverage does not require proof that a federal employee separately studied this crossing or made an express individualized choice of passive over active protection. This rule covers the independent adequacy theory presented, not every railroad duty or every allegation of violation of federal law.

Easterwood distinguished general planning from these specific regulations because, when applicable, they replace independent device choices with federal requirements or approval. Its missing predicate was an actual federally participating warning installation at the crossing: the proposed gates were abandoned, funds redirected and circuitry at another location did not warn motorists. Those facts differ from the funded, approved and completed crossbuck/advance-sign installation here. Sections 109(e) and 630.114(b), as supplied in the packet, support treating the approved funding and installation as proceeding under the federal device-choice scheme. They do not establish that an actual diagnostic study occurred, and no such invented factual finding is needed.

The strongest contrary argument is that minimum federal spending cannot establish that the agency carried out its mandatory adequacy responsibilities. That identifies an important distinction between coverage and compliance. The application of the device-selection regulations does not disappear whenever a party contends that the selection failed their standards. An independent state negligence rule demanding different devices remains a separate source of device-selection authority even when its factual criticism resembles an alleged regulatory breach. The Court can recognize federal coverage without declaring every installation choice lawful, immunizing breach of an otherwise available federal-standard duty or finding an agency waiver.

The dangerous-conditions argument invokes (b)(3)'s gate commands and cannot be dismissed as irrelevant. The pavement-marking argument likewise cannot be answered merely by saying that the installed signs themselves met their design standards. But neither supplies a separate preserved federal-compliance cause of action in this review. The proposed ruling removes only the independent state inadequacy theory actually presented. It does not decide an unpleaded federal claim, find that missing markings were permissible, hold that mandatory gates are optional, or declare every possible maintenance/noncompliance theory preempted. Whether any genuinely preserved remaining theory exists retains its actual footing; the mandate creates none.

**Remedy.** Reverse the ruling that permits independent state reassessment of the federally governed warning-device selection and remand through the Sixth Circuit to determine its effect on the verdict and relief under ordinary procedure. The existing award cannot be affirmed automatically on unspecified alternative grounds and the whole action cannot be dismissed automatically. Delayed braking, horn warning and vegetation retain their actual procedural posture and defenses; neither their survival nor liability on them is newly adjudicated. The earlier speed and ditch-light dismissals are not reopened. No fixed new damages figure, mandatory new trial, agency study, gate installation, new federal damages action or filing deadline is ordered.

## Opposing position: Stevens and Ginsburg

**Operative rule.** A federal minimum-signage expenditure and completion of that installation do not by themselves establish the substantive federal selection/approval predicate that displaces an otherwise applicable duty to provide adequate protection at the crossing. The railroad must show the actual regulatory basis for the allegedly displacing choice; applicability of the regulation and lawful satisfaction of its selection conditions cannot be collapsed into the single fact of funding where those conditions materially distinguish passive signs from gates.

Easterwood's rule is substantial coverage of the actual duty, not a presumption that all duties disappear when federal money pays for some warning equipment. The automatic-gate conditions and the specific diagnostic exception show that the regulation contemplates more than minimum sign design. The trial evidence and the pavement-marking contention make the distinction between installed-device conformity and complete protection material. Neither asserts a found violation, but they defeat treating funding alone as an adjudicated adequate-device selection. No local-hazard exception need be invented because the objection is to the asserted coverage predicate itself.

Medtronic distinguishes permission to market from actual federal approval of the design, and its warning/manufacturing analysis rejects the claim that an applicable regulatory scheme necessarily supplies the substantive federal choice that a tort duty would alter. Stevens and Ginsburg joined those propositions. The statute differs, but the actual-predicate requirement is instructive and consistent with Easterwood. An enforcement scheme which never made the relevant selection cannot gain a substantive adequacy determination by inference from expenditure alone.

Their strongest difficulty is that Easterwood recognizes displacement by these particular regulations when they apply, and requiring express individual study risks adding a prerequisite not written into them. Their position does not impose a universal form of study or require one identified official to personally inspect the site. Adequate objective proof of the substantive federal choice under the actual regulatory branch could suffice. The present defense, however, relies on funding and completion without establishing that choice; the lower court's refusal to infer it is correct in result. They would affirm rather than remand to give a new opportunity to make the same unestablished defense. They do not find negligence, invalidate the federal project, award additional damages or decide an independent federal-compliance claim.
