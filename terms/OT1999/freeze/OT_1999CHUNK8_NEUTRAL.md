### Governing text and separable issues

Rule 31(c)’s elements inquiry under Schmuck, 489 U.S. 705 (1989), compares legally construed statutory elements; factual overlap alone is insufficient. ### Identity, posture, and claim

The state malpractice claims proceeded to a jury judgment of $35,000 in her favor. That judgment is outside this fiduciary-status review. ### Statute and competing readings

ERISA §3(21)(A), 29 U.S.C. §1002(21)(A), is functional and limited “to the extent” of the specified conduct: discretionary plan-management authority or control; authority or control over asset management or disposition; compensated investment advice; and discretionary authority or responsibility in plan administration. The asset-disposition branch is not rewritten to require additional discretion. Subparagraph (B)’s registered-investment-company qualification is retained in the exact text but is not a factual issue here.

Section 404(a), 29 U.S.C. §1104(a), imposes loyalty and prudence on fiduciary functions subject to its enacted qualifications; it does not make every medically discretionary act fiduciary. Section 409, §1109, addresses responsibility and restoration to the plan for breach. Section 502(a)(2), §1132(a)(2), provides the corresponding authorized plaintiff channel. Section 502(a)(3) is a distinct equitable channel, subject to Varity and Mertens, not an automatic substitute for a deficient plan-loss claim.

**Carle:** Plan design and the choice of an HMO are sponsor acts; provision of medical treatment and ordinary medical-business choices are not discretionary administration merely because they affect benefits. A physician making a medically inseparable eligibility/treatment determination acts in providing care. Financial risk sharing is part of HMO structure, which Congress recognizes. The existence of an ownership interest or general profit incentive is not by itself an ERISA loyalty breach. Plan assets, actual fiduciary function, breach, and statutory remedy each require separate predicates.

**Herdrich:** The employer’s HMO selection does not give a non-sponsor administrator freedom to distribute benefit resources through self-interested decisions. Carle decides medical necessity, coverage of disputed claims, and referrals, and allegedly directs money toward the same doctors who decide what care participants receive. A functional statute can reach those acts without treating every medical judgment or every HMO as unlawful. The complaint should receive its liberal pleading construction. The HMO Act does not expressly approve every undisclosed or treatment-specific incentive arrangement. The actual brief also contests Carle’s attempt to characterize membership, rather than care, as the benefit.

**United States/Labor Department, actual amicus:** Supports reversal but distinguishes treatment allegations from administration allegations. Medical treatment and compensation for its provision are not fiduciary administration. Pure discretionary coverage determinations can be fiduciary. A general profit-sharing arrangement alone does not show a breach; a direct per-denied-claim payment or an arrangement making impartial application of the plan impossible presents a different issue. It also contests treating all HMO premiums and year-end profits as plan assets. This is a materially narrower route than categorical immunity for every HMO administrative act.

The lower majority expressly disclaimed automatic breach from every incentive arrangement and described withholding medically necessary care solely to enlarge bonuses. Its reasoning and the pleading’s actual allegations must both be identified; neither that judicial characterization nor the malpractice verdict establishes a plan-asset loss. The distinct statutory-functions and insufficient-breach-pleading paths can lead to different holdings and reservations even if they share a judgment.

## Reeves v. Sanderson Plumbing Products, Inc., No. 99-536

### Identity, posture, record, and questions

Argued March 21, 2000; June 12 merits event. Certiorari to the Fifth Circuit, 197 F.3d 688 (1999), which displaced a jury’s ADEA verdict for Roger Reeves on Rule 50 sufficiency grounds. Review concerns the permissible discrimination inference from prima facie evidence and disbelief of the employer’s account, and how to examine the whole record on judgment as a matter of law.

Reeves was fifty-seven, had worked for the company for forty years, and supervised the Hinge Room’s regular production line. Russell Caldwell, forty-five, managed the department, and Joe Oswalt, in his thirties, supervised the other line. After an audit prompted by production and attendance concerns, the employer said the supervisors inaccurately recorded attendance and time. Chesnut, director of manufacturing and spouse of president Sanderson, joined the recommendation that Reeves and Caldwell be discharged. Oswalt had left before the final action; the employer claimed it would otherwise have discharged him too. Do not convert that counterfactual employer assertion into an actual comparator discharge.

Reeves offered testimony and documentation disputing the recordkeeping accusations, including use of the time clock for coming and going during a day, permitted records practice, and the handling of disciplinary records. He also offered evidence that Chesnut said he was too old and made age-directed comments, treated him differently from Oswalt, and exercised dominant influence within the company. The employer disputes the accuracy, discriminatory meaning, timing, and decisional connection of that evidence; other participants and some managers were older. Rule 50, rather than an operator credibility choice, determines what can be credited against the verdict.

The jury found age discrimination and willfulness; the District Court awarded $35,000 compensatory relief and matching liquidated damages, and later $28,490.80 in front pay. The employer made and renewed Rule 50 motions and alternatively requested a new trial. The Fifth Circuit accepted that the reason could be found pretextual but found the overall proof of age motivation insufficient. Its observations about timing of comments, other decisionmakers, and other older employees are part of its rationale, not statutory exclusions of evidence.

The ADEA provision is 29 U.S.C. §623(a)(1), prohibiting covered employment actions because of age; age protection under §631(a) begins at forty. The ultimate issue is actual prohibited age motivation. Pension status, unfairness, retaliation for some different reason, personal animosity, and a false account are not automatically age discrimination. No need exists here to resolve every ADEA analytical framework or a mixed-motive allocation not presented by this record.

### Competing legal positions

**Reeves:** A prima facie case plus evidence that the proffered reason is false permits, though does not compel, a finding of intentional discrimination. His record also supplies age comments and influence evidence; this is not a proof-free demand to infer age from any unfair dismissal. Rule 50 forbids replacing jury credibility decisions with an appellate weighing of the employer’s witnesses. His brief proposes particular restrictions on crediting the movant’s evidence and seeks protection for the jury verdict.

**Sanderson:** The plaintiff retains the ultimate burden of proving age motivation; merely disputing business judgment or establishing some inaccuracy cannot make every prima facie case triable. The asserted errors were supported, the comments were unrelated to the dismissal, other decisionmakers lacked demonstrated animus, and similar conduct prompted action concerning younger supervisors. The whole record may foreclose an age inference even where a proffered explanation is rejected. The brief disputes both the claimed pretext and the inference drawn from it.

**United States/EEOC, actual amicus:** Supports Reeves. The whole record must be reviewed favorably to the verdict winner, with legitimate inferences and credibility choices preserved. Prima facie evidence and persuasive falsity evidence ordinarily can support discrimination; occasionally the plaintiff’s own proof conclusively reveals a different nondiscriminatory reason. That is a sufficiency application, not a compulsory category of “additional evidence.” The Government also rejects an absolute rule excluding every item introduced by the movant: the question is what a reasonable jury could reject or interpret in the nonmovant’s favor.

A complete rule must distinguish: employer production from ultimate persuasion; permission to infer from a compelled inference; ordinary sufficiency review from an extra direct-evidence requirement; review of the whole record from crediting disputed employer testimony; and nondiscriminatory reasons conclusively established from merely possible alternative motives. A false explanation alone is not an automatic judgment for the plaintiff. A bare assertion that another reason is imaginable does not automatically entitle the employer to judgment either.

## Dickerson — record and posture

**Route and scope.** Review of the Fourth Circuit's interlocutory suppression appeal, 166 F.3d 667 (1999), in a federal bank-robbery/firearm prosecution. The indictment charges conspiracy under §371, three bank-robbery counts under §2113(a), (d), and three §924(c) counts. There is no conviction or sentencing judgment to vacate. The granted question concerns whether §3501 permits case-in-chief admission of the statement notwithstanding the Miranda ruling. The separate challenge to the Fourth Circuit's authority to take up the statute, and its physical-evidence ruling, are outside the supplied grant; do not silently expand review to them.

**Suppression findings.** Agents interviewed Dickerson at the Washington FBI field office on January 27, 1997 following investigation of a January 24 bank robbery. At the suppression hearing Agent Lawlor and Dickerson disagreed about when warnings and a written waiver occurred. The district judge found custody and credited Dickerson's testimony that all disputed statements preceded the warnings and waiver. The Government later supplied Detective Durkin's affidavit and Dickerson's handwritten statement with its reconsideration motion; the district court declined to reopen because it had not shown that the material was previously unavailable. The Fourth Circuit affirmed that refusal as within the trial court's discretion. Later evidence proffer is not an accepted new finding that the original suppression ruling was false.

**Voluntariness qualification.** The Fourth Circuit treated the statements as voluntary, reasoning that the district court's refusal to suppress derivative evidence necessarily reflected voluntariness. Attribute that conclusion to the appellate court and its reasoning. Do not recast it as a fresh Supreme Court credibility finding or a finding that warnings in fact preceded the statement. The constitutional issue can address §3501's claimed authority to admit a voluntary statement excluded by Miranda without resolving all fact disputes anew.

**Separate evidence.** The district court suppressed physical evidence found in the apartment; the Fourth Circuit reversed that ruling. It was not within the granted review. A separate request to suppress a backpack and solvent recovered from the car was denied. Neither the pistol/cash search nor a new fruit-of-the-statement theory is the judgment component now presented.

**Who argues what.** The United States does **not** defend §3501's displacement of Miranda. Its merits brief supports Dickerson on that question and argues against overruling Miranda, while preserving its ability to prosecute with other evidence and discussing the earlier evidentiary dispute. The judgment's principal defense is Cassell's amicus merits brief, filed March 9, 2000. Do not attribute that defense to the Solicitor General. ### Dickerson — statute in full scope

The exact statutory body was mechanically copied from the [1994 official Code](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/USCODE-1994-title18/html/USCODE-1994-title18-partII-chap223-sec3501.htm) into [3501_1994_statute.txt](../sources/chunk8/c/3501_1994_statute.txt).

- **(a):** In federal or District of Columbia criminal prosecutions a voluntary confession is admissible. The judge resolves voluntariness outside the jury's presence. If voluntary, the jury hears relevant voluntariness evidence and receives an instruction to assign the statement the weight deserved under all circumstances.
- **(b):** The judge considers all circumstances, including arrest-to-arraignment time when applicable, knowledge of the offense, advice or knowledge of the right not to speak and use of the statement, prior advice of counsel rights, and whether counsel was absent during questioning and confession. Presence or absence of a listed factor **need not** be conclusive. The statute does not command the judge to ignore warnings or declare them irrelevant, and does not make absence of a warning invariably dispositive.
- **(c):** Separate presentment-delay protection: voluntary statements within six hours of the relevant arrest/detention are not excluded solely for presentment delay if weight is left to the jury. The six-hour limit does not apply where the trial judge finds longer transportation/distance-related delay reasonable to the nearest available qualifying officer. Alvarez-Sanchez controls the separate duty and timing inquiry; this case need not decide all post-six-hour or McNabb-Mallory questions.
- **(d):** The section does not bar voluntary confessions given without interrogation or when the speaker is not under arrest or detention. These alternatives and their voluntariness limit remain distinct.
- **(e):** Confession includes both an admission of guilt and any oral or written self-incriminating statement. It is not limited to a complete confession to every charge.

A conclusion concerning voluntariness-only displacement of Miranda should not be described as wholesale invalidation of every subsection or destruction of the independent coercion inquiry.

### Dickerson — entering law and rival source arguments

**Miranda** supplies the predivergence custodial-interrogation safeguards and its allowance for equally effective alternatives. **Harris/Hass** distinguish impeachment use; **Quarles** addresses public safety; **Tucker** and **Elstad** distinguish derivative evidence and later voluntary warned statements from automatic suppression of all fruits. **Connelly** preserves the coercive-police-activity condition in the due-process voluntariness inquiry. These decisions must be considered for their actual settings, rather than flattened into either an equation of every warning violation with actual compulsion or an answer that Miranda has no constitutional authority at all.

The simulated authorities preserve warning safeguards while leaving this statutory question open: **Withrow** refuses to extend Powell's Fourth Amendment habeas restriction to properly presented Miranda claims; **Stansbury** gives the objective custody inquiry; **Davis** adds the specified postwaiver clarification duty and expressly reserves §3501; **Thompson** treats ultimate custody as a constitutional mixed question while keeping supported historical facts distinct; **Alvarez-Sanchez** construes subsection (c) narrowly and expressly reserves the statute's overall relationship to Miranda. Their decisions are not a prior adjudication that Congress may or may not enact this replacement.

**Smith v. Robbins** is a material current-term analogy. All nine permit genuinely equivalent alternatives to Anders's exact form. Stevens, Stone-Zsela, Souter, Ginsburg and Breyer find Wende inadequate because retained counsel, a factual narrative, independent judicial review and possible later briefing do not supply the missing advocate-assisted legal screening. The operative lesson and each Justice's application are available without deciding that an appellate-counsel case automatically determines confession safeguards.

The petitioner's and United States' briefs argue that Miranda is a constitutional implementation, demonstrated by its application to state courts and habeas review; Congress cannot simply restore the voluntariness test that Miranda found insufficient. They invoke workable administration, settled law-enforcement practice, reliance and stare decisis. Their argument permits effective alternatives and recognized exceptions; it need not establish a verbatim warning requirement, a civil cause of action for every violation, or actual compulsion in every unwarned statement.

Cassell's defense argues that the Self-Incrimination Clause bars actual compelled evidence, while Miranda supplies prophylactic safeguards broader than that prohibition; Congress may replace that remedial system without redefining the right. His proposed alternative reads §3501 together with judicial screening, jury weighting, warning-related incentives, civil remedies and police training. He invokes Bivens/Bush/Chilicky replacement-remedy reasoning, the Robbins principle and the contrast between unwarned and actually compelled statements. He alternatively asks the Court itself to modify Miranda's categorical rule to permit proof of actual voluntariness, even if Congress cannot accomplish the change alone. Do not collapse the legislative-authority argument into that distinct judicial-reconsideration alternative.

The competing response is that the asserted substitutes must protect the constitutional function, not merely provide a later remedy for some proven coercion; their practical adequacy is disputed. Reliance on civil-remedy availability must preserve actual cause-of-action and immunity limits rather than promise universal recovery. Boerne's legislative-enforcement principles must be taken from the simulated holding, not the historical brief's unqualified formulation. Empirical claims of interrogation costs, training improvements and warning persistence are advocacy, not judicially established facts.

Remedy remains tied to an interlocutory evidentiary ruling. Rejection of §3501 as a sufficient replacement removes that basis for reversing suppression and permits a bounded remand; it does not dismiss the indictment, acquit Dickerson, decide the independent physical-evidence ruling, or decide ungranted reconsideration issues. Acceptance of the statute or a judicial revision requires specifying the governing admissibility rule and its application/remand limits. A claim that every constitutional safeguard is legislatively irrevisable exceeds the presented question just as much as a claim that Congress may always replace one by voluntariness alone.

### Dickerson: lower judgment and its dissent

The Fourth Circuit majority treated Miranda as a prophylactic rule beyond actual compulsion and therefore regarded section 3501 as Congress's governing federal admissibility rule. Source: petition appendix, Fourth Circuit majority and Michael writing at 25a–27a; exact OCR in `../sources/chunk8/c/dickerson_02._Petition_for_Writ_of_Certiorari.txt`. The supported paths are distinct. A constitutional-implementation path may retain Miranda's case-in-chief protection while allowing genuinely effective alternatives and decide that this voluntariness regime does not supply one. Its strongest challenge is the body of decisions allowing uses of unwarned voluntary evidence and describing prophylaxis. A legislative-replacement path may distinguish the right against actual compelled evidence from judicial safeguards and evaluate section 3501 together with its proposed supporting protections.
