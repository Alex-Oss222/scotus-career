## 1. Questions, posture and remedy under review

The question is whether section 207(o) of the Fair Labor Standards Act permits a public employer to require employees to use accrued compensatory time without an agreement specifically authorizing that use, and what legal weight attaches to the Labor Department's contrary opinion letter or to its reading of its regulations. Separate statutory construction, interpretation of the regulation, the letter's authority and persuasiveness, and the actual agreement question. A statutory choice to allow compensatory time in place of cash overtime is not necessarily the same question as who may schedule use of an already accrued balance.

A certified class of Harris County Sheriff's Department employees sued the County and associated defendants. Its claims included compelled use, wrongful refusal of requested time, and retaliation. On stipulated essential facts the District Court ordered cross-summary-judgment briefing on the compelled-use policy. Its November 26, 1996 interlocutory declaration barred forced use under the FLSA; its July 28, 1997 final judgment repeated that declaration and awarded $21,360 in attorneys' fees against the County.

The employees did not seek a ruling on wrongful-refusal and retaliation claims or pursue error concerning those claims on appeal. The Fifth Circuit considered finality expressly. Both sides acknowledged appellate jurisdiction, and it held that the District Court intended to dispose of all nonabandoned claims. That is the lower procedural basis for a final judgment, not merely the parties' consent creating jurisdiction. The Fifth Circuit reversed and entered judgment for the County and all other defendants. The pending review is of that adverse final judgment; it does not automatically revive abandoned theories or establish individual liability, monetary damages, retaliation, or a wrongful refusal of a particular request.

Dennis agreed that the employees had not established entitlement to the District Court's categorical judgment, but opposed entry of judgment for the County and would remand for agreement-specific proceedings. This creates a real disposition alternative distinct from simply affirming the County or restoring the employees' declaration.

## 2. Stipulated policy and record limits

The policy keeps nonexempt employees' accrued time below a level determined by each bureau commander according to personnel requirements. An employee reaching the allowable maximum is asked to reduce the balance. A supervisor may direct reduction at a time suitable to the bureau; an employee may informally complain up the supervisory chain. The dispute concerns shortening paid workweeks while charging accrued compensatory time, not uncompensated time off or forfeiture of credits without the corresponding paid absence. The parties' budget and overtime incentives explain the dispute but are not statutory exemptions.

The majority stated that the parties had identified no controlling contractual provision. It therefore treated its employer-control approach as a default that a lawful agreement could displace. That is not a finding that no compensatory-time agreements existed, nor a finding that all employees agreed to forced use. Dennis noted the apparent existence of individual agreements incorporating County regulations and found the record inadequate to determine whether they contained applicable preservation, use or cashout terms or whether such terms complied with section 207(o). His proposal to retain jurisdiction while permitting agreements was a remedial proposal, not an existing agreement or judicially imposed bargaining duty.

The existing simulated Moreau v. Klevenhagen judgment supplies the actual earlier statutory agreement rules, including the separate pre-April 15, 1986 practice route. It does not establish the complete terms, individual coverage, interpretation or validity of the agreements relevant to the present forced-use policy. No presumption that all class members entered identical agreements is adopted here.

## 3. Complete operative statute and material regulatory qualifications

The full section 207(o), the 1985 implementing grant, section 553.23 and the material use/cashout regulations have been mechanically copied from the saved official sources into `../sources/chunk5-neutral-validated/christensen-operative-text.txt`. Do not substitute a brief's shorthand for the statutory triggers and qualifications.

- **Creation of compensatory time.** Section 207(o)(1) permits qualifying public agencies to provide time at no less than one and one-half hours for each overtime hour. Section (o)(2) requires the applicable collective/representative agreement or, for employees not covered by that route, a prior employer-employee agreement or understanding, and observance of the accrual cap. For the latter category hired before April 15, 1986, the regular practice on that date itself constitutes the agreement. The statute does not require a further individual agreement for that transition group. The distinct later-hire and legally capable representative rules in Moreau remain controlling.
- **Caps and cash payments.** Section (o)(3)(A) permits up to 480 hours for the specified public safety, emergency response or seasonal work and otherwise 240 hours; at the applicable cap, additional overtime must be paid as overtime compensation. Section (o)(3)(B) fixes payment for accrued time at the employee's regular rate when payment is received. The cashout regulation permits such payments at any time. These provisions establish cash payment rules; whether they also support compelled paid use is the disputed inference, not their express command.
- **Termination.** Section (o)(4) requires unused time to be paid on termination at not less than the higher of the average regular rate during the last three years or the final regular rate. Section 553.27 preserves rules for a genuinely permanent break in service, prior cashout and averaging where the last period is shorter than three years. The case does not adjudicate a termination payment or rate calculation.
- **Employee requests.** Section (o)(5) applies when an employee has accrued authorized time and requests its use; the employer must permit use within a reasonable period if it will not unduly disrupt agency operations. The existence and exclusivity of any employee control beyond that express trigger are contested. Do not equate reasonable-period permission with an unconditional entitlement to the employee's exact chosen date, or equate mere employer inconvenience with undue disruption.
- **Definitions and unrelated exception.** Section (o)(7) defines compensatory time as hours not worked, excluded from hours worked for overtime purposes, for which the employee receives the regular rate. Section (o)(6)'s separate court-reporter transcript-preparation exception has its own per-page compensation and outside-duty-hours requirements; no supplied fact makes it a basis for this Sheriff's Department policy.
- **Prior-agreement regulation.** Section 553.23(a)(1) implements the prior-agreement requirement and earlier-hire exception. Under (a)(2), agreements may restrict use to certain hours and combine cash and time while maintaining at least time-and-one-half. Agreements **may** include preservation, use or cashout terms if consistent with section 7(o); inconsistent terms are superseded. The permissive word is not itself a mandate to negotiate every scheduling term. Whether the regulation as a whole requires specific consent for forced use is contested.
- **Formation safeguards.** Section 553.23(c)(1) permits an unwritten individual understanding but requires a record. An express employment condition requires knowing, voluntary acceptance and information that time may be preserved, used or cashed out consistently with section 7(o). Notice plus failure to object may evidence an agreement, but acceptance must be free from coercion or pressure. Existing representative agreements and the April 1986 transition provisions retain their own qualifications. These are formation rules, not findings of consent by this class.
- **Use regulation.** Section 553.25(a) preserves the request/reasonable-period/undue-disruption rule and excludes the separately defined other compensatory time, including pre-April 15, 1986 accruals. Paragraph (b) forbids using time to avoid statutory overtime and coercing acceptance of more time than the employer can realistically and in good faith grant within a reasonable period of request. Paragraph (c) looks to customary practices, normal schedules, expected peak work, emergency needs and substitutes; applicable agreement conditions govern the meaning of reasonable period. Paragraph (d) requires reasonable, good-faith anticipation of an unreasonable burden on acceptable public service; inconvenience alone does not justify refusal. The present case does not establish violation of these separate request or accrual safeguards.

The official 1999 Code's section 203 note reproduces section 6 of Public Law 99-150: the amendments take effect April 15, 1986 and the Secretary must before that date promulgate regulations required to implement them. This is actual implementing authority, not automatic resolution of whether every later opinion letter exercises authority to bind courts or employers. The official historical CFR files saved under 1999 filenames visibly carry 7-1-98 edition headers; this packet identifies that printed edition honestly. The operative text is date-eligible and corresponds to the provisions quoted in the 1998 lower opinion. No later regulation or modern deference doctrine is supplied.

## 6. Strongest supported positions and narrower alternatives

**Employees/statutory path.** Section 207(o) is a conditioned substitute for the usual cash overtime protection. Prior agreement, limited accumulation and an express employee request right may imply that the employee cannot be compelled to consume time absent an agreement specifically permitting it. Cash redemption transfers money at the prescribed rate and is not identical to imposed paid absence. Allowing compelled consumption could prevent balances ever reaching the cash-overtime threshold and impair the negotiated substitute's value. The employees' reliance on Heaton's negative-inference approach is documented by the lower majority; the Court must decide whether the request provision is an exclusive mode or only a protected minimum.

**County/statutory path.** Section (o)(5) regulates the response to an employee's request and does not expressly forbid employer-directed use. Cashout permission demonstrates that employees cannot necessarily insist on banking every hour to the cap. Paid use satisfies the credit's defined function, while caps govern maximum accrual rather than guarantee accumulation. Congress addressed the minimum protections without prescribing every workplace scheduling rule; compatible agreements can constrain the County. This path need not adopt the Fifth Circuit's much broader economic preference for employer-set defaults in every unregulated employment question.

**Regulatory/agency path for employees.** Dennis reads section 553.23 with sections 553.25 and 553.27 as a negotiated system that permits specific agreement about preservation and use, protects practical availability, and allows unilateral cash payment but not unilateral forced consumption absent agreed terms. The letter expressly takes that view. The implementing grant, agency responsibility and longstanding statutory administration support arguments for authoritative or persuasive weight. The strongest objection is that permissive agreement language may not itself prohibit conduct omitted from both statute and regulation; the agency may interpret but cannot add a binding condition inconsistent with the operative text.

**County answer on agency weight.** The regulation permits additional terms rather than mandating a forced-use agreement. The letter may therefore supply a new restriction instead of an interpretation. Its opinion-letter form and supported procedural status also invite distinguishing a formal rule or authoritative adjudication from advice warranting only its persuasive force. The counterargument is that Auer does not reject considered agency constructions merely for their presentation form, and actual responsibility and statutory delegation require examination rather than a categorical label.

**Agreement-specific remand.** Even if the employees' categorical exclusive-control theory fails, Dennis's path would reverse the broad District Court declaration but withhold final judgment for the County until actual agreements and incorporated policies are determined under the lawful rule. Conversely, if the Court holds the FLSA creates no compelled-use prohibition and no preserved controlling agreement is established, it may affirm judgment for defendants while preserving any independently valid agreement or other law. The majority's statement that no term was identified is not itself a judicial finding of universal consent, and Dennis's proposed further proof is not itself a guaranteed trial or bargaining requirement.

## 7. Judgment, remedy and scope controls

An affirmance can sustain the County against this categorical FLSA challenge without endorsing every employer default rule, denying employee-request protections, allowing confiscation, excusing overtime above the cap, or overriding a valid agreement. The Fifth Circuit's judgment for all defendants displaced the declaration and its dependent $21,360 fee award; no new damages or liability determination follows.

A reversal based on an agreement requirement must specify whether the present record supports employee relief or instead requires further proceedings on actual agreement terms. A limited vacatur/remand can reject both categorical judgments and preserve ordinary proof, preservation and agreement-validity questions. It must not direct negotiation, find every agreement invalid, assume identical class terms, revive abandoned claims, calculate damages or impose a hearing absent a supported basis. The fee consequence follows the selected judgment and the actual later entitlement; no new dollar award is justified by this packet.

No supplied issue requires adjudicating a constitutional property right in accrued time, a public-sector collective-bargaining right, compliance with a different labor statute, or a particular wrongful-refusal/retaliation claim. The lower majority expressly preserved any other applicable restrictions on unilateral workplace action. Statutory permission and contractual permission remain distinct.
