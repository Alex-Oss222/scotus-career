## The source landscape and why the revalidation changes a material premise

Harlow, Anderson and the simulated Elder require objective particularized notice from all relevant preconduct law, without a factual twin or a Supreme-Court-only rule. DeShaney rejects a general obligation to protect against private violence but distinguishes an official's own creation of danger or increased vulnerability. Daniels excludes negligence. The simulated 1992 Collins keeps the federal deprivation separate from state duties and municipal attribution. Those governing rules do not themselves decide whether every supervisory instruction is affirmative deprivation.

Freeman, 911 F.2d 52 (August 6, 1990), is the material addition. The proposed specific account was that a police chief directed other officers not to stop an identified assailant because he was the chief's friend. The potentially suppressed protection was other officers' **public** service. The court allowed an amended claim to be pleaded and evaluated while reserving ultimate sufficiency, the necessary degree of participation and increased danger, and personal immunity. It did not adjudicate the proposed facts. Its procedural limitation reduces the strength of any claim that Freeman alone decided Saenz, but does not erase its concrete supervisory-interference analysis. The dissent disputed permission to amend, not a merits rule that such interference is always constitutional.

Wood, 879 F.2d 583 (June 27, 1989), supplies an adopted affirmative-deprivation and conditional personal-immunity ruling on its favorable removal-of-driver, vehicle, keys and transportation account. It rejects a demand for identical facts, considers relevant out-of-circuit law, and leaves assistance, awareness, causation and culpability issues open. It does not establish a universal duty to provide transportation or protect persons in dangerous places. Its September 1984 conduct-date inquiry is distinct from the opinion's availability by June 1995.

Ross, 910 F.2d 1422 (1990), likewise supplies a concrete rule and immunity application involving coercive obstruction of immediately available qualified rescue of a known drowning child without a meaningful alternative. It does not require malice, but requires the supported culpability, causal deprivation and known immediate mortal danger. Salas, 980 F.2d 299 (1992), distinguishes continuing replacement rescue despite its inferiority and failure. Its separate August 1989 immunity conclusion cannot be transplanted into June 1995. The absence of a substitute response in Saenz strengthens the interference analogy without establishing that an investigation would have prevented this collision.

Gregory, 974 F.2d 1006 (September 9, 1992) (en banc), recognizes a distinct affirmative-danger route but finds inadequate intoxication-awareness evidence and, independently, no affirmative placement in danger on its record. The lawful arrest left the sober driver opportunities to arrange help or retain the keys; the private choice concerning those keys supplied the dangerous access. It distinguishes Wood, reserves culpability and does not decide immunity. Neither those private choices nor its factual assessment may be invented in Saenz.

Piotrowski, 51 F.3d 512 (April 21, 1995), identifies a blocked warning about a planned murder and assistance to conspirators as potentially affirmative conduct, while expressly reserving adoption of the theory and pleading sufficiency. It resolves municipal-policy pleading with an opportunity to amend and decides no individual's immunity. Its discussion is relevant notice evidence at its qualified scope, neither an established liability holding nor irrelevant dicta. Fifth Circuit nonadoption of a general doctrine is relevant to the landscape but cannot mechanically resolve a specific affirmative-interference right.

The May 30, 1995 en banc Pinder opinion, 54 F.3d 1169, is also preconduct law, but its immunity judgment concerns Johnson's March 10, 1989 conduct. Nine judges supported immunity, not nine undifferentiated merits joins. Wilkinson had five complete adherents; Widener withheld three sentences distinguishing direct affirmative danger; Motz joined Parts I–IV and confined the holding to 1989 immunity; Hamilton and Luttig concurred only in judgment; four judges dissented. The direct-causation distinction has six express adherents. The recent contrary reasoning about assurance, reliance, charging and release matters, but does not determine a direct interference case by vote count or overrule Freeman, Wood or Ross. Its exclusion of post-March 1989 sources from Johnson's notice analysis cannot exclude those sources from June 1995.

The former independent handoff identified a gap between existing private-rescue principles and a superior's suppression of proposed **public** investigation. Freeman materially answers the assumption that no public-supervisory analogue was available. Wood and Ross strengthen the specific affirmative-deprivation and notice argument; Gregory, Salas, Piotrowski and lower Pinder constrain its reach. The four changed commitments rest on that newly supplied legal mechanism and each Justice's own prior position, not a changed Saenz fact or a desired vote margin. The other four genuinely consider the closer analogue and retain a narrower causal/protective distinction rather than the obsolete categorical public/private distinction.
