## Balanced material record

The Police Jury's previously precleared twelve-district plan had no majority-Black district in a parish approximately twenty percent Black. The School Board adopted it October 1, 1992; nonretrogression is conceded, and no party now challenges the absence of retrogressive intent. Prior approval for a different body does not establish this Board's purpose.

The relevant contrary-purpose evidence includes stipulated longstanding resistance to desegregation; the ineffective advisory committee and its replacement's quick dissolution when minority members sought a policy role; unanswered participation requests; delay after an initial proposal to adopt the Police Jury plan; accelerated adoption after presentation of a feasible majority-Black alternative; and departures from ordinary school-districting considerations concerning incumbents, school zones, compactness and contiguity. Burns's attributed account and the attributed Musgrove statement remain evidence of their stated character, with Musgrove's denial preserved. Dilutive impact is admitted, but impact is not intent. Polarization evidence has limited-data qualifications, while other-office electoral successes have Air Force base and effective-electorate qualifications.

The Board's legitimate administrative explanations remain relevant: prompt preclearance and easier implementation, including avoidance of precinct splits. The NAACP proposal required forty-six splits, Cooper II twenty-seven and one-majority-Black-district construction fourteen; the Police Jury plan required none. The ability to seek precinct changes and failure to pursue that option bear on the explanation's credibility, but availability is not an order that changes would have been granted. The Court cannot invent such a finding.

The return court again focused on retrogressive intent and left broader prohibited purpose unresolved. Its finding of lawful reasons is not a finding that those reasons alone would have produced the identical plan absent a racial purpose. That mismatch with the actual mandate is a legal defect, not evidence of litigant default or proof of motive by itself.

## Separate decisional paths

**1. Live controversy.** All eight reject mootness. No replacement plan is established, and clearance of this plan has continuing legal significance, including the operative benchmark for a later covered change. The 1998 election and expected post-census redistricting do not erase that consequence. Adarand's January 12 opinion and Laidlaw preserve the distinction between a forecast of change and an established end to the controversy. The Board's strongest argument—that ordinary use will end before the 2002 election—is addressed on its own terms; no inevitability of vacancy voting or failure to redistrict is presumed. Claimed availability of a special election is not a judicial award or necessary premise of jurisdiction. The holding does not keep every superseded plan permanently reviewable.

**2. Effects.** All eight apply Bossier I and Beer: admitted nonretrogression disposes of the §5 effect objection. A more favorable feasible plan or a separate possible §2 violation supplies no different §5 effect benchmark. Stevens and Souter preserve their earlier §2-incorporation disagreement but do not reopen it as an available controlling rule. No §2 violation, proportional-representation entitlement or maximization requirement is adjudicated.

**3. Purpose and present judgment.** The narrow-purpose findings do not answer the broader question this Court already directed. Vacate the clearance judgment in both appeals and remand for a determination of unconstitutional purposeful dilution under that actual rule and the existing allocation of proof. The court must examine the full evidence and the lawful explanations rather than demand proof of a plan to worsen the benchmark.

The strongest affirmance path relies on the existing legitimate-purpose findings and the lack of retrogressive intent. It fails because a lawful reason can coexist with an unlawful one, and no-retrogression answers the wrong purpose question. The strongest immediate-denial path relies on the accumulated historical, sequencing, explanatory and districting evidence and the Board's statutory burden. That evidence is substantial and cannot be dismissed as a §2 proxy, but selecting it as conclusive now would require the appellate Court to resolve the broader intent inquiry the trial court did not actually perform, including disputed testimony and the weight of admitted lawful reasons. The existing mandate ordered a factfinding task; the error is failure to perform that task, not a supported finding that only one actual purpose is legally possible. Remand rests on that identified legal defect, not on absent operator research or a presumption that more documents exist.

**4. Same-decision contention.** All eight reject affirmance on an assumed identical-plan defense. Neither ease nor anticipated clearance proves that the same plan would actually have been selected without the prohibited consideration. If such a showing is legally relevant, the jurisdiction—not private challengers—must establish independently sufficient lawful reasons that would have produced this identical plan; mere conceivable reasons or a materially different lawful plan cannot suffice. That is a limit on the suggested alternative ground, not a holding that the statute necessarily permits the defense.

The legal availability and ultimate preclearance consequence of a *proved* same-decision defense need not be decided to vacate this judgment. Bossier I reserved it; Arlington Heights/Mt. Healthy provide a serious constitutional-causation analogy, while §5's separate preventive purpose and Lesage's preserved process injury caution against automatic transfer. These commitments reserve that ultimate question. They do not direct clearance if a hypothetical finding is later made, impose a new proof quantum, make challengers prove every lawful objective pretextual, or erase independently unlawful racial procedures.
