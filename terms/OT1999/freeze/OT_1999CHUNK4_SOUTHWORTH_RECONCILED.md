## Reconciliation outcome and why commitments change

All eight Associates support reversal of the Seventh Circuit's general compulsory-subsidy liability rule and remand. O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Thomas and Ginsburg directly join a constitutional rule permitting compulsory support of the defined broad educational student-expression program with viewpoint neutrality as its operational protection. Stevens, Souter and Breyer agree that this stipulated neutral ordinary program may constitutionally be financed without ideological opt-outs, but withhold assent from making a newly formulated viewpoint-neutrality requirement necessary to every such fee arrangement. They support a separate judgment rationale limited to the present program. All eight support further proceedings on the referendum; the three do not thereby join the five's complete constitutional framework.

This changes the provisional Scalia/Souter/Thomas votes against the ordinary program and changes Stevens's and Breyer's provisional full-framework joins. These are corrections after adversarial source testing, not an automatic command to reproduce a historical tally. The following proposed changed premises were examined and found insufficient to support the departures:

- **Current Glickman recognizes that financing can burden expression without personal attribution.** The historical Southworth Court expressly began with the same proposition and the objectors' Abood/Keller interests, 529 U.S. 230–232. The current rule's recognition of a burden therefore does not remove a premise on which those Justices supported the university program. A recognized burden requires a justification; it does not predetermine which justification can succeed.
- **Current Glickman limits its permission to nonideological generic promotion in substantial collective regulation.** That limit means Glickman does not itself authorize ideological student subsidies. It does not convert its bounded permission into a universal prohibition of every different common function. Historical Southworth instead evaluated a broad educational program for independently selected, competing speech. Its analysis did not rely on Glickman as an exemption from First Amendment scrutiny. The current law leaves that new question open.
- **Scalia, Souter and Thomas opposed Glickman's expressive assessment and emphasized the legally required representative function in union cases.** The actual Southworth writings are case-specific evidence that the university's neutral distributing arrangement and different educational purpose could matter even to those Justices. A fee to that program is not forced membership in, or an unmediated charge to support, one organization appointed to speak for all payers. The recipient organizations' actual advocacy, including lobbying, was known historically; it is not a new factual distinction. Their prior dissent cannot be treated as a categorical resolution they themselves rejected in the more specific setting.
- **Current Glickman gives Stevens and Breyer a reason to recognize the burden and require a genuine institutional justification.** Their historical Southworth judgment concurrence already accepted that constitutional interests could be implicated and supplied a program-specific justification. Nothing in the current Glickman holding requires them to decide whether viewpoint neutrality is always constitutionally necessary when the parties stipulate that it exists here. A full necessity-rule join would go beyond what their actual case-specific reasoning supports without a different operative premise.
- **Current Rosenberger and religious-function law are narrower than some historical formulations.** They require preserving the actual printing relationship and reserving unpresented devotional-financing claims. There is no adjudicated devotional expenditure or live Establishment question here. Agostini, Jackson and Strout therefore justify a qualification, not a different result on this speech-and-association challenge. No Justice's religious-financing position supplies a fact absent from the record.

No supported legal, factual, procedural or remedial change explains retaining the provisional contrary result for Scalia, Souter or Thomas. No supported change justifies treating Stevens and Breyer as rejecting their historical necessity-versus-sufficiency limit. The corrected positions below preserve the actually supported common-function analysis and all current-law qualifications. They do not adopt historical authorship, later law, a new factual finding, or an automatic ideological prediction.

## Five-Associate common constitutional ground

O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Thomas and Ginsburg directly join every proposition in this section, at this scope.

**Operative rule.** A public university may impose a generally applicable student activity fee to sustain a broad program of independent extracurricular student expression serving its educational mission, including political, ideological and religious viewpoints, when viewpoint neutrality governs eligibility, funding allocation and administration. In that program, disagreement with a recipient's expression does not establish a constitutional right to an ideological opt-out or pro rata refund. The program must actually facilitate independent expression; a selected official ideological campaign does not become such a program merely by receiving an educational label.

The objectors' compelled-financing interest is real. Abood and Keller protect against compelled support for ideological expression outside the duties justifying a union or integrated bar's representative role. Lehnert's particular union chargeability applications do not dictate how a university may sustain the exploration of competing ideas. The common educational function here is the maintenance of opportunities for that exploration, not requiring all students to support a designated representative's preferred political program. Asking courts to identify which ideas are germane to an official educational orthodoxy would frustrate that function. Neither lack of attribution nor shared benefit alone answers the burden; independent student control, the broad educational purpose and viewpoint-neutral administration together provide the justification and protection.

Current Glickman is distinguished in its concrete setting, not overruled or enlarged retrospectively. It establishes that a germane expense of its specified collective economic system can be supported under its own conditions; it also rejects the premise that money, lack of attribution or majority approval alone eliminates speech interests. This case supplies a different and independently examined justification. It does not hold that ideological speech is nonexpressive or that useful programs always permit compelled subsidies.

Current Rosenberger supplies viewpoint equality within its actual independently controlled student-publication benefit, while reserving the compulsory-fee question now presented. That reservation is resolved for this broad educational expression program. No student is required to adopt a recipient's views, join its organization, or refrain from criticism or competing expression. A university may choose an optional or refund arrangement, but the First Amendment does not require one merely because a student opposes particular views in a constitutionally operated program. No empirical finding that all opt-outs are impossible or that every subsidy is indispensable is made.

Viewpoint neutrality does not mean equal dollars for every organization, funding every activity, or abandoning lawful program limits. The University may use reasonable neutral limits defining the activity supported, financial accountability, performance of the funded work and protection against fraud. Neither its educational label nor a majority's preference licenses discrimination among viewpoints within the program. No constitutional campus-boundary rule applies: off-campus location alone does not make an otherwise eligible student expressive activity invalid. Conversely, no unexplained payment to an external campaign is conclusively approved merely because a recipient labels it educational. Any genuinely preserved question concerning a particular expenditure must be addressed on its actual facts and the proper claim.

The ordinary allocation stipulation defeats the categorical liability rule for that scheme on the issues presented. It does not validate the binding referendum, which the parties excluded from the stipulation. If funding or defunding turns on majority approval of a group's viewpoint, the referendum defeats the protection the program requires. Access for minority views cannot depend on majority ideological consent. The described binding treatment must be examined; no neutral override is assumed. Remand is required to determine the referendum's actual operation and the appropriate relief, rather than uphold it unconditionally or declare every conceivable voting procedure unconstitutional.

This rule concerns independent extracurricular expression. It does not subject the University's own curricular choices, faculty instruction, institutional advocacy or government speech to this same forum rule, and it decides no general rule immunizing those activities from constitutional review.

## Three-Associate judgment rationale and withheld joins

Stevens, Souter and Breyer directly agree that the stipulated neutral ordinary program does not establish the claimed entitlement to an ideological exemption or the injunction entered below. They support reversal and remand on that basis, but do not join the five's declaration that viewpoint neutrality is a newly necessary constitutional condition for every compulsory university expression program.

Their ground is cumulative and confined to the existing record. A student's payment goes to an independently administered distributing program that itself advances no particular ideology, funding many competing speakers; it is materially different from compelled membership or payment directly to a single organization speaking for the payer. Neither personal affirmation, alteration of the student's own message nor compulsory association with an ideological group is established. The program expands discourse and supplies an actual educational function, unlike unrelated political advocacy by an exclusive bargaining representative or integrated bar. The fact that recipients engage in advocacy is acknowledged; the program is not held valid by pretending the speech is nonpolitical.

The University's educational judgment matters without rendering universities immune from students' constitutional claims. The analysis does not assume that choosing enrollment waives speech rights or that every imposed fee is an unreviewable general tax. Nor is the absence of attribution alone sufficient. The combination of a broad independent program, an attenuated connection between a particular payer and any one message, legitimate educational objectives and the actual neutral administration defeats the present demand. Ordinary curricular and institutional expenditures illuminate the setting but do not decide unchallenged programs.

The parties' stipulation supplies viewpoint neutrality; deciding whether it is necessary in other circumstances goes beyond deciding that it is sufficient in this program. These Justices preserve the actual Rosenberger viewpoint-access holding and do not authorize discriminatory exclusion in an opened private-publication benefit. Their objection is to extending the present fee case into a comprehensive mandatory constitutional test, not to the existing rule against discrimination in the relationship already adjudicated in Rosenberger. They likewise do not decide academic autonomy as an independent immunity theory or restore Glickman's rejected nonexpression premise.

They agree the referendum was not adequately resolved below and lies outside the stipulation, and therefore support further proceedings. Their remand agreement does not adopt the five's complete necessity rule or a final adjudication of any ballot. All religious-function reservations below apply to their writing as well. No historical author is assigned to this rationale.

## Remedy, qualifications and precedent treatment

All eight support **reverse and remand**: the Seventh Circuit's categorical prohibition on using objectors' allocable fees for organizations engaged in political or ideological expression cannot stand for the stipulated ordinary program. They do not reinstate the district court's already vacated all-students injunction or detailed mandatory administrative mechanism. Remand addresses the referendum and genuinely preserved unresolved issues within the actual claim and stipulation. There is no award of damages, fixed refund, specific WISPIRG appropriation, universal opt-in mechanism, or order to fund every recipient.

The ordinary SGAF/GSSF stipulation does not establish the facial validity of every written exclusion. The historic source expressly declined the late challenge to the partisan provision in light of the reiterated stipulation. The reconciled ruling likewise does not reopen a foreclosed challenge merely by saying “preserved issues”: only an actually available claim or issue survives. No rescission, revision or neutral enforcement of every written policy is invented. The separate GSSF lobbying/primarily-political restrictions are not exceptions to a different religious-activity clause. The two reported WISPIRG sums are not combined or used to quantify relief.

Abood and Keller remain effective for their representative and integrated-bar relationships; Lehnert keeps its actual Court framework and divided applications. The lower opinion's inference that every Justice in Glickman necessarily prohibited all compulsory ideological funding is rejected as an inference about this distinct setting, not an adopted rule. Current Glickman retains its bounded generic-promotion holding and recognition of expressive burdens. Current Rosenberger retains its actual access and controlled printing holdings and is extended only to the compulsory-funding question now decided at the supported levels of agreement. No Marks combination is needed: five Associates directly support the constitutional rule and three directly support the separate judgment ground. Agreement on the result does not supply eight full joins.

Gibson and Miller retain the protective alternatives and nonconsensual-arbitration rules applicable when a chargeability violation is established. They do not establish one merely from disagreement with student expression. Their procedures are therefore not imposed on the lawful ordinary program. If a different actual violation calls for relief, no remedy may be broader than the violation and posture support.

No Establishment, free-exercise or RFRA claim is revived or adjudicated. Religious editorial viewpoint is not identical to devotional governmental instruction. Agostini's unified Lemon inquiry, Jackson's actual funded-function rule, Strout's integrated-tuition application and Rosenberger's permitted controlled publication relationship remain effective. Neutrality alone does not authorize a demonstrated governmental devotional purchase; none is established here. The ruling does not decide the constitutionality of every activity funded under every possible program. Nonallocable services, ordinary government functions, the unchallenged student newspaper and Distinguished Lecture Series remain outside the present dispute.
