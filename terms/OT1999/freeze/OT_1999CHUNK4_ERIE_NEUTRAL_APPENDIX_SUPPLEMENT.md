## Counsel's statements about theatrical productions

At the October 19, 1994 preliminary-injunction hearing, the judge asked whether the ordinance would prevent productions such as *Equus*, *Hair*, and *O Calcutta*. City counsel Gregory Karle answered that it would not, **to the extent the expressive activity rose to a higher level of protected expression**. He invoked section 6's construction/severability clauses and the preamble's respect for constitutional speech and expression. The judge questioned where the exception appeared in the enacted language; counsel also urged the state court to construe the ordinance to protect others' rights. The judge did not adopt that interpretation in the exchange. Pap's counsel disputed it and argued that the text and uncertainty would chill productions. See printed pages 53–55.

At the December 21 hearing, Karle again said that the ordinance did not apply to protected Playhouse productions and attributed that position to the City and Council, relying on section 6. When arts-community representative Bill Morton challenged who would decide which productions deserved protection, Karle acknowledged a measure of subjectivity and compared it to obscenity enforcement. See pages 86–90. These are identifiable on-record municipal litigation representations and legal interpretations, rather than only a council sponsor's isolated remarks. The original neutral packet must include them when assessing purpose, actual operation, enforcement discretion, generality, and the proposed narrowing.

Later in that hearing, the City expressly asked the state court for a construction protecting higher forms of expression. At pages 104–105, the judge observed that the proposed exemption would require adding language, rather than just deleting words; Karle agreed and defended the court's power to do so. At pages 111–115, co-counsel Gerald Villella characterized the request as construing the law and severing unconstitutional applications, rather than reconstructing legislation. The disagreement about the source and legitimacy of a limiting construction remained a legal issue. Neither counsel's request, the judge's questions, nor an admission that particular text would have to be added is itself an enacted exemption or an authoritative judicial construction.

## The agreed *Equus* enforcement fact

The December 21 record goes beyond an assurance of future restraint. During negotiation of proposed findings, Pap's counsel initially accepted that *Equus* had played in Erie but questioned the proof of nonenforcement. Karle offered to put Villella on the stand to establish the fact. Pap's counsel then agreed to the factual item, while objecting to language describing the work as possessing acknowledged artistic and social merit. The City agreed to remove that characterization. See pages 79–81.

At page 84 the judge read the final version into the record: *Equus* featured frontal nudity, ran for several weeks in October and November 1994 at the Roadhouse Theater in downtown Erie, and attracted no efforts to enforce the nudity prohibition, which became effective during the run. Pap's counsel expressly confirmed the statement. The City then returned to its separate proposed finding number 8 about the Council tape; it did not withdraw or dispute the agreed *Equus* item number 9.

This is an **agreed historical fact accepted into the trial record**, not merely a unilateral City promise or counsel's untested prediction. It should not be described as an unknown enforcement history. Conversely, the agreement does not establish a general judicial exemption for theater, a binding rule for future administrations, an adjudication of the work's artistic merit, or the motive for every nonenforcement decision. The deleted artistic/social-merit phrase must not reappear as a stipulated fact. The transcript supplies the accepted factual item; this review does not claim to have separately checked its use in the trial court's written opinion.

## Effect on the supported neutral analysis

The existing law must be applied to this fuller factual record. The merits analysis may no longer treat the case as having only general language and council remarks, without concrete theatrical nonenforcement or a stated enforcement interpretation. The actual *Equus* fact and the City's higher-protection statements bear on the general-law distinction in *Barnes*, the relationship between purpose and operation, and the original packet's *Ino Ino* requirement to identify actual scope, justification, and fit.

The two legally different uses of this evidence must remain separate. The City can point to constitutional accommodation and the actual absence of enforcement against a theatrical work when answering the claimed threat to such works. The challenger can point to the City's expressed hierarchy of protected expression, subjective judgments, and actual differential treatment when questioning whether the asserted rule is unrelated to communicative content. Those are supported analytical possibilities, not determinations that either factual inference or legal conclusion is compelled. A relevant distinction based on noncommunicative harms still requires its own record and legal support.

An absence of a binding theatrical exemption does not erase evidence of actual nonenforcement. Actual nonenforcement of one production does not by itself supply the authoritative narrowing construction that *Ino Ino* applied to different provisions, nor does it establish a comprehensive enforcement policy. The original packet's account that the Pennsylvania Supreme Court rejected judicial addition of an expressive-nudity exemption remains the supplied lower-court disposition; this transcript does not supersede it.

No new mootness fact is supplied by the 1994 theater record. It establishes neither Pap's present interest after closure nor an independent claim by the theaters in this litigation. The existing threshold analysis and all later-law refresh requirements remain separate. The affected merits reasoning needs fresh revalidation before later stages rely on the neutral packet; no outcome or particular changed commitment is prescribed.
