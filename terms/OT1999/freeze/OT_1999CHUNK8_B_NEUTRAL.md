## 1. Arizona v. California — No. 8, Original

This section and the supplementary procedural extraction were independently reviewed for neutral framing and completeness. The validation qualifications below control; no adjudicative conclusion is supplied.

### Event and component boundaries

Original jurisdiction; exceptions to Special Master Frank McGarr's report concerning the remaining reservation-boundary water claims, with two proposed settlements. Argument April 25, 2000; scheduled decision June 19, 2000. There is no appellate judgment to affirm or reverse. Distinguish: (1) preclusion asserted from the Court's earlier Colorado River litigation; (2) preclusion asserted from the 1983 Docket 320 consent judgment; (3) approval and implementation of the Fort Mojave settlement; (4) approval and implementation of the Colorado River Reservation settlement. A ruling rejecting a preclusion defense would not itself determine Fort Yuma title, boundaries, practicably irrigable acreage or water quantity.

### Supported record

The December 1999 United States exception and its reproduced documents establish the following. The 1884 executive order created the Fort Yuma Reservation. The 1893 agreement, ratified in 1894, recited a surrender of approximately 25,000 acres upon stated conditions, including allotment and irrigation arrangements. The legal effect of the conditions and government performance remained disputed. Interior initially treated the cession as valid; its December 20, 1978 order, followed by a more precise 1981 order, adopted the position that the conditional cession had not become effective and the boundary lands remained held in trust. An administrative position is not itself an adjudication binding the opposing States.

The Tribe's Indian Claims Commission petition, later transferred to the Claims Court, sought compensation on several theories concerning the 1893 transaction. In 1983 the United States and Tribe compromised Docket 320 for $15 million. The reproduced August 11, 1983 final judgment disposed of claims the Tribe asserted or could have asserted against the United States in Docket 320, and reciprocally disposed of the United States' claims, counterclaims and offsets against the Tribe in that docket. The instrument expressly made the judgment a compromise and settlement and disclaimed its construction as an admission by either party for precedent or argument in another case. The parties waived appeal. The precise language, including each reciprocal limitation, is in [the downloaded motion and judgment](../sources/chunk8/b/arizona-docket320-motion-and-judgment.txt).

The United States characterizes the settlement consideration as compensation for temporary loss of use of the boundary lands and permanent loss of other lands, consistently with Interior's recognition of tribal title. That characterization is supported in its brief by the contemporaneous Interior approval letter reproduced in the appendix. It must not become an independent judicial finding that the title dispute has already been resolved against the States.

The Master rejected the States' argument that Arizona I and Arizona II themselves barred the boundary-water claim, but accepted a Docket 320 preclusion defense. His quoted reasoning assumed that the boundary lands here were lands included in the earlier claims docket, treated the disposition of all claims in that docket as barring water claims based on those lands, and did not treat the earlier Interior order as changing the final judgment's effect. The United States and Tribe contest that reading of the consent judgment.

### Settlement terms that must survive any summary

**Fort Mojave:** The proposal specifies the reservation boundary near the Hay and Wood Reserve; preserves competing claims to title and jurisdiction over the bed of the last natural course of the Colorado River within that boundary; allows diversion of the lesser of an additional **3,022 acre-feet** or enough water for **468 acres**; precludes further reserved Colorado River water claims by the United States and Tribe for lands within the Hay and Wood Reserve; and disclaims determination of private land claims, title or jurisdiction. Do not say every boundary question is left open: this settlement specifies the identified reservation boundary while preserving the stated riverbed and private-right questions.

**Colorado River Reservation:** The proposal allows the lesser of an additional **2,100 acre-feet** or enough water to irrigate **315 acres**; precludes further reserved Colorado River water claims by the United States or Tribes for reservation lands in California; leaves the correct location of the disputed boundary unadjudicated in this proceeding; preserves competing riverbed-title and jurisdiction claims; and becomes effective only on approval by both the Master and Court. The water settlement does not resolve the competing ambulatory-west-bank and fixed-line boundary theories.

The Master recommended both settlements as equitable resolutions. The United States supports their approval. These terms are recited with references to Report appendices 3–5 in the December 1999 brief. The complete report/settlement instruments have not yet been separately recovered. No broader release, acreage entitlement or title determination should be inferred.

### Entering law and supported competing routes

Pre-divergence Arizona I, 373 U.S. 546 (1963), the 1964 decree, Arizona II, 460 U.S. 605 (1983), and the continuing-decree provisions govern at their actual scopes. Arizona II's treatment of finality in the interstate allocation and its preservation of disputed boundary matters must not be flattened into either unrestricted reopening or an absolute bar. The supplementary [neutral procedural extraction](../sources/chunk8/b/arizona-procedure-neutral-extract.md) supplies the identified filing chronology. The legal consequences remain for independent modeling; the dates do not establish a waiver, forfeiture, law-of-the-case or sua-sponte-preclusion conclusion.

For Docket 320, separate claim preclusion from issue preclusion. Claim identity, parties, representative capacity and the forum's available remedy matter; the earlier compensation claim against the United States and this reserved-water claim against competing users cannot be assumed identical merely because the same lands appear. For issue preclusion, ask what was actually litigated and necessarily determined, or whether the consent instrument itself clearly supplies an intended binding determination. The final judgment's release and no-admission clauses must be read together. United States v. International Building Co., 345 U.S. 502 (1953), concerns the uncertain issue-preclusive scope of a compromise disposition; United States v. Mendoza, 464 U.S. 154 (1984), separately limits nonmutual collateral estoppel against the United States. Parklane Hosiery Co. v. Shore, 439 U.S. 322 (1979), supplies actual-decision requirements, not a substitute for them.

Simulated Matsushita and Baker require respect for the actual adjudicative effect of consent judgments while preserving distinct questions about what rights and persons they bind. They concern state judgments and section 1738, so they do not directly establish a federal consent judgment's preclusive scope. Richards v. Jefferson County preserves the need for a lawful basis to bind another person's rights. These holdings can inform the distinction, but no blanket consent-judgment or public-interest exception should be invented.

Supported alternatives: enforce an established claim or issue bar at its demonstrated scope; reject the asserted bar and return the unresolved Fort Yuma merits to the Master; or obtain the particular missing procedural/settlement material if its construction is necessary. The strongest argument for preclusion is final settlement of the same asserted land-loss predicate and the importance of stable interstate water allocations. The strongest contrary argument is that the compensation compromise neither adjudicated the disputed title/water predicate nor released the distinct claims against the States, while the earlier original proceeding preserved boundary claims. These are analytical alternatives, not invented quotations from unrecovered State briefs.

## 2. Crosby v. National Foreign Trade Council — No. 99-474

This source-fact extraction was independently reviewed for neutral framing and completeness. It supplies no adjudicative conclusion.

### Posture and statutory material

Argument March 22, 2000; scheduled event June 19. NFTC brought an April 1998 declaratory/injunctive challenge to Massachusetts's Burma procurement law. Cross-motions for summary judgment rested on stipulated facts and uncontested affidavits; no party asserted a genuine material factual dispute. The District Court granted relief on federal foreign-affairs grounds, rejected the asserted actual statutory conflict as insufficiently shown, and did not decide the Foreign Commerce Clause alternative. The First Circuit, 181 F.3d 38 (1999), affirmed on three separate grounds: foreign-affairs interference, Foreign Commerce Clause invalidity, and federal preemption. The approved granted question focuses on preemption; the disposition must account for the judgment's independent grounds without silently enlarging the grant.

The Massachusetts provisions are reproduced in [sections 22G–22M](../sources/chunk8/b/crosby-mass-law22g-22m.txt). The restriction applies to specified state purchasers and persons on, or determined to satisfy criteria for, the restricted list. It reaches the enumerated business relationships with Burma, including foreign firms; it is not an outright ban on all private trade or investment. Material qualifications:

- Section 22H(b) permits procurement after the prescribed written certification that procurement is **essential** and application would eliminate the only bid/offer or produce inadequate competition. Section 22G defines essential as necessary to perform the agency mission, with no substitute, to avoid irreparable harm to its programs.
- Section 22H(d) permits awarding to a restricted bidder only if no unrestricted comparable low bid or offer exists. Section 22G defines comparable as responsive and responsible and no more than ten percent above the lowest offer. The administrative application by adding ten percent to restricted bids is a reported practice, distinct from the statutory words.
- Section 22H(e) exempts operations solely for news reporting or solely for international telecommunications.
- Section 22I separately permits the described life-preserving, disease-related medical purchases, with its no-medical-substitute and professional-standard determination where applicable and written notice/certification. Its supplier exemption applies only where the person's Burma dealings provide **only** the defined medical supplies; mixed medical and nonmedical dealings do not receive that supplier exemption. Do not merge these two medical provisions or replace them with a blanket medical-industry exemption.

Congress enacted Public Law 104-208 section 570 on September 30, 1996. Its complete operative section has been extracted from [official enrolled-law text](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/PLAW-104publ208/html/PLAW-104publ208.htm) into [section 570](../sources/chunk8/b/crosby-federal-section570.txt). Preserve:

- Section 570(a)'s endpoint: a presidential determination and certification of measurable and substantial improvement in human rights and democratic government. The bilateral-assistance prohibition excepts humanitarian aid, aid promoting human rights/democratic values, and counter-narcotics or crop-substitution assistance subject to the specified notification and Secretary certifications of full cooperation, consistency with human-rights concerns and national interest. Treasury must instruct US institutional directors to vote against the specified loans/funds. The visa provision uses **should**, with treaty and mission-staffing exceptions; do not convert it into an unconditional **shall**.
- Under (b), the President is authorized and required to prohibit US persons' new investment after the particular determination and certification of post-enactment harm, politically motivated rearrest or exile of Daw Aung San Suu Kyi, or large-scale repression/violence against the democratic opposition. Do not describe this as unconditioned presidential discretion.
- Under (c), the President shall seek a coordinated comprehensive multilateral strategy with ASEAN members and other major trading/investment countries, including dialogue between the governing council and democratic opposition. Subsection (d) requires specified six-month reports.
- Subsection (e) permits temporary or permanent waiver of sanctions referred to in (a) or (b) upon determination and certification that application would be contrary to US national-security interests. It is not an unconditioned power to waive every state or federal sanction.
- Subsection (f)'s new-investment definition uses the certification date, the specified development/ownership/profit-participation arrangements, and excludes entering, performing or financing a contract to sell or purchase goods, services or technology.

Executive Order 13047 of May 20, 1997 implemented the new-investment prohibition following the presidential findings. The parties' briefs and joint appendix contain the order and underlying policy materials; distinguish authorized executive implementation, executive litigation advocacy, foreign objections, and binding statutory terms.

### Positions actually documented in the briefs

NFTC argues that the state restrictions operate as extraterritorial sanctions, expand the federally chosen coverage, interfere with presidential flexibility, and impede the coordinated strategy. Its brief also defends the two independent constitutional grounds below. ## 3. Hawkins v. Freeman — 195 F.3d 732 (4th Cir. 1999) (en banc)

### Posture and corrected material record

Approved simulation grant March 6, argument May 1, event June 19, 2000. Review is of the en banc Fourth Circuit judgment affirming dismissal of a **1995-filed section 2254 habeas action**, after discovery and summary judgment. The earlier divided panel judgment was vacated on rehearing and is not the judgment under review. This is a substantive-due-process challenge to reincarceration, not an independently presented procedural-hearing claim. Apply the actual pre-AEDPA filing transition; do not add amended section 2254(d)'s merits gate to this application merely because the appeal occurred later.

Hawkins received a fifty-year habitual-felon sentence in February 1981, with concurrent lesser terms. His offense remained governed by the former requirement to serve 75 percent before parole consideration; the later seven-year law applied only to offenses after July 1, 1981. His legally prescribed parole-eligibility date was April 20, 2018. The Commission gave conflicting early advice, including an erroneous 2010 date, then correctly notified him of 2018 in September 1983. In 1992 it nonetheless considered and granted intensive community-service parole under an inapplicable program. He was released July 6, 1992 after an investigative recommendation citing his favorable institutional record, education, work history, overcrowding and planned intensive supervision, while acknowledging concern about his prior sexual-assault history.

He lived with his brother, worked regularly as a forklift operator and received favorable evaluations. He committed no new crimes and violated no behavioral parole condition. **He did fall substantially behind in community-service and financial obligations**, producing a threatened revocation before the eligibility error was discovered. Therefore the approved packet's unqualified assertion that he complied with release conditions requires this correction. The State did not revoke him on those arrears.

On March 24, 1994 officials discovered the eligibility error, issued a rescission certificate and arrest warrant, and arrested him the next day. He waived the preliminary hearing. A counseled formal hearing occurred June 8, 1994; decision was deferred to allow supporting documentation. The Commission received favorable work/release affidavits and his religious-rehabilitation statement, not formally contested by the State, and revoked solely because the original parole was unauthorized. Its continuing 2018 eligibility calculation credited time on release. The State courts summarily rejected his challenges. The federal magistrate and district court characterized the administrative course as negligence and mistake, finding no inference of malicious or gross error. Distinguish that adjudicative characterization from a binding universal definition of the constitutional standard.

The en banc court left unresolved whether state law required revocation or afforded discretion: the statutes did not expressly answer the erroneous-parole question, and the Commission's procedure did not resolve its understanding. It offered alternative executive-conduct and fundamental-right analyses. Judge Murnaghan's dissent emphasized the formal review inducing reasonable reliance, actual community reintegration and finality traditions; his systemic-policy characterization differs from the majority's reservation. Neither party's framing establishes a proved statutory command to revoke every mistaken parole.

### Entering law and live paths

The operative Glucksberg holding concerns the claimed class-wide assistance-in-suicide entitlement. It expressly adopts **no exclusive most-specific-history methodology for all liberties**. The lower court's portrayal of that method as governing every claim is not the simulated rule. The operative Lewis holding requires harm-unrelated-to-law-enforcement purpose for the supported **rapid pursuit**; it explicitly reserves materially deliberative settings and disclaims a universal intent requirement. It does not automatically decide a parole commission's considered decision. Young v. Harper gives actual parole-like community liberty procedural protection, even where legal custody remains, but does not extinguish a lawful uncompleted sentence or itself create substantive irrevocability. Morrissey distinguishes conditional liberty, initial custody and final revocation; the hearing here must not be silently treated as absent.

Supported paths include rejecting the substantive claim because the valid sentence, correction of an eligibility error, lawful punishment/administration interests and credited release period defeat the asserted constitutional prohibition; recognizing an outer constitutional reliance/fundamental-fairness limit and deciding whether this record meets it; or remanding only for a genuinely material application question the chosen lawful rule leaves unresolved. The strongest prisoner position concerns the State's deliberate return to custody after twenty months of officially induced, faultless-in-origin liberty and successful adjustment, not merely negligent arithmetic. The strongest State response is that neither rehabilitation nor elapsed supervised liberty changes the sentence, and neither private reliance nor negligent administration alone creates constitutional immunity from serving it. A sentence-increase case must be distinguished from enforcement of an unchanged sentence.

Pre-divergence sources include Daniels, Rochin, Morrissey, DiFrancesco, Dumschat, Breest, Lundien, Cook, Shields, Piper and Johnson v. Williford at their actual holdings. DeWitt v. Ventetoulo, 6 F.3d 32 (1st Cir. 1993), is a date-eligible lower-court comparison involving a judicial reduction followed by reinstatement; it is not a Supreme Court holding or identical record. Lower-court common-law estoppel, sentence-credit and installment-service theories are not automatically federal constitutional rules. No permanent release, damages, state-law credit adjustment, sentence invalidation, or new procedural-due-process claim follows merely from rejecting part of the en banc methodology.

## 4. Miller v. French / United States v. French — Nos. 99-224 and 99-582

### Posture and actual litigation

Argument April 18, event June 19, 2000. Both dockets concern the Seventh Circuit judgment, French v. Duckworth, 178 F.3d 437 (1999), affirming an order blocking the PLRA automatic stay. The underlying class action began in 1975 concerning Indiana's Pendleton facility. Following trial and Pennhurst reconsideration, a federal-right remedial decree survived in part; its coverage included overcrowding/double-celling, mechanical restraints, medical care, kitchen sanitation and staffing. Some original provisions were vacated and remaining issues resolved by stipulation. This was not merely an unexamined private settlement.

Indiana filed its termination motion June 5, 1997. The district court issued a temporary restraining order July 3 and preliminary injunction July 11, 1997, preserving prior relief while termination remained pending. The United States intervened on appeal to defend the federal statute. The 1997 amendments became effective while the appeal was pending. The Seventh Circuit rejected the United States' proposed continuing equitable power, treated the stay as mandatory, and held it unconstitutional under its final-judgment and rule-of-decision analyses. The denial of rehearing drew a dissent accepting mandatory operation but defending the mechanism as implementation of changed prospective law and permissible expedition.

The prisoners' merits brief reports that the termination hearing was originally set for December 13, 1999, postponed by agreement to March 27, 2000, and rescheduled on their motion to June 27, 2000. These are litigation facts reported by a party, not findings that a ninety-day period was constitutionally inadequate or that an eventual hearing occurred. The merits of termination and present prison conditions are not resolved by this appeal.

### Statutory operation and qualifications

The [quoted current statutory provisions](../sources/chunk8/b/miller-plra-quoted-statute.txt) are mechanically extracted from the United States' petition appendix. They include the complete relevant (b) and (e) language and selected surrounding provisions, with omissions visibly marked by the source. The complete governing statutory conditions also appear in the opening Standards and Tests entries named below.

Under section 3626(b)(2), qualifying prospective relief entered without the substantive need/narrowness/least-intrusion findings is subject to immediate termination on the specified defendant/intervener motion. Subsection (b)(3) independently bars termination if the court makes written, record-based findings that retained relief is necessary to correct a **current and ongoing** federal-right violation, extends no further than necessary, is narrowly drawn and is the least intrusive necessary means. Earlier lawful/agreed modification and termination remain possible. Do not replace the conjunction with the pre-amendment **or**, require ritual findings language, or presume all retained duties comply.

Section 3626(e)(1) requires prompt disposition and makes mandamus available for failure. The amended (e)(2) opening refers to a motion under subsection (b); its timing clauses specify the thirtieth day for a (b)(1)/(2) motion and the 180th day for a motion under other law, ending on the final order resolving the motion. The presently challenged motion is the immediate-termination category; no general resolution of the retained other-law wording is required. Subsection (e)(3) permits postponement of the starting date for good cause for **not more than sixty days** and excludes general calendar congestion as good cause. Subsection (e)(4) treats an order blocking the automatic stay, except the specified postponement, as refusal to dissolve/modify an injunction, appealable under section 1292(a)(1) regardless of label or preliminary/final characterization.

The separate section 3626(a)(2) ninety-day limit concerns newly entered preliminary prison relief unless timely final findings/order are made; it does not automatically reclassify an existing permanent decree. Coverage excludes habeas challenging fact/duration, treats prospective relief as relief other than compensatory damages, and distinguishes covered consent decrees from private agreements whose federal enforcement is only reinstatement. State-court relief solely under state law remains outside these remedial limitations.

### Distinct positions and lawful paths

Indiana defends the mandatory stay as a temporary consequence of changed legal conditions on prospective relief. The prisoners argue that a party-triggered suspension before judicial application of (b)(3) displaces an Article III function and can expose them to constitutional harm even when the decree satisfies the new law. Their argument distinguishes valid substantive termination standards from automatic suspension before adjudication.

The United States' merits position is **distinct**: traditional equitable power should permit a court to preserve the status quo upon irreparable injury and likelihood of success, despite the ordinary automatic operation. It relies on the presumption preserving equitable authority, the difference between sixty-day good-cause postponement and traditional injunction prerequisites, the express appeal provision, and avoidance. Its merits brief alternatively concludes that mandatory operation can be constitutional, while describing that question as close. Do not attribute only Indiana's unqualified construction to both petitioners, or repeat the government's earlier appeal posture as its final merits position.

The live questions are statutory displacement of equitable power; facial structural validity if the mechanism is mandatory; and the proper treatment of a supported application that actually forecloses meaningful judicial determination. A construction preserving equitable intervention, a mandatory-but-valid prospective-law construction, and the prisoners' structural objection are distinct paths. Statutory text and the six-month/sixty-day provisions require analysis before constitutional avoidance. No as-applied violation is established by calendar arithmetic alone; no lawful path may preserve obsolete duties by ignoring the new substantive law.

## 5. Santa Fe Independent School District v. Doe — No. 99-62

### Posture, policy and source distinctions

Argument March 29, event June 19, 2000. The granted question concerns the football policy, not every prayer practice litigated below. The District Court rejected the unrestricted version but permitted its nonsectarian/nonproselytizing backup; the Fifth Circuit, 168 F.3d 806 (1999), rejected both for football games. The separate graduation disposition and affirmed damages denial are not reopened through this grant.

The October 1995 policy, reproduced at joint appendix 104–105, begins with the board's choice to permit a brief invocation and/or message during home-varsity-football pregame ceremonies, for solemnization, sportsmanship, student safety and an appropriate competitive environment. Each spring the student council, **upon advice and direction of the principal**, conducts a secret-ballot election of the high-school student body on whether to include the statement/invocation; if approved, students elect one volunteer to deliver it. The selected student may choose the message/invocation consistently with the policy's goals. The backup repeats the policy and adds the nonsectarian/nonproselytizing limit if the first version is enjoined. See [exact downloaded policy](../sources/chunk8/b/santa-fe-football-policy-ja104-105.txt). Do not describe this as unrestricted access by every student or as an express command that the selected speaker must pray.

Before suit, the student-council chaplain delivered prayers over the public-address system at home games. The April 1995 suit led to interim orders and revised policies. An August version spoke of invocation alone; October added message language and the additional purposes. The stipulated August 31 and September 7 elections selected prayer and a speaker under the earlier arrangement. The parties dispute what legal significance the earlier practices and votes retain for the October policy. The record contains no text of prayers delivered under the challenged October policy; that absence does not erase the policy or earlier stipulated events.

The district invokes interim-order directions giving students election mechanics, forbidding school substantive input/text preapproval and restricting officials to order/safety. Respondents contest incorporation and continuing effect of those interim directions in the final October policy and judgment. They point to its own principal-direction and purpose constraints and to the student-council constitution's school responsibilities/adviser veto. Present those documents distinctly. Do not silently add the interim limitations to the written policy as undisputed terms, and do not invent actual editorial review of a particular October message.

The lower record also contained incidents of religious promotion and hostile treatment of objectors. Respondents use these as contextual attribution/purpose evidence; the district points to remedial measures and the denial of damages. The damages result does not prove that every underlying event was imaginary, while allegations or another lawsuit's later facts cannot be adopted as trial findings. Respondents' merits brief discusses Marian Ward's separate 1999 litigation as amicus-supplied material; that is not automatically part of the closed trial record or a new in-world Supreme Court action.

### Entering law

The governing **Lemon test**, as modified by simulated Agostini (June 23, 1997), uses purpose, effect and entanglement as evidence within an inquiry into governmental religious authority and function. The former three independent prongs do not remain a fallback. A specific prohibition controls when it resolves the governmental conduct; an established permissible relationship can resolve the claim; otherwise attribution/function is adjudicated under ordinary procedure. No conversion or completed penalty is necessary once prohibited official worship is established.

**Official graduation worship and independent religious pressure** includes two independently sufficient Lee rules: official organization of worship, and substantial pressure in the school-directed setting. Ingebretsen (March 19, 1997) expressly rejects student delivery/initiation and disclaimers as automatic insulation of school-organized worship. Tanford (December 22, 1997) extends the identified official-worship rule to university commencement without a separate coercion requirement. These holdings are narrower than a prohibition of all student religious speech and broader than a rule limited to officials personally composing prayers.

**Private religious viewpoints in public forums and printing benefits** preserves Lamb's Chapel, Pinette, Chabad, Rosenberger and Bronx Household (May 11, 1998) at their actual private-access relationships. Government location alone is insufficient, but a forum label alone is also insufficient; selection, adoption, control, financing and attribution matter. Strout (February 28, 2000) retains the distinction between genuine private-expression access and government purchase/performance of a religious function. It supplies no new football-specific rule.

### Competing paths and limits

The district's strongest supported path is that the written policy permits secular speech or no speech, relies on independent student choices, and preserves private religious viewpoints without school censorship; as a facial challenge it cannot be decided by assuming a particular future prayer. It relies on secular purposes, optional attendance, student authorship and the interim-order restrictions. Its principal obstacle is whether the selective representative election and official-event slot constitute school adoption rather than a neutral private forum.

Respondents' strongest supported path is that the board deliberately integrates a representative invocation slot into its own event and delegates its religious choice to a majority; retained policy purposes, principal involvement and history show institutional attribution despite student composition. They also argue that attendance obligations for participants and the occasion's social role can impose independent religious pressure. Distinguish those factual assertions and the legal significance of official sponsorship; do not make quantified coercion indispensable to a claim already established under the independent worship rule.

Possible lawful dispositions include sustaining the football-policy judgment on supported attribution/sponsorship grounds, sustaining an independently supported pressure ground, rejecting facial invalidation if actual neutral access is established, or a bounded remand if a material document-effect or factual issue is necessary and unresolved. A facial challenge to the governmental selection/election mechanism is not identical to censoring hypothetical future private speech; conversely, every neutral forum with possible religious speech is not facially invalid. Relief must preserve genuinely voluntary private prayer and independent expression, and must not reopen graduation or damages questions outside the grant.
