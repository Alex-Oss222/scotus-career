## Source and proposition checks

### Information, approval and the actual permissions

Section 222(a)'s confidentiality duty and section 222(c)(1)'s general approval language are distinct from the agency's method. Unless required by law or approved by the customer, individually identifiable CPNI obtained through providing telecommunications service is confined to the service from which it derives or services necessary to or used in providing that service, including directories. Aggregate customer information has separate treatment, and subscriber-list information is excluded from CPNI. Neither the case nor the general approval rule concerns all customer information or every form of marketing.

The original section 222(d) permissions cover initiating, rendering, billing and collecting for telecommunications services; protecting carrier rights or property and protecting users or other carriers against fraudulent, abusive or unlawful service use or subscription; and inbound telemarketing, referral or administrative services during a customer-initiated call when the customer approves use of the information to provide that service. The last permission is limited by the call's duration and approval; it does not authorize indefinite subsequent marketing.

The challenged total-service rule permits providing or marketing local, interexchange and commercial-mobile offerings within service categories the customer already buys from that carrier. Affiliate sharing is permitted among affiliated entities supplying the customer's subscribed service offerings when the customer subscribes to more than one category, and the corresponding affiliate permission is absent when the customer does not. Crossing into an unsubscribed category requires approval, subject to the rule's distinct permissions for inside-wiring installation, maintenance and repair; mobile-service health-effects research; and marketing former adjunct-to-basic services. The latter includes speed dialing, computer-provided directory assistance, monitoring, tracing, blocking, return, repeat dialing, tracking, waiting, caller identification, forwarding and certain centrex features. The source's adjunct examples are illustrative, not an exhaustive closed list.

The equipment/information-service restriction, competitor-tracking prohibition and former-customer winback prohibition have different texts. No constitutional approval of all those provisions follows from a decision on the general approval mechanism; no approval exception is inserted into a categorical provision merely because a nearby provision contains one. The intervening removal and amendment provisions retain their separate effect below.

Express approval may be written, oral or electronic. A carrier relying on oral approval bears the burden of demonstrating compliant approval. Approval continues until revoked or limited, and notification/approval records must be retained at least one year. Prior one-time notice identifies the CPNI, specific recipients and purposes, the customer's right and carrier's duty, precise steps for granting or denying access, the right to disapprove uses and withdraw access, and the fact that refusal does not affect subscribed services. Notice must be comprehensible and nonmisleading. Written notice must be legible, sufficiently large and readily apparent; translating any part requires translating all parts. The carrier may describe tailored-offering benefits and possible compelled disclosure upon the customer's affirmative written request, but may not encourage a freeze on third-party access. Notice must describe the continuing effect of approval or denial until affirmative revocation or limitation; solicitation must be proximate to notice. The removed same-document rule is not reinstated by this handoff.

These concrete avenues matter to fit but do not make the burden disappear. Other marketing remains possible, yet targeted commercial communication is protected. No Justice treats the availability of broad marketing as an exemption from First Amendment review.

### Evidence and the two supported constitutional paths

The record reports 33 percent expressly refusing approval, 28 percent approving, and 39 percent hanging up or asking not to be called again in the outbound study. The last group is not established to have made an informed privacy refusal. Seventy-two percent approved when approached after initiating contact. The residential direct-mail response rate was 6–11 percent and the business response rate 5–9 percent. Those mail figures measure response, not express rejection. Neither the agency's privacy interpretation nor the carrier's indifference interpretation is converted into a finding about every nonrespondent.

The agency's statement that sharing within an integrated firm does not raise significant privacy concerns is material adverse support for the carrier. All six sustaining the approval method address it. They distinguish the identity of the holder from the use made of confidential service-derived information: the customer has already chosen particular service categories, and extending use into a different market requires an affirmative choice. They do not invent an actual outside disclosure, an individual injury or a conclusion that every internal transfer is harmful. Existing-category, related-service and distinct health-research permissions do not reproduce the unapproved new-category use they find constitutionally regulable. The source and purpose of the information, the existing service relationship and a practical approval mechanism supply the concrete distinction.

Stevens and Kennedy regard that answer as insufficient on this record. The agency's own internal-sharing assessment and the breadth of permitted existing-category marketing weaken the asserted privacy distinction between similar internal uses. They do not deny the sensitivity of calling records or the substantial confidentiality interest. Their objection is that the actual selected category and consent burden remain inadequately justified, not that all privacy rules need numerical studies or regulation of every information source.

Fit receives independent examination on both paths. The six find the flexible express methods, continuing approval and direct opportunity to seek permission proportionate to preventing unknowing repurposing of confidential records. The FCC rejected written-only permission as too restrictive and considered opt-out, explaining that unread notices can make silence uninformed. The six do not infer that every silence is refusal or require a perfect consent mechanism. Stevens and Kennedy find the showing inadequate even assuming advancement: concern about unread notices does not itself establish that sufficiently clear notice with opt-out fails to protect the actual internal-use interest, while low affirmative response can exclude willing recipients from effective tailored communication. They recognize the actual oral/electronic choices and consideration of opt-out. Neither path adopts least restrictive means, dictates a replacement consent system, or treats another advertising channel as eliminating the burden.

### Statutory permission, independent scrutiny and the new express rule

All eight accept the textual proposition that general section 222(c)(1), read with the confidentiality duty, leaves room for affirmative approval; it does not unambiguously require that exclusive method for all general CPNI uses. The statutory range and constitutional application remain separate. For the six, independent constitutional review rejects the asserted speech-based serious-doubt ground, and flexible affirmative approval is a reasonable construction of the general text. For Stevens and Kennedy, textual room for an approval method cannot save its unconstitutional application, and deference does not extend to that application. Their agreement about the statutory range is not a reversal vote or agreement that every application is constitutional.

The still-unresolved takings objection is not swept into a declaration that every constitutional objection has failed. Nor does constitutional sufficiency resolve the preserved arbitrary-and-capricious objections. AT&T v. Iowa Utilities Board supports review within enacted delegations and limits, not blanket FCC immunity from constitutional or administrative review.

Public Law 106-81 section 5 took effect October 26, 1999 without a section 5 delay. New section 222(f) requires express prior authorization for wireless call-location information except the section 222(d)(4) uses, and for automatic-crash-notification information except use in operating an automatic-crash-notification system. The section 222(d)(4) permissions are: provision to a public safety answering point, emergency medical or emergency dispatch provider, public safety, fire-service or law-enforcement official, or hospital emergency or trauma-care facility to respond to the user's emergency-services call; informing the user's legal guardian or immediate family of the user's location in an emergency involving risk of death or serious physical harm; and provision to information- or database-management providers solely to assist delivery of emergency services in response to an emergency. These exceptions do not authorize ordinary marketing. The crash-notification exception is its own exception, not a shorthand incorporation of the call-location exceptions.

The amendment inserts location into the CPNI definition, relocates definitions to subsection (h), and imposes a separate emergency-service information duty. Its enacted subsection (g) cross-reference is not repaired or construed here. The general approval ruling neither weakens the express prior-authorization commands nor retroactively supplies evidence for the FCC's 1998 rationale. No constitutional challenge to those later commands is decided; no universal claim that opt-out is legally available for every CPNI category is permissible.
