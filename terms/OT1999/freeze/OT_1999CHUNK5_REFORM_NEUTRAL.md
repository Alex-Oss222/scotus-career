## Event, channel and scope

Reform Party of Allegheny County v. Allegheny County Department of Elections concerns the Third Circuit's March 30, 1999 en banc judgment, 174 F.3d 305, in Nos. 96-3677 and 97-3359. The authorized Supreme Court grant is January 10, 2000, argument March 6, and decision placement April 10. The county election authorities seek reversal. The three granted questions concern (1) major/minor-party unequal access to fusion for specified offices, (2) scrutiny of a law burdening electoral association and discriminating between those parties, and (3) whether the State must justify the distinction itself. They do not authorize a freestanding ruling that every party has a right to fusion.

The lower judgment has two procedural components. No. 97-3359 concerns denial of the Department's Rule 60(b)(6) motion to reopen the earlier Eshenbaugh judgment. No. 96-3677 concerns summary judgment for the Party in Childress's case and the associated prospective injunction. Preserve that distinction in any judgment description; the full original merits of the already final Eshenbaugh judgment are not automatically reopened by review of the later Rule 60 motion. The Rule 60(b)(4) theory was abandoned on appeal. No nonparticipation is established; the entering slice supplies the nine seated Justices and ordinary majority/quorum requirements.

## Supported record and statutory operation

The Party sued the Department and its director for declaratory and injunctive relief under §1983. The two elections had concluded, but the litigation concerns an ongoing restriction and prospective injunction affecting the Party's nominations. There is no admitted repeal, abandonment, or other event making the prospective controversy advisory. No new election, historical vote count, or damages claim is supplied.

Pennsylvania generally prevented multiple-party nominations, but §2870(f) permitted major-party cross-nomination for judge of a court of common pleas, judge of Philadelphia Municipal Court, judge of Philadelphia Traffic Court, elective school director, and justice of the peace. Major parties nominated through primary nomination petitions. The Party, treated as a minor party, used nomination papers bearing required signatures. Section 2936(e) barred acceptance of a nomination paper when the named candidate had filed a nomination petition for a public office at the ensuing primary or had been nominated through previously filed nomination papers. Section 2911(e)(5) required the candidate's affidavit disclaiming those prior presentations or nominations. The lower opinion's footnote 5 quotes the challenged portions, with ellipses; it is evidence of their pertinent operation, not a claim to reproduce the full Election Code or every unrelated exception.

Eshenbaugh sought both major parties' nominations for North Allegheny school director in the May 1993 primary, obtained the Democratic nomination but lost the Republican primary, and afterward accepted the Party's nomination. The Party's 1994 suit first failed in district court, then succeeded on appeal in September 1996; after denial of rehearing, the Department did not seek further review. On remand, declaratory and injunctive relief issued. The Department then sought Rule 60 relief after Timmons in April 1997.

Childress was selected by the Party on May 13, 1995 and filed its nomination papers on May 16 before the municipal primary. She also sought and won both major-party nominations. The Department rejected the Party nomination because she had previously filed major-party primary petitions. The district court granted the Party summary judgment and relief. The en banc court affirmed both the Rule 60 denial and Childress relief, retaining the injunction but directing deletion of the separate free-association declaration.

## Lower grounds, arguments and contested reach

The en banc majority treated the unequal opportunity as more burdensome than the fusion restriction in Timmons. It applied an intermediate, sufficiently-weighty-interest inquiry rather than automatic strict scrutiny, and found the Department's asserted interests inadequate to justify the asymmetry. It confined its merits judgment to equal protection and declined to decide the independent associational claim. The Department's four preserved interests were preventing sore-loser candidacies; preventing ballot monopolization, crowding and confusion; preventing candidates from using minor-party votes to bolster major-party endorsements; and encouraging new independent candidates. The court reasoned that major-party fusion undermined each distinction: Childress lost neither primary; Eshenbaugh could still run as a Democrat despite losing the Republican primary; allowing major-party alliance created comparable ballot effects; voluntary cross-nomination did not involuntarily siphon Party votes; and major-party consolidation did not encourage more competitors.

The majority recorded that the Department did not explain why the legislature allowed the major-party exception alone. The Commonwealth declined opportunities to intervene or submit a brief. These are litigation-history facts, not litigant defaults or automatic constitutional invalidity. At oral argument the Department first asserted a nonpartisanship interest; it also belatedly advanced major-party voters' interest in identifying candidates who would refuse minor-party affiliation. The lower court found those arguments unpreserved and also insufficient on their terms. They may be described as lower-court contentions, but must not be silently converted into properly preserved new Supreme Court factual showings.

McKee concurred in the result but argued that a discriminatory classification burdening fundamental electoral rights requires strict scrutiny. Greenberg joined the Rule 60 disposition and Childress's result but dissented from the injunction's broader reach. He considered pre-primary cross-filing unequal treatment but post-primary nomination materially different because major parties had already selected their nominees. He relied on Pennsylvania's severability statute, 1 Pa. Cons. Stat. §1925, to preserve valid applications. That statute ordinarily preserves provisions and applications unless they are inseparably dependent on the invalid part or the remainder is incomplete and incapable of execution consistently with legislative intent. This supplies a real narrower remedial path; timing must not disappear from the packet.

The Rule 60 majority found no extraordinary circumstances: Timmons did not displace the independent equal-protection ground, and reopening could not substitute for the deliberately forgone appeal. The second ground is distinct from the election merits. The relevant standard is abuse of discretion for denial of reopening, with legal errors reviewed as such; Childress summary judgment receives plenary review. Do not silently substitute the institutional-decree modification rule for the pleaded Rule 60(b)(6) motion.

## Entering law and supported alternatives

The exact governing holdings, coalitions and register titles are in the entering slice. Simulated Timmons, April 28, 1997, sustains the particular Minnesota restriction under Anderson and Burdick by Thomas, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy and Breyer. Its actual limits matter: it does not decide the distinct ban on two major parties nominating the same candidate, nor create universal anti-fusion approval. Its limited-burden application does not resolve Pennsylvania's unequal mechanism. Simulated Burdick's calibrated review remains binding: assess actual injury, precise asserted interests and necessity; severe restrictions require compelling interest and narrow tailoring, while reasonable nondiscriminatory restrictions ordinarily need important interests. Norman protects authorized party expansion and independently qualified components without forbidding meaningful support requirements. Earlier historical post-divergence opinions cited by the Third Circuit are evidence of that court's reasoning, not replacements for this baseline.

Supported paths to test independently include: affirm equal-protection relief using actual burden and inadequate justification without selecting strict scrutiny; adopt the concurrence's strict-scrutiny ground if justified by surviving constitutional authority and reconcile it with Burdick's calibration; or distinguish timely pre-primary cross-filing from later nominations and affirm Childress's entitlement while narrowing prospective relief under the severability principle. The Department's strongest supported answer is that fusion itself imposes a modest burden and ordinary nomination finality, differentiation of primary and petition mechanisms, and preventive election administration justify treatment reflecting different selection processes. Its strongest remedial argument is Greenberg's timing distinction. The counterargument is that Childress complied before the primary and the enacted bar depends on party status rather than merely a uniform finality deadline. General support for neutral election rules does not itself answer the unequal-treatment question.

An outright merits reversal requires an actual justification for the discrimination under the governing review, not an invented legislative motive. A narrower remand is available if the scope or administration of the prospective injunction needs adjustment. The existing injunction prevents the defendants from using the challenged sections to deny a minor party's nomination for an office listed in §2870(f) because the candidate files a major-party petition or wins a major-party primary. Relief must identify whether that breadth remains or is narrowed; it cannot merely declare an abstract equality rule. The Court may leave Pennsylvania free to enact an otherwise lawful uniform restriction, but does not enact one itself. No reopening of completed elections follows.
