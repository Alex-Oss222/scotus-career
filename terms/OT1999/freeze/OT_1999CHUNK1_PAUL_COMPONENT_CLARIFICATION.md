## Determination

Scalia and Thomas support leaving in force the State defendants' summary judgment on the information-disclosure claim, on their frozen ground that the claimed substantive constitutional extension has not been established. Their agreement on that component is judgment-only relative to P1–P2. They reject the privacy-right reasoning and would vacate the safeguard directive to the extent that it depends on that rejected extension, followed by treatment on remand consistent with their rejection and preservation of W.P.'s independent state-entitlement process.

This conclusion interprets the scope of the frozen limitation; it does not replace a vote to vacate the State's favorable summary judgment with a newly modeled vote to affirm it. Scalia's frozen row names the objects of vacatur: the privacy-right ruling and the safeguard directive, insofar as dependent on the extension. Thomas expressly adopts that same limited vacatur. Neither row says that the State's favorable information-disclosure judgment is vacated. Their substantive rejection removes the necessary right underlying that claim and supplies a sufficient ground for the State to prevail on it. No frozen threshold defect, alternative preserved disclosure ground, or needed factual determination is stated that could make rejection of that right a reason to set aside the State's favorable judgment.

The alternative reading—counting both Justices against the State's summary judgment merely because they reject the Court's recognition and balancing rationale—would conflate reasons with judgment. It would also enlarge their expressly limited vacatur beyond its named objects. Engine sections 7–9 require the opposite separation: different sufficient grounds may support a common judgment component without creating a common legal proposition.

The lower opinion itself makes the necessary separation. Section III rejects the information-disclosure challenge as a matter of law after balancing; section V addresses later motions and unnecessary dissemination; section VI retains summary judgment while sending that later material to the District Court. The six Associates' frozen P1–P3 support the former result through the limited interest and public-safety analysis, while preserving the latter inquiry. Scalia and Thomas support the former result because they reject the additional substantive right; their opposition to the latter directive remains exactly as frozen. Recognition of an interest is a legal proposition, not a separate award of relief to the plaintiffs that reverses their loss on the disclosure claim.

## Limits and disposition wording

A faithful component description for Scalia and Thomas is: affirm the judgment for the State defendants on the information-disclosure claim on the ground that the proposed substantive constitutional right has not been established; reject the contrary privacy-right rationale; vacate the safeguard directive insofar as it depends on that extension and remand consistently with that rejection, preserving W.P.'s independent process requirements. Their disagreement with P1–P2 must remain visible as judgment-only agreement on the favorable merits judgment and disagreement on the dependent remedial component.

Do not describe the eight Associates as adopting a common no-violation rationale, approving all actual dissemination, finding all existing safeguards adequate, deciding the separately identified family-autonomy theory, or dispensing with W.P. Nothing here changes the six Associates' limited holding or two Associates' limited vacatur. Any proposed enlargement to those subjects would require the Engine's substantive commitment process rather than this clarification.
