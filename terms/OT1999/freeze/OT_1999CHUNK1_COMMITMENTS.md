## Flippo v. West Virginia — October 18, 1999

**Reach and components.** Review the Fayette County Circuit Court's final operative judgment after the state high court's discretionary denial. Grant certiorari as the incident necessary to the submitted summary action; no separate published petition tally is proposed. F1 rejects the categorical homicide-scene exception. F2 rejects the premise that seeing a closed container permits opening it. F3 reverses the judgment insofar as it rests on that categorical rationale and remands for preserved independent exceptions and harmlessness. It does not finally direct exclusion, acquittal, a new trial, or findings against the State on unadjudicated alternatives.

**Common sufficient rule.** An actual emergency may justify entry and a search within its scope. The discovery of a homicide does not authorize a subsequent general evidentiary search after that justification ends. A closed briefcase's visibility does not expose its contents. Mincey and Thompson supply the categorical answer; Hicks supplies the observation/further-search distinction. Dickerson's governing scope rule applies the same requirement that evidence be discovered during an authorized intrusion. Consent, a properly confined inventory, another genuine exigency and Chapman harmlessness require their own supported application. The packet's absence of findings is not proof that those defenses are factually false.

## Texas v. Lesage — November 29, 1999

L2: such proof does not erase a separately established unconstitutional admissions-process injury. L3: the Fifth Circuit must examine Texas's showing under Hopwood's school-borne preponderance burden and a specified constitutional counterfactual, preserving Rule 56's genuine-dispute requirements. L4: separate source-specific remedies, defendants, immunity and future exposure; neither nominal relief nor an injunction is automatically awarded.

The operative rule is Hopwood, not a new race-neutral-only counterfactual. The first inquiry is whether unconstitutional unequal treatment occurred under the governing substantial-justification standard. If it did, Texas bears the preponderance same-decision burden for denial-of-admission consequences. The numbers of applicants with higher grades or scores and the District Court's comparative finding are evidence, not a substitute for specifying the lawful admissions process. Success on the defense defeats compensation for admission that would lawfully have been denied; it does not validate an unconstitutional process. An available claim against a proper defendant may support nominal or declaratory recognition, subject to statutory and immunity limits. Any prospective claim requires supported continuing or imminent unequal competition and ordinary equitable grounds; possible cessation or abandonment cannot be assumed from the abbreviated account.

## Paul P. v. Verniero — December 6, 1999

**Correct entering premise.** Doe v. SEPTA assumed an interest and rejected that particular internal-use claim; it created neither a general constitutional information right nor a seven-factor formula. Sheets recognized a confined substantive protection for unjustified official dissemination of intimate marital and family information. W.P. rests separately on New Jersey's substantive disclosure limits and requires ordinary advance review and State persuasion; its general plan-proof quantum remains unresolved. The present address-privacy question is therefore an extension question, not automatic application of an existing universal right.

**P1, limited substantive protection.** The State's compelled collection and deliberate dissemination of compiled, nonpublic current home-address information implicates a limited personal privacy interest in the exposure of the home and family to outside contact. It is not a secrecy entitlement in public conviction facts, every identifying datum, every address available elsewhere, or all government-held information. The interest does not bar public-safety notification, but requires a sufficient governmental connection between the actual information, recipient audience and protective purpose. Public availability bears on the interest; mere theoretical inspectability does not establish prior dissemination.

**P2, notification and safeguards.** New Jersey has a substantial protective interest in communicating accurate risk information to persons with a concrete safety need. Properly tailored Tier Two and Tier Three notification can serve that interest. Recognition of that objective alone does not justify unnecessary dissemination or redistribution of nonpublic residential information. Safeguards must reasonably serve the system's actual protective function and constrain unnecessary redisclosure; no perfect-containment guarantee, universal least-restrictive-means rule, prescribed assurance form, particular new proof quantum or seven-factor constitutional checklist is adopted. The existing W.P. requirements remain independently controlling at their actual scope.

**P3, disposition.** Affirm the Third Circuit's limited recognition of the residential privacy interest and public-safety objective, as confined by P1–P2, and retain its remand to consider adequate safeguards. Do not prohibit all notification, adjudicate unproved leakage or misconduct, prescribe a statewide distribution system, or reopen W.P.'s punishment and procedural judgments.

**K1, statutory clarity.** Section 630(b) expressly includes States and their agencies as employers. **K3, independent section 5 analysis for the immunity position.** The first group reserves section 5 because K2 is sufficient. The second group would reject section 5 as an independent ground for these blanket private remedies. Gregory's constitutional rational-basis treatment permits reasonable age classifications even when they imperfectly track individual ability; the ADEA generally prohibits age-based employment treatment subject to materially narrower statutory defenses. The bona fide occupational-qualification defense requires reasonable necessity to normal business operation; the reasonable-factors-other-than-age defense does not authorize otherwise rational distinctions made because of age. Good-cause discharge, protected seniority and qualified benefit plans preserve useful employer latitude but do not reduce the statutory protection to the constitutional baseline. Their statutory defense predicates and employer burdens remain distinct, and no defense is adjudicated established here.

The supplied legislative sources identify governmental complaints, federal-employment newspaper reports, private-sector findings, and a California public-agency survey in which most described limits concerned police and firefighters. Their strongest opposing argument is Florida Prepaid's acceptance of preventive legislation reaching innocent statutory conduct. They distinguish its identified property, exclusive federal remedy and practical remedial gap from broad substantive age protection where rationality rather than individualized necessity is the constitutional rule. They rely on substantive fit, not the bare proposition that Congress sometimes prohibits constitutional conduct.

The seniority defense requires a bona fide system not intended to evade the Act and cannot permit involuntary age-based retirement of a protected individual. The benefit-plan alternatives require either at least equal payment or cost for the older worker under the specified regulation, or a voluntary early-retirement incentive consistent with the Act's purposes. Neither alternative excuses failure to hire; a benefit plan cannot require or permit involuntary retirement because of age. The employer bears the stated burden of proving lawful action under these seniority and benefit provisions. The foreign-workplace defense has its separate condition that compliance would violate the foreign workplace country's law; it supplies no general domestic exception. These retained statutory conditions are not new judicial tests, and no defendant is found to satisfy them.
