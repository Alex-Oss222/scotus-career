## Lower-court reasoning and supported alternatives

The district court adopted the United States' territorial-incorporation argument. It treated Atalig, 723 F.2d 682 (9th Cir. 1984), and Wabol, 958 F.2d 1450, as requiring a distinction between rights fundamental against States and rights binding in unincorporated territories. It asked whether equal-population representation in both chambers is essential to every free government; the existence of free governments with unequal upper chambers supplied its negative answer. It accordingly sustained Congress's consent to section 203(c). This is the decision under review, not the entering Supreme Court rule.

The court declined to decide defendants' separate federal-Senate analogy and declined to reapply the earlier Hillblom and local Sablan rulings as a bar. Smaller-island refusal to join without protection was defendants' contention, supported by the legislative-history quotations in footnote 7; do not convert it into an independently proved counterfactual historical event. Footnote 11 doubted Wabol's additional practical-and-contextual inquiry and focused on fundamental rights, contrasting the Verdugo-Urquidez majority discussion with Justice Kennedy's separate discussion. That choice is a live analytical question, not an omitted binding Supreme Court holding.

The competing paths remain open: apply equal voting weight to this general legislative chamber because the applicable equal-protection guarantee and the nature of the right outweigh the accommodation; or sustain this particular territorial settlement because the applicable constitutional doctrine permits the specified accommodation. Either path must address the statutory interaction among sections 203(c), 501(a), and 501(b), rather than treating the Covenant as silent or treating territorial status as self-executing immunity from review. Section 105's mutual-consent provision and section 202's express judicial-consistency review must remain visible. Surviving Reynolds and Board of Estimate principles supply the equality side; Atalig, Wabol, the Insular Cases, and the actual Covenant supply the competing territorial framework. Shaw/Miller's entering-law rule concerns racial district design and preserves independent unequal-participation claims; it decides neither this territorial question nor its justification.

## Illinois v. Wardlow

- On September 9, 1995, uniformed Chicago officers Nolan and Harvey were in the last vehicle of a four-car caravan investigating narcotics transactions in an area described as known for heavy narcotics trafficking. The officers expected people including customers and lookouts.
- At 4035 West Van Buren, Nolan saw Wardlow standing beside a building carrying an opaque bag. Wardlow looked toward the officers and ran. Nolan and Harvey drove south, watched him run through a gangway and alley, and caught up with him on a street. Nolan left the car and stopped him.
- Nolan immediately performed a pat-down. His reported explanation was his experience that weapons were commonly near narcotics transactions. He squeezed the bag, felt a heavy, hard object shaped like a gun, opened the bag, and found a .38-caliber handgun with five live rounds. The officers then arrested Wardlow.
- The trial court denied suppression. Following a stipulated bench trial, Wardlow was convicted of unlawful use of a weapon by a felon. The Illinois Appellate Court reversed because it found no adequate reasonable suspicion for the initial stop, 287 Ill. App. 3d 367, 678 N.E.2d 65 (1997). The Illinois Supreme Court affirmed that appellate result, 183 Ill. 2d 306, 701 N.E.2d 484 (1998), although it rejected the intermediate court's conclusion that this was not a high-crime area. That court treated flight as potentially an exercise of the right to decline a police encounter and found no other independently suspicious circumstances.
- Keep the initial stop, the bag frisk, and the subsequent arrest distinct. The granted question is the initial stop's reasonable-suspicion basis. The recitation supplies no observed narcotics sale by Wardlow or observed contraband before the stop. This limitation is about the reported evidence, not an assumption that undisclosed evidence exists.

Its sequence supports distinct inquiries into the initial stop, bag frisk, and later arrest. The gun cannot retrospectively justify the stop. The record-limits addendum supplies necessary qualifications.
