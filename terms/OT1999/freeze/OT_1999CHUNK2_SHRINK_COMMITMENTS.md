Actual Russell, April 28, 1999, supplies the unanimously adopted standard: direct candidate-contribution limits must be closely drawn to a sufficiently important corruption or appearance-of-corruption interest while preserving meaningful association and effective campaigning. No completed bribe is required; equality of political influence is not an independent justification. The current **Candidate contribution limits and anticorruption fit** rule has that effective date. Campaign scale, actual fundraising burdens and cumulative restrictions matter; nominal amount and inflation establish no permanent floor or safe harbor. Russell's ordinary-PAC receipt holding and Day's **Intermediary contribution limits and meaningful association** rule concern distinct channels. Colorado Republican protects a genuinely independent party advertisement without immunizing direct candidate cash.

In Russell, O'Connor's Part I, joined by every Justice, invalidated the seven statewide $300 limits and the two named appellate-office $100 limits on their specific burdens. Stevens, O'Connor, Souter, Ginsburg and Breyer sustained the residual categorical candidate challenge and the particular PAC provisions on their evidence; Kennedy, Scalia and Thomas found those remaining restrictions insufficiently fitted as well. That recent public record controls each Justice's own starting point. No present Justice receives a generic position favoring all limits or all political spending.

## Actual presented category and evidence

Only the statewide/state-auditor ceiling presented by these litigants is adjudicated: $1,075, indexed from a $1,000 January 1, 1995 base, with biennial consumer-price adjustment rounded to the nearest $25. The PAC gave Fredman's committee $1,025 in 1997 and $50 in 1998 and sought to give more. Fredman asserted that he needed larger contributions to mount an effective campaign. Other-office limits are not independently before the Court.

The State supplied Goode's affidavit about large gifts' potential influence, accounts connecting a $20,000 bank contribution to the treasurer's bank selection, and $40,000 brewery and $20,000 bank gifts in an auditor contest. Related record material identified a $420,000 PAC contribution, reported sponsorship-for-kickback accusations, and the former attorney general's conspiracy plea involving misuse of state property. Those items differ: an allegation or inference is not a judicial finding of a completed exchange, and the plea establishes its actual offense only. The evidence nevertheless permits an objectively grounded appearance-of-corruption argument tied to concentrated giving and official financial choices rather than generalized hostility to money in politics.

The District Court found that candidates remained able to raise sufficient funds for effective campaigns. Proposition A's seventy-four-percent support indicates the political setting, not constitutional validity. The figure that 97.62 percent of 1994 auditor contributors gave no more than $2,000 measures contributors, not dollars, and does not prove that a $1,075 cap leaves the funding base unchanged.

The contrary evidence is substantial: total primary/general spending across five statewide offices fell from $21,599,000 to $9,337,000; primary spending fell from $14,249,000 to $1,625,000; contested primaries from ten of ten to one of ten; and primary candidates from thirty-two to eleven. These comparisons support a burden argument. They are not findings that the law caused every decline. Nothing in this handoff attributes the change to another proven cause, invents an incumbent/challenger finding or treats aggregate spending as conclusive about this auditor campaign.

## Adversarial legal assessment

**Path A — sustain the presented limit under Russell.** Missouri's actual and appearance evidence identifies a concrete risk from concentrated gifts. Closely drawn prophylaxis need not identify a completed exchange at precisely $1,076 or adopt the highest conceivable constitutional limit. The indexed ceiling and district findings of practical campaign capacity, examined together, support meaningful direct association and advocacy in the category presented. Russell's invalidation of materially lower limits on a different record and its rejection of permanent monetary boundaries require this distinct assessment.

The strongest objection is that a statewide campaign needs substantial resources and the observed collapse in spending and contested primaries is too serious to dismiss as correlation. That objection is weighed, not excluded. The supportive path finds the district court's affirmative practical-capacity determination and the record of sizeable available campaign funding more probative of this specific capacity question than aggregate changes unconnected by a supported causal determination to the challenged category. It does not require challengers to prove literal impossibility, declare the decline harmless, or shift the State's burden of justification onto contributors. The State's burden is met by the combined showing of identified risk, a direct candidate channel, the amount and indexing, and supported practical capacity; nominal comparison with Buckley or Russell alone would not suffice.

**Path B — invalidate the presented limit for inadequate close fit and practical burden.** Accept the corruption interest and contribution/expenditure distinction, but find the record inadequate to justify so restrictive a statewide ceiling in the face of the spending and competition evidence. Large-gift episodes make some cap plausible without establishing this cap's fit. Indexing prevents further nominal erosion but does not cure an initially excessive burden. General candidate-capacity findings and the contributor-count statistic do not answer lost resources or realistic fundraising. The sufficiently important interest is therefore not enough under Russell.

The strongest counterargument is that this would demand exact monetary proof or causal certainty for ordinary prophylaxis. The invalidating path requires neither completed bribery nor a statistical law fixing the optimal cap. Its conclusion is case-specific: the demonstrated aggregate contraction and statewide fundraising scale require a materially stronger fit justification than this record supplies. It supplies no constitutional dollar floor, unrestricted right to large contributions or rule that every future indexed limit fails.

**Alternative remedies.** Vacatur for additional data is possible in theory, but neither side's selected path depends on an identified missing historical fact that must be reconstructed. The record permits a legal judgment on sufficiency. No new study, office survey or trial is required simply because the evidence allows different inferences. The decision must be confined to the presented auditor-limit challenge and its existing injunction.

## Provisional coalition, scope and remedy

Stevens, O'Connor, Souter, Ginsburg and Breyer support reversal/remand on Path A; Scalia, Kennedy and Thomas support affirmance on Path B. These are provisional Associate commitments before Stone, not an announced Court vote. All eight apply actual Russell and retain the contribution/expenditure distinction, rejection of equalization, burden of justification and case-specific limits.

The sustaining path removes the Eighth Circuit's stated constitutional basis for enjoining the presented auditor ceiling and remands for conforming proceedings; it fixes no future enforcement liability, refund, damages, replacement dollar amount or new factual trial. The invalidating path preserves relief against the presented restriction on the stated ground, without invalidating unpresented office categories. Exact decree implementation remains tied to the real litigation rather than invented relief. A separate opinion explaining the opposing assessment must preserve both the corruption evidence and the material operational counterevidence; authorship is for assembly.
