## Three dates and two distinct uses

Officer Johnson's conduct occurred March 10, 1989, eighteen days after DeShaney. The en banc court decided May 30, 1995, before Saenz's June 19, 1995 conduct. Its immunity judgment therefore determines whether this different officer had sufficient notice in 1989, while the published opinion itself is a source available before the 1995 Saenz conduct.

The later simulated Supreme Court Pinder decision in 1996 occupies a separate role in current merits law under its actual holding. It cannot supply notice retroactively for June 1995. Equally, its later existence does not erase this en banc opinion as part of the legal landscape actually available at that earlier date. Neither the lower decision nor the later Supreme Court action can be substituted wholesale for a conduct-date, fact-specific notice inquiry.

## Record and actual appellate disposition

The appeal arose from denial of Officer Johnson's summary-judgment motion asserting qualified immunity. Johnson had arrested Pittman after violent abuse and threats against Pinder and her children. Pinder told Johnson about Pittman's recently completed sentence for attempted arson at her home. Johnson assured her that Pittman would remain locked up overnight and said a commissioner would not be available before morning. Pinder returned to work in reliance. Johnson nevertheless brought Pittman before a commissioner that evening on minor charges; the commissioner released him on recognizance. Pittman returned and burned the house, killing Pinder's three children.

The en banc court reversed the denial of personal qualified immunity. The case included municipal and other claims, but this interlocutory appeal and disposition concern Johnson's immunity. It is not a municipal-immunity judgment and does not automatically dispose of every claim originally pleaded. The prior divided panel's contrary judgment was superseded by en banc review; the panel must not be counted as an independently prevailing circuit holding at June 1995.

## Exact en banc alignment and scope

Thirteen judges participated. Nine supported reversal on qualified immunity; four dissented. The reasons were not an undifferentiated nine-judge merits opinion.

The dissent's support for affirmative-danger liability is not an extra formal join to those three sentences. A broader proposition cannot be attributed to all nine judgment supporters simply from the result.

## What the opinion actually distinguishes

Wilkinson's opinion treats the claim presented as failure to protect against Pittman's private violence. For the special-relationship theory, awareness of risk and promises of aid do not substitute for state restraint on the victim's ability to act. It treats the assurances, charging decision and release sequence here as insufficient to cross the line from omission into affirmative creation of danger. Its immunity rule requires particularized notice, while expressly disclaiming a requirement of a prior case with identical facts.

The opinion does not state that all direct state creation of danger outside custody is constitutionally irrelevant. The three page-1177 sentences distinguish cases in which government itself creates the dangerous situation and becomes akin to a direct causal actor. Its accompanying discussion contrasts such acts with reliance on assurances of protection against a pre-existing danger. The Part III footnote likewise identifies direct causation as an alternative liability framework rather than merely an exception that creates a duty to provide aid, while warning against relabeling ordinary omission as an affirmative act. Widener specifically disclaims the three page-1177 sentences; Motz specifically disclaims Part V's broad policy discussion. These qualifications are material to any claim that the en banc court rejected the whole category.

The lower opinion discusses such sources as Wells, Nishiyama and Archie as examples or comparisons. It also rejects reliance on Gregory, Freeman and Wood as notice to Johnson because those decisions postdated March 1989, while separately disputing their characterization as simple promise cases. That temporal exclusion cannot be reused against Saenz without checking their actual dates: a case too late for 1989 may be available by June 1995. This supplement verifies how Pinder treats them; it does not claim fresh full review or independently adopt every characterization of those other decisions.

Russell's dissent argues that Johnson induced Pinder to give up self-protection and leave the children vulnerable, then failed to correct the danger he had increased. It reads DeShaney as preserving affirmative-danger liability and relies particularly on Bowers, Archie and the affirmative-placement aspect of Jensen. Its footnote distinguishes Jensen's vulnerable promises/knowledge account from its asserted continuing affirmative-danger principle. The dissent's claim that a warning call was required is its proposed application, not the en banc court's holding. Both sides accept that a particularized legal principle can give notice without an identical prior case; they disagree over the governing characterization and application.

## Proper relevance to Saenz, Ross, Salas and Piotrowski

Pinder supplies substantial contrary evidence to a sweeping assertion that every undertaking, assurance, release decision or increase in exposure created an established constitutional protective duty. The recent en banc split and the nine-judge immunity result are relevant to the notice landscape, especially where the asserted Saenz theory merely redescribes an inadequate official response as an affirmative act. But a disagreement over this promise-and-release case does not logically establish immunity for every materially different act of suppressing identified, immediately available rescue.

Ross, separately verified, involves allegedly stopping actual qualified private rescuers from aiding a known drowning child without a meaningful replacement. Salas involves replacement of one official rescue response with another ongoing response, not simply the absence of every rescue. Piotrowski's preconduct discussion concerns a blocked specific warning and alleged assistance to prospective assailants, but reserves adoption and pleading sufficiency and resolves municipal-policy allegations. Those different facts and actual holdings must remain distinct.

The en banc Pinder opinion does not expressly overrule Ross or Salas, does not claim authority to do so across circuits, and does not adjudicate a private-rescuer blockade. Its distinction between assurance/omission and direct governmental causation permits a supported argument that actual rescue obstruction belongs to the other side of the line. Its contrary characterization of significant inducement and increased vulnerability requires that argument to identify the concrete aid foreclosed, coercion, causal displacement and factual knowledge rather than rely on labels. The legal status of public-investigation command decisions in Saenz must be assessed on their own record; neither the rescue's public character nor the word interference answers the question by itself.

For a June 19, 1995 Elder-style review, this lower Pinder decision must therefore be considered at its actual scope along with the other pertinent preconduct authorities and governing circuit law. A Fourth Circuit decision is not binding Seventh Circuit law, but can be relevant persuasive evidence. The later simulated Pinder holding may govern the 2000 merits analysis while remaining unavailable as retroactive notice. No current vote, new procedural default, municipal shield or automatic personal-immunity conclusion follows from this supplement.

## Exact source passage identifying Widener's reservation

The following three sentences are mechanically copied from the CAP source; they are the sentences Widener expressly declined to join. The copied text is supplied to fix the reservation's scope, not to enlarge the number of adherents or decide Saenz.

> These cases involve a wholly different paradigm than that presented here. When the state itself creates the dangerous situation that resulted in a victim’s injury, the absence of a custodial relationship may not be dispositive. In such instances, the state is not merely accused of a failure to act; it becomes much more akin to an actor itself directly causing harm to the injured party.
