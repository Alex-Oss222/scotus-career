## Civil Rights Cases: actual holding and qualifications

The Court held the 1875 Act's first two sections invalid as applied within the States under the Fourteenth and Thirteenth Amendment grounds presented. The challenged provisions established equal accommodations without racial distinctions in inns, public conveyances and public amusements, enforced through a civil penalty and criminal punishment. The majority treated that enactment as direct regulation of private conduct, irrespective of any hostile state law or administration, rather than corrective enforcement of the Fourteenth Amendment's prohibitions against the States.

Its Fourteenth Amendment premise is that the relevant prohibitions reach state action, including executive and judicial action, not merely legislation. Its approval of Ex parte Virginia is especially important: a state jury-selection officer's discriminatory exercise of official authority is state action even if the State's statute book does not prescribe the discrimination. A State cannot escape the prohibition simply because the wrongful act of its officer was unlawful under local law. The majority also recognizes customs having legal force and private conduct shielded by state authority as materially different from an entirely unsupported private wrong.

The majority does not hold that every enforcement statute must wait until after an individual violation before Congress legislates. It expressly contemplates legislation in advance of an anticipated state wrong, but requires adaptation to the constitutional mischief. Nor does it announce that only a State can ever be a named defendant in an enforcement remedy. Its examples include redress against a private party invoking an unconstitutional state-law defense to a contract. The legally relevant distinction is the remedy's relationship to prohibited state conduct, not the defendant's label standing alone.

The particular Act failed because, on the majority's account, it neither professed to correct a state constitutional wrong nor conditioned its operation on one; it governed equally in States that supplied and faithfully enforced equal rights. The Court left unspecified the complete range of appropriate corrective legislation. It assumed for analysis, without deciding, the constitutional status of the asserted equal-accommodation right. It reserved the law's operation in the Territories and District of Columbia and the Commerce Clause power over interstate conveyances. Those reservations preclude converting this decision into a holding against every possible federal accommodation law or every independent constitutional source of authority.

The majority's separate Thirteenth Amendment analysis distinguishes that Amendment's capacity to reach private slavery and its incidents from the Fourteenth Amendment's state-action structure. Its then-narrow treatment of discriminatory accommodations under the Thirteenth Amendment is not supplied here as an unchanged modern rule and does not decide Morrison's two granted sources of authority. The same discipline applies to other portions of the nineteenth-century reasoning outside the present issue.

## Harlan's dissent: strongest grounds and limits

Harlan read the Reconstruction Amendments as securing actual freedom and citizenship that Congress could protect through affirmative national legislation. He emphasized the express enforcement power, congressional choice of appropriate means, the comparison with earlier federal protection of slaveholders' rights, and the distinction between public legal accommodations and compulsory private social association. He also treated railroads, innkeepers and licensed public amusements as public or quasi-public instrumentalities, invoking Ex parte Virginia by analogy. His Thirteenth Amendment ground treated racial exclusion from those legal rights as a badge of servitude. His additional commerce discussion questioned the majority's reservation where an actual interstate rail trip was involved.

Those are a published dissent's arguments, not the Court's holding. The public-function ground does not classify every private assailant as a state instrumentality; the citizenship and remedial-power grounds cannot silently replace the majority's state-action requirement. Conversely, the dissent demonstrates that the historical controversy concerned the character of the right and means of enforcing it, not a consensus that Congress could provide only appellate correction of state judgments. No present Justice's vote is inferred from either nineteenth-century writing.

## Harris (1883): direct private conspiracy and the absence of a state wrong

Harris concerned Revised Statutes §5519, which punished conspiracies to deprive people of equal legal protection or to prevent state authorities from supplying it. The indictment alleged mob violence against persons held by a deputy sheriff, including a death, and interference with that officer's protection. The Court concluded that the provision exceeded the asserted constitutional powers. Its Fourteenth Amendment ground stressed that the statute applied regardless of how faithfully the State performed its duties. The indictment alleged no unconstitutional Tennessee law or state administrative conduct; it alleged that private conspirators defeated protection the State's law accorded.

Thus, custody by a deputy did not itself turn the attackers' alleged private conspiracy into the State's constitutional denial on the charge presented. The opinion distinguished state laws, judicial construction and executive administration from unsupported private crime. It refused to sustain a broad criminal provision by judicially supplying the missing constitutional limits. Its discussions of the Fifteenth Amendment, Article IV privileges and immunities, and the Thirteenth Amendment supplied distinct reasons concerning the statute before it; those grounds cannot be imported as a decision about §13981's separate Commerce Clause theory.

The Court first sustained its certificate-of-division jurisdiction by inferring the required request from the record. Harlan dissented on jurisdiction and expressly offered no view on the merits. There is consequently no Harlan merits dissent in Harris to compare with his later Civil Rights Cases dissent.

## Use with the actual law entering Morrison

The two majority opinions supply a serious obstacle to treating private violence itself as the Fourteenth Amendment violation. Congress's enforcement power must address the State's constitutional denial. But their actual reasoning must be read with the simulated Boerne rule and its later applications, which permit properly fitted preventive coverage beyond conduct independently provable as a constitutional violation. Neither an individualized completed violation in every covered case nor a universal geographical or temporal restriction may be inserted as a categorical requirement that current law rejects.

For the proponents of §13981, the strongest supported distinction is Congress's express remedial account: documented discriminatory treatment by state justice systems, and a civil avenue claimed to remedy or prevent that denial. Unlike the nineteenth-century statutes as those majorities described them, Congress has identified state conduct as its justification. That is a material argument, not a conclusion that the remedy fits. The model must connect the defendant, statutory trigger, coverage and actual relief to that constitutional injury under current law.

For the challengers, the strongest response is that a remedial legislative explanation cannot alone transform liability for the private assault into a fitted remedy for discriminatory state protection. The enacted action applies without an adjudicated link to deficient state protection in the particular case and without a limited class of state practices or jurisdictions. Whether that breadth is permissible prophylaxis or replacement of the substantive constitutional boundary must be reasoned through, not decided by counting findings or demanding a per-case violation. Statewide or nationwide findings, the civil character of relief and difficulty of proving discrimination each matter only through that fit.

The explicit reference in §13981(c) to persons acting under color of state law also remains in the enacted text. A decision about these alleged private assailants must state its actual scope and any supported severability or other-application consequence; it cannot erase that language or assume all applications are identical.
