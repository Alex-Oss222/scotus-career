City of Houston, 51 F.3d 512 (5th Cir. April 21, 1995), reproduced at `sources/chunk6-neutral/piotrowski-preconduct-cap.txt`; the source receipt identifies the matching case and date at [CAP reporter text](https://static.case.law/f3d/51/cases/0512-01.json). April 21 precedes the June 19, 1995 conduct relevant to Saenz. This supplement identifies the source's actual limits and supplies no resolution of Saenz.

## Procedural posture and actual result

Piotrowski sued the City in 1993 for a 1980 shooting. On a Rule 12(b)(6) motion, her well-pleaded allegations received favorable treatment; they were not findings. She alleged that officers instructed Waring not to warn her that Bell had solicited her murder, promised but failed to investigate, and gave the conspirators her photograph. She alleged learning of this affirmative conduct in a 1993 deposition. The police department's separate dismissal was outside the appeal.

The district court dismissed with prejudice on limitations grounds. The Fifth Circuit held that her asserted injury was not fairly characterized as mere failure to protect: knowledge of the shooting was not necessarily knowledge of the officers' causal affirmative acts. It could not determine on the pleadings that limitations necessarily began in 1980, and concealment could matter. It equally refused to find that accrual necessarily waited until the 1993 deposition; the City's earlier-knowledge contention depended on matters outside the pleadings.

The appellate court nevertheless affirmed dismissal on a different ground: no facts in the operative complaint linked the alleged increased danger to a municipal policy or custom. Actions by officers alone do not establish City responsibility through respondeat superior. The court modified dismissal to be without prejudice to amendment because it could not conclude that no facts could establish the required policy connection. The opinion thus did not hold the City liable or categorically foreclose a properly pleaded municipal claim.

## State-created danger discussion and express reservations

The opinion expressly states that the Fifth Circuit had not affirmatively recognized state-created danger as an exception to DeShaney. It describes increased danger and deliberate indifference as requirements discussed in circuit precedent if that theory is cognizable. Its footnotes explain that culpability requires more than merely making the risk greater: the described cases require a dangerous environment known to the officers and use of authority to create an opportunity for the third party's crime that otherwise would not have existed.

The court expressly reserves both whether the theory is cognizable and whether Piotrowski's particular allegations meet the pleading threshold for it. The blocked-warning and photograph allegations therefore illustrate potentially affirmative conduct, not an adjudicated constitutional violation. Its discussion of Salas identifies that earlier discussion as dicta and cites the circuit's uncertainty; it does not erase Salas's actual result or the content of its reasoning. The separate special-relationship argument fails under Walton because Piotrowski alleged no involuntary confinement through affirmative exercise of state power.

No individual officer was awarded or denied qualified immunity here. The citation to Siegert explains the antecedent constitutional-question inquiry; it does not turn this municipal pleading decision into an individual-immunity holding. The City-policy ground and limitations ground likewise do not establish the degree of notice an individual officer had in June 1995.

## Proper relevance to Saenz

For an Elder-style conduct-date inquiry, this is available authority before June 19, 1995 and should not be excluded because the ultimate dismissal rested on municipal pleading. Its identification of obstructing a specific warning, as distinct from simply failing to provide aid, is relevant to an argument that affirmative governmental interference can raise a different constitutional question. Its discussion can inform the specificity and strength of the asserted principle even though it does not adopt the cause of action. Treating all dicta as irrelevant would be as inaccurate as treating that discussion as a holding of liability.

The limiting force is equally concrete. A published appellate decision shortly before the conduct expressly acknowledged that its own circuit had not adopted the theory and withheld judgment even on the pleaded affirmative acts. That is relevant to the claimed breadth and uniformity of authority. It does not itself prove that no particular affirmative rescue-obstruction right was clearly established elsewhere or in a more specific setting. Ross's adopted private-rescue-obstruction rule, Salas's actual continuing-rescue facts and the governing circuit's eligible precedents require their own comparison. Piotrowski is Fifth Circuit authority; its reservation is not a binding Seventh Circuit rejection.

The alleged acts here combined preventing a particular third party's warning about an identified planned murder with active assistance to the conspirators. Saenz's command decisions about a public investigation or rescue must be compared to those facts without assuming that every delay is affirmative obstruction, every official replacement rescue is meaningful, or every instruction creates the danger. The degree of known danger, the specific aid foreclosed, the officers' causal use of power and the alternative response actually available all matter at their supported factual scope.

Merits at the later decision date, individual notice at June 19, 1995, and municipal responsibility remain separate questions. Later Supreme Court developments can govern merits within their proper temporal role without retroactively supplying this source's missing notice. Piotrowski supplies neither an automatic personal-immunity defense nor an automatic municipal claim. Any Saenz disposition must rest on the governing actual law, record and preserved components rather than on this supplement's existence.
