## Additional record material and its limits

**Maximum claim and warning.** The published account reproduces counsel for the United States representing in the original proceedings that the testimony and maps defined the maximum water claim then asserted. It also reproduces the original Master's warning that potential claims omitted from a decree settling the controversy could be lost. These are material litigation statements. They must be considered alongside, rather than displaced by, the later decrees' boundary-adjustment language.

The representation describes the maximum claim being asserted. Without the full exchange, it does not establish a concession concerning every possible boundary, a perpetual surrender of all additional water rights, or a decision about which absent persons could be bound. The warning gives notice of a possible consequence of omission; it is not by itself a decree disposing of every potential claim. Conversely, neither statement can be dismissed merely because it preceded the later boundary orders. A finality analysis must identify the claim covered, the party or representative capacity, the decree's operative scope, and any preserved matter.

**Availability of the underlying facts.** The scoped report describes the factual materials supporting the enlarged Fort Yuma claim as already possessed during the original proceedings. It establishes no later physical boundary change or newly discovered underlying fact. Interior's 1978 and 1981 actions changed the Government's legal treatment of the conditional cession; the existing packet does not establish that they uncovered a new physical event.

This distinction bears on whether the enlarged demand concerns an omitted claim supported by previously available materials, a reserved boundary question later placed in a different legal posture, or some combination requiring narrower treatment. It does not decide which characterization governs. Possession of factual materials does not alone establish that a particular claim was legally available, required to be asserted, actually adjudicated, or surrendered. Nor may the later legal position be described as newly discovered evidence merely to avoid the finality issue. The exact inventory and use of those materials remain limited by the published recitation.

**Necessary-party distinction.** The published account describes the original impediment concerning Fort Mojave and the Colorado River Reservation as prejudice to absent private claimants' title or political-jurisdiction interests. It contrasts the asserted Fort Yuma dispute as one between the United States and Tribe without private title claimants. Preserve that distinction as a reported characterization of the disputes.

This supports testing whether the rationale for declining to determine the first two boundaries also applied to Fort Yuma. It does not conclusively establish that the original court could or had to adjudicate Fort Yuma, that every affected interest was represented, or that the United States and Tribe had identical or adverse interests on every issue. Absence of the identified private claimants removes only the described factual premise; it does not itself resolve jurisdiction, claim scope, representation, finality, or the later decree reservations. A general statement that every reservation boundary was initially unadjudicable would therefore exceed this account, as would the opposite statement that Fort Yuma was necessarily adjudicable and forfeited.

## Competing readings that remain open

One reading gives weight to the original maximum-claim representation, the Master's warning, the prior availability of the materials, and the reported absence of the private-claimant obstacle for Fort Yuma. It treats the enlarged demand as an omitted claim within the controversy the original decree settled, and asks whether later adjustment provisions merely permit a change after a lawful final boundary determination. On that reading, capacity to adjust water quantities does not itself answer whether a party may reopen a previously foreclosed predicate to obtain that determination. The analogy to Arizona II's omitted-acreage finality issue requires analysis rather than assertion.

A competing reading distinguishes a boundary-dependent reserved-water claim from omitted acreage within accepted boundaries. It gives weight to the later decrees' coverage of all five reservations, the States' stipulation to the 1979 terms and later requests for boundary determination, and Arizona II's express reservation concerning further litigation in the original action if the district-court route failed. On that reading, the original maximum demand and warning may concern the claims then asserted without foreclosing the boundary mechanism subsequently preserved. Neither the administrative legal change alone nor the adjustment clause alone conclusively establishes this reading.

Both readings must account for the actual conditional language: quantities are subject to appropriate adjustment by agreement or decree if boundaries are finally determined. The 1964 provision addressed Fort Mojave and the Colorado River Reservation; the 1979 and 1984 provisions covered all five. Arizona II, 460 U.S. at 638, reserved whether the boundary issues could nevertheless be litigated in the original action if the district-court dismissal survived review. Neither the condition nor the reservation may be converted into an express decision of the present preclusion defense.

The timing and preservation question remains distinct from the merits of either reading. The earlier extraction identifies first assertion of the Arizona I defense in the 1989 reopening motion after its absence from the identified 1979 and 1982 presentations. The original 1989 responses remain unavailable. The chronology, earlier stipulation and requests may bear on procedural arguments, but they supply no automatic waiver, forfeiture, estoppel, consent, preserved objection, or exception to ordinary treatment. No inference about what a missing response said is permitted.
