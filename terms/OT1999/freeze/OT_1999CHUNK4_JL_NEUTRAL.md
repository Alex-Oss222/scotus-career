## Supported record and posture

The question is whether the anonymous report of a concealed gun, identifying a person and a public location, supplies reasonable suspicion for the police stop and protective frisk when the police promptly confirm the described people, clothing and location but observe no suspicious conduct. The Court reviews the Florida Supreme Court's December 17, 1998 judgment, 727 So. 2d 204, quashing the Third District's reversal of suppression. Rehearing was denied March 9, 1999. The petition invokes 28 U.S.C. §1257(a). The Florida opinion decides the Fourth Amendment question; article I, §12 of the Florida Constitution contains an express federal-conformity provision reproduced in the petition. No independent state-law basis or additional vehicle defect is established by the supplied materials.

On October 13, 1995, Officer Carmen Anderson and another unit were dispatched to a bus stop in front of a pawnshop in the area of Northwest 183rd Street and 24th Avenue. The anonymous complainant described several young Black males and said the one in a plaid-looking shirt had a gun. Anderson testified that she did not know the caller's identity. About six minutes after dispatch the officers arrived, finding three young men at the stated location, one wearing a plaid-looking shirt. The officers observed no suspicious conduct. Anderson approached J.L., directed him to put his hands on the bus stop, and began a frisk. As she began frisking toward the waist, she saw a gun butt protruding from his left pocket and removed the gun. Her testimony expressly places the observation after the frisk began; it supplies no pre-stop view of the gun. The other officer frisked the companions; Anderson testified that she believed this occurred.

These facts are supported by the Florida Supreme Court's recitation, the Third District opinion, and the officer's reproduced suppression-hearing testimony, not by the present Supreme Court decision. The petition appendix reproduces the hearing of March 28, 1996, with Anderson as the State's sole witness, and the April 11, 1996 order granting suppression on Butts v. State. The State appealed; the Third District reversed on February 12, 1997, 689 So. 2d 1116. The Florida Supreme Court then quashed that reversal, preserving suppression. The delinquency allegations were carrying a concealed firearm and possession of a firearm by a person under eighteen. This is suppression review, not review of a proved conviction or sentence.

The record provides no prediction of future activity, identified or previously reliable informant, face-to-face report, threatening movement, observed criminal transaction, or particularized emergency before the stop. It does not establish the caller's actual basis of knowledge. An inference that the caller probably saw the described scene is an argument from its detail and timing, not an established fact that the caller saw a gun. Do not add caller tracing, call recording, a 911 system's characteristics, an eyewitness declaration, a threat to use the weapon, or an officer's observation of J.L.'s precise age. Race identifies the reported person; it supplies no criminal inference.

## Decisions and competing reasoning below

The Florida Supreme Court applies Terry and Alabama v. White. It distinguishes corroboration that locates the person from information supporting an accusation of concealed illegality. It explains that reliability can arise from corroborated suspicious conduct, predictions displaying special familiarity, or additional circumstances discovered in an investigation; it does not say that every anonymous tip must contain predictions. It rejects the State's proposed firearms exception and holds that this tip plus identification corroboration was insufficient. It distinguishes Webb's report of completed armed robberies and reaffirms Hetland's general recognition that an anonymous tip can carry sufficient indicia of reliability. Its decree quashes the Third District judgment and approves Butts. Its observation that consensual questioning was available is not a finding that questioning occurred.

The Third District and the Florida dissent take the State's strongest competing path: prompt, accurate corroboration of the described persons and location supports reliability; the information is fresh; the allegation concerns a concealed gun in a public place; and officers should not have to await display or use of the weapon before protecting themselves and others. They invoke Terry's balancing and decisions including Bold, Clipper, and DeBerry. The dissent would recognize a rule confined to sufficiently described anonymous reports of illegally concealed firearms. Its discussion of generalized firearms violence supplies a legal/public-safety argument, not an additional observation about J.L.'s conduct. The Florida concurrence answers that an untested anonymous gun accusation cannot by itself eliminate the constitutional protection, while recognizing that school searches may present different legal circumstances.

## Law entering the event

The copied current-law authorities, not the historical versions of post-1991 Supreme Court cases, govern. The operative sources are the term-opening Holdings and Standards and Tests plus the effective Current-Term Neutral Projection.

- Terry v. Ohio, 392 U.S. 1 (1968), supplies an objective inquiry into articulable circumstances supporting an investigative seizure and a protective weapons search. The grounds and scope of each intrusion matter; good faith or a discovered gun cannot supply missing antecedent justification.
- Adams v. Williams, 407 U.S. 143 (1972), supplies a contrasting informant setting: personal, known informant information can support protective action without the officer's own observation of the reported gun. The current anonymous caller does not acquire that reliability merely because the allegation also concerns a firearm.
- Illinois v. Gates, 462 U.S. 213 (1983), and Alabama v. White, 496 U.S. 325 (1990), supply the contextual treatment of informant reliability and basis of knowledge. White concerns a corroborated prediction showing familiarity with future conduct and recognizes reasonable suspicion as less demanding than probable cause. Whether other concrete indicia can suffice remains a totality question; the mere absence of prediction is not automatically decisive.
- The simulated Ornelas holding supplies independent appellate assessment of ultimate reasonable suspicion while preserving historical facts, credibility, and genuine due weight for grounded experience. Experience does not create an unproved fact.
- The simulated Dickerson holding requires an authorized protective intrusion before its discoveries can justify seizure. Its actual holding on manipulation of nonthreatening contraband does not itself decide the initial-stop issue here.
- The simulated Wardlow decision unanimously retains objective totality and the requirement of particularized suspicion, rejecting categorical rules from a bare setting label and retrospective reliance on a recovered gun. Its five-Justice application finds that record insufficient; the four-Justice dissent finds its actual flight sequence and setting sufficient. Neither component decides the distinct anonymous-tip question. The published disagreement is included verbatim in the slice for later independent modeling.

The Florida Supreme Court notes that firearm possession is not universally unlawful, that Florida licenses some concealed carrying, and that some juvenile possession is permitted under statutory exceptions. No challenge to those statutes is presented here. Do not transform the allegation of a concealed weapon into a universal rule that any possession establishes criminality or dangerousness. Any new statutory-coverage holding would require exact text and a record basis; this prepared question does not require one.

## Supported alternatives and remedy boundaries

**Affirmance path:** The Court may conclude that prompt confirmation of identity and place does not sufficiently test the anonymous assertion of a concealed firearm, and that the ordinary safety interest does not dispense with reliable grounds. Affirmance leaves the Florida Supreme Court judgment and suppression ruling in place. It need not direct acquittal, dismissal of the delinquency matter, release, a new trial, or an additional hearing.

**Reversal path:** The Court may conclude that the contemporaneous detailed report, quick confirmation and specific concealed-gun allegation together supply reasonable suspicion under the actual circumstances, or adopt a supported narrower firearms-related rule. The corresponding relief is reversal and remand through the Florida Supreme Court under that rule. It does not itself establish guilt, enter a conviction, or determine any other genuinely preserved question. A claim that the gun was already visible before the encounter cannot support this path because the reproduced testimony forecloses that factual premise.

**Scope choices:** A rule making prediction indispensable is broader than necessary. So is a rule validating every anonymous gun report, treating every lawful firearm possessor as dangerous, or deciding an actual bomb threat, identified eyewitness report, school search, airport screening, or comparable emergency not presented. Such possibilities may explain limits but cannot become facts or independent holdings without their own predicates. The lower opinion's discussion of school searches is a reserved distinction, not a controlling application here.

The present record permits resolution of the framed suppression issue without inventing further record facts. If a later proposed rationale depends on call traceability, the exact words of the original caller, an independently preserved alternative ground, or a materially different emergency, that rationale requires further source preparation before modeling; it is not silently supplied by this packet.
