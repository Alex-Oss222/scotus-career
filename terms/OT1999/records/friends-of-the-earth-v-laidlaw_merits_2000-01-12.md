**A1, standing at commencement.** Six Associates—Stevens, O'Connor, Kennedy, Souter, Ginsburg and Breyer—would hold that the supported local-use and avoidance evidence supplies concrete personal injury. The relevant injury is interference with members' own recreation and enjoyment through reasonable concern about the ongoing mercury discharges, not the public's abstract interest in permit compliance. Curtis's proximity, identified downstream activities and expressed present wish differ from Lujan's indefinite foreign-trip intentions; Moore's limited prior visits must be weighed without fabricating a historical routine. Patterson's local prior activity and present curtailment independently support CLEAN's connection. A generalized desire to use every waterway or an unexplained subjective worry would not suffice. It is unnecessary to adjudicate every member's property valuation or give Sierra Club a separate standing holding to resolve the identical joined request supported by injured members of the other organizations.

Scalia and Thomas would find the asserted injury insufficient on this record. The science-based findings that water quality was adequate for recreation and that these exceedances caused no demonstrated adverse environmental effect materially undermine the claimed reasonable basis for curtailment. A personal reaction to permit noncompliance cannot by itself establish a threatened personal harm. They would not require proof of ecosystem destruction in every citizen suit, but would require a supported objective connection between the challenged discharge and the asserted loss of use rather than treat expressed concern as self-proving. Curtis's prior cessation and Moore's visit history reinforce the need to distinguish desired use from proved interference. They do not rely on nonrecovery of the affidavits as an evidentiary failure.

**A2, redressability.** The six would hold that an authorized civil penalty payable to the Treasury can redress the supported threatened personal injury by deterring continuing or reasonably recurring violations of the same discharge obligations. The 1992 filing occurred amid ongoing violations; the independent later mercury entries confirm that this was not Steel Co.'s prefiling cure with no continuing or imminent violation alleged. Direct payment to the plaintiff is not necessary when the remedy materially reduces the threatened conduct affecting that plaintiff. Penalties for wholly past violations with no remaining remedial connection, mere satisfaction from punishment, and litigation fees standing alone remain insufficient.

Scalia and Thomas would dismiss under A1 and additionally reject the factual A2 showing even if personal injury is assumed. They accept simulated Steel Co.'s recognition that deterrence may redress a supported threatened injury, so they do not adopt a categorical bar to all private reliance on Treasury penalties. But this record's state penalty, governmental enforcement, compliance and litigation exposure make it speculative that the added federal penalty will materially reduce the asserted future personal harm. General deterrent purpose and a finding of adequate deterrence do not, for them, establish the necessary marginal causal effect on these members. They do not require proof to certainty, and they do not dispute that the filing preceded subsequent mercury exceedances. Their Lujan-based concern about private control of public enforcement reinforces the demand for personal injury and likely personal redress; it does not supply a distinct Article II holding on an unpresented question. This is an alternative Article III ground, not a merits decision or a finding that the environmental statute is facially invalid.

**A3, later compliance, closure and mootness.** The six would vacate the Fourth Circuit's mootness judgment and remand. Voluntary cessation requires the defendant to make it absolutely clear that the challenged injurious conduct cannot reasonably be expected to recur. A judgment denying an injunction because of extended substantial compliance does not automatically establish that demanding mootness predicate or retroactively erase standing at filing. The lower court erred in treating the remaining Treasury penalty as necessarily incapable of affecting the controversy.

The September 1, 1998 last-discharge assertion, separate September 1998 and April 1999 closure-plan approvals, and the May 21, 1999 sworn account of dismantling and sale materially bear on recurrence. They postdate the appellate judgment and are not facts the Fourth Circuit decided. The affidavit expressly preserves the permit for a prospective buyer; the 1997 consent agreement stopped incineration under its schedule while permitting different equivalent treatment capacity subject to approval, permits and reconstruction conditions. Neither the permit nor dismantling alone decides recurrence. The court below must evaluate the supported closure facts and the actual legal restrictions; the Supreme Court makes no new factfinding that reopening will occur or is impossible. The alleged June 1999 reporting notice, known through Clark's database/conversation declaration rather than the notice itself, does not establish resumed mercury discharge or conclusively resolve mootness.

Scalia and Thomas do not reach later mootness as an operative ground after finding no standing at commencement. They conditionally accept that, if initial standing existed, voluntary-cessation and closure questions could sensibly be remanded for factual assessment. They do not join a reduction of Article III's live-controversy requirement, a sunk-cost rationale, or Stevens's A5 position. The conditional agreement supplies neither a vote to enter A3's remand nor a finding of recurrence.

No Supreme Court finding of collusion or a final independent statutory-bar ruling follows. The $405,800 penalty and $1,092,581 net-benefit finding are not newly affirmed or increased. Denial of injunctive relief was not appealed; the asserted characterization of liability as declaratory relief does not establish a new recovered decree or authorize a new injunction. Fee timing was stayed, and no existing fee award is adjudicated. Fees cannot supply missing Article III standing or alone defeat mootness. The remand creates no new fee entitlement and resolves no final application after cessation.

## Existing judgment, merits, and enforcement

1. **Appellate effect.** Reversal eliminates the appellate order's legal force, including its district-judgment vacatur and dismissal direction. It returns the district judgment and the parties' unresolved appellate contentions to the ordinary review process. If the earlier appellate mandate had never taken effect, the district judgment did not need revival from an operative appellate vacatur; the effect is removal of that adverse appellate disposition. No unrecovered mandate, implementing dismissal, or stay is presumed to have issued.

2. **No ultimate approval.** The $405,800 judgment again stands as the district judgment under review, subject to the remaining properly preserved questions, including diligent prosecution, amount and the later closure/mootness inquiry. The Supreme Court does not affirm its amount, increase it, decide every predicate of liability, or preclude a later lawful modification or vacatur on an unresolved ground. A4's reservation of those questions does not itself keep the district judgment vacated. A remand leaves genuinely open matters for decision while binding the lower courts on matters actually decided. [In re Sanford Fork & Tool Co., 160 U.S. 247, 255–259](https://www.law.cornell.edu/supremecourt/text/160/247).

3. **Enforcement is separate.** The award is not declared immediately collectible on the day this Court announces its decision. Supreme Court Rule 45(3), as adopted January 11, 1999 and effective May 3, provides for transmission of a certified judgment to a federal court instead of a formal mandate unless specially directed; the ordinary timing and rehearing provisions of Rule 45(2) apply. Appellate Rule 41(c), effective December 1, 1998, makes a court of appeals' mandate effective when issued. Civil Rule 62(d), as in force here, permits an appeal stay by an approved supersedeas bond, and Rule 62(g) preserves appellate stay authority. The existence and terms of an actual applicable stay must be established; an unresolved appeal is not by itself an invented stay, and a reservation of merits is not an execution order. No new stay, accelerated mandate, payment date, interest determination or security condition is added. [1999 Supreme Court rules](https://uscode.house.gov/view.xhtml?edition=1999&req=granuleid%3AUSC-1999-title28a-node337), [1999 Appellate Rules, Rule 41](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/USCODE-1999-title28/html/USCODE-1999-title-app-dup2.htm), [1999 Civil Rules, Rule 62](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/USCODE-1999-title28/html/USCODE-1999-title-app-dup3.htm).

4. **A5's wording resolved expressly.** Its sentence denying “automatic reinstatement” was too broad if read to deny restoration of the district judgment to ordinary appellate status. That literal reading is rejected by this clarification, rather than silently written into or out of the old freeze. The retained limit is **no automatic final affirmance of the penalty, increase, fee award, or unconditional immediate collection**. Stevens's additional view that a properly initiated monetary penalty claim survives later cessation remains his alone; it is especially incompatible with assigning him an unexplained requirement that the existing judgment remain vacated. The other five do not adopt his broader survival rule and retain the actual closure inquiry. None of these distinctions changes the common appellate return.

Friends of the Earth, Inc. v. Laidlaw Environmental Services (TOC), Inc., No. 98-822. Merits decision, January 12, 2000. Argued October 12, 1999. On writ of certiorari to the United States Court of Appeals for the Fourth Circuit. The questions concern standing to seek Clean Water Act civil penalties for violations ongoing when suit began, and whether later compliance and asserted facility closure render the case moot. The July 16, 1998 appellate judgment preceded the later closure evidence.

## Participation

Chief Justice Stone-Zsela and Justices Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg, and Breyer participate.

## Public Action

The Court reverses the Fourth Circuit's judgment and remands for proceedings consistent with its standing, deterrent-redress and voluntary-cessation holdings.

## Judgment & Remedy

Reversed and remanded, 7–2. Supporting: Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Kennedy, Souter, Ginsburg, and Breyer. Opposing: Scalia and Thomas, who would direct jurisdictional dismissal. The appellate vacatur and dismissal direction are set aside. The district penalty judgment returns to ordinary appellate review, subject to the unresolved issues and applicable stay procedures; this disposition does not affirm its merits or amount. No immediate collection order, new injunction, fee award or special stay is entered.

## Holdings

**Common authority:** The standing, deterrent-redress and bounded-remand holdings in O'Connor's opinion have direct support from Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Kennedy, Souter, Ginsburg, and Breyer. Scalia and Thomas disagree. Stevens's additional penalty-survival theory is not a holding of the Court.

**Concrete interference with members' own river use supplies injury.** Supported curtailment of identified local recreational and aesthetic use because of reasonable concern about ongoing mercury discharges is a concrete personal injury; Article III does not require separate proof of environmental damage when the asserted injury is this loss of use and enjoyment. General interest in compliance or unexplained subjective concern alone remains insufficient.

**Controlling explanation:** Lujan requires personal interests and stage-appropriate support, rather than a generalized desire for lawful government. Curtis identified nearby downstream activities and a present wish to return. His childhood cessation was not caused by pollution; that fact does not erase the distinct present-use assertion. Moore's two visits do not become a history of frequent recreation, but her desired activities likewise must be evaluated accurately. Patterson's earlier local recreation and present curtailment independently connect CLEAN's claim to the river. These accounts differ from indefinite intentions to visit distant places. The later findings of adequate water quality and no demonstrated significant environmental harm deserve weight, but do not conclusively resolve reasonable lost enjoyment during ongoing discharge violations at filing. The Court does not treat a permit breach as injury to everyone downstream, decide every property valuation, or separately establish Sierra Club's standing when injured members of the other organizations support the same joined request.

**Deterrent penalties can redress supported threatened personal injury.** A statutorily authorized civil penalty payable to the Treasury can provide Article III redress when it is likely to deter continuing or reasonably recurring violations affecting the plaintiff's supported personal interests. Payment to the plaintiff is unnecessary for that preventive effect, but punishment of wholly past conduct without a remaining remedial connection, satisfaction from punishment and fees alone are insufficient.

**Controlling explanation:** Steel Co. dismissed a suit filed after cure with no continuing or imminent violation alleged, while expressly leaving deterrent redress available for supported threatened injury. This action began amid mercury exceedances; the record contains fourteen on or after June 12, 1992 and thirteen strictly after that filing date. The penalty can affect compliance with those same obligations and therefore the use interests at risk. Government enforcement and other litigation exposure do not eliminate the supported deterrent effect on this record. The Court does not demand certainty or hold that every public penalty necessarily supplies redress.

**Later compliance does not automatically establish mootness; the closure evidence must be assessed.** A defendant relying on voluntary cessation must make it absolutely clear that the challenged injurious conduct cannot reasonably be expected to recur. Substantial compliance sufficient to defeat an injunction does not automatically satisfy that burden or erase standing that existed at filing.

**Controlling explanation:** The Fourth Circuit treated the remaining Treasury penalty as incapable of affecting a live controversy; that premise conflicts with the supported deterrent connection. Later closure presents a different factual question. The affidavit identifies the last discharge on September 1, 1998, distinct closure-plan approvals and dismantling/sale status as of May 21, 1999. It also admits permit retention. The 1997 agreement required incineration to cease, while allowing specified equivalent treatment capacity subject to approval, permits, reconstruction conditions and cessation of incineration before use. Neither a retained permit nor dismantling alone decides recurrence. The lower court must evaluate those facts and legal restraints without presuming resumed operations or permanent impossibility. An asserted 1999 reporting notice, known through a declaration's database/conversation account, establishes neither resumed mercury discharge nor conclusive mootness.

**Limits and questions not reached:** The Court does not newly affirm or increase the $405,800 penalty, adjudicate the preserved diligent-prosecution defense, award fees, reopen the unappealed denial of an injunction, or decide the unpresented Article II question. The dispute over whether the liability ruling itself was declaratory relief does not establish a separate declaratory decree in the record. Fees neither cure missing standing nor alone prevent mootness. Broader survival of a properly initiated penalty claim after proven permanent cessation is reserved by the Court.

## Precedent Treatment

Lujan v. Defenders of Wildlife is applied to concrete member-use interests and stage-appropriate proof; generalized environmental concern remains insufficient. Steel Co. is distinguished because violations were ongoing at filing and its expressly retained deterrence principle applies; its completed-injury limit remains intact. United Food & Commercial Workers v. Brown Group preserves the separation between representative enforcement and members' personal stakes. The established voluntary-cessation rule is applied without equating denial of prospective equitable relief with constitutional mootness. Tull v. United States informs Stevens's separate penalty analogy but supplies no additional controlling holding here.

## Law After Decision

Concrete, supported loss of local use may establish injury even without separately proved environmental damage, and an authorized Treasury penalty may redress continuing or reasonably recurring personal harm through deterrence. Standing at commencement remains distinct from later mootness. Closure must be evaluated under the demanding cessation rule, and penalties for wholly past conduct without a personal remedial connection remain outside this holding. Stevens's broader penalty-survival theory has no other join.

## Separate Writings

Justice Stevens joins the Court and adds a distinct monetary-remedy ground. In his view, a valid civil-penalty judgment cannot be retroactively invalidated simply because postjudgment closure makes recurrence impossible. He also concludes that a properly initiated civil-penalty claim survives postcomplaint cessation even before judgment: the Clean Water Act attaches monetary liability to violations, and Tull's distinction between monetary penalties and prospective injunctions supports that treatment. Initial standing and the preserved statutory diligent-prosecution bar remain necessary. He does not authorize suit by an uninjured person, rely on fees for a personal stake, finally approve the $405,800 amount or order collection. His broader survival theory has no other join; he supports the Court's sufficient grounds and ordinary appellate return while disagreeing that proof of permanent cessation necessarily defeats this properly initiated penalty claim.

Justice Scalia, joined by Thomas, dissents on two independently sufficient Article III grounds. First, the science-based findings of adequate recreational water quality and no demonstrated adverse environmental effect undermine an objective connection between these exceedances and the asserted use loss. Expressed concern is not self-proving injury. Curtis's childhood cessation for another reason and Moore's limited visits reinforce their objection to treating desired use as established interference, without requiring ecosystem destruction in every case or resting their objection on litigant default.

Second, even assuming injury, the State's penalty, governmental enforcement, later compliance and litigation exposure make the additional federal penalty's likely marginal effect on these members' future harm too speculative. They accept Steel Co.'s rule that Treasury penalties can deter supported threatened injury; they reject this factual showing rather than all private reliance on public penalties. General deterrent purpose is not enough, although certainty is not required. Their concern about private public-law enforcement supplies no separate Article II holding. If initial standing were assumed, they accept that cessation and closure could sensibly receive factual review, but that conditional view supplies no vote for the Court's operative reversal, restoration or remand. They would direct jurisdictional dismissal.

## Procedure After Action

The Fourth Circuit must address the remaining appeal and cross-appeal under the holdings stated here, including supported closure and recurrence facts, the preserved diligent-prosecution defense and remaining penalty questions. The district judgment returns to unresolved ordinary appellate status when reversal takes effect through the usual appellate process. No prior mandate, implementing dismissal, execution stay or bond is presumed. Collection remains governed by ordinary mandate and applicable stay rules; the Court orders neither immediate execution nor a new stay. The fee-petition timing stay supplies no independent jurisdiction or fee award.
