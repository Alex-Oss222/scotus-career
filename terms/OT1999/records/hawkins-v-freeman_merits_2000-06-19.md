## Event, chronology and participation

Hawkins v. Freeman, reviewing 195 F.3d 732 (4th Cir. 1999) (en banc). Plenary merits decision, June 19, 2000; argued May 1, 2000, following the March 6 grant. October Term 1999, chunk 8. On writ of certiorari to the United States Court of Appeals for the Fourth Circuit. The Court reviews its affirmance of the denial of a 1995-filed §2254 application after discovery and summary judgment. The vacated earlier panel decision is not the judgment under review. The question is whether return to custody after mistaken parole violated substantive due process, including the proper treatment of the actual liberty and executive conduct. No independent procedural-hearing claim is presented. The application's pre-AEDPA regime governs; the later appeal does not introduce amended §2254(d)'s merits restriction.

Hawkins received a fifty-year habitual-felon sentence in February 1981 with concurrent lesser terms. His offense retained the former seventy-five-percent parole-eligibility requirement; the later seven-year law governed only offenses after July 1, 1981. His prescribed eligibility date was April 20, 2018. After inconsistent advice, including an erroneous 2010 date, the Commission correctly advised him of 2018 in September 1983. In 1992 it nevertheless approved intensive community-service parole under an inapplicable program. An investigation recommended release based on his institutional record, education, work, overcrowding and intended supervision, while acknowledging concern about his prior sexual-assault history. He was released July 6, 1992.

He lived with his brother and worked regularly as a forklift operator, receiving favorable evaluations. He committed no new crime or behavioral parole violation. He did fall substantially behind in community-service and financial obligations, and revocation had been threatened before discovery of the eligibility error. The State did not revoke him on those arrears.

Officials discovered the eligibility mistake on March 24, 1994, issued a rescission certificate and arrest warrant, and arrested him the next day. Hawkins waived the preliminary hearing. A counseled formal hearing occurred June 8, and decision was deferred for supporting documentation. The Commission received favorable work and release affidavits and his religious-rehabilitation account, not formally contested by the State, then revoked parole solely because it had been unauthorized. The unchanged 2018 calculation credited his time at liberty. State courts rejected his challenges. The federal courts characterized the administrative error as negligence and mistake, without an inference of malicious or gross error. The en banc court did not decide whether state law compelled revocation or permitted discretion. Earlier correct notice does not establish that Hawkins knowingly procured or recognized the 1992 error.

### This mistaken conditional release does not constitutionally discharge the existing sentence

**Holding and operative rule:** A mistaken grant of parole does not require permanent release from the valid, unchanged and uncompleted sentence on this record: approximately twenty months of supervised community liberty, earlier correct eligibility notice, consideration of favorable adjustment evidence, a formal hearing, credit for the release period, and correction directed to enforcing the lawful sentence. The real liberty and successful adjustment do not themselves establish a fundamental entitlement to retain the unauthorized release or make this considered return to custody a substantive-due-process violation.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court treats the lived liberty as real. Young and Morrissey protect existing conditional community life rather than dismissing it because legal custody continues. They do not extinguish the sentence or make every unauthorized parole irrevocable. Hawkins's official release, employment, family life and favorable rehabilitation therefore deserve consideration, but the question remains whether their withdrawal here constitutionally bars enforcement of the unserved sentence.

Daniels excludes negligence alone as a constitutional deprivation. That rule does not finish this case: the original error and the later deliberate return to custody are different acts. The latter enforced the existing sentence after supervised release and an opportunity to present the favorable evidence, with release time credited. No supported bad-faith inducement, manipulation or attempt to evade a legally completed sentence changes that account. Neither the arrears nor an invented rejection of the rehabilitation evidence supplies the revocation ground.

The strongest contrary argument invokes formal State investigation and approval, twenty months of reintegration and reliance on the resulting community life. Those facts make the loss serious; they do not transform conditional parole into a final adjudication shortening the sentence. The earlier correct eligibility notice is relevant without proving knowing participation in the error. The State's continuing punishment and parole-administration interests justify this correction on the supported record.

Glucksberg rejects a particular assisted-suicide entitlement, not every liberty lacking the narrowest historical analogue. Lewis confines its harmful-purpose rule to rapid pursuit and reserves deliberative settings. Neither supplies the lower court's proposed universal formula. The Court reaches the bounded substantive conclusion without a generalized reliance-balancing test, an all-case malice requirement, or a finding that state law compelled return. Correcting the lower court's methodology requires no new hearing because the favorable facts and unchanged sentence permit decision without an unresolved material factual premise.

**Limits and questions not reached:** The Court decides no state-law credit, estoppel, compensation or independent procedural defect in retaking. It does not decide a much longer state-caused delay, affirmative inducement of irreversible reliance, deliberate manipulation, bad faith, or custody after a sentence has legally ended. Reserving those different cases establishes neither an exception nor an entitlement, estoppel test, automatic remand or new balancing procedure. No general rule permits return after every duration or form of official abandonment. The Court finds no new dangerousness, behavioral violation, knowing procurement of error, mandatory revocation or promise of irrevocable release.

### This mistaken conditional release does not constitutionally discharge the existing sentence

**Holding and operative rule:** A mistaken grant of parole does not require permanent release from the valid, unchanged and uncompleted sentence on this record: approximately twenty months of supervised community liberty, earlier correct eligibility notice, consideration of favorable adjustment evidence, a formal hearing, credit for the release period, and correction directed to enforcing the lawful sentence. The real liberty and successful adjustment do not themselves establish a fundamental entitlement to retain the unauthorized release or make this considered return to custody a substantive-due-process violation.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court treats the lived liberty as real. Young and Morrissey protect existing conditional community life rather than dismissing it because legal custody continues. They do not extinguish the sentence or make every unauthorized parole irrevocable. Hawkins's official release, employment, family life and favorable rehabilitation therefore deserve consideration, but the question remains whether their withdrawal here constitutionally bars enforcement of the unserved sentence.

Daniels excludes negligence alone as a constitutional deprivation. That rule does not finish this case: the original error and the later deliberate return to custody are different acts. The latter enforced the existing sentence after supervised release and an opportunity to present the favorable evidence, with release time credited. No supported bad-faith inducement, manipulation or attempt to evade a legally completed sentence changes that account. Neither the arrears nor an invented rejection of the rehabilitation evidence supplies the revocation ground.

The strongest contrary argument invokes formal State investigation and approval, twenty months of reintegration and reliance on the resulting community life. Those facts make the loss serious; they do not transform conditional parole into a final adjudication shortening the sentence. The earlier correct eligibility notice is relevant without proving knowing participation in the error. The State's continuing punishment and parole-administration interests justify this correction on the supported record.

Glucksberg rejects a particular assisted-suicide entitlement, not every liberty lacking the narrowest historical analogue. Lewis confines its harmful-purpose rule to rapid pursuit and reserves deliberative settings. Neither supplies the lower court's proposed universal formula. The Court reaches the bounded substantive conclusion without a generalized reliance-balancing test, an all-case malice requirement, or a finding that state law compelled return. Correcting the lower court's methodology requires no new hearing because the favorable facts and unchanged sentence permit decision without an unresolved material factual premise.

**Limits and questions not reached:** The Court decides no state-law credit, estoppel, compensation or independent procedural defect in retaking. It does not decide a much longer state-caused delay, affirmative inducement of irreversible reliance, deliberate manipulation, bad faith, or custody after a sentence has legally ended. Reserving those different cases establishes neither an exception nor an entitlement, estoppel test, automatic remand or new balancing procedure. No general rule permits return after every duration or form of official abandonment. The Court finds no new dangerousness, behavioral violation, knowing procurement of error, mandatory revocation or promise of irrevocable release.

## Precedent Treatment

- **Young v. Harper, March 18, 1997:** Applies the recognition of actual parole-like community liberty while preserving the distinction between procedural protection and substantive irrevocability. Its preliminary and final safeguards are not redecided on an unpresented procedural claim.
- **Morrissey v. Brewer, 408 U.S. 471:** Preserves the protected conditional liberty and distinction between initial detention and final revocation. It does not itself discharge an uncompleted sentence.
- **Daniels v. Williams, 474 U.S. 327:** Preserves the negligence exclusion, while examining the later considered correction separately from the initial administrative mistake.
- **Washington v. Glucksberg, June 26, 1997:** Preserves its bounded assistance-in-suicide holding and reservation of materially different particularized claims; rejects use of it as an exclusive most-specific-history method for all liberties.
- **County of Sacramento v. Lewis, May 26, 1998:** Preserves its rapid-pursuit harmful-purpose rule and deliberation reservation. That emergency rule does not decide the Commission's considered action.
- **Saenz v. Heldenfels Brothers, Inc., May 9, 2000:** Preserves separate identification of the interest, affirmative interference, culpability and causal effect. Its damages-immunity inquiry supplies no substitute merits bar in this habeas case.

## Law After Decision

Effective June 19, 2000, the supported mistaken-parole circumstances do not create a substantive entitlement to discharge from this valid, uncompleted sentence. Actual conditional liberty retains its procedural protection; sentence enforcement and the substantive constitutional claim remain distinct. The lower court's universal historical and executive-purpose formulations are not adopted. The decision adds a bounded application, creates no general reliance exception or mandatory methodology, and overrules no precedent.
