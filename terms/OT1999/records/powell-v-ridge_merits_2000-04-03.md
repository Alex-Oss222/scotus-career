### Issue 1: Private enforcement of Title VI regulations

* **Threshold and Merits Reach:** Stone reaches both presented channels. The schoolchildren's alleged racially unequal treatment in the distribution of state educational funding presents an Article III controversy, and enforceability is a merits question distinct from standing. Count I (a directly implied Title VI action) and Count II (an individual statutory right enforceable through § 1983) are separable questions, and each is decided on its own ground.
* **Stone's Legal Position:** On Count I, state a new statutory holding that entering law does not supply: Title VI supports a bounded private action for prospective compliance with valid § 602 effects regulations, applying and extending Cannon in its Title VI-derived context. On Count II, apply current law: Blessing, Suter, Wright and Wilder require a right the statute confers, and the asserted regulation-created right fails. The holding is intermediate: it governs private prospective enforcement of valid Title VI effects regulations, not every federal regulation and not retrospective relief.
* **Stone's Proposed Holding:** A person suffering a concrete injury within a federally assisted program may bring a private action for declaratory and appropriately confined prospective equitable relief against a covered recipient's ongoing violation of a valid, duly issued Title VI § 602 regulation prohibiting discriminatory effects. The action exists within Title VI's particular statutory context; it does not mean every federal regulation creates a private cause of action. The plaintiff must establish (1) an applicable § 602 regulation validly issued under the authority Congress conferred, consistent with Title VI's statutory limits; (2) a defendant subject to the regulation through the legally required federal-assistance relationship; (3) a concrete exclusion, denial of benefits, or other material discriminatory effect within the regulation's actual terms, attributable to an identified recipient policy or method of administration; (4) an actual or imminent continuing effect that the requested prospective relief can meaningfully correct; and (5) an appropriate defendant and a remedy compatible with applicable immunity, statutory and equitable limitations. No prior agency finding is required. The action supplies a declaration of an established violation, an injunction requiring the recipient to end or correct the prohibited method, a reasonable compliance period where consistent with preventing ongoing injury, and ordinary equitable supervision proportionate to the violation. Section 602's notice, voluntary-compliance, hearing, congressional-reporting and program-specific termination restrictions continue to govern the agency's own powers. Section 601's intentional-discrimination protection and § 602's directions to agencies do not confer the broader individual statutory right against disparate impact asserted in Count II: a valid regulation does not independently create the broader individual statutory right required for § 1983 where the underlying statute does not confer it, and the effects-only § 1983 claim remains unavailable under Blessing, Wright and Wilder. That rejection rests on the absence of a right, not on congressional foreclosure of an established one, and it does not foreclose § 1983 enforcement when a different statute actually confers the individual entitlement a regulation implements. The children may pursue Count I under these requirements; Count II is dismissed.
* **Essential Reasoning and Record Application:** Contextual statutory interpretation informed by established precedent decides both counts, and the distinction between equitable compliance and retrospective compensation carries decisive remedial weight. Section 601 protects identified beneficiaries from exclusion, denial of benefits and discrimination in federally assisted programs, and § 602 directs the funding agencies to effectuate that protection through rules consistent with the funding statute's objectives. Cannon recognized private judicial enforcement of this beneficiary-focused scheme, and Congress has legislated where private Title VI enforcement and effects regulations were familiar. Read with that history, Title VI supports a private action compelling a covered recipient's prospective compliance with a valid effects regulation that implements the statutory protection. Guardians, a fractured decision whose disputed plurality propositions are not treated as earlier majority holdings, distinguished prospective compliance from retrospective compensation; that distinction fixes the action's bounds, and Franklin's remedial principles presuppose a valid cause of action rather than supplying damages for effects alone. Structural reasoning about agency responsibilities supplies limits but does not make the funding-termination procedure the exclusive route to prospective compliance. The contrary reading — that § 601 reaches only intentional discrimination and § 602 speaks only to agencies — gives too little weight to the beneficiary-focused design and the recognized private enforcement of the scheme, and the action's prospective confinement keeps an unintended effect from becoming retrospective liability. Count II asks a different question. Section 1983 enforces rights secured by federal law, and Blessing requires a particular provision intended to benefit the plaintiff that imposes a binding duty and defines a sufficiently definite individual entitlement. Section 601 confers a right against intentional discrimination; § 602 addresses agency implementation. Wright and Wilder let an entitlement the statute confers take concrete content from authorized regulations, but § 100.3(b)(2)'s effects prohibition reaches beyond § 601's own prohibition, and Suter and the CAPTA decision confirm that a binding agency obligation need not confer the personal entitlement asserted. Bruneau's rule that funding oversight or an implied remedy does not by itself foreclose § 1983 enforcement of an established right is unchanged; this holding rests on right creation. The two answers are consistent: Title VI's own scheme supplies a bounded equitable compliance action; it does not convert a regulatory effects standard into a statutory individual right carrying § 1983's broader remedial consequences. The schoolchildren and parents allege that Pennsylvania's funding method gives districts with more nonwhite pupils less state funding at comparable poverty levels and that the resulting resource limits impair their educational opportunities; they seek declaratory and prospective injunctive relief, not damages. Those allegations state the Count I action. Whether the defendants are covered recipients, whether an identified funding method is a criterion or method of administration that causes the effects § 100.3(b)(2) prohibits, and what defenses the regulation supplies are for proof on remand. Count II fails at the right stage, whatever the strength of the allegations.

### Part I. Jurisdiction and the two separate questions (all nine)

**Holding:** The schoolchildren's alleged racially unequal treatment in the distribution of state educational funding presents an Article III controversy; whether a private plaintiff may enforce 34 C.F.R. §100.3(b)(2) is a merits question distinct from standing. Section 601, §602 and §100.3(b)(2) perform different work, and Counts I and II present separate questions: whether Title VI supports a directly implied action to enforce the regulation (Count I), and whether the regulation creates an individual statutory right enforceable through 42 U.S.C. §1983 (Count II). The answer to one does not decide the other.

**Controlling explanation:** Section 601 protects persons from exclusion, denial of benefits and discrimination on the ground of race, color or national origin in federally assisted programs. Section 602 directs the funding agencies to effectuate that protection by rules consistent with the funding statute's objectives and prescribes the agencies' compliance procedures. Section 100.3(b)(2) prohibits a covered recipient's criteria or methods of administration that have the effect of subjecting individuals to discrimination because of race, color or national origin, or of defeating or substantially impairing the program's objectives as respects individuals of a particular race, color or national origin. A directly implied action and a §1983 right are different legal inquiries; neither is the other's foreclosure question.

### Part III. Count II: no regulation-created right for section 1983 (Stone-Zsela, joined by O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy and Thomas; 5–4)

A valid regulation does not independently create the broader individual statutory right required for section 1983 where the underlying statute does not confer it; the effects-only section 1983 claim remains unavailable under Blessing, Wright and Wilder. The rejection rests on the absence of a right, not on congressional foreclosure of an established one. Count II is dismissed.

**Controlling explanation:** Blessing first asks for a particular provision intended to benefit the claimant that imposes a binding duty and defines a sufficiently definite individual entitlement; only after such a right exists do §1983's presumption of enforceability and the foreclosure inquiry arise. Suter and the CAPTA decision (Doe v. District of Columbia) show that a binding agency obligation need not confer the personal entitlement asserted. Wright and Wilder permit an enforceable statutory entitlement to take concrete content from authorized regulations; each rested on a statute that itself conferred the entitlement the regulation elaborated. Here §601 identifies protected individuals but confers a right against intentional discrimination, and §602 addresses agency implementation; §100.3(b)(2)'s effects prohibition reaches beyond §601's own prohibition. Answer to the dissent's strongest ground (the regulation elaborates §601's beneficiaries' protection as in Wright, so §1983 presumptively enforces it): elaboration gives content to a right the statute confers; it cannot create a substantively broader one. Bruneau's rule that administrative funding controls or an implied remedy do not by themselves foreclose §1983 enforcement of an established right is unchanged, and this Part does not rest on foreclosure.

## Public Projection

## Event

Powell v. Ridge. Merits decision, April 3, 2000. Certiorari granted December 13, 1999; submitted February 28, 2000. On review of the Third Circuit's judgment at 189 F.3d 387, reversing dismissal of two claims challenging alleged racial inequality in Pennsylvania's public-school funding. Count I seeks direct enforcement of 34 C.F.R. §100.3(b)(2); Count II invokes 42 U.S.C. §1983. Both seek declaratory and prospective injunctive relief, not damages.

The questions are whether Title VI's disparate-impact regulation is privately enforceable through either channel, and whether the regulatory theory permits relief without the intentional discrimination required by §601 itself. Parents and schoolchildren allege that districts with more nonwhite pupils receive less state funding at comparable poverty levels and that the resulting resource limits impair educational opportunities. These remain pleading-stage allegations, not findings of discrimination, causation or a funding entitlement.

## Participation

Chief Justice Stone-Zsela and Justices Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer participated at submission and decision.

## Public Action

The judgment is affirmed as to Count I and reversed as to Count II, and the case is remanded.

## Holdings

**Common authority:** Chief Justice Stone-Zsela's Opinion of the Court controls by Parts. Part I, joined by all nine, decides the common threshold. Part II, joined by Justices Stevens, Souter, Ginsburg and Breyer, controls Count I. Part III, joined by Justices O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy and Thomas, controls Count II.

### Part I. Jurisdiction and the two separate questions (all nine)

**Holding:** The schoolchildren's alleged racially unequal treatment in the distribution of state educational funding presents an Article III controversy; whether a private plaintiff may enforce 34 C.F.R. §100.3(b)(2) is a merits question distinct from standing. Section 601, §602 and §100.3(b)(2) perform different work, and Counts I and II present separate questions: whether Title VI supports a directly implied action to enforce the regulation (Count I), and whether the regulation creates an individual statutory right enforceable through 42 U.S.C. §1983 (Count II). The answer to one does not decide the other.

**Controlling explanation:** Section 601 protects persons from exclusion, denial of benefits and discrimination on the ground of race, color or national origin in federally assisted programs. Section 602 directs the funding agencies to effectuate that protection by rules consistent with the funding statute's objectives and prescribes the agencies' compliance procedures. Section 100.3(b)(2) prohibits a covered recipient's criteria or methods of administration that have the effect of subjecting individuals to discrimination because of race, color or national origin, or of defeating or substantially impairing the program's objectives as respects individuals of a particular race, color or national origin. A directly implied action and a §1983 right are different legal inquiries; neither is the other's foreclosure question.

### Part II. Count I: the bounded direct prospective action (Stone-Zsela, joined by Stevens, Souter, Ginsburg and Breyer; 5–4)

**Holding:** A person suffering a concrete injury within a federally assisted program may bring a private action for declaratory and appropriately confined prospective equitable relief against a covered recipient's ongoing violation of a valid, duly issued Title VI section 602 regulation prohibiting discriminatory effects. The action exists within Title VI's particular statutory context; it does not mean every federal regulation creates a private cause of action.

**Elements.** The plaintiff must establish: (1) an applicable section 602 regulation validly issued under the authority Congress conferred, consistent with Title VI's statutory limits; (2) a defendant subject to the regulation through the legally required federal-assistance relationship; (3) a concrete exclusion, denial of benefits, or other material discriminatory effect within the regulation's actual terms, attributable to an identified recipient policy or method of administration; (4) an actual or imminent continuing effect that the requested prospective relief can meaningfully correct; and (5) an appropriate defendant and a remedy compatible with applicable immunity, statutory and equitable limitations.

**Proof (answers Question 2).** The plaintiff proves a violation of the valid regulation on its own terms and need not prove the intentional discrimination §601 itself requires; a claim under §601 still requires intent. Disproportionate statistics may support proof, but a numerical racial difference alone does not conclusively establish a violation; the claimant must connect the disparity to the challenged administration and to the regulation's operative prohibition; the recipient may contest comparison, causation, materiality and regulatory coverage and invoke any qualification or defense the valid regulation supplies. No universal Title VII-style business-necessity framework applies; the text and authorized operation of each regulation govern.

**Remedies.** Declaration of an established violation; an injunction requiring the recipient to end or correct the prohibited method; a reasonable compliance period where consistent with preventing ongoing injury; ordinary equitable supervision proportionate to the violation. The action does not create damages for unintended disparate impact; punitive damages; retroactive compensation or backpay without an independent legal source; a private order terminating or withholding federal grants under section 602; authority to redesign an entire public program because one component produces a prohibited effect; or any exemption from Article III, proof, recipient-coverage or proper-defendant requirements. No prior agency finding is required. Section 602's notice, voluntary-compliance, hearing, congressional-reporting and program-specific termination restrictions continue to govern the agency's own powers.

**Application and remand.** The children and parents allege that Pennsylvania's funding method gives districts with more nonwhite pupils less state funding at comparable poverty levels and that the resulting resource limits impair their educational opportunities; they seek declaratory and prospective injunctive relief, not damages. Count I states this action and proceeds. Prevailing on the cause-of-action question yields no declaration that Pennsylvania's funding formula violates Title VI and no injunction. On remand the lower court must identify the challenged funding method, determine federal-assistance and regulatory coverage, evaluate material disparate effects and causation under the regulation, address valid defenses, and provide relief only if the violation is proved. The existing record may suffice for some questions; no new hearing is compelled and no liability is presumed.

**Controlling explanation:** Contextual statutory interpretation informed by established precedent decides the question: Title VI's beneficiary-focused protection, its authorized implementation, the recognized private judicial enforcement of the statutory scheme (Cannon), and the legislative and judicial history, including Congress legislating where private Title VI enforcement and effects regulations were familiar. Guardians, a fractured decision whose disputed plurality propositions are not treated as earlier majority holdings, drew the distinction between prospective compliance and retrospective compensation; that distinction carries decisive remedial weight and fixes the action's bounds. Franklin's remedial principles presuppose a valid cause of action and supply no damages for effects alone. Answer to the dissent's strongest ground (section 601 reaches only intentional discrimination and section 602 speaks only to agencies, so the effects obligation is enforceable only administratively): the beneficiary-focused design and the recognized private enforcement of the scheme support prospective compliance relief for the persons the regulation protects; structural reasoning about agency responsibilities supplies limits but does not make the funding-termination procedure the exclusive route to prospective compliance; and confining the action to prospective equitable relief keeps an unintended effect from becoming retrospective liability. Breadth: an intermediate statutory holding confined to private prospective enforcement of valid Title VI effects regulations.

### Part III. Count II: no regulation-created right for section 1983 (Stone-Zsela, joined by O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy and Thomas; 5–4)

**Holding:** Section 601's intentional-discrimination protection and §602's directions to agencies do not confer the broader individual statutory right against disparate impact asserted in Count II. A valid regulation does not independently create the broader individual statutory right required for section 1983 where the underlying statute does not confer it; the effects-only section 1983 claim remains unavailable under Blessing, Wright and Wilder. This does not foreclose section 1983 enforcement when a different statute actually confers the individual entitlement a regulation implements. The rejection rests on the absence of a right, not on congressional foreclosure of an established one. Count II is dismissed.

**Controlling explanation:** Blessing first asks for a particular provision intended to benefit the claimant that imposes a binding duty and defines a sufficiently definite individual entitlement; only after such a right exists do §1983's presumption of enforceability and the foreclosure inquiry arise. Suter and the CAPTA decision (Doe v. District of Columbia) show that a binding agency obligation need not confer the personal entitlement asserted. Wright and Wilder permit an enforceable statutory entitlement to take concrete content from authorized regulations; each rested on a statute that itself conferred the entitlement the regulation elaborated. Here §601 identifies protected individuals but confers a right against intentional discrimination, and §602 addresses agency implementation; §100.3(b)(2)'s effects prohibition reaches beyond §601's own prohibition. Answer to the dissent's strongest ground (the regulation elaborates §601's beneficiaries' protection as in Wright, so §1983 presumptively enforces it): elaboration gives content to a right the statute confers; it cannot create a substantively broader one. Bruneau's rule that administrative funding controls or an implied remedy do not by themselves foreclose §1983 enforcement of an established right is unchanged, and this Part does not rest on foreclosure.

### Scope and unresolved matters

The municipal plaintiffs' separate standing is unnecessary to the decision. Three legal questions remain open: the validity of any particular effects regulation, including §100.3(b)(2); damages for a distinct intentional violation; and remedies against differently situated state entities. No damages are sought and no §601 count was pleaded. Liability for the Pennsylvania funding system and actual defendant coverage are remand work under Part II, not open law. Properly presented intentional-discrimination claims remain subject to their own requirements.

## Precedent Treatment

- Cannon v. University of Chicago is applied and extended in its Title VI-derived context to support the bounded prospective action; it is not universal implied-action authority.
- Guardians Association v. Civil Service Commission is preserved as fractured precedent; no disputed plurality proposition is treated as an earlier majority holding, and its distinction between prospective compliance and retrospective compensation informs the action's remedial bounds.
- Alexander v. Choate is preserved; the statutory prohibition and the effects regulations remain distinct standards.
- Franklin v. Gwinnett County Public Schools is preserved; its remedial principles presuppose a valid cause of action and supply no damages for effects alone.
- Blessing v. Freestone, Wright v. Roanoke, Wilder v. Virginia Hospital Association and Suter v. Artist M. are preserved; Count II is consistent with them, because a regulation elaborates but does not create the right §1983 enforces.
- Gebser and Davis retain their intentional-discrimination attribution holdings; their mental-state requirements are not imported into every effects regulation.
- Section 602's agency termination provisions are preserved intact; no prior agency finding is required for the private prospective action.
- Doe v. District of Columbia supplies the distinction between a binding CAPTA agency command and the personal right asserted under §1983.
- Bruneau v. South Kortright Central School District is preserved: funding oversight or an implied remedy does not by itself foreclose §1983 enforcement of an established right, and Count II does not rest on foreclosure.
- NCAA v. Smith preserves the requirement of actual federal-assistance receipt separately from economic connection, applied on remand under element (2).
- Ex parte Young preserves prospective relief against a proper state officer, subject to element (5) on remand; it establishes no merits liability.

## Law After Decision

A person concretely injured within a federally assisted program may sue for declaratory and appropriately confined prospective equitable relief against a covered recipient's ongoing violation of a valid Title VI §602 effects regulation, on the five elements and proof rules of Part II. The action supplies no damages for unintended disparate impact, punitive damages, retroactive compensation or backpay without an independent legal source, a private order terminating or withholding federal grants under §602, or authority to redesign an entire public program because one component produces a prohibited effect, and requires no prior agency finding. A valid regulation does not create the broader individual statutory right §1983 requires where the statute does not confer it. The two holdings answer separate questions. A §601 claim still requires intent. Section 601 intentional-discrimination enforcement, genuine regulatory elaboration of existing rights, and authorized agency enforcement remain intact. Section 602's administrative conditions do not become private exhaustion requirements.

Agency termination or refusal of assistance remains subject to an express finding after a hearing opportunity, confined to the recipient and program or part found noncompliant; advice of noncompliance and inability to secure voluntary compliance; and a report to the appropriate congressional committees followed by thirty days before termination or refusal becomes effective. Other means authorized by law remain distinct. General rules remain subject to §602's statutory consistency and presidential-approval requirements. This judgment orders no agency enforcement action.

## Separate Writings

Justice Stevens, joined by Justices Souter, Ginsburg and Breyer, filed an opinion concurring in part and dissenting in part. The opinion joins Parts I and II and dissents from Part III; it would affirm the Third Circuit on Count II as well. Section 1983 independently supplies an enacted enforcement vehicle for this particular statutory/regulatory protection: the named beneficiaries' protection from racially discriminatory administration of covered educational benefits, elaborated by the authorized effects regulation. Blessing's intended-benefit, binding-duty and definiteness requirements are met in that combination, and Wright supports reading statutory protection with its authorized regulatory content; the right is not an abstract claim to good administration, sufficient funding or a preferred formula.

Once the right is identified, §1983's presumption applies; §602's funding machinery offers no comparable personal remedial procedure, and Bruneau rejects foreclosure from funding oversight or an existing implied remedy. Proving the regulatory violation does not additionally require §601 intent, which remains required for the distinct §601 claim. The opinion finds no violation, coverage or causation and would require a covered recipient, prohibited practice, causal injury and fitted relief.

Justice O'Connor, joined by Justices Scalia, Kennedy and Thomas, filed an opinion concurring in part and dissenting in part. The opinion joins Parts I and III and dissents from Part II; it would reverse on Count I as well. Congress supplied in §601 an individual protection against intentional discrimination and in §602 authority for agencies to implement it through the public compliance procedures Congress prescribed. The validity of an agency condition does not itself demonstrate congressional creation of the particular private remedy asserted for the broader effects obligation; Cannon's established action enforces the statutory right and does not automatically expand to every implementing condition; §602 supplies agency authority, not congressional creation of this private regulatory remedy. The form of relief requested does not supply that creation: the remedy cannot create its own substantive predicate. The opinion does not question the regulation's validity, restrict agency enforcement or funding remedies, convert §602's procedures into private exhaustion or foreclosure rules, or question the Article III controversy found in Part I; §601 intentional-discrimination claims remain enforceable.

No separate §601 claim is treated as already pleaded. No liability finding, declaration, injunction, funding order or retained Supreme Court return condition is created.
