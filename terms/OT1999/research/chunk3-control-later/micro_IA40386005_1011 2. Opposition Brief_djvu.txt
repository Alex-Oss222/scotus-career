RESPONDENT'S BRIEF IN OPPOSITION 


Respondent, J. Duke Albanese, Commissioner of the 
Maine Department of Education, respectfully requests 
that this Court deny the Petition for Writ of Certiorari 
seeking review of the decision and judgment of the 
United States Court of Appeals for the First Circuit, 
issued on May 27, 1999, reproduced in the Appendix to 
the Petition for Writ of Certiorari (“Pet. App.”) at la-24a, 
reported in Strout v. Albanese, 178 F.3d 57 (1st Cir. 1999). 


+ 


STATEMENT OF THE CASE 


This petition parallels that of Bagley v. Raymond 
School Department, 728 A.2d 127 (Me. 1999), petition for 
cert. filed, 68 U.S.L.W. 3105 (U.S. Aug. 10, 1999) (No. 
99-163). The Maine Law Court in Bagley and the First 
Circuit in this case each upheld Maine’s school voucher 
program against First Amendment challenges. Although 
petitioners in the present case seek to characterize their 
challenge more creatively, the core issue is their effort to 
require the State to pay an unrestricted, direct public 
subsidy to a pervasively sectarian high school for reli- 
gious education. Such an unfettered subsidy has never 
been required or even permitted by this Court or in any 
of the cases referred to by petitioners. 


The parties generally agree that the material facts are 
undisputed. The State of Maine operates a tuition pro- 
gram. Maine requires all school districts to provide edu- 
cation for their residents from kindergarten to the 12th 
grade. 20-A M.R.S.A. § 1001(8). Approximately one-half 
of the school districts satisfy this obligation by operating 


Nm 


their own elementary and secondary schools. Petitioners 
do not suggest that in such circumstance, the district 
must.pay for or in any way subsidize a separate sectarian 


education 


Due to the lack of density of population, the other 
half of the school districts, including that of the Town of 
Minot, fulfill their public education obligation through a 
tuition program established pursuant to 20-A M.R.S.A. 
§ 5204. Under this alternative program, these districts do 
not operate their own public schools, but rather pay 


tuition for their students to attend other schools. 


Pursuant to Maine’s school voucher program, the 
school district pays tuition directly to a public school or 
to a private school that has been approved for tuition 
purposes pursuant to 20-A M.R.S.A. § 2951. The statute 
specifically provides that sectarian schools are ineligible 
tor approval for tuition purposes. 20-A M.R.S.A. 
§ 2951(2). Nearly 14,000 students attend public or 
approved private schools under the tuition program, 
resulting in the expenditure of approximately $70 million 


in public funds. 


Minot operates a public school for kindergarten 
through 8th grade, which all of the petitioners’ children 
attend. However, Minot does not run a high school, and 
instead provides secondary education through the tuition 
program. The petitioners — three sets of parents in Minot 

decided to send their children to St. Dominic Regional 
High School in Lewiston, Maine. St. Dominic is a per- 
vasively sectarian Catholic high school. Because St. 
Dominic’s tuition is less than the maximum allowable 
tuition under the program, all of the tuition at St. 


Dominic’s would be paid by the government. Petitioners 
decided to send their children to St. Dominic for a variety 
of reasons, including availability, academics, and atmo- 
sphere, and although all of the petitioners believe that the 
religious aspects of St. Dominic are a positive factor, none 
of the petitioners contend that a central tenet of their 
religion compelled them to send their children to that 


school. 


On November 21, 1997, petitioners filed suit against 
the Commissioner of the Maine Department of Education, 
as well as several local defendants.' Petitioners con- 
tended that the district was required by the First Amend- 
ment to make direct, unrestricted payments to the 
pervasively sectarian high school for their children’s reli- 
gious education. The petitioners and the Commissioner 
filed cross-motions for summary judgment, and on 
August 11, 1998, the Districf Court (Hornby, J.) granted 
the Commissioner’s cross-motion, concluding that: 


I decide only whether Maine is constitutionally 
required to extend subsidies to sectarian 
schools. I find no authority for such a require- 
ment. Although the plaintiffs have phrased their 
arguments in terms of free exercise claims, 
establishment clause claims, equal protection 
claims and substantive due process claims, | 
find it unnecessary to address these separately 
or to analyze the various tests that have been 
enumerated. The same answer is obvious for all. 


' The local defendants included the Minot School 
Committee, Maine School Union #29, and the Superintendent of 
School Union #29. These defendants were dismissed out of the 
case, which is not an issue being raised before this Court. 


The Plaintiffs certainly are free to send their 
children to a sectarian school. The right is pro- 
tected by the Constitution. The law is clear, 
however, that they do not have the right to 
require taxpayers to subsidize that choice. That 
is true in a district that builds and operates its 
own public high school; it is also true in a 
district that has no public high school. 


Strout v. Commissioner, Maine Department of Education, 13 
F.Supp.2d 112, 114 (D.Me. 1998), reproduced at Pet. App. 
at 27a (citations and footnotes omitted). 


On appeal, the First Circuit affirmed unanimously. 
Unlike the District Court, Chief Judge Torruella, writing 
for Judge Stahl and himself, addressed seriatim each of 
the bases upon which petitioners challenged the school 
voucher system. First addressing the Establishment 


Clause, the court was 


at a loss to understand why plaintiffs-appellants 
believe that the Establishment Clause gives 
them a basis for recovery. The Establishment 
Clause forbids the making of a law respecting 
the establishment of any religion. There is no 
relevant precedent for using its negative prohi- 
bition as a basis for extending the right of a 
religiously affiliated group to secure state sub- 
sidies. 


Pet. App. at 15a. The court touched upon the use of the 
direct-indirect aid distinction as an instructive guideline 
but explained further that “one thing is certain: the 
Supreme Court has never permitted broad sponsorship of 
religious schools.” Id. at lla. The First Circuit observed 
that the only instances where the Supreme Court has 
permitted funding to flow to religious schools have been 


in the context of a targeted grant, available to a limited 


population, for a specific purpose. Id. The court correctly 
noted that the petitioners’ reliance upon “neutrality as 
the keystone of the Establishment Clause jurisprudence” 
forgets that neutrality is but one factor to be considered. 
Id. at 13a-14a. 


The court then went on to deal with petitioners’ 
equal protection argument, concluding that it failed 
because “[gliven the present state of jurisprudence, we 
see no way in which” requiring the State to pay grants 
directly to sectarian schools to subsidize children’s 
schooling “can be done without violating the Establish- 
ment Clause.” Id. at 16a. 


Petitioners’ free exercise claim was disposed of on a 
variety of grounds, including, inter alia, Maine’s voucher 
system does not prevent attendance at a religious school 
— all it means is that the costs of religious education must 
be borne by the parents and not the State; and religious 
education is not a central tenet of petitioners’ religious 
practice. Id. at 18a-19a. Finally, petitioners’ due process 
claim was summarily disposed of because, although sub- 
stantive due process precludes the State from preventing 
individuals from obtaining a religious education, it does 
not require the State to directly pay for a sectarian educa- 
tion. Id. at 19a. Regarding the free speech claim, the court 
correctly noted that there is “little more than academic 
articles to bolster the[ ] view” that the parents’ rights to 
communicate and instruct their children in the areas of 
religion, morals and ethics, are somehow infringed upon. 
Id. at 20a. 


Senior Circuit Judge Campbell wrote a concurring 
opinion, concluding that Maine law does not violate the 
free exercise clause nor principles of substantive due 
process. Id. at 20a. Judge Campbell further concluded that 
neither the Establishment nor equal protection clauses 
provide petitioners with an affirmative basis for requiring 
funding of sectarian schools, and, therefore, provide no 
basis for affirmative relief. Id. at 20a-21a. It was, thus, 
unnecessary, according to Judge Campbell, to reach the 
level of analysis of the Establishment Clause presented by 
Judge Torruella. Under the rational basis standard, 
Maine’s statute did not violate petitioners’ equal protec- 
tion rights. Id. at 21a-23a. 


REASONS FOR DENIAL OF THE WRIT 


Just as in the petition in Bagley, there is no compel- 
ling reason for the Court to review the First Circuit’s 
decision in this case. First, as in the case of Bagley, the 
issues the petitioners wish to present to the Court are not 
properly framed. Second, and more significantly, this 
Court, and the lower courts, have been both clear and 
consistent throughout the development of the jurispru- 
dence surrounding the First Amendment that unre- 
stricted, direct subsidies to pervasively sectarian schools 
for religious education violates the Establishment Clause. 
Moreover, as noted by both lower courts that have con- 
sidered the issue, this Court has never hinted that the 
Establishment Clause required the government to affirma- 


tively make such a subsidy. 


I. THE COURT SHOULD DENY THE WRIT BECAUSE 
THE ISSUES ARE NOT PROPERLY FRAMED. 


Petitioners here ignore a fatal deficiency rendering 
this matter inappropriate for certiorari. As a matter of 
fact, education at a parochial school is not a central 
religious belief or practice of these petitioners. Pet. App. 
at 18a. “The free exercise inquiry asks whether the gov- 
ernment has placed a substantial burden on the obser- 
vance of a central religious belief or practice... ” 
Hernandez v. Commissioner, 490 U.S. 680, 699 (1989) 
(emphasis added). Thus, this is not a case for the Court to 
consider the First Amendment issues as sought by peti- 


tioners. 


II. THE FIRST CIRCUIT DECISION DOES NOT 
CONFLICT WITH THIS COURT’S PRECEDENTS. 


The First Circuit’s decision is in full conformance 
with this Court’s precedents involving the Establishment 
Clause.? In developing its First Amendment jurispru- 
dence over the last three decades, the Court has focused 
on the specific features of the particular government 
action before it, cautioning further that the Court’s prece- 
dent remains intact unless directly overruled. Agostini v. 
Felton, 521 U.S. 203, 237 (1997); Rosenberger v. Rector & 


* Although petitioners parse their petition into five 
separate questions, presenting challenges based upon the 
Establishment Clause, the Equal Protection Clause, the Free 
Exercise Clause, substantive due process, and the Free Speech 
Clause (Petition at i-ii), the substance of their petition deals 
almost exclusively with Establishment Clause jurisprudence. 
We will therefore focus our attention in this area. 


Visitors of the University of Virginia, 515 U.S. 819, 852 
(1995) (O’Connor, J., concurring). The Court has consis- 
tently and clearly stated that neither the Establishment 
Clause nor the Free Exercise Clause requires, or even 
allows, direct, unrestricted governmental financial sub- 
sidies to a sectarian school for a religious education. The 
invalidity of such payments to religious schools for a 
religious education has not been questioned. The juris- 
prudence surrounding the Establishment Clause has 
developed in an effort to determine whether particular 
types of aid constitute unconstitutional direct payment of 
public funds from the government to a religious school. 


Petitioners certainly have the right to send their chil- 
dren to a private sectarian school. However, efforts to 
require a government to pay for assistance to sectarian 
schools have been consistently rejected. Luetkemeyer v. 
Kaufmann, 364 F.Supp. 376 (W.D. Mo. 1973) (3-judge 
court), aff'd mem. 419 U.S. 888 (1974) (refused to require 
state to provide transportation to parochial school chil- 
dren although it could do so under Establishment 
Clause); Brusca v. Missouri, 332 F.Supp. 275 (E.D. Mo. 
1971) (3-judge court), aff'd mem. 405 U.S. 1050 (1972) 
(claim that state must provide tuition assistance to chil- 
dren to attend sectarian school rejected under rational 
basis scrutiny). This Court has held in several contexts 
“that a legislature’s decision not to subsidize the exercise 
of a fundamental right does not infringe that right... ” 
Regan v. Taxation With Representation, 461 U.S. 540, 546, 
549 (1983) (rejected the “notion that First Amendment

rights are somehow not fully realized unless they are 
subsidized by the State”), quoting Cammarano v. United 
States, 358 U.S. 498, 515 (1959) (Douglas, J., concurring); 
see also Rust v. Sullivan, 500 U.S. 173, 201 (1991) (“the 
Government has no constitutional duty to subsidize an 
activity merely because the activity is constitutionally 
protected”) (citation omitted). As noted by the lower 
court, petitioners’ efforts to use the Establishment 
Clause’s “negative prohibition” as a sword to require state 
subsidies is without precedent. Pet. App. at 15a. 


Further, those cases that have considered the type of 
aid the government may, if it chooses, provide to sec- 
tarian schools have consistently rejected the type of sub- 
sidies sought by petitioners. In Community for Public 
Education v. Nyquist, 413 U.S. 756 (1973), the Court had 
before it three forms of educational aid: direct grants to 
nonpublic schools, tuition reimbursement to parents, and 
tax deductions to parents. The Court struck down direct 
unrestricted grants to sectarian schools as anathema to 
the Establishment Clause. Id. at 774-76. The majority also 
rejected the two other forms of aid with regard to sec- 
tarian schools. Writing for the dissent, Justice Rehnquist 
only disagreed with those portions of the decision relat- 
ing to tax credits and deductions, and did not dissent to 
the striking down of outright grants. Id. at 805-06. Indeed, 
Justice Rehnquist wrote that it was “clear that tax deduc- 
tions and exemptions, even when directed to religious 
institutions, occupy quite a different constitutional status 
under the religion clauses of the First Amendment than 
do outright grants to such institutions.” Id. at 806. 


In Roemer v. Maryland Public Works Bd., 426 U.S. 736, 
747 (1976), the plurality approved grants to private 


10 


colleges, including religious affiliated ones, of funds that 
could not be used for sectarian purposes. Again, the 
Court was clear that: 


The State may not, for example, pay for what is 
actually religious education, even though it pur- 
ports to be paying for a secular one, and even 
though it makes its aid available to secular and 
religious institutions alike. 


Subsequent decisions of this Court have not ques- 
tioned the invalidity of direct grants to sectarian schools 
for religious education. In Mueller v. Allen, 463 U.S. 388 
(1983), the Court upheld tax deductions for actual 
expenses incurred for the tuition, textbooks and transpor-— 
tation of dependents attending private sectarian as well 
as non-sectarian and public schools. In writing for the 
majority, Justice Rehnquist reiterated that the direct 
transmission of subsidies from the state to the sectarian 
schools themselves were invalidated by the Court and 
were of a different type of aid than that before the Court 
in Mueller. Id. at 399 & n. 11. 


In Witters v. Washington Department of Service for the 
Blind, 474 U.S. 481 (1986), in upholding assistance to a 
blind person at a religious college, the Court again stated 
that it is well-settled “that the State may not grant to a 
religious school, whether cash or in kind, where the effect 
of the aid is ‘that of a direct subsidy to the religious 
school’ from the State.” Id. at 487, quoting Grand Rapids 
School District v. Ball, 473 U.S. 373, 394 (1985). Indeed, the 
court noted “that aid to a religious institution unre- 


stricted in its potential uses, if properly attributable to the 


11 


State, is ‘clearly prohibited under the Establishment 
Clause’ because it may subsidize the religious functions 
of that institution.” Id. at 489 (citation omitted). Likewise, 
in upholding the provision of a sign language interpreter 
for a student at a religious school, the Court, in Zobrest v. 
Catalina Foothills School District, 509 U.S. 1, 12-13 (1993), 
emphasized that such services were valid because they 
were not direct financial support for non-public sectarian 


institutions. 


This Court’s decision in Rosenberger v. Rector & Vist- 
tors of the University of Virginia, 515 U.S. 819 (1995), 
upheld the expenditure of University money to pay an 
independent contractor to print a student religious news- 
paper. There, too, the Court “recognized the special 
Establishment Clause dangers where the government 
makes direct money payments to sectarian institutions,” 
which were not present in that case. Id. at 841, 842. Ina 
concurring opinion, Justice O’Connor emphasized that 
the Court’s decisions “provide no precedent for the use of 
public funds to finance religious activities,” and the 
Rosenberger decision “neither trumpets the supremacy of 
the neutrality principal nor signals the demise of the 
funding prohibition in Establishment Clause jurispru- 
dence.” Id. at 847, 852. Justice O’Connor pointed out that 
the focus must be on the specific features of the particular 
governmental action put before it. Id. at 852. Petitioners 
misread Rosenberger, and attempt to meld two different 
areas of First Amendment jurisprudence: the free speech 
forum cases and the public subsidy cases. Petition at 7-15. 
This Court, however, drew a clear distinction between the 
two, noting that there is a fundamental difference 
between access to a public forum and access to a public

12 


subsidy, and the decision “cannot be read as addressing 
an expenditure from a general tax fund...” 515 US. at 
841. 


Finally, this Court’s decision in Agostini v. Felton, 521 
U.S. 203 (1997), dealt with the provision of supplemental, 
remedial instruction — secular in nature — in private and 
parochial schools. The majority decision written by Jus- 
tice O’Connor reasoned that these services were supple- 
mental, and did not relieve sectarian schools of costs they 
otherwise must bear in educating their students. Id. at 
228. The Court emphasized that no public funds ever 
reached the coffers of religious schools, reiterating that 
the nature of the aid that the government chooses to 
provide remains a critical factor. Id. at 232. The peti- 
tioners’ brief is founded, in large part, upon the notion 
that the First Circuit made a fatal error misreading the 
direct versus indirect aid distinction. The argument sug- 
gests that indirect aid is always constitutional, and the 
Maine tuition voucher is indirect because the parent, not 
the government, decides where to send the child to 
school. Petition at 7-10. This theory is simply wrong. The 
First Circuit correctly relied upon this Court’s precedent 
(see, e.g., Agostini, 521 U.S. at 232), that the nature of the 
aid remains a critical factor. Pet. App. at 1la. Here, the 
aid is direct and unrestricted and for a religious education 
and covers the entire cost of the tuition for that education. 
No precedent of this Court suggests such a subsidy 
would conform with the Establishment Clause. Indeed, 
all of the Court’s statements are to the contrary. 


In sum, this Court’s cases have been unwavering in 
holding that direct, unrestricted financial subsidies to a 
pervasively sectarian school for religious education is 


eee 


13 


and remains unconstitutional. Certainly, there is no hint 
by the Court that a state is required to pay such subsidies. 
Thus, the First Circuit’s decision is in full conformance 
with this Court’s consistent holdings on the subject, and 
there is no ground for certiorari in this case. Sup.Ct.R. 
10(c). 


Ill. THE FIRST CIRCUIT DECISION DOES NOT 
CONFLICT WITH STATE DECISIONS. 


The First Circuit’s decision in Strout does not conflict 
with the three cases relied upon by petitioners at pages 
14-19 of their brief. Preliminary, as noted above, in Bagley 
v. Town of Raymond, the Maine’s highest court held this 
identical program to be in full conformance with the First 
Amendment.* 


The petitioners first refer to the Wisconsin decision in 
Jackson v. Benson, 578 N.W.2d 602 (Wis.), cert. denied, 119 
S.Ct. 466 (1998). In that case, the Wisconsin court had 
before it a program where public funds were paid to 
parents, not to the school, and the funds could only cover 
educational programs at sectarian or non-sectarian pri- 
vate schools. In a split decision, that court upheld the 
plan emphasizing that the program did not involve the 


direct flow of the subsidy from the government to the

Petitioners next turn to the Ohio decision in Simmons- 
Harris v. Goff, 711 N.E.2d 203 (Ohio, 1999). In that case, 
the Court struck down on state constitutional grounds a 
school reimbursement program involving scholarships 
paid to the student’s parents, who may use them at a 
sectarian school, noting that “[d]irect governmental sub- 
sidies to religious schools are clearly unconstitutional.” 
Id. at 209. There, the issue was whether the government 
could, not must, provide the disputed funding. The Ohio 
court’s dicta that the Establishment Clause is not violated 
by the scheme has since been repudiated, at least prelimi- 
narily, in Simmons-Harris v. Zelman, 1999 WL 668839 (N.D. 
Ohio 1999). The District Court granted a motion to pre- 
liminarily enjoin the Ohio program, concluding that the 
Ohio Supreme Court's reasoning regarding the Establish- 
ment Clause is unpersuasive and that there is “a very 
substantial chance” that the program will be found to 
violate the First Amendment. Id. at *16 & *17.5 Thus, 
again, there is no conflict between the First Circuit and 
any binding precedent out of Ohio.

As is obvious, these three cases do not involve direct 
subsidies from the government to a religious school to be 
used for religious purposes, and, indeed, each court went 
to great pains to distinguish the particular aid at issue 
from direct, financial subsidies. Moreover, they do not 
involve efforts to require the government to provide aid 
where it has chosen not to do so. Thus, there is no 
conflict, and there is no present need to address this 
issue. Sup.Ct.R. 10(a).
