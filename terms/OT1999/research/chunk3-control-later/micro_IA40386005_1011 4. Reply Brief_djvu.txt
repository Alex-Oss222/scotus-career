REPLY TO BRIEF IN OPPOSITION 


The issue in this litigation is whether the State of Maine can 
discriminatorily exclude from an educational assistance 
program, which program encompasses private schools, those 
students who attend private religious schools. The First Circuit 
held that while such governmental discrimination against 
religion would otherwise be unconstitutional, Pet. App. at 16a 
n.12, the Establishment Clause nevertheless requires such 
discrimination, id. at 15a, 16a, 19a. 


Petitioners do not contend in this case that a State is required 
to fund private schools equally with public schools. There is no 
issue before this Court of the propriety of discrimination 
between aid to public schools versus private religious (or 
nonreligious) schools. Hence, the summary affirmances which 
Respondent cites on that distinct question, Opp. at 8, are 
irrelevant. Rather, in this case, the state has chosen to provide 
financial assistance to parents for their children’s attendance at 
private schools but has categorically excluded the choice of 
private religious schools. This case, therefore, presents a 
situation of pure, de jure discrimination against religion. 


Anti-religious discrimination has a long and odious history 
in this country, culminating in the “know-nothing” movement 
of the 19" and early 20" century.' In fact, at least one noted 
historian has said that it has been the most pervasive form of

It is already settled law that the Establishment Clause 
permits neutral assistance plans in which private choice directs 
the flow of government aid. This is so even if the assistance is 
turned over to a religious school for the very purpose of 
religious training, see Witters v. Washington Dep't of Serv’s. 
for the Blind, 474 U.S. 481 (1986) (aid used for religious 
seminary training), serves as a vehicle for the implementation 
of a religious education, see Zobrest v. Catalina Foothills 
School Dist., 509 U.S. 1 (1993) (interpreter for deaf student), 
or takes place on the premises of a religious school, see 
Agostini v. Felton, 521 U.S. 203 (1997). These precedents turn 
on the crucial difference between government-directed 
schooling, on the one hand, and private parental or student 
choice of schooling, on the other. The Establishment Clause, 
which limits government action, poses no bar to the 
independent choice of private citizens to obtain, for themselves 
or their children, a religious education. Indeed, such a private 
choice enjoys constitutional protection. Pierce v. Society of 
Sisters, 268 U.S. 510 (1925); Wisconsin v. Yoder, 406 U.S. 205 
(1972).

governmental discrimination against private religious choices

, Respondent would preclude this Court’s consideration of 
Petitioner’s Free Exercise Clause claim on the grounds that the Clause 
only protects a "central religious belief or practice," Opp. at 7 (emphasis 
added). But this Court has unanimously rejected any “centrality” test for 
Free Exercise claims. Employment Division v. Smith, 494 U.S. 872, 886- 
87 (1990) (majority opinion); id. at 906-07 (O’Connor, J., concurring in 
the judgment); id. at 919 (Blackmun, J., joined by Brennan and Marshall, 
JJ., dissenting). The First Circuit, therefore, erred by invoking this 
standard and setting itself up as a judge of Roman Catholic teaching on the 
“centrality” of parochial education, Pet. App. at 18a. It is simply not 
appropriate for courts to judge the relative “centrality” of religious beliefs. 
Furthermore, the “substantial burden” test, which Respondent invokes, 
Opp. at 7, only pertains to challenges to facially neutral restrictions. See 
Hartmann v. Stone, 68 F.3d 973, 979 & n.3 (6th Cir. 1995). Explicit 
discrimination against religion -- e. g., an express ban on wearing a cross 
or 2 pendant with Hebrew lettering, but not on other jewelry -- triggers the 
highest level of scrutiny regardless of how "substantial" the burden might 
be or how “central” the practice. See Church of the Lukumi Babalu Aye 
v. City of Hialeah, 508 U.S. 520, 532-33 ( 1993).

The present case rests at the convergence of these two lines 
of precedent. Once government extends a benefit to private 
entities, the Constitution does not require, and indeed forbids, 
the discriminatory denial of that benefit to those who exercise 
their religious freedom. 


In conflict with the First Circuit’s decision below, numerous 
lower courts have held that the Establishment Clause does not 
affirmatively require the discriminatory denial of a benefit 
when used for religious purposes. See Pet. § II (citing decisions 
of supreme courts of Arizona, Ohio, and Wisconsin). Other 
lower courts, also in conflict with the decision below, have held 
that the U.S. Constitution affirmatively prohibits such 
discrimination. E.g., Peter v. Wedl, 155 F.3d 992, 994-98 (8th 
Cir. 1998) (denial of paraprofessional to assist disabled student 
attending private religious school, but not other private 
nonreligious schools, was unconstitutional discrimination 
against religion); Hartmann v. Stone, 68 F.3d 973 (6th Cir. 
1995) (discriminatory exclusion of private homes from Army’s 
privately-run child care program, on basis of caretaker’s 
religious practices like prayer before meals, was 
unconstitutional). 


This Court should grant review to resolve this conflict and 
to settle the doctrinal confusion created by the First Circuit’s 
ruling (as well as by the parallel ruling of the Maine Supreme 
Court, which is the subject of a separate petition for certiorari, 
No. 99-163). 


Respondent insistently portrays the issue as one of 
“unrestricted, direct subsidies to a pervasively sectarian school 
for a religious education.” Opp. at i; see id. at 1, 3, 5, 6, 8-15. 
But as demonstrated above, this completely mischaracterizes 
the case. The state has chosen to subsidize the parents’ choice 
of a private school in those districts where there is no public 


5 


school. Given that subsidy to parents, the question is whether 
the state can discriminatorily outlaw parental use of that 
subsidy at an otherwise eligible private school for the sole 
reason that the school the parents have chosen is religious. 


The First Circuit rested its decision entirely upon the 
proposition that allowing parents to use the Maine tuition 
assistance program to attend private religious schools would 
constitute “direct subsidies” to a “sectarian” school. Pet. App. 
9a; see id. at Sa (“direct payment of tuition”), 10a (“direct . . . 
aid”), lla (“broad sponsorship of religious schools”), 14a 
(“insurmountable impediment to the direct payments or 
subsidies”). One can interpret this holding in two possible 
ways, either of which militates in favor of review in this Court. 


First, the court below may have believed that it was 
constitutionally decisive that the grants went to pay for tuition 
and not just for some more specific program like aid for 
disabled students (as in Zobrest) or bus transportation (as in 
Everson v. Board of Education, 330 U.S. 1 (1947)). But this 
holding undeniably puts the First Circuit in square conflict with 
the lower court cases upholding tuition voucher and tuition tax 
credit programs. See Pet. § II. 


Second, the court below may have believed that it was 
constitutionally decisive that the payee on the tuition grant 
checks would be the school and not the parents, i.e., that this 
rendered the aid literally “direct.” But this notion would 
elevate form over substance, and to an absurd degree. It is the 
parents who choose a religious education for their children, not 
the state. It is thus the parents who direct the flow of funds, 
not the state. Whether the check is made out to the parents and 
then endorsed over to the school, or made out to the school in 
the first instance at the direction of the parents, is an irrelevant 
technicality. To read into the Establishment Clause a “bright

line” based on such a bureaucratic detail would be to undertake 
precisely the stilted formalism this Court has repudiated, see 
Agostini v. Felton, 521 U.S. 203 (1997).4
