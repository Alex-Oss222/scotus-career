QUESTIONS PRESENTED 


The State of Maine provides by statute that those parents 


who live in a school district without a public high school may 
send their children to the private school of their choice and that 
the State will pay the tuition for those children at that private 
school up to the statutory legal tuition rate. Maine excludes 
private religious schools from the program, however, by 
statute. See 20-A M.R.S.A. § 2951(2). The First Circuit, in 
rejecting parents’ challenge to that statutory exclusion, held 
that the Establishment Clause requires the discriminatory 
exclusion of otherwise eligible private religious schools. The 
questions presented are: 


l. 


nN 


U2 


Whether the Establishment Clause of the First Amendment 
requires the discriminatory exclusion of an otherwise 
eligible private school from a state tuitioning program just 
because that school is religious? 


Whether 20-A M.R.S.A. § 2951(2) violates the Free 
Speech and Establishment Clauses of the First Amendment 
because, rather than treating religion neutrally, it excludes 
otherwise eligible private schools from its tuitioning 
program based solely on the religious viewpoint presented 
in those schools. 


Whether 20-A M.R.S.A. § 2951(2) violates the Equal 
Protection Clause of the Fourteenth Amendment because 
the State’s refusal to pay tuition for otherwise eligible 
private sectarian education discriminates against parents 
on the basis of religion, religious beliefs, speech content 
and association. 


Whether 20-A M.R.S.A. § 2951(2) violates the Free 
Exercise Clause of the First Amendment because it 


i 


Nn 


ll 


excludes funding of otherwise eligible education solely on 
the basis of religion. 


Whether 20-A M.R.S.A. § 2951(2) violates the Fourteenth 
Amendment and the Free Speech Clause of the First 
Amendment by preventing parents from using their tuition 
allotments to expose their children to the educational 
message which best reflects their morals and values.

PROVISIONS INVOLVED IN THE CASE 


The following constitutional provisions are involved in this

statutory provision (set forth in the Appendix to the Petition) is: 
20-A M.R.S.A. § 2951(2). 


STATEMENT OF THE CASE 


Petitioners are three sets of parents who reside in Minot, 
Maine who have challenged the constitutionality of a state 
statute, 20-A M.R.S.A. § 2951(2), which precludes parents who 
send their children to sectarian schools from participating in the 
State’s private school tuitioning program. By statute, towns 
which do not operate secondary schools must pay up to the 
legal tuition rate to the public or private high schools selected 
by parents for their children. 20-A M.R.S.A. §5304(4). 
Because the Town of Minot does not have a high school, 
Petitioners chose to send their daughters to St. Dominic 
Regional High School ("St. Dominic’s"), a sectarian secondary 
school in nearby Lewiston, Maine. Because St. Dominic’s is 


2 


a religious school and therefore not a "non-sectarian" school as 
specified by the statute, the State of Maine has denied 
Petitioners the right to participate in the tuitioning program.’ 


Petitioners do not contend that the State of Maine has an 
affirmative obligation to fund private education - sectarian or 
nonsectarian. They do argue, however, that once Maine 
decides to fund private education, as it does here, it must fund 
sectarian and nonsectarian education equally and in a manner 
which is not hostile towards religion. 


A. Statement of Material Facts 


Petitioners are three sets of parents who live in a school 
district in Maine which does not have a public high school. 
Petitioners have challenged the constitutionality of a state 
statute, 20-A M.R.S.A. § 2951(2), which precludes parents who 
send their children to sectarian schools from participating in the 
State’s tuitioning program. By statute, all school boards in 
Maine must provide education for residents through the twelfth 
grade. 20-A M.R.S.A. § 1001(8). The Town of Minot, by the 
Minot School Committee, does not operate a secondary school. 


‘A challenge to 20-A M.R.S.A. § 2951(2), was also brought in 
Maine state court. Bagley v. Raymond School Dept., 728 A.2d 127 (Me. 
1999), petition for certiorari filed, - U.S.L.W. - (Jul. 22, 1999) (No. 99- 
163). The Maine Supreme Court’s opinion paralleled the First Circuit's 
opinion in Strout, holding that the discrimination against religion and the 
religious viewpoint is required by the Establishment Clause. 


In addition, in a recent decision, the Vermont Supreme Court held 
that pursuam. +) a very specific provision in the Vermont Constitution, no 
public funds could be used to finance sectarian education. Chittenden Town 
School Dist. v. Vermont Dept. of Educ., - A.2d - , 1999 WL 378244 (Vt., 
June 11, 1999). The court, however, concluded that under this Court’s 
Establishment Clause jurisprudence, it might reach a contrary result. 


3 


In order to fuifill its obligation under § 1001(8), the Committee 
pays tuition for high school aged residents to attend "a private 
school approved for tuition purposes, a public school in an 
adjoining unit which accepts tuition students, or a school 
approved for tuition purposes in another state or country" 
pursuant to 20-A M.R.S.A. § 5204(4). Pursuant to § 5204(4), 
the Committee is obligated to pay up to the legal tuition rate, 
established by 20-A M.R.S.A. § 5606, to the school which the 
parents select. Parents are responsible to pay that portion 
which exceeds the established rate. For the 1996-97 school 
year, the established rate was $4,900.00. 


At the core of this petition is 20-A M.R.S.A. § 2951(2), 
which states in pertinent part that "a private secondary school 
may be approved for the receipt of public funds for tuition 
purposes only if. . . [i]t is a nonsectarian school in accordance 
with the First Amendment of the United States Constitution." 
The only other requirements of the statute are that the school 
meet the basic approval of § 2901, namely that the school be 
incorporated, comply with the reporting and auditing 
requirements, and participate in Maine’s academic assessment 
program. 


St. Dominic Regional High School (St. Dominic’s) is a 
private, sectarian Catholic school located in Lewiston, Maine. 
Tuition for St. Dominic’s for the 1996-97 school year was 
approximately $3,850.00. St. Dominic’s is approved by the 
State of Maine Department of Education pursuant to 20-A 
M.R.S.A. § 2901 and is incorporated under the laws of the 
State of Maine. St. Dominic’s exceeds the curriculum 
requirements mandated by law. In short, St. Dominic’s 
qualified as an eligible school under Maine’s tuitioning 
program in all respects but one: because it is not "non- 
sectarian" as required by § 2951(2), it is not "approved for 


4 


tuition purposes” thereunder. Accordingly, parents who send 
their children to St. Dominic’s are ineligible to receive tuition 
reimbursement under the tuitioning scheme. 


Petitioners Elwood Strout and Deborah Strout requested 
reimbursement from School Union 29 to pay tuition for their 
daughter, Shea, to attend St. Dominic’s for the 1996-97 school 
year. School Union 29, by its Superintendent Robert E. Wail, 
denied their request, by letter dated October 10, 1996, stating, 
"(t]he law is clear that we are unable to pay tuition for St. 
Dom’s," referencing a letter from the Department of Education 
Deputy Commissioner Poulin which stated that to do so would 
be “unconstitutional and illegal." Plaintiffs’ Amended 
Complaint, Exhibit 1. The Strouts additionally sought 
reimbursement from the Department of Education by letter 
dated July 15, 1996. The Department of Education, by its 
Commissioner, Defendant-Appellee J. Duke Albanese, by letter 
dated August 20, 1996, denied their request. Plaintiffs’ 
Amended Complaint, Exhibit 2. Again, on September 23, 1996, 
the Strouts wrote to the Department of Education and 
Defendant-Appellee Albanese, seeking reimbursement, and 
again, by letter dated November 22, 1996, their request was 
denied. Plaintiffs’ Amended Complaint, Exhibit 3. The letter 
cited 20-A M.R.S.A. § 2951 as the basis for the denial of the 
request. Likewise, the Strouts’ request for tuition 
reimbursement for their daughter to attend St. Dominic’s for 
the 1997-98 year was denied. 


Petitioners John and Belinda Eulitt were orally informed by 
Superintendent Wall that tuition for their daughter, Alena, to 
attend St. Dominic’s for the 1996-97 school year would not be 
reimbursed. In light of this and with knowledge of the Strouts’ 
experience, they believed that a formal request would be futile. 


5 


Similarly, Petitioners Richard and Patricia Roy attended an 
open parents meeting in the Fall of 1997 at which time they 
were informed that tuition would not be reimbursed if they 
chose to send their daughter, Jennifer, to St. Dominic’s for the 
1997-98 school year, due to the religious nature of the school. 


If the Petitioners’ children attended an approved public or 
private non-sectarian school, outside the State of Maine or 
even outside the United States, the State of Maine would 
subsidize their education. But Maine singles out for 
discriminatory exclusion from its program those parents who 
choose to send their children to private religious school. This 
discriminatory practice manifests Maine’s hostility toward the 
religion. Petitioners seek to end this discrimination and to 
obtain funding on an equal basis with other Maine parents who 
send their children to non-sectarian private schools. 


B. Statement on Preservation Below of Federal Questions 


The federal questions presented by the Petitioners have been 
the central focus of this dispute between the parties and the 
principal grounds of the decisions below. Both the United 
States District Court for the District of Maine and the United 
States Court of Appeals for the First Circuit rejected 
Petitioners’ arguments that Maine’s discrimination against the 
religious viewpoint in public school violates: (1) the 
Establishment Clause of the First Amendment; (2) the Equal 
Protection Clause of the Fourteenth Amendment; (3) the Free 
Exercise Clause of the First Amendment; (4) the Due Process 
Clause of the Fourteenth Amendment; and (5) the Free Speech 
Clause of the First Amendment. Both lower courts held that 
the Establishment Clause of the First Amendment required 
Maine’s discrimination against religion. 


6 


REASONS FOR GRANTING THE WRIT 


The central issue in this case is one of great importance: 
whether the Establishment Clause of the First Amendment 
requires discrimination against the religious point of view in 
the provision of tuition assistance to parents of children 
attending private schools. This Court’s Establishment Clause 
jurisprudence and the highest courts of three states have 
concluded that the Establishment Clause is not offended by a 
neutral program of state assistance which allows parents to 
decide where the aid will be spent. In such a program there can 
be no argument that the state is endorsing religion or one 
religion over another. The First Circuit, by contrast, reached a 
decision in square conflict with these other cases. 


The Petitioners are parents of Maine children who seek 
review by this Court of the First Circuit’s decision which 
confused indirect aid (based on parental choice as to where to 
money will be spent) with direct aid (based on a state’s choice 
as to where money will be spent) and thereby upheld, as 
supposedly constitutionally required, a statutory program of 
discrimination against religious viewpoint. The decision below 
directly conflicts with the decisions by this Court forbidding 
such discrimination, particularly when, as here, the 
discrimination is justified by misplaced fear of an 
Establishment Clause violation. 


This Petition presents a direct conflict between the First 
Circuit and three state supreme courts. The Petition also 
presents an important and substantial issues which necessitate 
review. This Court’s should grant review.

I. THE DECISION BELOW CONFLICTS WITH 
DECISIONS OF THIS COURT 


The decision below directly contradicts this Court’s 
precedents involving the Establishment Clause in three 
respects. First, the court below misinterpreted this Court’s 
directives regarding direct and indirect funding of religious 
education. Second, the decision below conflicts with this 
Court’s numerous decisions protecting religious speech and the 
religious viewpoint from discrimination by the State. Third, 
the decision below fails to consider, and therefore contradicts, 
this Courts’s post-Nyquist jurisprudence. 


A. The Decision Below Misapplies the Concepts of 
Direct vs. Indirect Funding and Thus Conflicts With 
This Court’s Decisions. 


The First Circuit’s opinion held that any Free Speech, Equal 
Protection or Free Exercise rights of the Petitioners which are 
violated by Maine’s discriminatory funding of private 
education are trumped by the compelling state interest of the 
State of Maine to uphold the Establishment Clause of the 
United States Constitution. Strout v. Albanese, 178 F.3d 57, 64 
n.12 (1* Cir. 1999). The court based its ruling upon the 
premise that this Court’s decisions purportedly categorically 
forbid, under the Establishment Clause, any program in which 
funds are directly provided to sectarian institutions. The First 
Circuit misinterpreted directly provided to include any aid paid 
by the State to the sectarian institution, regardless of how and 
by whom the determination is made to direct the aid. 
According to the First Circuit, it makes no difference that the 
funds are paid to the sectarian school at the direction of the 
parent rather than at the direction of the State. The fact that the 


8 


private choices of parents has directed the State to pay the 
institution is of no consequence to the court below. 


This Court, however, in Agostini v. Felton, 521 U.S. 203, 
225 (1997), specifically rejected the notion "that all 
government aid that directly aids the educational function of 
religious schools is invalid." To the contrary, this Court has 
held that financial aid appropriated by the State can be 
constitutional if (1) it is neutral on its face regarding religion 
and (2) any aid to religious schools results from the private 
decisions of individuals. Witters v. Washington Dept. of 
Services for the Blind, 474 U.S. 481, 490-92 (1986) (Powell, J., 
concurring). In Witters, this Court held that the Establishment 
Clause did not constitute a bar to the issuance of vocational 
tuition to a blind person who wished to use the grant to attend 
a Christian college and obtain a religious education. The 
program was held valid because the money was made available 
neutrally, that is, without regard to whether it was going to be 
used for a sectarian or nonsectarian, public or non-public 
institution. In Agostini, the Court noted that it made no 
difference whether tuition money went to religious institutions, 
so long as that outcome came about "only as a result of the 
genuinely independent and private choices of individuals." 
Agostini, 521 U.S. at 226 (internal quotation omitted). 


Contrary to the First Circuit’s opinion, aid is not designated 
direct or indirect based on where the aid ends up but rather on 
who makes the decision as to where the aid is to be spent. If 
the parents make the choice then the aid to the sectarian 
institution is indirect and not direct. The First Circuit’s 
opinion below, therefore, rests on a fundamental 
misunderstanding of this Court’s distinction between direct 
(government-directed) and indirect (parent-directed) aid. 
Petitioners here are making the choice and not the State of 


9 


Maine; state dollars go to St. Dominic’s at the direction of the 
parents and not at the direction of the State of Maine. The 
funding is, thus, indirect.’ 


The Establishment Clause forbids the endorsement of 
religion by the State, not by private actors. Hence, this Court 
has emphasized the importance of personal private choice in 
this area of the law. In Mueller v. Allen, 463 U.S. 388 (1983), 
this Court disposed of the argument that all state aid which 
ends up in sectarian schools violates the Establishment Clause 
by noting that while state aid given to parents for use at a 
sectarian school has the same economic effect as state funds 
given directly to the sectarian institution, the parents’ choice 
renders the aid indirect and, thus, constitutionally acceptable. 
As this Court stated in Mueller: 


Where, as here, aid to parochial schools is available 
only as a result of decisions of individual parents no 
"imprimatur of state approval" . . . can be deemed to 
have been conferred on any particular religion, or on 
religion generally. 


Id. at 399 (quoting Widmar v. Vincent, 545 U.S. 263, 274 
(1981)). 


Similarly, in Zobrest v. Catalina Hills School District, 509 
U.S. 1 (1993), this Court upheld the provision of a language 
interpreter for a deaf student in a sectarian school. The Court 


"In Columbia Union College v. Clark, 159 F.3d 151, 159 (4" Cir. 
1998), cert. denied, 119 S. Ct. 2357 (1999), the Fourth Circuit identified the 
constitutional fault with an aid program as arising where "(t]he state aid at 
issue . . . reaches a religious school solely as a result of a decision ‘made by 
the state’ not the student." (Citing Witters, 474 U.S. at 488.) 


10 


upheld the Zobrest program because the payment for the 
interpreter was the result of the private decisions of individual 
parents. Moreover, because the program created "no financial 
incentive for parents to choose a sectarian school, an 
interpreter’s presence there ‘[could] not be attributed to state 
decision making.’ " Jd. at 10. 


B. The Decision Below Conflicts with this Court’s 
Numerous Decisions Protecting Religious Speech 
and the Religious Viewpoint against 
Government Discrimination. 


In a recent line of cases, this Court has expanded and 
clarified the protection for the nghts of religious speech and the 
religious viewpoint. Mueller v. Allen, 463 U.S. 388 (1983) 
(state income tax deduction for educational expenses including 
those incurred at sectarian schools); Witters v. Washington 
Dept. of Services for the Blind, 474 U.S. 481 (1986) (state 
financial assistance to blind student attending private Christian 
college); Zobrest v. Catalina Foothills Sch. Dist., 509 U.S. 1 
(1993) (sign-language interpreter provided for deaf student in 
sectarian high school); Rosenberger v. Rector and Visitors of 
the Univ. of Virginia, 515 U.S. 819 (1995) (state university 
funds used to pay printing costs of student newspaper 
espousing religious viewpoint); Agostini v. Felton, 521 U.S. 
203 (1997) (public school teachers proving remedial education 
to disadvantaged children in parochial schools). See also 
Viteritti, Joseph P. Blaine’s Wake: School Choice, The First 
Amendment, and State Constitutional Law, 21 Harv. J. L. & 
Pub. Pol’y 657, 709-716 (1998). 


Also, in its public forum analysis this Court has broken 
down the wall of "segregation" of religion and religious speech. 
Board of Educ. of Westside Comm. Schools (Dist. 66) v. 


1] 


Mergens, 496 U.S. 226 (1990); Lamb’s Chapel v. Center 
Moriches Union Free School Dist., 508 U.S. 384 (1993); 
Widmar v. Vincent, 454 U.S. 263 (1981). In each of these 
cases, this Court rejected a proffered Establishment Clause 
excuse for discriminating against religion or the religious 


viewpoint. 


In Agostini the Court relied inter alia on its decision in 
Widmar v. Vincent, 454 U.S. 263 (1981), a public forum case 
which required a state university facility to be open equally to 
religious and non-religious groups. By referencing Widmar, 
the Agostini Court applied equal access analysis to the issue of 
public aid to sectarian schools, holding that the neutrality of an 
aid program is "an important index of secular effect.” Agostini, 
521 U.S. at 231 (citing Widmar, 454 U.S. at 274). The aid 
program at issue in Agostini was upheld because it did not 
"give aid recipients any incentive to modify their religious 
beliefs or practices in order to obtain those services." /d. at 
232. 


In Widmar this Court forbade, as a violation of the First 
Amendment, discrimination against religious speech at a public 
university. The Court ruled that religious speech was entitled 
to no less protection than other kinds of speech and that once 
a forum was opened to expression, the Establishment Clause 
provided no defense for discrimination against religious 
speech. Widmar was followed by Mergens and then 
Rosenberger, in which the Court again held that the 
Establishment Clause provided no defense to a public 
university seeking to discriminate against a publication with a 
religious point of view. Much like the State of Maine, the 
University of Virginia in Rosenberger and University of 
Missouri at Kansas City in Widmar defended their exclusion of 
religious groups from participation in otherwise available 


12 


university programs, simply because a religious group has a 
religious point of view. This Court held that fear of an 
Establishment Clause violation does not justify discrimination 
against a religious point of view. It is important to note that the 
First Circuit in the case sub judice conceded that the 
constitutional rights of Petitioners have been violated but 
nevertheless held that the violation of these nghts was justified 
- indeed required - under the Establishment Clause. Strout, 
178 F.3d at 64 n.12. 


The Widmar/Mergens/Rosenberger/Agostini line of cases 
dispelled the too-common assumption that religion and those 
asserting a religious point of view could and should be 
discniminatorily excluded from government and educational 
assistance programs. As this Court re-stated in Mergens "(t]he 
Establishment Clause does not license government to treat 
religion and those who teach or practice it, simply by virtue of 
their status as such, as subversive of American ideals and 
therefore subject to unique disabilities." Mergens, 496 U.S. at 
248 (quoting McDaniel v. Paty, 435 U.S. 618, 641 (1978)) 
(Brennan, J., concurring in judgment). 


C. The Decision Below Fails to Consider, and Flatly 
Contradicts, this Court’s Post-Nyquist 
Jurisprudence, 


In Agostini, the Supreme Court discussed the development 
of Establishment Clause jurisprudence and the faulty 
assumption upon which this anti-religious discrimination had 
rested, concluding that: "[o]ur more recent cases have 
undermined the assumptions upon which [past decisions] 
relied." 521 U.S. at 222. Morever, "[w]e therefore conclude 
that our Establishment Clause law has ‘significant[ly] 


13 


chang[ed]’ since we decided [those cases]." /d. at 237 (citation 
omitted). 


The First Circuit’s decision in Strout is inconsistent with this 
Court’s more recent Establishment Clause jurisprudence, 
relying as it does on Committee for Public Education and 
Religious Liberty v. Nyquist, 413 U.S. 756 (1973), and other 
decisions predicated upon an incomplete, inaccurate, and 
outdated Establishment Clause jurisprudence. The First Circuit 
held that the Establishment Clause requires discrimination in 
funding between private sectarian and private non-sectarian 
schools. This holding contradicts the Supreme Court’s 
holdings in Agostini, Mergens, Zobrest, Witters, and Mueller. 
The denial of Petitioners’ constitutional rights cannot be 
justified by a fear of Establishment Clause violation because 
this Court’s decisions make it clear that there is no 
Establishment Clause violation when a program treats all 
students the same whether they attend sectarian or non- 
sectarian private schools and when the expenditure of public 
funds is made on the basis of parental choices rather than state 
choices. 


Commenting on the development in Establishment Clause 
jurisprudence, this Court, in Rosenberger v. Rector of the 
University of Virginia, 515 U.S. 819 (1995), noted the 
importance of neutrality: "[a] central lesson of our decisions is 
that a significant factor in upholding governmental programs in 
the face of Establishment Clause attack is their neutrality 
towards religion." Jd. at 839.

The Wisconsin, Arizona and Ohio Supreme Courts have held 
that State educational funding which ends up in sectarian 
institutions does not in and of itself create an Establishment 
Clause violation. These courts ruled that the Establishment 
Clause was not violated so long as the benefit to the sectarian 
institution was the result of a genuinely neutral program which 
preserved independent parental choice as to how the money is 
to be spent. These courts held that when the programs are 
neutral there can be no perceived endorsement of religion by 
the state. Each noted that parental choice ensures that parents, 
and not the state, will be making the decisions how the money 
will be spent. These cases also deny the applicability of 
Nyquist in light of the more recent developments in Supreme 
Court Establishment Clause jurisprudence.

parental choice. Citing this Court’s decision in Everson v. 
Board of Education, 330 U.S. 1 (1947), the Wisconsin

basis of viewpoint discrimination. See id. at 832-35, 115 
S.Ct. 2510; see also Regan v. Taxation with Representation, 
461 U.S. 540, 103 S.Ct. 1997, 76 L.Ed.2d 129 (1983) 
(holding that the state cannot discriminate based on the 
viewpoint of private persons whose speech it subsidizes). 


In answering the University's concerns that such payments 
might run afoul of the First Amendment, the Court ruled that 
the funding did not contravene the Establishment Clause 
because it was "neutral towards religion." Id. at 840, 115 
S.Ct. 2510. The neutrality of the program, the Court 
reasoned, distinguished it from a tax levied to directly support 
a church. [FN9] See id. There is a critical difference 
"between government speech [or action] endorsing religion, 
which the Establishment Clause forbids, and private speech 


l3a 


for action] endorsing religion, which the Free Speech and 
Free Exercise Clauses protect." Id. at 843, 115 S.Ct. 2510 
(emphasis in original, internal quotation marks omitted). 


FN9. "Our decision ... cannot be read as addressing an 
expenditure from a general tax fund...." Id. at 841, 
115 S.Ct. 2510. 


Most telling, from the perspective of the present case, is 
the majority's analysis of the issues presented by that appeal: 


The Court of Appeals (and the dissent) are correct to 
extract from our decisions the principle that we have 
recognized special Establishment Clause dangers 
where the government makes direct money 
payments to sectarian institutions. The error is not 
in identifying the principle, but in believing that it 
controls this case ... [T]he Court of Appeals decided 
a case that was, in essence, not before it, and the 
dissent would have us do the same. We do not 
confront a case where, even under a neutral program 
that includes nonsectarian recipients, the 
government is making direct money payments to an 
institution or group that is engaged in religious 
activity.... [T]he undisputed fact [is] that no public 
funds flow directly to [the student organization's] 
coffers. 


Id. at 842, 115 S.Ct. 2510 (emphasis added, internal citations 
omitted). 


in lauding neutrality as the keystone of Establishment 
Clause jurisprudence, plaintiff-appellants forget that 
neutrality is but one "hallmark of the Establishment Clause." 


14a 


Rosenberger, 515 U.S. at 846, 115 S.Ct. 2510 (O'Connor, J., 
concurring). Rosenberger neither trumpets the supremacy of 
the neutrality principle nor signals the demise of the funding 
prohibition in Establishment Clause jurisprudence. See id. at 
852, 115 S.Ct. 2510 (O'Connor, J., concurring). 


The historic barrier that has existed between church and 
state throughout the life of the Republic has up to the present 
acted as an insurmountable impediment to the direct 
payments or subsidies by the state to sectarian institutions, 
particularly in the context of primary and secondary schools. 
See Tilton v. Richardson, 403 U.S. at 684-87, 91 S.Ct. 2091 
(relying on the lessened danger of prohibited entanglement 
when college level institutions are involved); Hunt v. 
McNair, 413 U.S. 734, 93 S.Ct. 2868, 37 L.Ed.2d 923 (1973) 
(upholding the creation of a public agency designed to assist 
in the placement of revenue bonds for the construction of 
buildings designed for secular use at higher education 
facilities including parochial colleges). Although the 
guidance provided by the Supreme Court has been less than 
crystalline, perhaps often by necessity due to the subject 
matter involved, approving direct payments of tuition by the 
state to sectarian schools represents a quantum leap that we 
are unwilling to take. Creating such a breach in the wall 
separating the State from secular establishments [FN10] is a 
task best left for the Supreme Court to undertake. [FN11] 


FN10. "The First Amendment has erected a wall 
between church and state. That wall must be kept high 
and impregnable. We could not approve the slightest 
breach." Everson, 330 U.S. at 18, 67 S.Ct. 504. 
Nevertheless, the Court upheld the reimbursement to 
parents from public funds for the cost of fares paid by 
them for public transportation by public carriers of 


lSa 


children attending public and Catholic schools. See 
id. 


FN11. "We do not acknowledge, and we do not hold, 
that other courts should conclude our more recent 
cases have, by implication, overruled an earlier 
precedent. We reaffirm that if a precedent of this 
Court has direct application in a case, yet appears to 
rest on reasons rejected in some other line of decision, 
the Court of Appeals should follow the case which 
directly controls, leaving to this Court the prerogative 
of overruling its own decisions." Agostini, 521 U.S. 
at 238, 117 S.Ct. 1997 (citation and internal quotation 
marks omitted). 


Finally, we are at a loss to understand why plaintiff- 
appellants believe that the Establishment Clause gives them a 
basis for recovery. The Establishment Clause forbids the 
making of a law respecting the establishment of any religion. 
There is no relevant precedent for using its negative 
prohibition as a basis for extending the right of a religiously 
affiliated group to secure state subsidies. 


II. Equal Protection 


Plaintiff-appellants argue that in addition to violating the 
Establishment Clause, Maine's alleged hostility towards 
religion violates their rights under the Equal Protection 
Clause of the Fourteenth Amendment. They claim that by 
virtue of the statute in question they are being discriminated 
against on the basis of religion, religious beliefs, speech 
content, and association. 


Plaintiff-appellants' equal protection claims fail for many 


l6a 


of the same reasons that their Establishment Clause challenge 
founders. Writ simple, the state cannot be in the business of 
directly supporting religious schools. Plaintiff-appellants' 
equal protection claims fail, because, regardless of the 
appropriate level of scrutiny we employ, the state's 
compelling interest in avoiding an Establishment Clause 
violation requires that the statute exclude sectarian schools 
from the tuition program. [FN12] 


FN12. Unlike our concurring brother, we find it 
necessary to decide the Establishment Clause issue 
because we agree with the Maine Supreme Court that 
"If the State's justification [had been] based on an 
erroneous understanding of the Establishment Clause, 
its justification w{ould] not [have] withst[oo]d any 
level of scrutiny." Bagley v. Raymond School Dep't, 
728 A.2d 127 (Me.1999). 


Plaintiff-appellants allege on appeal that they "have been 
singled out simply because they desire their children to be 
educated in a Christian environment and from a biblical 
perspective, and for no other reason." Appellants’ Br. at 23- 
24. Plaintiff-appellants desire that the state pay grants 
directly to sectarian schools, in this case St. Dominic's, to 
subsidize their children's schooling. Given the present state 
of jurisprudence, we can see no way in which this can be 
done without violating the Establishment Clause. See 
Nyquist, 413 U.S. at 780, 93 S.Ct. 2955. Asa result, we find 
no Equal Protection Clause violation. 


We are fortified in our conclusion by the Maine Supreme 
Court's recent opinion in Bagley v. Raymond School 
Department, 728 A.2d 127 (Me.1999), dealing with a similar 
situation. In resolving a similar Equal Protection Clause 


17a 


challenge, the court stated: 


That state funds would flow directly into the coffers 
of religious schools in Maine were it not for the 
existing exclusion cannot be debated. If the 
religious school exclusion were eliminated, the State 
would likely pay more than $5,000 per student to 
Cheverus High School, without restriction on the 
use of those funds. In the entire history of the 
Supreme Court's struggle to interpret the 
Establishment Clause it has never concluded that 
such a direct, unrestricted financial subsidy to a 
religious school could escape the strictures of the 
Establishment Clause. While it may be possible for 
the legislature to craft a program that would allow 
parents greater flexibility in choosing private 
schools for their children, the current program could 
not be easily tailored to include religious schools 
without addressing significant problems of 
entanglement or the advancement of religion. 


Bagley, 728 A.2d at 147. 
III. Free Exercise 


Plaintiff-appellants argue that § 2951(2) violates the Free 
Exercise Clause of the First Amendment because it excludes 
funding of sectarian education solely on the basis of religion. 
However, we conclude that the Free Exercise Clause is not 
implicated here. See Sherbert v. Verner, 374 U.S. 398, 412, 
83 S.Ct. 1790, 10 L.Ed.2d 965 (1963) (Douglas, J., 
concurring) ("[T]he Free Exercise Clause is written in terms 
of what the government cannot do to the individual, not in 
terms of what the individual can exact from the 


18a 


government."). 


Four points support our conclusion. First, at least some 
of the parents in this litigation eschew any religious 
motivation for wishing to attend St. Dominic's. For them, 
academic superiority, rather than the school's Roman Catholic 
affiliation, drives their enrollment decisions. 


Second, no one plausibly reading the statute could come 
to any conclusion other than that § 2591(2) does not prevent 
attendance at a religious school. Nor does § 2591(2) prevent 
religious education of children in Maine. All it means is that 
the cost of religious education must be borne by the parents 
and not the state. 


Third, Hernandez v. Commissioner, 490 U.S. 680, 699, 
109 S.Ct. 2136, 104 L.Ed.2d 766 (1989), identifies the free 
exercise inquiry as asking "whether government has placed a 
substantial burden on the observation of a central belief or 
practice." (emphasis added). Factually speaking, education at 
a parochial school is not such a belief, for the Roman 
Catholic Church does not mandate it. See Catechism of the 
Catholic Church 537-38 (1994) (stating that parents’ 
responsibilities for religious education for religious education 
takes place primarily in the home and that choosing a 
religious school is a right but not an absolute duty). To be 
sure, the Catholic Church requires some religious education, 
see id. at 538, but as stated above, § 2591(2) does not prevent 
such religious education. Indeed, it says nothing about it. 
Moreover, no parent makes the claim that attendance at a 
religious school is a central tenet or practice of their faith. 


Fourth, the primary case cited by the parents, Church of 
the Lukumi Babalu Aye, Inc. v. City of Hialeah, 508 U.S. 


19a 


520, 113 S.Ct. 2217, 124 L.Ed.2d 472 (1993), is inapposite. 
In that case, the record showed a substantial animus against 
Santeria that motivated the law in question. See id. at 543, 
113 S.Ct. 2217 (stating that the "record in this case compels 
the conclusion that suppression of the central element of the 
Santeria worship service was the object of the ordinances."). 
No such showing has been made here. 


As we mentioned earlier, even if this were a limit on free 
exercise, the state has justified the limit by its purpose of 
avoiding violation of the Establishment Clause. 


IV. Due Process and Speech Clause Claims 


Plaintiff-appellants argue that § 2951(2) violates the Due 
Process Clause of the Fourteenth Amendment and the Speech 
Clause of the First Amendment by preventing appellants from 
using their tuition allotments to expose their children to the 
educational message which best reflects their morals and 
values. 


First, the parents argue that they have a fundamental right 
to direct the upbringing and education of their children based 
on substantive due process rights that arise from Supreme 
Court decisions. See, e.g., Pierce v. Society of Sisters, 268 
U.S. 510, 45 S.Ct. 571, 69 L.Ed. 1070 (1925). On that 
general proposition, we do not disagree. However, that 
fundamental right does not require the state to directly pay for 
a sectarian education. Here, the statute does not prevent the 
parents from doing what they wish; it only prevents the state 
from helping pay for their wishes. We reject this contention 
out of hand. 


Second, on the free speech side, plaintiff-appellants 


20a 


contend that § 2591(2) violates their nghts because it denies 
parents the right to communicate and instruct their children in 
the areas of religion, morals and ethics which they cannot 
accomplish in non-sectarian schools. With little more than 
academic articles to bolster their view, we find this claim to 
be meritless as well. 


CONCLUSION 


The judgment of the district court is affirmed. Costs are 
imposed against appellants. 


LEVIN H. CAMPBELL, Senior Circuit Judge, 
concurring. 


I agree that plaintiffs have not shown that Maine's statute 
subsidizing attendance at non-sectarian private schools, in 
communities lacking public schools, is unconstitutional. But 
I see no need for this court to decide that a broader statute 
(i.e., one also subsidizing attendance at sectarian private 
schools) would violate the establishment clause of the First 
Amendment ("Congress shall make no law respecting an 
establishment of religion"). 


For the reasons stated by my colleagues, supra, I agree 
that the Maine statute, as presently written, does not violate 
the free exercise clause of the First Amendment nor 
principles of substantive due process. 


My colleagues and I part company, however, in our 
analysis of plaintiffs’ establishment clause and equal 
protection clause claims. The Maine tuition statute was 
narrowed in 1981 to exclude religiously-affiliated schools in 
response to a decision of the Maine Attorney General 


2la 


concluding that the inclusion of religiously-affiliated schools 
in Maine's tuition program violated the establishment clause 
of the federal Constitution. See Bagley, et al. v. Raymond 
School Department, 728 A.2d 127 (Me.1999). Last month, 
the Maine Supreme Judicial Court upheld the continued 
constitutionality of the exclusion of sectarian schools, holding 
that to fund them would violate the establishment clause. My 
colleagues apparently believe, as did the Maine court, that 
since the Maine legislature excluded sectarian schools 
because of establishment clause concerns, the latter must be 
addressed by us: the Maine court, indeed, suggested that if 
allowing tuition benefits to the sectarian schools would not 
violate the establishment clause, then denying such benefits 
would violate the equal protection clause, as denial would not 
then withstand even the minimal, "rational basis," scrutiny 
under the Constitution's equal protection clause. [FN13] In 
such event, plaintiffs would have succeeded in demonstrating 
that the current statute is unconstitutional, being violative of 
the equal protection clause. 


FN13. The Maine Supreme Judicial Court actually 
believed that strict scrutiny was called for, religion 
being a fundamental right, but that even under a 
rational basis standard the exclusion of religious 
schools would violate equal protection (assuming no 
establishment clause bar) because of the irrationality 
of denying them tuition benefits if the legislature's 
sole apparent reason for denial was legally incorrect. 


While I understand the above rationale, I think it goes 
further than necessary. I prefer not to render an opinion 
based on speculation as to the constitutionality of a statute 
different from the one under discussion. Contrary to what my 
colleagues seem to suggest, supra, ours is not a case that 


22a 


involves the "imbroglio" of direct subsidies paid to sectarian 
schools. Rather, the Maine legislature, acting on the basis of 
an opinion issued by its Attorney General, has expressly 
excluded sectarian schools from the possibility of receiving 
subsidies. The question is whether the current statute is 
unconstitutional, not whether a more inclusive one would be. 


Plaintiffs argue that various constitutional provisions, 
including, oddly, the establishment clause itself, are offended 
by denying tuition benefits to sectarian schools. But none of 
plaintiffs’ theories of unconstitutionality work. The 
establishment clause certainly provides plaintiffs with no 
affirmative basis for requiring the funding of sectarian 
schools, for reasons well explained by the Maine Supreme 
Judicial Court in its recent decision, 728 A.2d at 134-35, and 
by my colleagues. 


Because plaintiffs' attempt to use the establishment clause 
as a sword fails, and because the Maine statute does not, for 
the reasons stated by my colleagues, violate the free exercise 
clause, no fundamental right is implicated here for equal 
protection purposes. Thus, the proper level of scrutiny under 
the equal protection clause is the most deferential--rational 
basis review. See Johnson v. Robison, 415 U.S. 361, 375 n. 
14, 94 S.Ct. 1160, 39 L.Ed.2d 389 (1974) (where Act did not 
violate appellee's right of free exercise of religion, court has 
no occasion to apply a standard of scrutiny stricter than 
rational basis test); see also Griffin High School v. Illinois 
High School Ass'n, 822 F.2d 671, 674 (7th Cir.1987). 


In my view, plaintiffs’ equal protection clause claim fails 
not because, as my colleagues conclude, the Maine Attorney 
General's opinion relied upon by the legislature was 
necessarily correct that including sectarian schools would or 


23a 


might violate the establishment clause, hence was better left 
alone. Rather, the action of the Maine legislature need only 
be supported by a rational basis, not one that turns out to be 
correct on the merits. Rational basis review is "a paradigm of 
judicial restraint" ... and "[w]Jhere there are 'plausible reasons' 
for [the action of the legislature], ‘our inquiry is at an end.’ " 
FCC v. Beach Communications, Inc., 508 U.S. 307, 314, 113 
S.Ct. 2096, 124 L.Ed.2d 211 (1993)(quoting United States 
R.R. Retirement Bd. v. Fritz, 449 U.S. 166, 179, 101 S.Ct. 
453, 66 L.Ed.2d 368 (1980)). See also Western & Southern 
Life Ins. Co. v. State Bd. of Equalization, 451 U.S. 648, 672, 
101 S.Ct. 2070, 68 L.Ed.2d 514 (1981) ("the Equal Protection 
Clause is satisfied if we conclude that the California 
Legislature rationally could have believed " that the measure 
promoted its objectives) (emphasis in original). 


The Maine legislature rationally could have believed that 
including sectarian schools within its funding scheme would 
or might violate the establishment clause, hence was better 
left alone. Several opinions of the United States Supreme 
Court cited by my colleagues and by the Maine Supreme 
Judicial Court undoubtedly lend support to such a viewpoint. 
See, e.g., Committee for Pub. Educ. & Religious Liberty v. 
Nyquist, 413 U.S. 756, 93 S.Ct. 2955, 37 L.Ed.2d 948 (1973). 
These precedents are not conclusive, however, and many 
commentators interpret the more recent cases as indicating 
that the Supreme Court will uphold state tuition schemes that 
provide funding to sectarian as well as non-sectarian schools. 
The fact is that establishment clause jurisprudence as applied 
to this particular area is so unclear today that no court other 
than the Supreme Court can reliably resolve whether a 
statutory scheme like Maine's would violate the establishment 
clause were it to fund sectarian as well as non-sectarian 
schools. In spite of this uncertainty, however, the Maine 


24a 


legislature's fear, based on its Attorney General's analysis, 
that including sectarian schools would violate the 
establishment clause was and remains entirely rational. 
While the odds were perhaps stronger in 1981 than they are 
today, existing case law still provides a reasonable basis for 
legislative refusal to extend tuition grants to sectarian 
schools. I see the Maine legislature, in 1981 and to this 
moment, as having rationally and prudently excluded 
sectarian schools because of a well-founded concern-- 
whether or not ultimately correct--that to do otherwise will or 
may violate the establishment clause. The existence of that 
fear is rational enough, I think, to meet equal protection (and 
free exercise) requirements, however defined. 


This is not to say that the Supreme Court may not 
someday decide that inclusion of sectarian schools in a 
scheme like this is permissible under the establishment 
clause. A strong argument can be made to that effect. Were 
the Supreme Court to so rule, plaintiffs might then be in a 
position to seek relief on free exercise or equal protection 
grounds, for under those circumstances the legislative basis 
for the exclusion of sectarian schools--the fear that the 
establishment clause bars their inclusion--will have been 
negated. But in today's climate, lacking further Supreme 
Court enlightenment, plaintiffs cannot demonstrate that the 
Maine statute violates any provision of the Constitution. 
Accordingly, I agree that we should affirm the judgment of 
the district court, but--like that court--I see no need whatever 
to reach out and decide the thorny establishment clause issue- 
-an issue that, as my colleagues also recognize, can be 
meaningfully resolved only by the Supreme Court itself.

HORNBY, Chief Judge. 


Some school districts in Maine do not have public high 
schools. Instead, they have found it more cost effective to 
pay tuition for their resident students directly to public high 
schools in other districts or private schools. Maine statutes 
permit the procedure but allow such payments to be made 
only to nonsectarian schools. The issue in this case is 
whether such a limitation is constitutional. I conclude that it 
is. 


26a 


Facts 

Maine law provides for compulsory education for school- 
aged residents. 20- A M.R.S.A. § 5001-A. Towns and 
school districts in Maine that do not operate their own public 
high schools can meet their statutory responsibilities by 
paying tuition to other public high schools or state-approved 
private schools at the legal tuition rate. 20-A M.R.S.A. § 
5204(4). Under state law, only nonsectarian schools are 
eligible for receipt of public funds for tuition purposes. 20-A 
M.R.S.A. § 2951(2). 


Each of the plaintiffs lives in Minot. The town of Minot 
is one of three towns comprising School Union No. 29, and 
neither Minot nor School Union No. 29 operates its own 
secondary school. These parents accordingly have chosen to 
send their children to St. Dominic's Regional High School, a 
private, Catholic sectarian school. After unsuccessfully 
seeking reimbursement for tuition paid to St. Dominic's in the 
fall of 1996, Mr. and Mrs. Strout, along with the other 
plaintiffs, brought this lawsuit against the Maine 
Commissioner of Education asserting that 20-A M.R.S.A. § 
2951(2) is unconstitutional. Both sides have moved for 
summary judgment and agree that there are no genuine issues 
of material fact. 


Analysis 


The general norm in Maine, as in most parts of the 
country, is for a school district to provide a secular high 
school education at a public high school financed, owned and 
operated by the school district. Such public high schools 
must be nonsectarian to comply with the Constitution if not 
with the electorate's preferences. If a state permits a school 
district to choose not to build and operate its own public high 


27a 


school, can it achieve the same objective of providing a 
secular education by allowing subsidy payments only to 
nonsectarian schools--whether they be public high schools in 
another district or private nonsectarian schools--while at the 
same time denying subsidy payments to sectarian schools? 
By all logic, the answer is yes, and the plaintiffs have 
provided no authority to the contrary. 


I am not called upon here to decide whether a state may 
make other choices-- for example, to make a payment to its 
students of high school age that they could then spend at any 
school they choose, whether sectarian or nonsectarian. The 
plaintiffs have provided caselaw that suggests the United 
States Supreme Court is not as restrictive as it once was in 
testing such programs against First Amendment restrictions. 
See, e.g., Agostini v. Felton, 521 U.S. 203, 117 S.Ct. 1997, 
138 L.Ed.2d 391 (1997); Zobrest v. Catalina Foothills Sch. 
Dist., 509 U.S. 1, 113 S.Ct. 2462, 125 L.Ed.2d 1 (1993); 
Witters v. Washington Dep't of Servs. for the Blind, 474 U.S. 
481, 106 S.Ct. 748, 88 L.Ed.2d 846 (1986). [FN1] Whether 
Maine might permit its school districts ultimately to choose 
to provide such options and whether the practice would be 
constitutional is a case for another day. I decide today only 
whether Maine is constitutionally required to extend 
subsidies to sectarian schools. I find no authority for such a 
requirement. Although the plaintiffs have phrased their 
arguments in terms of free exercise claims, establishment 
clause claims, equal protection claims and substantive due 
process claims, I find it unnecessary to address these 
separately or to analyze the various tests that have been 
enumerated. The same answer is obvious for all. [FN2] 


FN1. The Wisconsin Supreme Court has recently 
upheld a school voucher program that can be used at 


28a 


sectarian as well as nonsectarian schools. Jackson v. 
Benson, 218 Wis.2d 835, 578 N.W.2d 602, 620 
(1998). 


FN2. The state superior court has recently reached the 
same conclusion. See Bagley v. Maine Dep't of 
Education (Mills, J.), No. CV97-484 (Me.Super.Ct. 
Apr. 20, 1998). 


The plaintiffs certainly are free to send their children to a 
sectarian school. That is a right protected by the 
Constitution. Pierce v. Society of Sisters, 268 U.S. 510, 534, 
45 S.Ct. 571, 69 L.Ed. 1070 (1925). The law is clear, 
however, that they do not have the right to require taxpayers 
to subsidize that choice. That is true in a district that builds 
and operates its own public high school, Brusca v. State Bd. 
of Education, 332 F.Supp. 275, 277 (E.D.Mo.1971), aff'd 405 
U.S. 1050, 92 S.Ct. 1493, 31 L.Ed.2d 786 (1972); it is also 
true in a district that has no public high school. [FN3] 


FN3. This is not a forum case like Rosenberger v. 
Rector, 515 U.S. 819, 115 S.Ct. 2510, 132 L.Ed.2d 
700 (1995), and the analysis of that case does not 


apply. 


The defendants’ motion for summary judgment is 
GRANTED and the plaintiffs’ motion for summary judgment 
is DENIED.

what is a "sectarian" school and assert that the
