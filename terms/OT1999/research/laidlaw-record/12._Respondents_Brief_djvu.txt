QUESTIONS PRESENTED 


1. Consistent with the requirements of Article III, can 
a private citizen maintain an appeal in which the only 
aspect of the judgment being appealed concerns the amount 
of a civil penalty payable solely to the United States 
Treasury? 


2. In a case brought under the citizen suit provisions 
of Section 505 of the Clean Water Act, can the district 
court impose civil penalties payable solely to the United 
States Treasury even if it determines that the citizen- 
plaintiff is not entitled to any equitable relief, such as an 
injunction, in which it has a personal stake? 


3. Notwithstanding the Clean Water Act’s jurisdictional 
prohibition on a citizen suit if a state agency is diligently 
prosecuting compliance with a permit issued under the 
NPDES program (33 U.S.C. § 1365(b)(1)(B)), where 
the State has been active in seeking compliance, can a 
citizen-plaintiff meet the lack of diligent prosecution jur‘s- 
dictional requirement by showing that the amount of civil 
penalties previously imposed by the State was insufficient? 


4. Does a citizen-plaintiff have standing under Section 
595 of the Clean Water Act and Article III of the Con- 
stitution to seek or obtain any relief in the absence of any 
demonstrated harm to the environment when, pursuant to 
a prior agreement with a state enforcement agency, the 
defendant previously committed to make “every effort” to 
achieve compliance, and in fact achieved substantial 
compliance within two months of the filing of the citizen 
suit? 


5. Where a Clean Water Act citizen-plaintiff fails to 
obtain either a settlement, injunction, or declaratory relief 
that materially alters the legal relationship between itself 
and defendant, can it nonetheless be deemed a prevailing 
or substantially prevailing party for purposes of an award 


of attorneys’ fees?

STATEMENT OF THE CASE 


Petitioners are private parties who brought a citizen suit 
under Section 505 of the Clean Water Act (“CWA” or 
“Act”). They sought an injunction and the imposition of 
civil penaities to prevent what they alleged was continuing 
harm to themselves as a result of Respondent Laidlaw’s 
violations of an NPDES permit at a hazardous waste 
incineration facility. 


The district court rejected Petitioners’ prayer for prelimi- 
nary and permanent injunctive or other equitable relief in 
light of the lack of harm to the environment and Laidlaw’s 
persistent and successful efforts to comply with its permit. 
At Petitioners’ request, however, the district court evalu- 
ated the adequacy of civil penalties previously imposed by 
the State of South Carolina in an enforcement proceeding 
and determined that those state-imposed penalties—in 
which Petitioners had no cognizable legal interest or per- 
sonal stake—should be increased. The district court's 
determination that the penalties previously imposed by 
South Carolina were inadequate was the linchpin for its 
assertion of jurisdiction. Given the denial of any equitable 
relief and the imposition of a civil penalty payable solely 
to the United States Treasury, the district court’s judgment 
did not provide any relief that benefited Petitioners. 


On appeal, Petitioners did not challenge the district 
court’s denial of their prayer for equitable relief. Instead, 
they focused solely on the amount of civil penalties im- 
posed by the district court. With the ostensible appellants 
addressing, only the amount of fees payable to the United 
States, and not seeking any redress for themselves, the 
court of appeals dismissed the appeal and vacated the 
judgment. The Fourth Circuit correctly concluded that 
this action could not be continued on appeal by parties 
possessing no cognizable interest in the outcome. The 
court of appeals declined to resolve on the merits the 
various issues presented regarding the proper amount of 


2 


a civil penalty imposed by a state or a federal court in a 
Clean Water Act case. 


The detailed history of this case is set forth in extensive 
findings made in two opinions issued by the district court. 
Order of April 7, 1995 (JA 84-137), 890 F. Supp. 470, 
and Order of January 22, 1997 (JA 138-97), 956 F. 
Supp. 588. 

Laidlaw’s 1987 NPDES Permit 


Laidlaw’s NPDES permit for effluent discharges into 
the North Tyger River became effective in January 1987. 
With that permit, the South Carolina Department of Health 
and Environmental Control (“DHEC”)- imposed a “dra- 
matic reduction” in the mercury discharge limit from 20 
parts per billion (“ppb”) to 1.3 ppb. Laidlaw was given 
one year to achieve the new limit and directed to deter- 
mine whether it was technically possible to do so. The 
permit allowed Laidlaw to request a higher mercury limit 
upon completion of feasibility studies. JA 86-88. 


Accordingly, Laidlaw hired Environmental Technology 
Engineering, Inc. (“ETE”) to conduct a feasibility study. 
JA 88. ETE concluded that meeting the 1.3 ppb limit 
would be “an ‘extremely. difficult and complex problem’ 
inasmuch as all available manufacturer’s information and 
reliable equipment on the market demonstrated that current 
technology was capable of reducing mercury only down to 
a range of 2 ppb to 5 ppb.” JA 143. ETE recommended 
installation of a second carbon adsorption unit. Laidlaw 
installed the unit in 1988 and elected not to seek a higher 
mercury limit. JA 88. The district court found that 
Laidlaw would “have been justified in returning to DHEC 
to seek a higher limit. . . . Instead Laidlaw elected a 
more difficult and costly path. . . . In sum, all parties 
involved knew that it would be a daunting task to go from 
20 ppb to 1.3 ppb but Laidlaw elected to try.” JA 177-78. 


3 
DHEC’s 1988 Enforcement Action 


Despite the second carbon adsorption unit, Laidlaw’s 
facility failed to achieve consistent compliance with the 
1.3 ppb mercury limit. DHEC responded with an investi- 
gation, initiation of an administrative enforcement action, 
and entry of a consent order on September 7, 1988. The 
consent order required, inter alia, that Laidlaw investigate 
new treatment technologies, and imposed a $20,000 civil 
penalty. JA 88. 


To comply with the consent order, Laidlaw retained 
another firm, RMT, Inc. Under DHEC supervision, Laid- 
law and RMT tested three potential solutions. The results 
showed that the Lancy system was most likely to achieve 
permit limits for all metals, including mercury. The Lancy 
system was constructed and operational by February 
1991. JA 89. 


The new system brought Laidlaw into compiete com- 
pliance with all discharge limits for all metals other than 
mercury. JA 89. Laidlaw responded by making opera- 
tional adjustments to the Lancy system. The manufacturer 
of the system, U.S. Filter, ultimately concluded that it 
might be impossible for the Lancy system to achieve con- 
sistent discharges below the 1.3 ppb level. Therefore, on 
June 26, 1991, Laidlaw filed a request with DHEC for a 
higher mercury discharge limit. Nonetheless, through the 
remainder of 1991 and into 1992, Laidlaw continued to 
seek some method of achieving the 1.3 ppb mercury level. 
JA 89-90, 146. 


DHEC’s 1992 Enforcement Action 


After a series of abnormally high mercury excursions in 
December 1991, DHEC began considering an enforcement 
action against Laidlaw. On April 10, 1992, Petitioner 
Friends of the Earth sent a letter to Laidlaw, EPA and 


-DHEC stating an intent to file a citizen suit after sixty 


days. On May 21, 1992, DHEC served a formal enforce- 


4 


ment document on Laidlaw. On June 5 and 8, 1992, 
DHEC and Laidlaw negotiated a consent decree. JA 
90-91, 146. The consent decree, aproved by the Court 
of Common Pleas, Spartanburg County, South Carolina: 
(1) required payment of a “civil penalty” in the amount 
of $100,000 “[f]Jor all permit violations occurring up to 
the date of execution of th[e] Consent Order”; (2) re- 
quired Laidlaw to “use every effort . . . to achieve compli- 
ance with the limitation for mercury in its permit”; (3) 
subjected Laidlaw “to additional enforcement action” for 
“[flailure . . . to comply with the terms and conditions of 
th[e] Consent Order”; and (4) terminated only if and 
when Laidlaw achieved compliance with its current or any 
new permit. JA 76-83, 97. 


Pursuant to the decree, Laidlaw continued to explore 
technologies to remedy its mercury problems, and shut 
down its incinerator for substantial periods in July, August, 
September, and November 1992 “in an effort not to violate 
either its mercury parameter or the judicial consent order 
with DHEC.” JA 92. By August 1992, Laidlaw’s own 
personnel finally achieved substantial compliance with 
Laidlaw’s mercury limit. JA 92, 147, 155, 183. On 
August 6, 1993, after an extended period of permit com- 
pliance, DHEC notified the state court that Respondent 
had complied with the consent decree, which could then 
expire. JA 93. 


Petitioners’ Federal Court Complaint 


On June 12, 1992, Friends of the Earth filed this action 
in the district court under Section 505 of the Clean Water 
Act. JA 91. Petitioners complained about a variety of 
alleged violations of effluent limitations, and of monitor- 
ing and reporting requirements. JA 37-38. Petitioners 
acknowledged that DHEC had commenced a civil action 
concerning the violations alleged in the complaint, but 
asserted that action “was not diligently prosecuted” be- 
cause, inter alia, the resulting consent decree “provided

mit.” JA 29-30. The district court denied Petitioners’

The District Court’s Decision On The Merits 


After another evidentiary hearing, the district court 
issued its merits findings on January 21, 1997. The court 
found that DHEC played an extensive role in the process 
that ultimately solved the discharge problems: “DHEC 
was made aware on a continuous basis of the compliance

JA 162-63. The district court also found that the permit

The district court nonetheless assessed additional civil

SUMMARY OF ARGUMENT 


Because Petitioners’ appeal addressed only the civil 
penalty judgment in which they themselves had no Pper- 
sonal stake, their appeal could not proceed. They have no 
personal stake in an award of civil penalties that is not 
payable to them, but to the United States Treasury. Stee! 
Co. v. Citizens for a Better Environment, 118 S. Ct. 1003, 
1018 (1998). The possible deterrent effect of a judgment 
for civil penalties does not provide a private party, which 
can lay no claim to any part of those penalties, with a 
sufficient personal stake in the outcome of a case to 
sustain a federal court's jurisdiction. In the absence of 
parties on both sides of the question having a personal 
stake in the outcome of the appeal, it would have been 
improper for the court of appeals to resolve the difficult 
substantive issues concerning the proper standards to be 
used by the State or the district court in imposing a Civil 


penalty. 


Assuming that it is necessary for this Court to reach 
the issue, it is also clear that the district court could not 
award civil penalties in the first instance. Once the diStrict 
court properly denied Petitioners’ request for any equitable 
relief, it should not have imposed civil penalties. It is 
doubtful that a federal court ever has jurisdiction to enter- 
tain the claim of a private party that seeks the payment of 


9 


money not to itself but to the public treasury. At a mini- 
mum, however, the only way to assure that the court has 
some basis for jurisdiction at all stages of a citizen suit is 
for civil penalties to be awarded only as an adjunct to a 
proper and necessary injunction. 


In this case, however, no judgment was proper because 
Petitioners lacked standing from the outset. As the court 
found, there was no harm to the environment. Moreover, 
at the time of suit, Laidlaw had committed in a consent 
decree to use every effort to avoid future violations—an 
agreement that caused Laidlaw temporarily to shut down 
its facility, and under which Laidlaw acted to eliminate 
the last, nagging technical difficulty that would allow the 
facility to operate in complete compliance. This case, 
therefore, did not present the type of ongoing injury or 
ongoing violation on which a citizen suit can proceed. 
Finally, in light of the jurisdictional prohibition barring a 
citizen suit when the State is already diligently seeking 
compliance, jurisdiction was lacking here. Where the 
State actively has pursued compliance, a jurisdictional 
finding of lack of diligence cannot be based, as it was 
here, on dissatisfaction with the magnitude of the civil 
penalties previously imposed by the State. 


Assuming it is proper to reach the question here, at- 
torneys’ fees could not properly be awarded in this case 
because Petitioners did not obtain a judgment or settlement 
that provides any relief to them. Moreover, the district 
court found that Laidlaw’s compliance was prompted by 
the State agency, not the citizen suit.

ARGUMENT 


I. THE COURT OF APPEALS PROPERLY DIS- 
MISSED THE APPEAL OF A PARTY THAT HAD 
NO PERSONAL STAKE IN THE OUTCOME OF 
THE CASE 


A. The Court Of Appeals Did Not Have Jurisdiction 
Over An Appeal In Which Appellants Only Were 
Pursuing Relief In Which They Had No Personal 
Stake 


Petitioners presented an appeal to the Fourth Circuit 
starkly inconsistent with Article III's case-or-controversy 
requirement. On appeal, Petitioners abandoned their ear- 
lier claim for an injunction—a remedy in which they 
would, in theory, have a personal stake because it would 
bar the defendant from engaging in conduct that would 
cause Petitioners harm. Instead, Petitioners’ appeal was 
concerned solely with the amount of the civil penalty that 
the district court ordered payable to the United States and 
to which Petitioners could lay no claim. A citizen-plaintiff 
has no cognizable legal interest in, and thus no standing 
to prosecute, an action seeking the exaction of a civil 
penalty payable solely to the public treasury. See Steel 
Co., 118 S. Ct. 1003.4 


“Standing” is a fundamental part of the “case-or-con- 
troversy” requirement that circumscribes the jurisdiction 
of Article III courts. Standing has been divided into three 
elements: a plaintiff must have suffered, or in an equity 
action be threatened by, some concrete injury; the injury 
must be caused by defendant's conduct; the injury must 
be redressable by the court. See Steel Co., 118 S. Ct. at 


4 Steel Co. was decided during the pendency of the appeal, and 
plainly applies to citizen suits brought under the Clean Water Act. 
A civil penalty payable to the United States Treasury.can no more 
provide a personal benefit to the citizen-plaintiff under the Clean 
Water Act than it could under the Emergency Planning and Com- 
munity Right-To-Know Act of 1986 (“EPCRA”), 42 U.S.C. §§ 11001 
et seq., at issue in Steel Co. 


11 


1016-17. The basic requirement that a lawsuit in the 
federal courts can proceed only when it offers the plain- 
tiff an opportunity to obtain concrete relief reflects the 
traditional, limited role of the federal courts within our 
constitutional structure. 


Where a case involves past injury, the issue of redress- 
ability ordinarily presents little difficulty. If monetary 
relief is available in the form of damages, the award of 
such relief to the plaintiff will redress the injury by pro- 
viding plaintiff with compensation. When the case involves 
only the threat of future harm, the issue of redress tucns 
on the availability of some form of equitable relief that 
would bar the defendant from engaging in wrongful con- 
duct that otherwise would harm the plaintiff. 


Civil penalties that are not payable to the citizen- 
plaintiff do not redress its injuries or provide it with a 
personal stake in the outcome of the case sufficient to

C. Petitioners Did Not Obtain A Declaratory Judg-

Preserve Appellate Jurisdiction

Petitioners’ assertion is without basis in the record and 
without support in the law. 


No “declaratory judgment as to liability” was ever 
issued pursuant to 28 U.S.C. $$ 2201, 2202. Petitioners 
sought “both a declaratory judgment and injunctive relief” 
(JA 182), but the district court declined to grant either, 
explaining “the fact that Laidlaw is now and has for an 
extended period of time been in compliance with its permit

of equitable relief is appropriate. Accordingly, plaintiffs’ 
request for equitable relief is denied.” JA 183 (emphasis 
added). 


What Petitioners call a “declaration of liability” is the 
district court's finding of liability in imposing a civil pen- 
alty. This Court has held, however, that the incidental 
pronouncement of a court on a matter of law or fact is 
not equivalent to a declaratory judgment. To “suggest 
such an equivalency is to lose sight of the nature of the 
judicial process.” Hewitt v. Helms, 482 U.S. 755, 761 
(1987); see also id. at 762 (“[{T]here is a very practical 
objection to equating statements of law (even legal hold- 


ings en route to final judgment for the defendant) with 
declaratory judgments.” ). 


18 


Moreover, there is a fundamental difference between a 
“declaration of [past] liability” and the type of declaratory 
judgment, prospective in nature, that actually will fore- 
stall a threatened injury and thus provide some benefit to 
the plaintiff. For example, a declaration that a law is 
unconstitutional or a regulation invalid is presumed to 
provide redress as effective as an injunction because of its 
prospective effect. The government is expected to refrain 
from enforcing a law that a court has declared unlawful. 
Similarly, private parties whe seek a declaration about the 
meaning of a contract or title to property may have stand- 
ing to seek that declaration because it will control their 
future behavior. The effect of a judicially enforceable 
declaratory judgment on the parties’ ongoing relationship 
provides concrete relief to the plaintiff by materially alter- 
ing the legal relationship of the parties. Jd. at 761. 


In contrast, a “judicial statement that does not affect 
the relationship between the plaintiff and the defendant is 
not . . . equivalent [to a declaratory judgment].” /d. 
(emphasis in original). A “declaration of liability,” with- 
out more, is inherently retrospective. Likewise, an award 
of civil penalties for past wrongs will not preclude future 
wrongs. Petitioners fail to grasp the distinction between a 
civil penalty ruling concerning only past liability, which 
benefits them not at all, and the grant of some form of 
equitable relief that will protect them from future harm, 
which the district court found unnecessary.

A. A Private Party May Not Pursue Civil Penalties 
Payable Solely To The Public Treasury 


Under this Court’s decisions, it is doubtful that a private 
party can ever be awarded a judgment requiring payment 
of a civil penalty solely to the United States Treasury. 
This limitation on the remedies a private party may obtain 
exists whether or not the federal court has jurisdiction 
over some other claim presented by that private party. 


Article III is not simply a statement of jurisdiction. 
Rather, it is a description of the federal judicial power. 
It is inconsistent with the proper, historic limitations on 
judicial power to allow it to be exercised—i.e., to dis- 
pense punishment—on behalf of someone who has no 
legally cognizable interest in the remedy. 


30 


The federal court’s remedial power may only be exer- 
cised on behalf of a party who has standing to seek the 
remedy in question. Thus, in Lyons, this Court held that 
there was no jurisdiction over a party’s request for a 
broad injunction that, while possibly benefiting others, was 
unlikely to prevent any future harm to the plaintiff him- 
self. 461 U.S. 95. The fact that the plaintiff was pre- 
viously the victim of such harm did not provide standing 
to pursue equitable relief. 


This Court addressed a similar issue in Lewis v. Casey, 
518 U.S. 343 (1996). The Court found jurisdiction 
over certain claims of the prisoner plaintiffs. But this 
did not provide the district court with the authority to 
grant equitable relief throughout the prison system or to 
remedy harms quite unlike those suffered by the prisoner- 
plaintiffs. As the Court pointed out, “standing is not 
dispensed in gross. If the right to complain of one ad- 
ministrative deficiency automatically conferred the right to 
complain of all administrative deficiencies, any citizen 
aggrieved in one respect could bring the whole structure 
of state administration before the courts for review.” 518 
U.S. at 358-59 n.6. Whether or not a federal court has 
jurisdiction over some claim (as in Lyons, for an award 
of damages), plaintiffs may only obtain relief that benefits 
themselves. 


This Court’s historic concern that issues can properly 
be presented only by a party with a direct stake in the 
resolution of those issues is just as important in the con- 
text of a specific claim for relief as it is in connection 
with the initial filing of a suit. Indeed, the danger of 
allowing a party to seek relief that does not benefit that 
party is well illustrated by this case. Petitioners are not 
suitable advocates to advance the claim of the United 
States or South Carolina for the imposition of civil penal- 
ties. As this Court noted in Gwaltney, allowing a private

prerequisite for the fee award to a “prevailing” party. /d. 
at 111 (quoting Texas State Teachers Ass'n v. Garland 
Indep. School Dist., 489 U.S. 782, 792-93 (1989)). 
The Court held that “{[no] material alteration of the legal 
relationship between the parties occurs until the plaintiff 
becomes entitled to enforce a judgment, consent decree, 
or settlement against the defendant.” 506 U.S. at 113. 
The Court explained that 

to qualify as a prevailing party, a civil rights plaintiff 
must obtain at least some relief on the merits of his 
claim. The plaintiff must obtain an enforceable 
judgment against the defendant from whom fees are 
sought, or comparable relief through a consent decree 
or settlement. Whatever relief the plaintiff secures 
must directly benefit him at the time of the judgment 
or settlement. Otherwise, the judgment or settlement 
cannot be said to “affec[t] the behavior of the de- 
fendant toward the plaintiff.” 


506 U.S. at 111 (emphasis added) (internal citations 
omitted). Farrar directs the result reached by the court 
below. Farrar necessarily supersedes any earlier sugges- 
tion that the informal effect of a lawsuit on defendant's 
behavior may render a plaintiff a prevailing party. 


Only a party that has achieved some measure of en- 
forceable relief “prevails” in the lawsuit. Petitioners’ own 
discomfort with a defendant’s proclamation that it has 
voluntarily ceased violating the law highlights the very 
significant difference between voluntarily ceasing conduct

on the one hand, and an enforceable judgment or settle- 
ment agreement on the other. 


With Farrar as the guiding standard, this case illustrates 
inherent difficulties in trying to apply a “catalyst theory.’ 
Before this lawsuit was filed, Laidlaw committed itself to 
make “every effort” to eliminate any violations. JA 82. 
To the extent that any outside pressure was necessary, 
the State was applying that pressure, playing an active 
role in forcing compliance. Yet, under Petitioners’ theory, 
one can easily imagine a trial devoted to determining 
whether Petitioners or the State or Laidlaw’s consultants, 
or Laidlaw itself (which ultimately found the technical 
solution), was responsible for resolving | the mercury 
problem that earlier challenged the facility. Congress 
could not have wanted to encourage satellite litigation 
over fees where the basic issue of liability for fees would 
be so difficult to determine. See Hensley v. Eckerhart, 
461 U.S. 424, 437 (1983) (“A request for attorney's 
fees should not result in a second major litigation.”). 


Moreover, even assuming arguendo that the catalyst 
theory of prevailing party status has vitality, this case 
would take that theory farther than it could properly go, 
even by its own terms. To prevail under this theory, the 
June 12, 1992 citizen suit would have to have been re- 
sponsible for bringing Laidlaw into compliance with its 
NPDES permit. But the two opinions of the district court 
make clear that the citizen suit had nothing to do with 
Laidlaw’s compliance. 


This case does not involve an NPDES permit holder 
and state environmental agency galvanized into action by 
the filing of a citizen suit. The district court’s two deci- 
sions chronicle the nearly six years of continuous,’ pre- 
citizen suit activity by Laidlaw and DHEC to solve the 
mercury discharge problem. This included, among wre 
things, the commencement of an enforcement action Zs 
subsequent entry of a consent order in 1988 that led to

continual experimentation with new technology that ulti-
