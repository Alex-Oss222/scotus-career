INTRODUCTION 


Plaintiffs brought this action under the Clean Water Act, 33 
U.S.C. 1251, et seqg.. to enforce the National Pollutant 
Discharge Elimination System (NPDES) permit issued to 
respondent Laidlaw Environmental Services (TOC), Inc. 
("Laidlaw"). The district court found that Laidlaw had violated 
the mercury limitation in its permit 489 times. Friends of the 
Earth v. Laidlaw Envtl. Services (TOC), Inc., 956 F. Supp. 610, 
613-621 (D.S.C. 1997)(J.A.158, 185-196). The district court 
denied plaintiffs’ request for injunctive relief due to the fact 
that Laidlaw had achieved substantial compliance with the 
permit, but imposed a civil penalty to deter Laidlaw from future 
violations. /d. at 610-611 (J.A. 181-183). 

Because injunctive relief was denied and the denial was not 
appealed by plaintiffs, the Court of Appeals for the Fourth 
Circuit vacated and remanded with instructions to dismiss on 
the ground that the case was moot based on this Court's 
decision in Steel Co. v. Citizens for Better Env't, 118 S. Ct. 
1003 (1998). The court of appeals reasoned that "[b]ecause 
Plaintiffs have not appealed the denial of declaratory and 
injunctive relief, the only potential relief that may be available 
to redress their claimed injuries is the civil penalty imposed 
upon Laidlaw" and "[s)uch penalties * * * cannot redress any 
injury suffered by a citizen plaintiff." Friends of the Earth v. 
Laidlaw Envil. Services (TOC), Inc., 149 F.3d 303, 306 (Pet. 
App. 7a). 

On March |, 1999, this Court granted certiorari on the 
foiiowing questions (Br. for Pet., p. i): 


1. Whether a citizen suit seeking civil penalties under 
Section 505 of the Clean Water Act is constitutionally 
moot under Stee! Co. v. Citizens for Better Environment, 
118 S. Ct. 1003 (1998), due to lack of redressability, 
where plaintiffs had standing at the time of the complaint 
and have shown continuing injury-in-fact but have not 
obtained injunctive relief. 


2 


2. Whether a citizen suit seeking civil penalties under 
Section 505 of the Clean Water Act is constitutionally 
moot under Stee/ Co., due to lack of redressability, when 
the district court has rendered a declaratory judgment as 
to liability and the issue of liability was contested. 


3. Whether plaintiffs -ould not be awarded attorneys’ 
fees or litigation costs because the case was dismissed for 
mootness, even if the litigation was responsible for 
bringing the defendant into compliance with the Clean 
Water Act. 


Pursuant to Rule 21 of the Rules of this Court, Laidlaw now 
files a suggestion of mootness based on its claim that it has 
closed its facility. Suggestion of Mootness, p. 1. Laidlaw 
claims that closure of the facility raises an issue of mootness in 
addition to the issue of mootness already before the Court. 
Ibid Laidlaw also claims that plaintiffs raised this new issue 
of mootness in the Brief for Petitioners. /bid 

In Part I below, we show that these are not new facts. They 
were argued by the parties and amici in the briefing on the 
Petition for a Writ of Certiorari. In Part Il, we show that a 
Suggestion of Mootness in a case already held to be moot, 
albeit on alternative grounds, is unwarranted. In Part III, we 
show that the closure of the facility does not moot the case 
because the civil penalty continues to deter future violation of 
the Act by Laidlaw, as well as any successor to the permit. In 
Part IV, we show that even if the merits of the case are 
determined to be moot, the attorneys’ fees issue is indisputably 
not moot.

ARGUMENT 


I. THE CLOSURE OF THE FACILITY WAS BRIEFED 
BY THE PARTIES BEFORE THE PETITION WAS 
GRANTED 


The purpose of a suggestion of mootness is to raise for the 
first time an issue of mootness. It is not an opportunity to raise 
an issue of mootness previously presented to the Court. In its 
Suggestion of Mootness, Laidlaw suggests that the case is moot 
because it closed or shut down its incinerator. Yet, both it and 
the amici curiae supporting it raised exactly this point in 
Opposition to the Petition for a Writ of Certiorari. See Resp. 
Br. in Opp., p. 1, n.1 and p. 10; Br. of Amici Curiae South 
Carolina Chamber of Commerce, et al. in Support of Resp., p. 
7. Plaintiffs responded at length in their Reply Brief (pp. 4-7) 
showing how the closure of the facility did not moot the case. 

Thus, the issue that Laidlaw now presents in its Suggestion 
of Mootness was previously presented to this Court and this 
Court decided to issue a writ of certiorari. Since this Court has 
already determined that the closure of the facility was not a bar 
to the granting of certiorari, there is no basis for revisiting this 
issue.+ 


1 In his concurring opinion in Honig v. Doe, 484 U.S. 305, 331-332 (1988), 
Chief Justice Rehnquist urged adoption of an exception to the mootness 
doctrine for cases “where the events which render the case moot have 
supervened since our grant of certiorari or noting of probable jurisdiction 
in the case.” Here, the events which Laidlaw argues render the case moot 
did not supervene the grant of certiorari. Instead, the events occurred 
before the petition was filed and were addressed by the parties during the 
briefing on the petition. The circumstances here present an even stronger 
argument for this Court preceding to the merits than the exception urged by 
Chief Justice Rehnquist.

Il. A SUGGESTION OF MOOTNESS IN A CASE 
ALREADY ADJUDGED TO BE MOOT IS 
UNWARRANTED 


The court of appeals determined that this case was moot. 
149 F.3d at 306-307 (Pet. App. 8a). Two of the three questions 
upon which this Court granted certiorari involve mootness. See 
Br. for Pet., p. i. Since the case has already been adjudged to 
be moot and since this Court will be addressing mootness 
pursuant to its grant of certiorari, there is no basis for a 
suggestion of mootness. A suggestion of mootness is for cases 
not previously adjudged to be moot where the circumstances 
have changed so as to create an issue of mootness during the 
pendency of the case before this Court. 

That is not the situation here. Not only was the case 
previously adjudged to be moot, but the circumstances have not 
changed. As discussed above (p. 3), Laidlaw and its supporting 
amici raised this issue at the petition stage. Indeed, if 
Laidlaw’s argument had merit, it could have raised the closure 
of the facility before the court of appeals since it claims that its 
decision to close the facility was made by June 2, 1997 
(Suggestion of Mootness, p. 7), over a year before the court of 
appeals issued its decision on July 16, 1998. 

Since the case has already been adjudged to be moot, the 
Suggestion of Mootness merely raises an alternative ground for 
the court of appeals’ finding of mootness. It is not a basis upon 
which this Court must decline to reach the mootness issues 
already presented by the Petition. 

Moreover, the basis for mootness presented in the 
Suggestion of Mootness is closely related to the mootness 
issues presented by the Petition. Laidlaw’s Suggestion of 
Mootness is premised on the fact that civil penalty relief is 
moot when "‘there is no reasonable expectation that the wrong 
will be repeated." Suggestion of Mootness, p. 3 (quoting 

Gwaltney of Smithfield, Ltd. v. Chesapeake Bay Found., 484 


5 


U.S. 49, 66 (1987), which in turn was quoting United States v. 
W.T. Grant Co., 345 U.S. 629, 633 (1953)). This is the test for 
when injunctive relief is moot. See, e.g. United States v. 
Concentrated Phosphate Export Ass'n, 393 U.S. 199, 203 
(1968); W.T. Grant, 345 U.S. at 633. However, this Court has 
not determined when civil penalty relief becomes moot. That 
is precisely the issue presented by this case. 

In order to resolve both the merits of the mootness issues 
presented by the Petition and that presented in the Suggestion 
of Mootness, this Court must determine when civil penalty 
relief becomes moot in a citizen suit. More specifically, as 
discussed more fully below, this Court must determine the legal 
standard to be applied in ascertaining when a civil penalty 
payable to the United States Treasury provides a deterrent 
against future violations so as to satisfy the requirement of 
redressability for purposes of mootness. Thus, this Court’s 
decision as to the issues on which the writ of certiorari was 
granted will significantly affect the resolution of the issue 
Laidlaw raises in its Suggestion of Mootness. 


Ill. IN SPITE OF THE CLOSURE, LAIDLAW ITSELF, 
AS WELL AS ANY SUCCESSOR TO ITS PERMIT, 
WILL BE DETERRED FROM VIOLATING THE 
CLEAN WATER ACT 


In Steel Co., this Court held that civil penalties did not 
satisfy redressability in a case where the defendant was not 
violating the statute at the time of the complaint and there was 
no allegation in the complaint of continuing violation. 118 
S.Ct. at 1017-1018. In analyzing the redressability issue 
relating to civil penalties, the Court emphasized that the 
penalties were paid to the United States Treasury rather than 
the plaintiff. /d at 1018. This analysis suggests that general 
deterrence, the effect of the imposition of a civil penalty on 
other dischargers, does not support redressability. See also id 
at 1019. However, in view of this Court's emphasis on the fact 


6 


that Steel Co. involved only initial standing and not mootness 
after initial standing had been shown (id. at 1019-1020), it is 
not clear whether this determination affects the present case. 
Assuming arguendo that Steel Co. establishes that general 
deterrence does not satisfy the requirement of redressability for 
purposes of mootness, the imposition of a civil penalty in this 
case provides specific deterrence. 

In determining whether the penalty will deter future 
violations, the focus must be on Laidlaw itself, not its facility. 
Analysis of the specific deterrent value of a speeding ticket 
focuses on the driver, not on his vehicle. A speeder who has 
suffered the consequences of a speeding ticket is deterred from 
speeding regardless whether he sells or no longer drives the 
vehicle in which he was speeding. He is deterred when he 
drives another of his vehicles or when he buys and drives anew 
vehicle. Thus, the issue here is whether the civil penalty will 
serve to deter Laidlaw from violating the Clean Water Act in 
the future. 

Even though Laidlaw has closed its facility, it continues to 
be deterred by the penalty imposed by the district court in 
several ways. 

First, although Laidlaw has closed the facility and 
dismantled the incinerator, Laidlaw continues to hold its 
NPDES permit. It has not taken any action to terminate the 
NPDES permit. Indeed, Laidlaw admits that the permit is still 
active. Suggestion of Mootness, pp. 4-5 and App. B, p. 6a. 
Since its permit is still in effect, it has the right to discharge in 
the future whenever it cares to do so. 

Laidlaw argues that this is not the case because it has sold or 
discarded its equipment. Suggestion of Mootness, p. 4. The 
sale or disposal of its equipment does not eliminate the 
possibility that Laidlaw could discharge in the future. By 
keeping its NPDES permit active, Laidlaw is keeping its 


7 


options open.* Laidlaw could assemble other equipment and 
reconstitute its operation. See Government of the Virgin 
Islands v. Virgin Islands Paving, Inc., 714 F.2d 283, 284-285 
(3d Cir. 1983)(no mootness where defendants had moved the 
equipment out of the Virgin Islands and had ceased the 
offending operation because the defendants had not surrendered 
the lease to the property and could resume the challenged 
operations). The fact that Laidlaw may have to obtain permits 
and other government approvals before resuming operations on 
the site does not mean that it would not be "free to return to 
[its] old ways."* Concentrated Phosphate, 393 U.S. at 203. It 
just may take a few more steps to get there. 

Second, even if the permit is transferred to a purchaser, 
Laidlaw’s payment of a civil penalty would serve to deter 
violations by the purchaser. Unlike a general deterrent as to 
dischargers not connected to this permit, a purchaser would be 
discharging under the permit which Laidlaw was penalized for 
violating. Laidlaw’s successor, knowing that Laidlaw had been 
penalized for violating the permit it now holds, would be 
specifically deterred from repeating the same kinds of 
violations that caused Laidlaw to be penalized.

Third, even if Laidlaw never revived its operation at this 
location, it has at least has at least 16 other NPDES permitted 
facilities. Friends of the Earth and Sierra Club have members 
who use the waters affected by Laidlaw/Safety-Kleen’s* 
discharges from some of these facilities and their use and 
enjoyment of these waters is adversely affected by illegal 
discharges from these facilities. See Reply Br. in Support of 
Pet. for Writ of Cert., App. 33a. The issuance of civil penalties 
in this case would deter Laidlaw from violating the statute at 
these other facilities. 

Laidlaw claims in its Suggestion of Mootness that it has no 
other facilities and that plaintiffs have not cited any authority 
for claiming that civil penalties imposed on one company 
might have a deterrent effect on another company. Suggestion 
of Mootness, p. 5. Laidlaw’s argument is disingenuous. While 
the particular Laidlaw subsidiary that owns the South Carolina 
facility may not presently own any other facility, its parent 
corporation and sibling subsidiary corporations, all of which 
are wholly owned by the parent corporation (Resp. Br. in Opp.. 
p. ii), own the other facilities. Since a civil penalty paid by a 
wholly-owned subsidiary reduces the profits of the parent 
company, a civil penalty paid by a wholly-owned subsidiary 
constitutes a specific deterrent for the parent and all sibling 
subsidiary corporations. 

An appeal must be dismissed as moot when an intervening 
event makes it impossible for the court to grant "‘any effectual 
relief whatever’ in favor of the appellant." Calderon v. Moore, 
116S. Ct. 2066, 2067 (1996)(quoting Church of Scientology of 
California v. United States, 506 U.S. 9, 13 (1992)). The 
remedy does not need to be "‘fully satisfactory’" to avoid 
mootness. /bid. The relief still at stake here, a substantial civil 
penalty, is certainly effectual relief. Since the relief here 
provides specific deterrence against the possibility of future

violations, this case is not moot. 
IV. THE ATTORNEYS’ FEES ISSUE IS NOT MOOT 


In its Suggestion of Mootness, Laidlaw fa!s even to mention 
the attorneys’ fees issue upon which this Court also granted 
certiorari. That issue is not affected in any way by closure of 
the facility. 

When it vacated the district court’s order and remanded with 
instructions to dismiss, the court of appeals decided that 
"[pJlaintiffs’ failure to obtain relief on the merits of their claims 
precludes any recovery of attorneys’ fees or other litigation 
costs because such an award is available only to a ‘prevailing 
or substantially prevailing party.’" 149 F.3d at 307, n. 5 (Pet. 
App. 9a, n. 5). This Court granted certiorari on the issue of 
"(whether plaintiffs could not be awarded attorneys’ fees or 
litigation costs because the case was dismissed for mootness, 
even if the litigation was responsible for bringing the defendant 
into compliance with the Clean Water Act." Br. for Pet., p. i. 

If this Court holds that the case is moot, either for the reason 
decided by the court of appeals or the reason set forth in 
Laidlaw’s Suggestion of Mootness, the attorneys’ fees issue is 
squarely presented.* Laidlaw does not, and could not, argue 


> Plaintiffs have argued that the attorneys’ fees issue should be resolved 
even if this Court reverses the court of appeals’ ruling on mootness and 
holds the case not moot. Br. for Pet. 50, n. 28. 

An order vacating the court of appeals’ mootness decision will not 
eliminate the precedential value of the fees ruling since it is based on an 
earlier similar ruling by the court of appeals. See Br. for Pet., p. 43. 
Leaving this ruling in place when all other circuits have upheld the catalyst 
theory -- except the Eleventh and Federal Circuits which have not addressed 
the issue -- creates a situation where the Clean Water Act will not be 
uniformly enforced nationwide. Asa result, enforcement of the Act, which 
heavily depends on citizen enforcement (see id, pp. 35-38), would be 
substantially reduced in the Fourth Circuit. This, in turn, undermines 
Congress’ intent that the Act be uniformly enforced to prevent forum 
shopping by dischargers seeking to avoid the requirements of the Act. See

the contrary. For the reasons set forth in the Brief for 
Petitioners (pp. 42-50), the court of appeals’ ruling on the fees 
issue must be reversed.
