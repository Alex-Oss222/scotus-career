The preconduct authorities receive their actual weight:

- **DeShaney** rejects a general duty to supply protection from private violence and distinguishes state-created or increased vulnerability without supplying a complete rule for every proposed affirmative-danger theory. **Daniels** excludes negligence; its reserved culpability questions do not establish the present right. The simulated **Collins** (February 26, 1992) rejects the general workplace-safety duty while reserving a different affirmative-injury claim.
- **Ross v. United States**, 910 F.2d 1422 (1990), is affirmative support for a particular established interference rule. Its favorable allegations involved a known drowning child's immediately available qualified rescue, threats and physical obstruction, appreciated imminent mortal danger, no meaningful alternative and an assumed life-saving causal opportunity. Its denial of personal immunity is not ignored merely because it is another circuit's decision. But eliminating that rescue differs materially from directing one's own patrol unit not to begin a discretionary investigation of a third party before contact with later road users.
- **Salas v. Carpenter**, 980 F.2d 299 (1992), recognizes the relevance of rescue obstruction but sustains ongoing replacement protection despite its inferiority and failure. Its independent August 1989 immunity ground cannot simply be reused as a June 1995 answer. For this case, the absence of replacement action is the plaintiffs' strongest distinction; the different protection allegedly eliminated and absence of a rescue situation already confronting a person in known mortal peril remain material limits.
- **Piotrowski v. City of Houston**, 51 F.3d 512 (April 21, 1995), is relevant preconduct law. Its discussion of blocking a specific warning about a planned murder and furnishing conspirators a photograph identifies a possible affirmative-deprivation theory, with causal creation of an otherwise absent opportunity and sufficiently culpable conduct. That discussion is not discarded as dicta. But it expressly reserves adoption of the theory and even the pleading sufficiency of those allegations; it makes no personal-immunity determination. The actual disposition concerns missing municipal-policy linkage and permits amendment. Its acknowledgment that the circuit had not adopted the theory is evidence about the legal landscape, not an automatic immunity rule.
- **Harlow**, **Anderson** and the simulated **Elder** (February 23, 1994) require objective, particularized review of all relevant preconduct law. General condemnation of arbitrary official action does not by itself establish every attribution mechanism. Neither adverse outcomes in cited cases nor judicial disagreement automatically proves immunity.

## Competing current-merits and county positions

**Nonprotection position — O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy and Thomas.** Affirm the county judgment as well. On this supported account, directing a subordinate not to initiate the patrol unit's investigation remains a decision not to supply public protection against a private driver. It does not restrain a victim, direct her into danger, remove private protection, enlist the driver in a governmental act, or obstruct an already available rescue confronting an endangered person. Calling the instruction affirmative does not supply the missing deprivation. Its alleged callous motive and foreseeable consequences do not create a general constitutional enforcement duty. This sufficient ground does not adopt the lower court's universal known-victim prerequisite or extend Lewis's rapid-pursuit harmful-purpose rule to every vehicle-related injury. Without the asserted underlying federal wrong, this derivative county claim fails; policy attribution need not be decided.

**Affirmative-interference position — Stevens, Souter, Ginsburg and Breyer.** Vacate the county judgment and remand under ordinary summary-judgment procedures. They would hold that noncustody and lack of advance identification of the eventual victims do not categorically defeat a claim that an officer knowingly and arbitrarily used coercive authority to eliminate an actually available protective intervention against an appreciated serious and immediate danger, thereby causing increased exposure. The constitutional inquiry requires the officer's own attributable deprivation and sufficiently culpable, arbitrary or conscience-shocking conduct in the actual setting. Negligence, ordinary supervisory disagreement, a reasonable protective response that fails, and foreseeable harm without affirmative causal displacement remain insufficient.

For these four, the proposed public intervention's origin does not alone make its deliberate suppression incapable of being an official deprivation. The supported command, opportunity for deliberation and asserted personal rather than protective reason permit the theory to be examined rather than rejected through the lower court's categorical nonprotection and known-victim grounds. The favorable account does not establish every element: actual intervention availability, what investigation could lawfully have accomplished, causal removal of a protective opportunity, actual awareness and constitutional culpability remain to be evaluated on the existing supported record at the proper procedural stage. No universal fifteen-minute rule or automatic deliberate-indifference standard follows from Lewis. County policy/custom, attribution and causation remain independently required under Monell and Collins; allegations of tolerated misconduct and superior rank alone do not establish them. The remand does not guarantee discovery, trial or recovery and may permit another properly supported summary-judgment ground. It does not revive the individual claim that personal immunity resolves.

These are different merits judgments. Their agreement about a general possibility of noncustodial official wrongdoing is not a combined merits holding or a logical-subset rule disposing of this county claim.
