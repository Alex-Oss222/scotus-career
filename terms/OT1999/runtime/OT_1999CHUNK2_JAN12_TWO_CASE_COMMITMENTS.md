## Martinez v. Court of Appeal of California, Fourth Appellate District — No. 98-7809

### Sufficiency and reach

The lawful route is certiorari to the California Supreme Court following its denial of an application for a writ of mandate concerning the Court of Appeal's refusal to permit self-representation. Argument was November 9, 1999. The timely appeal and motion with waiver of counsel are established. The question is whether the Sixth or Fourteenth Amendment requires California to allow the convicted appellant to discharge appointed counsel and conduct the first appeal as of right himself. The conviction, sentence, competency, conflict-free representation, effective assistance, and access to the record are not presented for adjudication.

The neutral clarification removes the unsupported claims that counsel had already filed a brief and that Martinez actually could submit supplemental material. Neither is used. No finding about the quality of counsel's work or Martinez's actual appellate performance is necessary. The record is sufficient to decide the asserted categorical constitutional entitlement; it does not support a disposition of an independent counsel-performance or access claim.

### Competing paths and sufficient grounds

**M1 — Affirm on the separate constitutional source and function of appellate representation.** Faretta protects the accused's personal conduct of the defense at trial. It does not establish a Sixth Amendment right to conduct a postconviction appeal. Douglas and Evitts protect meaningful and effective assistance on the first appeal as of right through the Fourteenth Amendment. Their protection of fair appellate access does not itself confer a converse right to reject the representative through whom the State provides that access. Price distinguishes appellate participation from an entitlement to personally conduct the proceeding. California may therefore require counsel on this first appeal without violating the asserted right. The State remains free to allow self-representation or supplemental submissions; this judgment imposes neither practice.

The strongest objection to M1 is that appellate error correction still determines the appellant's liberty and that Faretta's respect for personal choice need not end with conviction. That concern is substantial but does not supply the missing constitutional extension. The trial right concerns the accused's own defense in the proceeding determining guilt; appellate proceedings review an existing record under a different constitutional arrangement. The State's responsibility to supply a fair and orderly means of review permits represented advocacy. The conclusion does not depend on declaring every lawyer more effective than every litigant, on the appellant's prior convictions, on a prediction that Martinez would perform badly, or on disapproval of Faretta's trial holding.

**M2 — Reverse by extending the personal-autonomy protection to the first appeal.** The strongest supported argument is that an informed appellant whose liberty remains at stake should control whether a lawyer speaks for him, and that the State's provision of counsel should not become a compulsory agency. Faretta supplies the autonomy analogy, while the importance of the first appeal under Douglas and Evitts supplies the asserted setting for a Fourteenth Amendment extension. Its strongest objection is that it treats a trial right and an appellate assistance guarantee as though they had identical sources and scope. Price points the other way; no supplied precedent establishes personal appellate control as a necessary component of a meaningful appeal. Godinez answers who can validly waive an existing right, not whether every proceeding must accept self-representation. Hill distinguishes basic personal choices from counsel's management authority but decides only an authorized scheduling agreement. It neither supplies M2 nor independently decides this case. On the supplied sources, M1 is the supported disposition; M2 does not command an Associate commitment.

### Provisional propositions, remedy, and limits

**M-a:** Faretta's Sixth Amendment trial right does not require self-representation on a first appeal as of right.

**M-b:** The Fourteenth Amendment's protection of fair first-appeal access and effective assistance does not give this convicted appellant a right to displace appointed counsel and personally conduct that appeal. A State may allow personal representation; the Constitution does not require that choice in the presented setting.

**M-c:** Affirm the California Supreme Court's denial of mandate. The Court of Appeal's refusal of the requested self-representation remains undisturbed, and the ordinary direct appeal proceeds under state rules. No new conviction or sentence ruling, substitute lawyer appointment, personal-waiver colloquy, or mandatory supplemental-brief procedure is ordered.

All eight Associates provisionally support M-a, M-b, and M-c at that scope. Faretta is distinguished, not overruled or diminished at trial. Douglas and Evitts retain their meaningful-assistance rules. Price supplies the distinction between permitted appellate participation and compulsory personal advocacy. Godinez retains its separate competence and actual-waiver requirements where a waiver is legally available. Hill remains a scheduling decision, not a rule that counsel controls every important defendant choice.

## Reno v. Condon — No. 98-1464

### Sufficiency and reach

The lawful route is certiorari to the Fourth Circuit's affirmance of summary judgment and a permanent injunction against enforcement of the DPPA against South Carolina and its officers. Argument was November 10, 1999. The granted issues concern the Commerce Clause and the structural objection to the federal direction of state government. The United States expressly disclaims reliance on Fourteenth Amendment section 5 here. No disclosure-specific damages, criminal penalty, daily civil penalty, First Amendment claim, or constitutional informational-privacy claim is presented for decision.

The source extracts establish state collection and sale of motor-vehicle personal information, significant sale revenue, state disclosure rules, and the DPPA's direct restrictions on state disclosures and private resale/redisclosure. The personal-information definition excludes accident, violation, and driver-status information. The statute has mandatory disclosures for the identified motor-vehicle-related federal purposes and distinct permitted uses; a permission is not an obligation. It regulates further disclosure by private recipients, including use-specific restrictions and five-year records identifying recipients and permitted purposes. The sanctions applicable to defined persons remain distinct from the Attorney General's civil penalty for an agency's policy or practice of substantial noncompliance. States and state agencies are excluded from the defined person category. None of those liability predicates is adjudicated.

Section 350 of Public Law 106-69 has been admitted only with its actual text and transition. Its enactment does not establish a present South Carolina opt-in obligation. Subsections (b), (c), and (d) have the June 1, 2000 provision with the listed state transition clauses, including South Carolina's final-decision/session trigger. Subsections (a) and (e) have their separate enactment and 90-day-final-decision clause for Wisconsin, South Carolina, and Oklahoma. No legislative-session fact, elapsed transition, default, or penalty liability is found. No separate adjudication of a funding condition or these later disclosure changes is included in the structural judgment.

**Completed limited source check:** The fresh neutral supplement establishes a published factual recitation that state-sold motor-vehicle information is used by interstate insurers, manufacturers, direct marketers, and others for customized driver solicitations and by public and private entities for interstate motoring. ### Commerce alternative paths

**C-commerce-1 — Sustain regulation of the identified information-distribution activity.** The enacted class is disclosure and further dissemination of the defined personal information obtained in connection with state motor-vehicle records, including the source-disclosure and private resale conditions. It is not the general activity of possessing a driver's license or the whole field of local licensing. The record supports an actual market in the information: States sell it, private recipients redistribute it, and interstate businesses use it in solicitations and interstate motoring. The Act governs the release and distribution of the information itself. Its connection is thus to an identified interstate data market, not a chain from privacy harms through health, productivity, or public expenditures to the national economy.

Lopez permits a properly defined economic class participating in or materially affecting an actual interstate market; Pic-A-State confirms that commercial information transmission across state lines is commerce itself. The DPPA's source and redisclosure rules address the distribution of the same record-derived data through public and private handlers. Congress need not confine its regulation to a sale for money or prove that each regulated release crosses a state line. Including uncompensated or intrastate releases of that same information is reasonably fitted to regulating the class's distribution; a price-based limit would separate modes of supplying the same data without changing its capacity to enter the documented market. This is a legal inference from the defined activity and market, not a finding that a particular person evaded the statute. No added commerce element or rewritten statutory exception is necessary.

The strongest objection is that the text covers disclosures unconnected to a sale and that States initially collect the data for governmental licensing. Home Builders forbids redefining a statutory class around the commercial features of selected examples. Here the actual regulated activity is information disclosure and redistribution, and the actual class contains the source and flow of the data market; the holding does not redefine all licensing as commerce or rely on revenue from unrelated activity. The product's governmental origin does not remove its subsequent commercial distribution from commerce authority. The distinction also supplies an enforceable boundary: Congress regulates the identified distribution activity, not all information possession, every state database, or any activity whose consequences might affect economic welfare. The existing mandatory and permissive disclosure routes and the definition's exclusions retain their own terms.

**C-commerce-2 — Invalidate or remand because the class extends beyond interstate commercial sales.** This path properly demands identification of the enacted class and adequate source support rather than accepting the government's commerce label. Its strongest support is Lopez's rejection of isolated commercial facts as substitutes for a connection fitted to the actual class, reinforced by Home Builders. But the requested factual supplement supplies the missing market support at the level relevant here. The restricted activity concerns distribution of the very information bought, resold, and used in that market; it is not a separate possession or conduct class connected only through an object's distant history. The class-wide connection does not depend on proving that every individual disclosure is interstate, compensated, or substantially consequential. Neither a new factfinding remand nor invalidation is supported merely because the neutral sources do not contain each underlying transaction. A different, narrower application could require its own record, but no such application is decided here.

The ordinary economic-market ground is sufficient. No independent holding under Home Builders' interstate-externality route, no generalized privacy-based commerce power, and no free-standing rule that every useful addition to a federal scheme is necessary and proper is proposed.

### Structural alternative paths

**C1 — Direct regulation of the State's own data activity.** Garcia permits otherwise valid generally applicable federal regulation of state activity, and Baker distinguishes a requirement to comply with federal standards in the State's own activity from compulsory regulation of private persons. New York preserves that distinction while forbidding the choice between federal regulation of private generators and forced ownership/liability. The DPPA governs what a State does with information it holds and regulates private downstream handlers of that same information. A DMV's use of employees to screen or administer its disclosures is compliance by the regulated actor; it does not itself make the State the federal regulator of unrelated private conduct.

The strongest objection is that States initially acquire DMV data through their governmental licensing functions and that compliance requires continuing screening, records, and personnel. A direct regulation can entail administrative work; treating all such work as unconstitutional would erase Garcia's preserved field. The relevant distinction is the regulated activity and allocation of responsibility, not whether the State obtained the asset while performing a governmental function or must use employees to comply. The DPPA neither commands a legislature to enact a regulatory program nor transfers general federal enforcement against private persons to state officials. This ground requires a valid enumerated-power connection; privacy's importance cannot supply one.

**C2 — Treat the DPPA as compulsory governmental administration.** South Carolina's strongest path stresses its exclusive initial possession of motor-vehicle records, the origin of those records in public licensing, and the direct continuing compliance burdens. New York bars an unavoidable federal command to assume governmental responsibility for regulation of others, and Printz/Mack does not validate permanent policy administration merely because the work uses records. Its strongest obstacle is that the DPPA governs the State's own disclosures and also reaches the private resale market. Its duties are attached to the regulated data handling, not a demand that the State operate an enforcement program against other persons. The Act is therefore within the direct-regulation distinction preserved by New York and Garcia. No decision whether Congress may regulate only States is necessary; this scheme also regulates private recipients.

Printz/Mack's actual Part I permits confined temporary assistance under its affirmative statutory direction and allocation of enforcement responsibility, while leaving fiscal objections to that arrangement unresolved. Neither a categorical ban on all federal instructions to state employees nor a blanket approval of permanent record duties can be attributed to it. C1 uses the separate state-activity ground; it does not extend Printz/Mack, resolve its fiscal remand, or impose an automatic compensation condition on all direct federal regulation of state activity.

### Provisional propositions, remedy, and limits

**C-a:** Congress may regulate the DPPA's disclosure and redistribution of personal motor-vehicle information as activity within the supported interstate information market. The source-disclosure and private-redisclosure restrictions bear a reasonable relationship to regulation of that class. It is unnecessary to show that each individual disclosure is compensated, interstate, or independently substantial. This does not authorize regulation of every state record or of local conduct on the basis of general privacy-related economic consequences.

**C-b:** The DPPA directly regulates States' handling and disclosure of their own data and private recipients' further handling of that data. Its compliance duties do not commandeer state legislation or require state officers to operate a federal regulatory or coercive-enforcement program against unrelated private persons. State personnel's work in complying with a valid direct regulation does not alone make that regulation an impermissible command. The separate question whether Congress may regulate States alone is reserved because this scheme also regulates private handlers.

**C-c:** Reverse the Fourth Circuit's judgment and remand for proceedings consistent with rejection of the challenged Commerce Clause and structural grounds. Those grounds cannot sustain the summary judgment or permanent injunction against enforcement. Implementation of the reversal must respect the Act's actual temporal provisions and any distinct issue properly remaining below; this statement invents no additional pending claim. It imposes no present opt-in deadline, statutory violation, sanction, award, or disclosure-specific liability determination.
