## Reachability and law entering each component

Section 1254(1) supplies review of the Sixth Circuit judgment. All eight Associates are seated and participate on the supplied facts; no recusal or participation defect is established. The accepted question is whether an ineffective-assistance claim offered as cause is categorically exempt from procedural default after exhaustion. It does not grant review of every Ohio evidence or appellate-counsel question.

**E1 — Availability of a defaulted constitutional cause claim.** Carrier requires constitutionally ineffective assistance, rather than ordinary attorney error, to supply this form of cause and ordinarily requires presentation of that independent constitutional claim to the State. Coleman retains actual independent and adequate state defaults and their recognized exceptions. Gray preserves distinct claims and procedural predicates. O'Sullivan's former-law complete-round rule distinguishes exhausted claims from claims whose available state route has expired; it does not equate exhaustion with freedom from default. The precise operative register is **One complete ordinary state-review round under former §2254**, present operation effective June 7, 1999. Carpenter filed federally on May 3, 1996; O'Sullivan does not classify his application as pre-AEDPA.

**E2 — Ohio adequacy and the present judgment.** The District Court held the Rule 26(B) good-cause practice inadequate because of inconsistent application. The Sixth Circuit substituted an exhaustion-only rationale and did not decide adequacy. Ohio's exhaustion concession supplies no concession of adequacy, excuse, constitutional deficiency or sufficiency. The Rule's own good-cause exception to late filing and federal cause excusing an adequate state default are different inquiries. **Sequencing nonretroactivity and procedural default on habeas review**, present operation effective Trest, December 9, 1997, and Lambrix, May 12, 1997, preserves default's nonjurisdictional character and actual predicates. It creates neither mandatory default-first sequencing nor a license to assume the disputed ground adequate.

**E3 — Constitutional merits, statutory conditions and relief.** Evitts and Jones protect effective first-appellate assistance without requiring every possible issue to be raised. Smith v. Robbins preserves its defined missing-screening-function remedy alongside individual Strickland review. Flores-Ortega distinguishes a causally lost appeal from counsel's selection of issues within an appeal. Terry Williams requires the actual lawful prejudice inquiry and independently applicable statutory conditions. Michael Williams prevents a record gap alone from becoming applicant fault or a hearing command. Carpenter's actual state procedural rejection is not silently converted into a state merits adjudication under section 2254(d). Any genuinely applicable statutory gate requires its own predicate. Schlup retains the distinct, demanding offense-innocence gateway; legal insufficiency, ordinary prejudice and gateway innocence are not interchangeable.

## Supported competing paths

**A. Reject the categorical exemption and remand.** Adjudicating whether appellate counsel committed a constitutional violation is necessary before that violation can supply cause. Calling the claim an explanation for another default does not remove the State's independent interest in adjudicating that constitutional claim under its adequate procedures. If the ineffective-assistance claim itself is barred by an actual adequate, independent and unexcused default, it cannot supply cause merely because state remedies are exhausted. Cause and actual prejudice excusing that default can restore its availability; the separate miscarriage-of-justice route retains its existing scope. The strongest objection is Carrier's emphasis on exhaustion and the distinction between a ground for a writ and a ground explaining another default. The answer is that the latter still requires the same intermediate constitutional adjudication, while the conditional rule preserves both adequacy review and recognized exceptions.

**B. Affirm the exhaustion-only rule.** Carpenter gave the State an opportunity to act on ineffective assistance and offers it for a distinct explanatory purpose. Carrier's express presentation language supports that position. The strongest contrary point is that a procedural rejection supplies no state merits resolution and exhaustion can occur precisely because a state remedy was forfeited. Gray's separate-claim treatment, Coleman and the shared Michael Williams separation of default from cause supply no categorical immunity based on the use to which the constitutional claim is put. This rationale is not adopted by the provisional commitments.

**C. Sustain relief on Rule 26(B)'s inadequacy without adopting the exemption.** The existing District Court ruling makes this a real alternative, not an invented exception. If supported after proper review, an inadequate state rule would remove the asserted bar without needing cause for that purported default. But the accepted question and the supplied published record do not resolve the contested adequacy inquiry for this Court. The Sixth Circuit's observations are not an appellate holding on it. The provisional commitments preserve review of that actual ruling on remand rather than affirming a conditional new culpability hearing on an unexamined alternative.

## Common operative scope and remaining alternatives

All eight provisional commitments support the following propositions at the same level of generality: a constitutional ineffective-assistance claim used as cause is not exempt from an otherwise applicable adequate and independent state default; a prisoner may establish cause and actual prejudice excusing that claim's own default; the existing miscarriage-of-justice route is preserved at its established scope; and exhaustion alone does not establish freedom from default. No commitment treats the exhaustion concession as a concession of the claim's constitutional merits or the default's excuse.

All eight support reversal of the Sixth Circuit judgment and remand at this confined scope. Review of the District Court's Rule 26(B) adequacy holding remains necessary if material to the lawful disposition. A successful inadequacy argument would mean this asserted state ground supplies no default barrier; an actually adequate and independent ground requires the relevant exception inquiry if that claim is to supply cause. This is not a mandatory sequence for every habeas defense and does not reopen matters otherwise lawfully resolved.

The Sixth Circuit did not decide the ultimate sufficiency claim, and it reserved consent to evidentiary use of the prosecutor's recitation. The commitments do not adopt that recitation as evidence, find the plea invalid, establish appellate ineffective assistance, settle Rule 26(B)'s federal adequacy or award the conditional culpability-hearing remedy on a different ground. The legal ruling alone orders no release, acquittal, new trial, new direct appeal, evidentiary hearing, final habeas denial or deadline. Any later relief must follow the claims and gates actually established.

No separate writing is independently required to preserve an incompatible proposition at this provisional stage. The different cautions in the table can coexist with the same expressly conditional rule. A formulation treating Ohio adequacy as decided, changing the preserved exceptions, dispensing with constitutional appellate-performance requirements, or imposing an unsupported hearing or merits remedy exceeds these commitments and requires renewed compatibility review.
