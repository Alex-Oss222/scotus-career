## The actual law and its evidentiary limits

The applicable Standards are **Visibility of protected nonobscene cabaret expression**, effective September 28, 1998; **Continuing individual controversy and legally unavailable damages**, effective March 3, 1997; and **Equitable vacatur after settlement moots review**, effective November 8, 1994. Current *Laidlaw*, January 12, 2000, supplies the distinct defendant-cessation and redress rules. *ASARCO*, May 30, 1989, addresses first federal review of a concrete injury from a state judgment; state courts are not bound by Article III. None of the new 1994 appendix facts changes Pap's later closure, sale, retirement or corporate-status evidence.

For the merits, the ordinary *O'Brien* inquiry requires governmental power, an important or substantial interest, an interest unrelated to suppressing expression, and an incidental expressive burden no greater than essential to that interest. *Ward* rejects a universal least-restrictive-means requirement but retains real advancement and sufficient fit. *Renton* allows relevant experience outside the locality; it does not make any invocation of crime a sufficient evidentiary answer or turn reaction to a message into a noncommunicative harm.

*Barnes* retains four separate rationales. O'Connor and Kennedy joined the plurality's general-public-nudity and public-order/morality analysis under *O'Brien*. Scalia's concurrence used the genuinely general-conduct distinction and reserved targeting expression in operation; his opinion also addressed claims of theater exceptions, and the dissent reported an absence of arrests for plays and ballets. Thus theatrical nonenforcement alone is not a factual feature wholly absent from the *Barnes* debate. Souter relied instead on crime-related effects, relevant experience in other places, a current justification rather than proof of original motive, and the limited covering burden. His footnote reserved different theatrical contexts lacking a relevant effects analogy. Stevens joined White's dissent concerning the communicative target and fit. Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer were not seated for *Barnes*. No fifth judgment vote is converted into a join of Souter's rationale, and this handoff establishes no generalized *Marks* synthesis.

*Ino Ino*, September 28, 1998, supplies direct controlling law joined throughout by all eight Associates. Kennedy's opinion applies expressive-conduct scrutiny to the actual cabaret stage, distance, lighting and hours restrictions; it rejects rational-basis-only treatment of the stage restriction. The accepted contact and detection evidence supports those bounded physical rules. Its separate protected-image holding rejects censorship merely because listed anatomy can be seen outside. Actual scope, authoritative construction, genuine harm and fit matter; no morality-only defense, general cabaret immunity or national clothing safe harbor results. An exception or narrowing in one provision cannot migrate to another.

The additional earlier authorities sharpen those requirements without importing their different factual tests wholesale:

- **Edenfield**, April 26, 1993: Kennedy's opinion, joined by Stevens, Scalia, Souter and Thomas among the eight-Justice majority, requires real harm and material advancement for the challenged truthful CPA solicitation restriction. O'Connor did not join that holding, and Ginsburg and Breyer were not then seated. ## Complete material record for this determination

The supplied grant concerns whether the ordinance may require minimal clothing for dancers in an adult-entertainment establishment, with necessary threshold review. The federal majority of the Pennsylvania Supreme Court supplied the judgment's ground; its two-Justice state constitutional concurrence does not bar review. That majority applied strict scrutiny, linked the restriction to suppressing the erotic message, and independently found no narrow fit between minimal clothing and sex-crime prevention. Its finding that the City's state briefing omitted strict-scrutiny analysis is not a Supreme Court default. It severed sections 1(c) and 2 under Pennsylvania law, leaving the intercourse, deviate-intercourse and genital-fondling provisions intact. It did not decide the Pennsylvania Constitution, substantial overbreadth or the standing objection to overbreadth, and it rejected judicial insertion of an expressive-nudity exception.

Section 1(c) criminalizes knowingly or intentionally appearing nude in a public place. Section 2 includes insufficiently opaque coverage of the specified genitals, pubic area, buttocks and female nipple, together with the specified simulated anatomy and realistic nipple/areola coverings. Section 3 includes outdoor public places and the listed enclosed premises, theaters and clubs, even if restricted to members, adults or invited patrons, with or without admission charges. Section 4 exempts children under ten and breast exposure while nursing an infant under two. Section 6 calls for the least constitutional infringement consistent with the ordinance's purposes and permits severance of a portion, application or enforcement. It is not itself an enacted theater exemption. The complete operative text remains in the authorized original extracts; no unverified penalty term is decided.

Kandyland offered nude female live entertainment in a commercial area and allowed patrons to bring alcohol without selling it. The December 1994 agreement identifies Pap's then as a corporation with Nick Panos as sole shareholder/director and the business as a bottle club outside Liquor Control Board regulation. It does not establish later ownership or reopening intent. The accepted advertisement and live-entertainment facts do not depend on an agreed erotic-message formulation: Pap's refused that proposed characterization without disclaiming expression or waiving its constitutional claim. The agreed clothing item describes what the ordinance **required**, not actual compliance or a citation. The comparison to liquor-licensed dancers was deleted and establishes no statewide clothing rule.

The Council tape contains mixed statements about indecency, erotic stimulation, crime, alcohol, community values and the continued availability of covered adult entertainment. The City's proposed characterization of the tape was not adopted as an agreed purpose/effects finding. The evidence remains the tape itself. Counsel's claims about crime prevention do not become proven criminal incidents at Kandyland.

The supplement materially adds two different categories of evidence:

1. **An agreed historical fact.** After the initial objection and negotiation, the judge read, and Pap's expressly accepted, that *Equus* featured frontal nudity, ran for several weeks in October and November 1994 at the Roadhouse Theater, and attracted no effort to enforce the nudity prohibition, which became effective during the run. The City's further objection concerned the separate proposed Council-tape finding, not this agreement. The proposed description of acknowledged artistic and social merit was deleted. No merits assessment of the work, motive for each enforcement decision or general future immunity was stipulated.
2. **Repeated identifiable municipal interpretations.** City counsel said the ordinance would not reach *Equus*, *Hair* and *O Calcutta* to the extent their expression deserved higher protection, attributed the protective position to the City and Council, acknowledged subjectivity, and invoked section 6. Counsel also urged the state court to create or recognize a limiting construction, with disagreement about construction versus adding language. These representations are more than a legislator's isolated remark, but are not themselves enacted text or an authoritative judicial construction. Co-counsel's argument that the City could limit a message that entices secondary effects is relevant to the asserted causal theory; it is not a stipulated fact that the message caused crime.

The historical nonenforcement and the legal interpretations can support competing inferences. The City can characterize them as constitutional accommodation for contexts lacking the adult-establishment harms. Pap's can characterize them as a subjective hierarchy of acceptable expression, reinforced by the Council's focus on erotic content. The early temporary restraint and uncertain effective-date recitations also prevent an assertion that officials could lawfully enforce the ordinance on every day of the run or that selective exemption was the sole possible cause of nonenforcement. The inference must rest on the entire record, including the City's stated interpretation and intended Kandyland application, not that incident alone.

Admission of pleadings for statements and admissions proves neither every allegation nor every legal conclusion. That identifies what was submitted; it creates no litigation default or rule demanding a local study. The Court must still evaluate reasonable inferences and relevant outside experience.

## Merits alternatives and the effect of the fuller record

**Sustain the granted application under intermediate scrutiny.** The law's text covers nonexpressive public nudity as well as performances, and minimal covering leaves erotic performance available. *Barnes* permits a reasonable adult-establishment effects inference; *Renton* does not require repeated local proof of every recognized harm. On this path, the theater representations are an effort to respect constitutional limits in different settings, and the actual nonenforcement is consistent with that accommodation. Neither a perceived hierarchy of artistic merit nor an asserted crime finding is necessary to sustain this narrow application.

The strongest objection is now affirmative: the City repeatedly defended different treatment in terms of higher expression, conceded subjectivity, and described control of an enticing message as a means to its goals. With the actual *Equus* fact and the Council evidence, the objection concerns operation and asserted justification, not only unattractive legislative remarks. *Turner I* prevents benign-purpose language from disposing of that objection. *Ino Ino*, the pertinent *Edenfield* analogy and the later evidentiary decisions also prevent a bare recital of crime plus a small burden from replacing advancement and fit. A sustaining rationale must explain why the recognized *Barnes* inference remains adequate in this particular adult-business application despite that record; it cannot announce that no differential enforcement evidence exists.

**Affirm the federal protection.** The combined record supports treating the asserted application as selecting which nude expression merits official tolerance. The general text is not conclusive when the City's actual interpretation distinguishes higher expression and defends moderating a message, while acknowledging that it seeks to apply the rule to Kandyland. The *Equus* agreement corroborates the practical relevance of that interpretation without proving its exclusive cause. Under the content-classification principle in *Turner I* and the actual *Ino Ino* protection, a hierarchy of expressive worth is not a noncommunicative harm. Crime prevention is compelling, but this record does not connect the covering requirement to that goal closely enough to sustain content-based suppression.

The strongest opposite reading treats the theater position as lawful constitutional accommodation and invokes the same debate in *Barnes*. This path distinguishes neither case by pretending the earlier Court never heard a theatrical-exception claim. It relies on the fuller affirmative municipal interpretation, the newly enacted targeting shown by the Council record, and the later direct simulated rules governing actual scope and support. It does not overrule the *Barnes* judgment, make every failure to prosecute discriminatory, or deny that different venues can have materially different noncommunicative effects. Nor does it require proof of a completed local crime or declare that all secondary effects arise from persuasion.

**Vacate and remand for the correct classification and evidentiary decision.** The state majority's categorical equation between secondary effects and the dance's message is too broad, but the City's contrary claim of generality does not settle the case either. The lower court did not determine whether the actual proposed differentiation rests on constitutionally relevant differences in noncommunicative effects, rather than a hierarchy of expressive value, using the full governing framework. If the justification is independent of content, the court must assess advancement and fit on the actual record, including reasonable relevant outside inferences and the contrary evidence. A recital of substantial interests or the number of square inches covered is insufficient by itself.

This remand identifies an undecided legal application, not a missing research document. It does not declare the present record sufficient or insufficient, automatically reopen evidence, demand a local study, instruct the City to invent an exception, or postpone decision merely to give a losing litigant another chance. The existing evidence may support decision without another hearing. State constitutional and preserved overbreadth questions remain available. No remanding Justice commits in advance to a later final outcome.
