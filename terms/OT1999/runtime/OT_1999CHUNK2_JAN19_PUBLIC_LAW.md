# January 19, 2000 — completed public law

## Event

Gutierrez v. Ada, No. 99-51. On writ of certiorari to the United States Court of Appeals for the Ninth Circuit; argued December 6, 1999; decided January 19, 2000. The Guam Election Commission had certified the Gutierrez slate as the winner. The Ninth Circuit, 179 F.3d 672, required a runoff after counting all general-election ballots in the denominator. The slate obtained a majority of votes for Governor and Lieutenant Governor but not of every general-election ballot. The question is which denominator the Guam Organic Act, 48 U.S.C. §1422, requires.

## Participation

Chief Justice Stone-Zsela and Justices Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer participate at argument and decision.

## Public Action

The Court reverses the Ninth Circuit's statutory denominator ruling and runoff order and remands.

## Judgment & Remedy

**Reversed and remanded, 9–0.**

**Supporting:** Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg, and Breyer.

The Ninth Circuit's runoff order is reversed. The matter is remanded for recognition that the existing certified Gutierrez slate obtained the statutory majority under the gubernatorial-vote denominator, removing the all-general-election-ballot ground for upsetting that certification and requiring a runoff. No first or additional certificate, new election, factual hearing, unsupported final officeholding decree or disposition of a distinct invalid-vote or fraud claim is ordered.

## Holdings

### The majority is calculated from votes cast for gubernatorial slates

**Holding and operative rule:** Under 48 U.S.C. §1422, a gubernatorial slate obtains the required majority when it receives more than half the votes cast for Governor and Lieutenant Governor jointly; general-election ballots expressing no choice in that contest are excluded from this denominator. If no slate receives a majority of those votes, the statute requires a runoff on the fourteenth day between the slates receiving the highest and second-highest vote totals; the ruling does not decide invalid candidate votes, fraud or materially different statutory language.

**Authority:** Justice Souter's opinion of the Court, joined in full by all eight other participating Justices, supplies a direct unanimous construction of §1422 and its application to the supplied election.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that the paragraph counts votes in the gubernatorial contest. Section 1422 provides for one joint vote for Governor and Lieutenant Governor and links the majority calculation to the votes candidates receive. A ballot used to vote in another contest but making no gubernatorial choice is not that joint vote. The reference to people qualified to vote for members of the Legislature identifies who may vote; the reference to the general election identifies when the gubernatorial election occurs. Neither changes the object being counted.

The competing reading would require a majority of everyone participating in the simultaneous general election, on the theory that a runoff secures a broader mandate. But the statutory mechanism speaks of cast votes received by candidates, not participation in unrelated races. An abstention from this contest cannot become a vote against each slate without a textual direction to count it that way. The candidate-vote construction leaves the runoff clause fully operative whenever the gubernatorial votes themselves produce no majority.

The established slate received a majority of the votes cast for gubernatorial candidates. The all-ballot denominator therefore cannot sustain the Ninth Circuit's runoff order. The Court reverses that order and remands under the correct denominator; the decision supplies no new numerical returns, fraud finding or rule for invalid candidate votes.

**Precedent treatment:** The holding construes this statutory mechanism directly. Rayphand/Torres concerns a separate territorial constitutional accommodation and supplies no different denominator for Guam.

## Precedent Treatment

Rayphand v. Sablan / Torres v. Sablan (December 13, 1999) remains confined to its territorial constitutional accommodation and does not construe this Guam election provision. No earlier constitutional election rule is changed or a territorial franchise exception created.

## Law After Decision

Section 1422's majority and runoff clauses use votes cast in the joint gubernatorial contest. Simultaneous participation in other races does not enlarge that denominator. The statutory runoff remains mandatory when no slate obtains a majority of the gubernatorial votes; this construction creates no general constitutional majority requirement and does not govern materially different language, fraud or invalid-vote disputes.

## Event

United States v. Martinez-Salazar, No. 98-1255. On writ of certiorari to the United States Court of Appeals for the Ninth Circuit; argued November 29, 1999; decided January 19, 2000. In the joint narcotics and weapons trial, the defendants received ten ordinary joint peremptory challenges and a separate alternate challenge. The court refused a cause challenge to Gilbert, who expressed a predisposition toward the prosecution. After two unsuccessful objections, Martinez-Salazar used an ordinary peremptory to remove him; all ordinary strikes were eventually used. The Ninth Circuit, 146 F.3d 653, found the cause ruling erroneous and no Sixth Amendment violation, but granted relief on a Fifth Amendment/full-allotment theory. The cause error is uncontested here. The question is whether corrective use denies a constitutional or Rule 24 right when the full allotment was supplied and an impartial jury sat.

Chief Justice Stone-Zsela and Justices Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer participate at argument and decision.

## Public Action

The Ninth Circuit's lost-peremptory ground for relief is reversed.

## Judgment & Remedy

**Reversed and remanded, 9–0.**

**Supporting:** Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg, and Breyer.

The Ninth Circuit's lost-peremptory basis for relief is reversed. The conviction remains undisturbed as against this jury-selection claim, and the matter returns for any proceedings properly remaining under the ordinary mandate. The Court orders no new trial for this cured cause error and adjudicates no additional claim.

## Holdings

**Common authority:** Justice Scalia's opinion of the Court is joined in full by Chief Justice Stone-Zsela and Justices Stevens, O'Connor, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer. All nine adopt both holdings, the express reservations and the remedy directly.

### Corrective use followed by an impartial jury causes no Sixth Amendment deprivation

**Holding and operative rule:** When a defendant preserves a cause objection, uses an allotted peremptory challenge to remove the juror and is tried by an impartial jury, that corrective use does not itself deny the Sixth Amendment impartial-jury guarantee. The erroneous cause ruling remains an error; this holding does not decide a case in which a biased juror sits or an independent discriminatory-selection injury occurs.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that Gilbert's removal and the impartiality of the jury that sat defeat the claimed Sixth Amendment deprivation. Ross distinguishes the constitutional entitlement to an impartial tribunal from the statutory opportunities for peremptory exclusion. Its distinction applies here even though its Oklahoma-law compulsory-use condition does not govern this federal case. The Court accepts the uncontested determination that Gilbert should have been removed for cause; exercising the peremptory does not make that ruling correct.

The defense's strategic loss is real: the strike might otherwise have been used against a different prospective juror. But a possible change in the jury's composition is not itself the constitutional injury asserted when the challenged juror did not sit and the seated jury's impartiality is not contested. Gray's capital exclusion of a qualified juror is a different setting. The distinct federal-rule entitlement requires its own construction below; impartiality is not treated as satisfying every independent selection right. Batson, McCollum and J.E.B. retain their protections against discrimination, which this case does not present.

**Precedent treatment:** Ross's impartial-jury distinction is applied while its state-law curative condition remains confined to that law. Gray is distinguished at its capital-exclusion scope. The independent discrimination protections of Batson, McCollum and J.E.B. remain intact.

### The defendants received the Rule 24 entitlement they exercised

**Holding and operative rule:** On this record, Rule 24(b)'s ten joint ordinary peremptory challenges were fully afforded and exercised; using one to remove a juror erroneously retained over a cause objection does not deny that allotment or create a Fifth Amendment deprivation merely because the remaining strikes are exhausted. Rule 24 itself imposes no general duty of corrective use and promises no automatic replenishment; its permission for additional challenges in a multiple-defendant case is not a command, and the separate alternate challenge is not interchangeable with an ordinary one.

**Controlling explanation:** Rule 24 grants the opportunities to exclude prospective jurors, not ten further exclusions after every cause ruling has been correctly resolved. The defendants received and exercised all ten ordinary strikes. Removing Gilbert used one of those opportunities for its intended purpose. Practical pressure following the adverse cause ruling does not establish that the court denied the exercise of a strike. Because the claimed rule-based entitlement was received, the Fifth Amendment deprivation theory built on its loss fails as well.

The strongest contrary construction treats all for-cause removals as logically prior to the full peremptory allotment. That concern recognizes the value of an exclusion without legal cause, but neither the rule nor this record establishes the additional entitlement asserted. Defendants asked for no further strike, identified no other juror they wished to remove and challenged no seated juror's impartiality. Those facts limit this result; they do not create an exclusive preservation checklist or erase the prior cause objections.

Ross expressly reserved the impairment question beyond Oklahoma's particular rule, so the Court answers the federal-rule question independently. Swain's language about reversal for impairment cannot establish the antecedent impairment itself. Finding none, the Court does not choose automatic reversal or harmless-error review for an actual deprivation. The Government's proposed compulsory-use rule is rejected, but that rejection supplies no assurance that a defendant who leaves a challenged juror seated may later appeal without regard to ordinary waiver or preservation rules. That different situation remains open.

**Express reservations:** The Court does not decide whether reversible error occurs when a defendant uses a strike to cure a cause error, shows that the full complement otherwise would be used for noncurative exclusions, and requests but is refused a makeup challenge beyond the otherwise applicable maximum. This record contains no such showing or request. Deliberate judicial manipulation, denial of the prescribed allotment, a biased seated juror and independent discrimination likewise remain distinct. No right to replenishment, categorical no-waiver rule or general harmlessness rule follows.

**Precedent treatment:** Ross's express reservation requires an independent Rule 24 construction; its state-law exhaustion condition is not imported. Swain's actual-impairment language is left unresolved as a remedy question because no impairment is established. Hill's bounded scheduling rule supplies no waiver determination here.

## Precedent Treatment

- Ross v. Oklahoma, 487 U.S. 81 (1988): applies its distinction between an impartial jury and statutory peremptories, while preserving its express reservation beyond Oklahoma's curative-use condition; it does not itself decide Rule 24 or impose that state's exhaustion rule.
- Gray v. Mississippi: distinguishes the erroneous exclusion of a qualified capital juror; its setting does not make every possible composition change a Sixth Amendment injury.
- Swain v. Alabama: leaves its language about the remedy for actual peremptory impairment unresolved here; no denial of this allotment is found, and no general automatic-reversal rule is overruled.
- Batson v. Kentucky; Georgia v. McCollum; J.E.B. v. Alabama ex rel. T.B.: preserve their distinct jury-selection equality protections; the impartiality of the final jury does not extinguish an independently established discrimination claim.
- New York v. Hill (January 11, 2000): remains confined to its authorized scheduling agreement and exceptions; counsel's final lack of objection here does not become a Hill-based waiver of the earlier cause objections.

## Law After Decision

Rule 24's received-and-exercised numerical allotment is not impaired by this corrective use. The rule creates no general duty to cure a cause error, no automatic replenishment entitlement and no new general preservation checklist. The actual cause error, impartial-jury right, rule entitlement and Fifth Amendment deprivation remain separate inquiries. The Court does not decide waiver when a challenged juror is left seated, Souter's specified showing/request/refused-makeup situation, a biased seated juror, deliberate manipulation, denied prescribed allotment or independent discrimination. The remedy for an actual impairment remains open; neither automatic reversal nor general harmlessness is established.
