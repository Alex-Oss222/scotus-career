## Temporal rule and its common basis

Revocation imprisonment and further supervision enforce the original sentence rather than punish a newly prosecuted offense. The statutory limits refer to the original offense and its imposed supervision term; revocation may rest on noncriminal conditions and a preponderance finding under its own process. Later misconduct is a new event but does not itself determine the legal source of its sanctions. The original October 1993 offense therefore controls the inquiry into whether subsection (h), enacted September 13, 1994, governs.

The required congressional temporal direction is absent. Subsection (h) is unavailable without first deciding whether its exact application would increase punishment beyond former law. The Court must separately construe the former statute. A lawful old-law ground may sustain the judgment while rejecting the Sixth Circuit's later-violation rationale; it does not give subsection (h) retrospective force.

Landgraf and Rivers supply the governing temporal-direction and actual-consequence distinctions. The shared conclusion does not merge Scalia's, Kennedy's and Thomas's relevant-activity approach with every step of the broader organizing framework. Thomas's frozen current commitment expressly derives original-offense attribution from the statute's offense references and his own relevant-activity position. The comparator records no contrary temporal holding by him; his historical judgment-only concurrence cannot supply the affirmative join, but neither does it disprove this supported current choice. His temporal assent is thus a recorded inference from the neutral grounds, not reconstructed historical topology.

Mining's current-control permit rule is different: this consequence remains attached to a criminal conviction, not merely a present condition for a prospective permit. Young concerns withdrawal of existing conditional liberty under revocation process without creating sentencing power. Carmell's proof-sufficiency category, Garner's parole-opportunity rule and Saenz's conduct-date notice method retain their own subjects. None supplies an automatic sentencing result or retroactive authority for (h).

## Former-law rule, exact arithmetic and reservations

The seven affirming Associates read former (e)(3)'s direction allowing prison service of all or part of the imposed supervised-release term as retaining a term that can be allocated partly to confinement and partly to supervision after confinement. Revocation withdraws the right to remain released under its existing conditions; it does not inevitably discharge every remaining aspect of the term. The separately expressed termination-and-discharge power in (e)(1) supports that distinction. The original-term reference performs actual legal work rather than merely supplying an abstract number after the term is treated as wholly nonexistent.

The contrary ordinary-cancellation reading is substantial. Scalia treats the original term as the measure of permissible imprisonment after cancellation, and finds no express authorization of a new release period. The seven instead find the residual-term construction in the actual all-or-part mechanism and distinguish termination from revocation. They do not depend on the 1994 amendment to create the missing power, combine (e)(2) with (e)(3), or infer power from usefulness alone. No unsupported historical parole practice is added to the model's sufficient rationale.

The actual measures are separate:

| Measure | Supported value and legal role |
|---|---|
| Supervised-release term actually imposed in March 1994 | Thirty-six months. This is the former-(e)(3) term used by the seven's remainder construction. |
| Former Class C/D cap on revocation imprisonment | Twenty-four months in prison. It is not the original supervision term and is not substituted for that term in the combined remainder calculation. |
| Revocation imprisonment imposed | Eighteen months, within the twenty-four-month prison cap. |
| Further supervision imposed | Twelve months. |
| Combined new imprisonment and supervision | Thirty months, within the thirty-six months actually imposed. The further supervision is also within the eighteen months remaining after subtracting the revocation imprisonment from that imposed term. |
| Earlier postrelease supervision | Approximately seven months. Former (e)(3) expressly denies credit for that time; it does not consume the amount used in this bounded calculation. |

The rule does not convert imprisonment and supervision into interchangeable experiences or approve every possible combination. It authorizes this remaining-term allocation under the old statutory language. No amended reference to the term **authorized by statute for the offense** replaces the former reference to the actual **imposed term**. The later subsection (h)'s less-than-the-maximum-imprisonment trigger and authorized-maximum-minus-prison formula do not govern through arithmetic coincidence.

Different originally imposed terms, felony caps, repeated revocations and other combinations are not decided. Former (e)(1)'s termination after one year retains its conduct-and-justice and procedural predicates. Former (e)(2) retains its limits on extension and condition changes before expiration or termination; it is not authority to create a fresh postrevocation term or evade another paragraph's limit. The offense-date subsection (a) excludes its later domestic-violence addition. No new sentencing or violation facts are found.
