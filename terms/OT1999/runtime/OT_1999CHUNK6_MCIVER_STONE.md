## B. JUDGMENT AND REMEDY

* **Stone's Controlled Judgment or Disposition:** Reverse in part and remand. The Court of Appeals' judgment is reversed insofar as its affirmance of McIver's conviction rests on the ruling that attaching the tracking devices to his vehicle was not a search, and the case is remanded. Stone enters no separate disposition on public-road monitoring, which McIver did not contest below.
* **Stone's Remedy and Remand Position:** Modify the search trigger; distinguish search from seizure; remand for warrant, exception, causation, and harmless-error questions. Stone does not decide the public-road monitoring question, which McIver did not contest below. *Knotts* remains existing law; this reservation does not decide the legality of obtaining information through an unlawful attachment. Stone orders no automatic suppression and adds no special evidentiary instruction. Matters not reviewed here keep their existing status. McIver's concession forecloses only a separate claim that the monitoring itself was a search. It does not establish that information obtained through the attached devices is independent of the attachment; that question belongs to the ordinary causal and remedial inquiry on remand.

## C. ISSUE POSITIONS

### Issue 1: Physical attachment of tracking devices

* **Threshold and Merits Reach:** Stone reaches the merits of this issue. McIver challenged the attachment in the Court of Appeals, and that court decided both whether it was a search and whether it was a seizure.
* **Stone's Legal Position:** The deliberate physical attachment of a government tracking device to a person's vehicle, an “effect,” to obtain information is a search and requires a warrant or recognized exception. Attachment is not necessarily a seizure unless it meaningfully interferes with possession, control, or use. Stone does not apply that standard to this record and enters no separate seizure disposition; his search ground alone supports his judgment. Short-term monitoring of movements exposed on public roads is not independently challenged as a search on this record, and *Knotts* remains the governing law for such monitoring, but information obtained through the unlawful attachment may still require ordinary causal and remedial analysis.
* **Stone's Proposed Holding:** Government physically searches an effect when it attaches a tracking device to that effect for the purpose of obtaining information. The attachment is a seizure only if it meaningfully interferes with possessory control, and short-term public-road monitoring remains separately governed by *Knotts*.
* **Essential Reasoning and Record Application:** Reverse in part and remand. The deliberate physical attachment of a government tracking device to a person's vehicle, an “effect,” to obtain information is a search and requires a warrant or recognized exception. Attachment is not necessarily a seizure unless it meaningfully interferes with possession, control, or use. Short-term monitoring of movements exposed on public roads is not independently challenged as a search on this record, and *Knotts* remains the governing law for such monitoring, but information obtained through the unlawful attachment may still require ordinary causal and remedial analysis. Stone’s attachment rule follows his reading of the Amendment’s protection of effects and the distinction between observation and purposeful physical occupation. It is his proposed resolution of this installation question, not a backward application of a later Supreme Court tracking decision. Knotts continues to govern the separate limited public-road observation issue on its actual facts.
* **Boundary or Reserved Question:** Stone does not decide prolonged aggregate monitoring, attachment with valid consent or a warrant, suppression without the ordinary causal and remedial inquiry, or whether this attachment meaningfully interfered with possession.
### Issue 2: Public-road monitoring

* **Threshold and Merits Reach:** Stone does not reach the independent lawfulness of public-road monitoring. In the Court of Appeals McIver expressly did not contend that monitoring the beeper as the vehicle traveled on the streets and highways infringed his Fourth Amendment rights, and he cited *Knotts* for the absence of a reasonable expectation of privacy on a public thoroughfare. The Court of Appeals made no monitoring ruling. Nothing before the Court withdraws that concession, and the grant's wording does not do so.
* **Stone's Legal Position:** No separate monitoring position. Issue 1's statement that short-term public-road monitoring is not independently challenged on this record, and that *Knotts* remains the governing law for it, marks the limit of the attachment rule; it is not a separate disposition. Whether information obtained through the attached devices must be excluded is decided under the attachment rule and the ordinary causal and remedial inquiry.
* **Stone's Proposed Holding:** None on this issue. Issue 1's holding, including that short-term public-road monitoring remains separately governed by *Knotts*, is unchanged.
* **Essential Reasoning and Record Application:** Reverse in part and remand. The Court's practice is to review questions pressed or passed upon below (*United States v. Williams*); this one was neither, and *Williams* is not a rule excusing an affirmative waiver. Nothing here calls for the discretionary consideration of a disavowed argument recognized in *Lebron*: the Court of Appeals did not decide the question, nothing before the Court renews it, and deciding it would only resolve against McIver a point he does not dispute. Correcting the attachment ruling does not reopen it. Lower doctrine otherwise allows a purposeful information-gathering occupation of an effect without activating the Amendment.
* **Boundary or Reserved Question:** Stone does not decide prolonged aggregate monitoring, attachment with valid consent or a warrant, suppression without the ordinary causal and remedial inquiry, or the lawfulness of the short-term monitoring McIver did not contest.
