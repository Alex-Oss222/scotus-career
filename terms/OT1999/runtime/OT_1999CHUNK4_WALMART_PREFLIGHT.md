## Verified record and exact review boundaries

The entire primary Second Circuit majority, partial dissent and footnotes have been read from the saved opinion. Samara manufactures children's clothes; the core line is spring/summer seersucker garments. Wal-Mart ordered garments from Judy-Philippine, Inc., using samples that included Samara garments and photographs on which some Samara labels were visible. JPI copied sixteen garments with small changes; Wal-Mart sold them under its Small Steps label. Thirteen copied garments carried Samara copyright registrations. The lower opinion reports more than $1.15 million in Wal-Mart gross profits. Other retailers and JPI settled; Wal-Mart alone remained for trial. These are the lower court's record recitations, not new factfindings.

The jury found for Samara on copyright, §43(a), New York deceptive-trade and unfair-competition claims, and found willful infringement. The district court denied Wal-Mart's Rule 50 motion, granted an injunction, awarded fees and costs, and denied enhancement and prejudgment interest. The Second Circuit affirmed the denial of judgment as a matter of law on trade dress and copyright, rejected the labeling-instruction challenge as unpreserved (and without plain error), and affirmed rejection of copyright preemption of the state claims. It nevertheless reversed the injunction's overly broad terms and remanded for a narrower injunction and reduced damages.

For the trade-dress claim, the majority expressly states that no acquired secondary meaning is asserted; inherent distinctiveness is the contested route. It uses the source-identification question, whether the design is likely to be understood as an indicator of source, and holds the jury's verdict supported when the record is viewed favorably to Samara. It distinguishes the intent of a producer to maintain a recognizable line from purely aesthetic one-season designs, while requiring an articulated combination of elements. The designs include seersucker, boldly colored appliques commonly integrated with collars or pockets, largely undecorated backgrounds, and full-cut bodies. The majority relies on the combination and placement, not ownership of isolated fabric, fruit, flowers, colors, or applique techniques. It also considers testimony recognizing a Samara type of look. No consumer survey, secondary-meaning finding, or newly established design function may be invented.

The majority separately sustains the confusion determination through its circuit's intentional-deception presumption. It distinguishes mere deliberate competition from deception, but infers deceptive intent from the extent and detail of copying and the record of knowing procurement. Labels do not automatically rebut confusion. This is an independent infringement element; a conclusion about copying or confusion does not itself establish legally protectable distinctiveness. The granted product-design question does not automatically reopen that separate circuit doctrine.

The appellate remedy is material. The district injunction barred any garments copied from, bearing appliques copied from, or designed using any Samara garment; the appellate court found that broader than the protectable dress. It required a defined combination, allowed inevitable variations, and excluded two of the sixteen copied garments from trade-dress coverage. One excluded garment had a separately protected copyright, so its copyright damages remained; the other, bearing a circus-car applique, lacked a copyright registration and lost its $68,633.53 trade-dress damages. The opinion has a small internal inconsistency in the original trade-dress-total cents; no new total need be fixed to decide the granted legal question. A later judgment must preserve independent claims and remedy components unless a properly presented ground actually reaches them.

Judge Newman dissented on trade dress and five challenged copyrights, concurring otherwise. On trade dress he accepts that design can in principle identify source, but stresses the narrow range for lawful product-line protection, common elements and inconsistent combinations, and the danger of granting a continuing monopoly on ordinary styles. He would not let an unreasonable jury finding enlarge the statutory scope; he rejects simply lowering the ordinary deference to reasonable jury factfinding. His copyright originality disagreement is distinct from the granted trade-dress question.

## Law entering the event

The controlling reading copy is [Walmart entering law](../entering-law/OT_1999CHUNK4_WALMART_ENTERING_LAW.md). It contains the exact operative Standards entries, Holdings entries, and prior public writings, together with copied statute text. The governing Standards are **Inherently distinctive nonfunctional restaurant trade dress**, effective *Two Pesos*, June 26, 1992, and **Source-identifying color and functionality**, effective *Qualitex*, March 28, 1995. Their complete rules and limits, rather than a general label, govern.

Simulated *Two Pesos* protects inherently distinctive nonfunctional restaurant trade dress under the pre-1988 §43(a), without an additional acquired-meaning requirement, if the remaining infringement requirements are met. It explicitly leaves other product-design categories undecided. *Qualitex* holds a color eligible when it has acquired source significance and is nonfunctional; it does not protect bare color without secondary meaning or decide a general aesthetic-functionality rule. Under *Inwood*, secondary meaning concerns the primary significance to the public as identifying source, rather than merely identifying the product. Its functionality rule excludes a feature essential to use or purpose or affecting cost or quality. Source identification, nonfunctionality and likelihood of confusion remain independent.

The governing post-1988 §43(a)(1)(A) reaches words, terms, names, symbols, devices, combinations, or false designations used in commerce likely to confuse as to affiliation, connection, association, origin, sponsorship or approval. Section 1127 describes a trademark by its source-identifying function, including an unknown source. The words permit competing arguments about the conditions under which a design performs that function; neither categorical protection nor categorical exclusion follows merely from the label “design.” The exact text is copied, not reconstructed, in the slice.

Pub. L. 106-43 §5, approved August 5, 1999, adds §43(a)(3): in an action involving trade dress unregistered on the principal register, the claimant bears the burden of proving the protected matter is not functional. This is a nonfunctionality allocation, not an express answer to inherent distinctiveness. Its application to earlier conduct or trial burdens is unnecessary to the granted question unless a party-supported component makes it material; do not infer a new trial or a new finding from the amendment alone.

The Current-Term Neutral Projection through March 6, 2000 contains no identified alteration of these trademark propositions. Its *Weisgram* decision, February 22, preserves actual fair opportunity and supported new-trial grounds when an appellate court directs judgment after correction of an evidentiary error. That general civil-remedy caution does not supply a secondary-meaning allegation or compel a new trial here. ## Supported alternative paths and arguments

These are neutral analytical alternatives under Engine §14(4), not claims that unrecovered Supreme Court briefs used these words.

* A source-based application of *Two Pesos* could permit product-design inherent distinctiveness, subject to a demanding demonstration that the specified design immediately identifies source, independent of nonfunctionality and confusion. Samara's strongest argument is the statutory absence of a category bar, the protectable combination found by the jury, and the narrow appellate injunction that preserves competition. It must answer why aesthetic appeal, producer intent, extensive copying and product familiarity establish source significance rather than simply attractive products.
* A categorical secondary-meaning prerequisite for product design could distinguish designs ordinarily sought for their usefulness or appeal from word marks and packaging ordinarily serving to identify source. Wal-Mart's strongest argument is predictable protection for competitors, a source-function criterion, and the difficulty of defining inherent source significance from ordinary design combinations. It must explain why that category rule follows from the statute and existing precedents, why restaurant presentation differs, and why functionality and confusion alone are inadequate safeguards. A design can still acquire protection; no categorical exclusion of all design from trademark law follows.
* A narrower record-specific disposition could conclude this claimed line insufficiently distinctive even if some designs may qualify inherently. It must respect the actual Rule 50 standard and identify a legal insufficiency, rather than substitute new factfinding. The Newman dissent supports this route without supplying a categorical rule.
* If the legal prerequisite changes or the appellate analysis is defective, reversal or vacatur of the trade-dress ruling and a bounded remand are available. The lower opinion's statement that secondary meaning was not asserted must be retained; neither its proof nor a preserved request to develop it may be invented. The proper remaining procedure is an ordinary remand question, not an automatically ordered evidentiary hearing. Copyright and state claims, existing reductions, and fees attributable to different bases require their own legal treatment and cannot disappear through shorthand “reversed.”

No supported jurisdictional defect appears in the final appellate route. That observation does not adjudicate a hypothetical waiver or a claim outside the limited grant.
