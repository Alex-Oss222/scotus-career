## Governing changed premise

On April 28, 1999, all nine Justices joined Russell's requirement that a candidate-contribution limit be closely drawn to a sufficiently important corruption or appearance interest while preserving meaningful political association. They applied it to invalidate seven statewide $300 limits and two appellate-office $100 limits, considering campaign scale and actual burdens rather than creating a dollar floor. The five present sustaining Associates also sustained Russell's residual categorical challenge and particular PAC provisions on their operational records.

Kennedy's actual Russell separate writing, joined by Scalia and Thomas, did not demand expenditure scrutiny for direct candidate cash, completed bribery, equal source ceilings or a permanent floor. It instead found specific burdens inadequately fitted, preserved tailored conduit and earmark regulation, and rejected treating limited financing successes as an automatic cure. That is a substantive public position more directly applicable here than the historical Shrink opinions' proposed wholesale overruling of Buckley. It is not merely a requirement that dissenters obey a precedent they previously rejected.

## Full application preserved

Only the presented statewide/state-auditor ceiling is decided: $1,075, indexed biennially from the January 1, 1995 $1,000 base and rounded to the nearest $25. Other-office categories are background, although broader statewide experience may provide relevant evidence. The PAC had contributed $1,025 in 1997 and $50 in 1998 and wished to give more; Fredman's practical-capacity allegation remains part of the dispute.

The five sustaining Associates do not demand a completed exchange or a study identifying the uniquely correct dollar amount. Goode's account, the reported bank contribution and official banking choice, the auditor gifts and related concentrated-giving episodes support an objectively grounded concern about official obligation and its appearance. Allegations remain allegations; the conspiracy plea establishes its actual property-related offense only. Proposition A's 74-percent approval may bear on public perception but does not determine a constitutional right. Agreement with donors or unequal political resources alone would not suffice.

Those five also find the district court's affirmative practical-capacity and substantial-fundraising findings, the direct candidate channel and indexing sufficiently probative to sustain this ceiling. This is an assessment of the State's combined showing; the absence of demonstrated literal campaign impossibility is not their test. They expressly confront the drop in combined spending from $21.599 million to $9.337 million, primary spending from $14.249 million to $1.625 million, contested primaries from ten to one, and candidates from thirty-two to eleven. Those figures support a substantial burden argument, but the comparative record does not establish causation of each change by this limit or displace the district capacity finding for the presented application. They invent no alternative causal explanation and do not declare the decline harmless. The contributor-count figure of 97.62 percent below $2,000 measures neither dollars nor the funding effect of the actual $1,075 cap; it supplies no dispositive safe harbor.

The three opposing Associates accept a sufficient anticorruption interest but find the actual amount insufficiently fitted to statewide needs on this evidence. Large-gift episodes justify some prophylaxis without justifying this ceiling. The severe decline in funding and competition requires a stronger answer than general campaign-capacity findings, indexing or successful financing elsewhere. They do not require statistical certainty, exact proof of the optimal ceiling or a completed bribe. Their conclusion rejects this record's justification and does not create an unlimited-giving right, a constitutional dollar floor, or immunity for actual conduits.
