## C. ISSUE POSITIONS

### Issue 1: Did Due Process require parole-ineligibility information

* **Threshold and Merits Reach:** Stone reaches the merits. The federal application was filed February 14, 1997, so amended § 2254(d) applies. The Supreme Court of Virginia decided this federal claim on its merits on November 4, 1994; no procedural bar rests on it. The § 2254(d)(1) inquiry uses the law clearly established by this Court on that date, including *Simmons* (June 17, 1994), *Gardner*, *Skipper* and *Ramos*. *Allridge*, *O'Dell*, *Calderon v. Coleman* and *Weeks* are later and are not treated as clearly established then. *Williams v. Taylor* (Terry Williams) supplies the present construction of § 2254(d)(1); Stone relies on its common holdings and adopts neither four-Justice general allocation of the two clauses.
* **Stone's Legal Position:** Vacate and remand. Simulated *Simmons* and *Allridge* ordinarily require legally fixed parole ineligibility and reject speculation about future parole-board decisions, but this case may present a different defect: the State made the robbery underlying an already returned guilty verdict, and Ramdass's trial on it, part of its case for future dangerousness while withholding the legal consequence of that same verdict. Under *Simmons*'s existing effective-opportunity principle, formal-entry timing cannot conclusively defeat the claim, because it would otherwise let the State create a materially false sentencing premise; the principle does not reach verdicts facing a genuine unresolved merits issue. Whether the Domino's proceeding had reached a completed substantive adjudication with only entry of judgment remaining, whether § 2254(d)(1) permits relief, any *Teague* question and the error's effect are for the lower courts.
* **Stone's Proposed Holding:** When the State places future dangerousness at issue, the absence of formal entry of judgment on a separate guilty verdict does not by itself defeat a claim that due process required a truthful account of that verdict's conditional parole consequence. A case in which substantive adjudication was complete and only entry remained may require that account; mere expectation of conviction, confidence in guilt or a favorable release prediction does not.
* **Essential Reasoning and Record Application:** Vacate and remand. * **Boundary or Reserved Question:** The rule does not require predictions about discretionary parole, treat every unentered verdict as final, or apply when substantive postverdict alteration is genuinely in dispute; the conviction remains untouched. The required information is the accurate conditional consequence of the returned verdict, that entry of judgment on it will make the defendant ineligible for parole; the rule does not permit telling a jury that a defendant is already ineligible when he is not. Stone does not decide the separate claim that Virginia arbitrarily manipulated its own law, or any separate claim about the April 2 judicial sentencing.
