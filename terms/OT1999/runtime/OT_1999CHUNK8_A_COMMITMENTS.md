## Bradley v. Arkansas Department of Education — June 12, 2000

### Review and sufficient common ground

All eight Associates would **affirm the IDEA immunity-denial judgment and remand for ordinary proceedings**, on Arkansas's actual acceptance of an identified federal program subject to an unambiguous federal-suit condition. This affirms only the granted Bradley component. It does not adjudicate Jim C.'s Section 504 component, IDEA compliance, exhaustion, a particular officer decree, personal liability, or any award. The Bradleys request prospective compliance relief, not compensatory or punitive damages.

The operative rule each would join is: when a State voluntarily accepts IDEA funds after the effective express federal-suit provision, sections 1403 and 1415 give unmistakable notice that the State accepts federal judicial enforcement of the program's obligations. Arkansas's established acceptance under that disclosed program condition supplies consent to this IDEA suit. This is an actual choice to take a specifically offered benefit, not consent inferred from carrying on otherwise lawful ordinary commerce. Section 1403(b) provides remedy parity only for remedies otherwise available under IDEA, and subsection (c) preserves its post-October-30-1990 violation boundary. The remaining statutory cause, administrative predicates and remedy conditions retain independent effect.

The notice question concerns judicial enforcement, not just awareness of substantive education duties. Section 1403 expressly identifies the State, federal court, the Act's violation, and removal of Eleventh Amendment immunity; section 1415 supplies the civil-action and jurisdiction channels within the funded program. Congress's choice of an abrogation heading does not conceal the plainly stated consequence attached to participating in this voluntarily accepted program. Atascadero's rejection of consent from receipt of federal funds without clear federal-suit notice is thus satisfied, not displaced. Dellmuth explains why Congress supplied the express language. College Savings's ordinary-commerce holding leaves this distinct accepted-benefit question open. Dole permits related funding conditions; judicial enforcement of IDEA safeguards directly concerns the funded education program. Arkansas conceded spending-power validity and receipt. Nothing supplied establishes coercive circumstances, nonreceipt, or a new ground for avoiding that actual acceptance.

### Enhanced alternatives and independent commitments

Path A's strongest objection is that attaching every consequence in a statute to funding could become constructive waiver. The adopted boundary is an identified program, actual postcondition acceptance, and explicit State/federal-suit language; ordinary federal regulatory coverage and generalized receipt of federal money are insufficient.

## Carter v. United States, No. 99-5716 — June 12, 2000

### Review and judgment positions

All eight Associates would **affirm rejection of Carter's requested felony-grade bank-larceny instruction**, but only four presently support the broader grounds excluding both grades of subsection (b). Four would affirm on the value element alone and reserve the broader questions. These are direct conclusions about this request; no nine-Justice coalition or authorship is supplied.

The common eight-Associate proposition is: subsection 2113(b)'s grade carrying a ten-year maximum requires the value of the property to exceed $1,000. That is an element of the aggravated larceny grade, not merely an instruction to a sentencing judge after conviction of an undifferentiated theft offense. The charged first-paragraph robbery offense under subsection (a) requires no such value threshold. Under Schmuck's elements comparison, the requested felony larceny grade therefore is not necessarily included in that robbery offense. Proof that Carter actually took far more, or the indictment's allegation of approximately $14,000, does not supply an element missing from the statutory greater offense.

The enacted provision embeds the value distinction in the conduct description and repeats a lower grade with a one-year maximum. Jones, Almendarez-Torres and Castillo supply statutory aggravated-offense analogues, with ordinary charging and jury consequences; they do not supply a universal constitutional rule. The much different maximum corroborates the offense-grade reading but does not decide it alone. Bell's enlargement of the theft modes does not erase the express grading distinction. Rule 31(c) compares construed elements, not just the names robbery and larceny or the evidence in this trial.

Carter's actual requested charge required value above the then-obsolete $100 figure and expressly sought conviction of that felony-grade larceny. The proper 1997 statutory figure is $1,000. The judgment does not turn on punishing counsel for the stale number; even the corrected felony instruction contains an additional statutory element. The record supplies no distinct requested instruction limited to the lower grade. The general argument that bank larceny is included does not require the Court to award a different unrequested instruction without examining its actual scope and the separate rational-jury requirement. The value-only Justices therefore leave lower-grade inclusion and any properly presented entitlement to such a charge unresolved. They do not hold that proving value of $1,000 or less is necessarily an affirmative element of every lower-grade conviction.

O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy and Thomas would also hold that subsection (b) requires intent to steal or purloin and taking plus carrying away, while the charged taking offense under subsection (a) requires knowing conduct satisfying its forcible-or-intimidating taking elements but not those additional requirements. On that construction neither larceny grade is necessarily included. This is their additional ground, not an eight-Associate holding. They would not rely on the mere existence of an attempt clause to resolve the completed taking charged here, or import the second paragraph's express entry intent into the first paragraph. Their mens rea construction retains culpability; it is not strict liability for an innocent accidental act.

Stevens, Souter, Ginsburg and Breyer do not join those additional grounds. They find substantial support in Morissette, the original offense's common-law setting and the asserted stylistic 1948 revision for construing the taking offense to contain theft purpose, and in the relationship between completed taking and slight movement for treating carrying away as already implicit. They reserve a conclusive holding on those questions because value independently resolves the actual felony request. This reservation is not affirmative assent to the broader noninclusion proposition, nor a holding that all forms of bank larceny are included.

## Pegram v. Herdrich, No. 98-1949 — June 12, 2000

### Review, claim and sufficient common ground

All eight Associates would **reverse the Seventh Circuit's revival of the reviewed amended ERISA claim and remand for dismissal of that claim on the grounds below**. The malpractice judgment remains undisturbed. The reviewed count seeks plan-focused relief under sections 409 and 502(a)(2), not simply compensation for personal medical injury. No individual physician is named in that amended count; no new individual claim or pure nondisclosure count is supplied.

Each would join the following bounded propositions, which perform different work:

1. Fiduciary status follows the particular statutory function, not the entity's title or its fiduciary status in other dealings. A physician's ordinary treatment decision, including a medical-necessity or referral decision inseparable from furnishing that treatment, does not become discretionary plan administration merely because it also affects what care an HMO supplies. The rule concerns care functions, not a categorical HMO exemption. A separate discretionary decision applying plan coverage terms can be fiduciary administration, including when a physician or HMO performs it.
2. Establishing or implementing ordinary general physician compensation and risk sharing for furnishing medical care does not itself establish fiduciary conduct or a breach of loyalty to an ERISA plan. An HMO is not the employer's ERISA plan merely because it supplies the benefits; its business revenues are not automatically plan assets. An independently supported act of actual plan-asset control would require its own statutory analysis, without an extra discretion condition on the asset-disposition branch.
3. The amended count's separate nonroutine-claims allegations can describe discretionary administration, but its assertion of breach remains the general year-end incentive arrangement. Accepting the pleaded facts, that arrangement alone does not establish administration contrary to the plan, direct reward for denying a claim, or an independently disloyal determination. The lower court's assertion that medically necessary care was withheld solely to enlarge bonuses is not a distinct pleaded finding that converts the ordinary arrangement into such a claim. The pleaded recovery of the supplemental medical-expense payments also cannot rest simply on labeling the entire HMO the plan. No finding is made that every possible premium arrangement is outside plan assets, or that all incentive arrangements are lawful.

Varity requires identifying the actual administrative function before applying loyalty; its deliberate deception involved an independently established fiduciary act. Spink and Hughes Aircraft separate design and genuine benefit payments from actual implementation, without immunizing the latter. John Hancock separates asset status, function, breach and relief. These authorities supply no rule that all incentive-compatible medical business is fiduciary disloyalty, and no rule that calling a coverage decision medical removes it from section 3(21)(A). Firestone permits the coexistence of benefits discretion and a financial interest while retaining review of actual abuse. Mertens's section 502(a)(3) compensation limit is not the ground for rejecting this section 409/502(a)(2) count. Neither the HMO Act's recognition of managed care nor ERISA preemption independently supplies blanket immunity.

### Enhanced alternatives and independent commitments

Path A treats the challenged treatment and general care-compensation functions as nonfiduciary while examining actual administration separately. Its strongest counterargument is that discretionary medical necessity can decide entitlement and the amended count expressly identifies nonroutine claims. The answer is to preserve genuine coverage administration and address that allegation on its own insufficient-breach ground, rather than treating every HMO act as care. Path B treats the complete incentive arrangement as fiduciary disloyalty because the same owners decide claims and receive savings. Its strongest objection is that it identifies no independent statutory breach beyond ordinary compensation and risk sharing and assumes the HMO's payments are plan losses. An ownership incentive can matter to a properly pleaded disloyal act; ownership plus a general bonus is not that act. The actual pleading is decisive, not a policy assessment of managed care.

## Reeves v. Sanderson Plumbing Products, Inc., No. 99-536 — June 12, 2000

### Review and sufficient common ground

All eight Associates would **reverse the appellate judgment granting the employer judgment as a matter of law and remand for remaining properly preserved issues**. The age-discrimination verdict is supported by legally sufficient evidence. The Court should not itself award a new amount, conclusively resolve an unreviewed new-trial ground, or treat the Rule 50 holding as adjudication of every remedy issue.

The rule each would join is that a prima facie case, together with evidence permitting rejection of the employer's proffered material reason, can support a finding of intentional discrimination without an additional mandatory category of direct or independent proof. It does not always compel that finding, and it does not invariably defeat judgment as a matter of law. The strength of the initial inference, the proof of falsity and the rest of the properly considered record govern ordinary sufficiency. A record conclusively establishing another nondiscriminatory reason, or only weak falsity evidence together with otherwise conclusive independent proof against discrimination, may fail; a merely imaginable other motive does not conclusively defeat an inference the jury reasonably drew.

Rule 50 requires consideration of the record as a whole with reasonable inferences for the verdict winner, without credibility choices or weighing competing evidence. The court may not credit disputed testimony for the moving employer simply because it was offered, nor discard every item the employer offered merely because of its source. Uncontradicted and unimpeached evidence a reasonable jury would have to accept retains effect; evidence from disinterested witnesses may supply that circumstance, while witness interest, contradiction and the reasonable competing inferences remain material. The standard is what the jury was legally entitled to accept or reject, not a categorical witness-label rule.

Here the jury could find the recordkeeping explanation materially false from Reeves's testimony and the records practices, and could connect the age-directed comments to the discharge through Chesnut's demonstrated role and influence. The employer's disputes about timing, meaning, the other participants and the older workforce are evidence for the jury, not categorical exclusions. Oswalt's earlier departure is not an actual younger-supervisor discharge; the claim that he would have been discharged does not require disbelief of the verdict. This record supports actual prohibited age motivation under Hazen Paper, not liability merely for unfairness. Hicks already rejects a mandatory pretext-plus category and automatic liability; Burdine's ultimate employee persuasion remains. Consolidated Coin rejects use of the protected age group's boundary as a proxy for the relevant inference. Weisgram confirms the appellate power to enter Rule 50 judgment but supplies no power to reweigh this valid record.

### Enhanced alternatives and independent commitments

Path A sustains the reasonable verdict inference on the combined evidence. Its strongest objection is that a false business explanation can conceal animosity or another lawful reason and that the comments may lack a sufficient nexus. The response is record-specific: no conclusive different motive is established, and the comments plus influence and falsity permit the causal connection. Path B sustains the employer's judgment on the whole record while retaining formal permission to infer from pretext. It is lawful in a genuinely insufficient case, but fails here because it must resolve disputed testimony and discount the very inferences a reasonable jury could draw. Neither side's categorical shortcut is accepted.
