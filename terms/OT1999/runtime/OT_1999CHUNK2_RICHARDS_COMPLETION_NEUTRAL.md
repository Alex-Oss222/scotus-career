### Issue 1: Feres and the actual service connection

* **Controlling Simulated Law:** Minns v. United States (February 25, 1999) leaves Feres, Stencel Aero, United States v. Shearer and United States v. Johnson in force. Its whole-dismissal judgment rests on nonnested grounds: only two judgment supporters relied on Feres, and no majority creates a new rule covering every injury traceable to military service.
* **Current Reusable Doctrine:** Apply the existing incident-to-service inquiry to the relationship between the claimant’s injury and military service. Military status, location, duty circumstances and activity matter; neither the identity of the tortfeasor nor the presence or absence of an assigned task alone supplies the whole inquiry.
* **Other Governing Law:** 28 U.S.C. §§1346(b), 2671–2680; Feres v. United States, 340 U.S. 135 (1950). Brooks v. United States, 337 U.S. 49 (1949), preserves a servicemember highway claim outside the relevant service relationship; United States v. Brown, 348 U.S. 110 (1954), concerns a distinct postservice wrong. Neither establishes that all road travel by a servicemember is outside Feres.
* **Open Point:** Whether this particular trip was incident to service on the complete record. A public highway does not resolve that issue, but active-duty enlistment alone does not erase Brooks.

### Issue 2: The distinct statutory exceptions

* **Controlling Simulated Law:** Minns’s eight-Justice protected-policy proposition under §2680(a) applies to actual choice susceptible to protected policy judgment, not every military act. Its separate Feres reasoning is not a majority discretionary-function rule.
* **Current Reusable Doctrine:** A policy-selection defense must identify protected discretion in the alleged conduct. Ordinary vehicle operation does not become protected policy solely because the truck belongs to the Army.
* **Other Governing Law:** Berkovitz v. United States, 486 U.S. 531 (1988); United States v. Gaubert, 499 U.S. 315 (1991).
* **Open Point:** The supplied question does not establish an independent discretionary-function defense, tort liability or damages.

## C. DECISION-MATERIAL LITIGATION POSITIONS

* **Controlling reasoning below:** The supplied account identifies Feres as the ground for the affirmed dismissal. It does not reproduce the full incident-to-service findings; the brief must not silently add an on-duty or furlough finding.
* **Petitioner’s or appellant’s strongest supported position:** The alleged negligence concerns ordinary highway driving and the private was performing no assigned task. Public access and state road maintenance support treating the accident as an ordinary tort, as in Brooks, rather than review of military commands.
* **Respondent’s or appellee’s strongest supported position:** Feres is not confined to performance of a specific command or to claims that directly challenge discipline. An active-duty member’s travel connected with service may remain incident to service on a public road; routine negligence and the civilian character of a road do not create an exception.
* **Necessary concessions and limiting principles:** Brooks prevents an all-servicemembers bar, while Feres and Johnson prevent an assigned-task-only test. The complete service relationship, not a preferred tort result, must determine the application.
