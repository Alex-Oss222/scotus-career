## Bradley v. Arkansas Department of Education

### Identity, posture, and scope

June 12, 2000 merits event under the recorded simulation grant of March 20 and submission of April 25. Review is confined to the IDEA immunity questions identified in the original neutral packet. The lower opinion is 189 F.3d 745 (8th Cir. August 31, 1999), on interlocutory appeal from the Eastern District of Arkansas, No. LR-C-96-1004, order of November 21, 1997. The relevant appellate docket is 98-1010. The accompanying Jim C. docket, 98-1830, is not an additional granted judgment component.

The State, representing the Arkansas Department of Education and employee Mike Crowley, sought dismissal or summary judgment on sovereign-immunity grounds. The District Court denied relief under a Section 5 theory and stayed the merits pending appeal. The Eighth Circuit affirmed the denial of immunity for the IDEA claim on funding-conditioned waiver grounds, rejecting its earlier categorical Section 5 analysis. It also recognized prospective officer relief and stated that the State’s waiver removed the same official-capacity immunity asserted by Crowley. These are lower-court grounds to review, not declarations of Supreme Court law.

The caption names the Bradleys as plaintiffs; the immunity appeal was brought by Arkansas. Do not turn the abbreviated case name into a finding that the Bradleys seek reversal of the favorable denial of immunity. The supplied generic “petitioner seeks reversal” language does not identify a new claimant or broader scope.

### Supported material record and limitations

The United States’ actual intervention brief, pp. 4–5, identifies allegations concerning completion of hearings within 45 days and a mechanism for compelling witnesses. Those are allegations and legal contentions; this immunity appeal resolves neither their truth nor the entitlement to an educational remedy.

### Exact statutory basis

Public Law 105-17, 111 Stat. 47, section 604, codified at 20 U.S.C. §1403, contains three distinct provisions: express exclusion of state Eleventh Amendment immunity from federal suit for an IDEA violation; remedies at law and equity only to the same extent available against another public entity; and application to violations occurring in whole or part after the enactment of the 1990 amendments, October 30, 1990. The parity language does not create a substantive category of relief absent from IDEA.

Section 615(a), 20 U.S.C. §1415(a), requires a recipient state educational agency, state agency, or local educational agency to maintain the procedural safeguards. Section 615(i)(2) authorizes an aggrieved party’s civil action after the specified administrative decisions and appeal path, in a competent state court or federal district court without an amount-in-controversy requirement. Section 615(i)(3)(A) separately supplies district-court jurisdiction. Section 615(i)(2)(B) directs receipt of the administrative record, additional evidence at a party’s request, and appropriate relief based on the preponderance of the evidence. None of these provisions dispenses with its own administrative predicates merely because immunity fails. Section 615(l) preserves other federal rights but requires exhaustion to the same extent as IDEA for a civil action under those laws seeking relief also available under IDEA. The exact extracts preserve the qualifications; the immunity appeal need not decide exhaustion on this undeveloped record.

### Supported competing positions

Distinguish assent to educational obligations from assent to a judicial enforcement condition. The Section 5 theory must identify constitutional wrongs and fit, rather than treating every violation of FAPE or procedural regulation as a constitutional violation. The record’s concessions concerning spending validity and actual acceptance constrain any new broad coercion or nonreceipt contention. No unsupported funding-dependence facts establish coercion here.

**Bradleys and United States:** Sections 1403 and 1415 together expressly disclose private federal judicial enforcement of the program’s obligations. Arkansas chose the program after that disclosure; this differs from inferring consent solely from participation in ordinary commerce. On Section 5, IDEA responds to exclusion of disabled children and institutional denial of meaningful process; prophylactic protection can exceed the constitutional minimum without creating a new constitutional right. The United States actually advanced both funding waiver and Section 5 arguments below. Its brief relies on Rowley, Mills, PARC, Cleburne, and the congressional record of educational exclusion, and does not rely solely on educational equality as an abstract slogan.

**Independent officer path:** If the State cannot be directly sued, a connected official may be subject to genuinely prospective relief against an ongoing federal violation, within the federal duty and authorized remedy. The claim’s merits and the exact decree remain for further proceedings.

## Carter v. United States, No. 99-5716

### Identity, posture, and record

Argued April 19, 2000; decision event June 12. Certiorari to the Third Circuit, judgment order reported at 185 F.3d 863 (1999), affirming a District of New Jersey bank-robbery conviction under the first paragraph of 18 U.S.C. §2113(a). The Court reviews whether §2113(b) is a necessarily included offense under Rule 31(c). The District Court denied the requested larceny instruction as a matter of law under United States v. Mosley, 126 F.3d 200 (3d Cir. 1997). The simulated Supreme Court Mosley action resolved no elements question.

On September 9, 1997, Carter entered a bank wearing a ski mask, crossed a teller counter, took approximately $16,000, and fled. He admitted taking the money and disputed the force/intimidation element. The briefs differ on his contact with a departing customer: Carter’s account describes an accidental bump and avoidance of threats; the Government describes pushing her back into the bank and frightening those present. Do not silently resolve that dispute by borrowing a later opinion’s narrative. An elements ruling can avoid the disputed trial application.

The indictment, JA2–3, alleges a knowing and willful taking by force, violence, and intimidation of approximately $14,000 from a teller’s person and presence, from an FDIC-insured bank; the trial account describes the larger amount actually taken. The indictment amount and trial amount are not interchangeable. No value dispute material to the greater offense is identified.

The requested instruction is preserved at JA4–9. Its proposed value element says “more than $100,” copied from an older instruction source. The applicable 1997 statute had a $1,000 threshold after the 1996 amendment. The legal question is not answered by repeating that obsolete number. The trial docket at JA1 dates the verdict January 23, 1998; a sentence in petitioner’s merits brief says 1999, inconsistent with its own chronology. Use the docket. The robbery sentence was 215 months; separate Pennsylvania larceny pleas were consolidated for sentencing and received concurrent terms. They are not additional convictions under review for this instruction issue.

### Governing text and separable issues

The official 1998 United States Code text is saved in `2113-1998.txt` and copied into the exact excerpts. Section 2113(a)’s first paragraph covers taking or attempting to take bank property by force and violence or intimidation, and obtaining or attempting to obtain it by extortion; the separate entry paragraph includes an express intent to commit a qualifying felony or larceny. Do not attribute the second paragraph’s express intent wording to the charged first-paragraph offense. Maximum imprisonment is twenty years.

Section 2113(b) requires taking and carrying away with intent to steal or purloin. Its first paragraph specifies value exceeding $1,000 and a ten-year maximum; its second paragraph covers value not exceeding $1,000 and carries the stated one-year maximum and fine. An analysis that a value exceeding $1,000 is an additional felony element does not, standing alone, resolve whether the lower-grade larceny is included. Specific intent and carrying away must be considered independently if used to reject both grades.

Rule 31(c)’s elements inquiry under Schmuck, 489 U.S. 705 (1989), compares legally construed statutory elements; factual overlap alone is insufficient. Even a legally included offense requires evidence permitting a rational jury to convict of the lesser while acquitting of the greater. Statutory inclusion and factual entitlement are distinct steps; a decision resolving the first need not decide the second. No present need arises to decide every extortion, attempt, entry, or other lesser-offense configuration.

### Competing arguments and limits

**Carter:** The statutory language, title, common-law relation of robbery to larceny, and amendment history preserve robbery as aggravated larceny. Morissette’s presumption concerning established common-law crimes supports reading a theft intent into the taking offense; deletion of “feloniously” in 1948 was stylistic rather than a deliberate element change. “Takes” can carry the ordinary common-law requirement of slight movement. The elements test requires interpretation, not a count of differently worded clauses. His original felony-value theory treats the threshold as sentencing; a distinct lesser-grade argument does not depend on proving the amount exceeds $1,000. The precise preservation and requested-instruction scope should be considered before granting relief on that alternative, rather than assumed.

**United States:** Congress separately wrote specific theft intent, carrying away, and value into subsection (b), and omitted them from the charged part of subsection (a). The latter already describes culpable knowing conduct involving force or intimidation; a mens rea presumption need not add a specific intent to steal to criminalize otherwise innocent behavior. Bell, 462 U.S. 356 (1983), cautions against treating subsection (b) as merely a codification of common-law larceny. Jerome, 318 U.S. 101 (1943), and Prince, 352 U.S. 322 (1957), do not erase the text. The Government’s actual merits brief independently argues insufficiency of the evidentiary basis for the proposed lesser instruction; accepting its statutory ground would avoid that disputed application.

A broader reading of common-law continuity and a text-based distinction are both analytical paths. An inclusion holding would not itself require acquittal or decide whether all evidence supports a lesser instruction; the usual remedial alternative is vacatur/reversal with appropriate further proceedings. A noninclusion holding would leave the conviction intact without endorsing every trial instruction or independent issue.

## Pegram v. Herdrich, No. 98-1949

### Identity, posture, and claim

Argued February 23, 2000; June 12 merits event. Certiorari to the Seventh Circuit, 154 F.3d 362 (1998), which reversed dismissal of an ERISA fiduciary-breach count. The reviewing stage is failure to state a claim, not trial findings on that ERISA theory.

State Farm offered its employees an HMO option; Herdrich participated through her husband. HAMP, the HMO insurer, was owned through Carle Clinic, whose physician shareholders provided care. The subscription documents made medical necessity a matter of Carle physicians’ judgment and generally required referral for outside diagnostic and treatment services. Alleged physician incentives rewarded lower testing, outside-facility use, and referrals. Do not describe the HMO corporation itself, all its revenue, or its entire business as an ERISA plan merely because it supplied the plan’s benefits.

Herdrich alleged delay of an ultrasound after discovery of an inflamed abdominal mass and a required trip to a distant Carle facility. Her appendix ruptured, causing peritonitis. The state malpractice claims proceeded to a jury judgment of $35,000 in her favor. That judgment is outside this fiduciary-status review. The ERISA count seeks plan-focused recovery for the alleged incentive arrangement under §409/§502(a)(2); it is not simply a second damages demand for her physical injury. The amended count does not name an individual doctor as a defendant. Herdrich’s actual respondent brief emphasizes both structural incentives and administration of disputed/nonroutine benefit claims.

Two state-law fraud counts led to removal. One was dismissed; the other was amended as the ERISA count. The actual amended count, not the superseded fraud allegations, controls whether a nondisclosure claim is presented. The respondent invokes prior defense statements that HAMP was an administrator/fiduciary and argues judicial estoppel; classification of the particular challenged function remains different from whether an entity performs fiduciary functions elsewhere. No general malpractice-preemption or independent disclosure ruling is automatically within the question.

### Statute and competing readings

ERISA §3(21)(A), 29 U.S.C. §1002(21)(A), is functional and limited “to the extent” of the specified conduct: discretionary plan-management authority or control; authority or control over asset management or disposition; compensated investment advice; and discretionary authority or responsibility in plan administration. The asset-disposition branch is not rewritten to require additional discretion. Subparagraph (B)’s registered-investment-company qualification is retained in the exact text but is not a factual issue here.

Section 404(a), 29 U.S.C. §1104(a), imposes loyalty and prudence on fiduciary functions subject to its enacted qualifications; it does not make every medically discretionary act fiduciary. Section 409, §1109, addresses responsibility and restoration to the plan for breach. Section 502(a)(2), §1132(a)(2), provides the corresponding authorized plaintiff channel. Section 502(a)(3) is a distinct equitable channel, subject to Varity and Mertens, not an automatic substitute for a deficient plan-loss claim.

**Carle:** Plan design and the choice of an HMO are sponsor acts; provision of medical treatment and ordinary medical-business choices are not discretionary administration merely because they affect benefits. A physician making a medically inseparable eligibility/treatment determination acts in providing care. Financial risk sharing is part of HMO structure, which Congress recognizes. The existence of an ownership interest or general profit incentive is not by itself an ERISA loyalty breach. Plan assets, actual fiduciary function, breach, and statutory remedy each require separate predicates.

**Herdrich:** The employer’s HMO selection does not give a non-sponsor administrator freedom to distribute benefit resources through self-interested decisions. Carle decides medical necessity, coverage of disputed claims, and referrals, and allegedly directs money toward the same doctors who decide what care participants receive. A functional statute can reach those acts without treating every medical judgment or every HMO as unlawful. The complaint should receive its liberal pleading construction. The HMO Act does not expressly approve every undisclosed or treatment-specific incentive arrangement. The actual brief also contests Carle’s attempt to characterize membership, rather than care, as the benefit.

**United States/Labor Department, actual amicus:** Supports reversal but distinguishes treatment allegations from administration allegations. Medical treatment and compensation for its provision are not fiduciary administration. Pure discretionary coverage determinations can be fiduciary. A general profit-sharing arrangement alone does not show a breach; a direct per-denied-claim payment or an arrangement making impartial application of the plan impossible presents a different issue. It also contests treating all HMO premiums and year-end profits as plan assets. This is a materially narrower route than categorical immunity for every HMO administrative act.

The lower majority expressly disclaimed automatic breach from every incentive arrangement and described withholding medically necessary care solely to enlarge bonuses. Its reasoning and the pleading’s actual allegations must both be identified; neither that judicial characterization nor the malpractice verdict establishes a plan-asset loss. The distinct statutory-functions and insufficient-breach-pleading paths can lead to different holdings and reservations even if they share a judgment.

## Ramdass v. Angelone, No. 99-7000

### Identity, chronology, and review constraints

Argued April 18, 2000; June 12 merits event. Certiorari to the Fourth Circuit, 187 F.3d 396 (1999), reviewing federal habeas relief under 28 U.S.C. §2254. The District Court, 28 F. Supp. 2d 343 (E.D. Va. 1998), granted sentencing relief; the Fourth Circuit reversed that relief. This is not direct review of the murder conviction and is not an unrestricted determination of Virginia law.

The capital charge concerns the September 2, 1992 murder of store clerk Mohammed Kayani during robbery. Relevant proceedings:

| Proceeding | Material date and status at the capital jury’s deliberations |
|---|---|
| Pizza Hut armed robberies | Jury verdict December 15, 1992; judgments and sentences January 22, 1993. Related counts in one transaction count as one qualifying strike. |
| Domino’s armed robbery | Jury found guilt and recommended eighteen years January 7, 1993; final trial judgment and sentence entered February 18, 1993. |
| Kayani capital murder | Guilt verdict January 28; penalty hearing January 29; jury death recommendation January 30, 1993. |
| Judicial sentencing in Kayani case | April 2, 1993 hearing, after entry of the Domino’s judgment. The judge retained authority to impose life despite the jury recommendation. |

The 1988 robbery does not count as an armed-robbery strike; its nonqualification is not challenged in this Supreme Court issue. Other crimes were described at sentencing, but later guilty pleas or convictions are not silently treated as completed at the jury hearing.

The state supreme court first rejected the parole-information claim under its then-existing rule against parole information; after reconsideration in light of Simmons it held that Domino’s did not yet count because no judgment had been entered. It treated the Kayani verdict, assuming a life sentence on it, as the second qualifying conviction. The specific federal question concerns what due process requires in that setting; an affirmative federal rule need not reinterpret the state statute itself.

The federal proceeding was filed in 1996 after AEDPA’s enactment; §2254(d)(1) therefore requires its own analysis. The actual simulated Terry Williams rule supplies the current meaning of contrary-to and unreasonable-application review. The existing simulated O’Dell rule addresses retroactivity of the confined opportunity-to-answer protection; it does not dispense with §2254(d), legal eligibility, effective presentation, or error consequences. Do not import the historical Fourth Circuit’s displaced “reasonable jurists” or historical O’Dell premises as controlling law.

### State law and supported trial record

Virginia Code §53.1-151(B1) excludes parole for three separate qualifying murder, rape, or armed-robbery offenses, or their combination, when they are not part of a common act, transaction, or scheme. Section (C) ordinarily makes a first life sentence parole-eligible after fifteen years, but specifies twenty-five years for the identified Class 1 felony or child-murder category, unless (B1) or (B2) excludes parole. The minimum-time provisions do not themselves make ordinary parole permanently unavailable.

Rule 3A:15(a) permits an insufficiency motion after the Commonwealth rests or all evidence ends and identifies a hung-jury renewal. Subsection (b) separately permits a motion, no later than twenty-one days after final order, to set aside a guilty verdict for trial error or legally insufficient evidence. Subsection (c) requires acquittal for insufficiency and a new trial for the other setting-aside ground. The full exact text is copied from the petitioner’s appendix; subsection (a) does not eliminate subsection (b).

The Commonwealth sought death on future dangerousness. Domino’s was first introduced in the defense presentation. The prosecutor then cross-examined Ramdass about the Domino’s armed robbery, his trial there, and his prior denial under oath; Ramdass admitted the robbery and prior false denial (JA57–59). In closing the prosecutor expressly included the Domino’s armed robbery in the violent sequence supporting future danger (JA81). This is documented use of the crime and trial history. It is not proof that a final Domino’s judgment was introduced or that the prosecutor legally needed a final conviction to use the conduct.

Defense counsel told the jury Ramdass would never leave prison, relying on multiple sentences and pending proceedings. The jury asked whether a life sentence allowed parole before natural death. The court instructed it not to concern itself with what might happen later. The precise requested response and actual response appear at JA88–91 and are extracted, rather than replaced with a generic request. The express three-strikes theory was argued to the judge at the April sentencing hearing. The state court addressed the federal claim on its merits; do not invent a new default solely because trial counsel used a different formulation. A separate challenge accusing the Virginia court of arbitrary state-law manipulation raised additional preservation issues below and must not be conflated with the presented Simmons claim.

At judicial sentencing, counsel reported conversations with unnamed jurors about how parole information would have affected them. Those unsworn reports are not a finding about the jury’s actual deliberations and are unnecessary to establish that the jury itself asked the parole question.

### Competing arguments and actual uncertainty

**Ramdass:** The federal opportunity to answer a future-danger case should depend on the legally certain sentencing alternative rather than an outstanding ministerial entry. Domino’s had already produced a guilty verdict and recommended sentence; the trial judge had rejected sufficiency attacks, according to petitioner’s brief. Entry followed before the capital judge imposed sentence. The Pizza Hut conviction also remained within a postjudgment challenge period, so hypothetical reversal possibilities do not distinguish the cases. The prosecutor used the very conduct giving rise to the additional strike. Gardner and Skipper support an effective response to the release premise, and the jury’s question identifies its relevance.

**Commonwealth:** Virginia law authoritatively requires a final judgment for that separate strike. A life sentence on Kayani would itself have supplied only the second completed qualifying conviction at the relevant jury stage. Domino’s was still subject to trial-court action; evidence of a crime may support dangerousness without qualifying as a parole-ineligibility conviction. Extending Simmons to anticipated convictions requires prediction and goes beyond its fixed-law condition. Even if such an extension is permissible on direct review, §2254(d)(1) independently constrains federal habeas relief. Defense counsel’s actual presentation and the full sentencing record also matter to any claimed denial of an effective opportunity or harmfulness.

The phrase “all substantive adjudication has occurred” in the original neutral packet is a disputed legal characterization, not an established record finding. Petitioner’s brief acknowledges Rule 3A:15(b) power and argues that it was only hypothetical in this case; the Commonwealth disputes calling judgment ministerial. The supplied material does not establish a judicial finding that no substantive defect or motion could exist. Conversely, legal availability of postverdict relief does not establish an actual meritorious defect. A decision may resolve the constitutional trigger on supported facts and the accepted state-law status without inventing either proposition. Any rule conditioned on actual practical certainty must state how that condition is met or leave its application for further proceedings; it cannot be certified solely from a party’s “no basis” assertion.

## Reeves v. Sanderson Plumbing Products, Inc., No. 99-536

### Identity, posture, record, and questions

Argued March 21, 2000; June 12 merits event. Certiorari to the Fifth Circuit, 197 F.3d 688 (1999), which displaced a jury’s ADEA verdict for Roger Reeves on Rule 50 sufficiency grounds. Review concerns the permissible discrimination inference from prima facie evidence and disbelief of the employer’s account, and how to examine the whole record on judgment as a matter of law.

Reeves was fifty-seven, had worked for the company for forty years, and supervised the Hinge Room’s regular production line. Russell Caldwell, forty-five, managed the department, and Joe Oswalt, in his thirties, supervised the other line. After an audit prompted by production and attendance concerns, the employer said the supervisors inaccurately recorded attendance and time. Chesnut, director of manufacturing and spouse of president Sanderson, joined the recommendation that Reeves and Caldwell be discharged. Oswalt had left before the final action; the employer claimed it would otherwise have discharged him too. Do not convert that counterfactual employer assertion into an actual comparator discharge.

Reeves offered testimony and documentation disputing the recordkeeping accusations, including use of the time clock for coming and going during a day, permitted records practice, and the handling of disciplinary records. He also offered evidence that Chesnut said he was too old and made age-directed comments, treated him differently from Oswalt, and exercised dominant influence within the company. The employer disputes the accuracy, discriminatory meaning, timing, and decisional connection of that evidence; other participants and some managers were older. Rule 50, rather than an operator credibility choice, determines what can be credited against the verdict.

The jury found age discrimination and willfulness; the District Court awarded $35,000 compensatory relief and matching liquidated damages, and later $28,490.80 in front pay. The employer made and renewed Rule 50 motions and alternatively requested a new trial. The Fifth Circuit accepted that the reason could be found pretextual but found the overall proof of age motivation insufficient. Its observations about timing of comments, other decisionmakers, and other older employees are part of its rationale, not statutory exclusions of evidence.

The ADEA provision is 29 U.S.C. §623(a)(1), prohibiting covered employment actions because of age; age protection under §631(a) begins at forty. The ultimate issue is actual prohibited age motivation. Pension status, unfairness, retaliation for some different reason, personal animosity, and a false account are not automatically age discrimination. No need exists here to resolve every ADEA analytical framework or a mixed-motive allocation not presented by this record.

### Competing legal positions

**Reeves:** A prima facie case plus evidence that the proffered reason is false permits, though does not compel, a finding of intentional discrimination. His record also supplies age comments and influence evidence; this is not a proof-free demand to infer age from any unfair dismissal. Rule 50 forbids replacing jury credibility decisions with an appellate weighing of the employer’s witnesses. His brief proposes particular restrictions on crediting the movant’s evidence and seeks protection for the jury verdict.

**Sanderson:** The plaintiff retains the ultimate burden of proving age motivation; merely disputing business judgment or establishing some inaccuracy cannot make every prima facie case triable. The asserted errors were supported, the comments were unrelated to the dismissal, other decisionmakers lacked demonstrated animus, and similar conduct prompted action concerning younger supervisors. The whole record may foreclose an age inference even where a proffered explanation is rejected. The brief disputes both the claimed pretext and the inference drawn from it.

**United States/EEOC, actual amicus:** Supports Reeves. The whole record must be reviewed favorably to the verdict winner, with legitimate inferences and credibility choices preserved. Prima facie evidence and persuasive falsity evidence ordinarily can support discrimination; occasionally the plaintiff’s own proof conclusively reveals a different nondiscriminatory reason. That is a sufficiency application, not a compulsory category of “additional evidence.” The Government also rejects an absolute rule excluding every item introduced by the movant: the question is what a reasonable jury could reject or interpret in the nonmovant’s favor.

A complete rule must distinguish: employer production from ultimate persuasion; permission to infer from a compelled inference; ordinary sufficiency review from an extra direct-evidence requirement; review of the whole record from crediting disputed employer testimony; and nondiscriminatory reasons conclusively established from merely possible alternative motives. A false explanation alone is not an automatic judgment for the plaintiff. A bare assertion that another reason is imaginable does not automatically entitle the employer to judgment either.
