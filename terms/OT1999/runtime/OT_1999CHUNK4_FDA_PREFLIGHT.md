Certiorari reviews the Fourth Circuit's August 14, 1998 judgment, 153 F.3d 155. The granted question is whether the FDCA authorizes FDA regulation of tobacco products as customarily marketed as drug-delivery devices. The event-date cutoff is March 21, 2000. All nine Justices in the copied opening roster are eligible; no specific nonparticipation is established.

## Verified posture and material record

The complete primary Fourth Circuit opinion, including Judge Hall's dissent and every footnote, has been read. The FDA's August 28, 1996 final rule, 61 Fed. Reg. 44396, regulates cigarettes and smokeless tobacco through youth sale/distribution and advertising restrictions. The FDA classified nicotine as a drug and the nicotine-delivery components as devices, calling the products combination products. It elected to regulate under device authorities, particularly §360j(e). These are the agency's legal classifications, not conclusions this packet adopts.

Manufacturers, retailers and advertisers sought declaratory and injunctive relief. The district court sustained FDA jurisdiction and device treatment but held the advertising restrictions outside the statute. It stayed most regulations while leaving the age-verification and under-eighteen-sales provisions in place. The appellate route combined certified interlocutory review under 28 U.S.C. §1292(b) and an injunction appeal under §1292(a)(1). The present certiorari route is therefore not defeated merely because the underlying litigation had not reached a final trial judgment.

The Fourth Circuit majority (Widener and Michael) reversed the authority ruling, holding all August 28 regulations invalid for want of statutory authority. Its final footnote expressly **vacated** the district court's separate construction of §360j(e) on advertising and disclaimed approval or disapproval of that construction. An affirmance here would not automatically create a Court holding adopting the district court's advertising rationale. A reversal of the jurisdiction ground would require distinct attention to unresolved regulation-specific and constitutional objections.

For this appeal the regulated parties did not dispute the FDA's factual findings. That limited litigation assumption is not an all-purpose concession of every statutory application. The record recited by the lower court includes nicotine's addictive, stimulant, tranquilizing and appetite-suppressing effects; extensive serious illness and deaths; consumers' use to obtain pharmacological effects; manufacturer knowledge; and evidence that manufacturers design products to deliver active nicotine doses. The FDA relied on reasonable foreseeability, actual consumer use, manufacturers' knowledge and deliberate design as distinct grounds of intended use. The district court addressed foreseeability and actual use; Hall additionally emphasized newly available manufacturer documents. The precise force of the statutory “intended” requirement is a live legal issue, not resolved simply by labeling all sales therapeutic claims.

The FDA recognized serious risks from tobacco use but argued that abruptly removing tobacco could produce greater health risks to addicted adults, including withdrawal and unsafe substitute products, while youth restrictions could prevent addiction. The majority rejected that relative-risk approach under the statute; Hall disputed the inference from regulatory difficulties to absence of authority. No additional epidemiological or withdrawal finding is made here. “Customarily marketed” distinguishes these products from tobacco marketed with specific therapeutic claims such as weight loss. Existing FDA authority over products bearing therapeutic claims is not the categorical target of this review.

## Statutory components and lower-court disagreement

The [statutory source annex](../entering-law/OT_1999CHUNK4_FDA_STATUTORY_TEXT.md) script-copies official 1998 Code provisions, retaining source notes. The actual source files remain available. The following maps the material provisions without treating lower-court constructions as the words of Congress.

1. **Coverage and intended use.** Section 321(g)(1)(C) includes nonfood articles intended to affect bodily structure or function; subsection (g) contains separate recognized-drug, disease-use, component, food-claim and dietary-supplement qualifications. Section 321(h) covers the listed instruments and related articles, parts and accessories, with recognized-pharmacopeia, disease-use or body-effect alternatives, but excludes achieving primary purposes through chemical action or metabolism. The FDA distinguishes nicotine from its delivery component. The fact that nicotine chemically acts does not by itself dispose of the asserted combination classification. Manufacturer marketing claims and evidence of objective design or intended pharmacological use remain competing interpretations of the relevant intent language.
2. **Combination products.** Section 353(g)(1) assigns primary jurisdiction to the agency component corresponding to the combination's primary mode of action; paragraph (2) preserves use of other FDA resources necessary for adequate safety/effectiveness/substantial-equivalence review. The FDA identifies the primary mode as a drug yet invokes device rules. The majority treats this as evasion of drug duties; Hall adopts the district court's approval of the combination-product route. Administrative component assignment and the statutory regulatory requirements must not be silently equated.
3. **Safety and approval.** Section 393(b) states FDA's safety/effectiveness mission. Section 360c(a) distinguishes classes I, II and III by the adequacy of available controls and information; it is not a rule that every risky device is immediately prohibited. Section 360c(a)(2) evaluates safety/effectiveness with respect to intended persons and labeled conditions and weighs probable health benefit from use against probable injury or illness from use. Sections 331(d), 355(a) and 355(d) impose the separate new-drug approval route and its statutory grounds; classification and applicable transition provisions cannot be assumed away. The majority reasons that the FDA's own unsafe-product findings cannot be reconciled with approval and continued marketing under the chosen scheme. The agency's strongest answer distinguishes relative safety for existing addicted users and authority from the legality of any specific method of regulation. Whether withdrawal consequences are a permissible use-related benefit is disputed; it is not an established statutory exception.
4. **Misbranding and labeling.** Sections 331(a) and 352(j) respectively prohibit interstate introduction/delivery of a misbranded drug/device and define misbranding for danger when used at labeled dosage, manner, frequency or duration. Section 352(f)(1) requires adequate directions; the Secretary **shall** provide a regulatory exemption where that requirement is unnecessary for public-health protection. The distinct §352(f)(2) requires appropriate warnings against dangerous use in pathological conditions or by children and unsafe dosage/method/duration as necessary to protect users. The clause-(1) exemption is not a warning exemption. The agency relies on familiar methods of tobacco use and existing congressional warnings; the majority disputes adequacy and the permissible exemption. These are contested applications, not a statutory command that tobacco warning text itself proves all requirements satisfied.
5. **Restricted devices.** Section 360j(e) **may** restrict sale, distribution or use to practitioner authorization or other prescribed conditions when harmful potential or necessary collateral measures mean reasonable assurance of safety/effectiveness otherwise cannot be achieved. Restrictions to trained persons or particular facilities require a safe/effective-use determination; a board-certification qualification alone cannot exclude a user. Paragraph (2) requires appropriate labeling statements of restrictions as prescribed. The FDA reads the sales/distribution power to reach promotional practices; the district court read advertising authority more narrowly. That statutory issue and the First Amendment objection remain distinct from general coverage.
6. **Banning and recall are different.** Section 360f **may** initiate banned-device rulemaking on specified substantial-deception or unreasonable/substantial-risk findings; its correction-by-labeling condition, manufacturer notice, and special immediate-effect procedure retain their own predicates. Section 360h(e), in contrast, **shall** order cessation of distribution and notification on a reasonable-probability finding of serious adverse consequences or death, then affords a prompt informal hearing and requires vacatur if support is inadequate. Recall requires a further determination and timetable; no recall from individuals is allowed, and a greater-risk exception protects device-user facilities. The permissive verb in §360f does not itself change §360h's verb, nor does the cease-distribution text dispense with the required findings and process. The majority treats the FDA's accepted danger findings as making continued marketing untenable. The agency contests the resulting categorical ban inference. No present recall or ban is ordered by this packet.
7. **Other tobacco enactments and state law.** The 1965 cigarette labeling statute and 1969 and 1984 amendments, the 1983 reporting enactment, the 1986 smokeless-tobacco statute and 1992 youth-access incentives are part of the statutory context. Sections 1331 and 1334 address a comprehensive program for cigarette **labeling and advertising**, including prescribed warnings and restrictions on added warning requirements; §4406 has its own smokeless-tobacco wording. Their exact subject limitations matter. The majority uses their combined policy, FDA disclaimers and rejected expansion bills as evidence that Congress retained tobacco policy; Hall regards them as limited measures that neither expressly remove FDCA authority nor repeal it by implication. Failed bills and agency statements are evidence of contested interpretive weight, not enacted prohibitions.
8. **Section 360k is qualified preemption.** It requires an applicable federal device requirement, a different/additional state requirement and the specified safety/effectiveness or other covered-subject relationship. Subsection (b) permits a State/local exemption by regulation, after application, notice and oral-hearing opportunity, for a more stringent requirement **or** one needed by compelling local conditions whose compliance would not violate federal law. The no-federal-violation qualification belongs to the second alternative. The majority's suggestion that FDA coverage necessarily disables state youth regulation must be tested against these words and simulated *Medtronic*; neither all overlap nor the mere availability of an exemption proves the outcome.

## Supported paths and remedial constraints

These are §14(4) analytical alternatives, not invented Supreme Court briefs or concessions.

* Sustain the Fourth Circuit if text, the statutory system and legally relevant enactments establish that the FDA lacks the claimed authority over these customarily marketed products. Specify whether the reason is the definitions, irreconcilable operative commands, subsequent enacted limits or a supported combination. This path need not approve every lower-court statement about congressional silence or state preemption, and leaves therapeutic-claim products distinct.
* Reverse the general authority ruling if the definitions reach the nicotine/delivery combination and the surrounding system permits that reading, either as the best construction or a reasonable delegated choice. Explain why statutory risks and later tobacco acts do not defeat authority. Authority alone does not approve every sales or advertising restriction, establish an exemption, resolve a constitutional challenge or impose an immediate ban.
* Sustain or reject a narrower regulatory component while leaving broader coverage open only if the actual presented question and record permit that disposition. A bounded remand for unresolved combination-product, regulatory-method, advertising or constitutional questions remains possible. The Fourth Circuit did not decide those subsidiary issues on their merits; its vacatur of the district advertising construction must be respected.
* If regulatory safety duties are determinative, identify each applicable command, exception, agency finding and procedural stage instead of ordering a ban from a generalized assertion that tobacco is unsafe. If a path turns on an unverified exact regulatory exemption or litigation preservation fact, obtain targeted primary support first. No factfinding hearing, replacement regulation, retrospective liability or monetary award follows automatically.
