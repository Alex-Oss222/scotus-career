## Statutory rule and its relationship to entering law

The opening clause attributes the failure to the applicant. It therefore requires a shortfall in reasonable efforts, or greater fault, in developing the relevant claim's factual basis; it does not punish a prisoner merely because the State denied a reasonable attempt to develop material facts. Diligence concerns effort in light of the information then available, not whether investigation succeeded. Counsel must pursue identified material leads through reasonably available procedures and request the factual development the claim actually requires. A generic request is neither invariably sufficient nor invariably deficient: its adequacy depends on what counsel could reasonably identify and do. Neither omniscient discovery of every public document nor a rule that any requested investigator establishes diligence is adopted.

If applicant-side failure triggers the opening condition, the applicant must satisfy either (A)(i), a previously unavailable new constitutional rule made retroactive by this Court to collateral review, or (A)(ii), a factual predicate not previously discoverable through due diligence, and also satisfy (B), clear-and-convincing facts establishing that but for constitutional error no reasonable factfinder would have found guilt of the underlying offense. These alternatives and cumulative conditions remain distinct. Williams's concession concerning (B) disposes of a triggered hearing bar without an unnecessary finding about every other exception. It is not a Court finding of guilt or a substitute for deciding whether the trigger applies.

The statute changes the former-law consequence of negligent factual underdevelopment where its conditions apply. This timeline's Keeney retained Townsend's deliberate-bypass qualification and held ordinary negligence insufficient to defeat a necessary hearing on a properly presented claim. That rule was judicial and operated under the former statute. Section 2254(e)(2) newly places an applicant-attributed failure under restrictive exceptions that expressly distinguish due diligence and require an innocence showing. Reading the new trigger as confined to intentional bypass would import a culpability limitation Congress did not state and largely preserve the former hearing entitlement despite the enacted restriction. The Court can respect Keeney as the former-law rule while giving the new provision its independent operation; it does not falsely describe the statute as ratifying a cause-and-prejudice Keeney holding that never controlled here.

The no-fault interpretation has the opposite defect. It treats the attribution to the applicant as unnecessary and would require the exceptional innocence/new-rule route even when a State prevented diligent factual development. The existence of (A)(ii)'s due-diligence alternative does not remove the opening attribution requirement. The exception has work when the applicant is responsible for failure to develop the claim's factual basis but later relies on a genuinely previously undiscoverable predicate; the Court need not decide every possible combination of old and new facts to reject the no-fault reading.

Keeney continues to govern its former-law domain and prevents the lower court's proposed historical cause-and-prejudice rule from becoming current law by citation. Coleman remains independently applicable to an actual adequate state procedural default. Vasquez continues to distinguish supplementation of an exhausted claim from a fundamentally altered new claim. No universal exemption from exhaustion, default or statutory merits restrictions follows from diligence.

## Claim-specific application and counterarguments

**Psychiatric report.** The identified report presents a concrete investigation lead. Counsel's own state submission included a transcript repeatedly referring to it. Federal counsel found the report in Cruse's court file, and state counsel's affidavit says he did not recall seeing it there. The supplied account identifies a broad request for psychological examinations refused absent a court order, but no particular further pursuit of this known report. Reasonable diligence required more than that generic effort. The alleged Brady violation does not convert known notice of an identifiable document into diligent pursuit; whether the Commonwealth suppressed the report or it was material is not established by the allegation. The report's deficiency cannot be transferred to every other claim, but on this claim the conceded inability to satisfy (B) makes the statutory prohibition decisive.

The strongest competing path would retain Keeney's deliberate-bypass requirement and permit development unless a conscious abandonment were proved, or remand because the State resisted disclosure. That former-law policy does not supply the new statute's trigger, and general resistance does not explain failure to pursue the specific notice already in counsel's possession. No finding of bad faith or strategic withholding is needed; none is made. The affirmance is a hearing-bar ruling, not a fresh Brady-merits adjudication.

**Jury relationships and prosecutor nondisclosure.** Counsel lacked information revealing the former marriage and prosecutor's role in the divorce. He requested an investigator to explore jury-selection irregularities and omissions; the Commonwealth opposed and the state court denied the request. The later discovery of a 1979 public divorce record shows discoverability in an abstract sense, not that a reasonably diligent lawyer with these available leads had to locate it. Diligence does not require checking every juror's entire public-record history despite apparently uninformative voir dire answers. Conversely, the ruling creates no exemption for ignoring known suspicious answers or identified relationships; no such neglected lead is supplied here.

The respondent's strongest argument is that broad investigative requests cannot immunize counsel's lack of particularity. That principle is sound but does not decide these facts: counsel cannot be required to supply the undisclosed relationship as the prerequisite to investigate whether an omission occurred. Denial of assistance is material to available means, not an automatic finding that the State caused every gap. The two claims share the relevant investigation history, while retaining different merits elements. Woodson's claimed failure to remember the divorce, Stinnett's unexpanded affidavit, the meaning of the relationship question and Meinhard's limited testimony may matter to ultimate entitlement; they do not retrospectively make the relationships known to counsel.

**Alternative ground and ordinary hearing requirements.** The Fourth Circuit also relied on cause and prejudice, invoking historical Keeney, McCleskey and Carrier. Its lack-of-cause conclusion largely restated its erroneous assessment of reasonably available jury facts. Its use of a hearing-specific negligence bar is incompatible with entering Keeney. A distinct Coleman default can still bar a claim, including one that is technically exhausted because further state review is unavailable, but the actual applicable state rule and its predicate must be identified. The packet's statement that further Virginia relief was unavailable establishes neither an adequate independent ground nor an automatic excuse. The Court should not invent either one.

The lower court's observations that the former marriage ended long ago, Meinhard testified briefly and Woodson's forgetfulness was plausible do not substitute for a resolved constitutional or credibility inquiry into both jury-related claims. At the same time, removal of the statutory prohibition does not compel a hearing on immaterial allegations or guarantee a writ. The remand requires determination of genuinely applicable default, ordinary hearing and merits conditions for the identified claims under current law, without using the rejected applicant-fault inference as an independent holding. Credibility, bias, misconduct and prejudice are left open. If an independently sufficient lawful ground resolves a claim on the existing record, no unnecessary hearing is ordered.

## Boundaries of the judgment and current-term law

The informal plea-agreement claim was separately adjudicated on its merits, with affidavits denying both formal and informal agreements and an independent materiality ground. It is not a fourth application of the appellate section 2254(e)(2) hearing bar merely because it appeared in the district court's initial three-claim hearing order. The present limited remand does not revive it or substitute the juror investigation ruling for section 2254(d).

Weeks unanimously preserves actual state-merits adjudication as the trigger for section 2254(d)'s independent condition on relief; a short explanation does not eliminate it. That principle applies wherever its predicate is actually met, without assuming every newly discovered federal claim was presented to the state court. Section 2254(e)(1)'s factual presumption and clear-and-convincing rebuttal remain separate. The amended exhaustion register permits, but does not command, denial of an unexhausted claim on the merits; lack of an available or effective state process and express counsel waiver remain their own statutory alternatives and limits.

The Williams cross-examination claim is not automatically resolved by Portuondo's narrower summation holding. Its independent harmlessness and Teague dispositions remain outside this grant; the Court neither adopts the lower court's broad historical substantial-effect rule as current law nor creates a new error category. Likewise, the assistance/confidentiality and filing-period rulings are not reopened. The Court does not declare Virginia qualified for chapter 154, approve the incorrect title-number transcription, or rewrite the statutory confidentiality conditions. Leaving an unreviewed ruling undisturbed is not adoption of its rationale as Supreme Court law.
