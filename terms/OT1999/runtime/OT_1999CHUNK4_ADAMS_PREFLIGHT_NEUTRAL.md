## Verified corrections and distinctions

Adams is a Quaker employed as a Peace Field Secretary. The opinion reports undisputed sincerity and efforts to avoid personal profit. She asserted exemption from withholding in 1985–1989; in 1989 the IRS instructed her employer to withhold as married with one allowance. The deficiencies and additions under review concern 1988, 1989, 1992, 1993, and 1994. She proposed paying her full tax into a nonmilitary fund or directing it to nonmilitary spending, and offered to consider other accommodations in consultation with her religious community. Her requested legal relief included no tax liability until accommodation was provided. These are her proposals and requested relief; feasibility, revenue equivalence, appropriation effects, and governmental acceptance are not findings.

The distinct additions actually analyzed below are failure-to-file relief under §6651(a), applying reasonable cause together with absence of willful neglect, and estimated-tax relief under §6654(e)(3), applying casualty, disaster, or other unusual circumstances that would make the addition against equity and good conscience. The latter is not a general reasonable-cause exception. The separate newly-retired-or-disabled clause has additional predicates and is not claimed to apply. The supplied general reference to associated additions must not be converted into an unidentified penalty component or a numerical award.

Adams argued that religious compulsion itself established reasonable cause and unusual circumstances. The Third Circuit rejected that legal contention, distinguishing sincerity from inability to comply. In note 10 it also rejected her Free Exercise argument that the statutory exceptions constituted individualized exemptions requiring equal treatment for religious hardship. That is a preserved legal argument, not a new factual claim of selective enforcement. No instance of comparable secular willful nonfiling receiving a waiver is established. The opinion supplies no claim of disability, destroyed records, reliance on tax advice, or arithmetic error. Such circumstances must not be invented or silently treated as proved absent.

The court treated RFRA as applicable to the federal government without deciding its constitutionality. Adams conceded a compelling interest in collecting taxes, but contested whether the government had justified rejection of her proposed accommodations by the least restrictive means. The lower court deemed further evidence unnecessary by relying on Lee's uniform-tax rationale. Its statement that RFRA's means inquiry effectively answered itself is the legal ruling under review, not an independent fact. The neutral packet's prior lack of identified sections and years is now cured; the precise amounts remain immaterial to the legal challenge presented.

## Entering law and strongest competing paths

The mechanically copied slice `entering-law/OT_1999CHUNK4_ADAMS.md` contains the controlling RFRA standard, Clearwater, Swanner, American Life League's statutory holding, Boerne's preserved federal field, and relevant current public law. The RFRA claimant bears substantial burden; after that showing the government bears production and persuasion on application-specific compelling interest and least restrictive means. The statutory rule is not displaced by Smith's constitutional neutral-law rule, and commercial participation or a tax label alone is not enough. Boerne does not decide federal RFRA's validity. No constitutional validity issue was contested below; deciding it sua sponte is not necessary to resolve the presented claim.

One supported path sustains the liability on the actual proposed accommodation: full payment conditioned on nonmilitary use is not the same as unconditional general-revenue payment, and a judicial earmark would impose the claimant's spending restriction on a common public system. Lee and Hernandez supply the uniform-tax interest and the distinction between legislative exceptions and a claimed personal veto. Boyle supplies the filing-duty and reasonable-cause framework, with the statutory exceptions separately respected. On this path, the court must explain why the proposals fail under the enacted standard rather than announce an IRS exemption from RFRA; the additions require their separate legal analysis.

The strongest contrary path vacates the RFRA determination for application-specific justification, because the lower court's reliance on categorical uniformity avoided the production-and-persuasion inquiry that Clearwater, Swanner, and American Life League retain. A theoretical earmark need not be proved workable to show that the lower court used an impermissible categorical premise, but remand alone establishes no accommodation, no tax cancellation, and no required evidentiary hearing. This path must confront Lee's very close general-tax discussion, Hernandez's rejection of exemption-counting, the undisputed character of the particular proposals, and the distinction between a legal insufficiency and an unresolved operational fact. The modeled Justices must state whether those sources permit a legal conclusion on this record or require further justification, and separately whether that conclusion reaches each addition.

For additions, a sufficient ground can reject religious objection alone as statutory reasonable cause and as the pleaded unusual circumstance without deciding every possible waiver. A contrary legal argument invokes the exemptions' individual operation, Boyle's preserved inability cases, and RFRA's separate application to enforcement burdens. The source supplies no inability evidence beyond conscience and no secular comparator; neither path may manufacture one. If the model chooses a remand on RFRA, it must say which tax or addition determination is affected and which independent statutory or constitutional conclusion survives. The opinion's note-10 constitutional argument remains subject to Smith and the controlling objective-neutrality/comparable-secular-harm rule, not a newly invented universal waiver rule.

## Justice-specific date-eligible source map

All eight Associates joined Clearwater's actual-person RFRA rule. O'Connor authored Swanner and American Life League's RFRA opinion, joined by Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, and Thomas. Stevens, Ginsburg, and Breyer dissented from those particular RFRA remands, while agreeing that RFRA governs; their rejected application does not change the controlling standard. These are relevant published adjudicative commitments, not votes in this case.

Stevens's own Lee concurrence distinguishes the ordinary war-tax objection from the Amish alternative-welfare arrangement and emphasizes the difficulty of allocating special tax exemptions without assessing religious claims. He also joined the Hernandez opinion. O'Connor joined Lee and Boyle, joined the separate Boyle concurrence preserving genuine inability cases, and dissented in Hernandez, joined by Scalia, on unequal treatment of comparable religious payments. That Hernandez dissent does not announce a war-tax exemption: its premise is an established administrative disparity between genuinely comparable transactions. Kennedy did not participate in Hernandez and receives no inferred join. Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg, and Breyer were not on the Court in those earlier opinions; their simulated RFRA joins supply the direct date-eligible evidence available here. No later RFRA case is used.

### Issue 1: RFRA’s application-specific inquiry in federal tax administration

* **Controlling Simulated Law:** Church of Scientology Flag Service Organization v. Clearwater (December 5, 1994) and Swanner v. Anchorage Equal Rights Commission (March 27, 1995) assign substantial burden to the claimant and compelling interest and least restrictive means to the government. Their statutory analysis survives within RFRA’s valid field. City of Boerne v. Flores (June 25, 1997) limits RFRA’s state/local §5 application; it does not invalidate its federal application. American Life League v. Reno (November 13, 1995) applies RFRA to federal law without an express statutory exclusion.
* **Current Reusable Doctrine:** Sincerity does not prove justification or create an exemption. If the claimant establishes substantial burden, the government must justify this application and address a concrete workable alternative. Neither a general label such as taxation nor the existence of ordinary exemptions completes that inquiry.
* **Other Governing Law:** 42 U.S.C. §§2000bb–1 and 2000bb–3; applicable Internal Revenue Code collection, interest and additions provisions. United States v. Lee, 455 U.S. 252 (1982), explains the interests in compulsory, uniform tax participation and the costs shifted by exemptions; it informs, rather than displaces, RFRA. Smith’s constitutional neutral-law rule is not the statutory test.
* **Open Point:** Whether the requested accommodation can preserve timely, complete revenue collection without transferring its costs or operational burdens to other taxpayers. No factual finding about an alternative beyond the supplied proposal is invented.

## C. DECISION-MATERIAL LITIGATION POSITIONS

* **Controlling reasoning below:** The supplied account reports rejection of the religious tax-accommodation claim. Lee’s uniform-tax rationale is relevant, but the simulated RFRA precedents require examination of the burden and the requested accommodation rather than an automatic tax exception to RFRA.
* **Petitioner’s or appellant’s strongest supported position:** Payment conflicts with sincere religious commitments. RFRA demands a justification for imposing the obligation on this person; revenue collection in general does not explain rejection of every accommodation or automatic use of additions against conscientious nonpayment.
* **Respondent’s or appellee’s strongest supported position:** Mandatory timely payment of the taxpayer’s assessed share is necessary to a common revenue system. Excusing payment or diverting the objected-to share changes Congress’s spending allocation and transfers revenue and enforcement costs to others. Sincere disagreement with expenditure cannot give each taxpayer a budgetary veto.
* **Necessary concessions and limiting principles:** Sincerity and substantial burden do not by themselves establish an exemption. The government must address a proposed means that preserves full, timely collection; ordinary statutory relief from additions remains distinct.
