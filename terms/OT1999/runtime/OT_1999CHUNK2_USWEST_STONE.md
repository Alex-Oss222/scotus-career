## B. JUDGMENT AND REMEDY

* **Stone's Controlled Judgment or Disposition:** Reverse the Tenth Circuit on the First Amendment and statutory-construction grounds for vacating the CPNI order. Uphold the challenged opt-in rule under Central Hudson and as a permissible construction of § 222(c)(1).
* **Stone's Remedy and Remand Position:** Remand the unreached takings and arbitrary-and-capricious challenges. The proposed judgment does not reject those claims, compel opt-in for every future rule, or hold opt-out unconstitutional.

## C. ISSUE POSITIONS


### Issue 1: Commercial-speech review and the confidentiality harm

* **Threshold and Merits Reach:** Reach the First Amendment question independently. Because the rule selects the use of information for commercial solicitation, Lanphere requires commercial-speech review; calling it information regulation does not avoid that scrutiny.
* **Stone's Legal Position:** Apply the existing Central Hudson framework, including real harms, direct and material advancement and reasonable fit. Fox requires no least-restrictive means, and the precedents permit supported predictive judgment without invariably demanding studies.
* **Stone's Proposed Holding:** Affirmative approval before a carrier uses confidential calling records for the challenged new-service marketing directly advances the substantial confidentiality interest and reasonably fits it.
* **Essential Reasoning and Record Application:** Privacy is substantial, as Went For It recognized. This interest is stronger than the interest in avoiding a letter derived from public information: CPNI reveals whom a person calls, when and for how long, is obtained through the telephone service, and carries a statutory confidentiality duty. Went For It and Lanphere dealt with public accident or other records; silencing solicitation there left the information public. Here the harm is the unconsented use of confidential information, and requiring consent prevents that harm. Went For It expressly reserved confidential-record misuse.

  The permitted uses do not reproduce the self-undermining scheme in Greater New Orleans. Uses tied to services the customer already selected differ from using the service records to solicit a new category. The FCC did consider opt-out and explained that silence after notices many customers will not read is not informed approval. That statutory and predictive judgment supplies a reason for requiring an affirmative choice. The carrier’s low opt-in rates support the argument that customers do not affirmatively want this additional use; they do not establish that silence signifies approval. Carriers remain able to market any service, use non-CPNI information and ask permission.

  The carrier’s strongest objection is that Edenfield, Lanphere and Went For It demand support for harm and fit and that opt-out burdens less speech. Those cases demand support, not certainty, and involved public information. The confidentiality and service relationship identify a concrete interest here; consent addresses the unconsented use itself. Having considered opt-out, the FCC need not select it merely because it is less burdensome to the speaker. Fox does not require the least restrictive means.
* **Boundary or Reserved Question:** No holding that opt-out is unconstitutional, no general exemption from commercial-speech review for data-use rules, and no ruling on public or nonconfidential information. Lanphere and Went For It remain unchanged.

### Issue 2: Statutory construction and independent constitutional judgment

* **Threshold and Merits Reach:** Decide the constitutional question independently, then the permissibility of the FCC’s construction of the enacted approval requirement.
* **Stone's Legal Position:** Apply Chevron to the statutory choice, with no deference on constitutional validity.
* **Stone's Proposed Holding:** “Approval of the customer” fairly bears an opt-in interpretation. The FCC’s choice is reasonable; after independent constitutional analysis sustains the rule, constitutional avoidance supplies no basis for withholding ordinary statutory deference.
* **Essential Reasoning and Record Application:** A duty of confidentiality coupled with customer approval reasonably allows the agency to demand an affirmative indication before a new marketing use. The statute need not compel that choice for it to permit it. Constitutional doubt cannot be invoked as a substitute for resolving the actual First Amendment question; that question is resolved under the governing test, not by deferring to the agency.
* **Boundary or Reserved Question:** The statute is not held to require opt-in exclusively. Takings and arbitrary-and-capricious claims remain for remand; sustaining the presented speech and interpretive questions does not resolve every administrative objection.

### Issue 3: Entity capacity and the authorized service relationship

* **Threshold and Merits Reach:** Identify the protected speaker and customer interests without treating corporate status or licensing as a waiver of constitutional rights.
* **Stone's Legal Position:** Capacity follows the particular right. Commercial-speech protection serves listeners as well as speakers, so a corporation can invoke it; legal personhood alone does not settle its scope.
* **Stone's Proposed Holding:** The carrier retains commercial-speech protection, but its lawful access to customer records does not exempt their subsequent use from the supported confidentiality terms attached to that service relationship.
* **Essential Reasoning and Record Application:** U.S. West’s existence comes from its charter, and its common-carrier position and access to calling records arise through federal and state authorization to provide telephone service. Section 222 attaches confidentiality to that position. Information obtained through this legally authorized service is held subject to the lawful terms governing it. That premise does not permit government to condition an entity’s existence on surrendering independent constitutional protections: a penalty for viewpoint or editorial choices would receive the scrutiny governing that discrimination. This rule targets neither.

  The carrier’s size earns no discount in constitutional protection. The rule applies to telecommunications carriers as such and regulates confidential information obtained in a consumer service relationship. Agreeing to telephone service is not consent to every later profitable use of its records. The restriction concerns that use, not a disfavored message, while its marketing trigger still warrants the commercial-speech scrutiny already performed. Customers retain their interests in their records, natural persons acting through the carrier retain their own speech, and the carrier can continue marketing and requesting consent.
* **Boundary or Reserved Question:** No general power to demand surrender of constitutional rights as a charter or licensing condition, no reduction based on wealth or size, and no displacement of the native commercial-speech test by a new entity-capacity test.
