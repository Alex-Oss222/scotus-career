## B. JUDGMENT AND REMEDY

* **Stone's Controlled Judgment or Disposition:** Grant review, reverse the Circuit Court's suppression judgment, and remand.
* **Stone's Remedy and Remand Position:** Reject the general homicide-scene exception. Permit the state courts to consider only preserved and legally supported alternative warrant exceptions and harmless error. Do not direct acquittal, suppress evidence on an undeveloped alternative record, or decide retrial questions not presented.

## C. ISSUE POSITIONS

### Issue 1: General homicide-scene exception

* **Threshold and Merits Reach:** Stone reaches the preserved federal search question. No threshold defect prevents review of the last state-court merits ruling.
* **Stone's Legal Position:** Apply the surviving warrant default and Mincey without modification.
* **Stone's Proposed Holding:** Police may enter a homicide scene to render emergency aid, search promptly for victims or a dangerous person, secure the premises, and preserve evidence while lawful process is obtained. A completed emergency does not authorize an extended warrantless evidentiary search merely because a homicide occurred.
* **Essential Reasoning and Record Application:** The cabin was secured before officers began a sixteen-hour evidence-gathering process. The trial court authorized examination of anything and everything at the scene, a rationale broader than any recognized exception and untethered to continued emergency need. The gravity of homicide strengthens the interest in prompt investigation but does not eliminate the warrant requirement. As Dickerson distinguishes an authorized frisk from later manipulation, lawful emergency entry cannot justify opening concealed materials after the emergency ends. That boundary supports reversal without inventing a crime-scene exception.
* **Boundary or Reserved Question:** Prompt emergency entry, a protective sweep, search for additional victims or an assailant, and temporary evidence preservation remain lawful when their own predicates exist.

### Issue 2: Closed briefcase and remand

* **Threshold and Merits Reach:** Stone reaches the legal consequence of the invalid categorical theory but not unresolved factual alternatives.
* **Stone's Legal Position:** Apply ordinary closed-container and appellate-remand principles.
* **Stone's Proposed Holding:** Opening the closed briefcase was a search requiring a warrant or a separately established exception; the container's presence in plain view and its unlocked condition did not expose its contents.
* **Essential Reasoning and Record Application:** The photographs and negatives were concealed inside the briefcase. The trial court did not find consent, a standardized inventory, or a continuing exigency. Because those theories and harmlessness may depend on facts and state procedure, the lawful remedy is reversal and remand rather than appellate factfinding.
* **Boundary or Reserved Question:** Stone expresses no view on implied consent, a valid inventory, another exigency, attenuation, or harmlessness if properly preserved and supported below.

## B. JUDGMENT AND REMEDY

* **Stone's Controlled Judgment or Disposition:** On the designated direct-appeal vehicle, reverse the judgment insofar as it sustains this permanent, extreme inequality of voting weight in a general legislative chamber, and remand for a constitutional remedy.
* **Stone's Remedy and Remand Position:** Permit the political branches to choose a lawful legislative design in the first instance. The remedial court must consider lawful transition and election timing without indefinitely preserving the violation.

## C. ISSUE POSITIONS

### Issue 1: An elected territorial legislature and the individual right to equal representation

* **Threshold and Merits Reach:** Resolve the apportionment question on the supplied direct-appeal vehicle. The constitutional source must be identified for the Commonwealth; statehood is neither assumed nor made a prerequisite to every protection of equal citizenship.
* **Stone's Legal Position:** Apply the equal-protection principle of Reynolds to this general territorial legislature, with express attention to its different governmental source. This is a proposed application to a question not resolved by the supplied simulated cases, not a claim that Reynolds already adjudicated the CNMI Covenant.
* **Stone's Proposed Holding:** Federal approval of a territorial political settlement does not by itself justify permanently giving inhabitants of one island substantially less voting weight in the election of a general legislative chamber. Equal-island representation cannot sustain the extreme inequality presented here.
* **Essential Reasoning and Record Application:** The injury is concrete: inhabitants elect legislators who exercise general governmental authority over them, but equal Senate delegations represent radically unequal populations. That is unequal participation, not the design-only objection excluded by Shaw. Morris’s attention to actual governmental power reinforces the distinction between this Senate and a limited intergovernmental council. The smaller islands’ concern about domination is substantial and deserves institutional protection. It does not explain why the vote of an inhabitant must permanently count for much more merely because of residence on a smaller island. Reynolds rejects that conversion of geography into unequal representation for a general legislature. The federal Senate is fixed by the Constitution’s own allocation among States; Congress’s approval of an ordinary governmental settlement does not supply the same constitutional text. The consensual origin of the Covenant matters to interpreting its institutions and designing relief, but one generation’s political bargain cannot by itself extinguish the continuing individual claim of equal representation. Territorial equality has a constitutional foundation, as Flores de Otero illustrates, although the precise governmental setting differs. Extending the population-equality principle here therefore requires justification by the elected body’s function and the right impaired, not an assertion that territories and States are identical for all purposes.
* **Boundary or Reserved Question:** No general holding about every constitutional provision in every territory, no invalidation of autonomous local island institutions, and no redesign of the Commonwealth beyond the challenged legislative inequality. The Covenant’s full remedial architecture is not reproduced in the supplied record.

## B. JUDGMENT AND REMEDY

* **Stone's Controlled Judgment or Disposition:** Affirm the Third Circuit’s dismissal of the civil RICO and Clayton Act § 4 claims on their respective remoteness grounds. Do not adopt the District Court’s rationale that the funds suffered no cognizable property injury.
* **Stone's Remedy and Remand Position:** No damages recovery or merits remand on these pleaded federal theories. The disposition reaches only the two federal questions; it neither adjudicates state-law claims nor decides whether an unpresented amendment could allege a different, direct fraud on a fund.

## C. ISSUE POSITIONS


### Issue 1: Civil RICO: contextual directness applied to these expenditures

* **Threshold and Merits Reach:** Reach the sufficiency of the pleaded RICO causal chain under Rule 12(b)(6), accepting the allegations for this purpose. The funds’ monetary expenditures are real property injuries; the defect is their relation to the asserted violation.
* **Stone's Legal Position:** Apply Holmes unchanged. Stone joined the Court’s directness rationale, which requires factual and proximate cause and some direct relation between the asserted injury and injurious conduct. Attribution, duplicate recovery and available direct plaintiffs inform that requirement; it is not a mechanical bar on every third-party injury.
* **Stone's Proposed Holding:** A welfare fund whose alleged loss arises only because participants smoked, became ill and were treated has not pleaded the direct relation § 1964(c) requires on these theories. An allegation that defendants meant the costs to fall on the fund does not shorten that chain.
* **Essential Reasoning and Record Application:** Each claimed payment exists because a participant smoked, became ill and sought treatment. The chain runs through the participant’s own choices and bodily injury. The cessation-program theory remains indirect: it requires both that an informed fund would have acted and that participants would have quit, with money lost only through subsequent illness. McCready is different because denial of reimbursement to the subscriber herself was the scheme’s mechanism, without an intervening third party’s injury.

  Attribution is decisive. Separating costs caused by deception from costs participants would have incurred despite knowledge of the risks or because of other causes depends on the intervening behavior and illness. Aggregate statistical models are methods of proving damages; they do not supply the missing direct relation. Duplicate recovery may be limited, as the Third Circuit found, but that consideration does not rescue this chain. The funds’ strongest argument is that smokers cannot recover personal-injury damages under RICO’s business-or-property provision. Congress chose that limit. The absence of that RICO remedy does not make these derivative expenditure claims direct; participants retain their tort remedies.

  The objection that the parties who paid can never sue overstates the holding. Documented payments establish injury, not directness. Intent to shift costs does not eliminate intervening illness, and freedom from defenses applicable to participants is a reason against treating the funds as the proper plaintiffs for this injury. RICO’s liberal-construction directive and private-enforcement purpose do not override the causal limit Congress borrowed from the Clayton Act. Neither the scale of the harm nor the importance of the claim creates liability.
* **Boundary or Reserved Question:** No categorical bar on third-party payers. A payer whose own business or property is the immediate object and mechanism of fraud—for example, false statements to the fund inducing its payments—may satisfy Holmes. Repleading such a claim is not presented. No general ruling on aggregate proof or state-law claims.

### Issue 2: Entity capacity and the interest belonging to each claimant

* **Threshold and Merits Reach:** Identify the claimant’s protected interest without adding a jurisdictional barrier or changing the governing causal test.
* **Stone's Legal Position:** Legal personhood alone settles no question about which rights an entity holds. Capacity follows the legal source and particular interest. The trusts’ benefit function and capacity to sue protect their own property on the governing law’s terms; participants’ bodily interests remain their own.
* **Stone's Proposed Holding:** The funds’ payments injure their property, but their statutory form and payment duty neither transfer participants’ bodily injury nor confer an exception to § 1964(c)’s directness requirement.
* **Essential Reasoning and Record Application:** The funds are trusts under § 302(c) of the Taft-Hartley Act, governed by ERISA, and exist to pay benefits to natural persons. Their capacity to sue is an incident of that legal form and reaches injury to their own property. Illness is a bodily injury inseparable from human personality; paying for it does not transfer that injury to the fund. The payment nevertheless reduces the fund’s property, so the District Court’s no-cognizable-injury rationale is rejected. Who the plaintiff is identifies the interests and legal sources involved; it earns no premium on directness. Recovery on these theories would also avoid defenses that could limit participants’ own claims, which counsels against treating the funds as the proper plaintiffs. The corporate defendants receive the same governing rule; their wealth and size neither lower nor raise the causal requirement.
* **Boundary or Reserved Question:** No generalized denial of entity property rights, no transfer of bodily rights, and no new burden displacing Holmes or the enacted statutory requirements.

### Issue 3: Clayton Act § 4

* **Threshold and Merits Reach:** Decide the § 4 claim separately from RICO.
* **Stone's Legal Position:** Apply AGC’s antitrust-specific inquiry. Holmes’s rejection of an antitrust-injury requirement for RICO leaves that requirement intact under § 4.
* **Stone's Proposed Holding:** The funds’ health-cost losses from the alleged suppression of safer cigarettes are too remote to support this § 4 action.
* **Essential Reasoning and Record Application:** These funds are neither consumers nor competitors in the cigarette market. The asserted suppression reaches their finances through participants’ smoking and illness, not an injury to the funds that itself implements the anticompetitive scheme. That causal distinction, not the market-participant labels alone, separates McCready. Remoteness independently defeats the Clayton Act claim even though the funds can identify expenditures and allege a deliberate scheme.
* **Boundary or Reserved Question:** No antitrust-injury element is imported into RICO, and no broader disposition of state-law causes is proposed.

Stone finds this description of flight and area insufficiently specific on the supplied record; that application does not categorically exclude evasive conduct from reasonable-suspicion analysis.
