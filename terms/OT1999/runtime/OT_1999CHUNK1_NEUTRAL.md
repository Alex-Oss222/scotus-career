*Flippo v. West Virginia*

# SECTION I: NEUTRAL MODELING PACKET

## A. CASE, RECORD, AND POSTURE

* **Case Name:** Flippo v. West Virginia
* **Citation or Docket:** No. 98-8770; 528 U.S. 11 (1999); underlying indictment No. 96-F-119, Circuit Court of West Virginia, Fayette County
* **October Term:** 1999
* **Entry Stage and Event:** Merits disposition after certiorari review of the last state court judgment on the federal suppression question; submitted without oral argument.
* **Simulated Event Date:** 1999-10-18 — Historical event date as recorded in supplied term materials.
* **Question Presented:** Whether the Fourth Amendment permits an extended warrantless evidentiary search of a secured homicide scene, including opening a closed briefcase, under a general crime-scene exception to the warrant requirement.
* **Question Granted:** Whether a general homicide-scene exception justified the warrantless evidentiary search and opening of the closed briefcase.
* **Court and Judgment Under Review:** Circuit Court of West Virginia, Fayette County. That court denied suppression on the ground that investigators could thoroughly search a homicide scene and anything found within it. The Supreme Court of Appeals of West Virginia denied discretionary review, so the writ runs to the Circuit Court as the last state court to decide the federal issue on the merits.
* **Relief Requested:** Reversal of the suppression ruling and further proceedings excluding the briefcase evidence unless the State establishes another preserved warrant exception or harmlessness.
* **Material Record:** In 1996, Flippo reported an attack at a state-park cabin. Police found his wife dead inside, secured the scene, searched outside for evidence, and later reentered to process the cabin for more than sixteen hours. Officers opened an unlocked but closed briefcase found near the body and seized photographs and negatives later used as evidence of motive. No warrant was obtained. The State argued crime-scene processing, plain view, inventory, exigency, and implied consent; the trial court relied on a general homicide-scene rationale rather than making findings on those alternatives.
* **Threshold, Preservation, and Vehicle Matters:** The suppression issue was presented to the trial court and in the petition for discretionary state review. The record does not contain findings sufficient for this Court to resolve consent, inventory, another exigency, or harmless error in the first instance. The state high court's discretionary denial does not alter the identity of the operative judgment.
* **Relevant Dates:** Homicide and search in 1996; suppression order May 28, 1997; state discretionary review denied January 13, 1999; Court event October 18, 1999.
* **Companion or Related Matters:** None identified.
* **Participation Matter:** No recusal or nonparticipation is identified.

### Issue 1: General homicide-scene exception

* **Controlling Simulated Law:** Mincey v. Arizona and Thompson v. Louisiana reject a homicide-scene exception; the recognized emergency ends when its actual justification ends. The simulated Minnesota v. Dickerson decision likewise confines an initially lawful protective intrusion to its authorized purpose.
* **Current Reusable Doctrine:** Emergency entry to find victims or an assailant does not itself authorize the later prolonged evidentiary search or opening of a closed container. Determine a preserved independent justification and its actual scope.
* **Other Governing Law:** Fourth and Fourteenth Amendments.
* **Material Other Authority:** Mincey v. Arizona, 437 U.S. 385 (1978); Thompson v. Louisiana, 469 U.S. 17 (1984); Katz v. United States, 389 U.S. 347 (1967).
* **Open Point:** None as to the categorical crime-scene theory. Whether another exception or harmless error applies remains unresolved on this record.

### Issue 2: Closed container and alternative grounds

* **Controlling Simulated Law:** A closed container retains Fourth Amendment protection unless a recognized exception authorizes its search. Plain view of the container does not itself expose concealed contents. Consent and inventory require findings concerning scope, purpose, and standardized or voluntary authorization.
* **Current Reusable Doctrine:** Appellate review cannot invent facts or adopt an alternative ground that the record and findings do not support. Remedy must track the unlawful search without predeciding preserved attenuation, harmlessness, or retrial questions.
* **Other Governing Law:** Ordinary suppression, harmless-error, consent, and exigency principles.
* **Material Other Authority:** Arizona v. Hicks, 480 U.S. 321 (1987), for the distinction between observation and further search; Chapman v. California, 386 U.S. 18 (1967), for harmless constitutional error.
* **Open Point:** The trial court made no findings on implied consent, a valid inventory, another exigency, or harmlessness.

## C. DECISION-MATERIAL LITIGATION POSITIONS

* The trial court's controlling reasoning was categorical: securing a homicide scene permitted examination of anything and everything within it.
* Flippo's strongest position is that the emergency had ended, the cabin was secured, the sixteen-hour evidentiary search was investigative, and opening the briefcase required a warrant or a distinct exception.
* West Virginia's strongest lawful alternatives are implied consent from Flippo's request for police assistance, plain view, evidence-preservation exigency, inventory, and harmlessness. Each depends on findings the trial court did not make.
* The State must concede that the occurrence of a homicide alone cannot authorize an unlimited evidentiary search after the emergency is controlled.
* No Solicitor General or amicus position is material.

## D. LAWFUL PATHS AND UNCERTAINTY

* Reverse because the categorical homicide-scene rule is legally invalid, then remand for preserved alternative grounds and harmlessness.
* Affirm only if an alternative ground is established as a matter of law on an undisputed record; the present findings do not support that route.
* Remand without deciding suppression consequences if state law or the record requires further findings.
* The principal uncertainty concerns alternative exceptions and the effect of the photographs on the verdict, not the invalidity of the general crime-scene theory.

# STONE PRE-VOTE CASE BRIEF

*Texas v. Lesage*

# SECTION I: NEUTRAL MODELING PACKET

## A. CASE, RECORD, AND POSTURE

* **Case Name:** Texas v. Lesage
* **Canonical Matter / Alias:** Texas et al. v. Lesage et al.
* **Citation or Docket:** No. 98-1111; 528 U.S. 18 (1999); judgment below, 158 F.3d 213 (5th Cir. 1998)
* **October Term:** 1999
* **Entry Stage and Event:** Merits disposition after certiorari review, submitted without oral argument.
* **Simulated Event Date:** 1999-11-29 — Historical event date as recorded in supplied term materials.
* **Question Presented:** What effect does a government defendant's same-decision showing have on liability and remedies when an applicant alleges that race entered a discrete admissions process, and what claims remain if the applicant also alleges continuing unequal competition.
* **Question Granted:** Whether same-decision proof defeats relief tied to the rejection and how retrospective and prospective injuries must be separated.
* **Court and Judgment Under Review:** United States Court of Appeals for the Fifth Circuit. The District Court granted summary judgment for the state defendants on all claims. The Fifth Circuit reversed because it regarded the asserted same-decision showing as irrelevant to the claimed injury from competing in a race-conscious process.
* **Relief Requested:** Reversal of the Fifth Circuit's damages ruling and reinstatement of summary judgment, while resolving or remanding any distinct statutory and prospective claims.
* **Material Record:** François Daniel Lesage, an African immigrant of Caucasian descent, applied to the University of Texas counseling-psychology Ph.D. program for 1996-1997. The program received 223 applications and offered roughly 20 admissions. Race was considered at some stage, and at least one minority candidate was admitted. Texas presented evidence that at least 80 applicants had higher undergraduate GPAs, 152 had higher GRE scores, 73 exceeded Lesage on both measures, and one admissions-committee member regarded his statement and recommendations as weak. The District Court found that race did not affect his rejection and that the admitted candidates had superior credentials. The Fifth Circuit did not review whether the lawful same decision was conclusively established; it treated that issue as legally irrelevant. Lesage pleaded Equal Protection, §§ 1981 and 1983, Title VI, damages, and injunctive relief.
* **Threshold, Preservation, and Vehicle Matters:** The claims and remedies must be separated by source. A same-decision defense addresses causation and outcome-based relief, not whether unequal treatment occurred. Nominal or declaratory relief requires an available cause, proper defendant, and immunity analysis. Prospective relief requires a concrete continuing or imminent exposure; the record suggests, but does not conclusively establish, that the challenged admissions practice had ceased or that the claim was abandoned.
* **Relevant Dates:** Application for 1996-1997 admission; Fifth Circuit judgment in 1998; Court event November 29, 1999.
* **Companion or Related Matters:** Hopwood v. Texas supplies the controlling admissions and remedy rules but is not a companion docket.
* **Participation Matter:** No recusal or nonparticipation is identified.

### Issue 1: Unequal admissions process and same-decision proof

* **Controlling Simulated Law:** Hopwood v. Texas applies Adarand’s common substantial-justification standard to state racial admissions classifications and that unequal opportunity to compete is an injury without proof of entitlement to a seat. Once an unconstitutional process is established, the school bears the burden, by a preponderance, to prove that the applicant would not have been admitted under a specified constitutional process in order to defeat relief for the denied admission and its consequences. A successful defense does not erase the unequal-process injury.
* **Current Reusable Doctrine:** Same-decision proof and the underlying violation are separate. The lawful counterfactual must be specified and must permit any use of race that controlling law allows. Outcome causation determines outcome-based relief; it does not retroactively validate the decision process.
* **Other Governing Law:** Equal Protection Clause; 42 U.S.C. §§ 1981, 1983, and 2000d; Mt. Healthy City Board of Education v. Doyle; Carey v. Piphus.
* **Material Other Authority:** Hopwood's individual-causation, nominal-recognition, and prospective-relief holdings.
* **Open Point:** Whether Texas carried its school-borne burden under the correct lawful counterfactual was not reviewed by the Fifth Circuit and must be decided on remand unless the record permits only one answer.

### Issue 2: Remedy, statutory claims, and prospective standing

* **Controlling Simulated Law:** Hopwood preserves declaratory and nominal recognition for an established unequal process independently of seat entitlement, subject to cause of action, proper defendant, and immunity. Compensatory loss must be proved. Prospective relief requires concrete future exposure and an equitable basis; a discontinued procedure or retrospective injury alone is insufficient.
* **Current Reusable Doctrine:** Right, cause of action, defendant, immunity, standing, and remedy remain distinct. A same-decision defense can defeat compensation for the denied admission without defeating nominal recognition of a completed constitutional wrong.
* **Other Governing Law:** Article III standing; source-specific requirements of §§ 1981, 1983, and Title VI.
* **Material Other Authority:** Carey v. Piphus; Northeastern Florida Chapter, Associated General Contractors of America v. Jacksonville.
* **Open Point:** Whether the §§ 1981 and 2000d claims remain viable, whether any defendant is proper for nominal or declaratory relief, and whether Lesage preserved a live prospective claim.

## C. DECISION-MATERIAL LITIGATION POSITIONS

* The Fifth Circuit's controlling reasoning was that elimination during a race-conscious stage itself created an equal-competition injury and made the ultimate same decision irrelevant to the § 1983 claim.
* Texas's strongest position is that undisputed evidence establishes rejection under any lawful process and therefore defeats liability and relief tied to the denial.
* Lesage's strongest position is that being subjected to an unconstitutional racial process is a completed injury even if a lawful process would have produced the same rejection, and that continuing use of the policy would support forward-looking relief without proof of admission entitlement.
* Texas must concede that same-decision proof does not validate a racial classification or erase a separately established process injury. Lesage must concede that it does not support compensation for an admission he would lawfully have been denied.
* The record must distinguish the constitutional claim from §§ 1981 and 2000d and must determine whether prospective exposure remains concrete.

## D. LAWFUL PATHS AND UNCERTAINTY

* Reverse the Fifth Circuit's legal rule and remand for application of Hopwood's school-borne same-decision burden, source-specific remedies, and prospective standing.
* Reinstate summary judgment on outcome-based damages if Texas has conclusively carried the correct same-decision burden and no genuine dispute remains.
* Preserve nominal or declaratory relief if an unconstitutional process is proved and an available cause against a proper defendant permits it.
* Preserve a prospective claim only if Lesage establishes continuing or imminent unequal competition; otherwise dismiss that relief for lack of standing or mootness.
* The principal uncertainty is not the governing doctrine but whether the existing evidence satisfies Hopwood's specified-lawful-process requirement and which pleaded claims remain live.

* **Case Name:** Fiore v. White

* **Material Record:** Fiore owned and operated a hazardous-waste facility and Scarpone served as general manager. Both possessed a permit. Pennsylvania prosecuted them under 35 Pa. Fiore's conviction was affirmed and became final in 1990. Scarpone's parallel conviction was reversed, and the Pennsylvania Supreme Court later held that the statute did not apply to a permit holder merely because operations deviated from permit terms. Pennsylvania courts declined to reopen Fiore's identical conviction. The unresolved premise is whether Scarpone declared the statute's original meaning or changed state law.

### Issue 1: Authoritative state-law meaning

* **Controlling Simulated Law:** No controlling decision displaces the rule that a State's highest court authoritatively defines the elements and temporal meaning of state criminal law. Federal courts decide the constitutional consequence of that meaning.
* **Current Reusable Doctrine:** Threshold and adequate nonconstitutional questions precede constitutional adjudication. A court must not assume a disputed element of state law when an available authoritative answer can determine the federal analysis.
* **Other Governing Law:** Pennsylvania certification procedure; principles of state-law authority and federal habeas review.
* **Material Other Authority:** Commonwealth v. Scarpone, 535 Pa. 273, 634 A.2d 1109 (1993).
* **Open Point:** Whether Scarpone stated the meaning § 6018.401(a) had when Fiore's conviction became final or changed the law then applicable.

### Issue 2: Due process and proof of an element

* **Controlling Simulated Law:** Due process requires proof beyond a reasonable doubt of every element of the offense. A conviction cannot rest on conduct that the authoritative state-law definition did not criminalize at the relevant time.
* **Current Reusable Doctrine:** The prosecution must prove the enacted offense; a court cannot judicially supply a missing element or replace the State's authoritative construction. Retroactivity and sufficiency are distinct: if Scarpone stated what the statute always meant, the question is absence of proof of an element, not retroactive application of a new rule.
* **Other Governing Law:** Due Process Clause; In re Winship; Jackson v. Virginia.
* **Material Other Authority:** Bouie v. City of Columbia for unforeseeable enlargement, if the State instead contends that pre-Scarpone law permitted the conviction.
* **Open Point:** The due-process consequence remains reserved until the Pennsylvania Supreme Court answers the certified question.

## C. DECISION-MATERIAL LITIGATION POSITIONS

* Fiore's strongest position is that Scarpone merely declared the statute's constant meaning, making absence of a permit an element the Commonwealth could not prove because he had one.
* Pennsylvania's strongest position is that Scarpone changed or newly clarified unsettled state law after Fiore's conviction became final, leaving a separate retroactivity question rather than a simple sufficiency defect.
* The Third Circuit treated the dispute principally as retroactivity. That characterization depends on the unresolved state-law premise.
* Both sides must accept that the Pennsylvania Supreme Court controls the statute's state-law meaning, while this Court retains the federal due-process judgment.
* The National Association of Criminal Defense Lawyers supported reversal; several States supported affirmance. Their policy positions do not answer the antecedent state-law question.

## D. LAWFUL PATHS AND UNCERTAINTY

* Certify whether Scarpone stated the statute's meaning at the time Fiore's conviction became final; retain jurisdiction and reserve judgment.
* If Pennsylvania answers that the statute always required operation without a permit, the Court may then address whether the conviction lacked proof of an element and the appropriate habeas remedy.
* If Pennsylvania answers that Scarpone changed the law, the Court must address any preserved retroactivity, notice, or due-process theory on that premise.
* The only material uncertainty at this stage is the authoritative historical meaning of § 6018.401(a); deciding the federal question now would require an unsupported state-law assumption.

## A. CASE, RECORD, AND POSTURE

* **Case Name:** Los Angeles Police Department v. United Reporting Publishing Corp.
* **Citation or Docket:** No. 98-678; 528 U.S. 32 (1999); judgment below, 146 F.3d 1133 (9th Cir. 1998)

### Issue 1: Speech-selected access condition

* **Controlling Simulated Law:** Lanphere & Urbaniak v.
