## Original-decree issue: two genuine paths and the record each requires

### A. Preserve the original allocation against the omitted enlarged demand

**Rule proposed by O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy and Thomas.** A final interstate reserved-water allocation is not reopened to enlarge a reservation's previously litigated demand merely because the Government later changes its legal understanding of a land transaction whose material factual basis it possessed during the original proceedings. A later conditional provision allowing quantity adjustment when boundaries are finally determined does not by itself extinguish an existing preclusion defense to reopening the predicate; the operative decree and the prior litigation must supply the permission or other lawful basis.

Arizona II's refusal to reopen omitted practicably irrigable acreage supplies the strong finality premise. The original proceeding addressed the United States' reserved-water demand for Fort Yuma as well as the other reservations. The reported maximum-claim representation and omitted-claim warning confirm the understood final-allocation setting, while the prior availability of the relevant materials distinguishes a new factual claim. The subsequent Interior position is a change in the Government's legal treatment of the cession, not newly discovered physical facts. Calling the requested increment boundary water does not by itself make the additional demand a different claim.

The reported necessary-party distinction also matters. The earlier decision declined to resolve the Fort Mojave and Colorado River boundaries because of the specified private title and political-jurisdiction interests. That particular explanation cannot be generalized into a finding that every reservation's boundary was equally incapable of resolution. These four do not independently find that every possible Fort Yuma title interest was represented or adjudicate title. Their finality conclusion concerns the United States' already litigated water demand and does not depend on treating a tribal compensation claim against the United States as the same litigation.

**Strongest contrary argument and response.** The 1979 and 1984 decrees expressly cover all five reservations, the States stipulated to the 1979 terms, and Arizona II left open the possibility of further original-action litigation if the district-court route failed. These are significant judicial provisions, not administrative assertions. But the operative terms remain conditional: quantity adjustment follows an appropriate final boundary determination. They do not promise that every claimed boundary can be redetermined in the original action irrespective of otherwise available preclusion. Arizona II's reservation preserves a question; it supplies no affirmative answer. The States' requests for a boundary adjudication likewise do not erase their presented legal defenses by implication.

These four distinguish Agostini's actual change in controlling law and the PLRA decisions' enacted new prospective rules. No corresponding judicial overruling, statutory entitlement to reopen or final boundary determination is supplied here. Morgan Stanley and Plaut remain controlling at their defined statutory-reinstatement scope; this case has no comparable newly enacted entitlement. Their own dissents in Plaut reinforce their insistence on a lawful source for withdrawing the conclusive effect of an adjudication, while their Agostini and prospective-relief joins prevent a blanket assertion that every continuing decree is immutable.

**Scope limits.** The conclusion does not turn counsel's statement into a perpetual contractual surrender of every future water right. The source of the bar is the completed claim and the absence of a demonstrated applicable reservation overriding that finality, with the reported statements and available facts supporting identification of the original controversy. It does not hold that a new physical boundary change, later independently valid agreement or other materially different claim could never support adjustment. No exact quotation, missing qualification in the unrecovered transcript, or newly inferred necessary-party finding is an indispensable premise. If later neutral evidence shows that the original demand was expressly limited in a way excluding this claim from adjudication, the affected commitment must be reconsidered.

### B. Permit adjudication of the boundary-dependent claim under the later decree framework

**Rule proposed by Stevens, Souter, Ginsburg and Breyer.** The original decree's finality must be read with the later judicial provisions preserving adjustment for final boundary determinations and with Arizona II's express reservation of the possible original-action route. In this procedural setting, the earlier maximum demand and possession of relevant materials do not categorically foreclose adjudication of the unresolved Fort Yuma boundary-dependent claim; they neither establish a later final boundary determination nor erase the operative judicial treatment of that boundary question.

These four do not infer permission from the adjustment clause alone. Their conclusion rests on the combined procedural and adjudicative context: all-five-reservations coverage in the 1979 and 1984 decrees, the distinction Arizona II actually drew between omitted acreage within recognized boundaries and the separate boundary controversy, its refusal to treat Interior's determination as binding, and its express reservation of a possible original-action route if the district-court route failed. The States' stipulated decree and subsequent requests for judicial boundary determination confirm the setting in which those judicial directions must be read. They are not treated as an automatic waiver, estoppel or newly proved consent to abandon the defense.

The safe predecision account establishes the original intervention by the United States on behalf of the five reservations, the 1979 treatment of the Tribes' intervention requests and later referral, Arizona II's permission for the Tribes to represent their interests, the different treatment of omitted acreage and boundary determinations, and the district-court route's jurisdictional failure. The four rely on the Court's actual continuing treatment of this boundary question, not a general proposition that trustee representation is inadequate or that Tribes are immune from ordinary preclusion. Richards and Matsushita preserve lawful representation and binding effect; neither supplies a new tribal exception.

**Strongest contrary argument and response.** The new materials make it substantially harder to characterize the claim as simply unavailable in the original proceedings. The Government possessed the underlying facts and presented a maximum demand, and the identified private-claimant obstacle for the other two reservations is not established for Fort Yuma. These four accept those points. They nevertheless distinguish identification of the claim originally presented from construction of the decree as later supplemented: an omitted-claim warning does not adjudicate the disputed scope of later judicial reservations. The question is not whether a litigant may escape an inconvenient final allocation by changing its legal theory, but whether these later orders and the unresolved-boundary treatment leave this particular predicate for adjudication.

Their reading gives effect to the condition rather than treating it as already fulfilled. Rejecting the preclusion defense does not supply a final boundary determination, authorize the Secretary to bind the States or establish irrigable acreage. The Master must adjudicate the necessary unresolved matters before any additional water can be considered. Finality continues to govern omitted acreage within accepted boundaries and other claims actually foreclosed; no unrestricted reopening power follows.
