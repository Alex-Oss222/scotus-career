## Standards and Tests selection: Candidate financing, political-committee thresholds and membership exclusions

### Candidate financing, political-committee thresholds and membership exclusions

**Governing question:** Whether predominantly issue-oriented groups are exempt from FECA political-committee duties for otherwise covered candidate financing.

**Current rule:** Buckley and MCFL supply no categorical organization-wide major-purpose exemption for genuinely covered candidate financing. Section 431(4)(A) reaches a committee, club, association or other group receiving contributions aggregating over $1,000 in a calendar year or making expenditures aggregating over $1,000 in a calendar year, subject to actual definitions, exclusions and constitutional limits. Establish membership, covered transactions, actual candidate connection and thresholds before imposing duties. Where a contribution theory requires cooperation, consultation, concert, request or suggestion, ideological alignment, lobbying or partisan identity alone is insufficient. Independent expression retains its protection.

**Authority by component:**
- No categorical major-purpose exemption: Akins, Akins v. FEC — III. Major purpose is not a categorical exemption for otherwise covered candidate financing, Souter Part III with Stevens, O'Connor, Ginsburg and Breyer (five for both candidate-cash/conduit and broader covered candidate-financing subparts; Kennedy joins only the former), 1997-06-25.
- Distinct exclusions and reporting conditions: Akins, Akins v. FEC — IV. Membership exclusions and their distinct reporting proviso govern the remand, seven-Justice Part IV, 1997-06-25.

**Present operation effective:** Akins, 1997-06-25.

**Controlling construction:** Section 431(9)(B)(iii) excludes membership-organization or corporate communications to members, stockholders, or executive or administrative personnel when the organization is not primarily organized to influence federal nomination or election. Its separate proviso requires reporting direct costs of communications expressly advocating a clearly identified candidate's election or defeat when costs exceed $2,000 per election, except communications primarily about other subjects, under §434(a)(4)(A)(i) and, for a general election, (ii). Section 441b(b)(2) separately excludes corporate communications on any subject to stockholders and executive or administrative personnel and their families, and labor communications to members and their families; its nonpartisan registration/get-out-the-vote exclusion covers those respective recipients. Its separate-fund exclusion covers establishment, administration and solicitation for a political separate segregated fund by a corporation, labor or membership organization, cooperative or corporation without capital stock. Its loan exception concerns ordinary-course national or State bank loans under banking law and regulations. Sections 431(8)(B)(vi) and (9)(B)(v) cross-reference activity allowed by §441b without importing the $2,000 proviso.

**Open question:** Particular organizational or donor-disclosure burdens retain supported constitutional challenges. The construction supplies no automatic membership, transaction, threshold or liability finding.

**Holdings navigation:** Akins v. FEC (1997-06-25) — III. Major purpose is not a categorical exemption for otherwise covered candidate financing; Akins v. FEC (1997-06-25) — IV. Membership exclusions and their distinct reporting proviso govern the remand.

## Standards and Tests selection: Express advocacy through limited context under §100.22(b)

### Express advocacy through limited context under §100.22(b)

**Governing question:** When a communication lacking a listed electoral slogan is express advocacy under the FEC rule and §441b.

**Current rule:** Paragraph (b) requires all of the following: assessment of the communication as a whole, only limited reference to external events, an unmistakable and unambiguous electoral portion suggestive of only one meaning, and no reasonable difference over whether it encourages election or defeat of a clearly identified candidate rather than another action. A reasonable nonelectoral interpretation excludes coverage. Within these cumulative restrictions, limited context does not exceed §441b merely because the communication lacks a listed slogan.

**Authority by component:**

- Maine Right to Life Holdings 2–3: five-Justice statutory construction: Federal Election Commission v. Maine Right to Life Committee, Inc. — controlling Holdings, 1998-09-14.

**Present operation effective:** 1998-09-14.

**Exceptions and limits:** Constitutional vagueness and particular applications remain open. The construction changes neither paragraph (a), distinct contributions, membership exclusions nor protection based on a nonprofit’s actual characteristics.

**Holdings navigation:** Federal Election Commission v. Maine Right to Life Committee, Inc. — Pre-enforcement campaign-finance review and optional advisory opinions; Federal Election Commission v. Maine Right to Life Committee, Inc. — Contextual express advocacy and statutory limits.

## Standards and Tests selection: Nonprofit political spending outside a literal safe harbor

### Nonprofit political spending outside a literal safe harbor

**Governing question:** Independent expenditures by a political nonprofit with incidental business activity and insignificant corporate funding.

**Current rule:** Failure to meet a statutory safe harbor because of incidental business activity or absence of a formal policy against significant corporate contributions does not alone defeat MCFL protection. A voluntary political organization whose resources reflect political support rather than commercial accumulation, whose structure avoids economic claimants and corporate conduits, and whose actual business income and corporate funding are minimal and insignificant may receive as-applied protection. Material commercial accumulation, economic claimants or corporate or union conduit funding requires a different assessment. This independent-expenditure protection does not require a categorical ideological-nonprofit exemption from a restriction on direct candidate contributions out of the general corporate treasury.

**Authority by component:**

- MCCL receives protection on its actual nonprofit characteristics: MCCL receives protection on its actual nonprofit characteristics, 1995-05-15. Stone-Zsela's Opinion of the Court, Part IV, joined by all eight Associates: nine direct votes on this as-applied holding. No general overruling or endorsement beyond Austin's surviving scope is adopted.

- Stengel Holding 3: direct candidate gifts distinguished from protected independent spending: Kentucky Right to Life, Inc. v. Stengel — controlling Holdings, 1998-09-14.

**Present operation effective:** 1998-09-14, for the new component; earlier component authorities retain their stated dates.

**Exceptions and limits:** Section 211B.15(15)’s safe harbor requires no business activity, no economic shareholders or claimants, no establishment by a business corporation or union, and a policy against significant contributions from them. Missing the literal checklist does not itself establish the constitutional regulatory predicate. This does not overrule Austin in its materially different corporate setting or immunize future changes in nonprofit operations. Stengel separately rejects the claimed categorical exemption for general-treasury direct candidate contributions. Day’s responsive-financing judgment has no controlling five-Justice framework and supplies none of this rule.

**Holdings navigation:** MCCL receives protection on its actual nonprofit characteristics. Kentucky Right to Life, Inc. v. Stengel — Moot amended election claims and equitable judgment treatment; Kentucky Right to Life, Inc. v. Stengel — Corporate gifts, paid identification and distinct contribution limits.

## Standards and Tests selection: Intermediary contribution limits and meaningful association

### Intermediary contribution limits and meaningful association

**Governing question:** Fit of limits on contributions received by political committees and political funds.

**Current rule:** Contributions remain distinct from independent expenditures. A broadly restrictive ceiling on contributions received by political committees or funds must preserve meaningful collective political association and fit an identified corruption or circumvention mechanism. Realistic routes around valid candidate limits may be regulated; an insufficiently fitted ceiling cannot be sustained merely by invoking circumvention. The invalidation of the reviewed $100 aggregate annual ceiling establishes no permanent constitutional dollar minimum. Annual duration, actual recipient function, per-recipient versus aggregate operation, amount and demonstrated effect on collective advocacy must be considered together. Other available committees and aggregate political success do not alone cure a burden on the chosen association.

**Authority by component:**

- This $100 intermediary-contribution ceiling lacks sufficient fit: This $100 intermediary-contribution ceiling lacks sufficient fit, 1995-05-15. **Present operation effective:** 1995-05-15; the component authorities identified above.

**Exceptions and limits:** The reviewed Day recipient class and its exclusions remain as originally stated. Russell supplies no general approval of a $200 intermediary receipt ceiling, no permanent dollar floor, no immunity for sham or materially different structures and no unlimited candidate-contribution authority. Genuine independence and actual candidate-conduit status retain their separate requirements.

## Standards and Tests selection: Candidate contribution limits and anticorruption fit

### Candidate contribution limits and anticorruption fit

**Governing question:** What justifies direct candidate-contribution limits and differentiated source allowances.

**Current rule:** Candidate contribution limits must be closely drawn to a sufficiently important interest in preventing corruption or its appearance while preserving meaningful political association. No completed bribe is necessary, and equality of political influence is not an independent justification. Amount and inflation comparisons establish no permanent constitutional boundary; actual campaign scale, fundraising burden and cumulative restrictions matter. Under the adjudicated Arkansas classification, a registered committee receiving contributions exclusively from individuals and no more than $25 cumulatively from any individual during a calendar year may receive a $2,500 per-candidate, per-election contribution allowance while ordinary sources face lower ceilings. The actual dispersed-donor structure and its anticorruption fit, rather than a small-donor label, support the distinction.

**Authority by component:**

- Direct-candidate interest and close fit: Russell v. Burris — Canonical Decision Record, Holdings, “Contribution limits require actual anticorruption fit; two candidate categories fail here”, April 28, 1999; O’Connor Part I, all nine Justices.
- Exact dispersed-donor classification: Russell — Holdings, “The exact small-donor qualification supports a different candidate allowance”, April 28, 1999; O’Connor Part IV with Stevens, Souter, Ginsburg and Breyer.

**Present operation effective:** Russell, April 28, 1999.

**Exceptions and limits:** Office-specific dollar applications establish no general floor or safe harbor. Every small-donor qualification remains necessary to the adjudicated distinction; no unlimited candidate-giving right, immunity for sham structures or automatic validity of materially different classifications follows. A failed categorical challenge does not resolve a genuinely different office-specific application.

**Related current rules:** Intermediary contribution limits and meaningful association separately governs committee receipt restrictions and actual candidate-conduit fit.

### Political-committee coverage and membership communications

#### Akins v. FEC

**Docket or dockets:** D.C. Circuit No. 94-5088; no separate Supreme Court docket supplied.

**Decided:** 1997-06-25.

For informational standing and §437g(a)(8) review, see Akins v. FEC, D.C. Circuit No. 94-5088, decided 1997-06-25, Federal Courts — Electoral informational injury and statutory review.

##### III. Major purpose is not a categorical exemption for otherwise covered candidate financing

**Holding:** Buckley and MCFL do not establish an organization-wide major-purpose exemption from political-committee treatment for genuinely covered candidate financing under §431(4)(A). That provision reaches a committee, club, association, or other group of persons receiving contributions aggregating in excess of $1,000 in a calendar year or making expenditures aggregating in excess of $1,000 in a calendar year, subject to the actual definitions, exclusions and constitutional limits; it does not literally make every group that makes a contribution a political committee.

**Proposition-level authority:** Part III, including III-A for actual candidate cash transfers and genuine conduits and III-B for other genuinely covered candidate-connected financing, is joined by Stevens, O'Connor, Ginsburg and Breyer: five direct votes with Souter. Kennedy supplies a sixth vote only for III-A. Stone-Zsela reserves both subparts. No combination of separate rationales is necessary to make the five-Justice rule controlling.

**Material application:** Covered purpose, amounts and exclusions must be found for actual transactions. Where contribution treatment depends on candidate connection, cooperation, consultation, concert, request or suggestion must actually exist; ideological alignment, policy cooperation, lobbying or partisan identity alone is insufficient.

**Limits and questions not reached:** Genuine independent expression remains protected. The Court does not conclusively classify AIPAC or uphold every organizational or donor-disclosure duty in every application; supported particular-duty challenges remain possible.

**Treatment of earlier authority:** Buckley and MCFL's independent-expression narrowing does not create the claimed categorical exemption for covered candidate financing. Colorado Republican preserves independent expression and requires real candidate connection rather than organizational identity.

##### IV. Membership exclusions and their distinct reporting proviso govern the remand

**Holding:** The Commission must determine membership, covered transactions, candidate connection, calendar-year thresholds and applicable exclusions before imposing political-committee duties. Section 431(9)(B)(iii)'s membership-communication exclusion retains its separate reporting proviso; §441b(b)(2)'s exclusions and the §431 cross-references retain their different terms.

**Proposition-level authority:** Part IV of Souter's Court opinion is joined by Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Kennedy, Ginsburg and Breyer: seven direct votes. Part III's five-Justice construction governs the remand notwithstanding Stone-Zsela's and Kennedy's narrower positions.

**Material application:** Section 431(9)(B)(iii) excludes communications by a membership organization or corporation to its members, stockholders, or executive or administrative personnel if it is not organized primarily to influence nomination or election to federal office. The proviso requires a membership organization, including a labor organization, or a corporation to report costs directly attributable to communications expressly advocating election or defeat of a clearly identified candidate when those costs exceed $2,000 per election, except a communication primarily devoted to other subjects. Reporting follows §434(a)(4)(A)(i) and, for a general election, §434(a)(4)(A)(ii). This is neither the $1,000 calendar-year committee threshold nor an exception transplanted into §441b. Section 441b(b)(2) separately excludes corporate communications on any subject to stockholders and executive or administrative personnel and their families, and labor-organization communications to members and their families. Its nonpartisan registration and get-out-the-vote exclusion covers the same respective recipients. Its separate-fund exclusion covers establishment, administration and solicitation of contributions to a separate segregated fund used for political purposes by a corporation, labor organization, membership organization, cooperative or corporation without capital stock. The loan exception concerns a national or State bank's ordinary-course loan made under applicable banking law and regulations. Sections 431(8)(B)(vi) and (9)(B)(v) cross-reference activity allowed by §441b without importing the $2,000 proviso. The agency must apply each provision to the actual record. No final membership finding, coordination finding, threshold calculation, later membership rule, donor list, liability or penalty is supplied by this decision.

**Operative remedy or transition:** The five-Justice coverage construction governs remand notwithstanding the narrower separate preferences. Membership, actual covered transactions and each distinct exclusion require lawful Commission determination; no registration, donor list, disclosure award, enforcement order, liability or penalty is supplied.

### Contextual express advocacy and statutory limits

#### Federal Election Commission v. Maine Right to Life Committee, Inc.

**Docket or dockets:** 96-1818.

**Decided:** 1998-09-14.

##### The rule's cumulative restrictions preserve the statutory express-advocacy boundary

**Holding:** Under 2 U.S.C. §441b, 11 C.F.R. §100.22(b) does not exceed the statute merely because it permits limited context rather than an exclusive list of electoral slogans. Its coverage requires a communication considered as a whole, only limited reference to external events, an unmistakable and unambiguous electoral portion suggestive of only one meaning, and no reasonable difference over whether it encourages election or defeat of a clearly identified candidate rather than another kind of action; a reasonable nonelectoral interpretation excludes coverage.

**Proposition-level authority:** Opinion of the Court (Stevens). Parts II–III of Stevens's Opinion of the Court are joined by O'Connor, Souter, Ginsburg and Breyer: five direct votes. Stone-Zsela, Scalia, Kennedy and Thomas dissent from the statutory disposition and do not join this construction.

**Material application:** The Court holds that Buckley v. Valeo and FEC v. Massachusetts Citizens for Life require express electoral advocacy without prescribing a single verbal formula. The lower court itself recognized that MCFL included a marginally less direct explicit appeal; the disagreement is whether paragraph (b)'s contextual route necessarily crosses the narrowed statute's boundary. Its cumulative conditions need not do so. Election proximity, expected electoral effect, policy agreement, hostility toward a candidate, or an agency's preferred reading cannot replace the required unambiguous electoral direction. Limited context may inform the communication's meaning, but cannot displace a reasonable call to issue debate or some other action. The contrary concern that unchanged issue speech will be reclassified whenever an election approaches is answered at this statutory level by enforcing every restriction together, not by deferring to the Commission. A court must independently test the communication against them. This interpretation rejects categorical statutory invalidity; it neither decides the unresolved constitutional vagueness claim nor holds the rule valid in every application. It finds no MRLC publication unlawful and does not order enforcement.

**Treatment of earlier authority:** Buckley and MCFL retain their narrowing construction and protection of genuine issue discussion; the rule is permissible only within that boundary. Akins v. FEC's insistence on actual statutory conditions and independent judicial resolution is applied, without enlarging its distinct coverage holdings.

##### The unresolved constitutional challenge returns for adjudication

**Holding:** The categorical declaration that §100.22(b) exceeds statutory authority is vacated, and the case is remanded for the unresolved vagueness challenge and any properly presented application issues under the restrictive construction stated above. Subsection (a), separate contribution rules, distinct membership exclusions, and nonprofit protection based on actual characteristics are not invalidated or generally approved by this disposition.

**Proposition-level authority:** Part III of Stevens's Opinion of the Court, joined by O'Connor, Souter, Ginsburg and Breyer; five direct votes.

**Material application:** The Court removes the statutory premise of the judgment below, not the separate questions that premise made unnecessary to decide. A statutory construction alone cannot establish constitutional clarity in every application. No injunction against an otherwise unsupported enforcement threat, replacement rule, or judicial supervision of rulemaking follows. Day v. Holahan and MCFL require attention to an organization's actual political resources and financing; the acceptance of some corporate contributions does not establish MRLC's ultimate category, and no such determination is needed here. Lawful contribution enforcement and the Attorney General's dismissal remain separate. The remand permits the unresolved claims to be adjudicated without treating today's decision as a directive to prosecute.

**Treatment of earlier authority:** Day and MCFL retain their fact-dependent nonprofit protection. Austin v. Michigan Chamber of Commerce remains within its surviving corporate-treasury scope; nothing here approves every corporate restriction.

Related questions in Federal Election Commission v. Maine Right to Life Committee, Inc., 1998-09-14, dockets 96-1818, appear under Federal Courts — Pre-enforcement campaign-finance review and optional advisory opinions.

### Independent voter guides and candidate inquiries

#### Clifton v. Federal Election Commission

**Docket or dockets:** 97-786.

**Decided:** 1998-09-14.

##### Asking a candidate about public issues does not itself finance the candidate

**Holding:** Under 2 U.S.C. §441b, 11 C.F.R. §114.4(c)(4) cannot prohibit a publisher's mere inquiry to a candidate about positions or recorded votes as a contribution or candidate-directed expenditure. The required connection concerns campaign spending: actual cooperation, consultation, concert, request or suggestion concerning the expenditure remains governed by its own statutory predicates.

**Proposition-level authority:** Opinion of the Court (Souter), unanimous, at the stated narrow statutory and review-specific scope.

**Material application:** The Court holds that gathering information is not itself spending on a candidate's behalf. Buckley v. Valeo distinguishes independent expression from candidate financing; Colorado Republican Federal Campaign Committee v. Federal Election Commission requires a real candidate connection rather than a label substituting for that connection; Akins v. FEC likewise requires actual covered financing. MRLC's effort to learn the meaning of public votes supplies no finding that a candidate directed the guide's content, collaborated in distribution, or received an agreed campaign service. The Commission may investigate such arrangements when supported. Its concern that a survey could conceal collaboration cannot make every inquiry collaboration. This holding protects neither staged questions supplying campaign work nor a paid political advertisement disguised as an independent guide.

**Treatment of earlier authority:** Buckley's contribution/expenditure distinction, Colorado Republican's actual-connection requirement and Akins's transaction-specific coverage remain in force and apply to this information-gathering setting.

##### The existing written-only and equal-space declaration stands

**Holding:** In this private-petitioner review, the declaration invalidating §114.4(c)(5)'s compulsory written questions and replies and equal space and prominence remains in place. General rulemaking and enforcement authority does not authorize treating every independently edited guide involving candidate inquiry as a prohibited contribution or imposing these indiscriminate contact and editorial conditions on that premise.

**Proposition-level authority:** Opinion of the Court (Souter), unanimous, at the stated narrow statutory and review-specific scope. Stevens and Ginsburg join only within this private-petitioner review and its limited statutory ground; they do not adopt a general constitutional ban on contact safeguards.

**Material application:** The Court's limited statutory ground respects the difference between a real contribution and dialogue used to compose independent speech. A written record can aid enforcement, but administrative convenience does not establish that an oral discussion transfers value to a candidate. Miami Herald Publishing Co. v. Tornillo protects editorial allocation; Hurley v. Irish-American Gay, Lesbian and Bisexual Group of Boston protects the speaker's selection of an expressive composition. Those principles reinforce the need for actual statutory authority before requiring a private publisher to give every candidate equal space and prominence. The petition seeks broader protection for the private plaintiffs; no established cross-petition seeks enlargement of the Commission's rights by restoring restrictions already invalidated. That review limit independently preserves the existing relief. The Court does not decide that Congress could never authorize a specifically fitted contact safeguard, that accurate disclosure could never be required under lawful authority, or that proved coordinated services are exempt. A government-authored guide or a condition on a properly defined public resource presents different questions.

**Treatment of earlier authority:** Tornillo and Hurley reinforce editorial protection without becoming a universal prohibition on regulation of campaign contributions. Austin v. Michigan Chamber of Commerce is neither overruled nor treated as a delegation of every restriction on corporate speech.

##### The unfinished provisions and organizational status remain for the existing remand

**Holding:** The Court does not expand the declaration to invalidate the whole regulation or uphold the unresolved electioneering-message clauses. Their operative meaning, ripeness and severability remain for the District Court; MRLC's entitlement to nonprofit constitutional protection remains undecided on its actual funding characteristics.

**Proposition-level authority:** Opinion of the Court (Souter), unanimous, at the stated narrow statutory and review-specific scope.

**Material application:** The Court cannot supply an operative definition the appellate record leaves unsettled or decide severability from the label alone. The existing remand permits those questions and any properly supported interim-relief request to be decided under their own standards. Day v. Holahan prevents automatic denial of MCFL protection solely because an ideological nonprofit accepts some corporate money; it does not establish that MRLC's funding is insignificant, noncommercial or free of conduit features. The Court therefore neither grants an organizational exemption nor assumes commercial-treasury status. Genuine-coordination restrictions, express-advocacy provisions not successfully challenged, and distinct statutory membership exclusions retain their lawful scope.

**Treatment of earlier authority:** Day and MCFL require actual organizational and funding characteristics; Austin's surviving corporate-treasury rule remains available in its materially different setting. No prior decision is overruled.

### Campaign financing, intermediary contributions and nonprofit advocacy

#### Day v. Holahan

**Docket or dockets:** No. 94-672.
**Decided:** May 15, 1995.

##### Authority of the responsive-financing judgment

**Authority of the judgment:** The section 10A.25(13) responsive-financing invalidation is affirmed 5–4: Stone-Zsela, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy and Thomas support the judgment. Kennedy's plurality, joined by O'Connor, Scalia and Thomas, treats the adverse proportional legal trigger as an expressive burden; Stone-Zsela's judgment concurrence rests on insufficient programmatic connection on this record and rejects categorical invalidity. Neither framework controls, and no controlling rationale is established under Marks. The judgment binds the parties; the three independently majority-supported holdings below do not supply a unified financing rule.

**Limits and questions not reached:** The invalidated section 10A.25(13) mechanism increases candidate limits by independent expenditures opposing the candidate plus expenditures for the candidate's major-party opponents, excluding an association's communications solely to its dues-paying members. The provision itself has no $100 threshold; that figure describes the lower court's operational reporting scenario. The Board must notify every candidate in the race within 48 hours after receipt of the report or notice under section 10A.20(3), (6) or (6b), and within three days after notice must pay half the expenditure only to a subsidy-eligible candidate who has raised twice the minimum match, from an appropriation from the general fund. The lower court's electoral-result-eligibility concern describes implementation; it is neither a statutory payment exception nor a finding that every subsidy is immediately payable.

##### The special immediate-notice obligation falls with this trigger

**Holding:** Section 10A.20, subdivision 6b's additional immediate affidavit and service obligation cannot be enforced independently of the invalid responsive-financing mechanism on this scheme's demonstrated function and legislative relationship. Under Minnesota section 645.20, the special mechanism is essentially and inseparably connected with the invalid provision such that the legislature cannot be presumed to have enacted it alone; ordinary campaign reports remain enforceable within their separate terms.

**Proposition-level authority:** Stone-Zsela's Opinion of the Court, Part II, joined by O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy and Thomas: five direct votes. Stevens, Souter, Ginsburg and Breyer dissent on this component.

**Limits and questions not reached:** The additional obligation is a twenty-four-hour affidavit and service on the candidates and campaign treasurers in the affected race. The holding rests on essential legislative connection under section 645.20, not physical impossibility of reporting. The independent vagueness and overbreadth theories are not reached.

**Treatment of earlier authority:**

- Buckley, 424 U.S. 1: preserves distinct disclosure interests and associational protections; no categorical disclosure invalidation follows.
- Gibson, 372 U.S. 539: applies the need for a substantial relation between information demanded and the legitimate subject; does not eliminate ordinary reports.
- Brown v. Socialist Workers, 459 U.S. 87: recognizes genuine disclosure interests with protection for demonstrated associational harms; no new general exemption is created.

##### This $100 intermediary-contribution ceiling lacks sufficient fit

**Holding:** Minnesota's $100 annual ceiling on contributions received by the covered political committees and political funds is unconstitutional on the established burden and insufficiently fitted corruption/circumvention justification. Contributions remain distinct from independent expenditures: realistic routes around valid candidate limits may be regulated, but a broadly restrictive ceiling must preserve meaningful collective political association and fit an identified corruption or circumvention mechanism; no permanent dollar minimum follows.

**Proposition-level authority:** Stone-Zsela's Opinion of the Court, Part III, joined by Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer: nine direct votes, at this stated functional application.

**Limits and questions not reached:** Section 10A.27(12) covers aggregate annual contributions from individuals, political committees and political funds, but excludes a candidate's principal campaign committee and a political party unit defined in section 10A.275 from its covered recipients. Higher limits, coordination controls, candidate transfers and demonstrated conduit restrictions remain open. The record's substantial pooling burden does not establish a permanent constitutional dollar floor or equate contributions with independent expenditures.

**Treatment of earlier authority:**

- Buckley: applies contribution/expenditure distinction, corruption fit and effective collective advocacy; its historical dollar figure is no minimum.
- California Medical, 453 U.S. 182: preserves candidate-transfer anticircumvention regulation; its plurality is not a categorical answer for every committee.
- Citizens Against Rent Control, 454 U.S. 290: applies protection of pooling for collective political expression; does not immunize a candidate-contribution conduit.
- NCPAC, 470 U.S. 480: preserves independent advocacy at its scope; contributions are not silently equated with expenditures.

##### MCCL receives protection on its actual nonprofit characteristics

**Holding:** Minnesota may not prohibit MCCL's independent expenditures solely because its incidental business activity and absence of a formal policy against significant corporate contributions fall outside section 211B.15, subdivision 15's literal safe harbor. On the undisputed minimal-business-income and insignificant-corporate-funding record, its relevant political-association characteristics support as-applied MCFL protection; material commercial accumulation, economic claimants or corporate/union conduit funding requires a different assessment.

**Proposition-level authority:** Stone-Zsela's Opinion of the Court, Part IV, joined by all eight Associates: nine direct votes on this as-applied holding. No general overruling or endorsement beyond Austin's surviving scope is adopted.

**Limits and questions not reached:** The section 211B.15(15) safe harbor requires no business activity, no economic shareholders or claimants, no establishment by a business corporation or union, and a policy against significant contributions from them. Protection rests on MCCL's actual minimal business income and undisputed insignificant corporate funding. That checklist cannot foreclose this as-applied protection; materially changed operations or corporate or union financing may require reassessment. Direct corporate candidate contributions and ordinary business-corporation treasury restrictions are not decided.

**Treatment of earlier authority:**

- MCFL, 479 U.S. 238: applies protection to actual political-resource, structure and funding characteristics; descriptive facts do not foreclose this narrow as-applied claim.
- Austin, 494 U.S. 652: distinguishes materially different commercially accumulated corporate wealth; its surviving corporate-treasury rule is not overruled.

**Operative remedy or transition:** Affirm the Eighth Circuit on all four components. Maintain the permanent restraints on section 10A.25(13)'s responsive financing, section 10A.20(6b)'s linked special notice and section 10A.27(12)'s $100 annual ceiling, and MCCL's bounded as-applied protection under section 211B.15(15). Ordinary reports and otherwise lawful corporate and contribution regulation remain; no damages, general public-financing prohibition or facial excision of corporate restrictions follows.

### Anonymous individual political leaflets

#### McIntyre v. Ohio Elections Commission

**Docket or dockets:** No. 93-986.
**Decided:** April 19, 1995.

##### Compelled identification on these political leaflets requires exacting scrutiny

**Holding:** A content-defined requirement that an individual identify herself on ordinary political issue leaflets burdens core political expression, including the choice to speak anonymously. The State must satisfy exacting scrutiny by showing narrow tailoring to an overriding state interest; viewpoint neutrality or the election setting alone does not lower that standard.

**Proposition-level authority:** Opinion of the Court (Stevens), joined by Stone-Zsela, O'Connor, Kennedy, Souter, Ginsburg and Breyer: seven Justices; Thomas concurs in the judgment only.

**Treatment of earlier authority:**

- Talley v. California, 362 U.S. 60: applies protection of anonymous handbills to this individual political leaflet; the Court does not establish immunity for fraudulent communications.
- Burson v. Freeman, May 26, 1992: applies the independently majority-supported scrutiny distinction; its fractured polling-place validity judgment does not settle this leaflet requirement.
- Anderson v. Celebrezze, 460 U.S. 780: distinguishes the election-administration setting; it does not reduce scrutiny for every election-related speech restriction.

##### The asserted voter-information interest does not justify this identification demand

**Holding:** A general interest in helping voters evaluate a source does not justify requiring this ordinary individual to place her name and residence or business address on her independently produced, truthful political leaflet. The Court leaves candidate finance, campaign committees, organizational spending, and other materially distinct disclosure settings for separate analysis.

**Proposition-level authority:** Opinion of the Court (Stevens), joined by Stone-Zsela, O'Connor, Kennedy, Souter, Ginsburg and Breyer: seven Justices; Thomas concurs in the judgment only.

**Treatment of earlier authority:**

- Talley v. California, 362 U.S. 60: applies protection for the ordinary author's choice of anonymous publication.
- Buckley v. Valeo, 424 U.S. 1: distinguishes campaign-finance reporting and its specific interests; those disclosure holdings remain undisturbed.
- First National Bank of Boston v. Bellotti, 435 U.S. 765: distinguishes corporate-advertising source information; its observation does not resolve this private individual's leaflet.

##### Fraud prevention does not justify this application to truthful independent advocacy

**Holding:** Although preventing electoral fraud is a weighty interest, Ohio may not impose this categorical identification requirement on truthful, independently produced individual issue leaflets without the necessary narrow fit to that interest. The enforcement order and $100 fine must be set aside; properly tailored fraud, impersonation, and materially different disclosure rules remain available.

**Proposition-level authority:** Opinion of the Court (Stevens), joined by Stone-Zsela, O'Connor, Kennedy, Souter, Ginsburg and Breyer: seven Justices; Thomas concurs in the judgment only.

**Treatment of earlier authority:**

- Talley v. California, 362 U.S. 60: applies anonymous-publication protection while distinguishing measures directed at fraud.
- Buckley v. Valeo, 424 U.S. 1: leaves candidate-finance disclosure and corruption-related interests intact rather than treating them as dispositive of this ordinary leaflet.
- First National Bank of Boston v. Bellotti, 435 U.S. 765: leaves materially different corporate source-disclosure questions outside the individual sanction decided here.

**Operative remedy or transition:** Ohio's judgment is reversed, 8–1, and the enforcement order and $100 fine must be set aside. No damages or invalidation of every disclosure provision is ordered; candidate, committee, organizational and other materially different disclosure settings remain outside this application.

**Nixon v. Shrink Missouri Government PAC — January 24, 2000; O'Connor opinion, 6–3.** Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Souter, Ginsburg and Breyer apply Russell's government burden: a direct candidate-contribution ceiling requires an objectively grounded, sufficiently important corruption or appearance interest and close fit preserving meaningful association and effective campaign advocacy. Missouri's combined risk and practical-capacity showing satisfies that standard for the presented $1,075 statewide/state-auditor ceiling, indexed biennially from the January 1, 1995 $1,000 base and rounded to the nearest $25. Large-gift episodes and related official actions or accusations support corruption concerns without a finding of a completed exchange in each episode or a requirement of a proved bribe; ordinary agreement, access, wealth inequality and Proposition A's popular approval do not themselves justify the law. The property-related plea establishes its actual offense only. District Court findings of campaign capacity and substantial fundraising, the direct candidate channel, amount and indexing affirmatively support fit. Serious contrary evidence—combined spending falling from $21.599 million to $9.337 million, primary spending from $14.249 million to $1.625 million, contested primaries from ten to one, and candidates from thirty-two to eleven—does not establish this ceiling as each decline's cause or displace the supported capacity finding; no alternative cause is found and no decline is deemed harmless. The 97.62-percent contributor figure for gifts no larger than $2,000 establishes neither dollars contributed nor this $1,075 ceiling's effect. Challengers need not prove literal campaign impossibility. Russell's invalidated $300 statewide and $100 appellate-office limits and sustained residual/PAC applications retain their actual scope; no monetary floor, dollar safe harbor or automatic analogy follows. Buckley's contribution/expenditure distinction survives without displacing fit with deference; Day preserves meaningful pooling and actual circumvention fit for intermediary restrictions, and Colorado Republican preserves genuinely independent party expression. The Court reverses the presented invalidation, conditionally vacates the pending-appeal restraint insofar as operative, and remands for judgment for state officials. It establishes no continued injunction, new factual study, replacement amount, other-office approval, retrospective liability, refund or damages. A materially different practical-suppression challenge remains open. [Record](../records/nixon-v-shrink-missouri-government-pac_merits_2000-01-24.md)
