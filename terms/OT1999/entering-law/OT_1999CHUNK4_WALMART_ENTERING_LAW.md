## Standards and Tests selection: Inherently distinctive nonfunctional restaurant trade dress

### Inherently distinctive nonfunctional restaurant trade dress

**Governing question:** Whether acquired secondary meaning is additionally necessary under pre-1988 Lanham Act §43(a).

**Current rule:** Nonfunctional restaurant trade dress that is inherently distinctive may be protected without acquired secondary meaning if the remaining infringement requirements, including likelihood of confusion, are met. Protection does not expire after an unspecified period merely because acquired meaning has not developed. Functionality, distinctiveness and confusion remain independent requirements.

**Authority by component:**

- Inherent-distinctiveness alternative and rejection of an unwritten expiration period: Two Pesos, Inc. v. Taco Cabana, Inc., “Inherent distinctiveness suffices without acquired secondary meaning”, June 26, 1992; White, Stone, Blackmun, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy and Souter.

**Present operation effective:** *Two Pesos*, June 26, 1992.

**Exceptions and limits:** No general ownership of cuisine, decorative style, useful designs or generic matter, later product-design rule, new proof burden or resolution of the excluded functionality challenge follows.

**Holdings navigation:** Two Pesos, Inc. v. Taco Cabana, Inc., 505 U.S. 763 (1992) — Trade dress.

## Standards and Tests selection: Source-identifying color and functionality

### Source-identifying color and functionality

**Governing question:** Eligibility of color for Lanham Act protection.

**Current rule:** Color may receive trademark protection when it acquires source-identifying significance and is nonfunctional, subject to ordinary Lanham Act requirements including likelihood of confusion where required. Bare color requires secondary meaning; the law protects neither color in the abstract nor useful features. A feature essential to use or purpose or affecting cost or quality is functional, and protection cannot foreclose color shown competitively necessary. Source significance does not overcome functionality.

**Authority by component:**

- Color may identify source under the Lanham Act: Color may identify source under the Lanham Act, 1995-03-28. Justice Souter's opinion of the Court, joined in full by Chief Justice Stone-Zsela and Justices Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Thomas, Ginsburg, and Breyer: nine direct rationale votes.
- Functionality preserves useful and competitively necessary color features: Functionality preserves useful and competitively necessary color features, 1995-03-28. Justice Souter's opinion of the Court, joined in full by Chief Justice Stone-Zsela and Justices Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Thomas, Ginsburg, and Breyer: nine direct rationale votes on this operative limitation.

**Present operation effective:** 1995-03-28; the component authorities identified above.

**Exceptions and limits:** The decision does not resolve every aesthetic feature or color-market competition question or establish infringement merely from eligibility. The existing inherently distinctive trade-dress rule is not replaced by a universal secondary-meaning demand.

**Holdings navigation:** Color may identify source under the Lanham Act; Functionality preserves useful and competitively necessary color features.

#### Two Pesos, Inc. v. Taco Cabana, Inc., 505 U.S. 763 (1992)

**Docket or dockets:** No. 91-971.
**Decided:** June 26, 1992.

##### Inherent distinctiveness suffices without acquired secondary meaning

**Holding:** Under the pre-1988 Lanham Act §43(a), nonfunctional restaurant trade dress that is inherently distinctive may receive protection without proof of acquired secondary meaning, provided the remaining infringement requirements, including likelihood of confusion, are met. Protection does not expire after an unspecified period merely because secondary meaning has not developed.

**Proposition-level authority:** White's Opinion of the Court, joined by Stone-Zsela, Blackmun, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy and Souter, supplies seven votes at this level of generality. Stevens and Thomas agree with the judgment on separate statutory accounts; the Court's rule does not depend on combining their writings.

**Material application:** The jury found inherent distinctiveness, nonfunctionality and likelihood of confusion, but no acquired secondary meaning. These findings concern separate elements and are accepted for the limited granted issue.

**Limits and questions not reached:** The accepted jury findings are not retried. No ownership of Mexican cuisine, a general decorative style, useful designs or generic matter follows. The Court decides no later categorical product-design rule, no excluded functionality issue, no new burden of proof and no damages amount. No Supreme Court precedent is overruled.

**Operative remedy or transition:** The Fifth Circuit's judgment is affirmed on the secondary-meaning question. The infringement judgment remains undisturbed on that issue, without a new damages calculation or merits remand. Functionality, trade-secret and licensing issues outside the grant are not adjudicated.

**Treatment of earlier authority:**

- Inwood Laboratories, Inc. v. Ives Laboratories, Inc., 456 U.S. 844 (1982): applied for secondary meaning, functionality and §43(a)'s protection beyond registered marks; its distinct contributory-infringement holding is unchanged.
- Park 'N Fly, Inc. v. Dollar Park & Fly, Inc., 469 U.S. 189 (1985): applied for the statutory distinction between inherent and acquired distinctiveness and protection of source-identifying goodwill; no registration requirement is imported into §43(a).
- Kellogg Co. v. National Biscuit Co., 305 U.S. 111 (1938): its acquired-source-significance concept is preserved; it does not impose secondary meaning on accepted inherently distinctive restaurant dress.
- Abercrombie & Fitch Co. v. Hunting World, Inc., 537 F.2d 4 (2d Cir. 1976): its classification is used to distinguish inherent from acquired distinctiveness; this circuit authority is not represented as an earlier Supreme Court trade-dress holding.
- Chevron Chemical Co. v. Voluntary Purchasing Groups, Inc., 659 F.2d 695 (5th Cir. 1981): its treatment of inherently distinctive trade dress is approved on the question presented.
- Vibrant Sales, Inc. v. New Body Boutique, Inc., 652 F.2d 299 (2d Cir. 1981), and the contrary categorical reading of the Second Circuit trade-dress cases: rejected insofar as they require acquired secondary meaning despite accepted inherent distinctiveness; their distinct functionality applications are not decided.

#### Qualitex Co. v. Jacobson Products Co.

**Decided:** March 28, 1995.

**Proposition-level authority:** Opinion of the Court (Souter), unanimous.

**Treatment of earlier authority:**

- Two Pesos, Inc. v. Taco Cabana, Inc., June 26, 1992: applies the separation of qualifying source identification, nonfunctionality, and confusion; its inherent-distinctiveness rule for restaurant trade dress is not extended to bare color.
- Brown v. Gardner, December 12, 1994: applies fidelity to statutory terms without an invented category exclusion; it supplies no separate trademark proof rule.

**Holding:** Acquired source significance does not make a functional color protectable: a feature essential to the product's use or purpose or affecting its cost or quality remains outside trademark protection, and protection cannot foreclose color features shown to be competitively necessary. The record's absence of a competitive need for this particular green-gold shade defeats a categorical functionality objection to its eligibility; no general rule about every aesthetic feature is decided.

**Proposition-level authority:** Opinion of the Court (Souter), unanimous.

**Treatment of earlier authority:**

- Inwood Laboratories, Inc. v. Ives Laboratories, Inc., 456 U.S. 844, 850 n.10: applies the essential-use-or-purpose and cost-or-quality functionality boundary to the asserted color feature; functionality remains independent of source significance.
- Two Pesos, Inc. v. Taco Cabana, Inc., June 26, 1992: preserves the separate nonfunctionality requirement; neither its holding nor this decision confers rights in useful designs or generic subject matter.

### Two Pesos, Inc. v. Taco Cabana, Inc., No. 91-971

Merits decision, June 26, 1992

The Court affirms the Fifth Circuit's judgment on the reviewed secondary-meaning question, 9–0. Chief Justice Alex-Lamar Stone-Zsela and Justices White, Blackmun, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter and Thomas participated at argument and decision and all support affirmance.

Argued April 21, 1992. On writ of certiorari to the Fifth Circuit, [932 F.2d 1113 (1991)](https://static.case.law/f2d/932/html/1113-01.html), affirming the Southern District of Texas infringement judgment. The granted question is whether inherently distinctive restaurant trade dress—the restaurant presentation that identifies its source—may receive protection under the applicable pre-1988 Lanham Act §43(a) without acquired secondary meaning, meaning public association with a source. Petitioner seeks reversal because the jury found no secondary meaning. The separate challenge to the nonfunctionality finding is outside the grant.

White delivers the Opinion of the Court, joined in full by Stone-Zsela, Blackmun, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy and Souter. Scalia also files a concurrence without additional joiners. Stevens and Thomas each concur in the judgment in separate writings without joiners; neither joins White's opinion. All writings support affirmance.

Under the pre-1988 Lanham Act §43(a), nonfunctional restaurant trade dress that is inherently distinctive may receive protection without proof of acquired secondary meaning, provided the remaining infringement requirements, including likelihood of confusion, are met. Protection does not expire after an unspecified period merely because secondary meaning has not developed.

White's Opinion of the Court, joined by Stone-Zsela, Blackmun, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy and Souter, supplies seven votes at this level of generality. Stevens and Thomas agree with the judgment on separate statutory accounts; the Court's rule does not depend on combining their writings.

The Court holds that a source identifier need not acquire distinctiveness twice. Inwood distinguishes acquired public association from functionality and recognizes the broader protection available under §43(a). Park 'N Fly explains the statutory distinction between descriptive marks requiring acquired meaning and inherently distinctive marks. The same source-identifying distinction applies to the nonfunctional restaurant presentation accepted here. The jury found inherent distinctiveness and likely confusion, yet found no acquired secondary meaning; those findings concern different propositions and are not contradictory. Chevron Chemical properly applies the familiar Abercrombie categories to trade dress, whereas a universal secondary-meaning requirement has no basis in the operative statute. Requiring an initial period of unprotected copying would obstruct source identification and goodwill. The concern that protection might exhaust useful designs is answered by the independent requirements of distinctiveness, nonfunctionality and confusion. A temporary exemption followed by an unwritten deadline is equally unsupported. The Court accepts the findings for this limited review and leaves the excluded functionality challenge and other forms of product design undecided.

The Court applies Inwood Laboratories, Inc. v. Ives Laboratories, Inc., 456 U.S. 844 (1982) for secondary meaning, functionality and §43(a)'s protection beyond registered marks; its distinct contributory-infringement holding is unchanged.

The Court applies Park 'N Fly, Inc. v. Dollar Park & Fly, Inc., 469 U.S. 189 (1985) for the statutory distinction between inherent and acquired distinctiveness and protection of source-identifying goodwill; no registration requirement is imported into §43(a).

Kellogg Co. v. National Biscuit Co., 305 U.S. 111 (1938), retains its concept of acquired source significance; it does not impose secondary meaning on accepted inherently distinctive restaurant dress.

The Court uses the classification in Abercrombie & Fitch Co. v. Hunting World, Inc., 537 F.2d 4 (2d Cir. 1976), to distinguish inherent from acquired distinctiveness; this circuit authority is not represented as an earlier Supreme Court trade-dress holding.

The Court approves Chevron Chemical Co. v. Voluntary Purchasing Groups, Inc., 659 F.2d 695 (5th Cir. 1981), on the presented question concerning inherently distinctive trade dress.

The Court rejects Vibrant Sales, Inc. v. New Body Boutique, Inc., 652 F.2d 299 (2d Cir. 1981), and the contrary categorical reading of the Second Circuit trade-dress cases insofar as they require acquired secondary meaning despite accepted inherent distinctiveness; their distinct functionality applications are not decided.

The accepted jury findings are not retried. No ownership of Mexican cuisine, a general decorative style, useful designs or generic matter follows. The Court decides no categorical rule for other forms of product design, no excluded functionality issue, no new burden of proof and no damages amount. No Supreme Court precedent is overruled.

Effective June 26, 1992, inherent distinctiveness supplies an alternative to acquired secondary meaning for this nonfunctional restaurant trade dress under the applicable §43(a). The Court resolves the circuit disagreement by rejecting a categorical additional acquired-meaning requirement and an unwritten expiration period. The ordinary civil infringement burden, independent confusion requirement, functionality limitation and remedies outside this review remain unchanged.

Stevens supports the rule through the developed judicial construction of §43(a), its statutory purposes and Congress's subsequent endorsement; he does not regard the original text as having fully specified that development. Thomas supports the same result through §43(a)'s own false-description language and common-law source-identification rules, without importing registration provisions. Scalia finds Thomas's explanation complementary to the Court's opinion. These methodological differences do not limit the seven-Justice holding.

Supreme Court merits review ends with affirmance. The reviewed infringement judgment remains effective. The Court orders no new damages calculation, new remedy or merits remand, and establishes no further nonroutine Supreme Court action or remand condition. The excluded functionality objection and the independently resolved trade-secret and licensing issues are not decided or reopened.

**End of entry: Two Pesos, Inc. v. Taco Cabana, Inc., merits decision, June 26, 1992.**

### Qualitex Co. v. Jacobson Products Co., No. 93-1577

Merits decision, March 28, 1995

Argued January 9, 1995; decided March 28, 1995. On writ of certiorari to the United States Court of Appeals for the Ninth Circuit, reviewing its January 3, 1994 judgment, 13 F.3d 1297. The question is whether color alone can qualify for registration and protection under the Lanham Act.

All nine Justices participated at argument and decision.

The Court unanimously reverses the Ninth Circuit's categorical exclusion of color-only marks and its consequent cancellation direction. Chief Justice Stone-Zsela and Justices Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg, and Breyer join the 9–0 judgment. Color is potentially registrable under ordinary Lanham Act requirements. The full Court leaves the separately sustained §43(a) unfair-competition judgment, damages, and injunction undisturbed, without a new vote on their independent merits, a new award, double recovery, or withdrawal of existing relief.

Justice Souter delivers the sole opinion of the Court, joined in full by Chief Justice Stone-Zsela and Justices Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Thomas, Ginsburg, and Breyer. All nine adopt both the color-eligibility holding and its functionality limitation, together with the bounded remedy described below.

A color is eligible for trademark protection when it has acquired source-identifying significance and is nonfunctional, subject to the ordinary Lanham Act requirements, including likelihood of confusion where required. Color alone is not categorically excluded, but neither bare color without secondary meaning nor ownership of color in the abstract is protected.

The Court holds that a color can perform the source-identifying function the Lanham Act protects. Sections 1052 and 1127 describe marks by that function and contain no categorical exclusion for color. Gardner requires applying the enacted conditions, not adding a policy restriction absent from the text. Two Pesos protects qualifying trade dress and keeps distinctiveness, functionality, and confusion separate; it does not establish that bare color is inherently distinctive. Here the district court found that Qualitex's green-gold press-pad color had acquired secondary meaning and that Jacobson's copying was likely to confuse purchasers. Those findings are not replaced with new findings by this Court. Shade comparisons can be addressed through ordinary proof of source significance and confusion. The possible depletion of useful colors requires attention to functionality and competitive need, not rejection of every color mark. The Ninth Circuit's contrary category rule and cancellation direction therefore cannot stand. The independently sustained unfair-competition judgment remains intact, and ordinary statutory requirements govern further proceedings.

Two Pesos, Inc. v. Taco Cabana, Inc., June 26, 1992, supplies the separation of qualifying source identification, nonfunctionality, and confusion; its inherent-distinctiveness rule for restaurant trade dress does not extend to bare color. Brown v. Gardner, December 12, 1994, supports fidelity to statutory terms without an invented category exclusion; it supplies no separate trademark proof rule.

Acquired source significance does not make a functional color protectable: a feature essential to the product's use or purpose or affecting its cost or quality remains outside trademark protection, and protection cannot foreclose color features shown to be competitively necessary. The record's absence of a competitive need for this particular green-gold shade defeats a categorical functionality objection to its eligibility; no general rule about every aesthetic feature is decided.

The Court holds that trademark law protects source identification without granting control of useful product features. Inwood identifies functionality when a feature is essential to use or purpose or affects cost or quality. That boundary remains independent of secondary meaning: consumers' recognition does not justify excluding competitors from features they need for reasons unrelated to source identification. A color may sometimes perform such a function, and the availability of alternative colors must be considered in the actual product setting. The lower findings distinguish the usefulness of color generally in concealing stains from a competitive need for this particular green-gold shade. They identify no improvement in use, quality, or longevity attributable to the shade and describe numerous available tones. The Court relies on those findings without creating a new market definition or evidentiary presumption. The possibility that another color or market presents different competitive needs therefore does not support a universal color bar. It remains a reason to apply functionality in each proper case, not to dispense with that requirement here.

Inwood Laboratories, Inc. v. Ives Laboratories, Inc., 456 U.S. 844, 850 n.10, supplies the essential-use-or-purpose and cost-or-quality boundary applied to the color feature. Functionality remains independent of source significance. Two Pesos, Inc. v. Taco Cabana, Inc., June 26, 1992, preserves the separate nonfunctionality requirement; neither that decision nor this one confers rights in useful designs or generic subject matter.

Two Pesos retains its protection of qualifying trade dress and its separate requirements; its restaurant-trade-dress rule does not establish inherent distinctiveness of bare color. Inwood's functionality boundary remains controlling and is applied to the claimed color feature. Gardner supports applying the enacted statutory conditions. No general aesthetic-functionality test or new evidentiary presumption is adopted.

Effective March 28, 1995, the Lanham Act permits protection of source-identifying, nonfunctional color subject to ordinary statutory requirements. The categorical color exclusion is rejected; functionality, acquired source significance for bare color, and confusion where required remain distinct. Protection does not extend to color in the abstract, functional color, or color lacking secondary meaning. This decision does not resolve every color-market competition question or grant rights in useful or generic product features.

#### Source Notes

The [Ninth Circuit opinion, 13 F.3d 1297](https://law.resource.org/pub/us/case/reporter/F3/013/13.F3d.1297.91-56260.html) supplies the trial findings, copying, registration, component-specific judgment, and surviving relief. Official [15 U.S.C. §1052](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/USCODE-1994-title15/html/USCODE-1994-title15-chap22-subchapI-sec1052.htm) and [§1127](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/USCODE-1994-title15/html/USCODE-1994-title15-chap22-subchapIII-sec1127.htm) supply the operative source-identifying language. The Court makes no additional survey, competitive-market, or monetary finding.

#### Mandate, Remedy, and Stage

The challenged registered-mark disposition is reversed, and the case returns for proceedings consistent with color's eligibility under ordinary Lanham Act rules governing infringement, secondary meaning, functionality, and confusion. The existing lower findings remain part of the record; no new evidence hearing is commanded. The independently affirmed §43(a) judgment, monetary relief, and injunction remain intact, with no new award or double recovery directed.

## Official 1998 statutory text: 15 U.S.C. section 1125(a)


(a) Civil action
(1) Any person who, on or in connection with any goods or services, or any container for goods, uses in commerce any word, term, name, symbol, or device, or any combination thereof, or any false designation of origin, false or misleading description of fact, or false or misleading representation of fact, which—
(A) is likely to cause confusion, or to cause mistake, or to deceive as to the affiliation, connection, or association of such person with another person, or as to the origin, sponsorship, or approval of his or her goods, services, or commercial activities by another person, or
(B) in commercial advertising or promotion, misrepresents the nature, characteristics, qualities, or geographic origin of his or her or another person's goods, services, or commercial activities,

shall be liable in a civil action by any person who believes that he or she is or is likely to be damaged by such act.
(2) As used in this subsection, the term “any person” includes any State, instrumentality of a State or employee of a State or instrumentality of a State acting in his or her official capacity. Any State, and any such instrumentality, officer, or employee, shall be subject to the provisions of this chapter in the same manner and to the same extent as any nongovernmental entity.

## Official 1998 statutory text: trademark definition


The term “trademark” includes any word, name, symbol, or device, or any combination thereof—
(1) used by a person, or
(2) which a person has a bona fide intention to use in commerce and applies to register on the principal register established by this chapter,

to identify and distinguish his or her goods, including a unique product, from those manufactured or sold by others and to indicate the source of the goods, even if that source is unknown.
