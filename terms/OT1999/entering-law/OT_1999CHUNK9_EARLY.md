Source: `state/holdings/election-law.md`; exact heading: Burdick v. Takushi, 504 U.S. 428 (1992)

#### Burdick v. Takushi, 504 U.S. 428 (1992)

**Docket or dockets:** No. 91-535.
**Decided:** June 8, 1992.

##### What level of constitutional scrutiny applies to a restriction on voting or candidate access

**Holding:** A court must weigh the character and magnitude of the injury to First and Fourteenth Amendment rights against the State's precise asserted interests and the extent to which they require the burden. Severe restrictions must be narrowly drawn to advance a compelling interest; reasonable, nondiscriminatory restrictions ordinarily are justified by important regulatory interests, so strict scrutiny does not govern every election rule.

**Proposition-level authority:** White's opinion of the Court, joined by O'Connor, Scalia, Souter and Thomas; five Justices control the complete rule. Kennedy's dissent, joined by Blackmun and Stevens, and Stone's dissent also accept the calibrated framework without making their contrary application controlling.

**Material application:** A write-in prohibition must be examined with the available routes to ballot access and its role in the selection system. Neither the prohibition alone nor the general need for election administration decides the burden.

**Limits and questions not reached:** Ordinary administration remains subject to meaningful scrutiny. The holding neither imposes strict scrutiny on all ballot regulations nor extends *Burson*'s public-sidewalk campaign-content holding to the official ballot.

**Treatment of earlier authority:** *Anderson v. Celebrezze*, 460 U.S. 780, supplies the burden, precise-interest and necessity inquiry. *Norman v. Reed*, January 14, 1992, preserves actual access review and permissible electorate-specific support requirements, including its protection against cross-component exclusion. *Storer v. Brown*, 415 U.S. 724, recognizes substantial regulation of candidate selection while preserving review of severe barriers. *Burson v. Freeman*, May 26, 1992, concerns ordinary public sidewalks; neither its strict-scrutiny proposition nor its fractured judgment changes this ballot analysis.

##### Whether Hawaii's complete write-in prohibition is valid within the ballot-access system reviewed

**Holding:** Where the complete ballot-access system imposes only reasonable burdens, a write-in prohibition is presumptively valid and may be sustained by proportionate state interests. Hawaii's party and nonpartisan routes provide adequate access, and its prohibition validly supports primary finality, prevention of sore-loser candidacies and party raiding, and its treatment of uncontested primaries.

**Proposition-level authority:** White's opinion of the Court, joined by O'Connor, Scalia, Souter and Thomas; five Justices support both the rationale and affirmance. Stone, Blackmun, Stevens and Kennedy disagree with this application.

**Current force:** Remains controlling for the complete access system reviewed and its reasonable burdens.

**Material application:** New parties need one percent of registered-voter signatures 150 days before the primary; qualifying established parties retain access. Candidates need 15 signatures for legislative or county offices, or 25 for statewide or federal offices, 60 days before the primary. Nonpartisan candidates advance with ten percent of the primary vote or the votes sufficient to nominate a partisan candidate, whichever is lower; voters choose one party or nonpartisan primary ballot for all offices. These combined routes and restrictions support the limited-burden finding. Lawful selection deadlines need not accommodate every later preference.

**Limits and questions not reached:** Both primary and general-election applications within this system are reached. The Court does not independently validate every surrounding access rule or decide a prohibition within materially more restrictive access rules. Candidate qualifications and reasonable write-in procedures elsewhere remain open; no absolute write-in right or guarantee of competitive races is created.

**Operative remedy or transition:** Affirm the Ninth Circuit; its reversal of the district court's injunction remains effective. No prospective injunction or replacement election-administration scheme issues.

**Treatment of earlier authority:** *Jenness v. Fortson*, 403 U.S. 431, permits reasonable demonstrations of support. *Munro v. Socialist Workers Party*, 479 U.S. 189, permits primary winnowing and preventive regulation without proof of a particular past disruption. *Storer v. Brown*, 415 U.S. 724, supports primary finality and restrictions against circumvention. *Anderson v. Celebrezze*, 460 U.S. 780, governs this limited-burden application; the decision creates no exception to its inquiry. *Norman v. Reed*, January 14, 1992, retains protection against unjustified party-name and cross-component disqualification.

##### Whether the Constitution requires the State to count and publish fictional candidates or arbitrary protest messages

**Holding:** The First and Fourteenth Amendments do not require a State to count and publicize write-in votes for fictional figures or arbitrary protest messages as an independent form of expression. Elections select public officials, and a desire to use the ballot for such messages does not create an entitlement to a write-in option.

**Proposition-level authority:** White's opinion, joined by O'Connor, Scalia, Souter and Thomas, controls. Kennedy's dissent, joined by Blackmun and Stevens, and Stone's dissent expressly agree on rejection of this broader claim; all nine support the component without issuing a joint opinion.

**Limits and questions not reached:** A genuine interest in supporting an actual alternative candidate requires the contextual access analysis separately stated above. Political expression outside the ballot and every restriction on eligible candidates in a different system are not decided.

**Operative remedy or transition:** Reject the arbitrary-message tally claim; it supplies no independent ground for relief from the prohibition.

**Treatment of earlier authority:** *Storer v. Brown*, 415 U.S. 724, supplies the office-selecting function of elections; the ballot is not a general expressive forum. *Anderson v. Celebrezze*, 460 U.S. 780, protects electoral participation without requiring an arbitrary-message tally.

#### Timmons v. Twin Cities Area New Party

**Docket or dockets:** No. 95-1608.
**Decided:** 1997-04-28.

For the separate questions in Timmons v. Twin Cities Area New Party, 1997-04-28, see Federal Courts — Mootness and conditional reenactment.

##### This limitation is justified within the complete nomination system

**Holding:** Minnesota may prevent this otherwise qualified minor party from placing its consenting nominee on the ballot under its name when he also seeks or holds the other major party's nomination, where the complete system leaves the candidate available and the party able to nominate other candidates, endorse and campaign. In this application the burden is less than severe and proportionate interests in ballot integrity, defined nomination choices and a stable electoral system justify the restriction under Anderson and Burdick.

**Proposition-level authority:** Thomas's Part II, joined by O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy and Breyer; five direct joins.

**Limits and questions not reached:** The challenged 1994 §204B.06 subdivision 1(b) barred an affidavit when another affidavit was on file for any office at the same primary or next general election. Section 204B.04 subdivision 2 barred petition nomination for the same office by a primary candidate, subject to two distinct exceptions. Under §204D.10 subdivision 2, if any major-party primary candidate received ten percent of the average vote for the party's state officers at the last state general election in the relevant district, its highest-vote candidates became nominees; if none met that threshold, none were nominated and all could seek petition nomination under §§204B.07–.09. The relevant state officers were governor, lieutenant governor, secretary of state, state auditor, state treasurer and attorney general. The §204B.13 subdivision 4 nonpartisan-vacancy exception allowed a petition within one week of the vacancy and no later than four calendar days before election; an eligible voter could sign despite voting in the primary or signing another petition. Neither exception applied to Dawkins. The distinct ban on nomination by two major parties is not decided.

The conditional replacement permitted a major-party and one-or-more-minor-party nomination for the same partisan office with written consent of the parties' state chairs; petition nomination also required the candidate's written consent. Losing the major-party primary forfeited the simultaneous minor-party nominations, and a party candidate could not also file as an independent. It retained the nonpartisan-vacancy exception, barred a defeated major-party primary candidate except to fill a §204B.13 vacancy, and repealed the old primary-turnout provision. Its affidavit rule referred to any other office, required petition acceptance and the consent forms, and contained a distinct dual-office exception for a soil-and-water district supervisor outside Anoka, Hennepin, Ramsey and Washington counties and a mayor/council or town-supervisor office in a city or town of no more than 2,500 people wholly or partly within the district. That different-office exception is not fusion. A multiply nominated candidate's name appeared once with party names; major/minor nomination used the major-party position and multiple-minor nomination the first filing party's position. Votes for multiply nominated candidates were excluded from the specified party-status qualification or retention counts. These features neither establish compliance in this case nor receive independent constitutional approval.

The Court recognizes no right to duplicate votes, separate party vote totals or a particular ballot design and adjudicates no independent challenge to consent, counting, format, support or deadline rules.

**Treatment of earlier authority:** Anderson and Burdick retain contextual burden, precise interest and fit review. Norman's authorized expansion and independent-candidate protections and Colorado Republican's independent campaign speech remain distinct. Tashjian and Eu protect association without compelling every ballot mechanism; Storer, Jenness and Munro permit reasonable preventive selection rules without proof of past disruption. No voter-incapacity premise, incumbent-protection rationale, pure rational-basis rule or universal anti-fusion approval is adopted.

##### Reversal has the enacted transition consequence, without a retrospective election ruling

**Holding:** Chapter 419 §10 makes its amendments effective for the 1996 state primary and thereafter, suspends them while the McKenna judgment is stayed or its mandate recalled, and provides that reversal causes the amendments to expire and prior law to revive. This reversal engages that expiration-and-revival condition; the remand must respect it without assuming an unproved stay, recall or continuous intervening statutory regime.

**Proposition-level authority:** Thomas's Parts I and III, joined by all seven other Associates; eight direct joins. Stone-Zsela agrees to reach the merits without an additional statutory rationale.

**Operative remedy or transition:** Reverse and remand, 5–4, subject to expiration of the amendments and revival of prior law. The completed 1994 election is not reopened, and no intervening filing is adjudicated.

Source: `state/holdings/first-amendment.md`; exact heading: Hurley v. Irish-American Gay, Lesbian and Bisexual Group of Boston

#### Hurley v. Irish-American Gay, Lesbian and Bisexual Group of Boston

**Docket or dockets:** No. 94-749.
**Decided:** June 19, 1995.

##### Private parade composition and compelled inclusion

**Holding:** Applying a public-accommodations law to compel private parade organizers to include an identified contingent conveying a message they do not choose violates the First Amendment. A parade remains expression despite broad admissions, multiple themes and the absence of a single precise message; the rule does not exempt ordinary nonexpressive goods or services from nondiscrimination law.

**Proposition-level authority:** Souter’s Opinion of the Court, joined by all eight other Justices; nine Justices adopt the as-applied private-expression holding.

**Limits and questions not reached:** Public-street use under a neutral permit does not convert this private parade into a governmental forum. The dismissed and unappealed state-action/public-forum theory against the City is not revived. Independent review of intertwined expression characterizations retains credibility deference. Message misattribution in other settings remains open; no general antidiscrimination exemption or premise that ordinary customer identity is itself a message is adopted.

**Treatment of earlier authority:**

- West Virginia State Board of Education v. Barnette: applies protection against compelled expression.
- Miami Herald Publishing Co. v. Tornillo: applies editorial selection protection.
- Turner Broadcasting System, Inc. v. FCC: applies existing medium-specific editorial protection, without importing unrelated cable results.
- Roberts v. United States Jaycees; PruneYard Shopping Center v. Robins: distinguish access not materially altering the private expressive composition.
- Bose Corp. v. Consumers Union; Fiske v. Kansas: apply independent review of intertwined First Amendment characterizations while retaining credibility deference.

**Operative remedy or transition:** Reverse and remand with the injunction compelling GLIB's inclusion dissolved. The public-accommodations law remains enforceable where its application does not compel private expression; no fee amount, fine or sanction is decided.

### Write-in voting within the complete ballot-access system

**Governing question:** Whether a ban on write-in voting violates electoral-choice or independent expressive rights.

**Current rule:** Under the existing Anderson inquiry, the court assesses the character and magnitude of the actual injury against the State's precise interests and the extent to which those interests require the burden. Severe burdens require narrow tailoring to a compelling interest; reasonable, nondiscriminatory burdens ordinarily may be justified by important regulatory interests. When the complete access system imposes only reasonable burdens, a write-in prohibition is presumptively valid and may be sustained by proportionate interests, including primary finality and prevention of evasive candidacies or party raiding. The ballot need not provide an official count and publication of votes for fictional figures or arbitrary protest messages as an independent expressive entitlement.

**Authority by component:**
- Governing inherited framework: Burdick v. Takushi, “1. Review of election regulations follows the actual burden”, June 8, 1992; White, joined by O'Connor, Scalia, Souter and Thomas (five), applying Anderson and the earlier simulated Norman rule.
- Limited-burden write-in presumption: Burdick, “2. Hawaii's complete write-in prohibition is valid in its reasonably accessible system,” June 8, 1992; same five Justices.
- Arbitrary-message claim: Burdick, “3. The ballot need not serve as an official tally of arbitrary political messages,” June 8, 1992; same controlling five-Justice opinion, with all nine agreeing in rejection of that component.

**Present operation effective:** Burdick v. Takushi, June 8, 1992, for the write-in presumption and expressive boundary; the underlying Anderson framework is applied unchanged.

**Exceptions and limits:** No absolute validation of all write-in bans follows. Materially more restrictive access systems may impose severe burdens. The rule does not replace strict scrutiny of content-based campaign restrictions on public sidewalks.

**Holdings navigation:** Burdick v. Takushi, 504 U.S. 428 (1992) — Ballot access and political parties.

### Private parade composition and compelled messages

**Governing question:** Application of a public-accommodations law to an unwanted expressive contingent in a private parade.

**Current rule:** The First Amendment prohibits compelling private parade organizers to include an identified contingent conveying a message they do not choose. Broad admissions, varied themes and absence of a single precise message do not remove a parade’s expressive character. The protection concerns compelled insertion of expression, not an exemption for ordinary nonexpressive goods or services; ordinary customer identity alone is not a message.

**Authority by component:**

- Private parade composition and compelled inclusion: Private parade composition and compelled inclusion, 1995-06-19. Souter’s Opinion of the Court, joined by all eight other Justices; nine Justices adopt the as-applied private-expression holding.

**Present operation effective:** 1995-06-19; the component authorities identified above.

**Exceptions and limits:** Other expressive-association disputes require a comparably material expressive burden rather than a categorical exemption from nondiscrimination law.

**Holdings navigation:** Private parade composition and compelled inclusion.

#### Separate Positions Relevant to the Decision

Stevens dissents, joined by Ginsburg throughout and Souter in Parts I–II. A willing, sufficiently supported standard bearer is a central associational choice, and endorsing him or choosing someone else does not replace the Party's legally operative nomination. Even without declaring every such burden severe, they find inadequate fit: no defeated candidate seeks reentry, the candidate consents, the other party does not object, support requirements have been met, and another party designation adds no new individual to the race. Norman, Tashjian and Eu require attention to that actual association; Burdick permits reasonable prevention but does not make every general interest sufficient. Stevens and Ginsburg further reject preserving established parties' competitive position as a reason to exclude this consensual alliance. They would affirm at the as-applied scope, without mandating duplicate votes, separate party totals or a court-designed ballot.

Souter also dissents separately. Less-than-severe burden does not end the inquiry into fit. He agrees that the present interests are inadequate, but does not join a categorical rejection of every stability justification. A demonstrated threat to effective governing and a connection between fusion and that threat might justify a restriction; he leaves that question open. His Norman and Burdick positions preserve support requirements and primary finality, neither of which explains this supported nomination of the primary winner.

Source: `state/holdings/business-and-commercial-law.md`; exact heading: United States v. Winstar Corp., 518 U.S. 839 (1996)

#### United States v. Winstar Corp., 518 U.S. 839 (1996)

**Docket or dockets:** No. 95-865; consolidated Glendale, Winstar and Statesman disputes.
**Decided:** July 1, 1996.

##### What actual undertaking and jurisdiction the contract claim requires

**Holding:** The Tucker Act, 28 U.S.C. §1491(a)(1), supplies contract jurisdiction but creates no substantive promise. A claimant must establish an actual authorized express or implied-in-fact undertaking, not an obligation imposed only for fairness; review of the certified interlocutory partial-liability judgment does not require a final damages award.

**Proposition-level authority:** Souter’s Part I, unanimous.

**Limits and questions not reached:** The Court of Appeals found Winstar’s agreement express; the implied-in-fact alternative and takings claims are not independently decided.

**Treatment of earlier authority:** Hercules supplies the actual-duty requirement; Romein distinguishes regulation from a contractual undertaking.

##### Whether the transaction writings promise the particular accounting and capital treatment

**Holding:** The integrated Glendale, Winstar and Statesman writings establish express promises of the specified accounting and capital treatment and allocate the financial risk of its withdrawal, subject to the actual contractual qualifications. Statesman’s capital-credit undertaking is distinct from its goodwill allowance; neither regulation alone nor general assistance policy creates the promises.

**Proposition-level authority:** Souter’s Parts II–IV and VII, joined by Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O’Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Thomas and Breyer; eight Justices.

**Material application:** The operative terms are distinct:

- **Glendale:** The November 19, 1981 Supervisory Action Agreement preserves contemporaneous Board/FSLIC resolutions and letters rather than superseding them. Resolution 81-710 requires a satisfactory independent accountant's opinion describing goodwill, justifying purchase accounting and substantiating amounts and amortization, with compliance with Memorandum R-31b. The approved accountant treatment allocates $18 million over twelve years and $716.666 million over forty years, straight line. The lower judgment found the accounting condition satisfied; the Court does not turn that finding into a concession or treat every dollar as forty-year goodwill.
- **Winstar:** The integrated agreement, Resolution 84-363 and July 13, 1984 letter permit straight-line amortization of purchase-accounting intangibles over a period not exceeding thirty-five years for regulatory reporting. The maximum is permission, not a required period or a promised damages term. Those accounting forbearances are not acceptable in financial statements required to follow GAAP. Ongoing reserve/net-worth records and regulatory reporting remain required. The Net Worth Maintenance obligation concerns the required capital level, while the agreement's accounting terms define what counts toward it; a change in level is not identical to withdrawal of a promised component.
- **Statesman goodwill:** The March 11, 1988 agreement and Resolution 88-169 permit straight-line amortization not exceeding twenty-five years, subject to a satisfactory accountant's analysis within ninety days describing intangibles and substantiating amounts and methods. The lower judgment found the condition satisfied. Approximately $25.8 million of acquisition goodwill is distinct from the capital credit.
- **Statesman capital credit:** The express $26 million credit consists of $21 million of the FSLIC contribution and $5 million debenture principal. It is credited to the regulatory capital account and constitutes regulatory capital under the identified Insurance Regulation §561.13 definition. Its amount and scope do not borrow the goodwill undertaking's twenty-five-year maximum.

**Limits and questions not reached:** No universal insurance against later regulation, perpetual exemption or finding of insolvency causation. The separate maximum periods are permissions, not mandatory amortization or damages periods.

##### How accounting hierarchy, regulatory reservations and termination terms qualify those promises

**Holding:** The incorporated specific benefits retain contractual significance alongside general assistance terms and ordinary regulatory evolution. The whole integrated agreement controls; neither its long accounting permission nor its shorter ordinary assistance term automatically erases the other, and no defined termination event is presumed.

**Proposition-level authority:** Souter’s Parts II–IV and VII, joined by Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O’Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Thomas and Breyer; eight Justices.

**Material application:** The following actual terms qualify the identified undertakings and subsequent relief:

**Accounting hierarchy and reserved powers.** Winstar §10 begins with GAAP for going-concern savings-and-loan computations except where the agreement, applicable regulations or contemporaneous Board approval requires otherwise. Ambiguity is resolved consistently with the regulations and approval; when those conflict, the Board's transaction resolution or action controls. The section also includes subsequent Board/FASB clarification, interpretation and amendment, with Board requirements controlling conflicts with FASB. Both priority and change language remain operative.

The July 13 letter reserves statutory powers except enforcement of its enumerated waived requirements and their authorizing statutory provisions insofar as waived. The referenced benefits concern direct net-worth credit for the FSLIC cash contribution; the amortization just described; loan-discount recognition by a level-yield method assuming contractual payments for fifteen years and repayment of the balance at that point, or another level-yield method described in R memoranda or other accounting documents acceptable under GAAP; and dividend limits. The dividend waiver permits sixty, seventy and sixty percent of net income for fiscal years beginning July 1, 1985, 1986 and 1987 respectively, while forbidding dividends that reduce net worth below the level §563.13 requires for institutions insured at least twenty years. These ancillary terms identify the reservation's scope and add no separate damages claim. Powers outside those waivers remain reserved; the exception is neither an unconditional cancellation right nor surrender of Congress's authority.

Statesman §17 similarly preserves agreement/approval exceptions to GAAP, resolves ambiguity by the regulations and transaction approval, and gives that approval priority over conflicting regulations. Its changing-accounting language refers to Board changes, amendments, clarifications and interpretations, including the proposal at 52 Fed. Reg. 39112, its staff, FASB and the stated AICPA-successor reference; Board interpretation controls a conflict with FASB or AICPA. Real-estate valuation separately follows the proposed regulations at 52 Fed. Reg. 39070, those regulations as finally adopted by the Board, or any successor regulations then in effect. It is not simply Winstar's clause under another name.

**Compliance, duration and termination.** Glendale's assistance agreement ordinarily terminates after ten years, when FSLIC payment obligations cease. Appointment of a receiver or other official to liquidate Glendale, or termination of Glendale's or its successor's FSLIC-insured status, terminates the agreement and all FSLIC obligations at that event. Contemporaneous resolutions and letters remain preserved; modifications require written agreement by the parties or successors. The agreement requires no unlawful action or inaction. The particular forward accounting benefit is construed with, not equated to, the ordinary assistance/payment term.

Winstar §13(a) ordinarily ends the agreement after two years except as otherwise specifically provided, or at a different date agreed in writing. Appointment of a receiver to liquidate the resulting association, or cessation of its insured-account status, separately terminates the agreement and FSLIC obligations at that event. Section 15 requires no unlawful action or inaction. Section 13(b) concerns limited continuing indemnity for the liabilities/claims and related or prosecution litigation expenses it identifies, conditioned on litigation at termination and continued observance of the agreement's conditions, covenants, cooperation and best-efforts duties, with recoveries payable over under its referenced limits. It is not a general continuation of every contractual claim; this judgment does not decide an interaction with a receiver event or find a qualifying indemnity claim.

Statesman §20 requires the acquiring association and its subsidiaries, affiliates and service corporations materially to comply with applicable public laws, regulations, orders and restrictions governing property and business, but does not itself require compliance while applicability is contested in good faith. The proviso establishes neither an actual contest nor an exemption from valid public law. Section 19 ordinarily supplies a five-year term or a different written agreed date, with its specific-exception language and separate §16 sale provision. Appointment of a receiver to liquidate the acquiring association or cessation of its insured-account status terminates the agreement and FSLIC obligations. Its lawful-action term remains. FIRREA's enactment within the five-year period defeats only an assertion that ordinary expiration had already eliminated every undertaking; it decides neither other triggers nor recoverable duration.

Statesman §19(b) directs distribution/payment of the Special Reserve Account's net credit or debit at termination under the agreement. Section 19(c)'s identified liability/claim and related litigation indemnities require litigation at termination and continuing covenants, cooperation, reporting and best efforts; recoveries must be paid over under the applicable limitations, and continuation remains subject to the liquidation/insurance terminating proviso. Section 19(d) requires action on reports, applications, requests and other submissions actually received by termination that require FSLIC approval, consent or action, with cooperation and final-accounting treatment. None is a general survival clause or a finding of an eligible claim or pending submission in this case.

**Statesman's separate sale conditions.** During seven years, a defined Sale by the acquiring association of any of its own assets, or of capital stock of a subsidiary or affiliate formed to conduct its operations or hold its assets, requires prior written approval and prompt payment of an agreed percentage of net proceeds, capped at fifty percent. In calculating net proceeds, an appropriate return of the acquirer's investment is allocated in proportion to the assets sold relative to total assets. Sale aggregates sales consummated or agreed during ninety days with an aggregate sales price of $20 million. An unapproved defined Sale during five years terminates the agreement and FSLIC obligations immediately after consummation notwithstanding §19; approval lies in FSLIC's sole discretion. The exclusion concerns transfers to an acquirer affiliate of stock or assets and liabilities of Federated Financial, Combined Mortgage Corporation, Homestead Mortgage Corporation or Perpetual Financial Corporation, not every affiliate transaction. Seven-year approval/proceeds duties and the five-year termination consequence remain distinct. No such Sale, approval, excluded transfer or termination is found.

**Limits and questions not reached:** No general indemnity clause preserves every claim. No liquidation, insurance termination, defined Sale, qualifying transfer, contest, indemnity claim, pending submission or damages cutoff is found merely because its clause exists.

##### Whether actual assistance authority permits financial undertakings while preserving legislation

**Holding:** The transaction-effective contracting and assistance powers, exercised through the actual resolutions and approvals, authorize the identified financial undertakings. Separate authority to prevent Congress from changing law is unnecessary for ordinary monetary responsibility because these contracts confer no regulatory veto; general agency mission alone would not cure an unauthorized promise.

**Proposition-level authority:** Souter’s Parts II–IV and VII, joined by Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O’Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Thomas and Breyer; eight Justices.

**Material application:** Former §1725(c)(3) authorized FSLIC contracting and transaction-effective §1729(f) authorized conditional assistance. Glendale authority covered default prevention/restoration and merger or liability assumption; contributions and guarantees were limited to reasonably necessary liquidation savings unless continued operation was found essential to adequate community savings or home-financing services. Actual resolutions supplied the required determinations and approvals. Winstar and Statesman likewise depend on effective powers and approvals.

**Limits and questions not reached:** Congress may change capital law and institutions must obey it. No hypothetical appropriation/approval defect, equitable right to payment, regulatory injunction or inferred general assistance warranty.

##### Whether withdrawal breaches the promises and assumed risk defeats sovereign-acts discharge

**Holding:** Withdrawal of the promised treatment breaches these financial undertakings. Even assuming public and general sovereign action, sovereign-acts protection and ordinary impossibility do not discharge the change-of-law risk the contracts allocated to the Government; this sufficient ground preserves legislative authority and imposes financial responsibility within the actual bargain.

**Proposition-level authority:** Souter’s Parts II–IV and VII, joined by Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O’Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Thomas and Breyer; eight Justices.

**Material application:** FIRREA’s goodwill limits and OTS’s distinct §567.1(w) capital-credit treatment prevent the promised performance. The latter treatment is regulatory, not statutory wording expressly naming capital credits. Horowitz does not improve the sovereign’s position over a private promisor on assumed risk.

**Limits and questions not reached:** No majority selects a complete unmistakability framework: Souter/Stevens/O’Connor/Breyer’s limited inapplicability ground has four votes, Scalia/Kennedy/Thomas’s application-and-satisfaction ground three, and Stone-Zsela supports the narrower actual risk allocation. The substantial-contractual-effect sovereign-acts alternative has Souter/Stevens/Breyer only and is not an independent alternative holding. No majority rule excludes all monetary claims from unmistakability or classifies all FIRREA as non-general.

##### What partial liability establishes about causation and relief

**Holding:** Partial contractual liability is affirmed for all three transactions, including Statesman’s separate goodwill and capital credit, with contract-specific causation, compensable loss, mitigation, applicable contractual periods and damages left for ordinary adjudication. Breach does not establish every consequence or a nominal goodwill/capital-credit award.

**Proposition-level authority:** Souter’s Parts II–IV and VII, joined by Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O’Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Thomas and Breyer; eight Justices.

**Material application:** Each institution must prove recoverable loss under its own bargain and lawful defenses. One component cannot borrow another’s amount or duration; any properly proved termination or other qualifier remains material.

**Limits and questions not reached:** No automatic insolvency causation, guaranteed usability of the whole goodwill balance, fixed recovery period, damages amount, takings entitlement or compelled specific performance against valid law.

**Operative remedy or transition:** Partial liability affirmed, 8–1, and cases remanded for genuinely remaining contract proceedings; no mandatory trial or hearing. Ginsburg would vacate present liability affirmance for bounded contract-by-contract reconsideration under unmistakability, not enter Government judgment.

**Treatment of earlier authority:**

- **Hercules:** Its actual-authorized-duty requirement controls. The Court distinguishes the absent reimbursement undertaking there from the specific integrated promises here, while preserving its limits on fairness-based liability and automatic damages.
- **Sutton and Merrill:** Actual governmental authority remains necessary; neither the Tucker Act nor equitable appeal supplies missing assent or authority. No particular approval defect is found here.
- **Romein:** Regulation governing a relationship is not itself an immutable contractual obligation. These holdings rest on the additional transaction writings and incorporated promises.
- **Cisneros:** The obligation is enforced with its real qualifications, not enlarged into an unconditional payment or regulatory guarantee.
- **United States v. Texas:** An existing obligation is distinct from a mechanism governing its enforcement. It supplies no damages amount or formula in this case.
- **Lynch and Perry:** Genuine government contractual obligations remain legally significant despite sovereign status. This decision preserves legislation and applies the financial undertakings actually made; it does not adopt a general power to enjoin legislative change.
- **Horowitz:** Public/general sovereign treatment does not itself answer ordinary assumed-risk impossibility. The eight-Justice holding rests on allocated risk, not the additional three-Justice substantial-effect theory.
- **Bowen:** Its retained-power and promise-construction principles remain. The Court does not convert the statutory arrangement there into a rule cancelling every express financial-risk undertaking here.
- **Merrion, United States Trust and Home Telephone:** Retained governmental powers and the particular commitments at issue must be distinguished. No majority adopts a universal rule making the sovereign-construction doctrine irrelevant to all money claims.
- **OPM v. Richmond:** Authority and appropriations limits are not erased by monetary relief; no unsupported defect or general equitable right to payment is found.

No earlier decision is overruled. The additional unmistakability and substantial-effect explanations have only the joins stated in the topology; they do not independently alter controlling doctrine.

### Actual federal contractual undertakings and allocated regulatory risk

**Governing question:** Formation, authority, scope and regulatory-risk allocation in a Tucker Act contract claim.

**Current rule:** Section 1491(a)(1) supplies jurisdiction, not a substantive promise: an actual authorized express or implied-in-fact undertaking is necessary, and fairness cannot create an obligation implied only in law. The Government’s ordinary specifications warranty assures performance of the prescribed procurement, but does not itself promise payment of a manufacturer’s third-party tort settlements or defense expenses. Compelled procurement and Defense Production Act §707 protection do not themselves create an indemnity or reimbursement promise. A demonstrated authorized financial undertaking may allocate the risk that valid later legislation prevents the promised treatment. Even assuming public and general sovereign action, ordinary impossibility does not discharge that assumed change-of-law risk. The actual bargain and its qualifications govern; general regulatory permission alone creates no immutable promise.

**Authority by component:**

- Hercules — actual authorized undertaking, specifications-warranty scope and §707 limits, 1996-03-04; Souter, Stone-Zsela, Scalia, Kennedy, Thomas and Ginsburg (six).
- Winstar — holdings 1 and 3–5, actual authority, allocated risk and ordinary contract loss, 1996-07-01; all nine support the actual-undertaking requirement; Souter, Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O’Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Thomas and Breyer (eight) support the common financial-risk and assumed-risk holdings.

**Present operation effective:** Winstar, July 1, 1996, for allocated regulatory risk; Hercules’s scope and authorization limits remain.

**Exceptions and limits:** Congress retains its legislative power and the promisee must obey valid law. Contractual responsibility creates no injunction, automatic causation, goodwill-balance award, guaranteed recovery duration or takings entitlement. Causation, recoverable loss, mitigation, actual periods, conditions and termination provisions remain independent. A genuine authorized undertaking may cover third-party losses without an express indemnity; neither uncertain damages nor tort immunity destroys an actual contract.

**Open question:** Winstar supplies no majority comprehensive unmistakability framework or substantial-contractual-effect exception to sovereign acts. Its four-Justice threshold account and three-Justice substantial-effect alternative are noncontrolling; the eight-Justice assumed-risk holding does not depend on either.

#### Separate Positions Relevant to the Decision

**Souter's Part V, joined by Stevens, O'Connor and Breyer:** The unmistakability doctrine protects against an effective surrender of sovereign authority, but these particular financial undertakings leave Congress free to regulate. Lynch's recognition of government obligations and the distinctions in Bowen and Merrion require identifying what the promise and remedy would actually do. A demand for money does not automatically avoid the doctrine: repayment that effectively supplies a tax or comparable sovereign exemption can implicate it. Here, however, the authorized transaction allocated financial risk rather than immunizing institutions from regulation. The Government cannot obtain the acquired liabilities' assumption on the particular promised terms and then erase the ordinary contractual consequence merely by invoking its power to change law. Stone does not join this complete threshold account, and Scalia, Kennedy and Thomas instead apply the doctrine.

**Souter's Part VI, joined by Stevens and Breyer:** Independently of assumed risk, these three would reject the sovereign-acts protection where release from the Government's own contractual obligations is a substantial effect of the challenged measure rather than an incidental consequence of public regulation. The inquiry is objective. They do not require subjective bad faith, a predominant contractual-avoidance motive or predominant contractual effect. Inclusion in a broad financial reform statute does not itself make direct release from the promised accounting/capital performance incidental. The particular disallowance of that treatment has the required substantial effect for them. O'Connor withholds this ground; the other affirmers rely on the independent assumed-risk conclusion. The additional theory has three votes.

**Scalia, joined by Kennedy and Thomas, concurring:** Unmistakability applies and is satisfied. But the unmistakable subject of these bargains was the identified future accounting and capital treatment. The Government need not utter a second promise that it will keep the first before that actual promise has effect. Incorporation, priority, duration, reservation and compliance terms must all be read, not replaced by a general rescue-policy assumption. They find the particular risk assignment clear even under the sovereign-contract canon, while recognizing immediate regulatory permission could form a different bargain in another case.

Clear assumption of this risk defeats the asserted sovereign-acts and impossibility discharge. Damages still require proof under the individual contracts.

#### The school may not delegate its official religious choice to a student majority

**Holding and operative rule:** A public secondary school may not organize an invocation as a representative component of its own football pregame ceremony and delegate to a student-majority election whether that official component will include religious exercise. The challenged board-created election mechanism violates the Establishment Clause under both its unrestricted and nonsectarian/nonproselytizing versions; relief reaches that mechanism while preserving genuinely voluntary private prayer, independent student expression, lawful secular messages and genuine neutral access.

**Authority:** Justice Breyer's opinion of the Court is joined in full by Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Kennedy, Souter, Ginsburg, supplying seven direct votes for the rule, explanation and limited affirmance. The particular government-created representative religious choice is essential to all seven joins; the Court adopts no independent religious-pressure holding.

**Controlling explanation:** Agostini requires identification of the governmental act and its function, using purpose, effect and institutional relationships as evidence within the modified Lemon inquiry. Here the board creates an invocation or message slot within its own ceremony; the student council conducts a majority election under the principal's advice and direction; the electorate selects a single volunteer; and the speaker must serve the board's prescribed purposes. This is an official representative component, not generally available access for independently chosen private speakers. Lee forbids official school organization of worship, and Ingebretsen explains why student initiation or delivery cannot alone make that exercise private. A majority vote cannot privatize the board's delegated religious choice.

The District stresses secular purposes, student authorship, no required prayer, optional attendance and the possibility of silence or a secular message. Those features do not remove the existing governmental mechanism. The Court assumes no official textual preapproval and no delivered October prayer; it does not rest on a finding that every secular purpose is a sham. The ability to decline use of the delegated religious authority does not transform it into neutral access. Pinette and Bronx Household protect actually private expression, where government has not adopted the devotion as its own event component. Tanford likewise distinguishes official organization from private composition, without making its adult setting identical to this school policy.

The backup's religious-content limits retain the same official mechanism and do not cure it. The Court does not direct officials to edit private religion into acceptable language. Nor does it prohibit every election, invited student speaker or religious message. The existing mechanism suffices to affirm; Lee and Ingebretsen's separate protection against substantial practical religious pressure remains intact without requiring legal compulsion or compulsory attendance. No additional pressure finding, universal facial-challenge rule, new forum entitlement, graduation ruling or damages award is necessary.

- **Lemon v. Kurtzman:** Continues in the form Agostini adopted; the Court revives neither an independent purpose test nor an independent endorsement test.
- **Lee v. Weisman:** Its official-organization rule applies to this school-created invocation mechanism. Its independently sufficient substantial-pressure rule remains, without a compulsory-attendance prerequisite; no new pressure holding is made here.
- **Ingebretsen v. Jackson Public School District:** Its rejection of automatic insulation by student delivery and its protection of lawful private expression apply. Its provision-sensitive interim ruling is not recast as a universal rule invalidating every future religious message.

The independent substantial-pressure rules of Lee and Ingebretsen continue without requiring legally compulsory attendance; this decision need not decide that additional ground.

### Genuine pedagogy and religious perspectives in graded assignments

**Governing question:** A student’s challenge to rejection of a religious research topic within a teacher-assessed curricular assignment.

#### Separate Positions Relevant to the Decision

Souter, joined by Stevens, Ginsburg, and Breyer, would vacate the dismissal and remand. The dissent accepts the school's final authority over instruction but recognizes a limited personal expressive interest against adverse action unrelated to genuine legitimate pedagogy. The dissent would extend Hazelwood's legitimate-pedagogical-relation approach to this claim, informed by Settle's distinction between real educational authority and constitutional immunity. Officials could alter content and take related personnel action reasonably related to legitimate pedagogical concerns. They would not have to satisfy strict scrutiny, prove actual disruption in every case, open a forum, or guarantee Boring's preferred production. #### United States v. Alaska

**Docket or dockets:** No. 84, Original.
**Decided:** June 19, 1997.

##### The normal baseline governs without a proved general ten-mile island-fringe rule

**Holding:** Alaska's statutory three-mile submerged-land grant is measured from the Convention's normal low-water baseline, including applicable river and bay closing rules and separate belts of qualifying islands. The evidence establishes no firm and continuing general federal rule converting all waters behind fringing islands with entrances under ten miles into inland waters; the earlier Mississippi Sound judgment does not preclude that conclusion.

**Proposition-level authority:** O'Connor's Parts I–II, unanimous.

**Limits and questions not reached:** Article 4 permits but does not require straight baselines; the United States made no selection here. The international twelve-mile territorial sea does not enlarge the statutory three-mile State grant. Alaska asserted general practice rather than specific historic title to Stefansson Sound; no different proved inland-water claim or general power to contract existing state territory is decided.

**Treatment of earlier authority:** California (1965) supplies Convention definitions. The 1985 Alabama/Mississippi decision established particular historic title, not a universal fringe rule; preclusion fails even assuming its availability, without expanding Mendoza or creating an original-action exception. Nome’s artificial-extension rule and the later Alabama/Mississippi and Massachusetts decrees retain their actual geographic scopes.

##### Dinkum Sands’ frequent seasonal submergence defeats island status

**Holding:** A naturally formed feature must ordinarily remain above the agreed mean-high-water datum to qualify as an island under Convention Article 10(1). Dinkum Sands' frequent seasonal submergence below that datum defeats island status and a separate three-mile grant; isolated emergence does not create alternating island entitlement, and abnormal temporary inundation of an otherwise enduring island remains distinct.

**Proposition-level authority:** O'Connor's Parts I–II, unanimous.

**Limits and questions not reached:** No percentage-of-days test is adopted. Natural accretion or erosion of an established island is distinct. Article 11 supplies no low-tide-elevation entitlement at the reported distances exceeding three miles from mainland and nearby islands.

**Treatment of earlier authority:** Borax and the California decree support the approximately nineteen-year mean-high-water datum, not an absolute physical-permanence condition. Measurements and the survey support the formation’s frequent submergence despite occasional emergence or maps derived from earlier observations.

##### The Petroleum Reserve instruments satisfy separate clear-inclusion and title-defeat requirements

**Holding:** Federal retention requires clear inclusion of the submerged lands and clear intent to defeat future state title for an appropriate public purpose; geography or general purpose alone does not suffice. The 1923 Petroleum Reserve order's boundary and petroleum function, together with Congress's specific Statehood Act treatment of that reserve, satisfy both requirements and retain the disputed submerged estate, under the same demanding inquiry for equal-footing and statutory territorial-sea lands.

**Proposition-level authority:** O'Connor's Parts III–IV, joined by Stone-Zsela, Stevens, Kennedy, Souter, Ginsburg and Breyer: seven Justices.

**Material application:** The 1923 order’s ocean-side barrier boundary, small-lagoon closures and express three-mile limit identify the estate. Ownership of the submerged petroleum deposits serves the national-defense purpose. Statehood Act §11(b)’s specific recognition of the reserve, read with that estate and function, confirms retained ownership; legislative jurisdiction alone would not establish title.

**Limits and questions not reached:** Section 1313(a) is not diluted by a general federal-grant presumption. Congressional confirmation makes it unnecessary to decide independent Pickett Act authority; no broad Executive retention power is announced.

**Treatment of earlier authority:** Shively preserves congressional retention for appropriate public purposes. Utah Division and Montana require distinct clear inclusion and title-defeating intent; their insufficient reservoir-control and unnecessary-riverbed showings differ from this defined petroleum estate.

##### The legally operative prestatehood wildlife segregation retained the described submerged lands

**Holding:** The 1957 application and legally operative temporary-segregation regulation set the specifically described submerged lands apart for the proposed wildlife refuge before statehood. Statehood Act §6(e)'s exclusion of lands withdrawn or otherwise set apart as wildlife refuges therefore retained those lands, although the application was not a completed final reservation; lands outside its boundaries or lacking comparable legal effect are not decided.

**Proposition-level authority:** O'Connor's Parts III–IV, joined by Stone-Zsela, Stevens, Kennedy, Souter, Ginsburg and Breyer: seven Justices.

**Material application:** The 1957 application’s extreme-low-water boundary, coastal features and identified aquatic habitat establish inclusion. The regulation immediately barred settlement, location, sale, selection, entry, lease and other disposals, including mining and mineral leasing, only insofar as the proposed reservation would prevent them; administrative jurisdiction remained unchanged. Those conditions nevertheless gave the specific segregation present legal effect.

**Limits and questions not reached:** Section 6(e) supplies title-defeating intent, separately from §6(m)’s incorporation of the Submerged Lands Act. Neither general ownership nor wildlife purpose alone suffices. The 1960 reservation receives no retroactive effect; the 1980 enlargement does not set the original boundary. Scalia and Thomas do not join the geographic determination.

**Operative remedy or transition:** Alaska’s baseline, Dinkum and Petroleum Reserve exceptions are overruled; the United States’ original Range exception is sustained. The parties must propose a conforming implementing decree; jurisdiction is retained and each bears its own costs. No final coordinate schedule, new survey, damages or oil-accounting award is entered.

**Treatment of earlier authority:** Utah Division and Montana’s distinct inclusion and retention requirements govern. The statutory wildlife exclusion reaches this legally effective temporary segregation without treating every pending application as a completed reservation.

### Ordinary high-water emergence for natural island status

**Governing question:** Whether a naturally formed coastal feature qualifies as an island for a separate submerged-land belt.

**Current rule:** Convention Article 10(1) requires a naturally formed feature ordinarily to remain above the agreed mean-high-water datum. Frequent ordinary seasonal submergence defeats island status; intermittent emergence does not create alternating island entitlement. Abnormal temporary inundation of an otherwise enduring island is different. Mean high water, averaged over approximately nineteen years, is the datum, not absolute permanence or an adopted percentage-of-days test.

**Authority by component:**
- Island definition and seasonal boundary: United States v. Alaska, United States v. Alaska — II. Dinkum Sands does not satisfy the island definition, O'Connor Parts I–II, all nine, 1997-06-19.

**Present operation effective:** United States v. Alaska, 1997-06-19.

**Exceptions and limits:** Natural accretion or erosion of an established island and independently qualifying low-tide elevations retain their distinct questions. This rule does not enlarge the statutory three-mile State grant to the international twelve-mile territorial sea.

**Holdings navigation:** United States v. Alaska (1997-06-19) — Property and Economic Rights / Submerged lands and fixed coastal baselines.

### Coordinate fixation of state-federal submerged-land boundaries

**Governing question:** Whether a state-federal submerged-land boundary fixed by coordinates in a final Supreme Court decree moves with the coast.

**Current rule:** Under 43 U.S.C. §1301(b), a state-federal boundary fixed by coordinates in a final decree of this Court remains immobilized at the decreed coordinates and does not move with later coastline change. The decree’s complete coordinate schedule, including its datum, zones, precision, sequence, segments and arcs, controls; an illustrative map does not replace it.

**Authority by component:**

- Coordinate-fixation consequence and schedule priority: Canonical Decision Record `united_states_v_louisiana_supplemental_decree_1998-10-13.md`, Public Projection / Holdings / The complete coordinates fix and immobilize the boundary, 1998-10-13.

**Present operation effective:** 1998-10-13.

**Exceptions and limits:** This rule does not allocate an unpresented State’s territory, decide reserved property interests or change the entitlement used to select the decreed line.
