## Event

Fischer v. United States, No. 99-116. Merits decision, May 15, 2000; argued February 22, 2000. On writ of certiorari to the United States Court of Appeals for the Eleventh Circuit. The question is whether Medicare payments received by West Volusia Hospital Authority constitute benefits under 18 U.S.C. §666(b).

Fischer, president and part owner of a billing-audit business, obtained a $1.2 million loan from the Authority in 1993. The prosecution concerned diverted proceeds, speculative investment losses and a $10,000 kickback to the Authority's chief financial officer. The Authority operated two participating hospitals and received $10–15 million in Medicare payments that year. The jury's thirteen-count convictions, sixty-five-month prison sentence, three years of supervised release and $1.2 million restitution describe the judgment below; the grant does not independently place every conviction or sentencing determination before the Court.

Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer participated in argument and decision.

## Holdings

### Medicare benefits received by a participating hospital

**Holding and operative rule:** A hospital receives benefits under 18 U.S.C. §666(b) when its actual participation in Medicare brings continuing federal payments that materially support the institution's operations in delivering the federal program. The ordinary Medicare payments proved here qualify even though they reimburse patient services; ordinary procurement, isolated market transactions, and payments lacking that programmatic assistance relationship do not qualify merely because they provide revenue or advance a public purpose.

**Authority:** O'Connor's Opinion of the Court, joined in full by Stone-Zsela, Stevens, Kennedy, Souter, Ginsburg and Breyer. These seven adopt both holdings and their stated limits; no fractured-decision synthesis is required.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court reads benefits together with the statutory requirement of a federal assistance program. Section 666(b) expressly includes assistance involving a grant, contract, subsidy, loan, guarantee or insurance. An institution need not receive a gift, earn a profit, or be the program's sole beneficiary to receive assistance. Medicare supports covered care through the participating institution's continuing program relationship, and the Authority's ordinary receipts sustain that delivery. The fact that patients also benefit does not make their hospital merely an incidental seller.

Fischer's contrary argument correctly insists that every federal purchase serves some public purpose and benefits its seller. Those ordinary incidents, and regulatory conditions alone, are insufficient. The distinction here rests on Medicare's actual assistance structure and the hospital's role within it, not a free-ranging balance of vendor characteristics. The Court does not hold that every contractor or regulated provider receives qualifying assistance. Nor does it rely on an unproved special subsidy or a particular Part A or Part B allocation.

Salinas v. United States supplies the requirement to preserve actual statutory conditions while rejecting an added demand to trace an effect on federal dollars. It assumed qualifying benefits and therefore did not settle this classification. The Authority received $10–15 million in ordinary Medicare payments during 1993. Once their qualifying character is established, those receipts exceed subsection (b)'s annual monetary threshold. The reviewed classification is affirmed.

### Compensation protection and distinct offense requirements

**Holding and operative rule:** Section 666(c) excludes bona fide salary, wages, fees and other compensation paid, and expenses paid or reimbursed, in the usual course of business; it does not exclude an institution's otherwise qualifying Medicare participation from §666(b) merely because its payments reimburse services. The lawful reimbursement is not the prohibited transaction, and its legitimate character does not protect a diversion or corrupt kickback that otherwise satisfies the applicable offense requirements.

**Authority:** The same seven-Justice Opinion of the Court.

**Controlling explanation:** The compensation provision remains effective on its own terms. It prevents lawful business payments from becoming the crime and reinforces the need to distinguish institutional assistance from ordinary purchasing. But treating every compensatory program payment as incapable of conveying institutional benefits would disregard subsection (b)'s inclusion of insurance and contractual forms of assistance. The Court gives effect to both provisions without deciding every possible application of subsection (c).

The benefits condition requires more than $10,000 in a continuous one-year period beginning no earlier than twelve months before the offense or ending no later than twelve months after it; the period may include time before and after the offense. That condition is separate from §666(a)(1)(A)'s property requirement: property worth $5,000 or more owned by, or under the care, custody or control of, the covered entity, together with agency and the enacted embezzlement, theft, fraud, unauthorized knowing conversion or intentional-misapplication requirement. Sections 666(a)(1)(B) and (a)(2) separately require their corrupt solicitation, demand, acceptance, agreement, giving or offer, their influencing-or-rewarding intent, and covered business, a transaction or series involving $5,000 or more. The bribe itself need not equal that amount. Salinas preserves these conditions rather than replacing them with the presence of federal money.

The Court decides the benefits question, not every count, valuation, agency finding, mental-state element or sentencing issue. Salinas's distinct reservations about a statutory connection to expenditure of federal funds, outer constitutional applications and intangible-benefit coverage remain unresolved.

## Precedent Treatment

- **Salinas v. United States (December 2, 1997):** Applied its rejection of a demonstrable federal-dollar-effect requirement while preserving every enacted institutional, transaction and culpability condition and §666(c). Its assumption of qualifying benefits is distinguished from the benefits question decided here; its separately reserved questions remain open.
- **Good Samaritan Hospital v. Shalala (June 7, 1993):** Its account of Medicare's lawful reimbursement system supplies program context. Its cost-method and corrective-adjustment rules are unchanged and do not establish criminal coverage or deference to a prosecutor.
- **Regions Hospital v. Shalala (February 24, 1998):** Its distinction between lawful historical cost inputs and final payments supplies limited institutional context. It neither proves this hospital received special assistance nor already decides §666; its cost and payment-finality holdings remain unchanged.

## Law After Decision

Effective May 15, 2000, §666(b) covers the ordinary Medicare payments received by a hospital in the program relationship established here. The decision adds that classification to Salinas's unchanged rule rejecting a demonstrable effect on federal dollars. It does not cover every federal receipt, erase the compensation exclusion, merge the annual benefits threshold with the offense-specific property or transaction threshold, or decide the reserved statutory-connection and outer constitutional questions. The precise rules and limits appear in Holdings; the dissent changes no governing law.

## Separate Writings

**Scalia, dissenting, joined by Thomas in the statutory construction and remedy but not the residual-lenity paragraph.** The dissent reads the ordinary receipts established here as compensation for treating the insured patients, not assistance furnished to the hospital. Benefits and federal assistance must limit the category of federal payments. Insurance and contracts describe forms that assistance can take; those words do not make every compensated participant an institutional beneficiary. Section 666(c)'s compensation protection reinforces that distinction, without making the alleged diversion or kickback legitimate.

The dissent confronts the Court's dual-beneficiary argument: genuine institutional aid can coexist with patient benefits, but continuing revenue, quality regulation and an important public purpose do not establish such aid in ordinary treatment receipts. It does not exclude all providers, demand profit, or revive the traced-dollar-effect requirement rejected in Salinas. It would reverse the §666 judgment dependent on the benefits determination and remand for identification of affected counts and necessary sentence consequences from the existing record. It would leave independent convictions outside the grant and would not provide a new opportunity to supply missing special-payment proof.

**Scalia alone, residual-lenity paragraph.** If statutory text and context leave genuine uncertainty about ordinary compensation, the remaining penal ambiguity should be resolved against extending criminal coverage. His position follows Ratzlaf's residual-ambiguity principle; Wells does not permit manufacturing ambiguity merely because the statute is penal. Thomas finds the benefits/assistance distinction sufficient and does not join this additional ground.

## Event

Johnson v. United States, No. 99-5153. Merits decision, May 15, 2000; argued February 22, 2000. On writ of certiorari to the United States Court of Appeals for the Sixth Circuit. The questions are whether the September 13, 1994 enactment of §3583(h) governs postrevocation consequences of an October 1993 offense, and whether former §3583(e)(3) independently permits the imposed prison-plus-supervision disposition.

Johnson's original Class D felony sentence, imposed in March 1994, was twenty-five months' imprisonment and thirty-six months' supervised release. Supervision began August 14, 1995. Approximately seven months later he was arrested and subsequently convicted on state forgery-related charges; he also left the district without permission. Revocation produced eighteen months' imprisonment and twelve months' supervision. The district court did not identify the statutory authority for the renewed supervision. The Sixth Circuit affirmed by treating the later violations as the relevant conduct under subsection (h).

**Opposing:** Stone-Zsela and Scalia, who would vacate the twelve-month supervised-release component while leaving valid revocation and authorized imprisonment undisturbed.

The Sixth Circuit's judgment sustaining eighteen months' imprisonment followed by twelve months' supervision is affirmed under former §3583(e)(3), although its reliance on subsection (h) is rejected. No new sentencing hearing or additional penalty is ordered.

## Holdings

### Original-offense attribution and the inapplicability of subsection (h)

**Holding and operative rule:** Postrevocation imprisonment and supervision are consequences of the original offense, so that offense supplies the relevant event for determining whether 18 U.S.C. §3583(h) applies. Congress did not direct subsection (h), enacted September 13, 1994, to govern Johnson's October 1993 offense; his sentence must therefore rest on the former statute, and the Court does not decide the constitutional Ex Post Facto challenge to subsection (h).

**Authority:** Souter's Part I-A, original-offense attribution, is joined by all eight other Justices. Part I-B, statutory nonapplication of subsection (h) without reaching its constitutionality, is joined by Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer, for eight Justices including Souter. Stone-Zsela does not join Part I-B and states his distinct retrospective-punishment ground separately.

**Controlling explanation:** The violation triggers revocation, but the original conviction supplies the authority and measure for its consequences. A violation may be noncriminal and may be established by a preponderance of the evidence under revocation procedures. Those features would not support treating the resulting imprisonment as punishment for a newly prosecuted crime. The statute itself ties the prison limit and supervision term to the original offense and sentence.

The Sixth Circuit relied on the later date of Johnson's violations. That approach mistakes the trigger for the source of punishment. The fact that he could have avoided new misconduct after enactment does not change the legal attribution of the sanction. Landgraf v. USI Film Products supplies the inquiry into congressional temporal direction and new consequences; Rivers v. Roadway Express, Inc. rejects corrective legislative purpose as a substitute for that direction. Their distinct supporting approaches are not merged into a unanimous general framework. Here the necessary direction is absent.

The Court separately interprets the former statute. Sustaining the sentence under that law neither gives subsection (h) retrospective force nor decides that the two versions always authorize identical punishment. The constitutional question is unnecessary to the Court's decision, and subsection (h)'s prospective operation remains undisturbed.

### Remaining-term authority under former subsection (e)(3)

**Holding and operative rule:** Former §3583(e)(3) permits a court, upon a qualifying revocation, to allocate part of the supervised-release term actually imposed to imprisonment and retain a remaining part for supervision after imprisonment. The imprisonment must satisfy the applicable felony cap and the combined new imprisonment and supervision cannot exceed that actual imposed term; the provision denies credit for earlier time served on supervision.

**Authority:** Souter's Part II, joined by Stevens, O'Connor, Kennedy, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer, supplies a direct seven-Justice majority. Stone-Zsela and Scalia dissent from this construction. Parts III-A and III-B provide the separately joined confirmation described below, not independent alternative sentencing powers.

**Controlling explanation:** The former provision authorized prison service of all or part of the imposed supervised-release term. That language treats the term as something that can be allocated, rather than necessarily extinguishing every aspect when release is revoked. Revocation withdraws the existing right to remain released; the distinct termination-and-discharge language of subsection (e)(1) shows that Congress could expressly end the term and discharge its recipient.

The contrary reading treats revocation as cancellation and uses the original term only to measure prison. That is a serious textual alternative, but it gives insufficient effect to the all-or-part mechanism and the distinction between revocation and discharge. The Court's authority comes from former (e)(3), not from the later amendment, a power to impose a new sentence under (a), or supervision's desirability alone. United States v. Granderson supplies the need to respect the chosen sentencing measure and to use lenity only for genuine remaining ambiguity; its different probation statute does not decide this release provision.

The original term was thirty-six months. The eighteen months of revocation imprisonment fall within the twenty-four-month Class D prison cap. Adding twelve months of supervision yields thirty months, below the original thirty-six; the supervision also fits the eighteen months remaining after subtracting revocation imprisonment. The twenty-four-month prison cap does not replace the thirty-six-month term in that combined calculation. Approximately seven months already spent on supervision do not reduce the measure, because the former text expressly denies that credit. This conclusion does not equate prison and supervised release as experiences or approve every possible combination.

### Conditions, additional confirmation and questions left open

**Operative limits:** The former statute's required consideration of §3553(a)(1), (a)(2)(B)–(D), (a)(4), (a)(5) and (a)(6) remains. Former (e)(3) requires a violation found by a preponderance of the evidence, the applicable probation-revocation procedures and Sentencing Commission policy statements, and its offense-specific imprisonment limit. The Court supplies no new violation finding, credit rule or authority for repeated revocations.

**Authority:** The seven Justices joining Part II adopt these limits. In Part III-A, Souter, Stevens, O'Connor, Ginsburg and Breyer additionally rely on subsection (a)'s supervision-after-prison structure as context. In Part III-B those five and Kennedy also rely on supervision's transition function as confirmation. Thomas joins neither additional discussion; Kennedy does not join Part III-A. Neither discussion independently authorizes a penalty absent the Part II construction.

**Controlling explanation:** United States v. Johnson, decided March 1, 2000, explains why supervision following actual release serves a different function from imprisonment and preserves separately conditioned statutory adjustment powers. That context supports, but does not establish, the postrevocation authority construed here. Subsection (a) describes supervision as part of a sentence to imprisonment; the five joining Part III-A use that structure to confirm the reading of (e)(3), not to reopen the original sentencing proceeding. The six joining Part III-B see postcustody transition as consistent with the residual-term reading. Thomas finds the text sufficient without those explanations, while Kennedy accepts the transition confirmation but rejects reliance on new-sentence authority.

Former (e)(1) allows termination and discharge only after one year of supervision, under the required procedure and when conduct and the interest of justice warrant it. Former (e)(2) retains its power to extend a term shorter than the authorized maximum and to modify conditions before expiration or termination, under its procedural and initial-setting requirements. The Court does not approve extending a term under (e)(2) and then revoking it to enlarge the measure under (e)(3). It leaves different original terms, felony caps and repeated revocations for their own cases. Subsection (h)'s less-than-maximum-prison trigger and authorized-maximum-minus-prison formula are not silently substituted for the former actual-term rule.

## Law After Decision

Effective May 15, 2000, postrevocation sanctions remain attributed to the original offense for this temporal inquiry. Subsection (h) supplies no authority for this October 1993 offense, but former (e)(3) sustains the particular eighteen-month imprisonment plus twelve-month supervision allocation within the thirty-six-month imposed term and the separate twenty-four-month prison cap. This adds a former-statute remainder construction without creating a general new-sentence power, an (e)(2) enlargement route, an automatic credit rule or an equivalence between the old and amended statutes. The Court reaches no constitutional invalidation of (h).

**Kennedy, concurring.** Kennedy joins the original-offense and statutory temporal conclusions, the sufficient former-(e)(3) construction and the transition-purpose confirmation. He also rejects extending the term under (e)(2) before revocation to increase the available punishment. **Thomas, concurring.** Thomas joins the express original-offense attribution and statutory nonapplication of (h), and finds former (e)(3)'s text sufficient to sustain this sentence. The actual imposed term remains the statutory measure. He declines both subsection (a)'s additional sentencing discussion and purpose-based support, so his judgment vote does not add a join to either part. The Court need not derive penal power from supervision's utility.

**Scalia, concurring in part and dissenting in part.** Scalia agrees that postrevocation consequences belong to the original offense and that (h) does not apply. Former (e)(3), however, revokes the release term and expressly authorizes prison, without authorizing a new period of supervision. A canceled term can still supply the numerical measure of imprisonment; referring to all or part of that measure does not preserve a second release entitlement. Neither (a) nor the utility of supervision supplies omitted criminal authority. His Granderson approach respects the actual statutory verbs, and genuine residual penal uncertainty reinforces the limit. He would vacate only the twelve-month supervision term and remand for lawful implementation, without disturbing valid imprisonment or authorizing compensating punishment.

**Stone-Zsela, concurring in part and dissenting in part.** Stone-Zsela agrees that the original conviction supplies the authority to punish. A later statute cannot add a custodial or supervisory consequence to that offense that former law did not authorize. He therefore would not apply (h) to supply the disputed additional punishment; he leaves prospective application and genuinely ameliorative changes open. In his reading, revocation ends the existing term, and former (e)(3) then specifies imprisonment without authorizing another term of supervision. Congress's later express addition of that authority cannot enlarge the earlier criminal sentence by implication. He would vacate renewed supervision and remand for a lawful sentence under the offense-date provisions, leaving valid revocation and authorized imprisonment untouched. He does not restrict modification or early termination of an existing term under their own provisions.

## Holdings

**Common authority:** Scalia's Opinion of the Court, joined in full by Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer, controls each of the three holdings and every stated operative qualification. The concurrences withhold no join.

### Grazing preference and permitted use

**Holding and operative rule:** The Secretary may define grazing preference as priority against competing permit or lease applicants attached to owned or controlled base property and specify the forage allocation separately as permitted use. This distinction does not vest a fixed forage quantity, eliminate the Taylor Grazing Act's adequate-safeguarding command, or authorize a particular unlawful reduction or cancellation.

**Operative qualifications:** Recognized grazing privileges must be adequately safeguarded so far as consistent with the Act. Its protection of water rights, permit duration and renewal remains; a compliant permittee's renewal cannot be denied where denial would impair the value of a grazing unit pledged as security for a bona fide loan. A permit itself creates no right, title, interest or estate in federal land, but that provision extinguishes no independently existing right.

The ordinary permitted-use rule includes authorized livestock use and suspended use. Designated ephemeral ranges, where authorization depends on forage availability, and designated annual ranges retain their different treatment; an applicable land-use or activity plan may prescribe vegetation standards instead of the ordinary forage-allocation measure. Changes in permitted use require monitoring, field observations, ecological inventory or other acceptable data. Ordinary reductions or management changes require consultation, cooperation and coordination with the affected permittee or lessee, the State and interested public, followed by documented agreement or a proposed decision.

If soil, vegetation or other resources need immediate protection because of conditions such as drought, fire, flood or insect infestation, or continued grazing presents an imminent likelihood of significant resource damage, the officer must close the affected area or modify use after consultation or a reasonable attempt to consult the specified participants. The notice may be a final decision effective on issuance or its specified date, remaining effective during appeal unless the Office of Hearings and Appeals grants a stay under 43 C.F.R. §4.21. This exception does not erase the ordinary route.

**Controlling explanation:** Sections 2 and 3 combine range protection and administration with enforceable protection of recognized privileges. Congress left numbers and seasons subject to management and expressly denied that a permit conveys an estate in public land. Protecting priority therefore does not freeze every historic forage quantity. The separate permitted-use measure retains an allocation instead of eliminating it.

The challengers correctly object that an empty label could not adequately safeguard a real privilege. The Secretary's representation that existing active and suspended-use priority remains is accordingly material to this facial assessment. A later action contradicting that protection would require its own review; the definitions do not authorize it. The monitoring and procedural requirements reinforce the distinction between a manageable quantity and unlimited discretion. Nor do conservation references in the amendment restore the separately invalidated conservation-use rule. The Court sustains the definitional arrangement, without approving a particular allocation, plan, reduction or administrative record.

### Deletion of the commercial-livestock-business qualification

**Holding and operative rule:** The Secretary may remove engagement in a commercial livestock business as a universal regulatory prerequisite for a grazing permit. The deletion preserves §3's eligibility for bona fide settlers, residents and other stock owners who meet lawful regulatory qualifications, together with its distinct preferences; it does not grant an unrestricted stock-free or conservation-only permit entitlement.

**Operative qualifications:** Section 3 separately prefers nearby landowners engaged in the livestock business, bona fide occupants or settlers, and owners of water or water rights where necessary for proper use of their property. Permits remain limited to United States citizens, persons who have filed the required declarations of intention, and groups, associations or corporations authorized to do business under the relevant State's law. Base-property and other lawful qualifications remain. Where an acquisition agreement, Act or Executive Order requires recognition of existing permits or leases on acquired land, their acquisition-date terms remain applicable; this decision creates no exemption from those instruments.

If substantial authorized use has not occurred for two consecutive grazing fee years, **or** water base property has not been maintained or used in the grazing operation, the officer **may** cancel the amount of permitted use not made after consultation, coordination and cooperation with the permittee or lessee and any recorded lienholder. Cancellation is discretionary, and the two-year condition is not added to the separate water-base ground.

Temporary nonuse requires conformity with the applicable land-use, allotment-management or other activity plan and rangeland-health rules, stated reasons and annual approval; it may continue for no more than three consecutive years. Reasons may include financial circumstances or annual livestock fluctuations. Temporarily available forage may be offered to qualified others under the governing procedure. These provisions neither validate permanent abandonment of grazing nor restore the conservation-use rule outside this case.

**Controlling explanation:** The statute distinguishes eligibility from priority. Its express priority for specified persons engaged in livestock business does not make commercial occupation a prerequisite for every otherwise eligible person. Noncommercial stock ownership is not the same as owning no stock; deletion of the commercial condition does not erase the statutory categories.

The challengers fear that applicants could acquire grazing rights simply to eliminate grazing. That concern cannot justify turning a preference into an eligibility command Congress did not enact. The retained actual-use, cancellation and annually approved temporary-nonuse provisions constrain the consequence alleged. They do not establish that every proposed arrangement is lawful, and the Court grants no permit to any applicant. The facial rule survives because it deletes only the commercial-business requirement while retaining the independent qualifications and use conditions. A stock-free organization receives no conservation-only entitlement through this holding.

### Title to future cooperative improvements

**Holding and operative rule:** Under the Taylor Act's cooperative-improvement authority, the Secretary may require federal title from the outset for future permanent improvements authorized after August 21, 1995, under cooperative agreements, subject to valid existing rights and the regulation's contribution protections. That prospective arrangement does not on its face transfer an already vested private improvement to the United States.

**Operative qualifications:** The rule covers permanent structures such as fences, wells and pipelines; all new permanent water developments require cooperative agreements. Each agreement must allocate cost or labor, or both, between the United States and cooperators. BLM must document contributed funds, labor and materials for the credits governed by §§4120.3-5 and 4120.3-6(c). No credit formula or individual award is decided.

Nonstructural improvements are federally titled. Under the separate range-improvement permit provision, a permittee or lessee **may** hold title to authorized removable livestock-handling improvements and temporary structural improvements; private title is not compulsory in every such case. Improvement work confers no exclusive right to the improvement or affected land.

Section 4 retains reasonable-value payment before a later permit entitles its holder to use improvements constructed and owned by a prior occupant, and State partition-fence cost and maintenance law remains applicable. Where temporary forage use by another operator is lawfully authorized, the existing operator's reasonable use and maintenance compensation and fair-share procedures remain. The officer may mediate and determine the share after consultation; an unresolved settlement proceeds through a temporary authorization containing the compensation requirement. This supplies no individual award or validation of an excluded conservation-use predicate.

**Controlling explanation:** Sections 2 and 4 authorize cooperative arrangements to improve the range. The protection of an improvement already constructed and privately owned does not require private ownership of every improvement built under a later agreement. The prospective title condition allows its ownership consequence to be known before construction and operates within the cooperative grant.

The challengers' investment objection identifies an important limit, but not a facial defect in these future terms. Public ownership of the land does not itself extinguish private property, and a prospective label cannot take away a valid existing right. The regulation preserves those rights and requires contribution documentation. The Court decides no particular title, taking, valuation, reliance injury or compensation claim.

National Mining Association v. Department of the Interior supplies the need to preserve actual delegated authority and distinguish present regulation from new consequences imposed on completed conduct. Its express mining-permit correction authority grants no general Taylor Act reopening or confiscation power. The grazing title rule stands on its own cooperative authority and prospective scope.

## Event

### Commerce authority for the enacted private-assault action

**Holding and operative rule:** Congress's findings of the serious economic consequences of gender-motivated violence do not supply the constitutionally adequate interstate connection needed to sustain §13981's presented private-assault action. The enacted class is not directed at an actual interstate market, included as an integral, reasonably adapted means of executing an identified, independently valid federal regulation of interstate commerce, or supported as a substantial interstate externality that individual States cannot reasonably internalize; downstream economic magnitude cannot replace that missing connection.

**Controlling explanation:** Lopez requires the Court to identify the class Congress regulated and an eligible interstate mechanism before measuring aggregate effects. The class here is gender-motivated crimes of violence. Congress documented serious consequences for work, travel, consumption, treatment and productivity. The Court does not dispute their gravity or empirical force. But the statute covers the assault whether or not its object is commercial service or market participation. American Life League and Bird concern interference directed at actual medical services; Robinson concerns operating business assets under its enacted commerce element; Condon concerns an identified information market. Those connections do not extend to every assault with later economic costs.

Home Builders permits inclusion of an intrastate class that is an integral, reasonably adapted means of executing an independently valid interstate regulation, and Hale, Kirk, Hiley and Gillespie permit fitted inclusion needed to protect valid interstate supply restrictions. This civil action is not an integral part of any identified valid interstate regulation and has no demonstrated comparable role. A broad legislative package does not by itself make every included remedy integral to an interstate regime. The Court may not invent the missing regime or relationship.

Home Builders supplies a genuine noneconomic route: materially supported, substantial cross-border effects that States cannot reasonably internalize, with a reasonable fit between the enacted prohibition and that spillover. Nationwide prevalence and the desirability of a national remedy do not establish those distinct conditions. The supported findings here do not identify the required cross-border mechanism and state inability. The claim that violence is a national economic barrier does not change the class or supply that missing mechanism.

The Court therefore rejects the concurrence's proposed substitution of rational aggregate class effects for Lopez's prerequisite. It adopts no categorical bar on noneconomic conduct or traditional state subjects, universal jurisdictional-element requirement, individual border-crossing rule, numerical threshold or findings checklist. No concrete eligible mechanism awaits an unresolved application below. Hinds's separate statutory property-use inquiry cannot insert a commerce element into §13981, and the Court orders no new opportunity to invent a commerce connection.

### Section 5 and the relationship to discriminatory state protection

**Holding and operative rule:** Section 5 sustains §13981's presented civil action against the perpetrator of gender-motivated violence as a corrective substitute remedy for discriminatory state denial of protection. Congress may create an independent civil remedy against private wrongdoers when (1) the constitutional injury involves discriminatory governmental denial of protection or of access to legal remedies; (2) Congress has a materially adequate basis to find that denial systematic; (3) the private conduct reached falls within the class of wrongs affected by that failure; (4) the substitute remedy meaningfully addresses the consequences of the deprivation; and (5) its reach is congruent and proportional. These are conditions on the legislation's validity, not elements a plaintiff must prove. The private assault does not itself violate the Fourteenth Amendment, and the assailant does not become a state actor; the constitutional injury remains the State's discriminatory denial.

**Controlling explanation:** The Civil Rights Cases and Harris (1883) distinguish prohibited state conduct from unsupported private violence, and that boundary is retained. State action includes official administration, judicial action and customs carrying legal force; it is not limited to a discriminatory statute. Those decisions do not categorically forbid every corrective remedy involving a private defendant or require Congress to await an individual completed violation. The statutes they held invalid neither professed to correct a state constitutional wrong nor were fitted to one; their defect was remedial fit. Boerne requires congruence and proportionality between the constitutional injury and the preventive or remedial means and forbids replacing the substantive right, while permitting coverage beyond individually proven violations. Katzenbach v. Morgan, as Boerne reads it, sustained a measure aimed at discriminatory governmental treatment that operated through a remedy not itself constitutionally required. In United States v. Guest, six Justices in separate opinions recognized that Section 5 legislation may reach private persons who interfere with Fourteenth Amendment rights; the Court adopts only the narrower route stated here, tied to a documented governmental denial. A corrective remedy may therefore operate against private persons when its fit to the State's constitutional wrong is sufficient. The five conditions state that fit.

Congress's record satisfies the first two conditions. It had before it gender-bias task-force reports from at least 21 States, and its committees found that stereotypes in state justice systems produced insufficient investigation and prosecution of gender-motivated violence, inappropriate focus on victims' behavior and credibility, and unduly lenient treatment of offenders. Sex-based official action requires an exceedingly persuasive justification under United States v. Virginia; protection withheld or discounted because the victim is a woman, or because the crime is motivated by gender, is unequal protection by the State. Those findings describe a systematic pattern of official responses, not isolated misconduct. They are Congress's findings, not findings about Virginia's treatment of this claimant.

The remaining conditions answer the objection that liability follows the private assault whether the State denies equal protection or faithfully provides it. The animus requirement confines the remedy to the class of wrongs the documented official failures affect: violence committed because of gender, the class those stereotypes discount. The remedy addresses the deprivation's consequences directly; it gives the victim a forum, a defendant and relief that do not depend on the biased official response, and it neither displaces state law nor directs any state officer. Requiring each victim first to prove that her own State denied her equal protection would preserve the obstacle Congress identified and would convert the remedy into the individual-violation requirement that Boerne and Florida Prepaid reject. National scope is therefore permissible prophylaxis: Congress found discriminatory failures across many States and could reasonably conclude that a State-by-State or case-by-case predicate would defeat the remedy. The remedy requires qualifying felony-level violence and proof of gender animus by a preponderance, excludes random violence, does not federalize all assaults and commandeers no state official.

The holding is limited. National prophylaxis would fail if the record showed only isolated violations, or if liability extended substantially beyond the class of wrongs affected by the official failure. Florida Prepaid's Section 5 holding stands. There, patent-property status, exclusive federal jurisdiction and possible limits on State remedies showed at most that State infringement can injure protected property and that State redress may be limited, and the Act imposed liability for every infringement without differentiating unconstitutional deprivation. Here Congress documented a systematic discriminatory denial within a classification that demands an exceedingly persuasive justification, and the animus element confines coverage to the affected class. The Court decides the private-assault action presented, not every hypothetical application to a person acting under color of state law, an unpresented severability issue or a new state-action claim. Saenz supplies no official intervention in this complaint; the holding rests on equal protection against discriminatory denial of protection, not on Saenz's due-process rule for an official's affirmative removal of protection, and Saenz's direct-claim predicates are not imported as conditions for preventive legislation.

### Statutory scope, remedies and reservations

The action concerns a crime of violence committed because of or on the basis of gender and due at least in part to gender animus. The referenced violence includes acts or a series constituting a felony against the person, or a felony against property presenting a serious risk of physical injury, within the State or federal offenses described in 18 U.S.C. §16; it also includes otherwise qualifying conduct excluded only because of the relationship between actor and victim. The definition does not require occurrence in federal maritime, territorial or prison jurisdiction. Gender motivation must be shown by a preponderance of the evidence; random violence unrelated to gender is excluded. No prior criminal complaint, prosecution or conviction is required.

Section 13981(c) includes a person acting under color of state statute, ordinance, regulation, custom or usage and provides compensatory and punitive damages, injunctions, declarations and other appropriate relief. Federal and state courts share jurisdiction. Neither §13981(c) nor 28 U.S.C. §1367, by reason of this claim, confers federal jurisdiction over state-law claims seeking a divorce, alimony, equitable distribution of marital property or child-custody decree. None of these distinct provisions supplies an omitted interstate-commerce or unconstitutional-state-denial element.

The Court reverses the constitutional dismissal of the private-assault claim in both consolidated dockets and remands for adjudication of the claim under its enacted requirements. It makes no finding that the alleged assault, gender animus, causation or liability has been established and awards no damages; Brzonkala must prove the conduct, gender motivation, causation and any relief. It neither rewrites the action by inserting a commerce or state-failure element nor decides a severability theory absent from the case. The separate Title IX university litigation, state remedies, other VAWA provisions and hypothetical color-of-law applications remain outside this judgment. No remand for new legislative proof, particular hearing or additional pleading is ordered, and no particular trial or award is guaranteed.

## Separate Writings

**Breyer, concurring, joined by Stevens, Souter and Ginsburg.** The four join the Court's Section 5 opinion and its judgment in full. They would also uphold the action under the Commerce Clause. Congress could rationally find that the regulated class of gender-motivated violence substantially obstructs participation in interstate labor, travel, education and service markets, and choose reasonable means against the conduct creating that obstruction. The inquiry would retain judicial review, substantiality, a rational class-wide relationship and reasonable means; it would not make a congressional label, national importance or any conceivable economic consequence conclusive.

This is a proposed change to Lopez's connection-before-magnitude prerequisite, not an application that leaves that prerequisite intact. The concurring Justices maintain the broader class-effects view expressed in their Lopez dissent. In their judgment, requiring an independently eligible mechanism before considering the supported class-wide effects excludes a reasonable congressional response to this demonstrated economic barrier. They would displace that restriction to the extent it forecloses the rational substantial-class-effects ground stated here. Their proposal would preserve market, necessary-regime and externality holdings as sufficient grounds without making them exclusive prerequisites to this broader approach.

Their joins in Condon, Gillespie and other market or supply cases do not withdraw the broader position merely because those cases had an additional sufficient connection. Their Home Builders joins in Parts II-A, III and IV, and Ginsburg and Breyer's join in Part II-B, likewise rested on sufficient grounds while expressly preserving their disagreement with Part I's restriction. The concurrence does not relabel national prevalence as proof of a noninternalizable externality, invent a necessary-inclusion relationship, or assume new legislative facts. It rejects the Commerce opinion's treatment of connection and magnitude on the supported record.

Because the Section 5 holding sustains the action and requires reversal, the Commerce proposal does not affect the judgment. The concurrence adopts no additional legislative-process test. Reversal returns the statutory claim to ordinary adjudication, without establishing assault, animus, causation or damages and without guaranteeing a particular hearing, trial or award.

**Kennedy, dissenting as to Section 5 and the judgment, joined by O'Connor, Scalia and Thomas.** In the part of his opinion that does not speak for the Court, Kennedy would affirm. Section 5 does not sustain the presented nationwide action against a private assailant for gender-motivated violence because its defendant, triggering private wrong and relief lack a sufficient remedial relationship to the unconstitutional state denial invoked to support it. A private assailant's gender animus does not itself establish the State's constitutional denial, and Congress's account of discriminatory state justice does not, without that fit, convert liability for the private assault into enforcement of equal state protection.

The dissenters accept that state action includes official administration, judicial action and customs carrying legal force, that the Civil Rights Cases and Harris do not categorically forbid every corrective remedy involving a private defendant, and that Boerne and Florida Prepaid permit prevention beyond individually proven violations without a findings quota. They acknowledge Congress's documented state-justice concerns, the serious violence, the animus requirement and the civil relief. Their objection is fit: liability and relief follow the private assault whether the State denies equal protection or faithfully provides it, and in every State alike. The assailant's animus does not identify discriminatory official administration, and the action neither replaces a specified constitutionally defective procedure nor defines a supported class of official practices to which its coverage and relief are fitted. In their view the same kind of gap defeated the undifferentiated patent liability in Florida Prepaid. Scalia joins while preserving the possibility of genuinely corrective private-party remedies, and the dissenters reserve hypothetical color-of-law applications.
