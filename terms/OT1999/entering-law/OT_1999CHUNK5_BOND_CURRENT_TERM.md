## Event

Flippo v. West Virginia, No. 98-8770. Summary merits disposition, October 18, 1999. Submitted without oral argument. On writ of certiorari to the Circuit Court of West Virginia, Fayette County, after the State Supreme Court denied discretionary review. The question is whether a general homicide-scene exception permitted the prolonged warrantless evidentiary search of the secured cabin and opening of a closed briefcase.

## Participation

Chief Justice Stone-Zsela and Justices Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg, and Breyer participate.

## Public Action

The petition for certiorari is granted. The Court summarily reverses and remands by a reasoned per curiam opinion. No individual petition vote is disclosed.

## Judgment & Remedy

Reversed and remanded, 9–0. Supporting: Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg, and Breyer. The Circuit Court's judgment cannot rest on a general homicide-scene exception. The state courts may consider preserved, legally supported independent warrant exceptions and harmless error. No acquittal, unconditional suppression, new trial, or resolution of unpresented retrial questions is ordered.

## Holdings

**Emergency authority does not become general investigative authority.** Police may enter to render emergency aid, locate victims or a dangerous person, and take supported protective or evidence-preservation measures within their actual justification. Once that justification ends, the occurrence of a homicide does not authorize a prolonged warrantless evidentiary search.

**Authority:** The unanimous per curiam opinion supplies the controlling rule and its application.

**Controlling explanation:** Mincey and Thompson reject an exception based on the crime's gravity. Here the cabin had been secured before the extended evidence-gathering search, and the trial court authorized a search of everything at the scene without tying it to an existing emergency. Dickerson likewise confines a lawful initial intrusion to its authorized purpose. The accepted emergency entry therefore cannot sustain the later categorical search. This conclusion preserves properly supported emergency action on its own facts.

**A closed briefcase requires separate search authority.** Seeing an unlocked but closed briefcase does not expose the photographs and negatives concealed within it. Opening it requires a warrant or a separately established exception.

**Authority:** The same unanimous opinion controls.

**Controlling explanation:** Hicks distinguishes observation from an additional search. The briefcase's location near the body and unlocked condition did not reveal its contents or create authority to inspect them. The trial court made no findings adequate for this Court to resolve implied consent, standardized inventory, another exigency, or harmlessness. Those preserved questions remain for the state courts; absence of findings does not establish that every alternative fails.

## Precedent Treatment

Mincey v. Arizona and Thompson v. Louisiana are applied to reject a homicide-scene exception. Minnesota v. Dickerson is applied for the limit imposed by an intrusion's actual authorization. Arizona v. Hicks is applied to distinguish viewing a container from searching concealed contents. Chapman v. California remains the standard for any properly presented harmless constitutional error. No precedent is overruled or broadened into an unconditional suppression rule.

## Law After Decision

The warrant default and recognized, fact-dependent exceptions remain unchanged. A secured homicide scene and a closed container cannot be searched on a categorical crime-scene rationale; independent justifications and harmlessness retain their separate requirements.

## Event

Illinois v. Wardlow, No. 98-1036. Merits decision, January 12, 2000. Argued November 2, 1999. On writ of certiorari to the Supreme Court of Illinois. The question is whether the actual pre-stop record supplied particularized reasonable suspicion after Wardlow looked toward officers and fled in an area associated with narcotics trafficking. The subsequent frisk and bag search are outside the granted question.

## Participation

Chief Justice Stone-Zsela and Justices Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg, and Breyer participate.

## Public Action

The Court affirms the Illinois Supreme Court's judgment sustaining suppression on the ground that the initial stop lacked reasonable suspicion.

## Judgment & Remedy

Affirmed, 5–4. Supporting: Stone-Zsela, Stevens, Souter, Ginsburg, and Breyer. Opposing: O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, and Thomas, who would reverse the initial-stop ruling and remand for any properly remaining issues. The existing suppression judgment remains in force; the Court does not separately decide the justification or scope of the frisk.

## Holdings

**I. Suspicion must remain individualized when flight and location are considered together.** Terry requires objective, particularized reasonable suspicion under the whole circumstances. Flight may contribute to that assessment, and a demonstrated connection between the location and crime may supply context, but location alone is insufficient and neither any flight nor every flight in a broadly described high-crime area creates a universal permission to stop.

**Authority:** Part I of Stone-Zsela's opinion has all nine Justices' direct support as the governing law applied here.

**Controlling explanation:** Terry permits grounded inferences rather than proof of an offense, while Brown v. Texas rejects substituting a neighborhood label for an individualized basis. A refusal to cooperate or departure from police is not automatically criminally suspicious. Ornelas requires independent review of ultimate constitutional sufficiency with respect for historical findings and due weight to supported local inferences; it permits no supplementation of testimony. The later discovery of a firearm cannot justify the earlier stop. This common rule leaves the Justices' disagreement about its application intact.

**II. This record does not establish the required suspicion.** Wardlow's observed departure and route, considered with the credited narcotics setting and a permissible police-recognition inference, do not supply a sufficiently particularized basis for suspecting him of wrongdoing on this record. The initial stop was therefore unlawful, and the Illinois judgment sustaining suppression is affirmed.

**Authority:** Part II of Stone-Zsela's opinion has direct support from Stone-Zsela, Stevens, Souter, Ginsburg, and Breyer.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court credits the state high court's finding about narcotics activity and does not exclude flight from consideration. Nolan saw Wardlow look toward the officers and run through a gangway and alley, but observed no law violation or transaction. His description of customers and lookouts concerned what normally occurred in such areas; it did not place a crowd or such activity beside Wardlow on this occasion. Nolan's uniform is established, while vehicle markings and Wardlow's observation of the whole caravan are not. Even allowing an inference that he recognized police, the flight's significance and the generalized setting remained too ambiguous to connect this person adequately to suspected criminal activity. The contrary reading gives those circumstances more particularizing force than this testimony supports. The Court finds neither an innocent motive nor actual guilt, requires no observed completed offense, and adds no categorical third factor.

**Questions not reached:** The separate safety basis and scope of the frisk and bag search are not adjudicated. A stronger flight-related record remains governed by Terry's existing totality inquiry.

## Precedent Treatment

Terry v. Ohio is applied without a new categorical flight or corroboration rule. Brown v. Texas preserves the insufficiency of location alone. Ornelas v. United States supplies independent ultimate review and due weight for grounded factual inferences; it is not authority to invent additional observations. Flippo v. West Virginia's separate-authorization principle is consistent with keeping the stop and later search distinct but does not decide the stop's factual sufficiency.

## Law After Decision

The existing totality standard remains unchanged. This pre-stop combination is insufficient on the supported record; flight remains relevant in other factually supported settings. The decision creates neither a right-to-flee exclusion of all evidentiary weight nor a mandatory third circumstance beyond flight and location.

## Separate Writings

Justice O'Connor, joined by Scalia, Kennedy and Thomas, agrees with the governing totality rule but finds reasonable suspicion here. Wardlow looked toward the officers and immediately ran through a gangway and alley in the credited narcotics setting. For the dissent, that temporal sequence and evasive route provide the individualized conduct missing from a location-only stop. Police recognition can reasonably be inferred without proving that he noticed every vehicle, and an innocent explanation need not defeat every grounded investigative inference. The dissent assumes neither marked cars nor an actual crowd, transaction, pre-stop firearm or guilty motive; the trial court's racial observation supplies no suspicion. It applies Ornelas's controlling independent review while giving weight to supported local inferences; Scalia retains his prior objection to that review allocation. The four would reverse the initial-stop ruling and remand for any properly remaining issue, without automatically reinstating the conviction or deciding the separate frisk and bag search.

## Procedure After Action

The Illinois Supreme Court's suppression judgment stands. No further ruling on the bag search or frisk is directed by this disposition.

The state high court accepted the narcotics-area finding. The firearm was discovered after the stop and cannot supply its predicate.

## Event

Florida v. J. L., No. 98-1993. Merits decision, March 28, 2000. Argued February 29, 2000. On writ of certiorari to the Supreme Court of Florida, reviewing 727 So. 2d 204, which quashed the reversal of the order suppressing the gun. The question is whether the anonymous gun report and its actual corroboration supplied reasonable suspicion for the initial stop and frisk.

An unknown caller identified several young Black males at a bus stop and reported that the one in a plaid-looking shirt carried a gun. Officers arrived about six minutes after dispatch, found the described group and clothing, and saw no suspicious conduct. The officer directed J.L. to place his hands on the bus stop and began a frisk; she saw the gun butt only after the frisk began. Race served to identify the reported person and supplied no inference of illegality. The case concerns suppression in delinquency proceedings, not review of a conviction or sentence.

## Participation

Chief Justice Stone-Zsela and Justices Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer participated at argument and decision.

## Public Action

The judgment of the Supreme Court of Florida is affirmed.

## Judgment & Remedy

Affirmed, 9–0. Supporting: Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer. Suppression remains in place. The Court directs no additional dismissal, acquittal, release, new trial, evidentiary hearing or remedy for either companion.

## Holdings

**Authority for both holdings:** Justice Stevens's Opinion of the Court, joined throughout by Stone-Zsela, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer. The rule, application and limits have direct unanimous support; no aggregation of separate rationales is involved.

### An accurately identified target does not make this gun accusation reliable

**Holding:** An anonymous assertion that the described person carries a concealed gun does not supply reasonable suspicion for a forcible stop and protective frisk when, as here, police promptly confirm only publicly observable identity and location and have no further circumstance adequately supporting the accusation's reliability. The inquiry remains the totality of the circumstances, and corroborated predictions are one possible means of supporting reliability rather than a requirement for every anonymous report.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that identifying the person accused and supplying adequate grounds to credit the accusation are different tasks. Terry requires objective grounds before police make the seizure and protective search, although reasonable suspicion is less demanding than probable cause. Gates and White require reliability and basis of knowledge to be assessed together. White's corroborated future conduct showed special familiarity that the present description of an openly visible scene does not establish. The prompt response and accurate description matter, but together they still provide no adequate assurance concerning the concealed gun. Adams v. Williams permitted action without an officer's own view of the weapon because the personally known, present informant supplied assurances missing here. Police need not invariably wait to see a gun; sufficiently reliable information may authorize action. The Court does not require predictions, exclude innocent facts, find this caller dishonest, or require a particular questioning protocol.

### The gun allegation and its later confirmation do not supply the missing authority

**Holding:** The ordinary safety concern raised by this gun allegation creates no exception to the requirement of sufficiently reliable grounds for the initial stop and frisk. A gun first observed after that intrusion began cannot justify commencing it.

**Controlling explanation:** Terry already permits a limited protective search on less than probable cause when its predicates exist. The danger that would follow if an allegation is true does not establish sufficient reason to credit the allegation. Otherwise, an unknown person could direct a search of any accurately described target merely by adding an untested gun accusation. Dickerson requires lawful protective access before a discovery may justify seizure, and Wardlow rejects retrospective reliance on the weapon ultimately found. Neither decision is treated as having already decided the anonymous-tip question. Here the officer's view of the gun followed the start of the frisk and supplies no pre-stop observation. The Court leaves different imminent threats, accountable eyewitness reports, school searches, airport screening and other special settings unresolved. Other concrete assurances, including nonpredictive ones, may establish reliability in another case. This decision adds no restriction to an independently lawful protective search after a valid stop and adopts no universal equation between firearm possession, illegality and dangerousness.

## Precedent Treatment

- Terry v. Ohio supplies the antecedent objective grounds and bounded protective-search authority; its lower reasonable-suspicion threshold remains.
- Adams v. Williams is distinguished by its personally known, present informant; officers still need not personally observe a weapon when sufficiently reliable information supports action.
- Illinois v. Gates and Alabama v. White supply contextual reliability analysis; White's predictive corroboration is a sufficient example, not an invariant checklist.
- Ornelas applies to independent review of constitutional sufficiency with protection for historical facts and genuine due weight for grounded experience; no experience-based unproved observation is supplied.
- Minnesota v. Dickerson preserves authorized contact and lawful access; its manipulation holding remains distinct from the initial-stop defect here.
- Illinois v. Wardlow supplies objective totality and the rejection of retrospective gun justification; its actual observed-conduct dispute does not establish an anonymous-tip rule.

## Law After Decision

An accurate anonymous description of a person in public does not alone make an otherwise unsupported concealed-gun accusation sufficient for this stop and frisk. Ordinary gun danger supplies no shortcut, while other concrete reliability circumstances and materially different settings remain subject to their own facts. The existing totality and protective-search standards continue in force.
