### Lee v. Weisman, No. 90-1014

Merits decision, June 24, 1992

#### Chronology and Posture

Argued November 6, 1991. On writ of certiorari to the United States Court of Appeals for the First Circuit, reviewing [908 F.2d 1090](https://static.case.law/f2d/908/cases/1090-01.json) (July 23, 1990), which affirmed the District of Rhode Island's prospective injunction, [728 F.Supp. 68](https://static.case.law/f-supp/728/cases/0068-01.json) (January 9, 1990). The question is whether school officials may select clergy and include clergy-led invocations and benedictions in official public secondary-school graduation ceremonies. Officials seek reversal; the Weismans seek preservation of the injunction. No companion or consolidation is involved. Procedural background source locator: [505 U.S. 577](https://tile.loc.gov/storage-services/service/ll/usrep/usrep505/usrep505577/usrep505577.pdf).

Stone-Zsela, White, Blackmun, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter and Thomas participated at argument and decision.

#### Judgment

Affirmed, 6–3.

| Judgment component | Disposition and vote | Supporting Justices | Opposing Justices | Remedy or remand |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Constitutional challenge to official clergy-led graduation prayers and prospective injunction | Affirmed, 6–3 | Stone-Zsela, Blackmun, Stevens, O'Connor, Kennedy, Souter | White, Scalia, Thomas | The First Circuit's judgment is affirmed. The injunction against school officials' authorizing or encouraging the challenged clergy-led graduation prayer practice remains in force. It reaches the official program and does not prohibit private prayer or genuinely private forum speech. |

#### Opinion Topology

| Writing | Author | Joined by | Relationship to judgment | Scope joined |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Opinion of the Court, Parts I–II | Blackmun | Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor and Souter in full; Kennedy in Part I only | Affirmance | Part I: continued prospective exposure and justiciability. Part II: official sponsorship as an independently sufficient merits ground; Kennedy does not join Part II. |
| Concurrence in part and in the judgment | Kennedy | Blackmun, Stevens, O'Connor, Souter | Affirmance; independent majority-supported ground | Entire writing: school-directed graduation prayer creates impermissible pressure on objecting adolescents. Does not join the sponsorship-only ground. |
| Concurrence | Souter | Stevens, O'Connor | Affirmance | Entire writing: broader historical and neutrality account; all-religion endorsement, coercion not a universally necessary element, and accommodation must lift a discernible burden. |
| Dissent | Scalia | White, Thomas | Would reverse | Entire writing: historical ceremonial accommodation; religious coercion requires law-backed compulsion; sectarian endorsement remains distinct. |

#### Controlling Propositions and Authority

##### Continued exposure supplies a live controversy

**Controlling proposition:** A student who remains in the same public-school system and faces the challenged official prayer policy at her forthcoming high-school graduation has a live claim for prospective protection even though her earlier graduation is over. Deborah Weisman's continued exposure permits review and preservation of the injunction without deciding her father's taxpayer standing.

**Authority:** Part I of Blackmun's opinion, joined by Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Kennedy and Souter; six Justices expressly agree at this threshold level.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that the claim remains live because the challenged policy continues to bear directly on Deborah's own education. She attended the middle-school ceremony after temporary relief was denied and now attends Classical High School within the same system. The stipulated record makes a clergy-led invocation and benediction at her later graduation likely. Her request therefore concerns an identified future application of the policy, not merely disagreement with a completed ceremony. Engel and Schempp supply the school-prayer interest for which prospective protection is sought; deciding that interest requires no conclusion about an unrelated taxpayer's right to sue. The officials' merits argument that attendance is optional does not erase the event's expected occurrence or Deborah's concrete relation to it. The Court preserves review of this ongoing practice and leaves taxpayer standing unresolved. It announces no exception permitting advisory decisions whenever an expired school dispute might recur.

**Precedent treatment:**

- Engel v. Vitale, 370 U.S. 421, and School District of Abington Township v. Schempp, 374 U.S. 203 — supply the substantive school-prayer interest; neither is expanded into a new taxpayer-standing rule.

##### Officially organized graduation worship is forbidden

**Controlling proposition:** Public secondary-school officials violate the Establishment Clause when they select clergy, request invocations and benedictions, guide the prayers' religious content, and incorporate that worship into the official graduation program. Neither nonsectarian wording nor formally optional attendance cures that official sponsorship; no separate showing of punishment or quantified social pressure is required to invalidate this practice.

**Authority:** Part II of Blackmun's opinion, joined by Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor and Souter; five Justices adopt this independently sufficient ground. Kennedy does not join Part II.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that the school may not organize worship as part of its own graduation ceremony. Engel forbids governmental composition and sponsorship of school prayer even when the words are denominationally neutral and observance is voluntary. Schempp likewise rejects excusal from school devotional exercises as a cure. Lemon's prohibition on a primary effect advancing religion applies directly: the principal selected a rabbi, requested prayer, supplied civic-prayer guidance and placed the exercise in the official program. Asking that the prayer be broadly acceptable still makes religious presentation an undertaking of the State. The officials' interest in solemnity does not authorize that undertaking; a secular ceremony can mark the achievement without selecting an approved devotional expression. Marsh concerns legislative prayer in a distinct institutional and historical setting. Mergens protects private religious activity under equal access, not school-directed worship. The Court preserves those distinctions, leaves general accommodation doctrine intact, and decides no question about funding, denominational aid or genuinely private religious speech.

**Precedent treatment:**

- Engel v. Vitale, 370 U.S. 421 — applied to official school-organized worship at graduation; denominational neutrality and voluntary observance do not cure sponsorship.
- School District of Abington Township v. Schempp, 374 U.S. 203 — applied: permission to abstain does not validate the official devotional exercise.
- Lemon v. Kurtzman, 403 U.S. 602 — applied through its prohibition on a primary effect advancing religion; no replacement test and no separate decision under its purpose or entanglement prongs.
- Marsh v. Chambers, 463 U.S. 783 — distinguished: legislative prayer does not authorize this school-directed graduation exercise.
- Board of Education of Westside Community Schools v. Mergens, 496 U.S. 226 — distinguished: private religious activity under equal access is materially different from official sponsorship.

##### The school-directed ceremony also imposes impermissible religious pressure

**Controlling proposition:** The Establishment Clause independently forbids school-directed clergy prayer at a public secondary-school graduation when the school's control and the ceremony's importance place objecting students under substantial pressure to participate, appear to participate, or forgo the occasion. On this record, formally optional attendance and an opportunity to stand or remain silent do not remove that unconstitutional pressure; mere offense alone is insufficient.

**Authority:** Kennedy's concurrence in part and in the judgment, joined by Blackmun, Stevens, O'Connor and Souter; five Justices expressly adopt this independently sufficient rationale. It controls by direct majority support, without a Marks inference. Stone-Zsela does not join this writing.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court also holds that the school may not use the importance and discipline of graduation to press students into a religious exercise. Engel and Schempp recognize the distinctive constitutional dangers of prayer organized for schoolchildren. Here officials selected the religious participant and controlled the ceremony in which classmates stood together. An objecting adolescent could reasonably understand standing or silence during the collective prayer as her participation or approval. Requiring her instead to protest publicly or miss a singular educational occasion does not preserve a meaningful choice. The absence of a disciplinary penalty therefore does not answer the constitutional injury. Marsh rests on an adult legislative setting where attendees can enter and leave without the same educational and social consequences. The Court does not equate every disagreeable message or ordinary demand for courtesy with religious coercion. It leaves the treatment of mature adult audiences and other religious accommodations open; the holding concerns this State-directed exercise and the pressures attending public secondary-school graduation.

**Precedent treatment:**

- Engel v. Vitale, 370 U.S. 421, and School District of Abington Township v. Schempp, 374 U.S. 203 — applied to indirect religious pressure in the secondary-school graduation setting; formal excusal is not dispositive.
- Marsh v. Chambers, 463 U.S. 783 — distinguished by the adult legislative setting and freedom to leave without the same consequences.
- Lynch v. Donnelly, 465 U.S. 668 — its minimum prohibition on coerced religious support or participation is applied, without extending its display holding.
- County of Allegheny v. ACLU, 492 U.S. 573 — the present decision does not reopen the display judgments or settle every dispute about accommodation.

#### Precedent Treatment and Current-Law Effect

Effective June 24, 1992, official organization of the identified secondary-school graduation worship violates the Establishment Clause independently of a separate showing of coercion, and the school's indirect pressure supplies a second independently sufficient ground. Before this decision Engel, Schempp and Lemon governed school worship, but their precise application to this graduation practice was unresolved. Both stated applications now control, each with five Justices. Each ground has express majority support, so no inference under Marks is necessary to identify controlling law.

No general replacement establishment test, rule against mere offense, quantitative pressure scale, funding rule or universal accommodation rule is adopted. Lemon remains in force; its purpose and entanglement prongs are not independently adjudicated. Adult legislative prayer and genuinely private religious speech remain governed by their own authorities.

#### Separate Positions Relevant to the Decision

Souter, joined by Stevens and O'Connor, adds a broader explanation that the Establishment Clause reaches nonpreferential religious endorsement and that coercion is not universally necessary; his further accommodation account requires a discernible burden on religious exercise. Those broader propositions are not additional holdings of this decision.

Scalia, joined by White and Thomas, would reverse: the historical tradition of nonsectarian ceremonial prayer and absence of law-backed compulsion distinguish graduation from mandatory classroom worship. The dissent treats respectful silence as civility, not compelled observance, while preserving a separate objection to sectarian governmental endorsement.

#### Mandate, Remedy, and Stage

The First Circuit's judgment is affirmed. The existing prospective injunction against school officials' authorizing or encouraging the challenged clergy-led graduation prayer practice remains in force. It reaches the official program and does not prohibit private prayer or genuinely private forum speech.

Supreme Court merits review is complete. No further Supreme Court hearing, remand program, altered institutional practice or retained enforcement proceeding is ordered. Future disputes require actual filings; no such filing is established.

**End of entry: Lee v. Weisman, merits decision, June 24, 1992.**

### Morgan Stanley & Co. v. Pacific Mutual Life Insurance Co., No. 93-609

Merits decision, 1994-05-23

#### Chronology and Posture

Reported at 511 U.S. 658. Argued April 26, 1994; decided May 23, 1994. On certiorari to the Fifth Circuit, 997 F.2d 39, which reversed denial of reinstatement under §27A(b). The question is whether Congress may require reopening of this completed private federal dismissal.

#### Judgment

**Affirmed, 5–3.**

**Supporting:** Stone-Zsela, Blackmun, Stevens, Souter and Ginsburg.
**Opposing:** Scalia, Kennedy and Thomas.
**Not participating:** O'Connor. No reason is stated.

#### Opinion Topology

Chief Justice Stone-Zsela delivers the Opinion of the Court, joined in full by Blackmun, Stevens, Souter and Ginsburg. Scalia dissents, joined in full by Kennedy and Thomas.

#### Controlling Propositions and Authority

##### Bounded statutory reinstatement of completed securities limitations dismissals

**Controlling proposition:** Article III permits §27A(b)'s expressly retrospective requirement to reinstate a private §10(b) action commenced on or before June 19, 1991, dismissed as time barred after June 19, 1991, and timely under the jurisdiction's law, including retroactivity principles, as that law existed on June 19, 1991, upon a motion filed within sixty days after the provision's December 19, 1991 enactment. The federal court must independently determine whether those cumulative conditions are satisfied and, if they are, must reinstate the action without a discretionary balancing test, retaining independent responsibility for the claims, defenses and remedies; this bounded entitlement to adjudication neither directs a merits judgment nor establishes an unrestricted power to reopen completed federal judgments.

**Authority:** Chief Justice Stone-Zsela's Opinion of the Court is joined in full by Blackmun, Stevens, Souter and Ginsburg. These five direct joins control the rule, explanation, qualifications and remedy; no combination of separate rationales is necessary.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that finality protects judicial independence without barring every statutory remedy affecting a completed proceeding. Robertson v. Seattle Audubon Society permits Congress to change governing law while courts determine its application; it leaves this final-judgment question open. United States v. Klein bars legislative prescription of judicial results, not every enacted change in the law governing access to adjudication.

Section 27A(b) supplies a new rule for a defined class of limitations dismissals following Lampf. Congress identifies the filing period, the intervening time-bar dismissal, the former limitations law and a short period for seeking relief. Courts determine those predicates. Congress has found no fraud, reliance, injury or damages and has prescribed no defendant's liability. Once the predicates are established, reinstatement is mandatory. Its validity does not depend on judicial discretion to withhold relief.

The defendants' strongest objection is that Pacific Mutual's unappealed dismissal was already final. That fact is accepted. The distinction is between creating a bounded legal entitlement to further adjudication and performing the adjudication itself. McCullough v. Virginia protects completed judicial determinations; the present statute supplies no contrary factual finding or legislatively fixed liability. The holding concerns a limitations dismissal, not compelled reopening of a judgment adjudicating the underlying securities merits. Wheeling Bridge's treatment of prospective relief and Sioux Nation's treatment of the Government's own defense are distinct settings, not controlling answers to this case.

Landgraf and Rivers distinguish an express temporal command from an inference about statutory reach; express reach does not itself settle constitutionality. Harper's rule for still-open direct review and Beam's protection of finality against later judicial doctrine do not establish that Congress can never enact this confined remedy. Chase Securities concerns due-process revival and does not independently resolve Article III. The courts retain the essential work of adjudication under the new law.

**Limits and questions not reached:** Congress may not declare a completed judicial factual finding erroneous and compel the contrary finding, dictate a merits judgment, or disguise an individualized award or assessment of liability as a procedural reopening statute. This holding does not decide compelled reopening of merits judgments or individualized legislative commands to adjudicate liability. The Court announces no independent due-process holding and does not decide §27A(a), general severability, fraud, liability, damages or entitlement to judgment on the securities merits. Pending litigation, prospective relief and independently authorized judicial reopening remain distinct.

**Precedent treatment:**

- Robertson v. Seattle Audubon Society, March 25, 1992: extends its distinction between changing governing law and prescribing adjudication to this bounded reinstatement procedure; resolves the final-judgment question left open there only within the present limits.
- United States v. Klein, 80 U.S. (13 Wall.) 128 (1872): applies its protection of independent adjudication; a new eligibility rule requiring renewed adjudication does not supply a legislative finding or dictate securities liability.
- McCullough v. Virginia, 172 U.S. 102 (1898): preserves protection of completed judicial determinations while distinguishing this new statutory entitlement to litigate claims dismissed on limitations grounds from legislative contradiction of adjudicated facts or liability.
- Pennsylvania v. Wheeling & Belmont Bridge Co., 59 U.S. (18 How.) 421 (1856): distinguishes a change in the continuing operation of prospective relief; its setting does not itself resolve reinstatement of this completed dismissal.
- United States v. Sioux Nation of Indians, 448 U.S. 371 (1980): distinguishes waiver of the Government's own defense; retained judicial adjudication is relevant, but no governmental waiver of a private defendant's defense is assumed here.
- Lampf, Pleva, Lipkind, Prupis & Petigrow v. Gilbertson, 501 U.S. 350 (1991): identifies the intervening limitations rule to which Congress responded; §27A(b) supplies the bounded statutory remedy without deciding the underlying fraud claims.
- James B. Beam Distilling Co. v. Georgia, 501 U.S. 529 (1991), and Harper v. Virginia Department of Taxation, June 18, 1993: retain the distinction between judicial retroactivity in still-open litigation and final judgments; the present entitlement arises from an express statute, not judicial retroactivity alone.
- Landgraf v. USI Film Products and Rivers v. Roadway Express, April 26, 1994: preserve the separation between express legislative temporal direction and constitutional limits; no ordinary presumption against retroactivity defeats §27A(b)'s explicit reach.
- Chase Securities Corp. v. Donaldson, 325 U.S. 304 (1945): preserves its due-process treatment of revived limitations claims without treating that decision as dispositive of Article III.

#### Precedent Treatment and Current-Law Effect

Robertson's distinction between a legislative change in law and legislative adjudication extends to the confined reinstatement procedure here. The final-judgment question left open there is resolved only for the specified securities limitations dismissals. Klein and McCullough continue to protect independent judicial findings and adjudication. Harper, Beam, Landgraf and Rivers retain their distinct temporal rules; no earlier decision is overruled.

Section 27A(b) supplies a constitutional statutory route to reinstatement for the specified completed securities limitations dismissals. Its four cumulative prerequisites govern, and proof of all four requires reinstatement. Independent judicial adjudication of eligibility and of the ensuing claims, defenses and remedies remains essential. Pacific Mutual's January 31, 1992 motion met the sixty-day deadline; that fact is not a finding of fraud or entitlement to a merits judgment.

Subsection (a) separately prescribes the jurisdiction's June 19, 1991 limitations law, including retroactivity principles, for the specified private actions commenced on or before that date. The holding concerns subsection (b), not an independent reopening power under subsection (a). Finality remains a protection of judicial independence, subject to the bounded statutory entitlement sustained here. No general constitutional permission for retrospective legislation, compelled reopening of merits judgments or individualized legislative determination of liability follows.

#### Separate Positions Relevant to the Decision

Scalia dissents, joined by Kennedy and Thomas. A dismissal with prejudice that has become final without appeal is a completed exercise of judicial power even when it rests on limitations rather than a finding about fraud. Congress may change the law governing pending litigation, but requiring a court to reopen this concluded case withdraws the conclusiveness of its judgment. Leaving the underlying securities issues for judicial determination does not answer that objection: the judicial act already completed is the dismissal itself.

The dissent reads McCullough's protection of completed adjudication and Klein's protection of the judicial function as limits on the mandatory reopening command. Robertson's changed-law rule addresses pending litigation; Harper's direct-review rule does not reach ended cases. Wheeling Bridge concerns continuing prospective relief, and Sioux Nation concerns the Government's own defense. Neither establishes power to withdraw the effect of this private final judgment. A general class and an express temporal command do not remove the constitutional objection. The dissent would reverse the Fifth Circuit and require denial of Pacific Mutual's §27A(b) reinstatement motion, without deciding §27A(a), due process or the securities merits. This position does not control.

#### Source Notes

The [official Supreme Court report, 511 U.S. 658](https://tile.loc.gov/storage-services/service/ll/usrep/usrep511/usrep511658/usrep511658.pdf) establishes identity and participation. The full [Fifth Circuit opinion, 997 F.2d 39](https://www.ca5.uscourts.gov/opinions/pub/92/92-1662.CV0.wpd.pdf) supplies the final dismissal, legislation and timely reopening motion. Other plaintiffs discussed in that opinion are outside this docket.

#### Mandate, Remedy, and Stage

The Fifth Circuit's judgment sustaining the reinstatement procedure is affirmed. Proceedings may continue under §27A(b). The federal court must determine the statutory prerequisites and reinstate the action when they are proved; it has no discretion to deny reinstatement through a balancing test. The securities claims, defenses and remedies remain for ordinary judicial adjudication. No fraud finding, liability judgment or damages award is entered.

**End of entry: Morgan Stanley & Co. v. Pacific Mutual Life Insurance Co., Merits decision, 1994-05-23.**

### Plaut v. Spendthrift Farm, Inc., No. 93-1121

Merits decision, April 18, 1995

#### Chronology and Posture

Argued November 30, 1994; decided April 18, 1995. On writ of certiorari to the United States Court of Appeals for the Sixth Circuit, reviewing its August 3, 1993 judgment, 1 F.3d 1487. The question is whether §27A(b) may require reopening this final, unappealed private federal securities dismissal.

Chief Justice Stone-Zsela and Justices Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg, and Breyer participated at argument and decision.

#### Judgment

**Reversed and remanded, 5–4.**

**Supporting:** Chief Justice Stone-Zsela and Justices Stevens, Souter, Ginsburg, and Breyer.
**Opposing:** Justices O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, and Thomas.

The statutory conditions are satisfied, including the timely February 11, 1992 motion. Reinstatement is mandatory; the remand does not authorize discretionary refusal through an equitable balancing test. The underlying securities claims, defenses, and remedies remain for judicial adjudication. No finding of fraud, merits judgment, or damages award is entered.

#### Opinion Topology

| Writing | Author | Joined by | Relationship to judgment | Scope joined |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Opinion of the Court | Chief Justice Stone-Zsela | Stevens, Souter, Ginsburg, and Breyer | Reversal and remand | Entire opinion, including the statutory conditions, Article III rule, response to narrow retrospective coverage, limits, and remedy. |
| Dissent | Scalia | O'Connor, Kennedy, and Thomas | Would affirm denial of reinstatement | Entire dissent; no part of the Court's opinion. |

#### Controlling Propositions and Authority

##### Section 27A(b) permits mandatory reinstatement of this completed limitations dismissal

**Controlling proposition:** Under Morgan Stanley, Article III permits Congress to require reinstatement under §27A(b) of a qualifying private §10(b) action even though its limitations dismissal became final without appeal before the provision's December 19, 1991 enactment: the action must have commenced on or before June 19, 1991, been dismissed as time barred after that date, and been timely under the jurisdiction's law, including retroactivity principles, as it existed June 19, 1991, with a reinstatement motion filed within sixty days after enactment. The court independently determines those cumulative conditions and, when they are satisfied, must reinstate without discretionary balancing, retaining independent responsibility for all claims, defenses, and remedies; this expressly retrospective entitlement does not authorize legislative findings, dictated liability, or unrestricted reopening of completed merits judgments.

**Authority:** Chief Justice Stone-Zsela's Opinion of the Court, joined in full by Stevens, Souter, Ginsburg, and Breyer. Those five direct rationale joins control every proposition, qualification, and remedy stated here. O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, and Thomas dissent; no combination of separate rationales is necessary.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that Morgan Stanley governs this completed limitations dismissal. Finality matters: the unappealed judgment ended the ordinary review process. But Morgan Stanley distinguishes Congress's creation of a bounded entitlement to further adjudication from legislative adjudication of the controversy itself. Section 27A(b) supplies the governing rule; judges determine whether its conditions are satisfied and decide the securities claims.

The statute's narrow coverage and exclusively retrospective operation require attention, but do not make it an individualized legislative judgment. It reaches every action satisfying objective conditions concerning commencement, dismissal, governing limitations law, and the time for seeking relief. It does not identify parties for an award or assessment of liability. A closed temporal class is still a class defined by law. Congress has directed no finding of deception, reliance, causation, or damages. Independent judicial determination remains both at the eligibility stage and on the merits. Mandatory reinstatement after eligibility is established is the consequence of the new legal rule, not a legislative finding that the plaintiffs prevail on their claims.

Robertson distinguishes a genuine change in governing law from prescribed adjudication; Morgan Stanley supplies the completed-dismissal application that Robertson left open. Klein protects the judicial function without forbidding this statutory entitlement. McCullough's protection of completed judicial determinations remains: Congress may not dictate contrary findings or declare a defendant liable. Beam's finality limit on judicial retroactivity does not erase an express statutory remedy. Wheeling Bridge and Sioux Nation concern continuing equitable relief and sovereign waiver, respectively; neither supplies an unrestricted reopening power. Chase Securities addresses due-process revival, not the Article III issue resolved here.

The February 11, 1992 motion satisfied the statutory conditions. Reinstatement therefore follows, with the securities merits left for the courts. The dissent's objection treats completion of this limitations dismissal as an absolute bar despite the retained judicial work; Morgan Stanley forecloses that rule in this setting. Other completed merits judgments, individualized legislative commands, and independent constitutional protections remain outside this holding.

**Limits and questions not reached:** Congress may not command findings contrary to the record, impose liability on identified defendants by legislative declaration, or disguise an individualized award as a procedural reopening law. The Court decides no general power to set aside completed judgments adjudicating the underlying merits, no constitutionality of every retrospective statute, and no independent due-process question; applicable due-process protections and other constitutional restrictions remain. Section 27A(a), other applications of subsection (b), general severability, federal waiver, continuing prospective relief, and independently authorized judicial reopening are not decided. The decision neither adjudicates other parties' completed cases nor creates criminal or innocence remedies. Fraud, reliance, causation, liability, damages, and ordinary merits defenses remain for the courts; reinstatement establishes none of them.

**Precedent treatment:**

- Morgan Stanley & Co. v. Pacific Mutual Life Insurance Co., May 23, 1994: applies its five-Justice Article III holding sustaining §27A(b)'s bounded mandatory reinstatement procedure to this qualifying completed private limitations dismissal; preserves independent eligibility and merits adjudication and all constitutional limits.
- Robertson v. Seattle Audubon Society, March 25, 1992: applies its distinction between genuine legislative change and prescribed judicial adjudication; Morgan Stanley supplies the completed-dismissal rule that Robertson did not decide.
- United States v. Klein, 80 U.S. (13 Wall.) 128: applies its protection of the independent judicial function; a class-defined entitlement to renewed adjudication does not dictate findings or securities liability.
- McCullough v. Virginia, 172 U.S. 102: preserves protection of completed judicial determinations while distinguishing the statutory entitlement to litigate a limitations-dismissed claim from a legislative declaration of facts or liability contrary to an adjudication.
- James B. Beam Distilling Co. v. Georgia, 501 U.S. 529: preserves finality against reopening by later judicial doctrine alone; the entitlement here comes from an express statute whose Article III validity Morgan Stanley establishes.
- Pennsylvania v. Wheeling & Belmont Bridge Co., 59 U.S. (18 How.) 421: distinguishes the continuing operation of prospective equitable relief; that setting does not itself decide this completed limitations dismissal.
- United States v. Sioux Nation of Indians, 448 U.S. 371: distinguishes the Government's waiver of its own defense; no governmental waiver of a private defendant's defense supplies the holding here.
- Chase Securities Corp. v. Donaldson, 325 U.S. 304: leaves its due-process revival rule intact without treating it as a controlling answer to Article III.
- Lampf, Pleva, Lipkind, Prupis & Petigrow v. Gilbertson, 501 U.S. 350: identifies the limitations rule that produced the dismissal and the statutory response; §27A(b) supplies reinstatement without establishing the securities merits.

#### Precedent Treatment and Current-Law Effect

Morgan Stanley controls the constitutional question and mandatory statutory remedy. Robertson and Klein retain the distinction between legislation governing adjudication and legislative performance of adjudication. McCullough continues to protect completed judicial determinations within the distinction Morgan Stanley draws. Beam's rule of judicial retroactivity alone creates no reopening entitlement. Wheeling Bridge, Sioux Nation, and Chase Securities remain authorities for their distinct prospective-relief, sovereign-waiver, and due-process settings. Lampf remains the intervening limitations decision to which §27A(b) responds. No precedent is overruled.

A completed, unappealed private securities limitations dismissal within §27A(b)'s objective criteria must be reinstated on a timely qualifying motion. Courts determine eligibility independently and retain all adjudicative responsibility for the underlying claims, defenses, and remedies. A narrowly defined, exclusively retrospective class does not by itself turn this procedure into a legislative judgment: the statute covers every action meeting its legal criteria and selects no parties for legislatively determined liability. The rule does not establish a general power to reopen completed merits judgments or remove independent constitutional protections. The dissent's contrary finality rule is noncontrolling.

#### Separate Positions Relevant to the Decision

Justice Scalia dissents, joined by Justices O'Connor, Kennedy, and Thomas. A dismissal with prejudice that has become final without appeal is a completed exercise of judicial power even when it rests on limitations. In their view, Congress may change the law for pending cases but may not require an Article III court to undo its conclusive disposition after ordinary review has ended. Leaving fraud and damages for a later judicial decision does not preserve the conclusiveness of the dismissal itself. The dissent acknowledges that Morgan Stanley governs the statutory setting, but rejects its distinction between a new entitlement to adjudication and interference with the completed judgment.

McCullough supplies the dissent's protection of completed adjudication, and Klein protects judicial decision from legislative control. Robertson permitted changed law in pending litigation; it did not decide completed judgments. Beam preserves finality despite later judicial doctrine. Wheeling Bridge concerns prospective equitable relief and Sioux Nation the Government's own defense. The dissent treats neither setting as authority for reopening a private final dismissal. Congress's use of objective criteria and an express temporal command does not answer the dissent's objection: every judgment within the defined class has already conclusively disposed of its case. Narrow and wholly retrospective coverage strengthens the concern about legislative interference, although the dissent's objection also reaches a broader command to reopen such completed private judgments. The dissent would affirm denial of reinstatement, without deciding §27A(a), independent due-process claims, fraud, liability, or damages.

#### Source Notes

The [Sixth Circuit opinion and Keith concurrence, 1 F.3d 1487](https://law.resource.org/pub/us/case/reporter/F3/001/1.F3d.1487.92-5591.html) supply the filing, final dismissal, timely motion, §27A(b) conditions, and lower-court grounds. The Court's [Morgan Stanley decision, May 23, 1994](../../OT1993/output/OT_1993CHUNK5.md) supplies the governing Article III rule and its limits. These sources establish no finding of fraud, liability, or damages.

#### Mandate, Remedy, and Stage

The judgment of the Sixth Circuit is reversed. The case is remanded for reinstatement under the satisfied conditions of §27A(b) and further judicial proceedings on the underlying securities claims. Reinstatement is required, not a discretionary remedy. The courts retain responsibility for the merits, defenses, and any relief; no merits outcome is prescribed.

**End of entry: Plaut v. Spendthrift Farm, Inc., merits decision, April 18, 1995.**

### Washington v. Glucksberg, No. 96-110

Merits decision, June 26, 1997

#### Chronology and Posture

Argued January 8, 1997; decided June 26, 1997. On writ of certiorari to the United States Court of Appeals for the Ninth Circuit. Its March 6, 1996 en banc judgment, 79 F.3d 790, invalidated Washington's assisted-suicide prohibition as applied to competent terminally ill adults seeking physician assistance. The question is whether the Due Process Clause protects that asserted right against the prohibition on knowingly causing or aiding suicide. Washington seeks reversal; the physician respondents seek affirmance. Compassion in Dying was a party below and is not a party in this Court. Vacco v. Quill, concerning New York's equal-protection distinction, is separately decided today.

All nine Justices participated at argument and decision.

#### Judgment

**Reversed and remanded, 9–0.**

**Supporting:** Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer.

The Ninth Circuit's class-wide constitutional invalidation is reversed. On remand it must direct entry of judgment for Washington on the asserted substantive-due-process right to physician-assisted suicide. Forced treatment, valid refusal, lawful pain relief, good-faith palliation and discriminatory administration are outside this disposition.

| Writing | Author | Joined by | Relationship to judgment | Scope joined |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Opinion of the Court | Kennedy | Stone-Zsela, O'Connor, Scalia, Thomas | Reversal and remand | Entire opinion; five direct joins including the author |

#### Controlling Propositions and Authority

##### I. The asserted physician-assistance right is not fundamental

**Holding and operative rule:** The Due Process Clause does not establish a fundamental right for a competent, terminally ill adult to obtain a willing physician's intentional assistance in suicide through a lethal prescription. The asserted class-wide entitlement therefore receives no heightened protection; a materially different, more particularized claim is not foreclosed merely by this disposition.

**Authority:** The five-Justice opinion of the Court identified above directly supports both holdings and their limits. The other four Justices concur in the judgment on the separate grounds identified in Opinion Topology.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court addresses the claim actually made: a competent person approaching death seeks willing medical assistance to cause death, without demanding compulsory service or public financing. Its terminal-illness and competence limits matter. Neither an unrestricted wish to die nor a generic appeal to autonomy adequately describes it.

The text and structure of the Due Process Clause, the relevant precedents and the enduring legal treatment of assistance in suicide do not establish this entitlement as a fundamental liberty. The tradition of prohibiting such assistance bears on this claimed right; the Court does not adopt an exclusive rule requiring the most specifically imaginable historical analogue for every constitutional liberty. Cruzan explains refusal of unwanted treatment through bodily integrity, battery and informed consent. It assumed a competent person's right to refuse lifesaving nutrition and hydration and upheld Missouri's evidentiary safeguard for an incompetent patient's wishes. Ending an unwanted intervention does not itself establish permission for another person intentionally to supply lethal means.

Casey retains the protected reproductive decision, individualized viability, timely necessary life-and-health care and its complete six-step regulation standard. Mazurek requires application of that entire standard, including its purpose and residual-interference protections. Those established holdings are not reduced to a generic substantial-obstacle rule or displaced by history alone. They concern a defined reproductive liberty, however, and do not recognize every important medical decision as fundamental. The involvement of a willing professional is not itself dispositive: protected reproductive care also involves assistance. What remains unestablished here is constitutional protection for this distinct intentional lethal act. The refusal and reproductive precedents therefore do not require the asserted extension.

**Limits:** The Court does not decide forced treatment, interference with competent refusal or valid directives, denial of lawful pain care, punishment of good-faith palliation merely because death is foreseen, or discriminatory administration. The reserved claim must be materially different from the class entitlement rejected here; the reservation neither recognizes that entitlement nor creates a new pending action.

##### II. Washington's prohibition rationally serves its protective interests

**Holding and operative rule:** Because the asserted assistance entitlement is not fundamental, Washington's prohibition is valid against this substantive-due-process challenge if it is rationally related to legitimate state interests. It meets that standard by protecting life, preventing suicide, guarding vulnerable people against error and pressure, maintaining medical responsibility and containing the risks of a legalized lethal practice; the Ninth Circuit's class-wide invalidation cannot stand.

**Authority:** The same five-Justice Opinion of the Court supplies direct majority support. This rationality determination completes the rejection of the claim; the absence of a fundamental right alone is not treated as sufficient to dispose of every constitutional objection.

**Controlling explanation:** The State may protect human life without grading the value of particular lives. Terminal illness does not eliminate the risks of mistaken diagnosis, depression, coercion, family pressure, unequal care or indifference toward vulnerable persons. A physician's involvement does not guarantee a reliable determination that a request is informed, responsible and voluntary. Washington may also preserve the professional duty to care and guard against expansion from the proposed class into voluntary or involuntary euthanasia.

The physicians answer that competent adults near death can choose deliberately and that safeguards could confine the practice. The Court does not find that any named person lacked capacity or that any physician acted improperly. The issue is whether the State can reasonably address risks attending authorization of the practice as a class. Difficulty assessing voluntariness, predicting terminal decline and enforcing the proposed boundary supplies the required connection. Rational review does not require certainty that every risk will occur, a finding that every request is involuntary, or proof that prohibition is the best available policy. Nor must the State adopt a less restrictive scheme solely because one can be proposed.

The legal availability of treatment refusal and good-faith pain relief preserves their distinct functions; the challenged prohibition does not require unwanted intervention or forbid palliation because a shorter life is foreseeable. That permission is not a guarantee that every patient's suffering is relieved. States remain free to authorize and regulate assisted death within applicable constitutional limits. This judgment sustains Washington's chosen protective response to the claim before the Court without imposing a national legislative policy.

**Remedy and scope:** The Ninth Circuit must direct entry of judgment for Washington on the asserted substantive-due-process right to physician-assisted suicide. The remand does not authorize lethal prescriptions, command a new patient-specific hearing or adjudicate a materially different claim merely because it could arise.

#### Precedent Treatment

- Cruzan v. Director, Missouri Department of Health: preserves its bodily-integrity and informed-consent foundation, its assumed competent-refusal premise concerning nutrition and hydration, and its approval of Missouri's clear-and-convincing safeguard for an incompetent patient's wishes. The Court declines to extend that premise to intentional lethal assistance.
- Planned Parenthood of Southeastern Pennsylvania v. Casey: leaves the protected reproductive choice, individualized viability, required life-and-health protection and complete six-step standard intact. Its established liberty is distinguished from the asserted assisted-suicide entitlement; neither its constitutional foundation nor any regulatory component is overruled.
- Mazurek v. Armstrong: preserves its requirement to apply the full Casey standard, including independent purpose review and the severe- and meaningful-interference inquiries that remain below a substantial obstacle. That abortion-regulation rule is not transplanted into a universal medical-liberty test.

#### Law After Decision

The asserted class-wide entitlement to willing physician assistance in suicide is not a fundamental right, and Washington's prohibition survives rational review against that claim. These are the controlling additions effective June 26, 1997. Existing refusal and reproductive precedents retain their scope. No exclusive history-only method governing all fundamental liberties, universal medical-regulation standard, constitutional pain-care test or prohibition on legislative authorization of assisted death is established. A materially different particularized claim remains subject to its own facts and governing law.

#### Separate Positions Relevant to the Decision

**O'Connor, concurring.** She joins the Court's opinion but does not decide whether a competent person facing imminent death and great suffering has a cognizable interest in controlling those circumstances. Washington permits qualified physicians to alleviate suffering with medication even to unconsciousness and despite foreseeable hastening of death. Given that legal availability, the risks of erroneous judgments about terminal condition, competence and voluntariness sufficiently support the prohibition even if the narrower interest is assumed. Her Cruzan concurrence supports States' initial development of safeguards and democratic consideration. This neither guarantees effective delivery of pain care, eliminates suffering associated with sedation, requires legislative exhaustion nor creates a freestanding constitutional pain-care standard. Ginsburg adopts substantially these separate reasons. Breyer joins the separate analysis except insofar as it joins the principal opinions.

**Stevens, concurring in the judgment.** No person has complete autonomy to end life regardless of others' interests, and the State may maintain a general prohibition. That conclusion does not decide a competent individual's well-supported claim concerning the manner of an unavoidable imminent death. His Cruzan dissent and Casey's defined protected choice support attention to dignity, intolerable suffering and genuine justification. Preservation of life does not automatically outweigh every such interest. Mistake, coercion and professional-integrity concerns justify the general rule but may carry different weight in an exceptional case. Palliation does not logically eliminate every form of suffering or the distinction between conscious relief and terminal sedation. He also questions whether intent and causation invariably distinguish treatment withdrawal, sedation and lethal prescriptions; those overlaps do not establish this class-wide entitlement. He finds no individual entitlement proved here and orders no additional hearing.

**Souter, concurring in the judgment.** Harlan's Poe analysis asks whether the restraint is arbitrary or purposeless in light of the relative importance and fit of the individual and state interests. Traditions retained and abandoned inform that inquiry; neither longstanding prohibition alone nor an abstract autonomy formula resolves it. Responsible terminally ill patients seeking medical counsel and assistance present an important interest connected to bodily integrity, treatment refusal and defined reproductive choice. Casey's complete framework is not a universal medical test. He accepts the competent and voluntary premises and provisionally accepts the professional premises for this claim without inventing incapacity or misconduct.

Protection against mistaken or involuntary suicide and expansion into euthanasia supplies the sufficient justification. Family pressure alone would not settle the matter, because proposed physician screening is intended to answer it. The harder problem is reliably assessing a knowing, responsible decision and ensuring that physicians preserve the asserted boundaries. The disputed evidence concerning safeguards and the Dutch experience establishes a serious empirical uncertainty, not an incontrovertible foreign-law conclusion. Courts cannot presently displace the State's protective judgment with adequate assurance; legislatures can investigate and develop experience.

His conclusion rejects this entitlement now without permanently foreclosing recognition, demanding legislative experiments, imposing a judicial moratorium or assuming inevitable logical expansion of every protected right. Legislative foot-dragging and a materially different individual record remain for another case. Incidental difficulty does not automatically require compelling justification, and not every liberty claim demands elaborate balancing: the importance of the interest and the burden determine the inquiry. His view does not become the Court's general standard.

**Ginsburg, concurring in the judgment.** She adopts substantially O'Connor's separate reasons about the unresolved imminent-death and suffering interest and the legal availability of palliation. She does not join the principal opinion or a declaration sustaining every future application. Her agreement creates no additional independent theory.

**Breyer, concurring in the judgment.** The potential liberty combines personal control over the manner of death, willing medical assistance and avoidance of unnecessary severe physical suffering. Describing it merely as any right to suicide misses the strongest claim without establishing that claim as fundamental. Poe, Cruzan and the defined personal-liberty authorities support serious consideration. Because the law does not forbid the pain treatment central to that interest, the present case need not decide its status; the stated protective interests suffice here. Delivery failures are not the same as a legal prohibition. He recognizes sedation and does not find every person's suffering treatable. A prohibition of necessary palliation would present a materially different case. These reasons supplement his qualified O'Connor join; they do not adopt the principal opinion or one universal liberty method for all judgment supporters.

The prohibition on knowingly causing or aiding suicide coexists with distinct treatment-refusal, advance-directive and palliative-care rules. #### Mandate, Remedy, and Stage

The case returns to the Ninth Circuit, which must direct entry of judgment for Washington on the asserted physician-assisted-suicide claim. No new patient action, clinical procedure, legislative study, compulsory experiment, additional evidentiary hearing or injunction authorizing prescriptions is ordered. The Court's reservations establish no actual pending claim or automatic further proceedings beyond implementing the mandate.

#### Controlling Propositions and Authority

##### I. Imminent personal exposure permits this challenge

**Rule and authority:** A student facing a concrete, imminent application of an official school-event mechanism may seek prospective relief before the threatened exposure occurs. Part I has all nine joins and controls; standing does not establish the threatened conduct's illegality.

**Controlling explanation:** Lujan requires personal injury, a causal connection and redressability, rather than abstract disagreement with a statute. These enrolled students face impending events under a mandatory permission mechanism administered by school officials, and the hearing record supports that concrete prospect. Their parents may represent their children's interests through the proper representative route. The absence of a completed local policy does not make the impending exposure remote, while the availability of lawful applications remains relevant to the merits and remedy. This holding establishes neither general taxpayer standing nor a right for an abstract objector elsewhere.

##### II. Official school worship cannot be insulated by student delivery or a disclaimer

**Rule and authority:** A school violates the Establishment Clause when it organizes, adopts, directs or religiously supervises worship as part of its own event; student initiation or delivery and a statutory disclaimer do not alone make that exercise private. Part II controls through Ginsburg, Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Souter and Breyer, independently of a separate quantified-pressure showing.

**Controlling explanation:** Lee supplies the prohibition on official sponsorship of school worship. The mandatory event permission and religious-content eligibility terms give officials an operational role that must be examined, rather than disregarded because the speaker is a student. Section 1(2) permits student-initiated, nonsectarian, nonproselytizing prayer at compulsory and noncompulsory events; the State may not administer those conditions through official devotional organization or religious supervision. Section 1(4)'s disclaimer does not erase actual official conduct. Section 2, by contrast, denies schools authority to prescribe prayer's form or content. That is a statutory command, not a factual finding that it is always obeyed or always violated. Pinette and Chabad require attention to attribution, while Rosenberger protects neutral support for independently edited private speech; none makes every school religious statement official. The supported prospective mechanism warrants bounded interim protection, not a final holding against every permissible application.

##### III. School-directed worship in a pressured setting is independently forbidden

**Rule and authority:** School direction of religious exercise in compulsory or comparably pressured school settings violates the Establishment Clause when the official setting places actual pressure on students to participate. Part III independently controls through Ginsburg, Stevens, O'Connor, Kennedy, Souter and Breyer; mere offense at another student's private viewpoint does not suffice.

**Controlling explanation:** Lee's distinct school-pressure holding supplies this ground. Official control, students' attendance and participation expectations, and the setting's practical demands matter even without a legal punishment for declining prayer. The State cannot dispose of that inquiry merely by describing participation as voluntary or substituting a student speaker. These circumstances support interim protection against the official mechanism. The Court does not presume coercion from hearing private religious speech, require a quantified social-pressure measurement, or hold every voluntary gathering coercive. This is an alternative ground with its own six-Justice coalition, not an aggregation with Part II.

##### IV. Correct the graduation exception and preserve lawful expression

**Rule and authority:** Preliminary relief must restrain supported unlawful official worship while preserving independently lawful statutory provisions and genuinely private expression. Part IV's legal limit has all nine joins; its continued restraint, removal of graduation's categorical exemption and remand have Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Kennedy, Souter, Ginsburg and Breyer.

**Controlling explanation:** Lee does not exempt graduation merely because students initiate and deliver prayer; the same official attribution and pressure inquiries apply. A genuinely independent student's religious viewpoint remains protected. The lower court must therefore remove its automatic graduation safe harbor and frame the interim order around the actual official mechanism. Section 1(3)'s protection of other voluntary expression, Section 2's separate voluntary-prayer provision and no-prescription command, and Section 1(5)'s severability direction must each receive effect. The supported threat of official worship establishes the relevant interim constitutional injury; narrowing protects the countervailing private-speech interests. The Court neither compels a new forum nor deletes the religious filters to create a broader statutory access entitlement. State-law construction and the precise severed decree remain below. Scalia and Thomas agree with the private-expression limit but would remove the retained restraint too.

##### V-A. Actual adequate representation defeats intervention as of right here

**Rule and authority:** Under Rule 24(a)(2), a timely applicant with a protectable interest potentially impaired by litigation is entitled to intervene if existing representation may be inadequate; a shared desired outcome or the State's official identity does not conclusively establish adequacy. All eight Associates join Part V-A and hold the actual representation adequate here, affirming denial without deciding unnecessary alternative elements.

**Controlling explanation:** Trbovich supplies the minimal burden for showing possible inadequacy and recognizes that governmental and private interests may differ. The Attorney General here actually advanced the proposed intervenors' private-expression interests, and they identify no relevant interest omitted from this statutory-validity controversy. The required narrowed decree protects those independent interests. Their private status alone therefore does not establish a representational gap on this record. Amicus participation could not replace an otherwise established right to intervene. Materially changed interests may be considered on a proper later application; this judgment orders neither a renewed hearing nor automatic future intervention.

##### V-B. A common question permits but does not compel permissive intervention

**Rule and authority:** Rule 24(b) permits intervention where its common-question condition is met, subject to the court's discretion and consideration of undue delay or prejudice to the original parties; it does not require admission merely because participation would be relevant. All eight Associates join Part V-B and affirm the denial as within discretion on this record.

**Controlling explanation:** The lower court's duplication and delay assessment is permissible in this narrow statutory proceeding. The opportunity to make amicus submissions helps explain why excluding duplicative party participation caused no demonstrated prejudice, but does not defeat intervention as of right. The Court adopts neither an absolute rule against applicants without novel legal arguments nor an unreviewable discretion or universal plain-error requirement. Stone-Zsela agrees with the disposition without adding a ground.

### Tanford v. Brand, Seventh Circuit No. 96-3053

#### Chronology and Posture

Tanford v. Brand, review of the Seventh Circuit's judgment in No. 96-3053, 104 F.3d 982. Decided December 22, 1997, on the merits of the challenge to Indiana University's official commencement invocations and benedictions. No separate Supreme Court docket or argument date is stated.

**Reversed and remanded, 6–3.**

**Opposing:** Scalia, Kennedy and Thomas, who would affirm.

Any prospective relief the lower courts find warranted, subject to the plaintiffs' continuing standing and the requirements for an injunction, must be limited to the University's selection or conduct of invocation and benediction prayers as official worship and must preserve independent prayer and lawful private expression. No damages, fee ruling, automatic hearing, mandatory new forum or general ban on religious speech is ordered.

#### Controlling Propositions and Authority

##### 1. The live controversy concerns the University's own devotional program.

The continuing policy and Urbanski's concrete expectation of attending his 1999 graduation support prospective review. The University selects a religious leader and incorporates actual invocation and benediction prayers into its official ceremony; independent composition of the words does not make the exercises private equal-access speech. Part I is unanimous.

Lee's prospective-exposure principle supports the live claim without treating other plaintiffs as perpetual students or relying on ideological objection alone. The program places the invocation after the national anthem and the benediction at the close. The reproduced benediction invites prayer, addresses God, seeks divine assistance and concludes with Amen. The University need not have written a full script for the exercise to be its own: official selection and incorporation establish attribution. The limited instruction to offer an uplifting general message and the clergy's independent wording remain part of the record, not evidence of private-forum access.

##### 2. Official university commencement worship violates the Establishment Clause on this record.

A public university violates the Establishment Clause when it incorporates officially arranged religious prayer into its commencement exercises, as the University did by selecting a religious leader to conduct actual invocation and benediction prayers as components of its official commencement. The violation does not depend on whether attendance is compulsory, whether the prayer is sectarian, or whether the ceremony also serves secular purposes. This governmental organization of worship falls under the specific-prohibition route of Lemon as Agostini modified it; mature attendance, meaningful freedom to abstain, brevity, nonsectarian language and a sincere ceremonial purpose do not cure it. Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Souter, Ginsburg and Breyer directly adopt Parts II–III.

Agostini retains Lemon as the governing test while treating purpose, effects and entanglement as evidence within one inquiry, not independent prongs. A specific prohibition or distinct doctrine governs conduct it resolves; an established permissible relationship defeats the claim on that ground; otherwise a legally sufficient unresolved allegation receives the attribution-and-function inquiry. Lee and Ingebretsen supply the official-worship prohibition applied here. The University selects and presents actual devotional exercises as its own commencement activity, so that specific prohibition resolves this case without a separate primary-effect test or a residual inquiry. Today's decision applies that principle to the previously unresolved university setting. The audience's maturity and genuine freedom to remain seated, leave or attend another ceremony bear materially on coercion, but do not change whose worship is presented. Most attendees remained seated, ordinary movement was common, and no penalty followed nonattendance. The Court accepts those facts without finding adolescent pressure or requiring a separate coercion showing.

The practice's long history and purpose of solemnity receive their actual weight but establish no permissible relationship authorizing this governmental devotional act. Marsh protects a distinct legislative-prayer setting; it does not establish an exception for every adult ceremony. Sherman permits a particular civic pledge and expressly distinguishes actual prayer; placing worship in a civic event does not convert it into civic acknowledgment. A secular ceremonial objective cannot justify the prohibited means. The exercises' brevity, and the view below that any advancement or entanglement was de minimis, do not determine whether the worship is officially organized. The ruling requires no sham-purpose, denominational-exclusion or excessive-entanglement finding. It applies Lemon as Agostini modified it and decides no other adult ceremonial arrangement.

##### 3. Relief must address the supported official practice and preserve private expression.

On remand, the lower courts shall determine appropriate prospective relief against the University's official selection and inclusion of these prayers, subject to the plaintiffs' continuing standing and the ordinary requirements for an injunction. Independently chosen private religious expression, neutral access to a genuine forum and nondevotional ceremonial remarks remain distinct; the judgment requires no new forum and no suppression of otherwise lawful viewpoints.

Ingebretsen supplies the remedy's official/private boundary. Any decree must reach the established institutional practice, not every religious reference, speaker or activity at the University. Private remarks by graduates, historical acknowledgment without devotional operation and a moment of silence are not prohibited by this holding. Any decree the lower courts enter must not prescribe new ceremonial content or treat these reservations as a ruling that arrangements not before the Court are permissible.

#### Precedent Treatment

Lee and Ingebretsen's official-worship prohibition is applied to the actual university commencement program; their separate substantial-pressure rules remain unchanged and need not be applied to find a violation here. Engel and Schempp retain their official-worship protections despite nonsectarian wording and permission to abstain. Lemon remains the governing test as Agostini modified it: the specific-prohibition route resolves this governmental organization of worship, and primary religious effect is evidence rather than an independent prong. Agostini's onsite secular-service holding does not authorize governmental worship. Marsh is distinguished by its legislative context, and Sherman by its civic exercise. Pinette, Rosenberger, Good News and Mergens preserve genuinely private religious expression; their neutral-access holdings do not authorize governmental worship. No precedent is overruled.

#### Law After Decision

Effective December 22, 1997, a public university violates the Establishment Clause when it incorporates officially arranged religious prayer into its commencement exercises; the violation does not depend on compulsory attendance, sectarian content or the ceremony's secular purposes. The University's official selection and incorporation of these devotional prayers is unconstitutional even for the mature voluntary audience shown here. Lemon, as Agostini modified it, resolves this governmental organization of worship through its specific-prohibition route, independently of a coercion finding. The separate pressure protections, private-speech rules, legislative-prayer precedent and ordinary ceremonial acknowledgment retain their existing limits. The decision creates no general prohibition on religion at universities or on every religious feature of an adult ceremony.

#### Separate Writings

Justice Scalia's dissent, joined by Justices Kennedy and Thomas in Part I, would affirm because this brief traditional adult ceremony, with genuine freedom to abstain and no demonstrated unconstitutional pressure, falls outside the established secondary-school prohibitions. All three joined Agostini's modification of Lemon. They regard this particular historical ceremonial relationship as permissible within that test, drawing on Marsh while recognizing that its holding concerns legislative prayer. They acknowledge that the exercises are official and devotional; they disagree with the Court over whether the official-worship prohibition or a permissible ceremonial relationship governs this adult setting. The Court rejects that extension of Marsh. Their position does not make every longstanding adult prayer practice permissible.

Justice Kennedy separately explains that Lee protects against substantial practical pressure without requiring a legal penalty. The mature audience, optional attendance, ordinary movement, seated abstention and available exit or reentry do not establish that pressure here. Consistently with his full join in Agostini, he accepts the modified Lemon test but regards this concrete voluntary adult ceremony as a permissible relationship, declining the Court's application of the official-worship prohibition to this university setting. ### County of Sacramento v. Lewis, No. 96-1337

Plenary merits decision, May 26, 1998

#### Chronology and Posture

Argued December 9, 1997. On writ of certiorari to the United States Court of Appeals for the Ninth Circuit, 98 F.3d 434. The case concerns the constitutional culpability required when a rapid police pursuit ends in an unintended fatal collision. After final summary judgment, the court of appeals reversed Deputy Smith's individual qualified-immunity judgment using a deliberate-indifference or reckless-disregard standard. Its affirmance of the municipal judgments and the unappealed state-law dispositions are outside this Court's review. No Fourth or Eighth Amendment claim is presented.

#### Judgment

**Reversed, 9–0.**

**Supporting:** Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer.

The case returns for proceedings consistent with termination of the reviewed federal individual claim, leaving the district court's favorable individual judgment in effect. Six Justices adopt the Court's contextual constitutional ground; Scalia and Thomas rely on a distinct constitutional ground, and Stevens relies only on qualified immunity. No damages, trial, new evidentiary hearing, municipal retrial or state-law judgment is directed.

#### Holdings

**Holding and operative rule:** In the rapidly evolving high-speed pursuit presented here, where officers must make instant choices amid competing enforcement and safety obligations, reckless or deliberately indifferent risk-taking alone does not establish a substantive-due-process violation. A purpose to cause harm unrelated to a legitimate law-enforcement objective is required; because this plaintiff-favorable record does not establish that purpose, the reviewed federal individual claim fails.

**Authority:** Kennedy's opinion, joined by Stone-Zsela, O'Connor, Souter, Ginsburg and Breyer, supplies six direct votes for the complete rule and application. Neither the two distinct constitutional votes nor Stevens's immunity vote supplies additional support for that rule. No combination of separate rationales is needed.

**Controlling explanation:** Rochin identifies the constitutional boundary against arbitrary executive abuse; its conscience-shocking standard must be applied through precedent, constitutional tradition and the actual setting, not a judge's personal reaction. Daniels excludes negligence but left intermediate culpability unresolved. Whitley explains why competing obligations and an emergency matter to fault, while reserving the civilian due-process question. Farmer's actual-awareness standard concerns prison safety with a materially different opportunity to respond; Doe likewise requires knowledge, authority and a reasonable opportunity for protection against deliberate abuse. Those holdings do not mechanically determine culpability in a rapid pursuit.

The officer chose to pursue, and the favorable account includes dangerous speed, close following distance, danger to others, inadequate communication and alleged policy violations. The lack of a serious predicate offense and the ability to abandon pursuit are substantial criticisms. They do not, in this rapidly developing encounter, establish the improper harmful purpose required for constitutional liability. The collision was unintended, and the supported record supplies no purpose to injure unrelated to enforcement. The Court finds neither wise police judgment nor an adjudicated benevolent motive.

Actual opportunity for deliberation remains important. This rule is not a universal intent requirement, an automatic deliberate-indifference rule whenever an official could desist, or permission to manufacture an emergency. Purposeful collisions, deliberate terrorizing conduct, contrived encounters and materially different opportunities for considered action require their own facts and analysis. The decision creates no seconds-based cutoff, broad noncustodial immunity or mandatory order between constitutional merits and qualified immunity. It resolves neither the excluded municipal and state claims nor a procedural-due-process challenge to state remedies.

#### Precedent Treatment

- **Rochin v. California, 342 U.S. 165:** Applied as the executive-abuse boundary, with the required culpability identified through the actual emergency setting.
- **Daniels v. Williams, 474 U.S. 327:** Its negligence exclusion remains; the Court resolves the previously open intermediate-culpability question only for this rapid pursuit.
- **Whitley v. Albers, 475 U.S. 312:** Its competing-obligation analysis informs the rule, but its prison-security holding is not treated as already deciding civilian pursuit liability.
- **Farmer v. Brennan, June 6, 1994:** Distinguished by the custodial duty and actual opportunity for response. Its awareness, reasonable-response and permissible-inference rules remain intact.
- **Doe v. Taylor Independent School District, October 16, 1995:** Distinguished because knowing supervisory acquiescence in deliberate abuse requires authority, opportunity and causal participation not supplied merely by continuing this pursuit.
- **Collins v. City of Harker Heights, February 26, 1992:** Its bounded workplace-omission holdings do not automatically resolve affirmative police conduct or merge the underlying right with municipal attribution.
- **Pinder v. Johnson, May 21, 1996:** Its distinction between nonprotection and affirmative deprivation remains; it did not decide this culpability question.
- **Uhlrig v. Harder, February 18, 1997:** Its conduct-time immunity disposition is preserved without importing the lower court's unadopted merits test or a mandatory merits-first sequence.
- **United States v. Lanier, March 31, 1997:** Its rejection of categorical immunity for noncustodial official wrongdoing remains. A governing specific constitutional provision must be used where it covers a claim; that principle does not itself dispose of every due-process claim.
- **Albright v. Oliver, January 24, 1994:** Its fractured judgment supplies no controlling general displacement rationale for this case.
- **Washington v. Glucksberg, June 26, 1997:** Its assistance-in-suicide holding remains limited; no exclusive most-specific-history methodology governing all liberties is established or displaced here.

#### Law After Decision

The decision establishes a purpose-to-harm requirement for the supported rapid-pursuit setting and rejects the circuit's use of reckless disregard or deliberate indifference alone there. Existing deliberate-indifference rules retain their distinct duties, opportunities for response and proof requirements. Constitutional wrongdoing remains separate from qualified immunity, municipal attribution and state-law liability. A purposeful or materially more deliberative encounter is not resolved merely by calling it a pursuit.

#### Separate Positions Relevant to the Decision

Stevens concurs only in the judgment. He would resolve the individual claim on qualified immunity without deciding the difficult constitutional standard. All relevant law existing on May 22, 1990 must be considered under the particularized-notice approach. Garner and Brower concern intentional force or intentionally applied stopping means; Checki concerns intentional misuse of police power to terrorize; Wood II concerns affirmative interference with protective circumstances. The reported pursuit decisions did not establish this claim's constitutional boundary merely by recognizing a possible egregious case, and Daniels reserved intermediate culpability. Neither the absence of an identical case nor a tally of judicial disagreement decides immunity. On the complete favorable account, the existing law did not make this pursuit's federal unlawfulness sufficiently apparent. Later decisions cannot supply retrospective notice. He makes no finding that the conduct was constitutional.

Scalia, joined by Thomas, concurs in the judgment on a different constitutional ground. The carefully described asserted right against this reckless pursuit and unintended collision lacks sufficient textual, historical or precedential support. A general protection of life or bodily security does not by itself define the constitutional tort. They reject the Court's reliance on an open-ended conscience standard and do not join its affirmative purpose-to-harm definition, although they agree that these facts establish no constitutional violation. Glucksberg does not impose a universally exclusive history method; their objection rests on the absence of adequate support for this particular asserted right and on the limits of judicially defined constitutional tort liability. Their position does not categorically exclude direct intentional assaults outside custody.

Breyer fully joins the Court. He separately preserves flexibility to decide a suitable case on qualified immunity before resolving a difficult or poorly presented constitutional question. That approach is consistent with Uhlrig and the distinction between actual wrongdoing and conduct-time notice. Today's choice to resolve the pursuit standard is not a compulsory merits-first sequence for every later case.
