### Freightliner Corp. v. Myrick, No. 94-286

Merits decision, April 18, 1995

Argued February 22, 1995; decided April 18, 1995. On writ of certiorari to the United States Court of Appeals for the Eleventh Circuit, reviewing its February 11, 1994 judgment, 13 F.3d 1516. The question is whether the Safety Act expressly or through an actual conflict preempts the presented state common-law claims alleging omitted antilock equipment.

All nine Justices participated at argument and decision.

The Court unanimously affirms the judgment returning the Myrick and Lindsey common-law claims for further proceedings. Chief Justice Stone-Zsela and Justices Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg, and Breyer support that judgment, 9–0. The asserted preemption grounds do not bar the claims, which proceed under ordinary proof and defenses. The Myricks' Georgia RICO summary judgment is outside the live appeal because that appellate contention was abandoned. It remains undisturbed, as do the Fruehauf dismissal and stipulated predecessor treatment; no nonlive party or component is revived or newly adjudicated.

Justice Ginsburg delivers the opinion of the Court, joined in full by Chief Justice Stone-Zsela and Justices Stevens, O'Connor, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, and Breyer. Those eight Justices adopt both the bounded express-preemption holding and the actual-conflict holding below. Justice Scalia concurs only in the judgment, without a separate opinion or stated reason; his agreement adds no vote to either explanation.

Section 1392(d)'s express restriction requires an in-effect federal motor-vehicle safety standard governing the same aspect of performance as the asserted state duty. The asserted antilock-equipment defect claims are not expressly displaced on this record because that federal predicate is absent; the Court does not decide whether common-law duties themselves are safety standards within that provision.

The Court holds that the express clause must be applied to the particular aspect of performance actually regulated. Easterwood requires attention to federal coverage of the duty at issue, and Cipollone requires examination of the enacted preemption language and predicate duty. Regulation of vehicle braking generally does not establish an operative antilock requirement for these claims. Paccar judicially invalidated inadequately supported stopping-distance requirements; that invalidation does not itself supply a federal safety standard forbidding state liability for omitted antilock equipment. Section 1392(d)'s condition is therefore unsatisfied without deciding whether common-law duties fall within its category of state standards. Section 1397(k) states that compliance with a federal safety standard does not exempt a person from common-law liability. The Court preserves that text but need not determine its every effect where an operative standard exists. The common-law claims return for ordinary adjudication; this statutory conclusion establishes neither a product defect nor causation and does not disturb the independent Georgia RICO judgment.

CSX Transportation, Inc. v. Easterwood, April 21, 1993, supplies attention to the aspect of performance actually covered; its separate railroad-speed holding is not imported, and every braking rule is not treated as coverage of antilock omission. Cipollone v. Liggett Group, Inc., June 24, 1992, supplies clause- and duty-specific analysis, without making its cigarette holdings dispositive of every Safety Act question. Paccar, Inc. v. National Highway Traffic Safety Administration, 573 F.2d 632, establishes judicial invalidation as part of the regulatory history, not a new federal right to avoid common-law liability.

An express preemption clause does not categorically foreclose inquiry into an independently established actual conflict with operative federal law or a demonstrated federal objective. Here neither impossibility nor interference with such an objective is established by judicial invalidation and the absence of an operative antilock requirement, so the asserted conflict does not bar these common-law claims.

The Court holds that the failure of express preemption does not end every possible conflict inquiry. The Eleventh Circuit read Cipollone too broadly when it treated Congress's express clause as invariably excluding implied preemption. Gade rejects automatic displacement from regulatory overlap but supplies no physical-impossibility-only rule. The manufacturers nevertheless must identify the federal obligation or policy that their asserted state duty would frustrate. Their strongest claim treats the ability to sell trucks without antilock equipment as a protected federal choice. Paccar does not establish that premise: the court rejected the agency's evidentiary support while allowing adequately supported future standards. It did not adopt a policy insulating manufacturers from state liability. Compliance with the asserted duty is not shown to violate federal law, and regulatory silence alone does not demonstrate the claimed obstacle. A valid operative standard or demonstrated affirmative policy requiring a uniform absence of regulation could present a different case. The Court leaves that case, and the ultimate tort merits here, undecided.

Cipollone v. Liggett Group, Inc., June 24, 1992, retains its enacted-clause holdings; the Court rejects the lower court's universal inference that any express provision exhausts every possible actual-conflict inquiry. Gade v. National Solid Wastes Management Association, June 18, 1992, retains the shared rule against automatic displacement from overlap; no physical-impossibility-only theory or broader separate theory becomes Court law. Paccar, Inc. v. National Highway Traffic Safety Administration, 573 F.2d 632, establishes inadequate support for a mandatory standard, not an affirmative policy protecting noninstallation against state duties.

Section 1397(k)'s compliance saving clause is not held to immunize every common-law action from an actual conflict. The Court does not decide whether tort duties are standards under §1392(d), every effect of the saving clause when a standard exists, or a federal field-wide ceiling. It does not find all air-brake regulation absent, invent an agency policy from an unproduced revision, or decide defect, accident causation, consortium entitlement, wrongful-death recovery, or damages.

Easterwood and Cipollone require attention to actual coverage and the enacted clause. Cipollone is not read as universally foreclosing an independent actual-conflict inquiry. Gade supplies no automatic displacement from overlap and no Court-wide physical-impossibility-only rule. Paccar's judicial invalidation does not establish a protected federal freedom from the asserted tort duties.

The presented claims survive the asserted express and conflict-preemption grounds because no applicable in-effect antilock standard or demonstrated conflicting federal command or objective establishes displacement. A saving clause preserves its actual terms without supplying immunity from every actual conflict. Valid operative standards and proved affirmative no-regulation policies remain distinct possibilities; no product-liability merits finding follows.

#### Mandate, Remedy, and Stage

The common-law claims return for ordinary proceedings on proof and defenses. The Georgia RICO summary judgment, Fruehauf dismissal and stipulated predecessor treatment remain undisturbed. No product defect, causation, consortium entitlement, wrongful-death recovery or damages is established.
