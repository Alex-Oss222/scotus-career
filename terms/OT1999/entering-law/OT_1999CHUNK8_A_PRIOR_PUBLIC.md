### St. Mary's Honor Center v. Hicks, No. 92-602

Merits disposition, 1993-06-25

#### Chronology and Posture

Argued 1993-04-20; decided 1993-06-25. On writ of certiorari to Eighth Circuit,970 F.2d487 (1992).

**Question:** Whether a Title VII plaintiff is entitled to judgment as a matter of law once the factfinder rejects all of the employer’s stated nondiscriminatory reasons, or must still persuade the factfinder of intentional discrimination.

**Material record:** Hicks, a Black correctional officer and shift commander, was demoted and discharged. He sued St. Mary's under Title VII and superintendent Steve Long under 42 U.S.C. § 1983 for the alleged racial discrimination. Following a bench trial, the district court found a prima facie case, found the employer’s articulated disciplinary explanations unworthy of belief, but did not find racial motivation proved. It suggested personal animosity rather than race. The court of appeals did not complete ordinary clear-error review of that finding because it treated rejection of all offered reasons as dispositive. Whether the alternative explanation is supported and was fairly litigated is distinct from the abstract burden rule.

**Relief sought:** The employer seeks reversal of the automatic-liability rule. Hicks seeks affirmance or review of the adverse factual finding under the correct evidentiary framework.

All nine Justices—Stone-Zsela, White, Blackmun, Stevens, O’Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter and Thomas—participated at argument and decision.

#### Judgment

The Eighth Circuit judgment directing automatic liability is vacated and the case remanded, 5–4. No majority adopts Stone’s new persuasion allocation or directs judgment for either party.

| Title VII automatic-liability mandate | Vacate appellate judgment and remand (5–4) | Stone-Zsela, O’Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Thomas | White, Blackmun, Stevens, Souter | Five reject compulsory judgment from pretext alone. Scalia/O’Connor/Kennedy/Thomas would reverse and remand for Rule 52(a) review under the existing persuasion allocation; Stone would additionally set aside the district judgment and apply his new allocation. Only the common appellate vacatur/remand is ordered. No majority orders a new allocation, district-court vacatur or judgment for either party. |

#### Controlling Propositions and Authority

##### Pretext alone does not command judgment, and circumstantial evidence need not be supplemented by a special category of proof

**Controlling proposition:** After an employer produces admissible evidence of nondiscriminatory reasons, a proven prima-facie case and rejection of the offered explanations do not automatically command judgment for the employee. Those circumstances may support a finding of intentional discrimination without a mandatory additional category of direct or independent proof; the Eighth Circuit’s automatic-liability mandate is vacated, while no majority here adopts a replacement persuasion allocation.

**Authority:** Scalia’s Opinion of the Court, this proposition; Stone-Zsela, O’Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Thomas (5 Justices). Direct majority support at the stated level of generality; no Marks aggregation.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that the appellate mandate cannot stand merely because the employer’s reasons were disbelieved. Title VII addresses the prohibited cause of an employment decision, not false explanation as an independent statutory wrong. McDonnell Douglas structures indirect proof, and Burdine requires the employer to produce admissible evidence rather than persuade at the ordinary explanation stage. That structure does not justify eliminating every opportunity to consider whether discrimination actually caused the action. At the same time, Aikens recognizes the practical force of circumstantial proof: a prima-facie showing and rejection of the employer’s material account may establish discrimination without eyewitness testimony or a separate kind of evidence. The Court therefore removes the compulsory-liability direction and remands. Five Justices do not agree on a new allocation after established pretext. The inherited rules remain governing to the extent not displaced by this narrow holding; neither Stone’s persuasion shift nor the dissent’s automatic-result rule gains controlling force. No finding that personal hostility actually motivated these decisions is made.

**Precedent treatment:**

- McDonnell Douglas Corp. v. Green,411 U.S.792: indirect framework retained; no automatic liability holding adopted.
- Texas Department of Community Affairs v. Burdine,450 U.S.248: production stage retained; no five-Justice replacement of its persuasion rule.
- Postal Service Board of Governors v. Aikens,460 U.S.711: actual discrimination and circumstantial evidence remain central.
- Hazen, April 20, 1993: prohibited motive remains distinct from general unfairness; no added evidence category follows.

#### Current-Law Effect

Effective June 25, the automatic-liability mandate is removed and the narrow five-Justice holding governs. Stone’s proposed trigger, shifted burden and extra remedial instructions remain published noncontrolling positions. No majority changes Burdine’s persuasion allocation or establishes a universal Title VII/§1983 equivalence. The appellate court proceeds under existing governing law consistent with the limited mandate; the Court does not decide clear error, retaliation, remaining defenses, relief or applicability of the 1991 Act.

#### Separate Positions Relevant to the Decision

Scalia, joined by O’Connor, Kennedy and Thomas, would retain the employee’s ultimate persuasion burden throughout. Rule 301 and Burdine distinguish evidence production from credibility, so a rejected explanation does not revive a compulsory presumption. They regard Aikens as requiring an actual discrimination finding and would review the district court’s finding for clear error under Rule 52(a), without a pretext-plus demand. Souter, joined by White, Blackmun and Stevens, would affirm. Their reading of Burdine gives operative force to the employer’s obligation to identify the reasons the employee must contest; after all are disproved, the indirect route is complete. They object to allowing a factfinder to search for a different unexplained motive after trial and invoke statutory stare decisis. Stone proposes a distinct middle rule, not joined by another Justice. The employee must prove the applicable prima-facie case and affirmatively establish that every material nondiscriminatory explanation offered was not an actual reason. Inaccuracy alone, peripheral discrepancies, or an actual remaining material reason do not trigger the shift; none prevents an otherwise sufficient discrimination claim. After the trigger, the employer must prove absence of the prohibited cause by a preponderance, using the whole admissible record. A merely possible lawful reason or unsupported personal hostility is insufficient; hostility itself may be racial. No fixed documentary form is required, and the employee receives notice and an opportunity to contest any decisive factual theory. Equipoise after the shift favors the employee on causation, while other elements, defenses and relief retain their own sources. Stone openly departs from Burdine’s perpetual persuasion assignment as a Title VII-specific proof construction; Rule 301 supplies no authority for that change, and Price Waterhouse’s different predicate is illustrative rather than controlling. He would retain valid findings, set aside the Title VII judgment, and permit a focused supplemental presentation only where fairness under the changed allocation requires it, with an equivalent response opportunity. The rule grants no routine retrial, successive explanations, same-decision defense or 1991 Act retroactivity determination. Stone would separately reconsider Long’s §1983 judgment without automatically importing the Title VII allocation. The two four-Justice groups do not join this departure: one insists on continuing employee persuasion, the other on completed indirect proof requiring judgment. Their theories and Stone’s are not logical subsets yielding a common affirmative allocation. No Marks conversion establishes the proposed shift.

#### Mandate, Remedy, and Stage

The appellate liability mandate is vacated and the matter returns to the Eighth Circuit. Existing district findings are not independently erased by this Court; review and any further proceedings follow governing law and the narrow mandate. The previously unaddressed retaliation issue remains for proper consideration below, not resolved by silence. The unappealed §1981 dismissal is unchanged. No Supreme Court docket component remains pending; ordinary mandate procedure applies.

### Hazen Paper Co. v. Biggins, No. 91-1600

Vacatur and remand, 1993-04-20

Argued 1993-01-13; decided 1993-04-20. Reported source: 507 U.S. 604 (1993). First Circuit, 953 F.2d 1405. The case is before the Court on writ of certiorari to that court.

Question: Whether interference with a service-based pension establishes ADEA disparate treatment, and whether Thurston’s willfulness rule governs an individual discharge.

Biggins was 62 and near the ten-year vesting point when discharged. A jury found ADEA liability and willfulness along with separate claims. The district court set aside willfulness; the First Circuit reinstated it and relied heavily on pension interference for age liability. The record also includes age-related remarks, a younger replacement and differing confidentiality requirements. The employer invoked competitive disloyalty. The sufficiency of this remaining evidence requires reconsideration; other claims are not extinguished.

Chief Justice Stone-Zsela and Justices White, Blackmun, Stevens, O’Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter and Thomas participated in argument and decision.

The Court vacates and remands, 9–0. Chief Justice Stone-Zsela and Justices White, Blackmun, Stevens, O’Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter and Thomas support the disposition of ADEA liability and willfulness. Justice O’Connor delivers the Opinion of the Court, Part I on liability, Part II on willfulness and Part III on remand, joined by all eight other Justices. Justice Kennedy concurs, joined by Thomas, emphasizing the unresolved question of disparate impact.

ADEA disparate treatment requires age actually to motivate the employment decision and have a determinative influence; pension interference based only on years of service does not by itself establish that requirement. Liability remains possible when pension status is deliberately used as an age proxy or age and pension status both motivate the decision; age-triggered vesting and disparate impact are not decided.

The holding is adopted in O'Connor’s Opinion of the Court, I by Stone-Zsela, White, Blackmun, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas. It controls as the rule of that 9-Justice majority.

The Court holds that correlation is not the statutory cause. A worker’s years with an employer and that worker’s age are analytically distinct, so avoiding a ten-year service-based pension does not necessarily show action because of age. Aikens and Burdine direct attention to the employer’s actual motivation, which may be established through direct or circumstantial proof. Ingersoll-Rand confirms that pension interference may violate ERISA without thereby establishing an ADEA violation. That distinction creates no pension safe harbor. An employer may deliberately target pension status because it identifies older employees, and age and pension concerns may both play a determinative role. Here the younger replacement, confidentiality demands and comments require reconsideration after removing the mistaken equivalence. The Court neither finds those facts sufficient nor treats every improper explanation as proof of age discrimination. It leaves age-based vesting, disparate impact and the separate non-ADEA claims outside this decision.

The Court applies Aikens, 460 U.S. 711, and Burdine, 450 U.S. 248: actual motivation may be proved circumstantially; no automatic inference from any unlawful motive.

The Court distinguishes Ingersoll-Rand, 498 U.S. 133: ERISA pension interference and age discrimination remain distinct.

The Court preserves McDonnell Douglas, 411 U.S. 792; its legitimate-reason terminology does not make every improper motive an ADEA violation.

An ADEA disparate-treatment violation is willful when the employer knew or recklessly disregarded whether its conduct violated the Act, whether the decision was individual or a formal policy. Once that showing is made, liquidated damages require no additional outrageousness, direct evidence or predominant-age motive; a good-faith, nonreckless mistake about a statutory permission is not willfulness.

The holding is adopted in O'Connor’s Opinion of the Court, II by Stone-Zsela, White, Blackmun, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas. It controls as the rule of that 9-Justice majority.

The Court retains Thurston’s knowledge-or-reckless-disregard standard. Nothing in the term willful changes when age discrimination occurs through a single discharge rather than an announced policy. Richland Shoe confirms that mere negligence or awareness that the statute might apply is insufficient. This preserves the two tiers Congress created without adding requirements found nowhere in the governing standard. An employer can intentionally consider age yet nonrecklessly and in good faith misunderstand a statutory defense or exemption, such as a bona fide occupational qualification; an incorrect understanding may leave ordinary liability without willful liability. Conversely, keeping the discriminatory motive private does not justify a stricter evidentiary barrier than an openly discriminatory policy receives. Direct evidence is unnecessary, outrageousness is not an additional element, and age need not be predominant rather than determinative. Because underlying liability must first be reconsidered, the Court awards or removes no liquidated damages itself.

The Court reaffirms Thurston, 469 U.S. 111 and applied to individual disparate-treatment decisions.

The Court applies Richland Shoe, 486 U.S. 128: negligence and mere awareness of statutory relevance are not willfulness.

Age/tenure distinction clarified and Thurston’s individual-decision application confirmed April 20. No later causation doctrine or disparate-impact rule adopted.

Kennedy, joined by Thomas, concurs while emphasizing that Biggins brought disparate treatment alone. He regards importing Title VII disparate-impact doctrine into the ADEA as presenting substantial unresolved objections and understands the Court to decide none of them. The Court’s express reservation supports his join; his skepticism is not a holding. Stone does not join this additional theory.

**Source Notes.**

[Reported source materials, 507 U.S. 604](https://tile.loc.gov/storage-services/service/ll/usrep/usrep507/usrep507604/usrep507604.pdf); [judgment below](https://static.case.law/f2d/953/html/1405-01.html). These materials support the dates, record and cited legal texts. No decisional language is presented as a verified verbatim quotation. Source cutoff: 1993-04-20; no later follow-on proceeding is reported. The questions are reconstructed issue statements, not quotations of a grant.

The First Circuit must reconsider the sufficiency of the age-discrimination evidence under the distinction between age and years of service. If ADEA liability survives, Thurston governs willfulness. The Court orders no automatic liquidated-damages award or reinstatement; independently valid non-ADEA claims remain.

Supreme Court review complete; the First Circuit reconsiders ADEA evidentiary sufficiency and, if necessary, willfulness. Independent ERISA and state-law relief remains subject to its own judgments. No new damages amount is ordered.

**End of entry: Hazen Paper Co. v. Biggins, Vacatur and remand, 1993-04-20.**

### Simmons v. South Carolina, No. 92-9059

Merits decision, June 17, 1994

#### Chronology and Posture

512 U.S. 154. Argued January 18, 1994; decided June 17, 1994. On writ of certiorari to the Supreme Court of South Carolina, 310 S.C. 439, 427 S.E.2d 175 (1993). The questions concern the right to communicate legal parole ineligibility when the State invokes future dangerousness at capital sentencing, the additional Eighth Amendment instruction claim, and the appropriate sentencing remedy.

South Carolina upheld the murder, first-degree burglary and petit-larceny convictions and their death, consecutive life and thirty-day sentences. The challenged error concerns only selection of death for the murder. The State urged future danger and community protection. The court barred references to parole during voir dire, evidence and argument; Simmons requested an ineligibility instruction and an explanation of life imprisonment. When the jury expressly asked whether life carried the possibility of parole, the court told it not to consider parole and to use the ordinary meaning of life and death.

The noncapital alternative was life without ordinary parole under the repeat-violent-offender rule, not because every South Carolina life sentence excludes parole. Section 24-21-640 bars parole for a second or subsequent violent conviction following separate sentencing for a prior conviction; offenses committed within one day or one continuous course are counted as one. The separately dated attacks, convictions and uncontested departmental testimony establish the operative eligibility premise here. The Court makes no new continuous-course or board-eligibility determination. Clemency and future legislation remain distinct from ordinary parole eligibility.

Chief Justice Alex-Lamar Stone-Zsela and Justices Blackmun, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas and Ginsburg participated at both argument and decision. No case-specific nonparticipation is established.

#### Judgment

The Court reverses the South Carolina Supreme Court judgment insofar as it sustains the murder death sentence and remands, 7–2. Five Justices direct a constitutionally adequate new penalty proceeding if the State continues to seek death. The convictions and separate sentences remain undisturbed.

| Judgment component | Disposition and vote | Supporting Justices | Other positions | Remedy or remand |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Appellate judgment sustaining the murder death sentence | Reverse and remand, 7–2 | Stone-Zsela, Blackmun, Stevens, O'Connor, Kennedy, Souter, Ginsburg | Scalia and Thomas would affirm on no-error grounds. | The existing death-sustaining judgment cannot stand; guilt and the separate sentences remain undisturbed. |
| Express direction for a new penalty proceeding if death is pursued | Adopted by five Justices | Stone-Zsela, Blackmun, Stevens, Souter, Ginsburg | O'Connor and Kennedy reserve further remedy and would leave the ordinary remedial inquiry for further proceedings; Scalia and Thomas would affirm and express no alternative harmlessness position. | The State must provide a constitutionally adequate new penalty proceeding if it continues to seek death; it may instead pursue a lawful noncapital disposition. |

The five-Justice direction controls the remand. O'Connor and Kennedy do not find the error harmless; their general-remand position is distinct from Scalia and Thomas's no-error affirmance. Blackmun accepts the new-hearing direction as minimum case-specific relief while independently seeking noncapital relief.

| Dissent | Scalia | Thomas | Would affirm | Finds no due-process violation and rejects the additional Eighth Amendment instruction ground; no conditional harmlessness vote. |

Parts I and II have separate direct majorities. Part III has two votes. No Marks synthesis supplies any holding or expands any join.

#### Controlling Propositions and Authority

##### Parole ineligibility must be available to answer the State's future-danger case

**Controlling proposition:** When the State places future dangerousness at issue in a capital sentencing proceeding and the only legally available noncapital sentence is life imprisonment without ordinary parole, due process requires an effective opportunity for the defendant to inform the jury of that ineligibility through accurate defense presentation or an adequate instruction. A direction to disregard parole and give life imprisonment its ordinary meaning does not satisfy that requirement on this record; the rule neither requires a particular script nor promises immunity from clemency or future legislation.

**Authority:** Part I of Justice Souter's opinion, joined by Chief Justice Stone-Zsela and Justices Blackmun, Stevens, O'Connor, Kennedy and Ginsburg. Seven Justices directly adopt this Fourteenth Amendment rule and its application; no combination of separate rationales is necessary.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that a defendant must be able to answer the reason the State gives for taking his life. Gardner protects the opportunity to respond to material sentencing information, and Skipper applies that concern when the prosecution relies on future danger. Here the State urged protection against future violence while the court barred the legal fact that a life sentence would carry no ordinary parole. Evidence of adjustment to prison answered a different question and did not communicate that fixed sentencing alternative. Ramos permits truthful release-related information; it does not authorize suppression of the legal answer to the State's own argument. The jury's express parole inquiry made the inadequacy of the ordinary-meaning instruction concrete, without establishing any juror's actual thoughts. The State remains free to prove danger to inmates or guards and to present truthful information concerning commutation or pardon. The Court decides neither cases involving only uncertain release predictions nor the duty to disclose parole information when the State has not placed future danger at issue.

**Precedent treatment:**

- Gardner v. Florida, 430 U.S. 349 (1977): its opportunity-to-answer principle supplies the due-process foundation for responding to material sentencing information.
- Skipper v. South Carolina, 476 U.S. 1 (1986): its future-danger rebuttal principle is applied to a fixed legal sentencing alternative; its independent mitigation rule remains intact.
- California v. Ramos, 463 U.S. 992 (1983): distinguished; permission to give truthful commutation information does not authorize withholding this responsive fact about ordinary parole.
- Graham v. Collins, January 25, 1993, and Johnson v. Texas, June 24, 1993: their effective-mitigation rules remain controlling within their scope; neither is treated as having already decided this separate legal-information question.

##### The error is not harmless on this record and requires a new penalty proceeding if death is pursued

**Controlling proposition:** Under Chapman's direct-review standard, the State cannot establish beyond a reasonable doubt that barring the parole-ineligibility explanation did not contribute to this jury's selection of death, given the State's future-danger argument, the complete exclusion of the legal answer and the jury's express parole inquiry. The death-sustaining judgment is reversed and the case remanded for a constitutionally adequate new penalty proceeding if the State continues to seek death; a lawful noncapital disposition remains available, and the convictions and separate sentences are undisturbed.

**Authority:** Part II of Justice Souter's opinion, joined by Chief Justice Stone-Zsela and Justices Blackmun, Stevens and Ginsburg. Five Justices directly adopt this record-specific harmlessness determination and mandatory further-hearing direction. O'Connor and Kennedy join reversal but reserve the further remedial determination.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that this death selection cannot be retained under ordinary constitutional harmless-error review. Chapman requires the State to establish beyond a reasonable doubt that the error did not contribute to the decision. The State made future danger a reason to choose death, the defense could not disclose the actual legal alternative, and the jury itself sought the missing information. The response neither supplied that information nor permitted the defense to answer. The violent conduct and the burglary aggravator establish important sentencing facts, but eligibility for death does not answer whether the excluded rebuttal affected its selection. The Court does not infer actual juror reasoning from the survey or the interval before the verdict. It evaluates the whole reported proceeding and cannot exclude the omission's effect under the governing burden. This conclusion does not classify every parole-information error as structural or foreclose harmlessness on a materially different record. Romano's separate responsibility problem remains distinct; its unresolved further-remedy question is not decided retrospectively here.

**Precedent treatment:**

- Chapman v. California, 386 U.S. 18 (1967): applied; the State must establish harmlessness beyond a reasonable doubt, and fails on this record.
- Romano v. Oklahoma, June 13, 1994: distinguished as an affirmative disclosure of another jury's death decision; this decision neither supplies a missing majority for its further-remedy positions nor changes that case's mandate.
- Johnson v. Texas, June 24, 1993: preserves the distinction between correcting a capital sentencing defect and disturbing guilt; no automatic noncapital sentence follows.

**Limits and questions not reached:** The Court adopts no independently sufficient Eighth Amendment instruction rule. It does not determine an entitlement to parole information where the State has not raised future danger, settle a disputed parole-eligibility question, require a fixed form of words, guarantee against clemency or future legislation, or announce automatic reversal for every omission. The State may pursue a lawful noncapital disposition instead of another capital hearing. No guilt retrial, acquittal, release, invalidation of the separate sentences or general prohibition of capital punishment is ordered.

#### Precedent Treatment and Current-Law Effect

The holding-specific treatment above controls. No precedent is overruled. Gardner and Skipper supply the narrow due-process response principle; Ramos retains its distinct truthful-information rule. Graham and Johnson's effective-mitigation holdings remain intact. Chapman governs the record-specific remedial determination. Romano's different responsibility holding and unresolved further-remedy issue remain unchanged.

Effective June 17, 1994, the Fourteenth Amendment requires an effective opportunity to explain legal parole ineligibility when the State raises future dangerousness and life without ordinary parole is the only noncapital alternative. Accurate defense presentation or an adequate instruction can satisfy the common rule. On this record, the ordinary-meaning response and complete bar on explanation violate that rule, and the State cannot establish harmlessness beyond a reasonable doubt. A new penalty proceeding is required if death is pursued.

This adds a bounded legal-information rule to the earlier mitigation and responsibility protections without replacing them. Chapman remains a record-specific inquiry; no structural-error classification or automatic-reversal rule is created. Souter and Stevens's broader Eighth Amendment instruction principle, Stone-Zsela's additional judicial-answer formulation and Blackmun's general noncapital position are not holdings of the Court. O'Connor and Kennedy's view of cases without a State-raised future-danger issue likewise does not settle that reserved question. Cavanaugh's separate parole-reconsideration rule does not establish eligibility for prisoners barred by the repeat-offender provision.

#### Separate Positions Relevant to the Decision

Part III of Justice Souter's opinion, joined by Justice Stevens, supplies an independent Eighth Amendment ground. A capital defendant who requests an explanation of a legal term describing the sentencing alternatives is entitled to a judicial explanation when there is a reasonable likelihood a juror will misunderstand that term. They rely on Woodson's heightened reliability, Gregg's guided sentencing, Beck's concern with an intelligible capital choice and Boyde's reasonable-likelihood inquiry, together with Graham, Johnson v. Texas and Romano's distinct protections for an effective, responsible choice. The fixed legal meaning of life imprisonment and the jury's direct inquiry satisfy that rule here. This ground does not depend on the State's arguing future danger. Counsel's advocacy does not generally carry the authority of a judicial statement of sentencing law, so permission for defense argument alone does not satisfy their separate instruction principle. The trigger is a request plus reasonable likelihood of misunderstanding, not a duty to catalogue every possible release contingency at every capital trial. The ground has two votes and is not controlling.

Chief Justice Stone-Zsela joins the Court's due-process and remedy holdings and separately explains the consequence of an express jury question. In the future-danger setting presented here, the trial court should establish the actual state-law alternative and ensure an accurate explanation, either by an accepted defense presentation or an instruction. Once the jury directly asks about that legal alternative, it warrants an authoritative answer from the court. An ordinary-meaning direction is inadequate when ordinary understanding may include parole. No prescribed script or promise against commutation or future legal change follows. The State may still establish danger inside prison. This additional judicial-answer requirement is his own due-process formulation; he does not join Souter and Stevens's broader Eighth Amendment rule for cases without a State-raised future-danger issue. His join in the five-Justice remedy rests on this record's failure of constitutional harmlessness, not automatic reversal for every omission.

Justice Blackmun joins the case-specific holdings and accepts a new penalty hearing as minimum relief if death is pursued. He separately retains his objection that capital punishment as presently administered fails to reconcile consistent selection, individualized fairness and reliable punishment. Gregg and Jurek's guided-sentencing framework and Lockett and Eddings's individualized consideration seek those ends; his joins in Graham and Johnson v. Texas recognize enforceable mitigation safeguards, but he regards those safeguards as insufficient to resolve the broader selection problem. He would require lawful noncapital relief, without selecting a particular sentence or ordering release. He does not claim that removing this error resolves his independent objection or make a new finding about Simmons's innocence or discriminatory treatment. The broader position is his alone and does not displace the Court's limited rule or authorize noncapital relief in every case.

Justice O'Connor, joined by Justice Kennedy, joins Part I and the judgment reversing and remanding. Their due-process rule requires a State-raised future-danger issue and life without parole as the only noncapital alternative. Accurate defense explanation or adequate instruction can satisfy it; the Constitution does not require a particular natural-life formula denying the possibility of clemency. Outside that State-raised-danger setting, their analysis would leave the State the choice whether to inform the jury about parole, even when the alternative is life without parole. They do not join the independent Eighth Amendment instruction rule. They also decline Part II's conclusive harmlessness ruling and additional new-hearing command, leaving the ordinary constitutional remedial issue for appropriate further proceedings. They neither find this error harmless nor characterize it as structural, mandate automatic life imprisonment or prescribe a sequence for that further adjudication.

Justice Scalia, joined by Justice Thomas, would affirm. The prosecutor did not expressly assert that Simmons could be paroled, the defense presented prison-adaptation evidence, and the trial court directed jurors away from release speculation. Gardner's undisclosed adverse information and Skipper's excluded prison-conduct evidence therefore do not, in their view, establish fundamental unfairness here. Ramos supports substantial state authority over release-related information. They reject the proposed Eighth Amendment extension because legal parole information is distinct from the character, record and offense evidence protected by individualized sentencing. They distinguish Romano's responsibility holding concerning another jury's death judgment rather than treating it as a general evidentiary rule. They accept the actual statutory ineligibility; their disagreement concerns the federal disclosure duty. A materially misleading State assertion about release could present a different due-process issue. Their no-error conclusion supplies no alternative conclusion about harmlessness or the proper remedy if error were found.

### Allridge v. Scott, Fifth Circuit No. 93-9137

Merits decision, January 15, 1996

Judgment below: 41 F.3d 213 (1994). On review under 28 U.S.C. §1254(1) of the Fifth Circuit's December 15, 1994 judgment affirming denial of federal habeas relief. Allridge remains alive in custody. The submitted questions concern parole information and effective use of mitigation under Texas's capital special issues. His father offered lay testimony about prior prison abuse and alleged mental illness; the defense requested a supplemental mitigation instruction. Unintroduced medical evidence and a separate asserted deterrent effect of the special issues are distinct from that admitted proof.

#### Opinion Topology

#### Controlling Propositions and Authority

##### Ordinary parole availability defeats the asserted release-prediction entitlement

**Operative rule:** Simmons's right to explain legal parole ineligibility does not require a favorable prediction about eventual release when the noncapital alternative permits ordinary parole. The rejection of that asserted entitlement is affirmed; this conclusion neither categorically bars truthful parole evidence nor authorizes a misleading description of the lawful sentencing alternative.

**Authority:** The unanimous coalition joining Part I-A of Souter's opinion, identified above, adopts this eligibility-versus-prediction ground.

**Controlling explanation:** Simmons v. South Carolina protects an effective opportunity to tell the jury that the only lawful noncapital sentence excludes ordinary parole when the State raises future dangerousness. That legal fact answers the State's sentencing case; speculative clemency does not change it. Here ordinary parole was legally available. A prediction that release is improbable therefore cannot be substituted for Simmons's absent predicate. The Court does not adopt the lower court's suggestion that danger to inmates universally defeats Simmons. The claim fails because of ordinary parole availability, without reaching harmlessness.

##### The proposed forecast-instruction extension is independently unavailable under Teague

**Operative rule:** Federal habeas relief on a proposed constitutional entitlement to a favorable parole-improbability instruction is unavailable here because no basis establishes that this different rule was dictated when Allridge's conviction became final before Penry in 1989, or that either then-existing Teague exception applies: rules excluding conduct, classes of persons, or punishments from the State's substantive power, or watershed procedures essential to accuracy and fundamental fairness. This is an independent ground for rejecting the proposed extension, not a new rule governing accurate information about a fixed statutory minimum.

**Authority:** The eight-Justice coalition joining Part I-B of Souter's opinion, identified above, directly adopts this alternative ground. Stone-Zsela does not join this additional rationale.

**Controlling explanation:** Teague requires claim-specific identification of law dictated at finality. Simmons's separate legal-ineligibility premise cannot establish a broader forecast right where ordinary parole remains available. The record supplies no prefinality requirement for that proposed prediction instruction. As Gilmore v. Taylor and Caspari v. Bohlen explain, the then-existing exceptions concern substantive limits on the State's power to punish and watershed procedures essential to accuracy and fundamental fairness. The requested forecast instruction regulates the jury's information; it does not remove Allridge's conduct, a class to which he belongs, or his punishment from the State's substantive power. Nothing establishes it as a watershed accuracy-and-fairness procedure; asserted usefulness in answering future-danger evidence does not itself meet that demanding category. Neither exception supplies a route to this requested extension. A properly presented claim about a fixed legal minimum would require its own preservation and retroactivity inquiry; this decision does not resolve such a claim.

##### Admitted mitigation must be assessed by its possible independent culpability significance

**Operative rule:** A court may not reject a preserved claim for effective consideration of admitted capital mitigation merely because the evidence fails to establish a uniquely severe permanent disability to which the crime is conclusively attributable. When the actual evidence supports material mitigation of culpability independent of deliberateness or future danger, the court must determine whether, considering the charge, evidence, and trial as a whole, there is a reasonable likelihood that jurors could not give it effect while answering the special issues truthfully; mere possibility is insufficient.

**Authority:** The unanimous coalition joining Part II of Souter's opinion, identified above, adopts this correction of the reviewing rule and bounded remand, without finding an actual instructional violation.

**Controlling explanation:** Graham v. Collins requires an effective sentencing route for record-supported mitigation with independent culpability significance and rejects a uniquely severe permanent-handicap restriction. Its background holding does not require that the evidence lack all relevance to future danger. Johnson v. Texas applies the whole-charge reasonable-likelihood inquiry: admission and partial future-danger relevance do not suffice if truthful special-issue answers prevent the supported mitigating use. Those rules preserve Jurek's conditional approval of the Texas scheme while enforcing Lockett and Eddings's requirement that relevant mitigation receive lawful effect.

The father testified about alleged mental illness and prior prison abuse. The lower court rejected the claim because his nonexpert account failed to establish the crime-attribution and severe-disability linkage. That categorical requirement is no longer a lawful reviewing premise. His lack of diagnostic expertise limits what the testimony proves; it does not erase admitted lay evidence or establish that no abuse occurred.

The competing contention—that the testimony may still be weak, unsupported, or adequately usable under the actual charge—remains for proper review. The Court has not found psychiatric illness, credibility, causation, material independent mitigating force, or the absence of an effective sentencing route. The jury need not assign favorable weight. No categorical additional special issue, facial invalidity, or new general mitigation category follows. Because the stated lower ground is legally insufficient, the rejection of this admitted-evidence claim is vacated and remanded.

##### Unoffered medical evidence supplies no independent instruction entitlement

**Operative rule:** A defendant cannot obtain a mitigation instruction on the assumed content of medical evidence never presented merely because presenting it might also support future dangerousness. The independent instruction claim based on that unoffered evidence fails, while a supported challenge to an actual unconstitutional exclusion or legal impediment remains distinct and is not decided here.

**Authority:** The eight-Justice coalition joining Part III of Souter's opinion, identified above, adopts the rationale; Stone-Zsela agrees with rejection of this component in the judgment only and adds no ground.

**Controlling explanation:** Delo v. Lashley permits a requirement of some evidence supporting a requested mitigating circumstance while protecting meaningful use of mitigation actually offered. Evidence carrying both mitigating and aggravating implications can create a difficult strategic choice. That concern alone does not establish the missing diagnosis, its content, admissibility, or mitigating significance, and does not require jurors to act on assumed proof. The separate admitted-evidence inquiry continues under Part II. This ruling does not compel a defendant to present evidence formally excluded by an unconstitutional rule, foreclose a supported challenge to an actual legal barrier, or adjudicate a new ineffective-assistance claim.

##### Correcting the reviewing standard does not itself require a sentencing writ

**Operative rule:** The admitted-evidence mitigation component returns for review of the actual evidence, charge, prefinality law, and any properly available defenses and error-effect inquiry; vacatur alone establishes neither an available violation nor entitlement to relief. If a defect ultimately requires correction, relief concerns punishment through adequate capital resentencing or a lawful noncapital sentence, leaving guilt untouched; no present writ, hearing, release, stay, deadline, or execution authorization issues.

**Authority:** The unanimous coalition joining Part IV of Souter's opinion, identified above, adopts the bounded remand and reservations.

**Controlling explanation:** Graham's prefinality analysis bars treating its established functional rule as newly created merely because another defendant invokes it. A materially new extension nevertheless requires its own Teague inquiry. The documented instruction request proves that a request was made, not exhaustion or preservation of every reformulated theory. Applicable factual presumptions and genuinely available exhaustion, default, and other habeas defenses remain; no forfeited defense is revived and no settled issue reopened.

Tuggle corrects a discrete legally insufficient review ground without resolving the underlying violation or issuing a writ. That distinction governs this remand too. Brecht governs preserved Doyle error and O'Neal preserved personal-intent instructional error. Neither already selects a universal collateral harmlessness rule for capital mitigation. The Court reserves whether a separate error-effect inquiry is required here, its applicable standard, the actual effect, and ultimate relief. The complete charge, relevant testimony, claim-specific procedural rulings, and any properly preserved error-effect submissions are needed before further relief. Unreviewed Brady and lesser-offense claims are untouched.

#### Precedent Treatment

- **Simmons v. South Carolina, 512 U.S. 154:** Distinguished because ordinary parole was legally available; its legal-ineligibility trigger remains, without a new prison-danger exception.
- **Teague v. Lane, 489 U.S. 288:** Applied separately to the proposed forecast extension. Its claim-specific prefinality inquiry and exceptions for substantive limits on punishment and watershed accuracy-and-fairness procedures remain; neither exception is established here.
- **Gilmore v. Taylor, 508 U.S. 333, and Caspari v. Bohlen, 510 U.S. 383:** Their application of the two then-existing Teague exceptions is followed; an important procedural protection does not by that importance alone satisfy the demanding watershed category.
- **Graham v. Collins, 506 U.S. 461:** Applied to reject the severe-permanent-disability and exclusive-future-danger restrictions. Its independent background and youth holdings remain; its fractured positive-character component supplies no general rule.
- **Johnson v. Texas, 509 U.S. 350, and Boyde v. California, 494 U.S. 370:** Applied through the complete-charge reasonable-likelihood standard, preserving the distinction between admission and effective mitigating use.
- **Jurek v. Texas, 428 U.S. 262:** Its conditional approval survives; Texas's special issues are not facially invalidated.
- **Lockett v. Ohio, 438 U.S. 586, and Eddings v. Oklahoma, 455 U.S. 104:** Their effective-mitigation requirement remains, without compelled favorable weight.
- **Delo v. Lashley, 507 U.S. 272:** Its supporting-evidence requirement is applied to the unoffered medical material; it neither excludes admitted lay mitigation nor bars a supported unconstitutional-exclusion challenge. It creates no expert-only rule, adverse inference from silence, or reduction of the State's aggravation burden.
- **Tuggle v. Netherland, October 30, 1995:** Its distinction between correction of an insufficient review ground and actual relief governs this remand; its separate Ake subject is not transferred.
- **Brecht v. Abrahamson, April 21, 1993, and O'Neal v. McAninch, February 21, 1995:** Their category-specific Chapman rules remain, without a controlling extension to capital mitigation.
- **Chapman v. California, 386 U.S. 18:** No new collateral application is adopted by the Court; the Chief Justice proposes one separately.

#### Law After Decision

Ordinary parole availability does not become legal ineligibility through a prediction of unlikely release. Admitted mitigation must receive the functional inquiry required by Graham and Johnson; an expert diagnosis or uniquely severe disability is not a categorical entrance requirement. Unoffered medical material establishes no independent instruction entitlement merely because its introduction would present strategic risks. The actual instructional violation, available habeas gates, any error-effect standard and finding, and relief remain unresolved. The proposed Chapman extension has no controlling force.

#### Separate Positions Relevant to the Decision

**Stone-Zsela, concurring in part and in the judgment.** The Chief Justice joins the parole-prediction holding, the admitted-mitigation ruling, and the bounded remand. A jury may not be told that a parole-eligible sentence legally excludes release. A fixed statutory minimum, if properly presented as a distinct claim, is a legal fact rather than a favorable prediction; that distinction establishes neither preservation nor retroactive entitlement here. He does not join the Court's independent Teague rationale or its additional rationale concerning unoffered medical evidence.

If a demonstrated, preserved denial of effective capital mitigation requires a separate harmless-error inquiry, he would extend the rationale of Brecht and O'Neal and require the State to establish beyond a reasonable doubt on the whole relevant record that the denial did not contribute to the death sentence. This is a proposed extension to a distinct category; those decisions do not already decide it. He does not find a violation, harmlessness, or a present right to a writ. Correction, if required, would be confined to punishment through adequate capital resentencing or a lawful noncapital sentence. The unintroduced medical evidence and separate chilling contention require their own lawful basis. No other Justice joins this proposal or opinion.

### O'Dell v. Netherland, No. 96-6867

Merits decision, June 19, 1997

#### Chronology and Posture

O'Dell v. Netherland, No. 96-6867. Argued March 18, 1997; decided June 19, 1997. On writ of certiorari to the Fourth Circuit, which sitting en banc on September 5, 1996 reversed the District Court's Simmons ruling under Teague, 95 F.3d 1214. O'Dell's judgment became final on October 3, 1988, after the Virginia Supreme Court's January 15, 1988 judgment. The granted question is whether Simmons's parole-ineligibility protection is available on habeas review of that final capital judgment.

Virginia argued future dangerousness, and the trial court refused the requested parole-ineligibility instruction and an attempted inquiry whether life meant life without parole. The jury found both future dangerousness and vileness and selected death. It also heard O'Dell describe sixteen years on an earlier parole violation and state that, at his age, he would never get out. The District Court's sentencing relief was expressly conditioned on proof of actual legal parole ineligibility; that predicate was not conclusively established by the order.

All nine Justices participated.

#### Judgment

|---|---|---|---|---|
| Teague bar and adverse disposition of the parole-information claim | Bar rejected and adverse disposition set aside, 6–3 | Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Souter, Ginsburg, Breyer | Scalia, Kennedy and Thomas would affirm under Teague | Retroactivity cannot again defeat the claim within the holdings' scope |
| Formal mandate and remaining inquiry | Vacate and remand on the five-Justice mandate; 5–4 on that exact disposition | Stevens, O'Connor, Souter, Ginsburg, Breyer | Stone-Zsela would reverse and restore the District Court's conditional sentencing-relief framework; Scalia, Kennedy and Thomas would affirm | Determine actual parole ineligibility, effective opportunity, any necessary claim-specific error consequence and genuinely applicable unresolved habeas conditions before an operative writ |

The five-Justice vacatur does not automatically reinstate the District Court's sentencing order. Stone-Zsela supports setting aside the Teague judgment and remanding but seeks the distinct conditional-restoration direction. No Justice votes for an unconditional present writ. O'Dell's conviction and unrelated claims remain undisturbed; no release, stay or execution action is entered.

O'Connor does not join the watershed alternative. Stone-Zsela does not join the old-rule holding or the exact formal mandate. The two retroactivity grounds are independently sufficient holdings; they are not a synthesis of incompatible rationales.

#### Holdings

##### I. The opportunity to answer this future-danger case was dictated at finality

**Holding and operative rule:** When the State relies on future dangerousness to seek death and the only lawful noncapital sentence actually excludes ordinary parole, the defendant must have an effective opportunity to give the jury accurate information about that legal ineligibility. Gardner and Skipper dictated that limited protection before O'Dell's October 3, 1988 finality; Teague therefore does not bar the claim, although eligibility, violation and relief still require their own predicates.

**Authority:** Part I of Stevens's opinion, directly adopted by Stevens, O'Connor, Souter, Ginsburg and Breyer. These five adopt the old-rule holding; Stone-Zsela does not join it.

**Controlling explanation:** Gardner protects the opportunity to deny or explain material information supporting a death sentence. Skipper expressly protects a response to the State's future-danger argument, independently of its additional mitigation rationale. Fixed legal ineligibility answers that argument by identifying the actual alternative to death. This concrete protected function, not a general appeal to fairness, supplies the dictated application.

The strongest objection distinguishes historical evidence about the defendant from legal information about postsentencing confinement. The distinction does not justify withholding the accurate response when the State makes future danger material. Ramos permits truthful information about commutation; it does not authorize exclusion of the ordinary-parole answer. Caldwell forbids misleading diminution of the sentencing jury's responsibility, not accurate explanation of its lawful choices.

Simmons states the present protection and its limits: accurate defense presentation or an adequate instruction may suffice, no fixed script or immunity from clemency is required, and the State may prove danger within prison. Graham, Gray and Lambrix require functional comparison without inserting their later decisions into the law existing in 1988. Gilmore and Caspari still permit genuinely new procedural extensions after that comparison. No rule that every capital protection is old, or that every defendant is entitled to release predictions, follows.

##### II. Independently, the confined protection satisfies the watershed exception if treated as new

**Independent alternative holding and operative rule:** Even if the parole-information protection were classified as new, Teague's watershed exception permits its application where future dangerousness is an operative basis for choosing death, the only lawful noncapital alternative excludes ordinary parole, and the defendant is denied an effective opportunity to correct the materially misleading release premise through accurate defense presentation or an adequate instruction. The rule establishes retroactive availability in that confined category, not that O'Dell has proved those predicates or is presently entitled to sentencing relief.

**Authority:** Part II of Stevens's opinion, directly adopted by Stone-Zsela, Stevens, Souter, Ginsburg and Breyer. Five adopt this independently sufficient alternative. The four Associates also join Part I; Stone-Zsela actually regards the rule as new. O'Connor takes no position on this alternative.

**Controlling explanation:** Teague's exception, as applied in Sawyer, Gilmore and Caspari, requires a basic safeguard of accuracy and fundamental fairness, not merely an important procedural improvement. The confined conjunction here meets that demanding requirement. Death is sought on a prediction of future danger while the defendant lacks an effective means to explain the legal confinement alternative that directly answers the release premise. That loss corrupts the adversarial choice between the two lawful sentences. Gideon supplies the model of a bedrock safeguard for a fair adversarial proceeding; Crane and Gardner identify the basic importance of an effective chance to answer the case against the defendant. The analogy concerns that function, not equivalence between every evidentiary rule and the right to counsel.

The objection that this is only a helpful instruction understates the holding. A refused instruction alone is insufficient: an accurate alternative defense presentation may satisfy Simmons. Nor does the protection's limited incidence diminish its basic role where the conjunction exists. The exception covers no general class of capital-sentencing rules, no case without a future-danger ground, no legally available ordinary parole or practical release prediction, and no case with an effective corrective opportunity. It does not make the error structural, displace a separate effect inquiry, or abolish the substantive/new-procedure distinction.

##### III. Retroactivity does not by itself establish a present sentencing writ

**Holding and operative rule:** The Fourth Circuit's Teague disposition must be vacated with that bar unavailable under the preceding holdings, but an operative sentencing writ requires actual legal parole ineligibility, denial of Simmons's effective opportunity, the applicable error consequence, and resolution of genuinely applicable unresolved habeas conditions. The District Court's conditional order is not automatically reinstated, and the conviction and unrelated claims remain undisturbed.

**Authority:** Part III of Stevens's opinion, directly adopted by Stevens, O'Connor, Souter, Ginsburg and Breyer. This five-Justice coalition supplies the formal vacatur and bounded-remand mandate; Stone-Zsela seeks the different conditional-restoration direction stated separately.

**Controlling explanation:** Simmons permits accurate defense presentation as well as an adequate instruction. The refused instruction therefore does not establish every element of violation. O'Dell's remarks about sixteen years on a prior parole violation and never leaving prison differ from accurate legal information about this conviction's sole alternative, but remain part of the complete presentation. The verdict's vileness finding and evidence of an earlier prison murder do not automatically cure an influence on sentence selection. Prison dangerousness remains a lawful State argument, without creating a categorical exception to the parole-information protection.

Brecht applies Chapman to preserved Doyle error, and O'Neal extends that treatment to its identified personal-intent claim. Gray found the capital preparation injury harmful under both competing approaches and selected no universal collateral standard. The necessary standard here must be resolved under those holdings, or a supported outcome reached under both; no general prisoner-prejudice rule is assumed. The Court makes no present harm finding.

Gray also preserves genuinely applicable unadjudicated statutory conditions, each with its own temporal and substantive predicates; the Court invents no condition, waiver or satisfaction. If an available violation ultimately warrants relief, Virginia may provide a constitutionally adequate new penalty proceeding or a lawful noncapital disposition. No new factual hearing, reinstated or expired deadline, release or execution order follows from this remand.

- Skipper v. South Carolina: applied to accurate rebuttal of the State's future-danger case; its independent mitigation holding remains intact.
- California v. Ramos: distinguished; truthful commutation information and state latitude do not authorize withholding the fixed ordinary-parole response.

#### Law After Decision

The confined Simmons opportunity-to-answer claim is available against the Teague defense through two independently majority-supported grounds: it was dictated at finality, and, even if new, it satisfies the watershed exception on Part II's exact conjunction. The alternative does not label Simmons new as Court law, extend watershed treatment to sentencing procedures generally or make relief automatic. #### Separate Writings

**Chief Justice Stone-Zsela, concurring in part and dissenting in part.** He joins the confined watershed holding in Part II and the rejection of the Teague bar, but concludes that Simmons was not dictated in 1988 and therefore does not join Part I. The new procedure qualifies, in his view, only when the State's future-danger case, actual legal parole ineligibility and loss of an effective means to correct the release premise combine to distort the sentencing choice. He would reverse and restore the District Court's conditional sentencing-relief framework. Restoration would still require proof of legal ineligibility, denial of an effective opportunity through accurate presentation or an adequate instruction, and the applicable error analysis. Neither instructional refusal alone nor the retroactivity holding proves entitlement. He would leave guilt undisturbed and permit Virginia a compliant new penalty proceeding or lawful noncapital sentence if the conditions warrant relief.

**Justice Thomas, joined by Justices Scalia and Kennedy, dissenting.** They would affirm under Teague. Gardner concerned undisclosed adverse information and Skipper excluded historical evidence; neither compelled in 1988 a legal-information obligation concerning the postsentencing regime. Ramos's state latitude and Caldwell's distinct responsibility rule support that substantive distinction. The rule may validly govern cases where it is available without having been dictated at finality. It regulates selection of an otherwise authorized penalty, so it is not substantive; its defined rebuttal protection also lacks the systemic character required for a watershed procedure. The dissenters preserve both exceptions but find neither satisfied. They decide no actual parole status, violation, harm, statutory condition or prison-danger exception.
