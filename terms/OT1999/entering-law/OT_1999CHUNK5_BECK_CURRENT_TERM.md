## Holdings

**Common authority:** Thomas's opinion is joined in full by all eight other Justices. Each rule, application and reservation below has direct unanimous support.

**Civil RICO requires a direct relation, and these ordinary expenditures are too remote.** Section 1964(c) requires factual causation and proximate cause, including a direct relation between the asserted business-or-property injury and the alleged violation. A fund's alleged medical payments arising through participants' smoking, illness and treatment do not satisfy that requirement on this pleaded chain, even when defendants allegedly intended the fund to bear the cost.

**Controlling explanation:** Holmes supplies a contextual inquiry informed by attribution, overlapping recovery and the availability of directly injured plaintiffs; those considerations are not three independent elements. Here the fund suffers a real monetary loss, but only after the participant's conduct and bodily injury generate treatment and payment. Alleged targeting and foreseeable costs do not eliminate those links. Attribution depends on separating loss produced by deception from illness and costs that would have occurred despite accurate information or from other causes. Limited duplication favors the funds, and participants cannot recover bodily-injury damages under RICO, but neither fact makes this expenditure chain direct. McCready involved a reimbursement denial that itself implemented the scheme, rather than a payment arising through another person's illness. The Court accepts the allegations and does not demand a damages model at the pleading stage. Statistical methods can quantify loss; they cannot supply an absent direct relation.

**The cessation-program theory independently fails on its pleaded chain.** Describing concealment as interference with the funds' cost-control decisions does not make the claimed medical payments direct when loss still depends on different fund action, changed participant behavior, avoided illness and avoided treatment.

**Controlling explanation:** This theory receives separate consideration because the funds identify their own decisionmaking. The alleged loss nevertheless remains downstream healthcare expenditure; no independently pleaded property loss occurs through the fund decision without the intervening illness chain. The Court finds no particular program ineffective and imposes no evidentiary damages burden now. A different direct fraud upon a fund, and whether any amendment could state one, are not presented.

**The funds' property and legal capacity remain distinct from participants' bodily injuries.** Benefit payments reduce the funds' own property, but their legal form and payment duty neither transfer participants' bodily injuries nor create an exemption from RICO's causal requirements.

**Controlling explanation:** ERISA and the trusts' Taft-Hartley form identify their benefit function and capacity; they establish neither a substantive recovery right against these manufacturers nor a categorical inability to protect their own property. The District Court's no-cognizable-property-injury rationale is not adopted. Entity status does not alter the same directness rule applied to the actual asserted interest.

**The Clayton Act claim is independently too remote.** Under AGC's antitrust inquiry, these healthcare expenditures following participants' purchases, smoking and illness are not sufficiently direct injuries from the alleged suppression of safer tobacco products.

**Controlling explanation:** The funds are not consumers or competitors in the cigarette market, but those labels are not an exclusive test. Their payments were consequences of illness, not the mechanism implementing the alleged market exclusion as reimbursement denial was in McCready. This directness defect suffices without defining every antitrust-injury boundary. RICO receives no imported antitrust-injury, special racketeering-injury or purchaser-seller requirement.

**Limits:** No exceptionless third-party-payer bar, categorical prohibition of aggregate proof, state-law disposition or ruling on an unpresented amendment is adopted.

## Precedent Treatment

Holmes v. Securities Investor Protection Corp. is applied without changing its contextual direct-relation requirement or turning its explanatory considerations into separate elements. Associated General Contractors supplies the distinct Clayton Act remoteness inquiry; its antitrust-injury requirement is not imported into RICO. Blue Shield v. McCready is distinguished because its reimbursement denial implemented the scheme itself. Sedima's rejection of special racketeering injury and prior-conviction requirements remains intact; statutory breadth does not dispense with causation. No general entity-property or third-party-injury restriction is added.

## Law After Decision

The particular federal expenditure and cessation theories remain dismissed for remoteness. Real property loss, capacity to sue and intentional targeting do not alone establish statutory directness. Different directly caused injuries remain governed by their own allegations and sources, and RICO and Clayton Act requirements remain separate.

## Event

Rotella v. Wood, No. 98-896. Merits decision; February 23, 2000. Argued November 3, 1999. On writ of certiorari to the United States Court of Appeals for the Fifth Circuit, 147 F.3d 438, affirming summary judgment on limitations grounds. ## Holdings

### A complete claim and known injury do not await discovery of the pattern

**Holding and operative rule:** When a civil RICO cause of action is complete and the plaintiff has discovered the injury, the four-year limitation period does not await actual or reasonable discovery of the racketeering pattern. Later recognition of the pattern cannot revive an otherwise stale claim for that injury; this decision does not choose between injury-occurrence and injury-discovery accrual generally or decide the case in which the injury precedes completion of the pattern.

**Authority:** Justice Souter's opinion of the Court, joined in full by all eight other Justices, supplies nine direct votes for the rule, its application and the preserved limits.

**Controlling explanation:** Agency Holding supplies the four-year period and the Clayton Act analogy. Klehr rejects revival of an old actionable injury through later predicates but expressly left the present pattern-discovery question open. Delaying the period until every element is discovered would allow a known, completed injury to remain actionable far beyond the period, weakening the incentive to investigate and the protection against stale claims. The statutory ten-year interval between predicate acts defines a possible pattern; it is not a ten-year civil filing period.

Rotella conceded that he knew his injury by 1986 and did not dispute that all claim elements were complete then. His alleged 1994 discovery of the pattern does not make the 1997 suit timely. He loses whether the period begins with the injury or its discovery, so the Court need not select between those general triggers. An injury preceding the existence of a completed pattern presents a different question. Difficulty uncovering a concealed scheme or satisfying pleading particularity does not itself postpone accrual until discovery of every element. The applicable pleading rules and separate diligent-concealment doctrine retain their work; the Court neither relaxes particularity nor authorizes baseless pleading.

### Distinct new injury, unprovable damages and diligent-concealment rules survive

**Holding and operative rule:** Rejection of pattern-discovery accrual does not displace Klehr's separate rules for a later wrongful act directly causing a genuinely new injury, damages genuinely too speculative or incapable of proof earlier, or concealment preventing timely discovery despite reasonable diligence. None of those routes is established merely by later recognition of the old injury's racketeering pattern.

**Authority:** The same unanimous opinion preserves these operative limitations while affirming the limitations judgment; it makes no independent award under any reserved route.

**Controlling explanation:** Klehr permits recovery for a genuinely new injury on its own causal predicate without reopening older damages; common product, defendant, damage type or pattern does not conclusively make every injury old. Zenith separately protects damages genuinely incapable of proof earlier, not continued calculable loss, inadequate investigation or a later calculation of old harm. Klehr also requires a plaintiff invoking fraudulent-concealment tolling to show that concealment prevented timely discovery despite reasonable diligence. Misleading conduct alone is insufficient if reasonable inquiry would reveal the claim, but extraordinary investigation that defeats otherwise successful concealment is not required. Those qualifications remain distinct from the basic accrual trigger. The present record establishes no independent new injury, previously unprovable damages or tolling entitlement. Preserving these doctrines does not decide the facts needed for any such route or make a separate finding about Rotella's diligence. No universal rule for every equitable-estoppel doctrine follows.

## Precedent Treatment

- Agency Holding Corp. v. Malley-Duff & Associates: applied for the four-year civil RICO period and Clayton Act analogy; it does not itself decide the universal accrual trigger.
- Klehr v. A. O. Smith Corp.: applied to prevent stale-injury revival and preserve the distinct new-injury, unprovable-damage and diligent-concealment rules; today's decision answers the previously reserved pattern-discovery question only on a completed claim and known injury.
- Zenith Radio Corp. v. Hazeltine Research: its protection for genuinely unprovable damages survives; delayed investigation or later computation of old harm is distinguished.
- Hanover Shoe v. United Shoe Machinery: its new-injurious-transaction principle remains, without revival of stale damages.

## Law After Decision

Civil RICO limitations for a completed claim and known injury cannot be delayed until discovery of the pattern. The general choice between occurrence and injury discovery, and the different problem of an injury preceding completion of the pattern, remain open. Klehr's separate recovery and concealment rules retain their stated predicates; none supplies automatic revival of the old known injury.
