#### Hazen Paper Co. v. Biggins, 507 U.S. 604 (1993)

**Docket or dockets:** No. 91-1600.

**Decided:** April 20, 1993.

##### Service-based vesting is distinct from age motivation

**Holding:** ADEA disparate treatment requires age actually to motivate the employment decision and have a determinative influence; pension interference based only on years of service does not by itself establish that requirement. Liability remains possible when pension status is deliberately used as an age proxy or age and pension status both motivate the decision; age-triggered vesting and disparate impact are not decided.

**Proposition-level authority:** O'Connor’s Opinion of the Court, I; Stone-Zsela, White, Blackmun, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas (9 Justices). Direct majority agreement at this level of generality; no Marks inference.

**Treatment of earlier authority:**

- Aikens, 460 U.S. 711, and Burdine, 450 U.S. 248 — applied: actual motivation may be proved circumstantially; no automatic inference from any unlawful motive.
- Ingersoll-Rand, 498 U.S. 133 — distinguished: ERISA pension interference and age discrimination remain distinct.
- McDonnell Douglas, 411 U.S. 792 — preserved; its legitimate-reason terminology does not make every improper motive an ADEA violation.

##### Thurston governs individual acts as well as formal policies

**Holding:** An ADEA disparate-treatment violation is willful when the employer knew or recklessly disregarded whether its conduct violated the Act, whether the decision was individual or a formal policy. Once that showing is made, liquidated damages require no additional outrageousness, direct evidence or predominant-age motive; a good-faith, nonreckless mistake about a statutory permission is not willfulness.

**Proposition-level authority:** O'Connor’s Opinion of the Court, II; Stone-Zsela, White, Blackmun, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas (9 Justices). Direct majority agreement at this level of generality; no Marks inference.

**Treatment of earlier authority:**

- Thurston, 469 U.S. 111 — reaffirmed and applied to individual disparate-treatment decisions.
- Richland Shoe, 486 U.S. 128 — applied: negligence and mere awareness of statutory relevance are not willfulness.

**Limits and questions not reached:** Pension status is no safe harbor when used as an age proxy. Age-based vesting and disparate impact are not decided. A nonreckless good-faith mistake about a statutory defense or exemption can defeat willfulness without defeating ordinary liability. No direct-evidence, outrageousness or predominant-age requirement is added.

**Operative remedy or transition:** Vacate and remand for reconsideration of ADEA sufficiency and, if needed, willfulness. The younger replacement, confidentiality demands and comments are not held sufficient or insufficient here. Independent ERISA and state-law judgments retain their own effect; no liquidated-damages award is made.

#### Mertens v. Hewitt Associates, 508 U.S. 248 (1993)

**Docket or dockets:** No. 91-1671.
**Decided:** June 1, 1993.

##### The reviewed compensatory award is not appropriate equitable relief

**Holding:** Section 502(a)(3)’s appropriate equitable relief encompasses traditionally equitable categories of relief, not compensatory damages merely because a court of equity could award them in trust litigation. The requested monetary compensation from this admitted nonfiduciary for the plan’s losses is therefore unavailable under that provision; the Court does not bar every monetary equitable remedy or decide the antecedent, expressly disclaimed cause-of-action question.

**Proposition-level authority:** Scalia’s Opinion of the Court; Blackmun, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas (5 Justices). Direct majority support for this exact proposition; no Marks synthesis.

**Limits and questions not reached:** The expressly disclaimed antecedent cause of action is reserved, not rejected as jurisdictional. Other equitable monetary remedies are not categorically barred; restitution, unappealed claims and the separate malpractice disposition remain untouched.

**Operative remedy or transition:** Affirm rejection of the reviewed compensatory claim under §502(a)(3); no liability finding or award.

**Treatment of earlier authority:**

- Curtis v. Loether, 415 U.S. 189: traditional legal compensation distinction applied.
- Chauffeurs, Teamsters & Helpers v. Terry, 494 U.S. 558: remedy classification applied; trust forum alone does not decide the statutory category.
- Firestone Tire & Rubber Co. v. Bruch, 489 U.S. 101: trust-law guidance preserved subject to express statutory limits.
- Clews v. Jamieson, 182 U.S. 461: equity’s monetary powers acknowledged but distinguished from the statutory category.
- Massachusetts Mutual Life Insurance Co. v. Russell, 473 U.S. 134: enforcement-structure caution applied; its reserved question is now resolved only within this holding.
- United States v. Burke, May 26, 1992: its fractured judgment supplies no controlling general remedial-range test.
- Franklin v. Gwinnett County Public Schools, February 26, 1992: appropriate-remedy presumption distinguished because Congress expressly limited this provision to equitable relief.

#### St. Mary's Honor Center v. Hicks, 509 U.S. 502 (1993)

**Docket or dockets:** No. 92-602.
**Decided:** June 25, 1993.

##### Pretext alone does not command judgment, and circumstantial evidence need not be supplemented by a special category of proof

**Holding:** After an employer produces admissible evidence of nondiscriminatory reasons, a proven prima-facie case and rejection of the offered explanations do not automatically command judgment for the employee. Those circumstances may support a finding of intentional discrimination without a mandatory additional category of direct or independent proof; the Eighth Circuit’s automatic-liability mandate is vacated, while no majority here adopts a replacement persuasion allocation.

**Proposition-level authority:** Scalia’s Opinion of the Court, this proposition; Stone-Zsela, O’Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Thomas (5 Justices). Direct majority support at the stated level of generality; no Marks aggregation.

**Limits and questions not reached:** Burdine’s persuasion allocation is not replaced. No universal Title VII/§1983 equivalence, clear-error decision, resolution of retaliation, remaining defenses, relief or applicability of the 1991 Act is adopted.

**Operative remedy or transition:** Vacate the appellate automatic-liability mandate under Title VII and the related §1983 claim against Long and remand. No district-court vacatur, new burden allocation or judgment for either side is ordered. Existing district findings remain subject to proper review; retaliation remains for consideration and the unappealed §1981 dismissal is unchanged.

**Treatment of earlier authority:**

- McDonnell Douglas Corp. v. Green, 411 U.S. 792: indirect framework retained; no automatic liability holding adopted.
- Texas Department of Community Affairs v. Burdine, 450 U.S. 248: production stage retained; no five-Justice replacement of its persuasion rule.
- Postal Service Board of Governors v. Aikens, 460 U.S. 711: actual discrimination and circumstantial evidence remain central.
- Hazen, April 20, 1993: prohibited motive remains distinct from general unfairness; no added evidence category follows.

#### John Hancock Mutual Life Insurance Co. v. Harris Trust & Savings Bank, 510 U.S. 86

**Docket or dockets:** No. 92-1074.

**Decided:** 1993-12-13.

For the other questions in John Hancock Mutual Life Insurance Co. v. Harris Trust & Savings Bank, 1993-12-13, No. 92-1074, see Administrative Law — An agency interpretation cannot erase the qualified exclusion.

##### The guaranteed-benefit exclusion operates by component

**Holding:** Under ERISA §401(b)(2), a guaranteed-benefit policy itself is a plan asset, while the insurer's underlying assets are not deemed plan assets solely because it issued the policy to the extent the policy genuinely guarantees the amount of benefits. The disputed nonguaranteed free-funds component is not excluded: its plan-asset character subjects Hancock to fiduciary obligations to the extent it exercises authority or control over management or disposition of those assets under §3(21)(A), without deciding breach, causation, loss, or remedy.

**Proposition-level authority:** Ginsburg's opinion for the Court, joined by Stone, Blackmun, Stevens, Scalia, and Souter. Six Justices adopt the component-specific construction and its application to the disputed free funds.

**Treatment of earlier authority:**

- SEC v. United Benefit Life Insurance Co.: used for the distinction between a genuine guarantee and retained investment risk; the ERISA text supplies the governing exclusion.
- Mertens v. Hewitt Associates: kept separate; its treatment of available equitable categories does not decide whether these funds are plan assets or whether Hancock committed a breach.

##### Insurance regulation does not displace ERISA's express direction

**Holding:** McCarran-Ferguson does not bar application of ERISA's express insurance-related fiduciary and guaranteed-benefit provisions to this general-account contract. ERISA's insurance-saving clause preserves compatible state insurance regulation, but does not make a conflicting state obligation controlling over ERISA itself; a concrete conflict must be assessed as such rather than treated as a categorical exemption for all insurer assets.

**Proposition-level authority:** Ginsburg's opinion for the Court, joined by Stone, Blackmun, Stevens, Scalia, and Souter. This six-Justice ground rejects the asserted state-insurance obstacle to affirmance.

**Treatment of earlier authority:**

- Metropolitan Life Insurance Co. v. Massachusetts: applied to the coexistence of ERISA and state insurance regulation; no blanket withdrawal of federal obligations follows.
- FMC Corp. v. Holliday: applied to the coexistence of ERISA and state insurance regulation; no blanket withdrawal of federal obligations follows.
- Silkwood v. Kerr-McGee Corp.: applied for the distinction between compatible state regulation and a conflicting obstacle to federal law.
- United States Department of Treasury v. Fabe: distinguished because its federal-priority and policyholder-protection issue is different; its holding remains intact.

**Limits and questions not reached:** The contract contains guarantees and a residual floor; those do not guarantee the disputed free-funds benefits. A guaranteed reasonable return and fixed conversion protection are indicators, not an exhaustive mandatory formula. No breach, loss or remedy follows from status alone, and no categorical anti-deference rule for inconsistency or litigation positions is adopted.

**Operative remedy or transition:** The Second Circuit's relevant judgment is affirmed, 6–3. Asset status and applicable fiduciary functions remain distinct from breach, causation, loss and relief, which must be addressed in further proceedings; no monetary or equitable award is made.

#### Curtiss-Wright Corp. v. Schoonejongen

**Docket or dockets:** No. 93-1935.
**Decided:** March 6, 1995.

##### The company-amendment clause satisfies §402(b)(3)

**Holding:** In this employer-sponsored welfare plan, a written reservation of amendment to the Company provides an amendment procedure and identifies an authorized person under ERISA §402(b)(3), because §3(9) includes corporations and applicable corporate law identifies actors empowered to bind the Company. The statute does not invariably require a list of individual officers; actual additional procedures adopted by the plan remain binding.

**Proposition-level authority:** Opinion of the Court (Souter), unanimous.

**Treatment of earlier authority:**

- Nationwide Mutual Insurance Co. v. Darden, 503 U.S. 318 — ordinary legal meaning informs ERISA where the enacted text supplies no contrary rule; no employee-classification test is imported into amendment authority.
- John Hancock Mutual Life Insurance Co. v. Harris Trust & Savings Bank (December 13, 1993) — status and function remain separate from breach and relief; procedural sufficiency establishes no final amendment or immunity.

##### A sufficient procedure still requires authorized corporate action

**Holding:** A legally sufficient reservation of amendment power does not establish adoption of the challenged amendment. The lower courts must determine who had express or implied authority, whether an authorized actor approved the 1983 cutoff, and, if necessary, whether later ratification was legally effective, including its timing and consequences, before entering a conforming benefits judgment.

**Proposition-level authority:** Opinion of the Court (Souter), unanimous.

**Treatment of earlier authority:**

- Mertens v. Hewitt Associates, 508 U.S. 248 — the reviewed §502(a)(3) compensation limit remains; it does not bar benefits due under §502(a)(1)(B).
- John Hancock Mutual Life Insurance Co. v. Harris Trust & Savings Bank — the distinction between legal status and ultimate liability is preserved; no new breach or remedy follows merely from identifying amendment power.

**Limits and questions not reached:** ERISA §402(b)(3), 29 U.S.C. §1102(b)(3), requires every plan to provide a procedure for amending it and for identifying the persons with amendment authority. Section 3(9), §1002(9), includes a corporation among persons, as well as an individual, partnership, joint venture, mutual company, joint-stock company, trust, estate, unincorporated organization, association, and employee organization. The plan Constitution reserves to the Company the right at any time and from time to time to modify or amend any or all provisions, in whole or in part, and separately reserves termination. Delaware §141(a) places corporate business and affairs under the board’s management or direction except as otherwise provided by that chapter or the certificate of incorporation. No charter, bylaw, delegation, or approval is established as a fact here. Section 402(a)(1)’s written-instrument and named-fiduciary requirements remain distinct from sponsor amendment authority. Sections 1022 and 1024 retain actual summary, disclosure, and instrument-access duties; an SPD is not proof of authorized amendment. Section 502(a)(1)(B) benefits enforcement is distinct from §502(a)(3)’s appropriate-equitable-relief category.

**Operative remedy or transition:** The Third Circuit is reversed and the case remanded to identify authorized corporate actors, determine actual approval and legally effective ratification, including timing and consequences, and enter a conforming benefits judgment. Actual termination and amounts are not decided; rejected lifetime vesting is not automatically reopened.

#### Lockheed Corp. v. Spink, 517 U.S. 882

**Docket or dockets:** 95-809
**Decided:** June 10, 1996

##### Does sponsor adoption of pension amendments create fiduciary prohibited-transaction liability?

**Holding:** Designing and adopting these pension-plan amendments is sponsor conduct, not ERISA fiduciary conduct. Subject to §408's statutory exceptions, §406(a)(1) requires a fiduciary to cause a transaction the fiduciary knows or should know is prohibited; employer party-in-interest status alone is insufficient, and actual implementation must be assessed separately under §3(21)(A)'s functional terms, including asset-disposition control without an additional discretion requirement.

**Proposition-level authority:** Opinion of the Court (Ginsburg), Part I, unanimous; Part IV-A's independent-claims directions likewise unanimous.

**Limits and questions not reached:** Amendment power establishes neither compliance with amendment procedures nor lawful administration. Surplus remains plan property. The sponsor-capacity rule forecloses §404 liability resting solely on design or adoption, while actually preserved fiduciary-implementation and §403(c)(1) anti-inurement grounds retain their own predicates and remedies.

**Operative remedy or transition:** The reviewed adverse judgment is reversed and remanded, 9–0 overall. The theory resting solely on amendment is dismissed; preserved independent Count III alternatives receive ordinary consideration without a new allegation, automatic liability or unconditional dismissal of Count III. Count V, refusal of offensive collateral estoppel and withdrawn Count IV remain outside the relief. Appellate fees are vacated for reconsideration because their stated grounds included the rejected substantive rulings; the fee rationale has eight Associate joins, with Stone-Zsela agreeing in that disposition only.

**Treatment of earlier authority:** Curtiss-Wright's sponsor-amendment role is applied to a pension plan while its procedural requirements remain distinct. Varity separates sponsorship from administration and preserves its qualified equitable-remedy rule. John Hancock keeps asset status, function, breach and remedy separate. Mertens and Peacock retain the distinct predicate and remedy requirements for any preserved claim.

##### Does employer benefit from a required release alone make enhanced retirement payments prohibited transfers?

**Holding:** Genuine enhanced retirement benefits under an otherwise lawful plan are not prohibited transactions under §406(a)(1)(D) merely because employment-related releases benefit the employer. The rule assumes fiduciary implementation for this inquiry and does not immunize sham transfers, kickbacks, an independently unlawful plan or an unenforceable release.

**Proposition-level authority:** Ginsburg's Part II, joined by Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy and Thomas; seven. Souter and Breyer would reserve the question, not decide the payments unlawful.

**Material application:** The accepted complaint allegations describe participant benefits rather than diversion solely because releases have employer value. No sham or kickback is alleged in the decided issue.

**Limits and questions not reached:** No §408 exemption or general net-benefit defense is created. Independent participation, funding, vesting, loyalty and anti-inurement duties survive. Section 406(b)'s distinct prohibitions and particular waiver enforceability under the Older Workers Benefit Protection Act or other law are not decided; no loss, deception or hidden diversion is found.

**Treatment of earlier authority:** Keystone's transfer of property to discharge an enforceable funding obligation is distinguished; valuable consideration alone does not equate that transaction with these participant benefits.

##### Does OBRA require credit for Spink's earlier lawful exclusion from pension participation?

**Holding:** OBRA §§9201–9202 do not require credit for service during Spink's earlier lawful nonparticipation. Section 9204(a)(1) applies the new accrual protections to plan years beginning on or after January 1, 1988 and employees with an hour of service in a covered year; a smaller total benefit caused by earlier uncredited service is not itself a reduced accrual rate during a covered year.

**Proposition-level authority:** Ginsburg's Part III, all eight Associate Justices; Stone-Zsela agrees in the judgment only. Dismissal of Counts I–II is restored, 9–0.

**Material application:** The relevant plan year began December 25, 1988. The later calculation date does not turn prior lawful nonparticipation into credited service or establish a separate violation earlier in that calendar year.

**Limits and questions not reached:** Section 9204(b) separately applies §9203's participation amendments to covered plan years and service performed on or after January 1, 1988; that added service-date condition does not enter subsection (a). Under §9204(a)(2), benefits pursuant to and persons covered by employer–employee-representative collective agreements ratified before March 1, 1986 use the first plan year beginning on or after the earlier of January 1, 1990 or the later of January 1, 1988 and termination of the last qualifying agreement, disregarding extensions after February 28, 1986; no allegation invokes that transition. Section 9204(c) permits formal amendment to await the first plan year beginning on or after January 1, 1989 only if interim operations comply and amendment covers that interval retroactively. Operation under that provision alone does not make benefits or contributions indeterminable or the plan inconsistent with its terms, and does not delay substantive compliance. No general prohibition on using past facts or new default temporal rule is adopted.

**Treatment of earlier authority:** Landgraf and Rivers require application of enacted temporal direction first; that inquiry resolves the claim without a new default presumption.

#### New York State Conference of Blue Cross & Blue Shield Plans v. Travelers Insurance Co. / Pataki v. Travelers Insurance Co. / Hospital Association of New York State v. Travelers Insurance Co.

**Docket or dockets:** Nos. 93-1408; 93-1414; 93-1415.
**Decided:** April 26, 1995.

For the other questions in this decision, see Federal Courts — Component-specific vacatur and independent relief (The reserved direct-plan claim remains live after limited vacatur).

##### The reviewed 13% hospital differential is not preempted

**Holding:** ERISA §514(a) does not preempt the reviewed 13% hospital differential merely because its price effect may influence a covered plan’s choice of insurer or coverage arrangement. In these commercial-insurance applications the measure neither uses ERISA status or covered-plan benefits as its legal benchmark nor commands plan benefits, eligibility, uniform administration or an alternative enforcement remedy; the Court reserves economic effects sufficiently acute to operate as a substantive coverage command or effectively restrict insurer choice.

**Proposition-level authority:** Opinion of the Court (Souter), unanimous.

**Treatment of earlier authority:**

- Shaw v. Delta Air Lines, Inc., 463 U.S. 85: applies the connection-or-reference inquiry and its remote-effects boundary without making every cost consequence preemptive.
- Mackey v. Lanier Collection Agency & Service, Inc., 486 U.S. 825: applies the distinction between singling out ERISA plans and ordinary legal effects, including costs, without making general applicability an automatic defense.
- District of Columbia v. Greater Washington Board of Trade, December 14, 1992: distinguishes its mandatory covered-plan benefit benchmark, which these reviewed payment categories do not contain; that holding remains intact.
- FMC Corp. v. Holliday, 498 U.S. 52: distinguishes its alteration of plan subrogation and benefit calculation; its separate saving/deemer architecture remains undisturbed.

**Limits and questions not reached:** Section 2807-c(1) excludes Medicare payments and permits alternative reimbursement methodologies authorized under subdivision four, paragraphs (e)–(i). Paragraph (a) covers state governmental agencies, article 43 insurance corporations’ expense-incurred policies, the described payments by those corporations for foreign-corporation subscribers under subdivision 12(d), and article 44 HMO subscribers. The diagnosis-related-group case payment includes reimbursable operating costs, capital expenses, bad-debt and charity-care allowances, the primary-care allowance, the allowance for financially distressed hospitals, the rate-year trend factor and the subdivision four (a)–(d) case-payment adjustments. Paragraph (a-1) gives local-government payments for local correctional inmates the state-agency rates.

Paragraph (b) covers expense-incurred payments under workers’ compensation, volunteer firefighters’, volunteer ambulance workers’ and no-fault laws; self-insured funds paying hospitals directly, except enrollees covered by a rate-method agreement under paragraph (a) of subdivision two; licensed commercial accident-and-health insurers whose policies cover inpatient services on that basis; and out-of-plan benefits authorized under §4406, except those offered by a not-for-profit corporation or an organization qualifying under Internal Revenue Code §501(c). The payment equals the article 43 case-payment basis, adjusted for uncovered services, increased by 13%, and adjusted under subdivision 11(i) where applicable. A hospital may not refuse a qualifying direct-payment arrangement without due cause. Paragraphs (a) and (b) payors may not choose the lower of charges and the case payment or apportion the case payment by excluding a preexisting or acquired condition that must be treated along with the reason for admission. These are statutory payment rules; they are not findings that an ERISA plan changed its benefits.

Paragraph (c) uses hospital charges for patients without an authorized case-payment arrangement under paragraphs (a) or (b), or coverage under subdivision two (a). The subdivision four (d) short-stay definition applies, with that paragraph’s developed payment increased by 13%. For a charge-paying patient the ceiling is 120% of the paragraph (b) diagnosis-related-group case payment, without the subdivision 11(i) adjustment. A hospital must apply its charge schedule uniformly. This is not another application of the full 24% differential.

##### The additional commercial-insurer 11% charge is not preempted

**Holding:** The reviewed additional 11% commercial-insurer charge likewise is not preempted under §514(a): its revenue destination and additional price differential do not establish a required plan benefit, insurer choice or administrative arrangement on this record. This application adopts neither a numerical safe harbor for combined charges nor immunity for financial pressure that actually operates as a substantive coverage command or effectively restricts insurer choice.

**Proposition-level authority:** Opinion of the Court (Souter), unanimous.

**Treatment of earlier authority:**

- Shaw: applies its relation boundary to the additional charge’s actual operation, rather than treating legislative purpose or percentage alone as conclusive.
- Mackey: applies the distinction between ordinary added economic costs and the relation ERISA preempts; it supplies no quantitative exemption.
- Board of Trade: preserves its covered-plan-content benchmark and distinguishes this additional payor charge, whose amount does not use plan benefits as the legal measure.

**Limits and questions not reached:** The reproduced subdivision 11(i) applies the additional 11%, totaling 24% with the existing 13%, to the identified commercial-insurer expense-incurred discharges from April 1, 1992 through March 31, 1993. The appendix contains an ellipsis after that provision’s total-24% statement. Subdivision 14-e creates the statewide supplementary pool: hospitals submit the additional component, and the commissioner deposits it and investment income into the general fund. The complete extension text and resulting current rate liability are not decided here.

##### The conditional HMO assessment is not preempted

**Holding:** The reviewed HMO assessment of up to 9%, subject to its actual Medicaid participation, enrollment, exemption, reduction and payment conditions, is not preempted by §514(a) on this record. It affects HMO prices and participation choices without a demonstrated command to an ERISA plan’s benefit structure, eligibility, insurer choice or uniform administration; its conditions receive no categorical immunity from preemption if a different application establishes such a connection or effective compulsion.

**Proposition-level authority:** Opinion of the Court (Souter), unanimous.

**Treatment of earlier authority:**

- Shaw: applies connection-or-reference analysis separately to the HMO measure and preserves the possibility that more acute indirect effects could suffice.
- Mackey: applies the distinction between actual administrative commands and economic consequences without dismissing duties merely because imposed through generally applicable law.
- FMC: distinguishes compulsory changes to plan reimbursement rights from the HMO participation and payment conditions shown here, retaining the insured/self-funded distinction where saving and deemer issues must be reached.

**Limits and questions not reached:** No direct self-funded merits ruling, new insurance-saving test, numerical safe harbor, uniform-cost guarantee, FEHBA ruling, stop-loss-letter ruling, Tax Injunction Act holding, or amended-law rate command is adopted. These are record-specific applications of the relation boundary, not categorical immunity for health regulation, price regulation, generally applicable statutes or indirect burdens.

Subdivision 2-a(a) addresses article 44 and article 43 HMO inpatient discharges on or after July 1, 1992. Medicaid-eligible subscribers and participants in the 1988 chapter 703 regional pilot projects are excluded. The base is the subdivision one case payment, subdivision four per-diem rate, or negotiated and approved subdivision two (b) rate, whichever applies. The additional factor is 9%, subject to elimination or reduction. Notice was due June 1 for the 1992 rate period and December 1 for each following annual January rate period. An appeal by May 1 can obtain a July 1 adjustment.

Elimination under subdivision 2-a(b)(i) requires status as a Social Services Law §364-j managed-care provider in each social-services district in the service area and enrollment of at least 90% of the sum of the target numbers of Medicaid recipients not exempt from managed care. The reproduced determination dates are May 1 for the 1992 rate period, November 1 preceding the 1993 rate year, and May 1 when an appeal is taken for the six-month period beginning July 1. Reaching 25%, 50% or 75% of total target enrollment gives the corresponding 25%, 50% or 75% reduction, rather than elimination.

An HMO may apply to the social-services commissioner for a district participation exemption by May 1 for the 1992 period or October 1 preceding the 1993 rate year, based on a good-faith effort to obtain a managed-care contract or other commissioner-established criteria. The following nonexclusive good-faith routes apply only to the reproduced 1992 and 1993 rate years. First, the HMO submitted an implementation plan and would have begun enrollment but for the district’s failure or refusal to contract. Second, it submitted letters of intent to all districts, contracted with half by July 1, 1992 and two-thirds by January 1, 1993, and could have contracted with the rest but for Department-agreed implementation delay. Third, the same letters and agreed-delay conditions apply where contracts cover districts containing half and then two-thirds of the service area’s nonexempt Medicaid population on those dates. Fourth, it meets the total enrollment target for its otherwise nonexempt districts despite incomplete contracts. For the fourth route, 1992–1993 fee-for-service enrollment is calculated from annual Medicaid visits to a licensed diagnostic and treatment center operated by the HMO or a distinct licensed nonprofit providing or arranging a majority of its services, divided by commercial subscribers’ annual average visits to those facilities. The commissioner also considers implementation of a plan to capitate a substantial percentage of that enrollment under an approved viable schedule. This fourth route permits only a 75% reduction, not elimination.

An exempt district is removed from the service area for the calculation. An HMO with noncontiguous regions may apply across its whole service area or separately on an aggregate basis for each region. The district target equals the ratio of its commercial subscribers to all HMO commercial subscribers in that district, excluding Medicaid, Medicare and regional-pilot subscribers from both sides, multiplied by the district enrollment objective. That objective is the greatest applicable measure: the approved nonexempt Medicaid enrollment goal as of July 1 of the preceding year; 5% of the district’s nonexempt Medicaid population before completion of the first full year of its approved managed-care plan; or actual nonexempt enrollment on July 1 of the preceding year, except May 1, 1992 for the first period. The actual-enrollment measure excludes providers that are neither HMOs nor entities authorized under §4403-a. The most recent subscriber statistics govern the ratio. Medicaid enrollees under an affiliation contract with a prepaid health-services plan may count when the commissioner approves the contract in consultation with the social-services commissioner.

Payment and administration: HMOs remit the adjusted assessment directly to a statewide pool for the preceding month’s discharges; pool funds and investment income go to the general fund. Estimated payment is due within fifteen days after month-end unless the actual payment has been made within that period. Collection stops when the commissioner, consulting the budget director, determines that $31 million has been or will be collected for the fiscal year ending March 31, 1993. Excess collections for that year are refunded in proportion to each HMO’s share of total payments. Arrearages bear subdivision twenty interest and penalties on the basis used for the bad-debt and charity-care pools. The commissioner may contract with the subdivision sixteen (c) pool administrator or, if unavailable, another designated administrator. If a contract is made, annual receipt-and-distribution audits are required; approved reasonable costs may be paid from the pool, with annual personnel services limited to $200,000. The $31 million cap is not declared permanent.

Subdivision 2-a(d) extends the adjusted factor to point-of-service inpatient business when an article 43 corporation offering those benefits was HMO-certified on April 1, 1992 and later transfers to its point-of-service product reduce HMO enrollment by more than 20%. The pre-April-1 subscriber ratio then controls the target. Satisfactory evidence that the transfers occurred for reasons beyond the corporation’s control prevents that provision’s application. The same pool and payment procedure apply. Subdivision 2-a(e) requires the information needed to administer the scheme, including county enrollment data by May 1 each year, identifying Medicaid, Medicare and regional-pilot subscribers separately and reflecting enrollment no earlier than the previous year.

ERISA §514(a), subject to subsection (b), supersedes state laws insofar as they relate to a §4(a) plan not exempt under §4(b); its effective date is January 1, 1975, and §514(b)(1) excludes earlier causes of action, acts and omissions. Section 514(b)(2)(A), subject to (B), preserves state insurance, banking and securities regulation. Subparagraph (B) prevents a covered nonexempt plan or its trust from being deemed an insurer, bank, trust company or investment company, or engaged in insurance or banking, for the stated state-regulatory purposes; its express exception is a plan established primarily to provide death benefits. A separate exempt workers’ compensation, unemployment or disability plan does not exempt a different covered health plan. Relation, saving and deeming are distinct questions. The direct self-funded application remains for remand; the separate insurance-saving theory for the up-to-9% assessment is not established as preserved. No new ruling on the Tax Injunction Act, laches, FEHBA, or the stop-loss letter is made.

**Operative remedy or transition:** In all three dockets, reverse the decided commercial-insurance and HMO ERISA rulings and dissolve the associated ERISA relief; vacate reviewed direct-self-funded adjudication and ERISA surcharge protection insofar as they reach the reserved application, and remand that live claim. This removes no independently surviving FEHBA or Actuarial Letter protection and decides no direct-plan merits or right to interim relief. No new payment, escrow, reimbursement or accrued-liability command is entered.

#### Varity Corp. v. Howe, 516 U.S. 489

**Docket or dockets:** No. 94-1471.
**Decided:** March 19, 1996.

##### The integrated benefit-security presentation was fiduciary administration

**Holding:** ERISA §3(21)(A) attaches fiduciary responsibility to the particular exercise of discretionary plan administration, including plan information communicated in that capacity. Varity exercised that function when it used administrative authority, benefit comparisons and linked assurances of benefit security to induce the class's transfer; employer status, corporate forecasting or participant perception alone would not suffice.

**Proposition-level authority:** Souter's Part I, joined by Stone-Zsela, Stevens, Kennedy, Ginsburg and Breyer: six Justices.

**Material application:** Current-benefit descriptions and deliberately linked security assurances formed one administrative presentation. Warnings that future conditions depended on MCC's success and that changes might occur did not displace the unchallenged trial findings that the combined presentation was knowingly misleading.

**Limits and questions not reached:** Every employment statement or corporate-solvency prediction is not thereby fiduciary. Corporate restructuring and plan-amendment powers remain distinct from administration.

**Treatment of earlier authority:** John Hancock's particular-function inquiry is applied while keeping status, breach and remedy separate. Curtiss-Wright preserves sponsor amendment authority but distinguishes retained power from authorized exercise; it creates no immunity for this administrative act.

##### Deliberate disloyal deception violates §404

**Holding:** An administrator acting as a fiduciary violates §404(a)(1)'s loyalty duty by knowingly and significantly misleading participants about benefit security to induce surrender of existing plan interests for the employer's benefit. Varity's found deception and the class's reliance meet that rule despite reserved lawful amendment or termination powers.

**Proposition-level authority:** Souter's Part II, joined by the same six Justices as Part I.

**Limits and questions not reached:** This intentional-deception holding decides no general duty to volunteer information, answer every inquiry, disclose every forecast or avoid negligent misstatement. **Decided:** April 1, 1996.

##### Replacement within the ADEA's protected age group does not defeat the initial inference

**Holding:** An ADEA plaintiff need not show replacement by someone under forty to establish the initial inference of age discrimination. A replacement's membership in the protected group does not itself defeat that inference.

**Proposition-level authority:** O'Connor's Part I, unanimous.

**Limits and questions not reached:** The Court assumes McDonnell Douglas applies for this question and takes the case as a non-reduction-in-force matter, without settling every ADEA framework or alternative prima facie formulation. #### Hughes Aircraft Co. v. Jacobson

**Docket or dockets:** No. 97-1287; Ninth Circuit judgment, 105 F.3d 1288.
**Decided:** 1999-01-25.

##### I. Genuine payments within the continuing plan do not inure to the employer

**Holding:** Section 403(c)(1) does not prohibit payment of genuine benefits to participants under the amended single plan merely because those benefits reduce future employer contributions or payroll costs. Surplus remains plan property, and the anti-inurement rule independently governs an actual diversion, unlawful reversion or payment outside the lawful plan.

**Proposition-level authority:** Ginsburg's opinion's Part I is joined by all eight Associate Justices and controls; Stone-Zsela agrees in dismissal of the anti-inurement claim without adding a ground.

##### II. Amendment design is distinct from fiduciary implementation

**Holding:** Design and adoption of these pension amendments are sponsor functions, even though employees contributed to the plan; those acts alone create no fiduciary breach under §§404–405. Actual asset management or disposition and administration remain subject to §3(21)(A)'s functional requirements, including authority or control over disposition without an additional discretion requirement.

**Proposition-level authority:** All nine Justices join the sponsor-capacity rule and its preservation of independent duties. The eight Associates additionally join the claim-specific conclusion that these original implementation and derivative cofiduciary theories identify no independent breach after their surplus-ownership and separate-plan predicates fail; Stone-Zsela agrees in that disposition without supplying an additional implementation ground.

##### III. Accrued benefits are protected without individual ownership of all surplus

**Holding:** Participants in this defined-benefit plan have enforceable rights to promised and statutorily protected accrued benefits, including the employee-contribution floor, but no present individual ownership of all actuarial surplus. Because the original theory identifies no reduction of those protected benefits, use of pooled surplus for lawful plan benefits establishes neither forfeiture nor an anti-cutback violation.

**Proposition-level authority:** The nine Justices join the no-surplus-ownership and no-reduction holding. The statutory distinction between required and voluntary contributions explains the eight Associates' rejection of the original employee-contribution theory; no benefit amount is calculated.

##### IV. Continuing benefit obligations do not trigger termination distribution

**Holding:** Closing the contributory structure to new entrants while continuing the plan and its benefit obligations is not a statutory termination triggering §4044 distribution. A common-law characterization as a wasting trust cannot replace §4041's standard-or-distress termination process or PBGC proceedings under §4042.

**Proposition-level authority:** All nine Justices join the nontermination holding on these continuing-plan facts. The eight Associates join the separate statutory distribution analysis; no general rule for every partial termination is decided.

##### V. Incidental employer advantage does not make these benefit payments prohibited transactions

**Holding:** Under §406(a)(1)(D), genuine pension payments under an otherwise lawful plan do not become prohibited transfers merely because they save the employer money. Nor does §406(b)(2), which bars a fiduciary from acting in a transaction for an adverse party, establish a violation merely from the sponsor's adoption of a benefit design advantageous to itself.

**Proposition-level authority:** All eight Associates join Part V, which controls both statutory grounds; Stone-Zsela agrees only in dismissal of the prohibited-transaction component.

##### VI. Fair administration does not promise a later incentive to ineligible retirees

**Holding:** The plan's fair-and-equitable administration, interpretation and application clause does not give every retiree a contractual right to an incentive later offered to specified active employees. The original sixth claim fails independently under §502(a)(1)(B) because no benefit due under the operative eligibility terms is withheld, and under §404(a)(1)(D) because the asserted difference is lawful eligibility design rather than unequal administration among eligible participants.

**Proposition-level authority:** All eight Associates join Part VI and its distinct statutory and plan grounds. Stone-Zsela agrees only in dismissal of this separate plan-enforcement component.

**Treatment of earlier authority:**

- *Lockheed Corp. v. Spink* is applied to sponsor design and genuine benefit payments while its independent-claims directions are enforced. The present dismissal rests on separate statutory and plan answers, not categorical sponsor immunity.
- *John Hancock Mutual Life Insurance Co. v. Harris Trust & Savings Bank* is applied to separate plan property, function and breach; it establishes no participant ownership of every dollar of actuarial surplus.
- *Varity Corp. v. Howe* is preserved for function-specific administration, disloyal communications and appropriate relief. No conduct of that kind is immunized by amendment authority.
- *Curtiss-Wright Corp. v. Schoonejongen* retains its distinction between a sufficient amendment procedure and actual authorized adoption; no separate adoption defect is decided here.
- *Mertens v. Hewitt Associates* retains its §502(a)(3) equitable-category limitation. It is distinguished from the §502(a)(1)(B) claim, which fails for lack of a promised due benefit rather than a categorical bar on benefits enforcement.

**Limits and questions not reached:** employee contributions do not convert amendment design into a fiduciary act or establish individual ownership of an ongoing defined-benefit plan's surplus. Plan-property restrictions, employee-derived accrued benefits, functional fiduciary obligations, genuine termination requirements and contractual benefit promises remain independently enforceable. These six original theories fail after those distinct predicates are examined. No blanket immunity for implementation, new distribution entitlement or general rule disposing of a later merger claim is established.

**Operative remedy or transition:** **Reversed and remanded, 9–0.**

**Supporting:** Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer.

The Ninth Circuit's revival of the six original claims is reversed. On remand those theories shall be dismissed on the independent grounds stated in the opinion. No surplus distribution, neutral trustee, asset restoration, benefit recalculation or new fee award is ordered. A genuinely independent administration, misrepresentation, asset-diversion or nonpayment claim remains governed by its own elements; later amended-complaint merger allegations are outside this judgment.

#### O'Dell v. Netherland

**Docket or dockets:** No. 96-6867.
**Decided:** June 19, 1997.

##### The confined Simmons opportunity-to-answer claim is available against Teague on two independent grounds

**Holding:** When the State relies on future dangerousness to seek death and the only lawful noncapital sentence actually excludes ordinary parole, the defendant must have an effective opportunity to give the jury accurate information about that legal ineligibility. Gardner and Skipper dictated that limited protection before O'Dell's October 3, 1988 finality; Teague therefore does not bar the claim, although eligibility, violation and relief still require their own predicates.

**Proposition-level authority:** Stevens's Part I, joined by O'Connor, Souter, Ginsburg and Breyer: five Justices. Stone-Zsela does not join the old-rule holding.

**Independent alternative holding:** Even if the parole-information protection were classified as new, Teague's watershed exception permits its application where future dangerousness is an operative basis for choosing death, the only lawful noncapital alternative excludes ordinary parole, and the defendant is denied an effective opportunity to correct the materially misleading release premise through accurate defense presentation or an adequate instruction. The rule establishes retroactive availability in that confined category, not that O'Dell has proved those predicates or is presently entitled to sentencing relief.

**Proposition-level authority for alternative holding:** Stevens's Part II, joined by Stone-Zsela, Souter, Ginsburg and Breyer: five direct votes. O'Connor does not join; no Marks synthesis supplies either ground.

**Limits and questions not reached:** Accurate defense presentation or an adequate instruction may suffice; no fixed script or guarantee against clemency is required, and the State may prove danger within prison. Refusal of an instruction alone does not establish the watershed conjunction. The alternative establishes neither that Simmons is new nor that every capital procedure is watershed. It does not make an error structural or dispense with its consequence analysis.

**Operative remedy or transition:** Part III, joined by Stevens, O’Connor, Souter, Ginsburg and Breyer, vacates and remands. The Teague bar is foreclosed, but an operative sentencing writ requires actual legal parole ineligibility, lack of effective corrective opportunity, the applicable error consequence and genuinely applicable unresolved habeas conditions. The prior conditional order is not automatically reinstated. The conviction and unrelated claims remain undisturbed; no hearing, clock, release, stay or execution order follows.

**Material judgment:** Six Justices reject the Teague bar and set aside the adverse disposition; five support the exact vacatur mandate. Stone-Zsela instead favors reversal and restoration of the conditional-relief framework. No Justice grants an unconditional present writ.

**Material application:** O’Dell’s statements about sixteen years for a prior parole violation and never leaving prison remain part of the presentation but do not themselves establish accurate fixed-law information. The vileness aggravator and earlier prison murder do not automatically cure sentence-selection error.

**Treatment of earlier authority:** Gardner and Skipper supply the pre-1988 effective-response protection; Ramos and Caldwell do not authorize withholding the fixed ordinary-parole answer. Simmons retains its triggers and alternative means of informing the jury. Teague and Sawyer preserve distinct old-rule and watershed inquiries; Gideon and Crane supply a confined fair-adversarial-process analogy. Graham, Gray and Lambrix support functional comparison without adding later decisions to the 1988 law. Gilmore and Caspari preserve genuinely new procedural rules and both contemporary exceptions. Allridge’s ordinary-parole release predictions differ. Brecht and O’Neal retain category-specific Chapman holdings; Gray chose no universal collateral standard. This claim’s necessary standard must be resolved under those holdings or an outcome supported under both competing approaches; no harm result or universal prisoner-prejudice rule is assumed.

**Holding:** Using Brecht’s silence after Miranda warnings to impeach his trial explanation violates Doyle’s due-process protection; permitted impeachment with prewarning silence is analytically separate. This violation is trial error subject to harmless-error review, not a merely nonconstitutional rule or a structural defect requiring automatic reversal.

**Holding:** For this preserved Doyle trial error properly before the federal habeas court, the State must establish beyond a reasonable doubt that the constitutional error did not contribute to the verdict. Collateral posture alone does not replace that burden with Kotteakos; the reviewing court must apply Chapman to the whole record, with relief conditional on failure of the State’s showing rather than automatic from the violation.

#### Simmons v. South Carolina, 512 U.S. 154

**Docket or dockets:** No. 92-9059.

**Decided:** 1994-06-17.

##### Parole ineligibility must be available to answer the State's future-danger case

**Holding:** When the State places future dangerousness at issue in a capital sentencing proceeding and the only legally available noncapital sentence is life imprisonment without ordinary parole, due process requires an effective opportunity for the defendant to inform the jury of that ineligibility through accurate defense presentation or an adequate instruction. A direction to disregard parole and give life imprisonment its ordinary meaning does not satisfy that requirement on this record; the rule neither requires a particular script nor promises immunity from clemency or future legislation.

**Proposition-level authority:** Part I of Justice Souter's opinion, joined by Chief Justice Stone-Zsela and Justices Blackmun, Stevens, O'Connor, Kennedy and Ginsburg. Seven Justices directly adopt this Fourteenth Amendment rule and its application; no combination of separate rationales is necessary.

**Material application:** The operative alternative was life without ordinary parole under §24-21-640's second-or-subsequent violent-conviction rule following separate sentencing for the prior conviction; offenses within one day or one continuous course count as one. The eligibility premise is uncontested here, not a rule that every life sentence excludes parole. The State may still prove danger to inmates or guards and offer truthful information about commutation or pardon.

**Treatment of earlier authority:**

- Gardner v. Florida, 430 U.S. 349 (1977): its opportunity-to-answer principle supplies the due-process foundation for responding to material sentencing information.
- Skipper v. South Carolina, 476 U.S. 1 (1986): its future-danger rebuttal principle is applied to a fixed legal sentencing alternative; its independent mitigation rule remains intact.
- California v. Ramos, 463 U.S. 992 (1983): distinguished; permission to give truthful commutation information does not authorize withholding this responsive fact about ordinary parole.
- Graham v. Collins, January 25, 1993, and Johnson v. Texas, June 24, 1993: their effective-mitigation rules remain controlling within their scope; neither is treated as having already decided this separate legal-information question.

**Later-authority backlinks:** O'Dell v. Netherland, June 19, 1997, No. 96-6867: the confined claim is available against Teague under separate majority old-rule and conditional watershed holdings; eligibility, effective opportunity, error consequence and genuine habeas conditions remain.

##### The error is not harmless on this record and requires a new penalty proceeding if death is pursued

**Holding:** Under Chapman's direct-review standard, the State cannot establish beyond a reasonable doubt that barring the parole-ineligibility explanation did not contribute to this jury's selection of death, given the State's future-danger argument, the complete exclusion of the legal answer and the jury's express parole inquiry. The death-sustaining judgment is reversed and the case remanded for a constitutionally adequate new penalty proceeding if the State continues to seek death; a lawful noncapital disposition remains available, and the convictions and separate sentences are undisturbed.

**Proposition-level authority:** Part II of Justice Souter's opinion, joined by Chief Justice Stone-Zsela and Justices Blackmun, Stevens and Ginsburg. Five Justices directly adopt this record-specific harmlessness determination and mandatory further-hearing direction. O'Connor and Kennedy join reversal but reserve the further remedial determination.

**Limits and questions not reached:** The Court adopts no independently sufficient Eighth Amendment instruction rule. It does not determine an entitlement to parole information where the State has not raised future danger, settle a disputed parole-eligibility question, require a fixed form of words, guarantee against clemency or future legislation, or announce automatic reversal for every omission. The State may pursue a lawful noncapital disposition instead of another capital hearing. No guilt retrial, acquittal, release, invalidation of the separate sentences or general prohibition of capital punishment is ordered.

**Treatment of earlier authority:**

- Chapman v. California, 386 U.S. 18 (1967): applied; the State must establish harmlessness beyond a reasonable doubt, and fails on this record.
- Romano v. Oklahoma, June 13, 1994: distinguished as an affirmative disclosure of another jury's death decision; this decision neither supplies a missing majority for its further-remedy positions nor changes that case's mandate.
- Johnson v. Texas, June 24, 1993: preserves the distinction between correcting a capital sentencing defect and disturbing guilt; no automatic noncapital sentence follows.

**Operative remedy or transition:** The death-sustaining judgment is reversed, 7–2, and the case remanded. Five Justices require a constitutionally adequate new penalty proceeding if the State continues to seek death; the State may instead pursue a lawful noncapital disposition. The existing death sentence cannot be retained by a further harmlessness finding on this omission. Guilt and the separate life and thirty-day sentences remain undisturbed.

#### O'Neal v. McAninch

**Docket or dockets:** No. 93-7407.
**Decided:** February 21, 1995.

##### Chapman governs the preserved personal-intent instructional claim

**Holding:** For a preserved constitutional trial violation, properly available on federal habeas, in which the combined jury instructions and prosecutor's statement permit conviction without proof of the defendant's own intent required for the offense, the State must establish beyond a reasonable doubt, on the whole record, that the error did not contribute to the verdict. Kotteakos's substantial-and-injurious-effect and grave-doubt framework does not govern this claim; the standard for other reserved categories is not decided.

**Proposition-level authority:** O'Connor's Parts II and IV, joined by Stone-Zsela, Souter, Ginsburg and Breyer: five Justices. The broader Part III extension has no controlling coalition.

**Treatment of earlier authority:**

- Brecht v. Abrahamson, April 21, 1993: extended from preserved Doyle error to the preserved personal-intent instructional/argument category; its original rule survives, while other reserved categories remain unresolved by the Court.
- Chapman v. California, 386 U.S. 18 (1967): applied with the State's beyond-a-reasonable-doubt burden and actual-verdict contribution inquiry intact.
- Kotteakos v. United States, 328 U.S. 750 (1946): not extended to replace Chapman for this constitutional claim; its existing operation for genuinely nonconstitutional trial error remains unchanged.
- In re Winship, 397 U.S. 358 (1970): applied as the constitutional requirement that the prosecution prove the elements of the charged offense; no new personal-intent element is imposed on offenses that do not require it.
- Sullivan v. Louisiana, June 1, 1993: preserved as the distinct rule for a defect vitiating the jury's governing reasonable-doubt standard; the assumed error here is not declared structural.

**Limits and questions not reached:** The Court does not adopt a general Chapman rule for all preserved constitutional trial errors. Proof of the violation, preservation, exhaustion, independent habeas gates, structural-error rules and a prejudice element defining another constitutional claim remain separate. Actual harmlessness is not decided.

**Later-authority backlinks:** California v. Roy, November 4, 1996, No. 95-2025: extends the State-borne Chapman rule to a preserved personal-intent omission without accompanying prosecutorial argument, while leaving a general extension to all constitutional trial errors unadopted. Calderon v. Coleman, No. 98-437, December 14, 1998: extends State-borne Chapman review to the defined, properly available capital-selection commutation-instruction claim; the earlier holding remains unchanged and no universal constitutional-habeas extension is adopted.

##### The proper standard requires renewed review, not an automatic writ

**Holding:** The Sixth Circuit's substantial-influence assessment with a prisoner prejudice burden does not supply the required Chapman determination; its challenged judgment must be vacated for review of the combined claim under the correct rule. If an available constitutional violation is established and the State fails to prove beyond a reasonable doubt on the existing record that it did not contribute to the verdict, the writ shall issue for the affected conviction or convictions unless the State retries O'Neal within a reasonable time; no immediate writ or present retrial deadline is imposed.

**Proposition-level authority:** O'Connor's Parts II and IV, joined by Stone-Zsela, Souter, Ginsburg and Breyer: five Justices. The broader Part III extension has no controlling coalition.

**Treatment of earlier authority:**

- Brecht v. Abrahamson, April 21, 1993: its whole-record remand and conditional-relief distinction are extended to this instructional claim, without automatic reinstatement of a prior writ.
- Chapman v. California, 386 U.S. 18 (1967): applied to the existing trial record; a weaker substantial-influence finding is not treated as the required finding beyond a reasonable doubt.
- Estelle v. McGuire, December 4, 1991, and Victor v. Nebraska / Sandoval v. California, March 22, 1994: their whole-charge reasonable-likelihood inquiry remains the rule for identifying an ambiguous instruction's constitutional defect; the Court does not decide that antecedent application here.
- Schlup v. Delo, January 23, 1995: its gateway for otherwise barred claims remains independent; neither passage through that gateway nor an innocence showing is invented for this claim.

**Operative remedy or transition:** The Sixth Circuit is vacated and remanded, 7–2, with Chapman directions supported by five Justices. Otherwise the conditional writ issues for the affected convictions unless the State retries within a reasonable time; no present deadline, immediate release or reinstatement of the earlier conditional order is directed.

#### Allridge v. Scott

**Decided:** January 15, 1996.

##### Ordinary parole availability and a proposed release-prediction instruction

**Holding:** Simmons's right to explain legal parole ineligibility does not require a favorable prediction of eventual release when the noncapital alternative permits ordinary parole. **Proposition-level authority:** Souter's Part I-A, unanimous.

#### Florida Prepaid Postsecondary Education Expense Board v. College Savings Bank

**Docket or dockets:** No. 98-531.
**Decided:** 1999-06-23.

##### Congress unmistakably authorized this federal patent action against States

**Holding:** Sections 271(h) and 296 expressly subject States and their instrumentalities to private patent actions in federal court for covered violations on or after October 28, 1992. Section 296(b) makes the otherwise available patent remedies available on their own statutory terms; that authorization does not itself establish constitutional power, State consent, infringement, or entitlement to a particular award.

**Proposition-level authority:** Stevens's Part I, joined by all nine Justices, directly adopts this statutory proposition and its limits.

**Holding:** A respondent may defend an unchanged favorable judgment on an appropriately supported alternative that neither enlarges its rights nor reduces the adversary's rights under that judgment. CSB's posture permits consideration of its preserved defenses of the immunity denial, but preservation and briefing alone do not expand the §5 question or establish an alternative's merits.

**Proposition-level authority:** Part I has all nine Justices' joins at this precise level. Their actual decisions on the alternative grounds remain distinct.

##### Section 5 independently sustains the authorized patent action

**Holding:** Section 5 permits Congress to prevent unconstitutional property deprivations through congruent and proportional enforcement legislation reaching some conduct that is not independently unconstitutional. Sections 271(h) and 296 validly authorize this patent action on that basis; the plaintiff need not establish a separate constitutional violation to proceed under the Act.

**Proposition-level authority:** Stevens's Part II, joined by Stone-Zsela, Souter, Ginsburg, and Breyer, states an independently sufficient holding of the Court. O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, and Thomas disagree.

##### Article I independently sustains the expressly authorized action

**Holding:** Congress may authorize private enforcement against States when the underlying obligation and enforcement measure fall within its Article I authority and Congress unmistakably authorizes the State's exposure to suit. The Patent Clause, together with the Necessary and Proper Clause, supports this federal patent remedy as an independent ground for sustaining the immunity denial.

**Proposition-level authority:** Stevens's Part III, joined by Stone-Zsela, Souter, Ginsburg, and Breyer, states an independently sufficient holding of the Court. O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, and Thomas oppose the extension.

**Holding:** A State's continued commercial participation after Congress authorizes suit does not constitute consent: Florida Prepaid did not consent to federal patent suit by continuing its prepaid-tuition program after §§271(h) and 296 took effect. Actual consent through a distinct State choice, such as an express waiver or voluntary invocation of federal jurisdiction, remains available. **Proposition-level authority:** Stevens's Part IV, joined by all nine Justices, applies College Savings Part III and states these limits. #### Buckley v. Fitzsimmons, 509 U.S. 259 (1993)

**Docket or dockets:** No. 91-7849.
**Decided:** June 24, 1993.

##### Investigative fabrication is not protected by absolute prosecutorial immunity

**Holding:** A prosecutor who performs ordinary investigative work to obtain evidence identifying a perpetrator before probable cause has no absolute prosecutorial immunity for alleged fabrication in that function; later courtroom use does not transform the earlier work into advocacy. Genuine evaluation and preparation in an advocate’s role remain protected, and neither all pre-indictment acts nor all post-indictment acts receive a categorical classification.

**Proposition-level authority:** Stevens’s Opinion of the Court, this proposition; Stone-Zsela, Blackmun, Stevens, O’Connor, Scalia, Thomas (6 Justices). Direct majority support at the stated level of generality; no Marks aggregation.

**Treatment of earlier authority:**

- Imbler v. Pachtman, 424 U.S. 409: protected advocacy and genuine preparation preserved; ordinary investigation distinguished.
- Burns v. Reed, 500 U.S. 478: function-specific limits applied to investigation.
- Forrester v. White, 484 U.S. 219, and Antoine, June 7, 1993: function and claimant’s burden control; importance to litigation is insufficient.

**Later-authority backlinks:** Fletcher v. Kalina, June 16, 1997, Ninth Circuit No. 95-36129: the functional distinction extends to a prosecutor’s personal arrest-warrant certification while preserving protected advocacy.

##### A press conference does not carry an advocate’s absolute immunity

**Holding:** A prosecutor’s statements to the press announcing an indictment have no absolute prosecutorial immunity merely because they concern a pending prosecution or cause injury through later judicial proceedings. The ordinary qualified-immunity inquiry and the requirement of an actionable constitutional deprivation remain separate.

**Proposition-level authority:** Stevens’s Opinion of the Court, this proposition; Stone-Zsela, White, Blackmun, Stevens, O’Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas (9 Justices). Direct majority support at the stated level of generality; no Marks aggregation.

**Limits and questions not reached:** Allegations are not findings. Genuine advocacy preparation and unreviewed in-court presentation immunity remain; paid or coerced witness allegations require initial treatment below. ## Standards and Tests selection: Parole ineligibility as an answer to future-danger evidence

### Parole ineligibility as an answer to future-danger evidence

**Governing question:** When a capital defendant must be allowed to explain the only lawful alternative to death.

**Current rule:** When the State puts future dangerousness in issue and the only lawful alternative to death is life imprisonment without ordinary parole, due process requires an effective opportunity to inform the jury of that ineligibility, through defense presentation or an adequate instruction. No single verbal formula is required. Speculative clemency does not make ordinary parole available. On direct review, the State must establish beyond a reasonable doubt that denying the explanation did not contribute to the jury’s selection of death; where it cannot, a new lawful penalty proceeding is required if it continues to seek death. The error is not classified as structural.

For federal collateral review, Gardner and Skipper dictated this confined effective-opportunity protection before October 3, 1988; Teague does not bar that claim as new. Independently, even if classified as new, it falls within the watershed exception when future dangerousness is an operative basis for death, the sole lawful noncapital alternative excludes ordinary parole, and no accurate defense presentation or adequate instruction affords an effective opportunity to correct the materially misleading release premise. Neither retroactivity ground establishes the predicates of a violation, its error consequence or other genuinely applicable habeas conditions. Prison dangerousness remains a permissible State argument, without an automatic exception to the information protection.

**Authority by component:**

- Trigger, effective means and error remedy: Simmons v. South Carolina — Parole ineligibility must be available to answer the State's future-danger case; The error is not harmless on this record and requires a new penalty proceeding if death is pursued, 1994-06-17. Part I of Justice Souter's opinion, joined by Chief Justice Stone-Zsela and Justices Blackmun, Stevens, O'Connor, Kennedy and Ginsburg. Seven Justices directly adopt this Fourteenth Amendment rule and its application; no combination of separate rationales is necessary. Part II of Justice Souter's opinion, joined by Chief Justice Stone-Zsela and Justices Blackmun, Stevens and Ginsburg. Five Justices directly adopt this record-specific harmlessness determination and mandatory further-hearing direction. O'Connor and Kennedy join reversal but reserve the further remedial determination.
- Old-rule holding: O'Dell, O'Dell v. Netherland — I. The opportunity to answer this future-danger case was dictated at finality, Stevens with O'Connor, Souter, Ginsburg and Breyer (five); independent watershed alternative: O'Dell v. Netherland — II. Independently, the confined protection satisfies the watershed exception if treated as new, Stevens with Stone-Zsela, Souter, Ginsburg and Breyer (five); separate relief predicates: O'Dell v. Netherland — III. Retroactivity does not by itself establish a present sentencing writ, five-Justice Part III including O'Connor and excluding Stone-Zsela, 1997-06-19.

**Present operation effective:** O'Dell v. Netherland, 1997-06-19, for retroactive availability; Simmons's operative protection and direct-review consequence date from 1994-06-17.

**Open question:** The decision does not establish the instruction requirement when the State has not raised future danger or when the legally available alternatives materially differ.

## Standards and Tests selection: Commercial participation and State consent to federal suit

### Commercial participation and State consent to federal suit

**Governing question:** Whether explicit prospective regulation and continued ordinary commercial activity establish State consent to private federal enforcement.

**Current rule:** A State’s continued operation of an otherwise lawful commercial undertaking is not unequivocal submission to federal jurisdiction merely because Congress clearly and prospectively subjects that activity to private federal enforcement, even if the undertaking is realistically optional. Unmistakable congressional intent remains distinct from the State’s voluntary act of consent; an opportunity to leave ordinary commerce does not itself constitute acceptance of a specifically offered federal benefit or voluntary invocation of judicial authority. A State’s continued commercial participation after Congress authorizes federal patent suit likewise is not consent. Actual consent through a distinct State choice, such as an express waiver or voluntary invocation of federal jurisdiction, remains available.

**Authority by component:**

- College Savings Bank v. Florida Prepaid Postsecondary Education Expense Board — Canonical Decision Record, Holdings, “Continued ordinary commerce is not unequivocal consent to federal suit”, 1999-06-23; Part III with Stone-Zsela, O’Connor, Scalia, Kennedy and Thomas.
- Florida Prepaid Postsecondary Education Expense Board v. College Savings Bank — Canonical Decision Record, Holdings, “Continued commercial participation does not supply consent to this suit”, 1999-06-23; Stevens Part IV, all nine Justices. Applies College Savings Bank Part III to continued operation of the prepaid-tuition program after the express patent remedy took effect; Stevens, Souter, Ginsburg and Breyer accept that holding as controlling.

**Present operation effective:** College Savings Bank and Florida Prepaid, June 23, 1999.

**Exceptions and limits:** Actual voluntary waiver, properly conditioned federal benefits and interstate compacts remain distinct. Neither decision reaches a particular funding or coercion question, litigation-waiver act or governmental-function classification. Hilton’s retained FELA coverage and specialized state-court enforcement remain intact.

**Former-rule note:** The residual Parden inference of federal-forum consent from ordinary commercial participation fails even under the new explicit prospective enactment. This does not wholesale overrule retained substantive FELA coverage or Hilton’s state-court rule.

**Open question:** Whether a State’s litigation conduct—appearance, a §1404(a) transfer request or requests for declarations of invalidity, noninfringement and unenforceability—waives immunity remains undecided. Florida Prepaid need not decide it because its Section 5 and Article I holdings sustain the judgment, and it makes no waiver finding.

**Holdings navigation:** [College Savings Bank v. Florida Prepaid Postsecondary Education Expense Board — Continued ordinary commerce is not unequivocal consent to federal suit](HOLDINGS.md#continued-ordinary-commerce-is-not-unequivocal-consent-to-federal-suit); [Florida Prepaid Postsecondary Education Expense Board v. ## Standards and Tests selection: Article I enforcement against States in federal court

### Article I enforcement against States in federal court

**Governing question:** Whether Congress, acting within Article I, may subject a nonconsenting State to private federal-court enforcement of a valid federal obligation.

**Current rule:** Congress may authorize private enforcement against States when the underlying obligation and enforcement measure fall within its Article I authority and Congress unmistakably authorizes the State’s exposure to suit. The Patent Clause, together with the Necessary and Proper Clause, supports the express federal patent remedy of §§271(h) and 296. The Eleventh Amendment limits the citizen-State diversity grants, not jurisdiction over a federal question; an out-of-State plaintiff’s citizenship does not change the claim’s federal source, and Hans does not establish an immunity Congress cannot displace in this setting. The rule is claim-specific: it removes immunity from the authorized action without finding a violation or an entitlement to relief.

**Authority by component:**

- Florida Prepaid Postsecondary Education Expense Board v. College Savings Bank — Canonical Decision Record, Holdings, “Article I independently sustains the expressly authorized action”, 1999-06-23; Stevens Part III with Stone-Zsela, Souter, Ginsburg and Breyer. Independently sufficient alongside the Section 5 holding.

**Present operation effective:** Florida Prepaid, June 23, 1999.

**Exceptions and limits:** The rule requires a valid enumerated-power obligation, an enforcement measure within that power and unmistakable congressional authorization; enacted clarity alone does not supply power. It does not rest on commercial-participation consent, which College Savings Bank rejects, and decides no actual litigation waiver. Validity, infringement, culpability and each remedy’s statutory and properly presented constitutional limits remain for ordinary adjudication; no compensation, injunction, fee or enhanced award is preapproved. The action is not converted into an officer suit; Young, Coeur d’Alene, Edelman and Pennhurst retain their boundaries.

**Former-rule note:** Union Gas’s retained five-vote constitutional conclusion supplies the foundation; the rule extends that conclusion without treating every proposition in its plurality rationale as binding. Seminole Tribe separated unmistakable statutory authorization from constitutional amenability and set aside the State-dismissal direction without a controlling constitutional rationale; it established no categorical Article I prohibition, overruling of Union Gas or abrogation-before-officer sequence. Florida Prepaid resolves the constitutional question Seminole left open.

**Related current rules:** “Congruent and proportional Fourteenth Amendment enforcement” supplies the independent Section 5 ground for the same patent remedy. “Express state-court FLSA remedies against public employers” separately governs the state-court forum. “Commercial participation and State consent to federal suit” remains intact.

**Holdings navigation:** [Florida Prepaid Postsecondary Education Expense Board v. College Savings Bank — Article I independently sustains the expressly authorized action](HOLDINGS.md#article-i-independently-sustains-the-expressly-authorized-action).

## Standards and Tests selection: ERISA sponsor design, fiduciary implementation and genuine benefit payments

### ERISA sponsor design, fiduciary implementation and genuine benefit payments

**Governing question:** Whether plan amendment or release-conditioned retirement benefits establish a prohibited transaction.

**Current rule:** An employer designing and adopting pension-plan amendments acts as sponsor, not fiduciary. Actual implementation is a separate functional inquiry; party-in-interest status alone does not satisfy §406(a)(1)’s requirement that a fiduciary cause a transaction knowing or having reason to know that it is prohibited, subject to §408’s statutory exceptions. The asset-disposition branch does not acquire an extra discretion requirement. Payment of genuine enhanced retirement benefits under an otherwise lawful plan is not prohibited by §406(a)(1)(D) merely because required employment-related releases benefit the employer. This assumes fiduciary implementation for that classification and creates neither a §408 exemption nor a general net-benefit defense.

Employee contributions do not turn amendment design into fiduciary conduct or make participants co-sponsors. Genuine benefit payments within a continuing single plan do not inure to the employer under §403(c)(1), or become prohibited §406(a)(1)(D) transfers, merely because they reduce employer contributions or payroll costs. Surplus remains plan property. Section 406(b)(2)’s adverse-party prohibition is not triggered merely by sponsor adoption of an advantageous benefit design; independent fiduciary implementation and prohibited transactions retain their actual elements.

**Authority by component:**

- Spink — Part I, sponsor capacity and separate implementation, 1996-06-10; Ginsburg for all nine.
- Spink — Part II, genuine release-conditioned benefit payments, 1996-06-10; Ginsburg, Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O’Connor, Scalia, Kennedy and Thomas (seven); Souter and Breyer reserve the question.

- Contributory-plan sponsor capacity, anti-inurement and genuine-payment boundaries: Canonical Decision Record `hughes_aircraft_co_v_jacobson_merits_1999-01-25.md`, Public Projection / Holdings / I. Genuine payments within the continuing plan do not inure to the employer; II. Amendment design is distinct from fiduciary implementation; V. Incidental employer advantage does not make these benefit payments prohibited transactions, 1999-01-25; nine direct joins to sponsor capacity, eight to the independently stated anti-inurement and transaction grounds.

**Present operation effective:** Hughes Aircraft, 1999-01-25, for the contributory-plan, anti-inurement and §406(b)(2) components; Spink’s existing components remain effective from 1996-06-10.

**Exceptions and limits:** Amendment procedures, participation, funding, vesting, loyalty, anti-inurement and independently applicable §406(b) duties survive. No sham transfer, kickback, unlawful plan or unenforceable release is immunized. Particular release validity under the Older Workers Benefit Protection Act or other law remains open. Surplus does not become employer property.

**Related current rules:** “Corporate procedures for ERISA plan amendments” governs actual authorization and amendment procedure; “Discharge of pension funding obligations as an exchange” governs the distinct property-for-release transaction.

## Standards and Tests selection: Fiduciary benefit communications and deliberate disloyal deception

### Fiduciary benefit communications and deliberate disloyal deception

**Governing question:** When an employer’s benefit communication is ERISA administration and breaches loyalty.

**Current rule:** Fiduciary responsibility under §3(21)(A) follows the particular exercise of discretionary plan administration, including benefit information communicated in that capacity. Authority over administration, the occasion and linked benefit content may establish that function; general employer status, forecasting or participant perception alone does not. An administrator acting in that capacity violates §404(a)(1) by knowingly and significantly misleading participants about benefit security to induce surrender of existing plan interests for the employer’s benefit. Lawful amendment or termination power does not authorize that deliberate disloyal deception.

**Authority by component:**

- Varity — Parts I–II, functional administration and loyalty breach, 1996-03-19; Souter, Stone-Zsela, Stevens, Kennedy, Ginsburg and Breyer (six).

**Present operation effective:** Varity, March 19, 1996.

**Open question:** No general duty to volunteer information, answer every inquiry, disclose every forecast or avoid negligent misstatement is decided; not every corporate prediction is fiduciary administration. The distinct retirees’ assignment determination establishes no new substantive assignment rule.

**Holdings navigation:** Varity Corp. v. Howe, 516 U.S. 489.
