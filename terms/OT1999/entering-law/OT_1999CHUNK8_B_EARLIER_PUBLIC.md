## Bradley v. Arkansas Department of Education — 2000-06-12

## Event

Bradley v. Arkansas Department of Education. Merits decision, June 12, 2000; certiorari granted March 20, 2000; submitted on full merits briefing April 25, 2000. On writ of certiorari to the United States Court of Appeals for the Eighth Circuit, reviewing 189 F.3d 745 (August 31, 1999), appellate No. 98-1010. Review is confined to the Bradley IDEA immunity ruling; the accompanying appellate No. 98-1830, Jim C., is outside the grant.

Arkansas seeks reversal of the denial of immunity to the Department of Education and its employee Mike Crowley. The District Court's November 21, 1997 order in No. LR-C-96-1004 denied dismissal or summary judgment and stayed the merits during the immunity appeal. The Eighth Circuit affirmed the IDEA denial on accepted-funding terms and also discussed prospective officer relief. The questions concern clear federal-suit notice, actual acceptance and the scope of that consent, with independent abrogation grounds unnecessary if consent suffices.

Thomas and Dianna Bradley, on behalf of their son David, allege deficient procedures for review of his individualized education program and failure to perform IDEA responsibilities. The asserted hearing-timing and witness-process failures are allegations, not findings. They seek prospective compliance rather than compensatory or punitive damages.

## Participation

Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer participated at submission and decision.

## Public Action

The Court affirms the denial of sovereign immunity in the granted Bradley IDEA action and remands for ordinary proceedings.

## Judgment & Remedy

**Affirmed and remanded, 9–0.**

**Supporting:** Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer.

The judgment denying sovereign immunity in the Bradley IDEA action is affirmed on the funding-consent ground. The case returns through the Eighth Circuit for ordinary adjudication in the District Court. The Court establishes no violation of a free appropriate public education obligation or hearing requirement and awards no damages, injunction, fees or other relief. The same sovereign-immunity defense asserted for Crowley in official capacity fails within the accepted condition; personal-capacity liability is not decided. No judgment is entered concerning Jim C., Section 504 or other ungranted claims.

## Holdings

**Common authority:** Justice Thomas's Opinion of the Court is joined in full by Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Ginsburg and Breyer. All nine directly adopt the following holding, application and limits.

### Actual acceptance of the disclosed IDEA funding condition supplies consent

**Holding and operative rule:** A State that voluntarily accepts IDEA assistance after the effective express condition in §§1403 and 1415 consents to federal judicial enforcement of the obligations of that funded program, within the recipient, claim and remedy covered by the condition. Arkansas's acceptance under that notice removes sovereign immunity from the Bradley IDEA action, including the same sovereign immunity asserted for the named officer in his official capacity; it supplies neither a general waiver for Arkansas nor consent inferred from ordinary regulated activity.

**Controlling explanation:** The condition identifies the State, the federal forum and violations of IDEA. Section 1415 places federal judicial review among the procedures attached to the funded education program. Arkansas conceded both the spending-power validity of IDEA and its receipt of IDEA funds; its acceptance followed the express immunity condition. The present record supplies no retroactive surprise or coercive demand defeating that acceptance.

Atascadero requires unmistakable notice of federal suit. That requirement is satisfied here. Dellmuth identifies the earlier absence of the necessary statutory clarity; the express provision cures that notice defect. The heading and abrogation phrasing of §1403 do not obscure the consequence that the operative text discloses to a recipient. Congressional intent alone is insufficient, but the State's actual acceptance supplies the additional choice.

College Savings Bank rejects consent inferred from continued ordinary commerce and leaves particular accepted-benefit conditions unresolved. Accepting this identified assistance on disclosed enforcement terms is the different transaction that was absent there. South Dakota v. Dole requires a relationship between the condition and the funded undertaking; enforcing the duties of this very program satisfies that requirement. Cedar Rapids preserves an expressly disclosed IDEA obligation despite substantial compliance costs; cost alone does not erase the condition. None of these decisions dispenses with actual acceptance or establishes a universal waiver from federal funding.

The consent ground resolves the immunity appeal. The Court need not decide independent Article I or Section 5 abrogation. A difference between IDEA's protection and the constitutional minimum does not itself defeat preventive enforcement, as Boerne and Florida Prepaid establish, but neither does it establish IDEA's constitutional validity. No individual constitutional violation is an added prerequisite to proceeding on the consent ground.

**Operative qualifications and limits:** Section 1403(c) applies to violations occurring in whole or in part after October 30, 1990. Section 1403(b) makes legal and equitable remedies available only to the same extent as against another public entity; it creates no additional category of relief. The Bradleys seek prospective compliance, not compensatory or punitive damages. Lane's distinction between substantive coverage and consent to a particular remedy remains applicable; no unrequested damages question is decided.

Section 1415(i)(2) permits an aggrieved party's civil action concerning the presented complaint after the specified administrative decision and appeal route: a party aggrieved under subsection (f) or (k) without a subsection (g) appeal, or a party aggrieved by the administrative appeal decision, may proceed in a competent state court or federal district court without an amount-in-controversy requirement. The court receives the administrative record, hears additional evidence at a party's request, and grants appropriate relief on the preponderance of the evidence. Section 1415(l) preserves rights, procedures and remedies under the Constitution, the Americans with Disabilities Act of 1990, title V of the Rehabilitation Act of 1973, and other federal laws protecting children with disabilities. Before a civil action under those laws seeking relief also available under IDEA is filed, it requires exhaustion of subsections (f) and (g) to the same extent as for an IDEA action. Immunity denial neither finds these predicates satisfied nor excuses them.

The Court decides no educational violation, hearing violation, exhaustion issue, individual liability or particular decree. An independent prospective-officer claim still requires a connected officer, an ongoing federal violation and genuinely prospective relief; a treasury claim for past injury cannot become prospective by naming an employee. The Court creates no new defendant or officer claim. Jim C. and its Section 504 claim are outside the grant; IDEA acceptance establishes no Section 504 waiver. That statute's own federal-suit notice, accepted assistance and remedial scope require separate consideration in a case properly presenting them.

## Law After Decision

Effective June 12, 2000, actual voluntary acceptance of IDEA assistance under the express federal-suit condition supplies program-specific consent. Lower courts must distinguish that acceptance from general federal receipts, ordinary regulated activity and congressional intent alone. This holding adds an application of accepted-benefit consent left unresolved in College Savings; its commercial-consent rule remains unchanged. Consent removes this immunity defense while preserving the statute's temporal and remedial limits and the ordinary prerequisites to relief. Existing Article I and preventive Section 5 law remains effective, without a new IDEA abrogation holding. No law concerning Section 504 immunity is adjudicated.

## Carter v. United States — 2000-06-12

## Event

Carter v. United States, No. 99-5716. Merits decision, June 12, 2000; argued April 19, 2000. On writ of certiorari to the Third Circuit, 185 F.3d 863 (1999), affirming the District of New Jersey conviction under the completed-taking branch of the first paragraph of 18 U.S.C. §2113(a). The question is whether bank larceny under §2113(b) is a necessarily included offense under Federal Rule of Criminal Procedure 31(c).

Carter admitted taking bank money but disputed force or intimidation. He entered the bank on September 9, 1997 wearing a ski mask, crossed the teller counter and fled with approximately $16,000. His contact with a departing customer is disputed: his account describes an accidental bump, while the Government describes a push back into the bank. The indictment alleges approximately $14,000, a distinct amount, in a knowing and willful taking by force, violence and intimidation from a teller's person and presence at an FDIC-insured bank. The jury returned its verdict January 23, 1998; the robbery sentence was 215 months. Separate Pennsylvania larceny convictions and concurrent sentences are outside this review.

The trial court rejected the larceny instruction as categorically unavailable under circuit precedent. Carter's letter asserted a broader larceny theory; the attached charge described the higher grade using the obsolete $100 threshold. The applicable statute uses $1,000. The Court does not rewrite that request or resolve the disputed contact.

## Participation

Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer participated at argument and decision.

## Public Action

The Court affirms the Third Circuit's judgment on the denied bank-larceny instruction.

## Judgment & Remedy

**Affirmed, 5–4.**

**Supporting:** Stone-Zsela, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy and Thomas.
**Opposing:** Stevens, Souter, Ginsburg and Breyer.

The Third Circuit's judgment is affirmed as against the refusal of the requested §2113(b) instruction. Carter's robbery conviction and sentence remain undisturbed on this ground. No acquittal, substituted larceny conviction, sentence reduction or new trial is ordered. Independent issues not before the Court and the separate Pennsylvania convictions are unaffected.

## Holdings

**Authority:** O'Connor's Opinion of the Court is joined throughout by Stone-Zsela, Scalia, Kennedy and Thomas. These five directly support Parts I, II and IV. All nine join Part III's additional-value proposition and its limited subset conclusion. Each independent ground below has its own stated scope; a shared value holding does not resolve basic larceny by itself.

### I. Specific theft intent is an additional larceny element

**Holding and operative rule:** In the completed forcible-or-intimidating taking charged here, §2113(a)'s first paragraph requires knowing conduct satisfying its taking conditions, but does not require §2113(b)'s additional intent to steal or purloin. That additional purpose independently prevents either grade of §2113(b) from being necessarily included under Rule 31(c).

**Controlling explanation:** Schmuck compares statutory elements as legally construed, not the facts that happen to establish both crimes. Section 2113(b) expressly adds a theft purpose to its taking requirement; the charged portion of subsection (a) specifies knowing conduct involving force and violence or intimidation without that additional purpose. The separate entry paragraph expressly requires an intent to commit a qualifying felony or larceny. Its wording cannot be transplanted into the completed-taking branch.

Morissette requires attention to inherited culpability, and Staples and X-Citement Video require the fault appropriate to the enacted offense. They do not require every common-law incident of an analogous offense when the statutory description already identifies culpable conduct. This construction does not punish an innocent accidental taking or remove the knowledge requirement. The claim that removal of feloniously was stylistic and robbery traditionally aggravated larceny cannot overcome the enacted contrast. Bell confirms that this statutory scheme is not simply an unchanged common-law code. The Court construes those descriptions; different wording alone is not a mechanical element test.

### II. Carrying away independently prevents inclusion

**Holding and operative rule:** Section 2113(b) requires taking and carrying away the property, while the charged completed-taking branch of §2113(a) does not require the additional carrying away. That difference independently defeats inclusion of either larceny grade, even if their culpability requirements were assumed to coincide.

**Controlling explanation:** Congress wrote both taking and carrying away in subsection (b), but taking by the stated forcible means in the relevant part of subsection (a). The argument that takes implicitly includes the slight movement associated with common-law larceny is substantial, but it would erase the separately enacted movement requirement in comparing these descriptions. The common-law relationship does not compel identical statutory ingredients. Schmuck requires that each lesser element be necessary to the greater, rather than that the charged conduct ordinarily satisfies both. Carter's actual flight with the money cannot establish that legal necessity. The result rests on construction of the completed offense, not simply on the separate availability of an attempt prosecution.

### III. The higher grade also requires value exceeding $1,000

**Holding and operative rule:** Value exceeding $1,000 is an element of §2113(b)'s grade carrying a ten-year maximum, and is not merely a sentencing factor. Because the charged §2113(a) taking has no value threshold, that higher larceny grade is not itself a statutory subset of robbery.

**Controlling explanation:** The value language occurs in the description of prohibited conduct and is repeated in the separate higher and lower grades. Its role and sharply different maximum punishments support offense-element treatment. Almendarez-Torres, Jones and Castillo supply statute-specific aggravated-element analogues, without creating a universal constitutional rule for every maximum-increasing fact. Carter's original sentencing-factor theory fails under the enacted structure.

The approximately $14,000 allegation and approximately $16,000 trial account do not make a value threshold an element of statutory robbery. Schmuck's comparison does not become an indictment-specific comparison merely because the amount is alleged. The correct threshold is $1,000, following the 1996 amendment; the proposed $100 instruction is not silently corrected. This holding alone does not decide the lower larceny grade, whose inclusion the Court rejects on Parts I and II. The Court does not determine whether the lower paragraph demands affirmative proof that value cannot exceed $1,000.

### IV. The elements failure resolves the requested instruction

**Holding and operative rule:** Because neither larceny grade is necessarily included in the charged completed robbery under the Court's intent and movement constructions, the denied §2113(b) instruction supplies no ground to disturb the conviction. A defendant's request alone does not create a Rule 31(c) exception to those statutory differences.

**Controlling explanation:** The elements test protects charging responsibility, notice and the jury's legally available choices. Carter's admission of taking money and dispute about force cannot change it. Keeble explains why an included alternative may be necessary when a rational jury could convict of the lesser while acquitting of the greater; it does not dispense with statutory inclusion. The Court therefore need not decide the Government's independent rational-jury argument or resolve the disputed physical contact.

The proposed election of a higher grade presupposes an independently included basic theft, which Parts I and II reject. Unilateral consent to notice also does not itself answer Schmuck's reciprocity objection or the independent charging safeguards. Stirone protects the actual grand-jury charge but does not itself decide every defendant-requested grade proposal. Awareness of facts, consent to an instruction, legal sufficiency of the indictment, grand-jury waiver and admission of an offense element remain distinct. No missing value allegation or completed waiver is found. The Court reserves whether the full indictment independently charges another offense and decides no separate charging route.

**Limits common to the judgment:** The decision concerns the charged completed-taking configuration, not every extortion, attempt or entry offense. It decides neither entitlement to another offense instruction nor any independent trial error. Section 2113(a) permits a fine under title 18, imprisonment up to twenty years, or both. Section 2113(b)'s higher grade permits a fine under title 18, imprisonment up to ten years, or both; its lower paragraph specifies a fine no greater than $1,000, imprisonment up to one year, or both. These descriptions create no new sentencing determination in Carter's case.

- **Morissette v. United States, 342 U.S. 246 (1952):** Preserves appropriate culpability and common-law context without importing every common-law ingredient into the distinct statutory descriptions.
- **Staples v. United States; United States v. X-Citement Video:** Apply contextual fault analysis; neither requires the additional theft purpose for this already culpable taking. X-Citement Video's distinction between an existing factual finding and unresolved indictment consequences remains.
- **Bell v. United States, 462 U.S. 356 (1983):** Applies the recognition that bank-larceny coverage is not confined to unchanged common-law larceny; Bell does not itself decide Rule 31(c) inclusion.
- **Jerome v. United States; Prince v. United States:** Preserve their actual statutory settings without using the statute's title or general structure to erase the contrasting completed-taking elements.
- **Almendarez-Torres v. United States; Jones v. United States (March 24, 1999); Castillo v. ## Law After Decision

The Court adds a controlling construction of the completed-taking bank-robbery offense: §2113(b)'s specific theft purpose and carrying away each independently preclude lesser-offense inclusion. The higher-value element separately defeats felony inclusion, with all nine Justices agreeing on that limited proposition. Rule 31(c) remains an elements inquiry, followed by a distinct rational-jury inquiry only when inclusion is established. ## Separate Writings

**Ginsburg, dissenting in part, joined by Stevens, Souter and Breyer; all four join Part III.** The dissent construes the charged completed forcible taking to retain theft intent and movement. Robbery's inherited relation to larceny, the statutory context and the stylistic deletion of feloniously support those legally construed elements. Morissette and the contextual approaches of Staples and X-Citement Video support that reading; Bell's broader theft coverage does not require removing these ingredients from completed robbery. The dissent applies Schmuck's elements test rather than reviving a factual-relationship test.

The four agree that the higher grade adds value exceeding $1,000 and is not a statutory subset. They construe the lower paragraph as the basic theft grade, with a one-year maximum when aggravated value is not established as an offense element. The Government need not prove that the sum could not exceed $1,000: taking more money does not make basic theft innocent. The lower-value language limits unaggravated punishment; proof of a larger amount alone does not authorize the higher maximum without the aggravated offense's requirements.

On that construction basic larceny is included. Carter's letter asserted larceny as the answer to disputed force or intimidation, while the attached charge used a deficient higher-grade formulation. The categorical circuit rule bypassed whether the actual broader request sought a legally available basic instruction. The dissent would vacate that categorical determination and return for request scope, ordinary preservation, treatment of the correct threshold and the Government's independent rational-jury contention. Proof sufficient for robbery does not establish that every rational jury must reject only-theft treatment. The dissent makes no credibility finding and requires no instruction on every unrequested lesser crime.

The dissent reserves both adoption and categorical rejection of a higher-grade election. An affirmative request and the indictment's actual amount allegation are material, but do not alone settle Schmuck's reciprocity concern or all charging consequences. The limited grade-election proposal must be distinguished from factual relatedness and supported under the actual charge and any legally necessary consent or waiver. Almendarez-Torres, Jones and Castillo preserve the distinction between factual awareness and the proper aggravated charge; X-Citement Video preserves indictment questions despite an existing culpability finding. Those decisions do not forbid every valid defendant election or establish a missing allegation here. Stirone likewise does not by itself resolve this proposal. No grand-jury waiver, element admission or new charge is inferred.

The proposed return could still end in denial on a lawful request or evidentiary ground. Error and remedy follow only if an included, properly requested and evidence-supported instruction should have been given. The dissent orders neither acquittal, entry of a larceny conviction, sentence reduction nor an automatic new trial and finds neither harmfulness nor harmlessness.

## Pegram v. Herdrich — 2000-06-12

## Event

Pegram v. Herdrich, No. 98-1949. Merits decision, June 12, 2000; argued February 23, 2000. On writ of certiorari to the Seventh Circuit, 154 F.3d 362 (1998), reviewing its revival of an amended ERISA fiduciary-breach count. The issue is whether the challenged HMO treatment and incentive functions support that count. The posture is failure to state a claim, not findings after an ERISA trial.

Herdrich participated through her husband's State Farm employment in an HMO option. HAMP was owned through Carle Clinic, whose physician shareholders supplied care. Medical necessity and outside referrals depended on Carle physicians' judgment; the alleged incentives rewarded lower testing, outside-facility use and referrals. Herdrich alleged that an ultrasound was delayed after discovery of an inflamed abdominal mass and that she was required to travel to a distant Carle facility. Her appendix ruptured, causing peritonitis. Her malpractice claims produced a $35,000 jury judgment, which is outside this review.

The reviewed amended count seeks plan-focused relief under §§409 and 502(a)(2) for the general incentive arrangement and refers to administration of disputed or nonroutine claims. It names no individual physician. Two state fraud counts preceded removal; one was dismissed and the other amended into this ERISA count. The superseded fraud pleading is not an independently presented nondisclosure claim.

## Participation

Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer participated at argument and decision.

## Public Action

The Court reverses revival of the amended ERISA fiduciary count and directs its dismissal on remand.

## Judgment & Remedy

**Reversed and remanded, 9–0.**

**Supporting:** Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer.

The Seventh Circuit's revival of the reviewed amended fiduciary-breach count is reversed. On remand that count shall be dismissed. The $35,000 malpractice judgment and unreviewed counts remain undisturbed. No damages, plan restoration amount, disgorgement, fee award, new individual-physician claim or entitlement to amend is ordered.

| Concurrence in part and in the judgment | Stone-Zsela | No other Justice | Joins Part I and reversal with dismissal of the amended count | Mixed-treatment ground; reserves independent administration, misrepresentation and asset-misuse questions rather than joining Parts II–III |

## Holdings

### I. Inseparable treatment decisions are not fiduciary administration

**Holding and operative rule:** A treating physician does not act as an ERISA fiduciary when making an inseparable decision about medically appropriate treatment and the benefit delivered through that treatment. A separate discretionary decision applying plan-coverage terms can be fiduciary administration, whoever makes it; the rule creates no general HMO exemption and does not exclude every decision involving medical information.

**Authority:** Part I of Ginsburg's Opinion of the Court, joined by all eight other Justices, supplies nine direct votes.

**Controlling explanation:** Section 3(21)(A) attaches fiduciary status to the specified function, rather than every act of a person who performs some plan duties. Varity applies that inquiry to actual administration and protects participants from disloyal administrative conduct. It does not convert medical judgment into plan administration simply because treatment also determines the benefit furnished. The medical-necessity and referral decisions inseparable from furnishing the care here belong to the treatment function. HMO incentives do not, merely by their existence, change that capacity.

Herdrich correctly distinguishes the employer's choice of a plan from a nonsponsor's later acts. Spink and Hughes Aircraft preserve independent fiduciary implementation. The present boundary therefore depends on the act, not employer or corporate status. A physician can administer coverage, and calling a decision medical cannot conceal that distinct function. Conversely, financial consequences do not turn ordinary treatment into ERISA administration. Travelers preserves the distinct setting of ordinary medical regulation; this holding resolves fiduciary status and enters no general malpractice-preemption judgment.

### II. An ordinary incentive arrangement alone establishes neither fiduciary breach nor plan assets

**Holding and operative rule:** Establishing or carrying out an ordinary general compensation and risk-sharing arrangement for providing medical care does not, by that fact alone, establish ERISA fiduciary conduct or breach. The HMO itself is not the employer's ERISA plan, and its revenues and profits are not automatically plan assets; actual authority or control over management or disposition of a plan asset remains governed by the statutory asset branch without an additional discretion requirement.

**Authority:** Part II has eight direct supporters: Ginsburg, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas and Breyer. Stone-Zsela does not join.

**Controlling explanation:** John Hancock requires separate consideration of property, function and liability. The premise that the business furnishing the benefit is itself the plan would improperly make its entire income subject to plan restoration. No general ownership rule governing every premium arrangement follows from rejecting that premise.

Spink separates design from implementation, and Hughes Aircraft examines independently alleged breaches instead of treating economic advantage as breach by itself. The same distinctions matter here. Ordinary medical risk sharing compensates for the service arrangement; it does not alone establish the exercise of an ERISA function or a disloyal act in such a function. Physician ownership does not erase the statutory requirements. This is neither a general net-benefit defense to proved misconduct nor approval of every incentive system. A direct reward for denying a claim, an arrangement making impartial administration impossible, genuine asset diversion and actual disloyal administration present different predicates, none adjudicated by the pleaded general-arrangement theory.

### III. The amended structural theory fails even assuming genuine claims administration

**Holding and operative rule:** Even assuming in Herdrich's favor that the amended reference to disputed and nonroutine claims describes genuine discretionary plan administration, the pleaded general year-end incentive arrangement alone states no independently disloyal claims determination, administration contrary to the plan or direct reward for denying a claim. The reviewed amended count therefore fails on that independent ground, and restoration of supplemental medical-expense payments cannot follow merely by labeling the entire HMO the plan.

**Authority:** Part III is joined by the same eight Associates as Part II and independently supports dismissal. Stone-Zsela joins the judgment on Part I's treatment ground, not this additional rationale.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court gives the administration allegation its asserted scope for this inquiry. It does not find a new administrative event or erase that allegation by treating every HMO decision as treatment. Varity preserves concrete administrative loyalty duties, while Hughes Aircraft requires the alleged implementation theory itself to identify a breach after its legal predicates are examined. The ordinary structural incentive does not alone supply that missing breach.

The strongest contrary argument combines owner control, decision authority and the incentive into self-interested administration. Those circumstances may matter when an actual disloyal determination is alleged, but they do not establish that the ordinary arrangement itself violates the duty or that all business funds belong to the plan. The lower court disclaimed automatic liability for all incentives and characterized care as withheld solely to enlarge bonuses. That characterization and the malpractice verdict are not independent findings of disloyal plan administration or plan loss. The Court applies the ordinary pleading standard and resolves no disputed medical-adequacy question.

Section 409's responsibility and plan-restoration framework and §502(a)(2)'s authorized enforcement channel remain distinct from §502(a)(3)'s individual equitable channel. Mertens's limit on §502(a)(3) compensation is not the ground for dismissal of this §409/§502(a)(2) claim. The requested plan remedy fails on the pleaded predicates, not a categorical rule barring lawful plan restoration.

**Limits and questions not reached:** Section 3(21)(A) separately reaches discretionary plan management, authority or control over management or disposition of plan assets, compensated investment advice or authority or responsibility to provide it, and discretionary authority or responsibility in plan administration. It also includes persons designated under §405(c)(1)(B). Its registered-investment-company qualification provides that plan investment in a registered company's securities alone does not make the company, adviser or principal underwriter a fiduciary or party in interest, except insofar as the company, its investment adviser or its principal underwriter acts in connection with a plan covering employees of the company, adviser or principal underwriter; duties under other law remain. That qualification is not a factual issue here.

No independent disclosure, benefits-reimbursement, contract, malpractice-preemption, regulatory, personal-physician-liability or particular asset-ownership claim is decided. Prior statements about administrator or fiduciary status elsewhere do not establish the challenged function or a supported judicial-estoppel bar here; disclosure responsibility and responsibility for the disclosed incentive are distinct. The amended count does not revive superseded fraud allegations. Direct per-denied-claim rewards, actual asset misuse, misrepresentations and genuinely disloyal administration retain their own statutory predicates and remedies. The Court awards no plan amount and establishes no fee or amendment entitlement.

## Precedent Treatment

- **Varity Corp. v. Howe:** Applies the function-specific inquiry and preserves concrete fiduciary administration and disloyal-misrepresentation liability; its individual equitable-redress holding does not convert this plan-focused claim into a different remedy channel.
- **Lockheed Corp. v. Spink:** Applies the separation of design and actual implementation without immunizing independent administration; sponsor status alone does not decide this claim.
- **Hughes Aircraft Co. v. Jacobson:** Applies its independent examination of implementation and breach predicates rather than equating an advantageous structure with misconduct. Its actual-asset and genuine-administration reservations remain.
- **John Hancock Mutual Life Insurance Co. v. Harris Trust & Savings Bank:** Applies separate inquiries into asset status, function and breach, rather than equating business funds with plan property.
- **Firestone Tire & Rubber Co. v. Bruch:** Preserves trust-law guidance within the enacted fiduciary functions; discretion over treatment alone is not discretion in plan administration.
- **Mertens v. Hewitt Associates:** Preserves §502(a)(3)'s traditional-equity limits but does not bar lawful §409 restoration or supply the reason for dismissing this §502(a)(2) claim.
- **New York State Conference of Blue Cross & Blue Shield Plans v. Travelers Insurance Co.; De Buono v. NYSA-ILA Medical and Clinical Services Fund:** Preserve the distinct operation of ordinary medical regulation; the Court makes no general preemption ruling here.

## Law After Decision

Medical treatment inseparable from the benefit furnished is outside the fiduciary-administration function, while genuine coverage administration remains subject to ERISA. The eight-Justice alternative separately requires an independently supported breach even when administration is assumed; the ordinary general incentive alone does not supply it. HMO form supplies neither fiduciary status for treatment nor immunity for administration. Function, actual plan property, breach and statutory relief remain separate inquiries.

## Separate Writings

**Stone-Zsela, concurring in part and in the judgment.** He joins the function-specific rule in Part I and the reversal directing dismissal of this amended fiduciary claim. His ground concerns treatment decisions inseparable from the benefit delivered through treatment; payment incentives do not turn that medical function into ERISA administration. He does not join the additional business/asset rationale or the independent no-breach determination that assumes genuine administration in Parts II–III. His position leaves pure administrative decisions, fiduciary misrepresentations, plan-asset misuse and viable malpractice, disclosure, contract and regulatory theories to their own requirements. That reservation supplies no disagreement with the dismissal or new adjudication of those distinct theories.

## Procedure After Action

The case returns to the lower courts for dismissal of the reviewed amended ERISA fiduciary count. The existing $35,000 malpractice judgment and matters outside this review remain undisturbed. The mandate imposes no new medical, disclosure, regulatory or plan-payment duty and grants no separate right to amend.

## Reeves v. Sanderson Plumbing Products, Inc — 2000-06-12

## Event

Reeves v. Sanderson Plumbing Products, Inc., No. 99-536. Merits decision, June 12, 2000; argued March 21, 2000. On writ of certiorari to the Fifth Circuit, 197 F.3d 688 (1999), which displaced a jury's age-discrimination verdict on Rule 50 sufficiency grounds. The questions concern the inference from prima facie proof and disbelief of the employer's stated reason, and the proper examination of the trial record on judgment as a matter of law.

Reeves was fifty-seven and had worked for Sanderson for forty years. He supervised a regular production line in the Hinge Room. Department manager Caldwell was forty-five; Oswalt, in his thirties, supervised another line. After an audit, the company asserted inaccurate attendance and time records. Manufacturing director Chesnut, the company president's spouse, joined the recommendation to discharge Reeves and Caldwell. Oswalt had already left; the employer's assertion that it would also have discharged him is not an actual discharge.

Reeves disputed the recordkeeping accusations through testimony and documents and offered age-directed comments, differential treatment and evidence of Chesnut's dominant influence. The company disputes that evidence's accuracy, timing, meaning and connection to discharge and relies on other participants and older managers. The jury found age discrimination and willfulness. The District Court awarded $35,000 and matching liquidated damages and later $28,490.80 in front pay. The employer made and renewed Rule 50 motions and alternatively requested a new trial. The Court reviews the sufficiency ruling, not a new calculation of these awards.

## Participation

Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer participated at argument and decision.

## Public Action

The Court reverses the Fifth Circuit's judgment for the employer and remands within the verdict-preserving limits stated below.

## Judgment & Remedy

**Reversed and remanded, 9–0.**

**Supporting:** Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer.

The Fifth Circuit's Rule 50 judgment for the employer is reversed. The trial evidence is legally sufficient to support the age-discrimination verdict; the verdict is to stand unless an ordinary, properly preserved and verdict-compatible independent ground warrants further relief. The remand permits consideration of remaining appellate issues, including an unresolved alternative new-trial ground if otherwise reviewable. It does not reopen the sufficiency question decided here, direct a particular new trial or make a new monetary, liquidated-damages or front-pay award.

## Holdings

**Common authority:** Justice Scalia's Opinion of the Court is joined in full by the other eight Justices identified in Opinion Topology. All nine directly support each rule, application and remedial qualification below.

### I. Prima facie proof and material falsity can support discrimination

**Holding and operative rule:** A prima facie case together with evidence permitting rejection of the employer's material explanation may support a finding of intentional discrimination without an additional category of direct or independent proof. It neither compels liability nor invariably defeats judgment as a matter of law: the strength of the initial inference, the probative force of falsity and the rest of the evidence the court must credit determine whether discrimination is a reasonable inference.

**Controlling explanation:** Hicks already rejects both automatic liability and a demand for a special further kind of evidence. Aikens recognizes that the employer's actual motivation can be proved circumstantially. Once the employer produces a nondiscriminatory account, Burdine leaves the ultimate burden of persuasion with the plaintiff; rejecting the account does not revive a compulsory presumption. But a factfinder may reasonably regard an invented account as evidence concealing discrimination, considered with the initial showing and the whole record.

Hazen Paper requires age actually to motivate the decision. Falsehood, unfairness, personal hostility or another unlawful motive is not automatically age discrimination. A conclusively established different lawful reason can defeat the proposed inference. So can weak falsity evidence together with otherwise conclusive independent evidence against discrimination. These are applications of ordinary sufficiency, not a checklist demanding more evidence in every case. Merely imagining another possible motive does not defeat an inference reasonably supported by the actual proof. Substantial prima facie and falsity evidence can therefore sustain liability without a separate evidentiary category. The Court does not define every insufficient future case, alter an unpresented mixed-motive allocation or decide disparate impact.

### II. Rule 50 preserves reasonable jury inferences while considering the whole record

**Holding and operative rule:** Rule 50 requires consideration of the whole record, drawing reasonable inferences for the verdict winner and making no credibility choices or weighing of evidence. The court disregards evidence favorable to the movant that the jury was free to disbelieve, while considering uncontradicted and unimpeached evidence the jury was required to accept; testimony from disinterested witnesses is a relevant circumstance, not an inflexible witness-label rule.

**Controlling explanation:** Anderson supplies the distinction between determining whether a reasonable jury can find for a party and deciding which evidence is more persuasive. A court cannot displace the verdict by crediting disputed employer testimony or selecting among reasonable accounts. It also cannot exclude every item simply because the employer introduced it: source alone does not determine whether the jury was free to reject the evidence.

Weisgram preserves appellate authority to direct judgment on a properly preserved Rule 50 motion when the admissible record is insufficient, including after correction of an evidentiary admission. That authority is not permission to reweigh a valid record. Evidence conclusively establishing a lawful explanation remains material, but disputed testimony and reasonable contrary inferences must receive the treatment Rule 50 requires. The Court uses the ordinary sufficiency standard rather than either complete deference to all verdicts or categorical invisibility of movant evidence.

### III. This record supports the verdict and cannot be rejected for lack of extra proof

**Holding and operative rule:** Reeves's prima facie showing, material evidence rebutting the recordkeeping explanation, and evidence connecting age-directed comments to Chesnut's role and influence permit a reasonable finding of intentional age discrimination. The employer is not entitled to judgment as a matter of law on this record, and the remand cannot restore the same sufficiency judgment through a renewed demand for additional proof.

**Controlling explanation:** Reeves's testimony and documents addressed the actual attendance practices, time-clock use and disciplinary records. The jury could find the employer's account materially unworthy of belief. The age-directed comments were accompanied by evidence of Chesnut's participation in the recommendation and influence within the company. The question is their connection to the decision in the complete record, not whether a label excludes them from consideration.

The contrary accounts about timing, other decisionmakers and older employees do not conclusively defeat that inference. Crediting those accounts as decisive would require the weighing Rule 50 forbids. Oswalt's earlier departure cannot be treated as a completed discharge of a younger comparator; the claimed hypothetical action remains the employer's assertion. Consolidated Coin keeps relative-age evidence tied to the actual inference rather than making protected-group membership dispositive. The combined evidence supports the jury's age finding under Hazen Paper, not merely a conclusion that the dismissal was unfair.

This is a determination of the actual evidence's legal sufficiency, not a rule that every pretext case succeeds. Weisgram preserves genuinely independent, properly presented new-trial and remedial issues. Any such issue must be addressed on its own basis and consistently with the sufficiency holding. The Court neither assumes that an alternative ground is preserved and meritorious nor prescribes a new trial or award. Willfulness and the existing monetary components receive no new determination merely from this reversal.

**Limits:** Section 623(a)(1) concerns covered employment action because of age; §631(a) begins the Act's age protection at forty. The Court does not decide every ADEA proof framework or impose an under-forty comparator requirement. Employer production, plaintiff persuasion, evidentiary sufficiency and remaining remedial questions remain distinct. No liability follows merely from pension status, a mistaken business judgment, personal animosity or any false explanation.

## Precedent Treatment

- **St. Mary's Honor Center v. Hicks:** Applies its existing rejection of both compulsory liability from pretext alone and a mandatory further category of proof. No replacement persuasion allocation or revived presumption is adopted.
- **Texas Department of Community Affairs v. Burdine; McDonnell Douglas Corp. v. Green:** Preserve employer production and ultimate plaintiff persuasion under the assumed indirect framework; disbelief has evidentiary force without shifting that burden.
- **Postal Service Board of Governors v. Aikens:** Applies the focus on actual discrimination and the availability of circumstantial proof.
- **Hazen Paper Co. v. Biggins:** Applies actual age motivation as the liability question; falsehood or another improper motive is not by itself age discrimination. Its willfulness and reserved disparate-impact issues are not redecided.
- **O'Connor v. Consolidated Coin Caterers Corp.:** Preserves the evidentiary significance of relative age without an under-forty replacement condition or a categorical result from workforce ages.
- **Anderson v. Liberty Lobby, Inc.:** Applies the prohibition on weighing and credibility choices in determining whether a reasonable factfinder may rule for the protected party.
- **Weisgram v. Marley Co.; Neely v. Martin K. Eby Construction Co.:** Preserve lawful appellate Rule 50 authority and proper consideration of independently supported new-trial grounds. Neither permits reweighing this sufficient record or guarantees a new trial on remand.

## Law After Decision

Hicks's permissive inference governs ordinary discrimination proof without a special additional-evidence requirement. Courts evaluate its strength in the whole record under Rule 50, keeping ultimate persuasion with the plaintiff and preserving truly conclusive contrary proof. The Court adds a controlling application sustaining this verdict's evidentiary basis. Remaining independent appellate grounds may be considered within ordinary preservation and remedy limits; they cannot recreate the rejected sufficiency demand.
