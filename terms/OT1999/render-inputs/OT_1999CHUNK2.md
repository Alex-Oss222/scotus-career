## Event

Adarand Constructors, Inc. v. Slater, No. 99-295. Summary merits disposition on petition for a writ of certiorari to the United States Court of Appeals for the Tenth Circuit; decided January 12, 2000. ## Holdings

### The uncertain certificate does not end this established competitive controversy

**Holding and operative rule:** When an able and ready bidder with concrete continuing exposure challenges unequal competitive treatment, the party relying on postfiling cessation must adequately establish that the challenged disadvantage will not reasonably recur. Adarand's state certification does not carry that showing because its federal acceptance and durable effect remain unsettled; this conclusion does not relax initial standing or stage-appropriate proof, and a supported durable elimination of prospective disadvantage could require a different mootness determination.

**Authority:** The per curiam opinion, joined in full by all nine participating Justices identified in Opinion Topology, supplies a direct unanimous holding on the threshold question.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that the certificate does not establish an end to the controversy. Adarand v. Peña recognizes injury to equal competitive opportunity without requiring proof of a lost contract and requires concrete renewed exposure for prospective relief. Adarand's repeated bidding supplies that exposure. Northeastern Florida distinguishes a change that actually ends the challenged exclusion from one that leaves it in place; City of Mesquite supplies the continuing voluntary-cessation principle. Lesage keeps injury distinct from entitlement to particular relief.

Colorado accepted self-certification under its changed procedure, but the federal government has not approved that procedure or endorsed this certification. It retains review if a third-party challenge reaches DOT, and DOT may require withdrawal proceedings upon reasonable cause to believe federal eligibility criteria are unmet. The government's reliance on existing certification therefore leaves the certificate's federal effect unresolved. The conclusion rests on these combined facts and continuing bidding exposure, not on a rule that every reviewable certificate creates permanent jurisdiction or that withdrawal is inevitable. No actual third-party challenge, withdrawal order or present ineligibility is found.

The appellate mootness judgment must be reversed. That result removes mootness as the ground for terminating this action and vacating the favorable district judgment; it does not affirm that judgment's constitutional reasoning or automatically reinstate all disputed relief. The remaining preserved issues and the decree's lawful effect return for ordinary proceedings under the June 12, 1995 mandate. That mandate requires application of the common substantial-justification standard and did not finally uphold the program. The Court orders no new complaint, injunction, award, program redesign or special evidentiary procedure.

**Precedent treatment:** Peña's competitive-injury holding and bounded remand govern; Northeastern Florida and City of Mesquite supply the continuing-injury and cessation distinction; Lesage preserves separate remedial predicates. None is changed.

## Event

Martinez v. Court of Appeal of California, Fourth Appellate District, No. 98-7809. On writ of certiorari to the Supreme Court of California; argued November 9, 1999; decided January 12, 2000. Martinez represented himself at trial, was convicted of embezzlement and filed a timely direct appeal with a motion to represent himself and a waiver of counsel. The Court of Appeal denied the motion, and the California Supreme Court denied his application for a writ of mandate. The question is whether the Sixth or Fourteenth Amendment requires California to permit him to discharge appointed counsel and conduct his first appeal as of right personally. No conviction or sentencing issue is before the Court.

### Trial self-representation does not establish an appellate Sixth Amendment right

**Holding and operative rule:** Faretta's right to conduct one's own defense at trial does not give a convicted defendant a Sixth Amendment right to discharge appointed counsel and personally conduct a first appeal as of right. Faretta remains fully effective at trial; this decision does not diminish an existing trial choice because a judge thinks counsel would serve the defendant better.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that the trial and appellate questions have different constitutional settings. Faretta protects the accused's personal conduct of the defense in the proceeding determining guilt. An appeal instead examines a completed record through legal claims and the procedures for reviewing alleged error. Price distinguishes permission to participate in appellate proceedings from a constitutional entitlement to conduct them personally. The importance of the appellant's liberty and preference for personal control does not make the Sixth Amendment trial guarantee coextensive with every later review proceeding.

The Court does not question Faretta's trial holding or find Martinez unable to represent himself. Godinez separates competence to choose from an actual knowing, intelligent and voluntary waiver when that choice is legally available; capacity to waive cannot create an appellate entitlement absent from the governing right. Hill concerns a confined scheduling agreement and likewise supplies no rule that counsel controls every basic personal choice. Because the asserted Sixth Amendment entitlement does not extend to this appeal, California's refusal to discharge counsel violates no such right.

**Precedent treatment:** Faretta is distinguished by proceeding and preserved at trial; Price's appellate-participation distinction is applied. Godinez's competence/actual-waiver separation and Hill's bounded scheduling rule remain intact.

### Fair first-appeal access permits represented advocacy

**Holding and operative rule:** The Fourteenth Amendment's guarantees of meaningful access and effective assistance on a first appeal as of right do not confer a personal right to reject appointed counsel and conduct that appeal entirely without counsel. A State may require represented advocacy while remaining free to allow self-representation or supplemental personal submissions; this decision requires neither option and resolves no independent competence, conflict, ineffective-assistance or access-to-record claim.

**Controlling explanation:** Douglas and Evitts require fair appellate access and effective assistance when a State provides a first appeal as of right. Those protections do not create a converse entitlement to reject the representative through whom the State provides meaningful review. The appellant's autonomy interest is substantial, but the State may structure its record-based error review through represented advocacy. That conclusion does not rest on a prediction that Martinez would perform poorly, an empirical claim that every lawyer outperforms every litigant, or a premise that conviction extinguishes all personal constitutional decisions.

California may therefore deny this request to displace appointed counsel. Martinez's asserted right is resolved without relying on an unestablished claim that counsel already filed a brief or that supplemental submissions were actually available to him. The California Supreme Court's denial of mandate is affirmed, appointed counsel remains in place, and the direct appeal proceeds under lawful state procedures. No compulsory supplemental-brief process, new waiver colloquy or substitute appointment is ordered. Whether another defect in representation or access exists is outside the question decided.

**Precedent treatment:** Douglas and Evitts are applied within their fair-access and effective-assistance scope, without treating the assistance guarantee as a compulsory personal-advocacy option. Price supports the appellate distinction; Faretta's trial rule is unchanged.

## Event

Reno v. Condon, No. 98-1464. On writ of certiorari to the United States Court of Appeals for the Fourth Circuit; argued November 10, 1999; decided January 12, 2000. South Carolina and its Attorney General challenged the Driver's Privacy Protection Act. The District Court, 972 F. Supp. 977, granted the State summary judgment and permanently enjoined enforcement against the State and its officers; the Fourth Circuit, 155 F.3d 453, affirmed on structural federalism grounds. ### Congress may regulate this information-distribution market

**Holding and operative rule:** Congress may regulate disclosure and redistribution of the DPPA's defined personal motor-vehicle information because the enacted distribution class participates in the supported interstate information market. The source-disclosure and private-redisclosure rules reasonably fit that class; Congress need not show that each release is compensated, interstate or independently substantial, but this holding creates no power over every state record or local activity through generalized privacy-related economic consequences.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that the regulated activity has an identified interstate connection. States sell motor-vehicle information; private recipients resell and redistribute it; interstate insurers, manufacturers and marketers use it in solicitations, and public and private entities use it in interstate motoring. The statute regulates disclosure and further distribution of that information itself. Lopez requires the enacted class and its interstate mechanism to be identified before aggregate magnitude is considered. Pic-A-State distinguishes actual commercial information transmission from remote economic consequences, and Robinson likewise requires a concrete commercial connection. Home Builders requires material support and a reasonable fit to the class actually enacted.

Those requirements are met here. South Carolina emphasizes governmental collection of the records and coverage of releases that are not paid interstate transactions. But the class is information distribution, including its public source and private subsequent handlers, rather than local licensing as a whole. Uncompensated and intrastate releases supply the same information into the identified distribution class; separating those modes solely by price or state boundary would not change the nature of the regulated activity. The connection does not rest on an object's distant history or a chain from privacy harm to national productivity.

The Court does not redefine all licensing, information possession or every state database as commerce. It inserts no commerce element and decides no materially different transaction-specific challenge. The documented market ground suffices without a new interstate-externality rule, generalized necessary-and-proper holding, or constitutional information-privacy right. The statute's actual definitions, mandatory disclosures, permitted uses and consent routes retain their separate terms and effective dates.

**Precedent treatment:** Lopez's connection-before-magnitude rule, Pic-A-State's actual-transmission distinction, Robinson's concrete-market requirement and Home Builders' enacted-class and evidentiary-fit rules are applied without alteration.

### Direct regulation of state disclosure is not compelled government of others

**Holding and operative rule:** A valid federal law may directly regulate a State's own handling and disclosure of motor-vehicle information and private recipients' redistribution of that information without compelling state legislation or assigning state officers a federal regulatory or coercive-enforcement program against unrelated private persons. The personnel work needed to comply does not itself transform the DPPA into an impermissible command; whether general applicability is constitutionally indispensable or Congress could regulate States alone remains undecided because this scheme also regulates private handlers.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that the DPPA regulates South Carolina as a holder and discloser of information. Garcia preserves otherwise valid federal regulation of state activity, and South Carolina v. Baker distinguishes compliance in the State's own activity from a duty to regulate other persons. New York preserves that distinction while forbidding the compulsory choice between governing private conduct under federal instructions and assuming ownership and liability for private obligations.

South Carolina's licensing function and its need to screen requests and use personnel do not change the allocation of responsibility. Every direct regulation can require administrative compliance. Treating that work alone as compulsory government of others would erase the field Garcia and Baker retain. The DPPA commands no state legislature to enact a program and transfers no general federal enforcement responsibility against private recipients to state officers. Its direct duty remains supported by the commerce connection separately established above.

Printz/Mack concerns a different arrangement: confined temporary records assistance under an affirmative command while federal law supplies the substantive transaction rule and federal authorities retain coercive responsibility. Its limited permission and unresolved fiscal and unauthorized-expenditure objections are neither extended nor resolved here. This decision authorizes no general permanent policy administration, field investigation or command to regulate others, and creates no universal compensation condition for direct regulation. The separate privacy, speech, immunity and sanction questions retain their own legal predicates.

**Precedent treatment:** Garcia and Baker's direct-state-activity ground is applied; New York's prohibition and preserved field remain intact. Printz/Mack is distinguished at its actual source, function and fiscal limits rather than expanded into a general rule about all directions to state officers.

### Statutory scope retained

The DPPA governs personal information obtained in connection with a state motor-vehicle permit, title, registration or identification-card record. The definition includes identifying information such as a photograph, Social Security or driver-identification number, name, address other than the five-digit ZIP code, telephone number, and medical or disability information; accident information, driving violations and driver status are excluded. The general restriction is on knowing disclosure or making that information available.

The Act's mandatory motor-vehicle-purpose disclosures are distinct from its permitted uses and consent alternatives. The mandatory provision covers motor-vehicle or driver safety and theft, emissions, product alterations, recalls and advisories, manufacturer performance monitoring, and removal of nonowner manufacturer records to carry out the identified federal motor-vehicle statutes. Among the permitted uses, each retaining its statutory limits, are: governmental functions; vehicle safety; verification of information submitted to a business, with fraud-prevention and legal-remedy limits; court, agency and self-regulatory proceedings; research without further disclosure or contact; insurance claims, antifraud, rating and underwriting; towing or impoundment notice; licensed investigation or security for an otherwise permitted purpose; private toll transportation; and state-authorized motor-vehicle or public-safety purposes. The Court adjudicates no particular permitted-use claim.

Original consent routes and the enacted amendments operate only according to their respective effective provisions. Under the original scheme, general driver consent permits broader redistribution, while information obtained for a permitted use may be redisclosed for a permitted use; information obtained specifically for direct marketing may be resold for direct marketing, not unrelated purposes. The private resale and redisclosure provisions retain their five-year recipient-and-permitted-purpose record requirement. Those distinct routes are not collapsed into unconditional disclosure authority.

States and state agencies are excluded from the defined person category used by the separate person sanctions. Knowing prohibited acquisition or disclosure and false representation to obtain information have their own statutory predicates; the driver's civil action for knowing acquisition, disclosure or use for an impermissible purpose is separate from criminal fines. The Attorney General's distinct agency penalty requires a policy or practice of substantial noncompliance and is limited to $5,000 per day of substantial noncompliance. Rejection of the facial challenge establishes no violation or sanction liability.

Fraternal Order of Police v. United States; D.C. Circuit No. 97-5304; judgment below, 173 F.3d 898. Plenary merits decision, January 18, 2000. Review granted October 12, 1999; submitted December 13, 1999. On review of the D.C. Circuit return judgment after the October 7, 1998 mandate. The granted questions concern the preserved Commerce Clause and anti-commandeering objections to section 922(g)(9); the already decided standing, official-use arms and equal-protection questions are not reopened.

## Opinion Topology

## Holdings

**Common authority:** Justice O'Connor delivers the opinion of the Court, joined in full by Chief Justice Stone-Zsela and Justices Stevens, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer. All nine adopt both holdings and the bounded affirmance.

### The recipient possession restriction fits the valid federal supply prohibition

**Holding and operative rule:** Section 922(g)(9)'s restriction on possession by persons with qualifying misdemeanor convictions for domestic violence is within Congress's Commerce and Necessary and Proper Clause authority because it is a fitted anti-evasion and anti-impairment component of the valid prohibition on supplying firearms and ammunition to that class. This prospective associational judgment establishes no individual's commerce connection; the Government must prove the applicable enacted commerce element in an actual prosecution.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that Hiley supplies the constitutional ground for this facial challenge. A prohibition on supplying firearms to the defined recipients would be materially impaired if their complementary acquisition and retention were outside the fitted prohibition. The relationship between the possession rule and the valid supply restriction, rather than a firearm's remote passage across a border, supplies the necessary connection. Lopez requires that connection to be identified before generalized economic effects can establish federal authority. Hale and Kirk preserve Congress's authority to include intrastate conduct necessary to an identified valid regime; they do not create a general possession power.

The association objects that local possession is not economic activity and that the lower court treated the statutory jurisdictional words as sufficient by themselves. The latter shortcut is unnecessary and is not adopted. Hiley addresses this very recipient-and-supply relationship without relying on generalized domestic-violence costs. The association's uniform prospective claim fails under that rule; it is not an individual prosecution needing a finding that a particular firearm crossed a border.

Section 922(g) separately addresses shipment or transportation in interstate or foreign commerce, possession in or affecting commerce, and receipt of an item shipped or transported in interstate or foreign commerce. Those enacted elements retain their force. The Court neither inserts a different nexus nor imports section 922(d)(9)'s knowledge-or-reasonable-cause supply element into the possession offense. No individual's culpability, qualifying conviction, restoration entitlement or liability is decided.

### A substantive firearm disability does not assign the State a federal enforcement program

**Holding and operative rule:** Applying section 922(g)(9) to state and local officers does not commandeer state government where the federal law directly restricts possession and requires no state legislation, termination, investigation, prosecution, firearm seizure or administration of a federal program. The loss of an armed-duty staffing option is a consequence of the valid substantive restriction, not itself a federal command to govern others.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that Hiley's no-command rule governs the staffing objection. The State retains its employment choices within the valid firearm restriction. Federal authorities enforce that restriction; the reported direction to contact ATF when a local agency lacks seizure authority does not supply a compulsory state enforcement duty. The record establishes no command requiring a State to appropriate funds, investigate disabilities or prosecute federal offenses.

New York forbids compulsory state legislation or compulsory ownership and liability, while Garcia and South Carolina v. Baker preserve valid regulation of state activity. Condon likewise distinguishes regulation of the State's own conduct from conscription to regulate other persons. The association's concern about selecting and equipping officers does not erase that distinction or turn an existing statutory exemption into a constitutional entitlement.

Printz/Mack addresses a different arrangement: affirmative direction for confined temporary records assistance, with fiscal objections and broader administration reserved. That limited holding is neither expanded nor displaced. This case requires no decision about the validity of an actual administrative command or an agency instruction exceeding its statutory authority. Such an application would require its own legal and factual predicates.

**Limits and questions not reached:** The October 7, 1998 judgment remains controlling on the developed standing, official-use arms and equal-protection questions. Those questions receive no second merits adjudication. Private possession outside that controversy, conviction classification, restoration, individual commerce proof and any actual compulsory state-administration order remain outside this judgment.

## Event

Gutierrez v. Ada, No. 99-51. On writ of certiorari to the United States Court of Appeals for the Ninth Circuit; argued December 6, 1999; decided January 19, 2000. The Guam Election Commission had certified the Gutierrez slate as the winner. The Ninth Circuit, 179 F.3d 672, required a runoff after counting all general-election ballots in the denominator. The slate obtained a majority of votes for Governor and Lieutenant Governor but not of every general-election ballot. The question is which denominator the Guam Organic Act, 48 U.S.C. §1422, requires.

## Participation

## Public Action

## Holdings

### The majority is calculated from votes cast for gubernatorial slates

**Holding and operative rule:** Under 48 U.S.C. §1422, a gubernatorial slate obtains the required majority when it receives more than half the votes cast for Governor and Lieutenant Governor jointly; general-election ballots expressing no choice in that contest are excluded from this denominator. If no slate receives a majority of those votes, the statute requires a runoff on the fourteenth day between the slates receiving the highest and second-highest vote totals; the ruling does not decide invalid candidate votes, fraud or materially different statutory language.

**Authority:** Justice Souter's opinion of the Court, joined in full by all eight other participating Justices, supplies a direct unanimous construction of §1422 and its application to the supplied election.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that the paragraph counts votes in the gubernatorial contest. Section 1422 provides for one joint vote for Governor and Lieutenant Governor and links the majority calculation to the votes candidates receive. A ballot used to vote in another contest but making no gubernatorial choice is not that joint vote. The reference to people qualified to vote for members of the Legislature identifies who may vote; the reference to the general election identifies when the gubernatorial election occurs. Neither changes the object being counted.

The competing reading would require a majority of everyone participating in the simultaneous general election, on the theory that a runoff secures a broader mandate. But the statutory mechanism speaks of cast votes received by candidates, not participation in unrelated races. An abstention from this contest cannot become a vote against each slate without a textual direction to count it that way. The candidate-vote construction leaves the runoff clause fully operative whenever the gubernatorial votes themselves produce no majority.

The established slate received a majority of the votes cast for gubernatorial candidates. The all-ballot denominator therefore cannot sustain the Ninth Circuit's runoff order. The Court reverses that order and remands under the correct denominator; the decision supplies no new numerical returns, fraud finding or rule for invalid candidate votes.

**Precedent treatment:** The holding construes this statutory mechanism directly. Rayphand/Torres concerns a separate territorial constitutional accommodation and supplies no different denominator for Guam.

The statutory runoff remains mandatory when no slate obtains a majority of the gubernatorial votes; this construction creates no general constitutional majority requirement and does not govern materially different language, fraud or invalid-vote disputes.

## Event

Smith v. Robbins; No. 98-1037. Argued October 5, 1999; decided January 19, 2000. On writ of certiorari to the Ninth Circuit, which affirmed a conditional habeas writ on the appellate-counsel claim and remanded the remaining exhausted trial claims. The question is whether California may employ a procedure different from Anders, including whether Wende supplies constitutionally adequate appellate advocacy. The State also asserts Teague nonretroactivity. The federal application preceded April 24, 1996; amended section 2254(d) does not govern.

## Participation

Chief Justice Stone-Zsela and Justices Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer participated at argument and decision. No nonparticipation is established.

## Public Action

The judgment of the Ninth Circuit is affirmed, 5–4, preserving its conditional appellate-process relief and remand for the remaining habeas claims.

## Judgment & Remedy

**Affirmed, 5–4.** **Supporting:** Stone-Zsela, Stevens, Souter, Ginsburg and Breyer. **Opposing:** O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy and Thomas. The conditional entitlement is to constitutionally adequate counsel-assisted appellate consideration, not a successful appeal or compulsory briefing of frivolous claims. The existing remand on eleven remaining exhausted trial claims stands. The Court adds no separate remedial instruction, new clock or immediate-release command.

## Opinion Topology

| Writing | Author | Joined by | Relationship to judgment | Scope joined |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Opinion of the Court | Stevens | Stone-Zsela, Souter, Ginsburg and Breyer throughout; O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy and Thomas in Part I only | Five-Justice affirmance | Part I: equivalent alternatives; Part II: Wende's inadequacy; Part III: old-rule Teague holding; Part IV: conditional process and remaining-claims remand |
| Dissent from Parts II–IV | Thomas | O'Connor, Scalia and Kennedy | Would reverse the categorical appellate-counsel ground and remand | Validates Wende; requires ordinary individual Strickland review; joins Part I and reserves an additional Teague ruling |

## Holdings

### I. Equivalent safeguards may replace the precise Anders format

**Holding and operative rule:** A State may use a procedure different from the precise filing, withdrawal and labeling arrangements described in Anders if it preserves meaningful assistance by an appellate advocate and supplies an equivalent means of determining whether the first appeal as of right is wholly frivolous. The Constitution protects the function, not a mandatory title or verbatim filing form.

**Authority:** Part I of Justice Stevens's opinion is joined by all nine Justices. Chief Justice Stone-Zsela and Justices Souter, Ginsburg and Breyer also join Parts II–IV; Justices O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy and Thomas join Part I only.

**Controlling explanation:** Douglas protects counsel on the first appeal a State supplies, and Evitts requires effective assistance in that appeal. Anders applies those protections when counsel believes that further advocacy would be frivolous. McCoy explains the reviewing court's two tasks: determining that counsel diligently searched for possible grounds and that the conclusion of frivolousness is sound. Those functions permit genuinely equivalent safeguards without requiring every State to reproduce each procedural detail. Neither counsel nor the court must advance a frivolous claim. The agreement on permissible alternatives does not settle whether California's particular procedure is equivalent.

### II. Wende omits necessary counsel-assisted legal screening

**Holding and operative rule:** Before a first appeal as of right is disposed of as frivolous, appointed counsel must supply meaningful legal assistance concerning issues that might arguably support the appeal, or participate in a genuinely equivalent safeguard allowing the court to test the conclusion of frivolousness. Wende is inadequate because it permits counsel to identify no such issue or legal ground and shifts that examination to the court and indigent appellant; a cited factual narrative, continued appointment, independent judicial review and a personal submission opportunity do not supply the missing advocate's function.

**Authority:** Part II of the Court's opinion, joined by Stone-Zsela, Stevens, Souter, Ginsburg and Breyer, controls with five direct votes.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that an advocate must assist the legal assessment that determines whether further represented appellate consideration is withheld. Anders rejects counsel's unsupported conclusion followed by judicial review as an adequate substitute. McCoy makes that duty concrete: the court must be able to evaluate both the diligence of counsel's search and the legal soundness of the frivolousness conclusion. Penson preserves those screening functions independently of its additional circumstance that the court had found arguable claims.

Counsel here performed real work, reviewed the record, supplied a cited narrative, remained appointed and available, and consulted an appellate project. Wende also permits Robbins to submit his own points, requires judicial examination of the record and calls for further briefing if the court discovers a nonfrivolous issue. The defect is therefore not literal withdrawal or the absence of every form of assistance. It is that the tribunal receives no required legal guidance from counsel about potentially supportive grounds before it makes the decisive screening determination. Further advocacy depends on the court first identifying an issue without that assistance. Actual project consultation does not establish that the tribunal received an equivalent legal assessment.

The State's safeguards have value, but neither nominal retention nor judicial issue-spotting supplies the missing adversarial contribution. No particular Anders form, merits argument for a frivolous claim or finding that Robbins will prevail is required. The lower courts identified possible library-access and counsel-waiver-withdrawal issues; this Court makes no finding that either is legally available, nonfrivolous or likely to succeed. The State asserts that Robbins failed to preserve the library-access claim by objecting at trial; the lower federal courts did not address that contention when identifying possible issues. That assertion is not a finding of waiver or default and remains distinct from the counsel-waiver-withdrawal ground.

### III. The defined protection and restoration of its process apply as old law

**Holding and operative rule:** Teague does not bar relief restoring the counsel-assisted appellate screening required here because Anders, McCoy and Penson already dictated that specific protection and its restoration before Robbins's conviction could have become final. This application creates neither a new procedural template nor a new exception to nonretroactivity.

**Authority:** Part III of the Court's opinion is joined by Stone-Zsela, Stevens, Souter, Ginsburg and Breyer. Five Justices reject the asserted bar on its terms, assuming it properly entertainable; no presentation waiver is found.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that the relevant old rule is the advocate's meaningful legal assistance in determining whether an indigent first appellant may be denied further represented consideration. It is not merely the general right to a lawyer. Anders required adequate assistance in that determination and restored appellate consideration without requiring this Court first to find an available nonfrivolous issue. McCoy explained the necessary legal-screening functions; Penson reinforced them before addressing its separate finding of arguable claims. All three preceded Robbins's 1990 conviction and thus any possible later finality date.

The State argues that Wende's retained counsel and substantial review distinguish those cases and that restoration without an established nonfrivolous ground extends their remedies. Those distinctions inform the merits disagreement but do not change the function the prior cases required. Counsel's legal contribution is necessary to test the very conclusion on which further advocacy depends. Requiring Robbins to establish the issue through the deficient process before obtaining that process would withhold the earlier protection.

Teague and Caspari require comparison of the particular protection and remedy with earlier law; disagreement alone does not establish novelty, and genuine new extensions remain subject to the bar. Schiro and Caspari keep presentation history distinct from novelty. The Court assumes proper consideration of the defense and rejects it without determining its earlier presentation history. No watershed or substantive exception is invoked, and later appellate-assistance cases supply no missing prefinality entitlement.

### IV. The conditional appellate-process relief and remaining-claims remand stand

**Holding and operative rule:** The omission of the required advocate-assisted screening function warrants restoration of constitutionally adequate appellate consideration without requiring Robbins first to prove that a particular omitted issue probably would have changed the appellate outcome. The Ninth Circuit's judgment affirming the conditional writ and remanding the remaining exhausted trial claims is affirmed; adequate screening may still find all available appellate issues frivolous.

**Authority:** Part IV of the Court's opinion is joined by Stone-Zsela, Stevens, Souter, Ginsburg and Breyer and supplies the five-Justice remedy.

**Controlling explanation:** Anders supplies the restoration of adequate appellate consideration. McCoy and Penson explain the necessary function that is missing, while Strickland and Cronic distinguish that deprivation from defective performance within an otherwise supplied function. The remedy restores counsel's role at the screening stage; it is not an award of a successful appeal or a declaration that an identified issue must receive merits briefing regardless of lawful preservation rules.

The State's strongest remedial objection is that counsel remained appointed and performed substantial work, making any defect an individual performance-and-prejudice question. But the Court's holding concerns the absence of the specific legal-assistance function necessary to the screening decision. Robbins need not prove the outcome of the process that must now be supplied. This conclusion does not transform every technical departure from Anders into a deprivation requiring this remedy.

The State's unresolved library objection receives its proper treatment if that issue is relied upon. The separate counsel-waiver-withdrawal issue and the eleven remaining exhausted trial claims retain their own predicates. Relief on those claims may make a further appellate process unnecessary. No immediate release, new deadline, mandatory evidentiary hearing or merits finding on those claims is ordered.

## Precedent Treatment

Restoration of that process is an application of prefinality law and does not depend on first proving a successful or nonfrivolous appellate ground. Ordinary Strickland principles continue to govern defective performance within an otherwise provided assistance function. Lawful preservation rules, the merits of the eleven remaining trial claims, and any materially distinct nonretroactivity question retain their independent force.

## Separate Writings

Justice Thomas dissents from Parts II–IV and the affirmance, joined by Justices O'Connor, Scalia and Kennedy. They join Part I's recognition of equivalent alternatives to Anders. ## Event

United States v. Martinez-Salazar, No. 98-1255. On writ of certiorari to the United States Court of Appeals for the Ninth Circuit; argued November 29, 1999; decided January 19, 2000. In the joint narcotics and weapons trial, the defendants received ten ordinary joint peremptory challenges and a separate alternate challenge. The court refused a cause challenge to Gilbert, who expressed a predisposition toward the prosecution. After two unsuccessful objections, Martinez-Salazar used an ordinary peremptory to remove him; all ordinary strikes were eventually used. The Ninth Circuit, 146 F.3d 653, found the cause ruling erroneous and no Sixth Amendment violation, but granted relief on a Fifth Amendment/full-allotment theory. The cause error is uncontested here. The question is whether corrective use denies a constitutional or Rule 24 right when the full allotment was supplied and an impartial jury sat.

## Participation

Chief Justice Stone-Zsela and Justices Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer participate at argument and decision.

The Ninth Circuit's lost-peremptory ground for relief is reversed.

## Judgment & Remedy

**Reversed and remanded, 9–0.**

**Supporting:** Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg, and Breyer.

The conviction remains undisturbed as against this jury-selection claim, and the matter returns for any proceedings properly remaining under the ordinary mandate. The Court orders no new trial for this cured cause error and adjudicates no additional claim.

## Opinion Topology

| Writing | Author | Joined by | Relationship to judgment | Scope joined |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Opinion of the Court | Scalia | Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg, Breyer | Unanimous reversal | Entire opinion, including the waiver and makeup-challenge reservations |

## Holdings

**Common authority:** Justice Scalia's opinion of the Court is joined in full by Chief Justice Stone-Zsela and Justices Stevens, O'Connor, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer. All nine adopt both holdings, the express reservations and the remedy directly.

### Corrective use followed by an impartial jury causes no Sixth Amendment deprivation

**Holding and operative rule:** When a defendant preserves a cause objection, uses an allotted peremptory challenge to remove the juror and is tried by an impartial jury, that corrective use does not itself deny the Sixth Amendment impartial-jury guarantee. The erroneous cause ruling remains an error; this holding does not decide a case in which a biased juror sits or an independent discriminatory-selection injury occurs.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that Gilbert's removal and the impartiality of the jury that sat defeat the claimed Sixth Amendment deprivation. Ross distinguishes the constitutional entitlement to an impartial tribunal from the statutory opportunities for peremptory exclusion. Its distinction applies here even though its Oklahoma-law compulsory-use condition does not govern this federal case. The Court accepts the uncontested determination that Gilbert should have been removed for cause; exercising the peremptory does not make that ruling correct.

The defense's strategic loss is real: the strike might otherwise have been used against a different prospective juror. But a possible change in the jury's composition is not itself the constitutional injury asserted when the challenged juror did not sit and the seated jury's impartiality is not contested. Gray's capital exclusion of a qualified juror is a different setting. The distinct federal-rule entitlement requires its own construction below; impartiality is not treated as satisfying every independent selection right. Batson, McCollum and J.E.B. retain their protections against discrimination, which this case does not present.

**Precedent treatment:** Ross's impartial-jury distinction is applied while its state-law curative condition remains confined to that law. Gray is distinguished at its capital-exclusion scope. The independent discrimination protections of Batson, McCollum and J.E.B. remain intact.

### The defendants received the Rule 24 entitlement they exercised

**Holding and operative rule:** On this record, Rule 24(b)'s ten joint ordinary peremptory challenges were fully afforded and exercised; using one to remove a juror erroneously retained over a cause objection does not deny that allotment or create a Fifth Amendment deprivation merely because the remaining strikes are exhausted. Rule 24 itself imposes no general duty of corrective use and promises no automatic replenishment; its permission for additional challenges in a multiple-defendant case is not a command, and the separate alternate challenge is not interchangeable with an ordinary one.

**Controlling explanation:** Rule 24 grants the opportunities to exclude prospective jurors, not ten further exclusions after every cause ruling has been correctly resolved. The defendants received and exercised all ten ordinary strikes. Removing Gilbert used one of those opportunities for its intended purpose. Practical pressure following the adverse cause ruling does not establish that the court denied the exercise of a strike. Because the claimed rule-based entitlement was received, the Fifth Amendment deprivation theory built on its loss fails as well.

The strongest contrary construction treats all for-cause removals as logically prior to the full peremptory allotment. That concern recognizes the value of an exclusion without legal cause, but neither the rule nor this record establishes the additional entitlement asserted. Defendants asked for no further strike, identified no other juror they wished to remove and challenged no seated juror's impartiality. Those facts limit this result; they do not create an exclusive preservation checklist or erase the prior cause objections.

Ross expressly reserved the impairment question beyond Oklahoma's particular rule, so the Court answers the federal-rule question independently. Swain's language about reversal for impairment cannot establish the antecedent impairment itself. Finding none, the Court does not choose automatic reversal or harmless-error review for an actual deprivation. The Government's proposed compulsory-use rule is rejected, but that rejection supplies no assurance that a defendant who leaves a challenged juror seated may later appeal without regard to ordinary waiver or preservation rules. That different situation remains open.

**Express reservations:** The Court does not decide whether reversible error occurs when a defendant uses a strike to cure a cause error, shows that the full complement otherwise would be used for noncurative exclusions, and requests but is refused a makeup challenge beyond the otherwise applicable maximum. This record contains no such showing or request. Deliberate judicial manipulation, denial of the prescribed allotment, a biased seated juror and independent discrimination likewise remain distinct. No right to replenishment, categorical no-waiver rule or general harmlessness rule follows.

**Precedent treatment:** Ross's express reservation requires an independent Rule 24 construction; its state-law exhaustion condition is not imported. Swain's actual-impairment language is left unresolved as a remedy question because no impairment is established. Hill's bounded scheduling rule supplies no waiver determination here.

## Event

## Holdings

### I. This response claim was fairly presented and is not procedurally defaulted

**Rule and authority.** A federal claim is fairly presented when the state submission identifies the challenged conduct and the same federal protection later invoked; a merits rejection without an actual independent state procedural ground supplies no such bar. Part I of Breyer's opinion, joined by all eight other Justices, holds that assignment 44 presented this subsequent question-and-response claim, incorporated assignment 41's federal effective-mitigation grounds, and received a state merits adjudication. The asserted nonexhaustion and default defenses therefore fail on their predicates.

**Controlling explanation.** Picard and Harless require the substance of the same federal claim, not facts from which an unrelated claim might be constructed. Assignment 44 identified the later inquiry, requested clarification and refusal, and incorporated the federal mitigation argument. The State's objection that this challenged only the original charge overlooks that identified conduct. The state court distinguished assignments it would not consider from those, including 44, that it considered and rejected on the merits. Harris and Coleman preserve actual adequate independent procedural grounds even when a state court also addresses the merits; none rests on this claim. Gray preserves distinct claims rather than splitting this express response challenge from its incorporated federal ground. O'Sullivan's complete-review principle does not require repetitive collateral presentation after an ordinary review round is complete. The later untimely collateral petition does not undo the direct presentation. Amended §2254(b)(2) permits, but does not require, merits denial despite nonexhaustion; that permission does not itself authorize a grant. Section 2254(b)(1) separately preserves relief when no available state corrective process exists or circumstances render it ineffective to protect the applicant's rights. Section 2254(b)(3) permits no deemed State waiver or estoppel without counsel's express waiver. No exception or waiver is needed here. The renewed request and objection preserve the response issue; initial use of the charge does not waive it.

### II. Amended §2254(d)(1) remains an independent condition on relief

**Rule and authority.** Because the federal application was filed after April 24, 1996 and the state court adjudicated this claim on the merits, a writ requires satisfaction of §2254(d)(1): a state decision contrary to, or involving an unreasonable application of, clearly established federal law determined by this Court. The unanimous Part I holds that a brief state merits explanation does not remove that restriction and a constitutional-error finding does not itself satisfy it.

**Controlling explanation.** Lindh and Carriger preserve the specified pre-enactment applications; this February 7, 1997 application is outside that premise. The relevant state decision is the November 4, 1994 adjudication. The earlier mitigation authorities, not later Buchanan or a later construction of §2254(d), supply the clearly established law for that task. Both statutory alternatives retain independent meaning. Competing readings of undisputed instructions establish no factual-unreasonableness ground under §2254(d)(2) or hearing entitlement under §2254(e). The Court does not adopt the Fourth Circuit's general reasonable-jurists or minimal-consistency formulations, and does not establish a comprehensive replacement construction. The five-Justice judgment permits independent statutory reconsideration and denial on return; the four Associates' reasons for withholding a present eligibility finding are not Stone's reasons or a five-Justice statutory rationale.

### III. The effective-mitigation protection applied here is not a new rule

**Rule and authority.** The requirement that the capital sentencing mechanism permit effective use of relevant mitigation applies to a response reasonably likely to leave that function unavailable; it creates no new entitlement to particular words or mandatory rephrasing of an adequate instruction. Part II, joined by Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Souter, Ginsburg and Breyer, rejects the asserted Teague bar on that defined claim without invoking an exception.

**Controlling explanation.** Lockett and Eddings protect mitigating use, and Boyde supplies contextual review of an allegedly restrictive instruction. Those authorities preceded the state adjudication and any subsequent finality. Graham applies the existing protected function rather than inserting a new procedural formula at finality. Saffle's distinction between unrestricted sympathy and relevant culpability mitigation remains; this claim demands the latter. Caspari retains the dictated-at-finality inquiry, and Gray and Lambrix distinguish a functional application from a new procedural entitlement. The Court assumes the asserted defense is properly considered and rejects it substantively. It neither infers waiver from alleged lateness nor changes Schiro's discretionary treatment of a genuinely late defense. No exact later finality date, retrospective use of Buchanan or Teague exception is necessary.

### IV. The referral left a reasonable likelihood that mitigation could not authorize life

**Rule and authority.** When a capital jury asks whether an aggravator makes death mandatory, the response, read with the complete charge and trial, must resolve a reasonable likelihood that the jury cannot give supported mitigation effect in choosing life. Part III, joined by Stone-Zsela, Stevens, Souter, Ginsburg and Breyer, holds that this response failed that requirement. No particular wording is required; reference to an existing instruction may suffice when it unambiguously resolves the actual difficulty.

**Controlling explanation.** Lockett and Eddings prohibit legal exclusion of mitigating effect while leaving weight to the sentencer. Graham requires a route for material culpability mitigation independent of eligibility for death. Boyde asks whether prohibited operation is reasonably likely in the charge and trial as a whole, not merely possible and not proved through a juror's subjective thoughts. Buchanan sustained a complete charge that supplied a lawful post-aggravator life choice; evidence and advocacy reinforced that judicial route rather than curing a restriction.

Instruction 2's accurate life alternative and B1's broad consideration command are substantial reasons supporting the State. But the later question expressly asked whether proof of an aggravator left a punishment choice after hearing those instructions and both arguments. Returning the jury to the same conditional paragraph did not resolve that expressed relationship. B1 directed consideration and left weight to the jury without independently answering the post-aggravator choice. The complete record therefore establishes a reasonable likelihood that the jury treated eligibility as requiring death. Counsel's explanations, further deliberation, the mitigation recital and polling do not conclusively remove that likelihood. Bollenbach underscores the importance of answering an expressed difficulty, but its federal direct-review ruling does not create a universal state constitutional duty to rephrase. The holding concerns this later response, preserves Buchanan's original-charge ruling, requires no favorable mitigation weight and establishes neither automatic structural relief nor present causal effect on the sentence.

### V. Authority and limits of the return

The five-Justice judgment fixes the constitutional error but leaves statutory availability and causal effect for independent adjudication. The court below may deny the claim if a necessary condition fails. Breyer's Part IV, joined by Stevens, Souter and Ginsburg, explains that the lower court's minimal-consistency formulation and reliance on later Buchanan did not separately evaluate the 1994 adjudication under the enacted alternatives. The accurate paragraph remains a substantial argument for statutory denial despite constitutional error. Stone agrees with the return only in the judgment, on his separately stated grounds. That disagreement supplies no controlling Marks rationale on present statutory eligibility.

No single effect standard or allocation of uncertainty commands a majority for this jury-response category. Brecht's Chapman holding concerns preserved Doyle error; O'Neal and Roy cover the defined personal-intent category; Calderon v. Coleman adds commutation misinformation. Their whole-record and causal distinctions remain, but none conclusively selects the standard for this different category. If statutory eligibility is established, the receiving court must resolve the open effect-standard question, including the governing burden and treatment of uncertainty, on a legal basis consistent with those defined holdings. The reservation neither rejects Chapman nor permits an arbitrary choice between standards; this mandate prescribes neither a categorical Chapman extension nor a general substantial-and-injurious-effect rule. The proposed burdens are not combined into a controlling narrowest-ground rule. Identifying Boyde error alone is insufficient to issue a writ. Any lawful effect determination must concern the actual sentence and the complete record, including the charge, mitigation, prosecution theory and jury inquiry; neither permissible death eligibility nor strong aggravation alone resolves it.

### The presented indexed ceiling satisfies the existing contribution standard on this record

**Holding and operative rule:** Under Russell, government must justify a direct candidate-contribution ceiling by an objectively grounded, sufficiently important corruption or appearance interest and a close fit that preserves meaningful association and effective campaign advocacy. Missouri meets that burden for the presented $1,075 statewide/state-auditor ceiling, indexed biennially from the January 1, 1995 $1,000 base and rounded to the nearest $25; this record-specific ruling establishes no dollar floor, safe harbor, other-office approval or immunity from a future materially different suppression challenge.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that Missouri's combined risk and operational evidence justifies this ceiling under Russell. Goode's account, the reported $20,000 bank contribution and related official banking choice, $40,000 brewery and $20,000 bank gifts in the auditor contest, a $420,000 PAC contribution and reported sponsorship-for-kickback accusations ground a concern about official obligation and its appearance. Accusations remain accusations, and the property-related conspiracy plea proves its actual offense only. The Court does not find completed exchanges in every episode, require a proved bribe, or treat ordinary donor agreement, access or resource inequality as sufficient. The record supplies no finding of an alternative cause, and no decline is treated as harmless. The figure that 97.62 percent of contributors gave no more than $2,000 measures neither contributed dollars nor the effect of a $1,075 ceiling.

Missouri's affirmative combined showing meets its burden; the test is not whether challengers prove literal campaign impossibility. **Holding and operative rule:** A preclearance dispute remains live when the challenged plan's legal status has a concrete continuing consequence for the lawful benchmark used to assess a later covered voting change. The 1992 plan has that consequence here, notwithstanding the completed election and anticipated replacement; the rule does not make every superseded plan perpetually reviewable.

**Authority:** All nine Justices join Part I of the Court's opinion.

**Controlling explanation:** Preclearance determines whether this plan may enter the operative baseline for the next section 5 comparison. Expected redistricting before the next regular election does not remove that legal consequence. The existing preclearance judgment therefore remains a concrete controversy. Bossier I supplies the benchmark's operative significance; the current continuing-controversy decisions require an actual end to the relevant legal stake, not a forecast of future change. The Court need not find that a vacancy election will occur, that agreement on a replacement will fail, or that appellants are entitled to a special election. Those suggested contingencies are not the holding's premise.

**Precedent treatment:** Bossier I's benchmark rule supplies the continuing consequence; Adarand's January 12 continuing-controversy distinction is applied at that limited scope, without importing its certification or program-merits questions.

### Nonretrogression resolves the effects objection, not the separate purpose inquiry

**Holding and operative rule:** Section 5's effects prong asks whether a covered change worsens protected voters' effective electoral position relative to the lawful operative benchmark. The conceded nonretrogression defeats this plan's effects objection; preservation of dilution, a feasible more favorable alternative or possible section 2 liability does not substitute for retrogression or resolve unlawful purpose.

**Authority:** All nine Justices join Part II's effects holding and preservation of the independent purpose inquiry.

**Controlling explanation:** Bossier I already fixed the effect benchmark and kept section 2 enforcement separate. Beer and Lockhart supply the retrogression comparison. The parties' nonretrogression premise therefore disposes of the effect theory. A potential majority-Black district does not become the benchmark merely because it is feasible, and De Grandy's separate section 2 inquiry establishes no maximization or proportional-representation duty here. This ruling neither decides whether the plan violates section 2 nor prevents its independent enforcement. Purpose must still receive its own analysis under the controlling mandate.

**Precedent treatment:** Bossier I, Beer and Lockhart are applied without changing the effects benchmark. De Grandy's distinct totality inquiry and rejection of automatic maximization remain intact.

### The prior mandate requires examination of unconstitutional purpose beyond retrogression

**Holding and operative rule:** The May 12, 1997 mandate controls these appeals: section 5 bars a voting change adopted with an unconstitutional racial purpose to deny or abridge voting opportunity even if that purpose does not include worsening the benchmark. The jurisdiction retains its burden to establish absence of prohibited purpose; racial awareness, dilution, rejection of a feasible alternative or possible section 2 liability alone is not conclusive.

**Authority:** All nine join Part III's application of the existing mandate and its relevant-evidence obligation. O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy and Thomas apply that binding instruction without withdrawing their original reservations about its broader statutory rationale. The original five-Justice purpose holding remains the source of that rule; no contrary narrow-purpose holding is adopted.

**Controlling explanation:** The returned judgment repeats the narrower retrogressive-intent inquiry. That does not perform the task already required by Bossier I. Arlington Heights identifies relevant contextual evidence: impact, history, sequence, departures, contemporary explanations and pretext. Evidence is not excluded merely because it also bears on a section 2 theory. Pleasant Grove and Richmond preserve the independent work of purpose despite nonretrogressive effects. Findings that prompt approval and easier implementation were legitimate objectives do not themselves establish that the identical plan would have been chosen without prohibited purpose. The present clearance judgment therefore cannot stand on the narrow inquiry or on an assumed identical-plan finding. The Justices disagree over whether the developed record requires denial now or another properly framed purpose determination.

**Precedent treatment:** Bossier I's purpose mandate is enforced, not reopened. Arlington Heights supplies contextual evidence without converting any item into a presumption; Pleasant Grove and Richmond preserve purpose's independence from effect.

### The developed record requires denial of this plan's clearance

**Holding and operative rule:** On the combined record, the Board has not established absence of the unconstitutional purpose to preserve racial dilution, and its 1992 plan must be denied preclearance in both appeals. Even assuming a same-decision defense is legally available, the Board has not established that independently sufficient lawful reasons would have produced this identical plan; no controlling rule here decides that defense's ultimate statutory availability or creates a new proof quantum.

**Authority:** Part IV, joined by Stone-Zsela, Stevens, Souter, Ginsburg and Breyer, supplies five direct votes for the record-based denial and remedy. Stone-Zsela separately resolves the defense's legal availability in his own concurrence; the four other denial Justices reserve it. O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy and Thomas would vacate clearance and return for the broader purpose determination rather than direct denial.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that the developed record defeats the Board's claim to clearance. Stipulated resistance to desegregation and the participation history give context. The Board left the already approved Police Jury plan unadopted for over a year despite an early proposal, pursued other districting work, and accelerated adoption only after a feasible minority alternative appeared. The selected plan departed from ordinary school, incumbent, compactness and contiguity concerns. The combined sequence and departures support prohibited purpose; dilution corroborates that inference but does not replace it.

The administrative explanations are real: the Police Jury plan needed no precinct splits, against forty-six for the NAACP proposal, twenty-seven for Cooper II and fourteen for a one-majority-Black option. Prior approval offered reason to expect prompt clearance. Yet the Board knew it could seek precinct changes and did not pursue that route; no finding assumes a request would have succeeded. Avoiding splits does not explain the refusal to consider any effective minority option or the timing of the actual choice. The legitimate concerns do not dispel the demonstrated purpose or establish independently sufficient reasons for these identical lines.

The Court accounts for the polarization data's limits and other-office successes' Air Force base and effective-electorate qualifications. Musgrove denied the attributed statement; that disputed account is not indispensable to the conclusion and is not treated as an admission. If the lower opinions contain a finding of no broader discriminatory purpose, it is clearly erroneous on this aggregate record; if they failed to perform the mandated inquiry, the developed proof and the Board's burden still require no further opportunity to establish the same entitlement. The first remand neither predetermined purpose nor guaranteed repeated remands. Both appeals therefore receive reversal with directions to deny clearance, without a replacement map, added district or election order.

**Precedent treatment:** Bossier I's contextual inquiry and unchanged jurisdictional burden are applied to the developed return record. Arlington Heights/Mt. Healthy supply a serious causation analogy, but no majority adopts their automatic transplantation into section 5; Lesage's distinct process-injury and remedy rules likewise create no section 5 defense.

## Event

U.S. West, Inc. v. Federal Communications Commission. On writ of certiorari to the United States Court of Appeals for the Tenth Circuit; certiorari granted November 1, 1999; submitted January 10, 2000; decided February 7, 2000. The Tenth Circuit's August 18, 1999 judgment, No. 98-9518, 182 F.3d 1224, reviewed FCC 98-27 under 28 U.S.C. §2342(1) and 47 U.S.C. §402(a). It vacated the CPNI order on First Amendment and constitutional-doubt grounds, without deciding the takings and other arbitrary-and-capricious objections. The questions are whether the challenged affirmative-approval method for marketing unsubscribed service categories satisfies commercial-speech scrutiny and is permitted by the general approval language of 47 U.S.C. §222(c)(1).

## Participation

Chief Justice Stone-Zsela and Justices Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer participate at submission and decision.

## Public Action

The Court rejects the First Amendment and general approval-construction grounds for vacatur and returns the unresolved claims for review.

## Judgment & Remedy

**Reversed in the identified respects and remanded, 7–2.**

**Supporting:** Stone-Zsela, O'Connor, Scalia, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer.

**Opposing:** Stevens and Kennedy, who would retain constitutional invalidity confined to the challenged approval application, with relief conforming to the intervening instruments.

The Tenth Circuit's judgment is reversed insofar as it vacated the challenged approval mechanism on the First Amendment and general approval-construction grounds decided here. The matter returns to that court for the unreached takings and arbitrary-and-capricious objections and for relief conforming to the intervening statutory and agency instruments. No unconditional reinstatement of FCC 98-27, new consent system, property determination, compensation, violation or sanction is ordered.

## Holdings

### Part I. The marketing condition receives independent commercial-speech review

**Holding and operative rule:** A rule conditioning use of information on its intended use for truthful, lawful commercial solicitation receives commercial-speech review even when the carrier lawfully possesses the information and other marketing remains available. Protecting confidential calling records against unknowing use outside the customer's chosen service relationship is a substantial interest, but the government must independently establish material advancement and reasonable fit.

**Authority:** Justice Ginsburg's Part I is joined by all eight other Justices. Stevens and Kennedy join this part while rejecting the constitutional application and judgment.

**Controlling explanation:** Lanphere and United Reporting require review of this marketing-selected condition. Targeted communication remains protected; permission to address an indiscriminate audience does not remove its burden. Neither corporate form, common-carrier status, custody of information nor the unchallenged statutory baseline turns the chosen approval method into unprotected conduct. Constitutional validity is the Court's independent responsibility.

The asserted confidentiality interest concerns information about whom a customer calls, when and for how long, acquired through providing telephone service. Its protection does not depend on finding a new constitutional ownership right in every record or confining harm to embarrassment. Went For It recognized substantial privacy interests while invalidating the particular public-record mail restraint; it reserved confidential-record misuse. The substantial interest therefore warrants the next inquiries, not automatic validation. Paul P.'s limited residential-information right and Condon's commerce and structural holdings do not decide this commercial-speech question. The Court does not rely on competition as an independently sufficient interest.

**Precedent treatment:** Lanphere and United Reporting supply the speech-selected-use trigger. Went For It preserves both substantial privacy and the need for supported application. Paul P. and Condon remain confined to their different questions.

### Part II. Affirmative choice materially advances the identified confidentiality interest

**Holding and operative rule:** On this record, requiring approval before using confidential service-derived CPNI to market an unsubscribed service category directly and materially protects against unknowing repurposing outside the existing relationship. The service-related and distinct research permissions do not defeat that application; this holding neither treats all internal handling as harmful nor validates every collateral CPNI restriction.

**Authority:** Part II is joined by Stone-Zsela, O'Connor, Scalia, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer. Stevens and Kennedy disagree with its application.

**Controlling explanation:** The approval mechanism addresses the proposed new use itself. Confidential calling records come to the carrier through services the customer selected. Requiring an affirmative choice before extending their marketing use to another category prevents an unread notice from being treated as informed permission. Edenfield permits supported prediction without requiring an empirical study in every setting; the service source, actual proposed use and available customer choice provide the concrete connection here.

The Commission's statement that sharing within an integrated firm raises no significant privacy concern is substantial counterevidence. It does not answer the different question of purpose. Possession by the same firm does not make every later marketing use part of the relationship already chosen. The within-category permission and sharing among affiliates already supplying subscribed categories approximate that existing relationship. Inside wiring and adjunct services relate to it; mobile-service health research serves a distinct nonmarketing purpose. The statutory operational, protective and limited customer-initiated-call permissions likewise do not authorize unrestricted new-category marketing.

Discovery Network, Rubin and Greater New Orleans require examination of comparable permissions, not automatic invalidity whenever exceptions exist. These permissions do not reproduce the unconsented new-category use at issue. Unlike the public records in Lanphere and Went For It, the underlying information here is subject to a service-derived confidentiality duty. The Court finds no actual outside disclosure or individual injury and recognizes no universal data-ownership right. Protecting confidentiality with informed expansion, rather than keeping lawful buyers ignorant to influence purchases, is indispensable to the holding.

**Precedent treatment:** Edenfield's real-harm and material-advancement burden is applied without a mandatory-study rule. Discovery Network, Rubin and Greater New Orleans require the actual permission comparison; Lanphere and Went For It are distinguished at their public-information setting without weakening them.

### Part III. The flexible approval mechanism reasonably fits this use

**Holding and operative rule:** Oral, written or electronic affirmative approval, lasting until revoked or limited and preceded by intelligible notice, reasonably and proportionately fits the confidentiality interest in this challenged new-category use. The government need not select opt-out merely because it burdens the carrier less, but neither opt-out's general constitutional invalidity nor a least-restrictive-means requirement follows.

**Authority:** The same seven Justices join Part III. Stevens and Kennedy independently reject its fit conclusion even assuming material advancement.

**Controlling explanation:** Fox requires reasonable proportional fit, not the least restrictive conceivable method. The FCC considered written-only consent, flexible express consent and notice with opt-out. It rejected written-only consent as unduly restrictive and chose the flexible route because unread notices can leave silence uninformed. Approval continues until revoked or limited; the carrier can seek it through several methods and can market through other information. Those alternatives do not erase the burden of losing effective targeting, but they make the consent requirement proportionate to the confidentiality choice.

The study supplies mixed evidence. In outbound calls, 33 percent expressly refused, 28 percent approved, and 39 percent hung up or requested no further calls; the last group is not an established informed privacy refusal. Seventy-two percent approved when approached after initiating contact, showing a usable avenue without establishing others' consent. Residential direct-mail response was 6–11 percent and business response 5–9 percent; response is not refusal. The informational burden and solicitation expense are real. These results neither prove that every nonrespondent opposes use nor establish indifference or informed permission.

The strongest objection is that clearer notice with opt-out could preserve willing recipients' access to tailored offers. The FCC addressed that choice, and its explanation concerning unknowing repurposing, considered with the service boundary and flexible methods, meets the government's burden here. Mere discussion of an alternative would not suffice; this actual mechanism and justification do. No perfect consent system, universal study, public-information restriction or permanent suppression of truthful product knowledge is approved.

**Precedent treatment:** Fox supplies proportional fit without least restrictive means. Edenfield, Lanphere and Went For It retain the burden of a supported application; Greater New Orleans does not transform every less burdensome alternative into a constitutional command.

### Part IV. The general approval text permits this construction, with residual claims preserved

**Holding and operative rule:** Section 222(c)(1), read with the confidentiality duty, permits the FCC to require an affirmative customer decision through oral, written or electronic means without unambiguously compelling that exclusive method for every general CPNI use. After independent rejection of the presented speech challenge, that choice receives ordinary Chevron deference; the asserted speech-based constitutional doubt cannot sustain vacatur, while the unreached takings and other arbitrary-and-capricious claims remain for review.

**Authority:** The same seven join Part IV and the reversal/remand. Stevens and Kennedy agree that the general text leaves room for affirmative approval but withhold deference to this application because of their constitutional conclusions; that textual agreement is not a join in Part IV or reversal.

**Controlling explanation:** Congress required customer approval for the covered uses but left the general approval method unspecified. Within the confidentiality duty, an affirmative indication is a reasonable way to establish a knowing choice. The text need not command that method exclusively for Chevron to permit it. AT&T v. Iowa Utilities Board requires an agency to remain within actual delegation and enacted limits; it supplies no blanket approval of FCC choices.

Statutory deference cannot decide First Amendment validity. The Court has independently applied the governing speech standard, including the government's burden, the adverse evidence and alternatives. That analysis defeats the speech-based serious-doubt ground invoked below. It does not declare every constitutional objection resolved: the lower majority did not reach takings, and its dissent's contrary view does not decide that claim. The remaining arbitrary-and-capricious objections likewise receive their own review.

This construction concerns the general text. The later express wireless-location and automatic-crash-notification commands and their distinct exceptions retain their enacted operation; their constitutionality and the emergency-information cross-reference are not adjudicated. The FCC's reconsideration also has distinct removals, effective-date conditions and enforcement timing. Reversal therefore removes the grounds decided here and returns the residual claims and proper implementation; it does not restore every former regulation or decide compliance, sanctions, ownership or compensation.

**Precedent treatment:** Chevron is applied to the general approval gap after independent constitutional review. AT&T preserves statutory limits and separate review of unresolved objections. No avoidance principle permits assumed speech invalidity to replace the analysis required by the governing cases.

## Law After Decision

The challenged new-category approval method satisfies independent commercial-speech review on this record and is a permissible reading of the general approval text. No conduct-only exemption, universal opt-out prohibition, exclusive statutory opt-in rule for every general CPNI use, constitutional data-ownership right or general reduction in corporate speech protection follows. Takings and other administrative-law claims remain unresolved.

### Scope of the approval determination

The decision concerns confidential, individually identifiable customer proprietary network information obtained through the telecommunications service relationship, including calling patterns and billing information. Subscriber-list information is excluded, and aggregate customer information has separate treatment. It does not establish a rule for all data or all targeted marketing.

Under the general statutory text, use required by law or approved by the customer is distinct from use for the service from which the information derives or services necessary to or used in providing it, including directories. Section 222(d)'s original permissions separately cover initiating, rendering, billing and collecting for telecommunications services; protecting carrier rights or property and users or other carriers against fraudulent, abusive or unlawful service use or subscription; and inbound telemarketing, referral or administrative services during a customer-initiated call when the customer approves use of the information to provide that service. The last permission does not extend beyond that call or authorize indefinite later marketing.

The challenged total-service rule permits use to provide or market local, interexchange and commercial-mobile services within categories already subscribed to from the carrier. Sharing among affiliates supplying those subscribed categories is permitted when the customer subscribes to more than one category; the corresponding permission is absent when the customer does not. Approval is required for marketing an unsubscribed category, with distinct permissions for inside-wiring installation, maintenance and repair; mobile-service health-effects research; and former adjunct-to-basic services. Illustrative adjunct services include speed dialing, computer-provided directory assistance, monitoring, tracing, blocking, return, repeat dialing, tracking, waiting, caller identification, forwarding and certain centrex features. This is not an exhaustive list.

Approval can be written, oral or electronic; the carrier bears the burden of demonstrating compliant oral approval. It continues until revoked or limited; notification and approval records must be kept at least one year. Prior one-time notice identifies the information, specific recipients, purposes, customer rights and carrier duty, precise granting or denying steps, rights to disapprove or withdraw, and the continuing effect of approval or denial until affirmative revocation or limitation. It must state that refusal does not affect subscribed services. Notice must be comprehensible and nonmisleading; written notice must be legible, sufficiently large and readily apparent, and translation of part requires translation of all. Notice may describe tailored-offering benefits and possible compelled disclosure on the customer's affirmative written request; it may not encourage freezing third-party access. Solicitation must be proximate to notice. These conditions are distinct from the later removal of the same-document requirement.

The original equipment/information-service restriction, competitor-tracking prohibition and former-customer winback prohibition are separate provisions. No approval exception is inserted into a prohibition that lacks it, and none receives an independent constitutional ruling merely from this approval-method decision.

### Intervening enactment and agency action

Public Law 106-81 section 5 took effect October 26, 1999 without a section 5 delay. Section 222(f) separately requires express prior authorization for wireless call-location information except the specified section 222(d)(4) emergency uses, and for automatic-crash-notification information except use in operating an automatic-crash-notification system. The location exceptions permit provision to a public safety answering point, emergency medical or dispatch provider, public safety, fire-service or law-enforcement official, or hospital emergency or trauma-care facility to respond to the user's emergency-services call; informing the legal guardian or immediate family of the user's location in an emergency risking death or serious physical harm; and provision to information- or database-management providers solely to assist delivery of emergency services in response to an emergency. Those exceptions do not permit ordinary marketing; the crash-notification exception remains separate.

The amendment adds location to CPNI, moves definitions to subsection (h), and imposes a separate emergency-service information duty. The Court neither construes subsection (g)'s enacted cross-reference nor adjudicates those later provisions' constitutionality. They do not retroactively establish the FCC's 1998 explanation or permit a general assertion that opt-out is available for every CPNI category.

FCC 99-223, adopted August 16, released September 3 and published October 1, 1999, retains affirmative approval in paragraph 95. Paragraphs 185 and 190 condition amended information-collection rules on OMB approval and a later effective-date publication; no such publication is established in this record. Paragraphs 187 and 188 separately remove the winback and same-document notice restrictions. Paragraph 189 postpones enforcement of amended section 64.2009(a) and (c) safeguards until eight months after release. Equipment/information-service and safeguard amendments are not all treated as effective on publication, and reversal does not resurrect removed provisions. No compliance, default, sanction exposure or expired transition is determined.

## Separate Writings

**Chief Justice Stone-Zsela, concurring; no other Justice joins.**

Stone-Zsela joins the Court's entire opinion and separately explains the carrier's capacity to claim constitutional protection. Capacity follows the particular right: commercial-speech protection serves listeners as well as speakers, so the corporation may invoke it; legal personhood alone does not settle the scope of that right. Its charter supplies its existence, and authorization to provide telephone service supplies its service position and access to calling records. That access carries lawful confidentiality terms under section 222, subject to the independent commercial-speech scrutiny the Court applies.

Telephone service is not consent to every later profitable use of its records. But neither charter nor licensing permits government to demand surrender of independent constitutional protections. A penalty for viewpoint or editorial choice would receive the scrutiny governing that discrimination; this rule selects neither. The carrier's wealth or size reduces no protection, natural persons acting through it retain their own speech, and customers retain their interests in their records. Marketing and requests for consent remain available.

Lanphere's marketing trigger and Central Hudson/Fox, rather than a new entity-capacity test, govern the rule's validity. This additional explanation supplies no general charter-condition power, categorical inferiority of corporate speech or new constitutional ownership of all CPNI. No other Justice joins the concurrence.

**Justice Stevens, concurring in part and dissenting in part, joined by Justice Kennedy.**

Stevens and Kennedy join Part I's independent speech review and substantial confidentiality interest. They would retain invalidity of the challenged approval application on two independently sufficient grounds, with relief confined to that application and reconciled with the intervening instruments.

First, the agency has not adequately justified the selected distinction under Discovery Network, Edenfield, Lanphere and their United Reporting position. Confidential calling records materially differ from public records, but that difference does not establish every internal marketing use as a new harm. The Commission's own integrated-firm assessment and broad permission for existing-category marketing weaken its explanation for treating the selected new-category use differently. Sensitivity alone cannot supply advancement, and the dissent demands no empirical study in every case or regulation of all information sources.

Second, even assuming advancement, fit independently fails. The FCC considered opt-out and permits oral or electronic approval; neither fact is disputed. Its concern about unread notices does not adequately show why sufficiently clear notice with opt-out would fail to protect the actual internal-use interest. Low affirmative response is not widespread informed opposition; willing recipients may lose effective tailored communication. Fox does not impose least restrictive means, but still requires a supported comparison proportionate to the burden. The additional marketing channels do not eliminate that burden.

The general statutory text can permit affirmative approval, but statutory permission and Chevron deference cannot cure this unconstitutional application. The dissent would not invalidate section 222, require opt-out universally, adjudicate the later express wireless commands, adopt a takings conclusion or treat unresolved administrative claims as rejected. It does not defend blanket vacatur of every collateral provision.

## Event

Richards v. United States; No. 99-731. Submitted January 10, 2000; decided February 14, 2000. Certiorari was granted November 1, 1999 to review the Third Circuit’s affirmance, 176 F.3d 652, of dismissal under Feres. The question is whether the accepted authorized personal journey home was incident to service and how that inquiry applies to the five pleaded negligence theories. The Court does not undertake a general reconsideration of Feres or decide liability.

## Participation

Chief Justice Stone-Zsela and Justices Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer participated at submission and decision. No nonparticipation is established.

## Public Action

The judgment is affirmed in part and reversed in part, and the case is remanded. The ordinary road-operation and equipment theories survive the Feres dismissal, 5–4; the two command theories remain dismissed, 8–1.

## Holdings

### I. The ordinary road-operation and equipment theories may proceed

**Holding and operative rule:** Feres does not bar the pleaded negligent-operation, negligent-maintenance and knowingly-unsafe-road-use theories arising from Richards's authorized personal journey home in his own car on a public road after permission to leave work early. His continuing active-duty status, lack of a formal pass or furlough and location within the installation bear on the connection with service but do not make this accepted private journey service-incident; the dismissal of those theories and their derivative claims is reversed and remanded for the remaining FTCA requirements and defenses.

**Authority:** Part I of Justice Scalia's opinion is joined by Chief Justice Stone-Zsela and Justices Stevens, Souter and Ginsburg. Those five Justices join both the operative rule and its limited remand.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that the journey's actual character matters. Feres bars injuries incident to military service; Brooks preserves claims arising outside that relationship. Richards had permission to leave early, drove his own automobile, and was traveling home for a family purpose accepted on dismissal review. The road was publicly used, state-maintained and state-patrolled. Uniform, active status, lack of formal leave and location within Fort Knox weigh toward service, but do not overcome those combined circumstances for the ordinary road-safety duties alleged here.

The Government's strongest argument joins the departure from a military workplace to continuing nonfurlough service within the installation. Its additional account places the home on the main post and the journey on a connecting road. Even assuming that geography, without finding it established, the accepted release and private purpose remain. No required residence, prescribed route or assigned errand is established. The disputed Education Center destination cannot support dismissal as a fact already found.

Johnson preserves the service-incident bar regardless of the tortfeasor's civilian or military status. Stanley forecloses demanding a further showing of actual disciplinary disruption once service incidence is established; its implied-constitutional-remedy ruling does not settle this different FTCA threshold. This decision rests on that threshold, not an additional disruption requirement. It creates neither a general commuting exception nor an assigned-task-only test, and neither all public-road injuries nor all private purposes receive automatic exemption.

The maintenance and unsafe-use allegations concern roadworthy equipment, not an established policy choice. Minns preserves independent protected-policy defenses but supplies no automatic immunity for military vehicles. Potts's scope of federal employment is admitted; negligence, causation, local-law entitlement, contributory negligence, damages and independent FTCA defenses remain for ordinary proceedings. No liability finding, compelled discovery, hearing, deadline or benefit offset follows. A genuinely material destination dispute may be addressed below. The command theories receive their separate treatment in Part II.

### II. The military permission and order theories remain barred

**Holding and operative rule:** Shearer bars the theories that the Army negligently allowed an allegedly unlicensed soldier to drive this class of truck and negligently ordered him to drive despite inadequate skill or experience. Those allegations directly challenge military selection and direction of a servicemember for an assignment; the dismissal of those theories and their derivative claims is affirmed notwithstanding the injured member's accepted private travel.

**Authority:** Part II of Justice Scalia's opinion is joined by Justices Stevens, O'Connor, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer. Those eight Justices adopt the Shearer ground; Chief Justice Stone-Zsela dissents from this component.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that these two allegations put military command decisions themselves in issue. Shearer looks to the actual challenge to the military's supervision and control of servicemembers; an off-duty victim or ordinary-negligence label does not remove that challenge. Permitting an allegedly unqualified soldier to perform a military driving assignment and ordering him to do so despite inadequate skill require examination of that selection and direction. Those are the allegations presented, not grounds inferred merely from truck ownership.

The contrary argument treats every theory as an ordinary traffic claim because Richards's journey was private and the accident occurred on a public road. That describes the injured person's activity but does not erase the separate command duties these theories ask a court to review. The Court therefore preserves Brooks's nonservice boundary for the road-operation and equipment theories while applying Shearer to the actual permission and order claims. Claim labels alone do not govern either conclusion.

This holding neither finds the allegations true nor decides whether a specific military directive existed or was violated. It requires no additional holding that the same conduct also falls within the discretionary-function exception. Minns retains its own protected-choice and binding-direction qualifications; no independent section 2680(a) disposition is made here. The family losses arise from Richards's death and follow the component treatment of that injury; the claimants suffered no separate civilian bodily injury. The surviving claims may proceed on remand, but these two command theories remain dismissed.

## Precedent Treatment

- Feres v. United States — retained as the bar for service-incident injuries; neither active status nor installation geography alone supplies its threshold.
- Brooks v. United States — applied to the accepted independent personal journey and ordinary road-safety claims; it does not create a universal public-road, commuting or formal-leave rule.
- United States v. Brown — its distinction between military causal history and the later wrong is preserved; its postdischarge facts do not make Richards a civilian.
- Stencel Aero Engineering Corp. v. United States — distinguished because its indemnity holding begins with a service-incident injury and does not decide the disputed classification here.
- United States v. Johnson — retained: the tortfeasor's civilian status supplies no exception for an injury already incident to service; no such exception is adopted for this military driver.
- United States v. Stanley — retained as foreclosing an additional case-specific disruption requirement once service incidence is established; its Bivens holding concerns an implied constitutional remedy, and its FTCA jurisdictional ruling does not decide Richards's classification.
- United States v. Shearer — applied to the actual permission and order theories; off-duty character and ordinary-negligence terminology do not remove a direct command challenge.
- Minns v. United States — its controlling protection of actual policy discretion, subject to binding-direction and protected-choice requirements, remains in force; its fragmented judgment supplies no controlling Feres extension or automatic military-vehicle immunity.

## Law After Decision

Feres continues to bar service-incident injuries. Its application to the accepted authorized private journey permits the three ordinary road-operation and equipment theories to proceed, while Shearer separately bars the actual military permission and order challenges. Neither commuting, public-road geography, formal leave nor an assigned task becomes a categorical test. Once service incidence is established, no further actual-disruption showing is required. Minns retains its separate qualified protection of policy discretion, and the surviving FTCA requirements and defenses remain open.

## Separate Writings

Chief Justice Stone-Zsela joins Part I and dissents from Part II. He would reverse the Feres dismissal in full and remand for all remaining FTCA requirements and defenses. His ground is the actual character of this authorized personal trip: active-duty status, lack of a pass or furlough, and on-base location matter but do not override the accepted private journey home in his own car on a public road. Brooks preserves that distinction. His disagreement with the partial affirmance rests on that incident-to-service classification; he adds no separate rule exempting every command allegation, no general commuting exception, and no requirement that only an assigned task can be service-incident. He would decide neither negligence nor damages, and would preserve independently applicable FTCA defenses.

Justice O'Connor dissents from Part I, joined by Justices Kennedy, Thomas and Breyer, and joins Part II. They would affirm the entire Feres dismissal. The immediate departure from a military duty station while still on the installation in active service without pass or furlough remains sufficiently connected with service despite authorized early release, a private automobile and the accepted family purpose. Feres and Johnson extend beyond an immediate assigned task; Stanley requires no additional demonstration of actual discipline interference after the service relationship is established. The added on-post-home and connecting-road account reinforces that view if assumed, but is not necessary to it and is not treated as a fact found.

The dissent accepts Brooks's nonservice boundary and rejects an absolute rule for every on-base injury, every private purpose or every absence of formal furlough. It disagrees with the majority's application to this combination of circumstances. The admitted employment scope of the driver does not decide the injured member's relationship to service. Compensation and the federal relationship support the dissent's Feres analysis but create no universal benefits election or offset. The dissent independently joins the Shearer dismissal of the permission and order theories, adopts no unadjudicated discretionary-function ground, and makes no finding of negligence or liability.

## Procedure After Action

The case returns through the Third Circuit for ordinary proceedings on negligent operation, negligent maintenance and knowingly unsafe road use and their derivative claims. The permission and order dismissals stand. The lower courts may address any genuinely material destination dispute and the remaining negligence, causation, local-law, contributory-negligence, damages and independent FTCA questions. Potts’s admitted federal employment scope is not reopened by this mandate. No new filing deadline, discovery entitlement, evidentiary hearing, benefit offset or damages award is ordered.
