## Event

Bradley v. Arkansas Department of Education. Merits decision, June 12, 2000; certiorari granted March 20, 2000; submitted on full merits briefing April 25, 2000. On writ of certiorari to the United States Court of Appeals for the Eighth Circuit, reviewing 189 F.3d 745 (August 31, 1999), appellate No. 98-1010. Review is confined to the Bradley IDEA immunity ruling; the accompanying appellate No. 98-1830, Jim C., is outside the grant.

Arkansas seeks reversal of the denial of immunity to the Department of Education and its employee Mike Crowley. The District Court's November 21, 1997 order in No. LR-C-96-1004 denied dismissal or summary judgment and stayed the merits during the immunity appeal. The Eighth Circuit affirmed the IDEA denial on accepted-funding terms and also discussed prospective officer relief. The questions concern clear federal-suit notice, actual acceptance and the scope of that consent, with independent abrogation grounds unnecessary if consent suffices.

Thomas and Dianna Bradley, on behalf of their son David, allege deficient procedures for review of his individualized education program and failure to perform IDEA responsibilities. The asserted hearing-timing and witness-process failures are allegations, not findings. They seek prospective compliance rather than compensatory or punitive damages.

## Participation

Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer participated at submission and decision.

## Public Action

The Court affirms the denial of sovereign immunity in the granted Bradley IDEA action and remands for ordinary proceedings.

## Judgment & Remedy

**Affirmed and remanded, 9–0.**

**Supporting:** Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer.

The judgment denying sovereign immunity in the Bradley IDEA action is affirmed on the funding-consent ground. The case returns through the Eighth Circuit for ordinary adjudication in the District Court. The Court establishes no violation of a free appropriate public education obligation or hearing requirement and awards no damages, injunction, fees or other relief. The same sovereign-immunity defense asserted for Crowley in official capacity fails within the accepted condition; personal-capacity liability is not decided. No judgment is entered concerning Jim C., Section 504 or other ungranted claims.

## Holdings

**Common authority:** Justice Thomas's Opinion of the Court is joined in full by Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Ginsburg and Breyer. All nine directly adopt the following holding, application and limits.

### Actual acceptance of the disclosed IDEA funding condition supplies consent

**Holding and operative rule:** A State that voluntarily accepts IDEA assistance after the effective express condition in §§1403 and 1415 consents to federal judicial enforcement of the obligations of that funded program, within the recipient, claim and remedy covered by the condition. Arkansas's acceptance under that notice removes sovereign immunity from the Bradley IDEA action, including the same sovereign immunity asserted for the named officer in his official capacity; it supplies neither a general waiver for Arkansas nor consent inferred from ordinary regulated activity.

**Controlling explanation:** The condition identifies the State, the federal forum and violations of IDEA. Section 1415 places federal judicial review among the procedures attached to the funded education program. Arkansas conceded both the spending-power validity of IDEA and its receipt of IDEA funds; its acceptance followed the express immunity condition. The present record supplies no retroactive surprise or coercive demand defeating that acceptance.

Atascadero requires unmistakable notice of federal suit. That requirement is satisfied here. Dellmuth identifies the earlier absence of the necessary statutory clarity; the express provision cures that notice defect. The heading and abrogation phrasing of §1403 do not obscure the consequence that the operative text discloses to a recipient. Congressional intent alone is insufficient, but the State's actual acceptance supplies the additional choice.

College Savings Bank rejects consent inferred from continued ordinary commerce and leaves particular accepted-benefit conditions unresolved. Accepting this identified assistance on disclosed enforcement terms is the different transaction that was absent there. South Dakota v. Dole requires a relationship between the condition and the funded undertaking; enforcing the duties of this very program satisfies that requirement. Cedar Rapids preserves an expressly disclosed IDEA obligation despite substantial compliance costs; cost alone does not erase the condition. None of these decisions dispenses with actual acceptance or establishes a universal waiver from federal funding.

The consent ground resolves the immunity appeal. The Court need not decide independent Article I or Section 5 abrogation. A difference between IDEA's protection and the constitutional minimum does not itself defeat preventive enforcement, as Boerne and Florida Prepaid establish, but neither does it establish IDEA's constitutional validity. No individual constitutional violation is an added prerequisite to proceeding on the consent ground.

**Operative qualifications and limits:** Section 1403(c) applies to violations occurring in whole or in part after October 30, 1990. Section 1403(b) makes legal and equitable remedies available only to the same extent as against another public entity; it creates no additional category of relief. The Bradleys seek prospective compliance, not compensatory or punitive damages. Lane's distinction between substantive coverage and consent to a particular remedy remains applicable; no unrequested damages question is decided.

Section 1415(i)(2) permits an aggrieved party's civil action concerning the presented complaint after the specified administrative decision and appeal route: a party aggrieved under subsection (f) or (k) without a subsection (g) appeal, or a party aggrieved by the administrative appeal decision, may proceed in a competent state court or federal district court without an amount-in-controversy requirement. The court receives the administrative record, hears additional evidence at a party's request, and grants appropriate relief on the preponderance of the evidence. Section 1415(l) preserves rights, procedures and remedies under the Constitution, the Americans with Disabilities Act of 1990, title V of the Rehabilitation Act of 1973, and other federal laws protecting children with disabilities. Before a civil action under those laws seeking relief also available under IDEA is filed, it requires exhaustion of subsections (f) and (g) to the same extent as for an IDEA action. Immunity denial neither finds these predicates satisfied nor excuses them.

The Court decides no educational violation, hearing violation, exhaustion issue, individual liability or particular decree. An independent prospective-officer claim still requires a connected officer, an ongoing federal violation and genuinely prospective relief; a treasury claim for past injury cannot become prospective by naming an employee. The Court creates no new defendant or officer claim. Jim C. and its Section 504 claim are outside the grant; IDEA acceptance establishes no Section 504 waiver. That statute's own federal-suit notice, accepted assistance and remedial scope require separate consideration in a case properly presenting them.

## Event

Carter v. United States, No. 99-5716. Merits decision, June 12, 2000; argued April 19, 2000. On writ of certiorari to the Third Circuit, 185 F.3d 863 (1999), affirming the District of New Jersey conviction under the completed-taking branch of the first paragraph of 18 U.S.C. §2113(a). The question is whether bank larceny under §2113(b) is a necessarily included offense under Federal Rule of Criminal Procedure 31(c).

Carter admitted taking bank money but disputed force or intimidation. He entered the bank on September 9, 1997 wearing a ski mask, crossed the teller counter and fled with approximately $16,000. His contact with a departing customer is disputed: his account describes an accidental bump, while the Government describes a push back into the bank. The indictment alleges approximately $14,000, a distinct amount, in a knowing and willful taking by force, violence and intimidation from a teller's person and presence at an FDIC-insured bank. The jury returned its verdict January 23, 1998; the robbery sentence was 215 months. Separate Pennsylvania larceny convictions and concurrent sentences are outside this review.

The trial court rejected the larceny instruction as categorically unavailable under circuit precedent. Carter's letter asserted a broader larceny theory; the attached charge described the higher grade using the obsolete $100 threshold. The applicable statute uses $1,000. The Court does not rewrite that request or resolve the disputed contact.

## Participation

Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer participated at argument and decision.

## Public Action

The Court affirms the Third Circuit's judgment on the denied bank-larceny instruction.

## Judgment & Remedy

**Supporting:** Stone-Zsela, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy and Thomas.
**Opposing:** Stevens, Souter, Ginsburg and Breyer.

The Third Circuit's judgment is affirmed as against the refusal of the requested §2113(b) instruction. Carter's robbery conviction and sentence remain undisturbed on this ground. No acquittal, substituted larceny conviction, sentence reduction or new trial is ordered. Independent issues not before the Court and the separate Pennsylvania convictions are unaffected.

## Holdings

**Authority:** O'Connor's Opinion of the Court is joined throughout by Stone-Zsela, Scalia, Kennedy and Thomas. These five directly support Parts I, II and IV. All nine join Part III's additional-value proposition and its limited subset conclusion. Each independent ground below has its own stated scope; a shared value holding does not resolve basic larceny by itself.

### I. Specific theft intent is an additional larceny element

**Holding and operative rule:** In the completed forcible-or-intimidating taking charged here, §2113(a)'s first paragraph requires knowing conduct satisfying its taking conditions, but does not require §2113(b)'s additional intent to steal or purloin. That additional purpose independently prevents either grade of §2113(b) from being necessarily included under Rule 31(c).

**Controlling explanation:** Schmuck compares statutory elements as legally construed, not the facts that happen to establish both crimes. Section 2113(b) expressly adds a theft purpose to its taking requirement; the charged portion of subsection (a) specifies knowing conduct involving force and violence or intimidation without that additional purpose. The separate entry paragraph expressly requires an intent to commit a qualifying felony or larceny. Its wording cannot be transplanted into the completed-taking branch.

Morissette requires attention to inherited culpability, and Staples and X-Citement Video require the fault appropriate to the enacted offense. They do not require every common-law incident of an analogous offense when the statutory description already identifies culpable conduct. This construction does not punish an innocent accidental taking or remove the knowledge requirement. The claim that removal of feloniously was stylistic and robbery traditionally aggravated larceny cannot overcome the enacted contrast. Bell confirms that this statutory scheme is not simply an unchanged common-law code. The Court construes those descriptions; different wording alone is not a mechanical element test.

### II. Carrying away independently prevents inclusion

**Holding and operative rule:** Section 2113(b) requires taking and carrying away the property, while the charged completed-taking branch of §2113(a) does not require the additional carrying away. That difference independently defeats inclusion of either larceny grade, even if their culpability requirements were assumed to coincide.

**Controlling explanation:** Congress wrote both taking and carrying away in subsection (b), but taking by the stated forcible means in the relevant part of subsection (a). The argument that takes implicitly includes the slight movement associated with common-law larceny is substantial, but it would erase the separately enacted movement requirement in comparing these descriptions. The common-law relationship does not compel identical statutory ingredients. Schmuck requires that each lesser element be necessary to the greater, rather than that the charged conduct ordinarily satisfies both. Carter's actual flight with the money cannot establish that legal necessity. The result rests on construction of the completed offense, not simply on the separate availability of an attempt prosecution.

### III. The higher grade also requires value exceeding $1,000

**Holding and operative rule:** Value exceeding $1,000 is an element of §2113(b)'s grade carrying a ten-year maximum, and is not merely a sentencing factor. Because the charged §2113(a) taking has no value threshold, that higher larceny grade is not itself a statutory subset of robbery.

**Controlling explanation:** The value language occurs in the description of prohibited conduct and is repeated in the separate higher and lower grades. Its role and sharply different maximum punishments support offense-element treatment. Almendarez-Torres, Jones and Castillo supply statute-specific aggravated-element analogues, without creating a universal constitutional rule for every maximum-increasing fact. Carter's original sentencing-factor theory fails under the enacted structure.

The approximately $14,000 allegation and approximately $16,000 trial account do not make a value threshold an element of statutory robbery. Schmuck's comparison does not become an indictment-specific comparison merely because the amount is alleged. The correct threshold is $1,000, following the 1996 amendment; the proposed $100 instruction is not silently corrected. This holding alone does not decide the lower larceny grade, whose inclusion the Court rejects on Parts I and II. The Court does not determine whether the lower paragraph demands affirmative proof that value cannot exceed $1,000.

### IV. The elements failure resolves the requested instruction

**Holding and operative rule:** Because neither larceny grade is necessarily included in the charged completed robbery under the Court's intent and movement constructions, the denied §2113(b) instruction supplies no ground to disturb the conviction. A defendant's request alone does not create a Rule 31(c) exception to those statutory differences.

**Controlling explanation:** The elements test protects charging responsibility, notice and the jury's legally available choices. Carter's admission of taking money and dispute about force cannot change it. Keeble explains why an included alternative may be necessary when a rational jury could convict of the lesser while acquitting of the greater; it does not dispense with statutory inclusion. The Court therefore need not decide the Government's independent rational-jury argument or resolve the disputed physical contact.

The proposed election of a higher grade presupposes an independently included basic theft, which Parts I and II reject. Unilateral consent to notice also does not itself answer Schmuck's reciprocity objection or the independent charging safeguards. Stirone protects the actual grand-jury charge but does not itself decide every defendant-requested grade proposal. Awareness of facts, consent to an instruction, legal sufficiency of the indictment, grand-jury waiver and admission of an offense element remain distinct. No missing value allegation or completed waiver is found. The Court reserves whether the full indictment independently charges another offense and decides no separate charging route.

**Limits common to the judgment:** The decision concerns the charged completed-taking configuration, not every extortion, attempt or entry offense. It decides neither entitlement to another offense instruction nor any independent trial error. Section 2113(a) permits a fine under title 18, imprisonment up to twenty years, or both. Section 2113(b)'s higher grade permits a fine under title 18, imprisonment up to ten years, or both; its lower paragraph specifies a fine no greater than $1,000, imprisonment up to one year, or both. These descriptions create no new sentencing determination in Carter's case.

- **Staples v. United States; United States v. X-Citement Video:** Apply contextual fault analysis; neither requires the additional theft purpose for this already culpable taking. X-Citement Video's distinction between an existing factual finding and unresolved indictment consequences remains.

## Separate Writings

**Ginsburg, dissenting in part, joined by Stevens, Souter and Breyer; all four join Part III.** The dissent construes the charged completed forcible taking to retain theft intent and movement. Robbery's inherited relation to larceny, the statutory context and the stylistic deletion of feloniously support those legally construed elements. Morissette and the contextual approaches of Staples and X-Citement Video support that reading; Bell's broader theft coverage does not require removing these ingredients from completed robbery. The dissent applies Schmuck's elements test rather than reviving a factual-relationship test.

The four agree that the higher grade adds value exceeding $1,000 and is not a statutory subset. They construe the lower paragraph as the basic theft grade, with a one-year maximum when aggravated value is not established as an offense element. The Government need not prove that the sum could not exceed $1,000: taking more money does not make basic theft innocent. The lower-value language limits unaggravated punishment; proof of a larger amount alone does not authorize the higher maximum without the aggravated offense's requirements.

On that construction basic larceny is included. Carter's letter asserted larceny as the answer to disputed force or intimidation, while the attached charge used a deficient higher-grade formulation. The categorical circuit rule bypassed whether the actual broader request sought a legally available basic instruction. The dissent would vacate that categorical determination and return for request scope, ordinary preservation, treatment of the correct threshold and the Government's independent rational-jury contention. Proof sufficient for robbery does not establish that every rational jury must reject only-theft treatment. The dissent makes no credibility finding and requires no instruction on every unrequested lesser crime.

The dissent reserves both adoption and categorical rejection of a higher-grade election. An affirmative request and the indictment's actual amount allegation are material, but do not alone settle Schmuck's reciprocity concern or all charging consequences. The limited grade-election proposal must be distinguished from factual relatedness and supported under the actual charge and any legally necessary consent or waiver. Almendarez-Torres, Jones and Castillo preserve the distinction between factual awareness and the proper aggravated charge; X-Citement Video preserves indictment questions despite an existing culpability finding. Those decisions do not forbid every valid defendant election or establish a missing allegation here. Stirone likewise does not by itself resolve this proposal. No grand-jury waiver, element admission or new charge is inferred.

The proposed return could still end in denial on a lawful request or evidentiary ground. Error and remedy follow only if an included, properly requested and evidence-supported instruction should have been given. The dissent orders neither acquittal, entry of a larceny conviction, sentence reduction nor an automatic new trial and finds neither harmfulness nor harmlessness.

## Event

Pegram v. Herdrich, No. 98-1949. Merits decision, June 12, 2000; argued February 23, 2000. On writ of certiorari to the Seventh Circuit, 154 F.3d 362 (1998), reviewing its revival of an amended ERISA fiduciary-breach count. The issue is whether the challenged HMO treatment and incentive functions support that count. The posture is failure to state a claim, not findings after an ERISA trial.

Herdrich participated through her husband's State Farm employment in an HMO option. HAMP was owned through Carle Clinic, whose physician shareholders supplied care. Medical necessity and outside referrals depended on Carle physicians' judgment; the alleged incentives rewarded lower testing, outside-facility use and referrals. Herdrich alleged that an ultrasound was delayed after discovery of an inflamed abdominal mass and that she was required to travel to a distant Carle facility. Her appendix ruptured, causing peritonitis. Her malpractice claims produced a $35,000 jury judgment, which is outside this review.

The reviewed amended count seeks plan-focused relief under §§409 and 502(a)(2) for the general incentive arrangement and refers to administration of disputed or nonroutine claims. It names no individual physician. Two state fraud counts preceded removal; one was dismissed and the other amended into this ERISA count. The superseded fraud pleading is not an independently presented nondisclosure claim.

## Participation

Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer participated at argument and decision.

## Public Action

The Court reverses revival of the amended ERISA fiduciary count and directs its dismissal on remand.

## Judgment & Remedy

**Reversed and remanded, 9–0.**

**Supporting:** Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer.

The Seventh Circuit's revival of the reviewed amended fiduciary-breach count is reversed. On remand that count shall be dismissed. The $35,000 malpractice judgment and unreviewed counts remain undisturbed. No damages, plan restoration amount, disgorgement, fee award, new individual-physician claim or entitlement to amend is ordered.

## Opinion Topology

| Writing | Author | Joined by | Relationship to judgment | Scope joined |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Opinion of the Court | Ginsburg | Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas and Breyer throughout; Stone-Zsela in Part I only | Unanimous reversal and dismissal mandate | I: function-specific mixed-treatment rule; II: ordinary risk sharing and plan-asset predicates; III: independent assumed-administration pleading ground and its limits |
| Concurrence in part and in the judgment | Stone-Zsela | No other Justice | Joins Part I and reversal with dismissal of the amended count | Mixed-treatment ground; reserves independent administration, misrepresentation and asset-misuse questions rather than joining Parts II–III |

## Holdings

### I. Inseparable treatment decisions are not fiduciary administration

**Holding and operative rule:** A treating physician does not act as an ERISA fiduciary when making an inseparable decision about medically appropriate treatment and the benefit delivered through that treatment. A separate discretionary decision applying plan-coverage terms can be fiduciary administration, whoever makes it; the rule creates no general HMO exemption and does not exclude every decision involving medical information.

**Authority:** Part I of Ginsburg's Opinion of the Court, joined by all eight other Justices, supplies nine direct votes.

**Controlling explanation:** Section 3(21)(A) attaches fiduciary status to the specified function, rather than every act of a person who performs some plan duties. Varity applies that inquiry to actual administration and protects participants from disloyal administrative conduct. It does not convert medical judgment into plan administration simply because treatment also determines the benefit furnished. The medical-necessity and referral decisions inseparable from furnishing the care here belong to the treatment function. HMO incentives do not, merely by their existence, change that capacity.

Herdrich correctly distinguishes the employer's choice of a plan from a nonsponsor's later acts. Spink and Hughes Aircraft preserve independent fiduciary implementation. The present boundary therefore depends on the act, not employer or corporate status. A physician can administer coverage, and calling a decision medical cannot conceal that distinct function. Conversely, financial consequences do not turn ordinary treatment into ERISA administration. Travelers preserves the distinct setting of ordinary medical regulation; this holding resolves fiduciary status and enters no general malpractice-preemption judgment.

### II. An ordinary incentive arrangement alone establishes neither fiduciary breach nor plan assets

**Holding and operative rule:** Establishing or carrying out an ordinary general compensation and risk-sharing arrangement for providing medical care does not, by that fact alone, establish ERISA fiduciary conduct or breach. The HMO itself is not the employer's ERISA plan, and its revenues and profits are not automatically plan assets; actual authority or control over management or disposition of a plan asset remains governed by the statutory asset branch without an additional discretion requirement.

**Authority:** Part II has eight direct supporters: Ginsburg, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas and Breyer. Stone-Zsela does not join.

**Controlling explanation:** John Hancock requires separate consideration of property, function and liability. The premise that the business furnishing the benefit is itself the plan would improperly make its entire income subject to plan restoration. No general ownership rule governing every premium arrangement follows from rejecting that premise.

Spink separates design from implementation, and Hughes Aircraft examines independently alleged breaches instead of treating economic advantage as breach by itself. The same distinctions matter here. Ordinary medical risk sharing compensates for the service arrangement; it does not alone establish the exercise of an ERISA function or a disloyal act in such a function. Physician ownership does not erase the statutory requirements. This is neither a general net-benefit defense to proved misconduct nor approval of every incentive system. A direct reward for denying a claim, an arrangement making impartial administration impossible, genuine asset diversion and actual disloyal administration present different predicates, none adjudicated by the pleaded general-arrangement theory.

### III. The amended structural theory fails even assuming genuine claims administration

**Holding and operative rule:** Even assuming in Herdrich's favor that the amended reference to disputed and nonroutine claims describes genuine discretionary plan administration, the pleaded general year-end incentive arrangement alone states no independently disloyal claims determination, administration contrary to the plan or direct reward for denying a claim. The reviewed amended count therefore fails on that independent ground, and restoration of supplemental medical-expense payments cannot follow merely by labeling the entire HMO the plan.

**Authority:** Part III is joined by the same eight Associates as Part II and independently supports dismissal. Stone-Zsela joins the judgment on Part I's treatment ground, not this additional rationale.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court gives the administration allegation its asserted scope for this inquiry. It does not find a new administrative event or erase that allegation by treating every HMO decision as treatment. Varity preserves concrete administrative loyalty duties, while Hughes Aircraft requires the alleged implementation theory itself to identify a breach after its legal predicates are examined. The ordinary structural incentive does not alone supply that missing breach.

The strongest contrary argument combines owner control, decision authority and the incentive into self-interested administration. Those circumstances may matter when an actual disloyal determination is alleged, but they do not establish that the ordinary arrangement itself violates the duty or that all business funds belong to the plan. The lower court disclaimed automatic liability for all incentives and characterized care as withheld solely to enlarge bonuses. That characterization and the malpractice verdict are not independent findings of disloyal plan administration or plan loss. The Court applies the ordinary pleading standard and resolves no disputed medical-adequacy question.

Section 409's responsibility and plan-restoration framework and §502(a)(2)'s authorized enforcement channel remain distinct from §502(a)(3)'s individual equitable channel. Mertens's limit on §502(a)(3) compensation is not the ground for dismissal of this §409/§502(a)(2) claim. The requested plan remedy fails on the pleaded predicates, not a categorical rule barring lawful plan restoration.

**Limits and questions not reached:** Section 3(21)(A) separately reaches discretionary plan management, authority or control over management or disposition of plan assets, compensated investment advice or authority or responsibility to provide it, and discretionary authority or responsibility in plan administration. It also includes persons designated under §405(c)(1)(B). Its registered-investment-company qualification provides that plan investment in a registered company's securities alone does not make the company, adviser or principal underwriter a fiduciary or party in interest, except insofar as the company, its investment adviser or its principal underwriter acts in connection with a plan covering employees of the company, adviser or principal underwriter; duties under other law remain. That qualification is not a factual issue here.

No independent disclosure, benefits-reimbursement, contract, malpractice-preemption, regulatory, personal-physician-liability or particular asset-ownership claim is decided. Prior statements about administrator or fiduciary status elsewhere do not establish the challenged function or a supported judicial-estoppel bar here; disclosure responsibility and responsibility for the disclosed incentive are distinct. The amended count does not revive superseded fraud allegations. Direct per-denied-claim rewards, actual asset misuse, misrepresentations and genuinely disloyal administration retain their own statutory predicates and remedies. The Court awards no plan amount and establishes no fee or amendment entitlement.

## Precedent Treatment

- **Varity Corp. v. Howe:** Applies the function-specific inquiry and preserves concrete fiduciary administration and disloyal-misrepresentation liability; its individual equitable-redress holding does not convert this plan-focused claim into a different remedy channel.
- **Lockheed Corp. v. Spink:** Applies the separation of design and actual implementation without immunizing independent administration; sponsor status alone does not decide this claim.
- **Hughes Aircraft Co. v. Jacobson:** Applies its independent examination of implementation and breach predicates rather than equating an advantageous structure with misconduct. Its actual-asset and genuine-administration reservations remain.

- **Firestone Tire & Rubber Co. v. Bruch:** Preserves trust-law guidance within the enacted fiduciary functions; discretion over treatment alone is not discretion in plan administration.
- **Mertens v. Hewitt Associates:** Preserves §502(a)(3)'s traditional-equity limits but does not bar lawful §409 restoration or supply the reason for dismissing this §502(a)(2) claim.
- **New York State Conference of Blue Cross & Blue Shield Plans v. Travelers Insurance Co.; De Buono v. NYSA-ILA Medical and Clinical Services Fund:** Preserve the distinct operation of ordinary medical regulation; the Court makes no general preemption ruling here.

## Law After Decision

Medical treatment inseparable from the benefit furnished is outside the fiduciary-administration function, while genuine coverage administration remains subject to ERISA. The eight-Justice alternative separately requires an independently supported breach even when administration is assumed; the ordinary general incentive alone does not supply it. HMO form supplies neither fiduciary status for treatment nor immunity for administration. Function, actual plan property, breach and statutory relief remain separate inquiries.

## Event

Reeves v. Sanderson Plumbing Products, Inc., No. 99-536. Merits decision, June 12, 2000; argued March 21, 2000. On writ of certiorari to the Fifth Circuit, 197 F.3d 688 (1999), which displaced a jury's age-discrimination verdict on Rule 50 sufficiency grounds. The questions concern the inference from prima facie proof and disbelief of the employer's stated reason, and the proper examination of the trial record on judgment as a matter of law.

Reeves was fifty-seven and had worked for Sanderson for forty years. He supervised a regular production line in the Hinge Room. Department manager Caldwell was forty-five; Oswalt, in his thirties, supervised another line. After an audit, the company asserted inaccurate attendance and time records. Manufacturing director Chesnut, the company president's spouse, joined the recommendation to discharge Reeves and Caldwell. Oswalt had already left; the employer's assertion that it would also have discharged him is not an actual discharge.

Reeves disputed the recordkeeping accusations through testimony and documents and offered age-directed comments, differential treatment and evidence of Chesnut's dominant influence. The company disputes that evidence's accuracy, timing, meaning and connection to discharge and relies on other participants and older managers. The jury found age discrimination and willfulness. The District Court awarded $35,000 and matching liquidated damages and later $28,490.80 in front pay. The employer made and renewed Rule 50 motions and alternatively requested a new trial. The Court reviews the sufficiency ruling, not a new calculation of these awards.

## Participation

Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer participated at argument and decision.

## Public Action

The Court reverses the Fifth Circuit's judgment for the employer and remands within the verdict-preserving limits stated below.

## Judgment & Remedy

**Reversed and remanded, 9–0.**

**Supporting:** Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer.

The Fifth Circuit's Rule 50 judgment for the employer is reversed. The trial evidence is legally sufficient to support the age-discrimination verdict; the verdict is to stand unless an ordinary, properly preserved and verdict-compatible independent ground warrants further relief. The remand permits consideration of remaining appellate issues, including an unresolved alternative new-trial ground if otherwise reviewable. It does not reopen the sufficiency question decided here, direct a particular new trial or make a new monetary, liquidated-damages or front-pay award.

## Opinion Topology

| Writing | Author | Joined by | Relationship to judgment | Scope joined |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Opinion of the Court | Scalia | Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer | Unanimous reversal and bounded remand | Entire permissive-inference, whole-record Rule 50, actual-sufficiency and remedy holding |

## Holdings

**Common authority:** Justice Scalia's Opinion of the Court is joined in full by the other eight Justices identified in Opinion Topology. All nine directly support each rule, application and remedial qualification below.

### I. Prima facie proof and material falsity can support discrimination

**Holding and operative rule:** A prima facie case together with evidence permitting rejection of the employer's material explanation may support a finding of intentional discrimination without an additional category of direct or independent proof. It neither compels liability nor invariably defeats judgment as a matter of law: the strength of the initial inference, the probative force of falsity and the rest of the evidence the court must credit determine whether discrimination is a reasonable inference.

**Controlling explanation:** Hicks already rejects both automatic liability and a demand for a special further kind of evidence. Aikens recognizes that the employer's actual motivation can be proved circumstantially. Once the employer produces a nondiscriminatory account, Burdine leaves the ultimate burden of persuasion with the plaintiff; rejecting the account does not revive a compulsory presumption. But a factfinder may reasonably regard an invented account as evidence concealing discrimination, considered with the initial showing and the whole record.

Hazen Paper requires age actually to motivate the decision. Falsehood, unfairness, personal hostility or another unlawful motive is not automatically age discrimination. A conclusively established different lawful reason can defeat the proposed inference. So can weak falsity evidence together with otherwise conclusive independent evidence against discrimination. These are applications of ordinary sufficiency, not a checklist demanding more evidence in every case. Merely imagining another possible motive does not defeat an inference reasonably supported by the actual proof. Substantial prima facie and falsity evidence can therefore sustain liability without a separate evidentiary category. The Court does not define every insufficient future case, alter an unpresented mixed-motive allocation or decide disparate impact.

### II. Rule 50 preserves reasonable jury inferences while considering the whole record

**Holding and operative rule:** Rule 50 requires consideration of the whole record, drawing reasonable inferences for the verdict winner and making no credibility choices or weighing of evidence. The court disregards evidence favorable to the movant that the jury was free to disbelieve, while considering uncontradicted and unimpeached evidence the jury was required to accept; testimony from disinterested witnesses is a relevant circumstance, not an inflexible witness-label rule.

**Controlling explanation:** Anderson supplies the distinction between determining whether a reasonable jury can find for a party and deciding which evidence is more persuasive. A court cannot displace the verdict by crediting disputed employer testimony or selecting among reasonable accounts. It also cannot exclude every item simply because the employer introduced it: source alone does not determine whether the jury was free to reject the evidence.

Weisgram preserves appellate authority to direct judgment on a properly preserved Rule 50 motion when the admissible record is insufficient, including after correction of an evidentiary admission. That authority is not permission to reweigh a valid record. Evidence conclusively establishing a lawful explanation remains material, but disputed testimony and reasonable contrary inferences must receive the treatment Rule 50 requires. The Court uses the ordinary sufficiency standard rather than either complete deference to all verdicts or categorical invisibility of movant evidence.

### III. This record supports the verdict and cannot be rejected for lack of extra proof

**Holding and operative rule:** Reeves's prima facie showing, material evidence rebutting the recordkeeping explanation, and evidence connecting age-directed comments to Chesnut's role and influence permit a reasonable finding of intentional age discrimination. The employer is not entitled to judgment as a matter of law on this record, and the remand cannot restore the same sufficiency judgment through a renewed demand for additional proof.

**Controlling explanation:** Reeves's testimony and documents addressed the actual attendance practices, time-clock use and disciplinary records. The jury could find the employer's account materially unworthy of belief. The age-directed comments were accompanied by evidence of Chesnut's participation in the recommendation and influence within the company. The question is their connection to the decision in the complete record, not whether a label excludes them from consideration.

The contrary accounts about timing, other decisionmakers and older employees do not conclusively defeat that inference. Crediting those accounts as decisive would require the weighing Rule 50 forbids. Oswalt's earlier departure cannot be treated as a completed discharge of a younger comparator; the claimed hypothetical action remains the employer's assertion. Consolidated Coin keeps relative-age evidence tied to the actual inference rather than making protected-group membership dispositive. The combined evidence supports the jury's age finding under Hazen Paper, not merely a conclusion that the dismissal was unfair.

This is a determination of the actual evidence's legal sufficiency, not a rule that every pretext case succeeds. Weisgram preserves genuinely independent, properly presented new-trial and remedial issues. Any such issue must be addressed on its own basis and consistently with the sufficiency holding. The Court neither assumes that an alternative ground is preserved and meritorious nor prescribes a new trial or award. Willfulness and the existing monetary components receive no new determination merely from this reversal.

**Limits:** Section 623(a)(1) concerns covered employment action because of age; §631(a) begins the Act's age protection at forty. The Court does not decide every ADEA proof framework or impose an under-forty comparator requirement. Employer production, plaintiff persuasion, evidentiary sufficiency and remaining remedial questions remain distinct. No liability follows merely from pension status, a mistaken business judgment, personal animosity or any false explanation.

## Precedent Treatment

- **St. Mary's Honor Center v. Hicks:** Applies its existing rejection of both compulsory liability from pretext alone and a mandatory further category of proof. No replacement persuasion allocation or revived presumption is adopted.
- **Texas Department of Community Affairs v. Burdine; McDonnell Douglas Corp. v. Green:** Preserve employer production and ultimate plaintiff persuasion under the assumed indirect framework; disbelief has evidentiary force without shifting that burden.
- **Postal Service Board of Governors v. Aikens:** Applies the focus on actual discrimination and the availability of circumstantial proof.
- **Hazen Paper Co. v. Biggins:** Applies actual age motivation as the liability question; falsehood or another improper motive is not by itself age discrimination. Its willfulness and reserved disparate-impact issues are not redecided.
- **O'Connor v. Consolidated Coin Caterers Corp.:** Preserves the evidentiary significance of relative age without an under-forty replacement condition or a categorical result from workforce ages.
- **Anderson v. Liberty Lobby, Inc.:** Applies the prohibition on weighing and credibility choices in determining whether a reasonable factfinder may rule for the protected party.
- **Weisgram v. Marley Co.; Neely v. Martin K. Eby Construction Co.:** Preserve lawful appellate Rule 50 authority and proper consideration of independently supported new-trial grounds. Neither permits reweighing this sufficient record or guarantees a new trial on remand.

## Law After Decision

Hicks's permissive inference governs ordinary discrimination proof without a special additional-evidence requirement. Courts evaluate its strength in the whole record under Rule 50, keeping ultimate persuasion with the plaintiff and preserving truly conclusive contrary proof. The Court adds a controlling application sustaining this verdict's evidentiary basis. Remaining independent appellate grounds may be considered within ordinary preservation and remedy limits; they cannot recreate the rejected sufficiency demand.

Its judgment for the employer is reversed; Reeves's verdict is reinstated unless an ordinary independent, properly preserved and verdict-compatible ground warrants further relief. Any unresolved alternative new-trial ground must be considered only if otherwise reviewable. The Court orders no particular retrial, new award or front-pay calculation and leaves no license to redetermine the legal sufficiency resolved here.

## Event

Arizona v. California, No. 8, Original. Original-jurisdiction decision on exceptions to Special Master Frank McGarr's report and two settlement recommendations; argued April 25, 2000; decided June 19, 2000.

The Court considers whether the State parties may assert the Arizona I preclusion defense to the enlarged Fort Yuma reserved-water claim, whether the 1983 Docket 320 compromise or section 22 of the Indian Claims Commission Act bars that claim, and whether to approve the Fort Mojave and Colorado River Reservation agreements. There is no appellate judgment to affirm or reverse.

The Master rejected the original-decree defense but accepted a bar based on Docket 320. The State parties except to the former ruling; the United States and Quechan Tribe except to the latter. The Master recommended the two independent settlements, and no party filed an exception to those recommendations. The absence of exceptions does not itself compel approval.

The disputed Fort Yuma claim arises from the conditional 1893 agreement ratified in 1894. Interior's 1978 and 1981 orders changed the Government's legal treatment of that transaction. The United States and Tribe later compromised Docket 320 for $15 million. Neither the administrative position nor the compensation agreement supplies a final boundary determination binding the competing States.

## Participation

Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer participate at argument and decision.

## Public Action

The Court overrules the State parties' Arizona I exception, sustains the United States' and Tribe's Docket 320 exceptions, rejects the separately asserted statutory bar, approves both settlement recommendations, and returns the unresolved Fort Yuma issues to the Master.

## Holdings

### I. The belated Arizona I defense is unavailable in this proceeding

**Holding and operative rule:** In this continuing original action, the State parties forfeited their Arizona I claim-preclusion defense by omitting it when the enlarged Fort Yuma demand was presented in 1979 and again during the 1982 proceedings, despite the opportunity to assert it, and first presenting it in 1989 after the litigation had proceeded on the different grounds and boundary-review course described here. The Court overrules that exception without deciding whether a timely defense would have barred the enlarged claim.

**Authority:** Part I of Stevens's Opinion of the Court, joined by Stone-Zsela, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Ginsburg and Breyer, controls by seven direct joins.

**Controlling explanation:** Arizona II recognizes the importance of finality within an ongoing original action. That framework permits consideration of the ordinary responsibility to present an affirmative defense when the controversy supplies the opportunity, without mechanically importing every pleading deadline. Rules 8(c) and 15(d) illustrate that responsibility and the availability of later pleadings; the original pleadings' earlier date did not remove the later opportunities here.

Counsel's later recognition of the argument does not excuse the missed stages. Nor did resolution of other questions at those stages prevent presentation. The States stipulated to the 1979 adjustment provisions and sought judicial determination of disputed boundaries. That course reinforces the particular procedural conclusion; it is not contractual surrender of every defense. The later decrees' coverage of all five reservations and Arizona II's reservation of a possible original-action route likewise do not decide the preclusion merits or confer unlimited reopening authority.

The later reopening order and the Master's consideration of the defense do not require disregarding the earlier forfeiture. Limited judicial authority to raise preclusion to avoid repeating an actual prior adjudication supplies no mandatory rescue here: the disputed title predicate was not actually decided. The Court adopts no general prohibition on appropriate judicial initiative and does not deny that claim preclusion can reach unlitigated theories.

This is forfeiture from the supported litigation course, not a finding of intentional waiver, estoppel or a concession in an unavailable response. Interior's changed legal view is not newly discovered fact defeating otherwise applicable preclusion. The unresolved title, cession, boundary, irrigable-acreage and water-quantity predicates require adjudication before any additional Fort Yuma water may be awarded.

### II. The compensation compromise does not decide the reserved-water claim

**Holding and operative rule:** Docket 320's consent judgment finally settles the United States' and Tribe's claims against one another within that docket's reciprocal release, but it neither actually nor by a clearly intended binding determination resolves the disputed title predicate and does not release the distinct reserved-water demand against competing users. The Master's asserted consent-judgment bar therefore fails, while the compromise's lawful claim-finality effect remains intact.

**Authority:** All nine Justices join Part II. Agreement on this independent bar does not eliminate O'Connor and Thomas's different original-allocation ground.

**Controlling explanation:** The $15 million judgment compromises alternative invalid-cession, trespass and uncompensated-taking theories. The disposition of those alternatives does not establish that one particular title theory was adjudicated. International Building requires attention to what a compromise actually determined before assigning issue-preclusive force. The reciprocal release addresses claims between the United States and Tribe; its no-admission clause reinforces the absence of an intended title adjudication without nullifying the judgment or its final releases. The appeal waiver also stands.

Matsushita preserves lawful settlement effect rather than exempting consent judgments from recognition. Baker distinguishes that effect from an additional operation the instrument does not establish. Richards requires a lawful basis for binding another person's rights. Those decisions concern distinct settings, including state judgments under section 1738; none directly determines this federal judgment's scope or creates a general tribal, public-interest or consent-judgment exception.

The State parties' strongest argument is that the same lands underlie both controversies. That factual overlap does not supply the missing claim identity, release scope or actual title determination. Conversely, the Court does not independently adopt the United States' entire characterization of the consideration as compensation for particular temporary and permanent losses. The judgment's actual terms and unresolved alternatives suffice to reject the asserted bar, without finding title or additional water entitlement.

### III. Section 22 does not establish the asserted third-party bar from this payment

**Holding and operative rule:** Payment of this alternative-theory compromise does not itself establish a taking, extinguishment of tribal title or release of dependent reserved-water claims against all persons under section 22 of the Indian Claims Commission Act. The distinct statutory discharge asserted by the State parties therefore does not bar the Fort Yuma claim on this record; differently supported judgments with an actual title or extinguishment predicate remain open for their own analysis.

**Authority:** Part III is joined by all eight Associate Justices. Stone-Zsela agrees only in the disposition of this independent statutory component and adds no ground.

**Controlling explanation:** Section 22(a) gives a Commission report determining entitlement to recovery, when filed with Congress, the effect of a final Court of Claims judgment and authorizes payment; payment discharges the United States from claims and demands touching the controversy. Section 22(b) separately makes an adverse final determination, made and reported under the Act, a bar to further claims against the United States arising from that matter. The recovery/payment trigger and the adverse-determination trigger are distinct, and both provisions identify claims against the United States.

The States contend that payment can also carry title consequences affecting claims against others. But the present instrument compromised alternative theories without determining that a taking had occurred or title had been extinguished. The statute does not turn that ambiguity into a conclusive finding of the missing predicate. The Court therefore need not decide the possible third-party consequences of an award that actually rests on an established extinguishment. Rejecting the statutory argument here neither restores land by decree nor establishes tribal ownership; it removes only the asserted automatic consequence of this compromise. The result rests independently of the ordinary claim- and issue-preclusion analysis in Part II.

### IV. The two submitted settlements are approved on their particular terms

**Holding and operative rule:** The Court approves the Fort Mojave and Colorado River Reservation agreements recommended by the Master and permits their incorporation into implementing decree provisions at the terms stated below. Approval rests on these agreements and this record under the Court's existing original-jurisdiction authority; it establishes no general approval test and resolves no additional title or water question.

**Authority:** All nine Justices join Part IV and both independent approvals.

**Controlling explanation:** The agreements resolve identified water and boundary disputes while stating the rights they reserve. The Master's recommendations and the supported terms provide the basis for approval. The parties' lack of exceptions does not itself command the result. The Fort Yuma division does not affect either separate agreement, and no conclusion about Fort Yuma's title or acreage supplies a premise for them. An implementing decree must give effect to each agreement's actual limits rather than enlarge its releases or convert reserved questions into adjudications.

**Fort Mojave terms:** The agreement specifies the reservation boundary near the Hay and Wood Reserve. It permits the lesser of an additional 3,022 acre-feet or enough water for 468 acres. The United States and Tribe may make no further reserved Colorado River water claims for lands within the Hay and Wood Reserve. Competing claims to title and jurisdiction over the bed of the last natural course of the Colorado River within that boundary remain reserved, as do private land claims, title and jurisdiction. Approval does not leave the specified reservation boundary wholly undetermined, but it does not decide those reserved riverbed and private rights.

**Colorado River Reservation terms:** The agreement permits the lesser of an additional 2,100 acre-feet or enough water to irrigate 315 acres. The United States and Tribes may make no further reserved Colorado River water claims for reservation lands in California. The disputed boundary remains unadjudicated in this proceeding, including the competing ambulatory-west-bank and fixed-line theories; competing riverbed-title and jurisdiction claims remain reserved. The agreement requires both the Master's and Court's approval; the Court supplies its approval here on the Master's recommended agreement.

**Common limits and procedure:** No new release, additional water number, private-title adjudication, universal settlement framework or present final Fort Yuma decree follows. The Master shall continue the unresolved Fort Yuma proceedings and provide appropriate implementing decree provisions for the approved agreements, subject to the Court's further action. No objection deadline or final supplemental-decree text is entered by this decision.

## Separate Writings

**O'Connor, dissenting in part, joined by Thomas; both join Parts II–IV.** They would entertain the original-decree defense and sustain it, refusing further adjudication of this enlarged Fort Yuma water demand. The action remains before the Court, the defense was expressly presented in the 1989 reopening motion, and the Court referred the dispute for the Master's consideration. The earlier limited proceedings and later reservation of possible boundary litigation support consideration now. They do not premise that conclusion on a finding that an unrecovered response lacked an objection.

On the merits, Arizona II's omitted-acreage treatment and Nevada v. United States's protection of a completed reserved-water allocation support finality. The Government possessed the relevant factual materials during the original litigation; a later legal reinterpretation of the 1893 transaction is not a new physical event or newly discovered fact. The reported maximum-demand representation and Master's warning reinforce the scope of the controversy. The absent-private-claimant obstacle associated with Fort Mojave and Colorado River cannot simply be attributed to Fort Yuma, although the reported distinction does not establish the absence of every conceivable interest.

The later adjustment clauses permit appropriate water changes following a lawful final boundary determination; they do not themselves remove a preclusion defense to reopening an omitted predicate. Matsushita and Baker preserve the actual lawful effect of the instruments. Their own finality positions distinguish a completed allocation from a lawfully changed prospective operation, without treating retained jurisdiction as unlimited reconsideration. ### The independent Burma procurement sanction conflicts with the enacted federal arrangement

**Holding and operative rule:** Massachusetts's Burma procurement restriction is preempted because its broader, independently continuing economic sanction frustrates Congress's integrated choice of covered conduct and persons, conditional sanctions, presidential flexibility under stated conditions, and a coordinated multilateral strategy in §570. A purchasing label and shared humanitarian ends do not save that demonstrated conflict; the injunction against enforcement of the challenged restriction remains in place.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court compares the actual state obligation with the enacted federal method. Geier requires that concrete comparison, rather than an inference from compliance, overlap or policy preference. Congress coupled limited coverage with conditional sanctions, removal and national-security waiver provisions, and a mandatory effort to develop a coordinated strategy. Massachusetts places an additional procurement disability on a broader range of dealings and persons, including foreign firms and ordinary trade, without corresponding responsiveness to those federal conditions. Its sanction continues to reduce the flexibility and bargaining capacity Congress gave the national program.

The State's exceptions matter but do not cure that conflict. Essential-purchase, competition, comparable-bid, news, telecommunications and distinct medical permissions alleviate particular purchases; they do not align the sanction's coverage and continued operation with the federal arrangement. Physical ability to obey both statutes and agreement about Burma's repression do not establish compatibility of their means.

Boston Harbor protects genuine project-specific proprietary action while reserving actual congressional displacement. It does not immunize a condition on otherwise unrelated purchases that conflicts with this federal law. Myrick refused to create a federal objective from regulatory absence; here the affirmative statutory terms establish the method. Barclays distinguishes binding national law from Executive preferences, foreign objections and congressional silence. That distinction supports the inquiry here: the enacted arrangement, rather than any foreign complaint or Executive assertion, supplies the displacement.

The Court does not construe §570(e) as a direct power to waive state law. Preemption follows from the Supremacy Clause and the demonstrated statutory conflict. Nor does federal permission for a transaction alone immunize it from every state purchasing choice. The integrated provisions supply the sufficient ground for affirmance, leaving the other constitutional theories below undecided.

**Limits and questions not reached:** No general foreign-affairs field, dormant foreign-affairs, Foreign Commerce Clause, WTO-merits or private Uruguay Round enforcement holding is entered. The Court decides no general presumption against preemption and relies on no legislative-history, failed-bill or congressional-silence inference. Executive implementation must stay within the statute; litigation advocacy and diplomatic objections do not create preemptive law. Neutral procurement rules, nonbinding state statements and state action within genuine federal room remain available, subject to otherwise applicable law. Ordinary divestment, contract eligibility and every state measure with foreign effects are not adjudicated.

### This mistaken conditional release does not constitutionally discharge the existing sentence

**Holding and operative rule:** A mistaken grant of parole does not require permanent release from the valid, unchanged and uncompleted sentence on this record: approximately twenty months of supervised community liberty, earlier correct eligibility notice, consideration of favorable adjustment evidence, a formal hearing, credit for the release period, and correction directed to enforcing the lawful sentence. The real liberty and successful adjustment do not themselves establish a fundamental entitlement to retain the unauthorized release or make this considered return to custody a substantive-due-process violation.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court treats the lived liberty as real. Young and Morrissey protect existing conditional community life rather than dismissing it because legal custody continues. They do not extinguish the sentence or make every unauthorized parole irrevocable. Hawkins's official release, employment, family life and favorable rehabilitation therefore deserve consideration, but the question remains whether their withdrawal here constitutionally bars enforcement of the unserved sentence.

Daniels excludes negligence alone as a constitutional deprivation. That rule does not finish this case: the original error and the later deliberate return to custody are different acts. The latter enforced the existing sentence after supervised release and an opportunity to present the favorable evidence, with release time credited. No supported bad-faith inducement, manipulation or attempt to evade a legally completed sentence changes that account. Neither the arrears nor an invented rejection of the rehabilitation evidence supplies the revocation ground.

The strongest contrary argument invokes formal State investigation and approval, twenty months of reintegration and reliance on the resulting community life. Those facts make the loss serious; they do not transform conditional parole into a final adjudication shortening the sentence. The earlier correct eligibility notice is relevant without proving knowing participation in the error. The State's continuing punishment and parole-administration interests justify this correction on the supported record.

Glucksberg rejects a particular assisted-suicide entitlement, not every liberty lacking the narrowest historical analogue. Lewis confines its harmful-purpose rule to rapid pursuit and reserves deliberative settings. Neither supplies the lower court's proposed universal formula. The Court reaches the bounded substantive conclusion without a generalized reliance-balancing test, an all-case malice requirement, or a finding that state law compelled return. ## Event

**Miller v. French / United States v. French**, Nos. 99-224 and 99-582. Plenary merits decision, October Term 1999, chunk 8. Argued April 18, 2000; decided June 19, 2000. ## Holdings

### I. The automatic stay displaces ordinary equitable continuation

**Holding and operative rule:** For the presented motion under 18 U.S.C. §3626(b)(2), subsection (e) automatically stays covered prospective relief beginning on the thirtieth day after filing and ending when the court enters a final order resolving the motion; the court may postpone the starting date for good cause by no more than sixty days, but general calendar congestion is not good cause. Beyond that express postponement, ordinary equitable authority does not permit the court to suspend the statutory stay.

**Authority:** Part I of Justice Souter's opinion of the Court has six direct rationale votes: O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas and Ginsburg. Chief Justice Stone-Zsela joins the judgment without joining this statutory-construction part; Stevens and Breyer dissent from the construction.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court reads the stay, postponement and appeal provisions together. The enacted command makes the motion operate as a stay; the express postponement both limits its length and excludes general calendar congestion. Preserving the same decree through ordinary injunction balancing beyond those limits would defeat that structure. The United States argues that traditional equitable authority survives because extraordinary injunction requirements differ from good cause and subsection (e)(4) expressly addresses orders blocking the stay. But that subsection makes such orders appealable as refusals to dissolve or modify an injunction under 28 U.S.C. §1292(a)(1), regardless of their label or preliminary or final character; it supplies review, not additional substantive authority to block the stay. The express subsection (e)(3) postponement is excluded from that special appeal treatment. Constitutional avoidance cannot replace this detailed statutory direction with a competing scheme. Subsection (e)(1)'s prompt-disposition duty and mandamus remedy remain. The retained 180-day timing language for motions under other law does not govern the immediate-termination motion presented here; its broader interaction with the amended under-subsection-(b) opening is not decided. Section 3626(a)(2)'s separate ninety-day expiration for newly entered preliminary relief does not turn this existing permanent decree into a preliminary order.

### II. Congress may change interim prospective law without deciding the case

**Holding and operative rule:** The automatic stay is not categorically unconstitutional under Article III merely because it temporarily suspends prospective relief before final adjudication of the termination motion. Congress may change the law governing future enforcement while leaving the court to decide the new law's factual and legal predicates; this holding does not resolve an application that actually deprives the court of a meaningful opportunity to adjudicate.

**Authority:** Part II of Justice Souter's opinion of the Court is joined by Stone-Zsela, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas and Ginsburg, supplying seven direct votes for this bounded holding and the remand in Holding III.

**Controlling explanation:** Robertson distinguishes changing governing law from prescribing a judicial answer under unchanged law; Klein preserves the independent judicial task. Wheeling Bridge and Agostini distinguish future operation of an injunction from completed adjudication. Rouse and Hadix apply that distinction to conditional prison-relief termination. Here the interim enforcement rule itself changes prospective law. A party's motion triggers the legislated consequence; the party does not thereby establish lawful prison conditions or defeat the findings required to retain relief. Although the Pendleton decree followed adjudication in material part, the stay neither contradicts those past findings nor cancels accrued damages or completed lawful effects. Morgan Stanley and Plaut permit their defined reinstatement of securities limitations dismissals while preserving independent judicial work; they establish neither categorical immunity for every judgment nor unrestricted legislative control of adjudication. The prisoners correctly distinguish termination after findings from suspension before findings. That distinction requires attention to an actual denial of adjudication, but it does not make every interim prospective rule legislative adjudication. The Court rejects the Seventh Circuit's categorical structural ruling without declaring this timetable sufficient in every complex case or deciding a supported inability-to-adjudicate claim against the prisoners.

### III. The remand preserves a genuine adjudication of supported application objections

**Holding and operative rule:** In each docket, the Seventh Circuit's categorical invalidation is reversed and the case remanded, permitting a properly supported claim that the statutory timing, including available postponement, actually denies a meaningful opportunity to adjudicate to be presented and decided under Article III as well as any applicable due-process protection. No such violation is presumed, no feasibility hearing is required merely because time has elapsed, and no ordinary equitable exception or automatic continuation of the existing blocking injunction follows.

**Authority:** Part II of Justice Souter's opinion of the Court; the seven Justices named for Holding II directly join this operative remand and its limits.

**Controlling explanation:** Benjamin requires a real opportunity to offer material current proof when it is necessary for statutory adjudication; Gavin and Hadix require the unperformed termination inquiry without making a new hearing universal. The reported postponements of the termination hearing establish neither constitutional impossibility nor prison compliance, present violations or deliberate delay. They therefore neither compel an evidentiary hearing about timing nor foreclose a supported application objection. On remand, such an objection must receive genuine consideration under ordinary procedure; this judgment does not restrict it to due process or decide the appropriate remedy in advance. The court must also decide the actual termination conditions: the movant must establish that covered relief was entered without substantive necessity, narrowness and least-intrusion findings, and sufficient findings require no ritual wording. Under amended §3626(b)(3), relief shall not terminate if written, record-based findings establish that it remains necessary to correct a current and ongoing federal-right violation, extends no further than necessary, is narrowly drawn and is the least intrusive necessary means. Each retained duty requires those findings; obsolete relief may not survive through disregard of the new law. This appeal neither decides those merits nor terminates the decree. Hadix's distinct pending automatic-stay appeal is not adjudicated here.

**Justice Breyer, dissenting, joined by Justice Stevens.** They would vacate the Seventh Circuit's judgment in both dockets and remand under a construction preserving exceptional equitable protection. Congress ordinarily leaves the courts' equitable power intact, and subsection (e)(4)'s provision for appeal from a blocking order supports a reading that allows a genuinely exceptional injunction. The sixty-day good-cause postponement and traditional injunction authority require different showings and can coexist without making the automatic stay meaningless. An applicant would have to establish irreparable injury, likelihood of defeating termination under the amended law, inability to resolve the merits before the stay operates, and favorable traditional balancing of the parties' and public interests. No present finding establishes those predicates. Their reading avoids the difficult Article III timing question while requiring the actual current-and-ongoing-violation and remedial-scope findings. Morgan Stanley and Plaut preserve independent adjudication under changed law; Benjamin, Gavin and Hadix require meaningful consideration of material current proof. They do not make the old decree immutable or endorse the Seventh Circuit's categorical constitutional rule. On remand they would permit consideration of appropriately supported temporary protection, without directing indefinite continuation or finding current prison conditions unlawful. They therefore dissent from mandatory statutory operation and the resulting reversal rather than vote to affirm categorical unconstitutionality.

## Event

**Santa Fe Independent School District v. Doe**, No. 99-62. Plenary merits decision, October Term 1999, chunk 8. Argued March 29, 2000; decided June 19, 2000. The October 1995 policy permits a brief invocation and/or message during home-varsity-football pregame ceremonies to solemnize the event, promote sportsmanship and student safety, and establish an appropriate competitive environment. ## Holdings

### The school may not delegate its official religious choice to a student majority

**Holding and operative rule:** A public secondary school may not organize an invocation as a representative component of its own football pregame ceremony and delegate to a student-majority election whether that official component will include religious exercise. The challenged board-created election mechanism violates the Establishment Clause under both its unrestricted and nonsectarian/nonproselytizing versions; relief reaches that mechanism while preserving genuinely voluntary private prayer, independent student expression, lawful secular messages and genuine neutral access.

**Authority:** Justice Breyer's opinion of the Court is joined in full by Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Kennedy, Souter, Ginsburg, supplying seven direct votes for the rule, explanation and limited affirmance. The particular government-created representative religious choice is essential to all seven joins; the Court adopts no independent religious-pressure holding.

**Controlling explanation:** Agostini requires identification of the governmental act and its function, using purpose, effect and institutional relationships as evidence within the modified Lemon inquiry. Here the board creates an invocation or message slot within its own ceremony; the student council conducts a majority election under the principal's advice and direction; the electorate selects a single volunteer; and the speaker must serve the board's prescribed purposes. This is an official representative component, not generally available access for independently chosen private speakers. Lee forbids official school organization of worship, and Ingebretsen explains why student initiation or delivery cannot alone make that exercise private. A majority vote cannot privatize the board's delegated religious choice.

The District stresses secular purposes, student authorship, no required prayer, optional attendance and the possibility of silence or a secular message. Those features do not remove the existing governmental mechanism. The Court assumes no official textual preapproval and no delivered October prayer; it does not rest on a finding that every secular purpose is a sham. The ability to decline use of the delegated religious authority does not transform it into neutral access. Pinette and Bronx Household protect actually private expression, where government has not adopted the devotion as its own event component. Tanford likewise distinguishes official organization from private composition, without making its adult setting identical to this school policy.

The backup's religious-content limits retain the same official mechanism and do not cure it. The Court does not direct officials to edit private religion into acceptable language. Nor does it prohibit every election, invited student speaker or religious message. The existing mechanism suffices to affirm; Lee and Ingebretsen's separate protection against substantial practical religious pressure remains intact without requiring legal compulsion or compulsory attendance. No additional pressure finding, universal facial-challenge rule, new forum entitlement, graduation ruling or damages award is necessary.

## Event

**Dickerson v. United States**, No. 99-5525. Plenary merits decision, October Term 1999, chunk 8. Argued April 19, 2000; decided June 26, 2000. On writ of certiorari to the Fourth Circuit, reviewing 166 F.3d 667 (1999), an interlocutory suppression appeal in a federal bank-robbery and firearm prosecution. There is no conviction or sentence under review. The granted question concerns section 3501's asserted authorization of case-in-chief admission notwithstanding Miranda; the separate challenges to the circuit's taking up that statute and its physical-evidence ruling are outside the grant.

The district court found that Dickerson was in custody during the January 27, 1997 FBI interview and that the disputed statements preceded warnings and waiver. It declined to reopen the record for the Government's later affidavit and handwritten-statement proffer because previous unavailability was not shown; the Fourth Circuit sustained that refusal. The circuit treated the statements as voluntary by inference from the district court's derivative-evidence ruling. That is the appellate court's inference, not a fresh finding by this Court or proof that warnings preceded the statements.

The United States supports Dickerson on the statutory-displacement question and opposes overruling Miranda. Paul Cassell's defending amicus brief presents the principal contrary arguments, including the distinct request for judicial reconsideration. The Government retains its prosecution; its merits position is not an admission that every item of evidence must be suppressed.

## Holdings

### I. Miranda's constitutional safeguard cannot be replaced by voluntariness alone

**Holding and operative rule:** Miranda's warnings and waiver requirements implement the constitutional privilege against compelled self-incrimination in custodial interrogation, and Congress cannot authorize case-in-chief use of a statement that those requirements exclude merely because the statement is voluntary under 18 U.S.C. §3501(a)–(b). Genuinely equally effective safeguards remain permissible; no exact verbal formula is indispensable, and an unwarned statement is not necessarily actually coerced for every purpose.

**Authority:** Part I of Justice Stevens's Opinion of the Court, joined by Stone-Zsela, O'Connor, Kennedy, Souter, Ginsburg and Breyer. Seven direct rationale votes control the constitutional rule, statutory application, qualifications and suppression remedy.

**Controlling explanation:** Miranda supplies a prospective safeguard for an informed choice during custodial interrogation, not merely a later inquiry into whether a completed statement was coerced. Its operation in state prosecutions and the constitutional review preserved by Withrow cannot rest on this Court's supervisory authority over federal procedure. Congress may provide effective alternatives, but it may not remove the protection merely by reinstating the voluntariness inquiry Miranda found insufficient.

Section 3501 requires the judge to examine voluntariness outside the jury's presence, consider all circumstances including warnings and counsel, and permit the jury to assess the voluntary statement's weight. These are real protections. Their defect as a replacement is functional: retrospective examination does not itself inform the suspect before speaking. Smith v. Robbins distinguishes freedom to vary a safeguard's form from omission of its protected function; it does not decide this different safeguard's adequacy by analogy alone. Proposed training, civil remedies and warning incentives do not establish an equally effective replacement on this record, and civil relief cannot be assumed available regardless of its cause-of-action and immunity conditions.

The contrary argument relies on Tucker, Elstad, Harris, Hass and Quarles. Those decisions distinguish actual compulsion from warning-based exclusion and preserve particular derivative uses, later warned statements, impeachment or public-safety questioning. They do not make the original unwarned statement generally admissible in the prosecution's case in chief. The Court preserves those distinctions rather than equating every warning failure with actual coercion.

Here the district court found custody and found the disputed statements preceded warnings and waiver. The Fourth Circuit's voluntariness conclusion cannot substitute for the missing safeguard. Its section-3501 basis for reversing statement suppression fails. The statement-suppression order is restored and the prosecution remanded, subject to a properly preserved exception; no new credibility finding is made.

**Statutory qualifications:** Subsection (a) concerns federal or District of Columbia criminal prosecutions. It requires the judge to resolve voluntariness outside the jury's presence and, upon finding the confession voluntary, to allow the jury to hear relevant voluntariness evidence and instruct it to give the confession the weight it deserves in light of all the circumstances. Its admission command remains subject to the constitutional safeguard stated above. Under subsection (b), all circumstances must be considered, including the arrest-to-arraignment interval when applicable, knowledge of the suspected offense, advice or knowledge concerning silence and use, prior advice of counsel rights, and absence of counsel during questioning and confession. The presence or absence of a listed factor need not be conclusive; consideration is mandatory, conclusiveness is not. Independent voluntariness review survives.

The Court does not invalidate the whole section. Subsection (c) protects a voluntary confession against exclusion solely for presentment delay when its weight is left to the jury and it is given within six hours of the relevant arrest or detention; the time limit does not apply when the judge finds a longer delay reasonable considering transportation and distance to the nearest available qualifying magistrate or other committing officer. Alvarez-Sanchez's relevant-duty inquiry and unresolved post-six-hour and McNabb–Mallory questions remain. Subsection (d) does not bar voluntary statements given without interrogation or while the speaker is not under arrest or detention; those alternatives remain distinct. Subsection (e) includes an admission of guilt and any oral or written self-incriminating statement. Each provision remains subject to constitutional requirements.

**Limits and questions not reached:** No automatic civil liability, general fruit-of-the-statement rule, mandatory warning wording or elimination of recognized exceptions follows. The separate apartment-evidence ruling, car-evidence dispute, refusal to reopen the suppression record and circuit authority to raise section 3501 are outside this review. The Court orders no new suppression hearing, dismissal, acquittal, release or vacatur of a conviction; none exists at this interlocutory stage.

### II. The Court retains Miranda's core against the separate request for judicial abandonment

**Holding and operative rule:** The Court declines to replace Miranda's core case-in-chief safeguard with a voluntariness-only rule: congressional preference for that rule and the existing distinctions between unwarned and actually compelled statements do not supply sufficient justification for abandonment here. Retention of the core leaves genuinely effective alternatives and the recognized, separately bounded exceptions available.

**Authority:** Part II of Stevens's Opinion of the Court is joined by O'Connor, Kennedy, Souter, Ginsburg and Breyer. These six directly adopt the additional stare-decisis ground. Stone-Zsela agrees that the alternative request for judicial abandonment is rejected but adds no ground and does not join Part II. No Marks combination is needed.

**Controlling explanation:** The amicus defending the judgment separately asks this Court to revise Miranda even if Congress cannot displace it. That request requires a judicial justification independent of the statute's existence. Miranda has supplied the governing framework for custodial interrogation, and subsequent decisions have retained its case-in-chief core while defining limits. Existing administration and reliance count against replacing that framework without an adequate reason.

The exceptions do not establish the claimed doctrinal collapse. Elstad distinguishes a later voluntary warned statement from the original unwarned statement; Tucker concerns derivative evidence; Harris and Hass concern impeachment; Quarles addresses public safety. Their tailored consequences can coexist with a constitutional safeguard broader than a retrospective finding of actual compulsion. They do not silently overrule Miranda.

Claims about interrogation costs, improved training and other remedies deserve consideration, but they do not establish that the prospective informed-choice function has become unnecessary or has an adequate substitute here. The Court need not declare every application costless, every warning formulation immutable or every judicial implementation rule beyond revision. The constitutional function and the absence of sufficient grounds to abandon this settled core justify rejecting the requested change.

## Precedent Treatment

- **Miranda v. Arizona, 384 U.S. 436 (1966):** Its constitutional custodial-interrogation safeguard, case-in-chief exclusion and allowance for equally effective alternatives are retained. Voluntariness alone cannot replace its applicable requirements.
- **Withrow v. Williams, 507 U.S. 680 (1993):** Its constitutional habeas operation supports Miranda's status; its distinction between a warning claim and separately presented coercion remains. No new collateral-review rule is adopted.
- **Michigan v. Tucker, 417 U.S. 433 (1974):** Its derivative-evidence setting and distinction between safeguards and actual compulsion remain; it does not authorize general case-in-chief use of the original unwarned statement.
- **Oregon v. Elstad, 470 U.S. 298 (1985):** Its treatment of a later voluntary warned statement remains; the original unwarned statement's exclusion is not erased.
- **Harris v. New York, 401 U.S. 222 (1971), and Oregon v. Hass, 420 U.S. 714 (1975):** Their impeachment uses remain subject to their own requirements and do not become ordinary prosecution case-in-chief permission.
- **New York v. Quarles, 467 U.S. 649 (1984):** Its public-safety exception remains limited to its governing conditions; the decision does not replace the general warning rule.
- **Colorado v. Connelly, 479 U.S. 157 (1986):** Police coercion remains necessary to due-process involuntariness; absence of warnings alone does not establish that distinct defect.
- **Smith v. Robbins, January 19, 2000:** Its distinction between equivalent form and preservation of the required function informs the substitute inquiry; its appellate-counsel setting does not itself decide confession admissibility.
- **United States v. Alvarez-Sanchez, 511 U.S. 350 (1994):** Its subsection-(c) relevant-presentment-duty rule, six-hour protection, transportation-and-distance proviso and reservations remain. Today's decision resolves the distinct voluntariness-only displacement question that it reserved.
- **Stansbury v. California, 511 U.S. 318 (1994), and Thompson v. Keohane, 516 U.S. 99 (1995):** Their objective custody rule and distinction between ultimate constitutional classification and historical facts remain; no new custody or credibility finding is made.
- **Davis v. United States, 512 U.S. 452 (1994):** Its actual postwaiver clarification, clear-invocation and waiver rules remain; its reservation of the overall section-3501 question is not treated as an earlier answer.

## Law After Decision

Effective June 26, 2000, Miranda's constitutional case-in-chief safeguard cannot be displaced by section 3501's voluntariness-only admission rule. The Court resolves that previously reserved relationship without invalidating the section's distinct lawful functions. Equally effective alternative safeguards remain possible, independent coercion remains a separate inquiry, and recognized exceptions keep their own conditions. Six Justices also reject the separate request to discard the core under stare decisis. No precedent is overruled and no universal rule immunizing every judicial procedure from legislation is adopted.

## Separate Writings

**Justice Scalia, dissenting, joined by Justice Thomas.** They would sustain the Fourth Circuit's statutory-admissibility basis against the presented Miranda-displacement objection, provided voluntariness is sustainably determined and the statute's required circumstances are considered. Their ground distinguishes the constitutional prohibition of actual compelled self-incrimination from a categorical exclusion triggered solely by omitted warnings. Tucker, Elstad, Harris, Hass and Quarles demonstrate that the Court already treats warning violations differently from actual compulsion in consequential settings.

The dissent confronts Miranda's application in state prosecutions and constitutional habeas review. It does not claim federal supervisory power over state courts or assert that Congress can override a constitutional rule the dissent simultaneously accepts as binding. Rather, it would judicially reject the asserted constitutional necessity of an irrebuttable case-in-chief bar for a sustainably voluntary statement, and then apply section 3501's federal admissibility framework. That reconsideration is a proposed doctrinal change, not a claim that prior decisions have already overruled Miranda.

Scalia and Thomas regard the tension between actual compulsion and categorical exclusion as sufficient reason for reconsideration despite settled practice and their own prior applications of Miranda doctrine. In their view, institutional familiarity cannot cure a constitutional-authority defect. Their disagreement concerns that premise, not permission to coerce, a finding that warnings preceded these statements or a promise of universally available civil remedies. Subsection (b)'s circumstances remain mandatory considerations, though the presence or absence of an individual factor need not be conclusive. They would leave the prosecution to ordinary further proceedings and decide neither the independent search grounds nor the refusal to reopen the suppression record. The Court rejects their proposed change.

Chief Justice Stone-Zsela's additional disposition-only agreement concerns the separate request for judicial abandonment. His joined Part I supplies his public constitutional and substitute-adequacy grounds; he adds no stare-decisis rationale or separate writing.
