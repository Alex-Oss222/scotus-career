QUESTIONS PRESENTED 


1. Whether the U.S. Court of Appeals for the District of 
Columbia Circuit erred by holding, in direct conflict with five 
state courts of last resort, that an automobile manufacturer’s 
compliance with a Federal motor vehicle safety standard that 
permits, but does not require, installation of airbags in 
passenger vehicles preempts state common law ciaims that an 
automobile was defectively designed because it lacked an 
airbag? 


2. Whether the D.C. Circuit erred by holding that, 
because this Court engaged in implied preemption analysis in 
Freightliner Corp. v. Myrick, 514 U.S. 280 (1995), the lower 
courts are free to disregard the limitations on implied 
preemption most recently emphasized in Cipollone v. Liggett 
Group, Inc., 505 U.S. 504 (1992), to find that a Federal motor 
vehicle safety standard promulgated pursuant to the National 
Traffic and Motor Vehicle Safety Act of 1966, 15 U.S.C. §§ 
1381-1431, impliedly preempts common law claims, even 
though the Act expressly provides that “[c]ompliance with any 
Federal motor vehicle safety standard . . . does not exempt any 
person from any liability under common law’’?

STATEMENT OF THE CASE 


On January 16, 1992, Alexis Geier was operating a 1987 
Honda Accord in the District of Columbia. Ms. Geier was 
wearing the manual three-point lap belt and shoulder harness 
provided for the driver in the design of the car. Rounding a 
curve, the vehicle spun out of control and collided with a tree. 
Despite the fact that she was wearing the available lap belt and 
shoulder harness, Ms. Geier sustained grievous and serious 
head injuries. See App. 2; Complaint ff 4-7, 18-21. 


When the 1987 Honda Accord at issue in this case was 
manufactured a federal regulation promulgated pursuant to the 
National Traffic and Motor Vehicle Safety Act of 1966, 15 


U.S.C. §§ 1381 et seg. (“the Safety Act’’) —i.e., Federal Motor 
Vehicle Safety Standard 208 — gave Honda the option to install 


3 


airbags in its car. See 49 C.F.R. § 571.208. Specifically, for 
cars manufactured between September 1, 1986, and September 
1, 1987, Standard 208 required Honda, at a minimum, to install 
some form of passive restraint in a specified percentage of its 
cars and to install at least a manual three-point lap belt and 
shoulder harness in the remaining percentage. Jd. See also 
App. 3. It also allowed all manufacturers to do more than the 
bare minimum required (including installing both an airbag and 
a manual three-point lap belt and shoulder harness in all cars). 


On January 12, 1995, Ms. Geier and her parents filed 
this lawsuit seeking damages under the common law of the 
District of Columbia against respondents American Honda 
Motor Company, Inc., Honda of America Manufacturing, Inc., 
and Honda Motor Company, Ltd. (collectively, “Honda”) on 
theories of negligence, breach of warranty, and strict product 
liability. The Geiers alleged that the 1987 Honda Accord was 
negligently and defectively designed and that, if the car had 
contained a driver’s-side airbag in addition to the manual lap 
belt and shoulder harness, Ms. Geier would not have been 
seriously injured in the crash. 


Even though the design advocated by petitioners was 
fully consistent with Standard 208, Honda moved for summary 
judgment on the ground that the Geiers’ claims were preempted 
by the Safety Act and Standard 208. App. 17. In response, 
petitioners pointed out, inter alia, that the Safety Act explicitly 


' As the lower court observed, the requirements of Standard 208 
have since become more stringent. After September 1, 1989, car 
manufacturers had to install either an airbag or an automatic seatbelt that 
would signal the driver with a warning light if the belt became unhooked. 
App. 3. Currently, passenger cars manufactured after September 1, 1997, 
must have “an inflatable restraint system at the driver’s and right front 
passenger’s position.” App. 3. 


provides that “[c]ompliance with any Federal motor vehicle 
standard . . . does not exempt any person from any liability 
under common law.” 15 U.S.C. § 1397(k). On December 10, 
1997, however, the district court found that petitioners’ claims 
were preempted on the theory that a jury verdict in their favor 
might establish a safety standard that was not identical to 
Standard 208. App. 17-20. 


The court of appeals affirmed. App. 1-16. In its 
decision, the court first acknowledged that any conclusion that 
petitioners’ claims are expressly preempted would be 
“problematic” in light of both the Act’s “broadly worded 
savings clause” and the strong presumption against finding 
express preemption of common law claims when Congress’ 
intent to preempt “is not clear from the statute’s language.” 
App. 10-11. Given the broad wording of the savings clause, 
the court reasoned, “it would be difficult to discern a ‘clear and 
manifest purpose of Congress’ to preempt a design defect claim 
based on the absence of an airbag.” App. 1 1 (citations omitted). 


The court of appeals ultimately decided that it need not 
resolve the express preemption question, however, since it 
found instead that petitioners’ claims are impliedly preempted 
by Standard 208. App. 12. The court first rejected petitioners’ 
claim that, under Cipollone v. Liggett Group, Inc., 505 U.S. 504 
(1992), the Safety Act’s express provisions addressing the 
scope of preemption bar any inquiry into implied preemption. 
After acknowledging that Cipollone does contain language to 
that effect (App. 12), the court held that this Court’s subsequent 
decision in Freightliner Corp. v. Myrick, 514 U.S. 280 (1995), 
repudiated Cipollone’s apparent holding that implied 
preemption may not be considered in cases where — as here — 
the statute itself expressly defines the scope of preemption. 
App. 12-13. 


The court went on to hold that petitioners’ claims are 
impliedly preempted on the ground that they conflict with 
federal law. While conceding that Standard 208 “does not 
mandate or forbid the use of airbags” (App. 14), the court 
adopted Honda’s argument that imposition of common law 
liability for failure to install an airbag would interfere with the 
federal government’s decision to make airbags only “one of 
several options car manufacturers could choose to comply with 
the passive restraint requirement of Standard 208 . . ..” App. 
15. Creation of these options, the court reasoned, was integra! 
to the federal government’s policy “of encouraging both public 
acceptance of the airbag technology and experimentation with 
better passive restraint systems.” App. 15. Because common 
law liability for failing to install an airbag could “interfere with 
the regulatory method” embodied in Standard 208, the court 
concluded that petitioners’ claims are “implicitly pre-empted” 
by the Act. App. 16. 


REASONS FOR GRANTING THE WRIT 


When Congress enacted the Safety Act in 1966, it 
unequivocally stated the preemptive effect that compliance with 
a Federal safety standard was to have on common law liability: 
“Compliance with any Federal motor vehicle safety standard 
issued under this subchapter does not exempt any person from 
any liability under common law.” 15 U.S.C. § 1397(k) 
(emphasis added). The D.C. Circuit’s preemrtion decision 
contravenes these plain words and conflicts with the ruling of 
five state courts of last resort to address this preemption issue. 
The lower court’s ruling also demonstrates, and increases, the 
confusion created by this Court’s decision in Myrick about the 
Safety Act’s preemptive effect. Finally, the decision below 
incorrectly answers an important federal question that has not 
been. but should be, decided by this Court: whether Congress 
intended the Safety Act to preempt state common law claims. 


6 


This Court shou'd grant review to resolve the conflict among 
the federal courts of appeal and state courts of last resort, 
eliminate the confusion caused by Myrick, and decide whether 
Congress’ words mean what they say. 


1. The Decision Below Conflicts With the 
Decisions of Five State Courts of Last Resort.

the growing conflict among the federal courts of appeals and 
the state courts of last resort over the Safety Act’s preemptive

because it lacks an airbag. Only this Court can bring the 
conflict to an end. If this Court does not step in, the conflict 
will only get worse, as the decision below reflects.

This ongoing dispute about the Safety Act’s preemptive 
effect has serious practical consequences. There are currently 
thousands of automobile design defect lawsuits pending 
throughout the nation, hundreds of which involve the 
manufacturers’ failure to install lap belts, shoulder harnesses, 
and/or airbags. The preemption issue has been raised or is 
lurking in all of these cases. The legal and judicial resources 
being spent on this issue are mushrooming, with no end to the 
conflict in sight.

ROGERS, Circuit Judge: The question on appeal is 
whether federal law pre-empts a defective design lawsuit 
against the American Honda Motor Company for damages 
arising from injuries suffered by Alexis Geier' when her 1987 
Honda Accord, which did not have an airbag, crashed into a 
tree. The district court granted summary judgment for Honda 
on the ground that the lawsuit was pre-empted by the National 
Traffic and Motor Vehicle Safety Act of 1966, as amended, 
15 U.S.C. § 1381 et seg. (Safety Act), and the Federal Motor 
Vehicle Safety Standard 208, 49 C.F.R. § 571.208 (1997) 
(Standard 208), which govern the passive safety restraints that 
automobile manufacturers must install.’ Joining our sister 
circuits, we affirm, concluding that Geier’s lawsuit is 
impliedly pre-empted.

laws of the United States “shall be the supreme Law of the 
Land; . . . any Thing in the Constitution or Laws of any State 
to the Contrary notwithstanding.” U.S. Const. art. VI, cl. 2.

The language of § 1392(d) is fairly sweeping, providing 
that when a federal motor vehicle safety standard is in effect, 
“no State or political subdivision of a State shall have any 
authority either to establish, or to continue in effect, with 
respect to any motor vehicle or item of motor vehicle 
equipment any safety standard applicable to the same aspect 
of performance of such vehicle or item of equipment which is 
not identical to the Federal standard.” 15 U.S.C. § 1392(d) 
(emphasis added). At a minimum, this language restricts a 
State’s authority to enact legislation or regulations that 
affirmatively require car manufacturers to adopt standards not 
identical to Standard 208. See Wood, 865 F.2d at 408. In 
other words, a State could not require, by statute or 
regulation, that a car manufacturer install airbags in models 
for which Standard 208 makes them only an option.

and federal district courts that have looked at this question, 
the federal circuit courts have unanimously concluded that a 
cause of action for negligence based on the failure of a car 
manufacturer to install airbag during the 1980s is pre-empted 
by federal law. Harris v. Ford Motor Company, 110 F.3d 
1410, 1416 (9th Cir. 1997); Montag v. Honda Motor Co. Ltd., 
75 F.3d 1414, 1417 (10th Cir. 1996); Pokorney v. Ford Motor 
Co., 902 F.2d 1116, 1118 (3rd Cir. 1990); Taylor v. General 
Motors Corp., 875 F.2d 816, 828 (11th Cir. 1989); Wood v. 
General Motors Corp., 865 F.2d 395, 419 (ist Cir. 1988). 
Two other judges within this district have also found common 
law causes of action pre-empted by the national Traffic and 
Motor Vehicle Safety Act and the Federal Motor Vehicle 
Safety Standard 208. Wiley v. General Motors Corp., No. 96- 
1692, order granting summary judgment, (D.D.C. 1996); 
Hoftel v. General Motors Corp., 1988 WL 19615, *3 (D.D.C. 
1988). 


The Supremacy Clause of the Constitution, U.S. Const. 
art. VI, cl. 2, ensures that state law which conflicts with 
federal law is not given effect. The court should look to 
congressional intent when determining whether state law is 
pre-empted by federal law. Medtronic v. Lohr, 116 S. Ct. 
2240, 2250. The clearest expression of congressional intent is 
found in the language of the law itself as well as the 
“statutory framework” surrounding it. Jd. 


When examining the language of the National Traffic 
and Motor Vehicle Safety Act we see that § 1392(d) prohibits 
states from establishing “any safety standard” not identical to 
the Federal Motor Vehicle Safety Standard 208 in effect at the 
time the vehicle is manufactured, which, in 1987, gave car 
manufacturers a choice as to whether to install airbags or not. 
Recovery on tort claims, such as that asserted by Plaintiffs, is 


App. 18 


equivalent to a safety standard promulgated by the state 
legislature or a state regulatory body. “State regulation can 
be as effectively exerted through an award of damages as 
through some form of preventive relief. The obligation to pay 
compensation can be, indeed is designed to be, a potent 
method of governing conduct and controlling policy.” 
Cipollone v. Liggett Group, Inc., 505 U.S. 504, 521. Because 
the Federal Motor Vehicle Safety Standard 208 demonstrates 
that “the Federal Government has weighed the competing 


interests relevant to the particular requirement in question, 


case or set of cases, and implemented that conclusion via a 
specific mandate on manufacturers or producers,” Harris, at 
1414 (quoting Medtronic v. Lohr, 116 S. Ct. 2240 at 2258), 
Plaintiffs’ claim seems to be pre-empted by § 1392(d). 
However, the court must also determine the import and effect 
of § 1397(k) of the National Traffic and Motor Vehicle Safety 
Act which provides that “compliance with any Federal motor 
vehicle safety standard issued under this subchapter does not 
exempt any person from liability under common law.” At 
first, § 1397(k), the savings clause, and § 1392(d), the pre- 
emption clause, seem to be in direct conflict. However, 
reading these sections together in light of the Supreme 
Court’s pronouncements on the effect of common law 
regulation, it is clear that both sections can be given effect if 
the court finds that “compliance with Federal standards does 
not exempt anyone from any liability that the States have 
authority to impose.” Harris at 1415. The clearest example 
of this is common law liability connected with design defects. 
This persuasive reading of the language of the law 
demonstrates conclusively that Plaintiffs’ claim is pre-empted 
by federal law. 


The “statutory framework” as revealed in Congress’ 


App. 19 


entire regulatory scheme further supports the conclusion that 
Congress intended the National Traffic and Motor Vehicle 
Safety Act to preempt findings of common law negligence 
when manufacturers failed to install airbags in cars 
manufactured in 1987. Throughout the 1980s the Department 
of Transportation refused to require airbags based on a 
reasoned consideration of the long-term safety effects of such 
a mandate, hoping that even more effective and efficient 
occupant protection systems would be developed. 

For these reasons Defendants’ motion for summary 
judgment will be granted in an order accompanying this 
Memorandum.
