| STATEMENT OF INTEREST 


The Attorneys Information Exchange Group (AIEG) is an 
organization of attorneys who practice civil litigation throughout 
these United States. AIEG was founded in the mid-1970's by 
attorneys who were then representing burn victims who were 
motorists in Ford cars and trucks. Its founding members dedicated 
themselves, and this organization, to the creation of a private 
cooperative entity, which would serve to educate and coordinate the 
acquisition of technical information germane to the fair and honest 
representation of Americans who have suffered serious or 
catastrophic injury as a result of defective designs in motor vehicles. 
Over the years, AIEG members have represented the victims of such 
infamous vehicles as the Ford Pinto, General Motors cars and pick- 
up trucks with poorly designed fuel systems (e.g., side saddle fuel 
tanks), Audi vehicles with uncontrollable acceleration, and 
thousands of vehicles and trucks with transmission flaws allowing 
the transmission to unknowingly slip from Park to Reverse. 


Members of AIEG on a daily basis are involved in pursuing 
products liability claims typically stemming from faulty design or 
manufacture of motor vehicles. Our clients are those Americans 
who have suffered catastrophic injury needlessly. In the prosecution 
of these common law claims, we are called upon to study and 
address the relationship between vehicular design, injury causation 
and any relevancy of compliance with federal standards promulgated 
pursuant to the Safety Act. Most often, it has been found that there 
is very little relationship between a vehicle's compliance with these 
standards and the safety performance of these products in real 
world accidents. AIEG's members have a vital interest in the effect 
this decision may have on its members and the clients they represent 
because motor vehicle manufacturers continue to produce vehicles 
which only meet the bare minimum federal safety standards, thereby 
resulting in the sale of vehicles which may not be reasonably safe. 
This circumstance allows some manufacturers to ignore their 
common law responsibility to build and sell crashworthy vehicles, 
and it has resulted in injuries and deaths to thousands of Americans 


who should not have otherwise been injured or killed. ' 


ll. SUMMARY OF THE ARGUMENT 


In 1966, the United States Congress passed the National 
Traffic Safety Act (referenced as the Safety Act), in an effort to 
establish regulations for vehicle performance which would provide 
basic and minimum safety for vehicles sold in this country. 
Simultaneously, Congress authorized the establishment of a 
government agency to develop and promulgate these regulations-- 
which became known as the National Highway Traffic Safety 
Administration (NHTSA). The legislators who passed the Safety 
Act in 1966 were quite aware of then existing regulations and 
standards which many of the states had promulgated, and which 
required automobile manufacturers to only sell cars in their 
respective states if these vehicles were equipped with certain 
devices, such as front seat seatbelts. Congress was asked by some 
facets of the motor vehicle industry and safety community to pass 
legislation which would have the effect of eliminating diverse state 


regulations, so that only one governmental agency would control the 


development and promulgation of standards which vehicle | 
companies would be obliged to meet in order to market their 
products in this country. These standards were defined as 
"minimum standard{s] for motor vehicle performance.”

law principles to the issue of a motor vehicle manufacturers’ duty to 
build vehicles which are crashworthy. Larsen v. General Motors 
Corporation, 397 F.2d 495 (8th Cir. 1968). See generally, Hoening, 
Resolution of "Crashworthiness" Design Claims, 55 St. John's 
L.Rev. 633 (1981). 


As a result of Congress’ awareness of personal injury 
litigation invoking the application of the common law to motor 
vehicle design safety, and the promulgation of some non-uniform 
state safety legislation, the Safety Act addressed both issues. The 
Safety Act and its legislative history establish that Congress did not 
want conflicting safety regulations promulgated by state and fideral 
agencies. This was made quite clear by the inclusion of a 
preemption provision; yet, Congress also specifically provided that 
the Safety Act would not undermine the common law: 


"While the federal statute under which these 
regulations were passed preempts inconsistent state

federal regulations does not exempt any person from

The preemption section of the Safety Act prohibits any state 
or political sub-division from establishing any safety standard 
dissimilar to published federal standards. Virtually every federal 
and state appellate court has concluded that the language and intent 
of the preemption section cannot be read to extend to common law 


3 


damage claims. Unlike the language used by Congress in other 
legislation, here Congress expressed its intent to effect only the 
conduct of states and political subdivisions, rather than juries. In 
this instance, it is not just that Congress’ statement of preemption 
does not mention common law, but rather that Congress specifically 
identified the state activity which it intended to preempt, i.e., 
activities of state and political subdivisions. And, Congress also 
expressed its intent to preserve the common law by stating that 
compliance with federal safety standards does not exempt any 
person from any liability under common law. 15 U.S.C. § 1397(c). 


Congress intended by its language to establish minimum 
standards which all companies were required to meet before they can 
market their products in the United States. On the other hand, since 
these are only minimum standards, Congress intended the "threat" of 
common law liability to spur manufacturers to do much more than 
simply comply with federal standards--or suffer potential liability for 
injury caused by not providing the most reasonably safe product 
possible. 


When Congress passed the Safety Act it did not establish any 
sort of administrative remedy for Americans injured by defectively 
designed and manufactured products, because it was then viewed 
that there were available common law remedies to address any such 
harm. 


Over the past thirty years, the federal standards have, with 
few exceptions, not changed. A prime example of the stagnant 
nature of these standards is FMVSS 208. First promulgated as a 
"passive restraint" regulation in the early 1970's, the effective date of 
this standard was stalled, delayed, eliminated and then reissued 
many, many times. These delays often reflected the very political 
nature of the process of promulgating federal regulations. See, 


Automobile Insurance Company, 163 U.S. 29, 103 S.Ct. 2856. 
Despite these delays in the effective date requiring passive safety 


features in vehicles, some manufacturers voluntarily produced and 
sold cars in this country with passive (air bag) safety systems. Car 


4 


companies including General Motors Corporation, Ford Motor 
Company, Mercedes Benz and Volvo Corporation introduced air 
bag systems in their vehicles long before they were required by 
FMVSS 208.* 


Despite the relative stagnation of federal standards, vehicle 
manufacturers have continued to make safety improvements in their 
products. Obviously, technology and safety have been driven by the 
market place and the potential exposure to common law liability. 


When Congress enacted the Safety Act it did not provide 
protection against common law liability--which would have 
essentially granted these corporations status as "protected" 
businesses. There was never any indication that Congress had any 
interest in precluding this facet of industry from the time tested 
criteria of "due care." Congress obviously appreciated the 
relationship between governmental regulation and our civil justice 
system, which provides all Americans with access to our courts to 
redress civil wrongdoing. Until the birth of "no air bag litigation," 
not one appellate court in this country had ever entertained the 
notion that Congress chose to preempt common law liability in the 
field of motor vehicle product design. The several state and federal 
appellate courts which have, during the past ten years, studied the

gamut on both their decisions and reasoning. However, one 
conclusion seems to be clear from all of these cases, including those 
finding some form of preemption: they have treated "no air bag 
claims" as a unique issue in the context of whether or not Congress 
or NHTSA intended or planned to preempt this specific common 
law claim. There has been no suggestion that a finding of federal

That is exactly what Congress declared when it promulgated the

victims of non-crashworthy vehicles an opportunity to prove to

The issue of design defect in relation to the question of

from tort liability when they do no more for safety than the bear 
minimum required by NHTSA. We think the answer to this 
question is quite clear: there is no immunity from common law 
liability for mere compliance with minimum safety regulations. 


Ill. ARGUMENT 


A. BACKGROUND 


Nearly thirty years ago, the publication of Ralph Nader's 
Unsafe at Any Speed (1965) helped spur two major developments in 
the law. First, it prompted attorneys and the courts to give increased 
attention and recognition to what was then a relatively recent 
engineering development--the design of vehicles to be crashworthy. 
Second, it prompted Congress to give increased attention to the need 
for federal motor vehicle safety legislation and, ultimately, to the 
passage of the Safety Act of 1966. In the intervening years, as 
motor vehicle design defect litigation has grown, manufacturers 
have consistently argued to courts that when their vehicles have 
complied with applicable federal motor vehicle safety standards, 
they cannot be held liable for failing to do more. And, equally 


consistently, courts have rejected that argument. Courts have 
uniformly stated that Congress meant what it said in 15 U.S.C. § 
1397(c): "[c]ompliance with any federal motor vehicle safety 
standard does not exempt any person from any liability under 
common law." Larsen; Perry. The application of the common law 
to the field of automotive safety has, therefore, borne actions at 
common law for the deaths and injuries that the motor vehicle 
industry knew would result from their longstanding refusal to install 


safety devices, such as air bag systems. See, Motor Vehicle 


Manufacturers Association, ("[T]he automobile industry waged the 
regulatory equivalent of war against the airbag...feven 


though]...passive restraint could prevent approximately 12,000 
deaths and 100,000 serious injuries annually"). Yet, during the past 
several years, the manufacturers have had success in convincing 
many courts that they should still not be held accountable for failing 
to install these safety systems in motor vehicles. Strangely, until this 
Court's decision in Cipollone, many courts “bought into" the 
argument of preemption advanced by the manufacturers. Only a few 
stalwart judicial defenders of Congress’ obvious intent in the passage 
of the Safety Act were brave enough to “say no" to the rationale 
employed by many courts to preempt claims which Congress never 
intended to preempt. See, Gingold v, Audi-NSU-Auto Union, A.G., 
567 A.2d 312 (Pa. Super. 1989); Loulos. Then, in the “no air bag" 
case of Tebbetts v. Ford Motor Co., 665 A.2d 345 (N.H. 1995), cert. 


denied (U.S. 1996)--decided shortly after Cipollone, the New 
Hampshire Supreme Court addressed Ford's argument that the 


plaintiff's claim was expressly preempted by the Safety Act as 
follows: 


"Although the language of the preemption clause 
alone might suggest that common law actions are 
preempted, we do not interpret the preemption clause 
in isolation. Rather we read the Safety Act as a 
whole, because 'the meaning of statutory language, 
plain or not, depends on context.’ ...Hence we must 
interpret the preemption clause in tandem with the 
savings Clause. 


"The savings clause provides that ‘compliance with 
any Federal motor vehicle safety standard...does not 
exempt any person from any liability under common 
law. ...It is difficult to imagine broader language.’ 
...We therefore conclude that state common law is not 
explicitly preempted..."° 


Similarly, when General Motors raised the defense of express 
preemption to the plaintiff's no-air bag claim in Wilson vy, Pleasant, 
660 N.E.2d 327 (1995), the Indiana Supreme Court responded: 


" the presence of the savings clause negates any 
notion of express preemption of state common law 
claims. Therefore, we find that the Safety Act does 
not expressly preempt a state common law claim in 
this case..." 


And, most recently, the Arizona Supreme Court rejected GM's : 
arguments stating: 


"In using the term ‘safety standards,’ Congress 
made clear just what it meant: ‘Motor vehicle 
safety standards 'means a minimum standard 
for motor vehicle performance, or motor 
vehicle equipment performance..." 


These words tell us Congress gave the agency authority to 
adopt minimum safety standards to promote highway safety and 
reduce traffic accidents. Thus, we consider the standard as being 
just what Congress authorized: a minimum standard. If safety, not 
uniformity, is the underlying congressional purpose, the adoption of 
minimum standards would not ordinarily indicate that common-law 
liability imposed as a failure to adopt higher standards is precluded, 
or even in conflict with congressional intent." Munroe y. Galati, 
938 P.2d 1114 (Arizona 1997).

B. THE SAFETY ACT SAYS THERE IS NO 
PREEMPTION 


A reading of the Safety Act makes it clear that the 
preemption provision prohibits a state or political subdivision from 
establishing a “safety standard” that conflicts with a "Federal Motor 
Vehicle Safety Standard". Clearly, this phrase was intended to relate 
only to enactments of positive law, not common law liability. Cf 
Medtronic at 2252 (citing other uses of the term "requirements" 
throughout a statute to demonstrate its focus is on the "enactments 
of positive law by legislative or administrative bodies, not the 
application of general rules of common law by judges and juries"). 
Thus, the only sensible reading of the Safety Act's preemption 
provision is that a "safety standard" promulgated under the Act will 
only preempt a state legislative or administrative "safety standard" 
that is not identical to the federal standard. 


If Congress intended the preemption provision to apply to 
common law claims, it would have chosen words that clearly did so. 
The term "safety standards" is hardly a clear reference to common 


law claims. See, Medtronic. 


Common law claims do not set specific minimum 
performance standards; they focus--as motor vehicle safety 
standards do not--on the manner in which a manufacturer has 
chosen to achieve a reasonable level of safety, and whether certain 
engineering features caused the vehicle to be causatively unsafe for 


the foreseeable events at issue. See, Silkwood v. Kerr-McGee 
Corporation, 464 U.S. 238, 104 S.Ct. 615, 78 L.Ed.2d 443 (1984). 


The Congress intentionally defined a “motor vehicle safety 
standard" as a "minimum standard for motor vehicle performance, 
which is practicable, which meets the need for motor vehicle safety 
and which provides objective criteria." U.S.C. § 1391 (2). This 
definition plainly encompasses state statutory and administrative 
performance requirements and, just as plainly, does not encompass 
either state common law standards or jury verdicts which apply the 
common law in specific cases. 


Finally, if Congress intended to preempt all common law 
claims which applied to the same manner of performance as these 
minimum standards, why include the Savings Clause? Such an 
interpretation would, by its terms, preempt virtually a// common law 
claims which challenge vehicular design features that are covered by 
federal-minimum standards--including those claims routinely 


allowed by the courts over the past thirty years.° Hyundai Motor 
Company vy. Alvardo, 974 S.W.2d 1 (Texas 1998). 


Our traditional state tort law is at risk from the Court's 
preemption doctrine. Year after year, this Court and lower courts 
have been increasingly called upon to decide whether an Act of 
Congress was intended to preclude civil liability at common law. 
Because common law actions have traditionally been preserved and 
entrusted to states’ domain, we urge the Court to issue an Opinion 
which provides that state common law actions for damages receive 
the highest order of protection against preemption. It seems 
relatively easy to provide this protection if the Court will confirm 
that common law actions hav. minimal regulatory effect; if these 
actions have only incidental regulatory effect, then ambiguous 
preemptive language attempting to preempt some state regulation 
would not effect our citizens fundamental civil rights.’ This 
approach would be one of a strict textualist and would ensure that

CONCLUSION 


American common law is traditionally the guage of human 
decency and fair play. The common law allows average Americans, 
as jurors, to judge the actions or omissions of their fellow citizens. 
One can honestly say that without the common law many people 
would behave badly with impunity. Often, particularly when the 
acting party is a large business, inappropriate behavior goes without 
aremedy. Nevertheless, our common law gives each of us the 
opportunity to register our complaint and have our fellow citizens 
judge the rightness or wrongness of the conduct of another. 


Compliance with the Safety Act and the minimum standards 
set forth therein allow automotive manufacturers to sell cars in this 
country--nothing more. The purpose of the Safety Act was to 
protect consumers, not to shield manufacturers from liability. To 
paraphrase Justice Blackmun's concurrence in Cipollone, by finding 
federal preemption of certain state common-law damage claims, the 
Court eliminates a “critical component" of the States’ ability to 
protect the health and safety of its citizens. Thus, only when there is 
clear evidence that Congress intended such a result will preemption 
be found. Honda cannot demonstrate that Congress intended to 
displace our civil justice system as the forum for the resolution of 
the plaintiff's claims. The Safety Act means what it says: common 
law claims are not preempted. This Court should not condone 
preemption by regulatory fiat. Particularly when reliance upon such 
regulation can lead to the sale of a product which is not nearly as 
safe as one containing additional safety features. Congress did not
