RESPONDENTS’ BRIEF IN OPPOSITION 


Joining five other circuit courts, the District of 
Columbia Circuit Court of Appeals concluded that 
petitioners’ no-airbag tort claim was impliedly preempted by 
federal law because such tort claims conflict with the method 
chosen by the federal government to achieve the goals of the 
National Traffic and Motor Vehicle Safety Act of 1966 (“the 


2 


Safety Act”). In particular, the Court of Appeals carefully 
reviewed the federal regulatory scheme, and recognized that 
common law no-airbag tort claims would frustrate the 
federal government’s policy of encouraging (1) public 
acceptance of airbags, and (2) “experimentation with better 
passive restraint systems.” App. 15. The Court of Appeals 
correctly concluded that a no-airbag claim is preempted 
because the Department of Transportation specifically 
determined that “an all airbag rule would disserve the safety 
purposes of the Act,” and that this policy “would be 
disrupted by tort liability.” App. 16. Moreover, the Court of 
Appeals correctly followed and applied controlling Supreme 
Court precedents governing federal preemption of state 
claims. Since this Court has repeatedly denied petitions for 
writs of certiorari on this precise issue, including as recently 
as last year, there is no reason for this Court to now exercise 
its discretionary powers in this case. See e.g. Irving v. Mazda 
Motor Corp., 136 F.3d 764 (11™ Cir. 1998), cert. denied, 119 
S. Ct. 544 (1998). Moreover, this case is not the right 
vehicle to resolve the FMVSS preemption issue inasmuch as 
the Court of Appeals never reached express preemption -- 

thus, a ruling in this Court could not resolve the entire 
preemption issue. Finally, changes in the law since the 
subject 1987 Honda Accord was manufactured require 
airbags in all new passenger cars beginning September 1, 
1997: therefore, this Court need not expend its judicial 
resources on this issue of diminishing importance.

G. Section 1397(k) of the Safety Act Does 
Not Preserve Preempted State Claims 


_ Petitioners argue that there can be no preemption of

petitioners mischaracterize as an “anti-preemption

an “anti-preemption provision” goes well beyond the actual 
words of the statute. 


Section 1397(k) provides: 


Compliance with any federal motor vehicle
