In response, Honda argues that review should be denied

1. As explained in our petition at 7-13, there is in fact 
a deep split among the lower courts on the precise question 
presented in this case: whether a manufacturer may be sued for 
failing to install airbags in one of its vehicles. Honda attempts 
to minimize this conflict by arguing that only a “small number 
of state courts” have held that no-airbag claims are preempted 
by federal law. Opp. at 15-16. This is an understatement of the 
first order given that, in the last five years, five state courts of 
last resort have decided the question in a manner directly 
contrary to all five federal courts of appeals to consider the 
issue. Pet. at 7-9. 


Honda does not attempt to argue that these state court 
decisions are not truly in conflict with the federal rulings. 


Instead, it pretends that a state/federal split of this magnitude is 
insignificant given the unanimity of the federal courts of 
appeals. This, of course, is not the law. See Supreme Court 
Rule 10(a); Stern, Supreme Court Practice 7” Edition, § 4.9, at 
181 (1993). In fact, this Court has often granted review in 
preemption cases based on a conflict between federal courts of 
appeals and state courts of last resort. See, e.g., Cipollone v. 
Liggett Group, Inc., 505 U.S. 504, 508-09 nn.2 & 3 (1992) 
(citing split between five federal courts of appeals and two 
highest state courts as basis for granting review of cigarette 
labeling preemption decision); Lewis v. Brunswick, 107 F.3d 
1494 (11th Cir. 1997), cert. granted, 118 S. Ct. 439 (1998) 
(granting review of boat safety preemption decision based on 
split between two federal courts of appeals and one highest state 
court). Thus, Honda’s argument that there is insufficient 
conflict below to warrant review here — where there is a much 
deeper split than gave rise to either Cipollone or Lewis — is 
simply unavailing.'

this Court lacks authority under 28 U.S.C. § 1257(a) to review

3. Honda’s third argument is that review should be 
denied because “the entire preemption question is not before the 
Court.” Opp. at 17. In reality, as Honda knows, both express 
and implied preemption were squarely presented to ihe courts 
below. The district court held that petitioners’ claims were 
expressly preempted by the Safety Act. See App. 19-20. On 
appeal, petitioners argued that their claims were neither 
expressly nor impliedly preempted. The D.C. Circuit 
considered and addressed the express preemption question at 
some length, see id. at 8-12, acknowledging that the Safety 
Act’s “broadly worded savings clause . . . indicates that 
Congress did not wish to deprive plaintiffs of all their remedies 
at common law.” App. 11. Although the Cour. ultimately 
declined to resolve the express preemption question, id. at 12, 
ruling instead that petitioners’ claims are impliedly preempted 
by Standard 208, id. at 14-16, there is no question that both 
issues were presented below and preserved for review by this 
Court.

that “[o]nly the questions set out in the petition, or fairly

question presented, by its terms, raises the entire issue of 
preemption; it does not separately mention, much less 
distinguish between, express and implied preemption.

4. Honda also argues that review should be denied 
because “[a]ll cars manufactured after September 1, 1997 are 
required to have airbags” (Opp. at 18) and thus the issue will 
eventually disappear. This argument is belied by Honda’s 
previous statements and is factually inaccurate. 


First, Honda’s claim of “diminishing importance” is 
flatly contrary to its amicus brief filed in support of the petition 
for certiorari in Ford Motor Company v. Tebbetts, No. 95-709 
(October Term, 1995). See Pet. at 21-22. In that brief, Honda 
and its fellow auto manufacturers asked this Court to review the 
New Hampshire Supreme Court’s decision on the ground that 
federal preemption of no-airbag claims “is an issue of 
exceptional importance affecting national automobile safety . 
... Id. The auto manufacturers added that there are “/iterally 
millions of automobiles on the road today that are not equipped 
with airbags,” and that “[l]itigation over the preemptive effects 
of [Standard] 208 has proceeded for a decade, with no end in 
sight. . .. Only this Court can put an end to this controversy.” 
Id. (emphases added). In light of these representations made 
only a few years ago in a case involving the identical question 
presented here, it is disingenuous at best for Honda to claim 
now that review is unwarranted and that “[p]etitioners greatly 
exaggerate the number of future no-airbag claims.” /d. at 18. 


Honda’s argument is also wrong on the facts. As Honda 
admits, federal law only required airbags as of September 1, 
1997. Cars, of course, often stay on the road for years and 
years. In fact, the automobile at issue in this case was 
manufactured over /2 years ago, yet petitioners are only now 


presenting their arguments to this Court. Thus, at a minimum, 
no-airbag claims will continue to be litigated for at least another 
decade — and probably much longer. Unless this Court 
intervenes, the chaos among the lower courts will continue and 
spread, with victims’ right to seek compensation depending 
solely on whether they happen to reside in a no-preemption 
jurisdiction. 


Finally, this case has ramifications far beyond the airbag 
context. ivot only is preemption an open question with respect 
to other types of cases arising under the Safety Act, e.g., 
Gracia, 112 F.3d 291 (windshields); Irving, 136 F.3d 764 
(manual lap belts), but the Safety Act’s express preemption 
provision and savings clause are strikingly similar to the 
language of many other statutes. See Pet. at 25. Thus, a 
decision in this case could affect preemption analysis in a broad 
range of cases far beyond the no-airbag context. Honda’s 
attempt to minimize the importance of this case is accordingly 
without merit.’
