SUMMARY OF THE ARGUMENT 


A Fourth Amendment Search Occurred When a Law En- 
forcement Officer Manipulated Bond’s Carry-On Luggage 
to Determine Its Contents. 

Around the country, law enforcement officers—acting 
without probable cause, reasonable suspicion, or consent— 
manipulate travelers’ carry-on luggage to inspect it for 
drugs. Such inspections are Fourth Amendment searches. 
The Fifth Circuit Court of Appeals, however, ruled that 
they are not. The court reasoned that no search occurs 
because travelers who place their luggage in an overhead 
bin expose it to handling by others, and consequently have 
no expectation of privacy in their luggage. The Fifth 
Circuit was incorrect. Travelers like Bond may subject 
their luggage to casual contact from others by placing 
it in an overhead bin. But neither Bond nor any other 
traveler knowingly exposes his luggage to the sort of intru- 
sive and revealing manipulation that Agent Cantu used to 
inspect luggage. Cantu’s manipulation of Bond’s carry-on 
bag infringed Bond’s reasonable expectation of privacy in 
his luggage and therefore was a search. 


A. The Fourth Amendment specifically protects “per- 
sons, houses, papers, and effects” against unreasonable 
search and seizure. Luggage is a repository of one’s per- 
sonal “effects” and society accepts as reasonable the ex- 


pectation that the contents of luggage will remain private. 
Bond manifested and preserved his expectation that the 
contents of his luggage would remain privatt by carrying 
his bag onto the bus and placing it in the overhead bin 
directly above his seat. 


B. By manipulating Bond’s carry-on bag in a manner 
calculated to reveal its contents, Agent Cantu infringed 
Bond’s reasonable expectation of privacy. The agent’s 
manipulation of Bond’s bag bore the hallmarks of a search: 
it was physically intrusive, and it revealed private informa- 
tion about the contents of Bond’s personal luggage. Like 
a patdown of outer clothing to find weapons, or manipula- 
tion of a pocket to investigate its contents—inspections that 
this Court has held are searches—Agent Cantu’s tactile 
examination of Bond’s luggage was a search. 


C. The Fifth Circuit incorrectly concluded that Bond 
gave up the expectation of privacy in his luggage when he 
exposed it to the public by placing his bag in an overhead 
luggage bin. Neither Bond nor any other traveler relin- 
quishes the expectation that the contents of a carry-on 
bag will remain private by temporarily stowing it in an 
overhead bin. Luggage is meant to secure the privacy and 
safety of one’s personal effects while traveling. The Fourth 
Amendment’s protection of luggage means nothing if 
merely placing a bag in an overhead bin extinguishes the 
expectation of privacy in its contents. By putting his bag 
in the bin, Bond might have subjected it to casual contact 
by other passengers, but Agent Cantu’s squeezing and feel- 
ing of Bond’s bag far exceeded what Bond could reason- 
ably have expected from other passengers. Cantu thus 
conducted a search when he manually inspected Bond’s 
bag for drugs by squeezing and feeling it—manipulation 
that Bond would never have reasonably expected from his 
fellow passengers.

ARGUMENT 


A FOURTH AMENDMENT SEARCH OCCURRED 
WHEN A LAW ENFORCEMENT OFFICER MANIPU- 
LATED BOND’S CARRY-ON LUGGAGE TO DETER- 
MINE ITS CONTENTS. 


For purposes of the Fourth Amendment, a search occurs 
when the government infringes an individual’s reasonable 
expectation of privacy. Bond, a passenger on a Greyhound 
bus, had a reasonable expectation of privacy in the carry- 
on bag that he placed in the overhead bin directly above 
his seat. Agent Cantu infringed Bond’s expectation that 
the contents of his luggage would remain private when he 
manipulated Bond’s bag in a manner calculated to deter- 
mine whether it contained drugs. The agent’s intrusive 
tactile examination of Bond’s bag—undertaken without 
probable cause, reasonable suspicion, or consent—deprived 
Bond of the Fourth Amendment right “to be secure in 
[his] . . . effects, against unreasonable searches.” 


A. Bond Had a Reasonable Expectation of Privacy in the 
Contents of His Carry-On Luggage. 


The Fourth Amendment’s prohibition against unreason- 
able searches protects those “expectation[s] of privacy that 
society is prepared to consider reasonable.” United States 
v. Jacobsen, 466 U.S. 109, 113 (1984). Society con- 
siders it reasonable for travelers like Bond to expect that 
the contents of their luggage will remain private. This 
Court has recognized that “a person possesses a privacy 
interest in the contents of personal luggage that is pro- 
tected by the Fourth Amendment.” United States v. Place, 
462 U.S. 696, 707 (1983); see also New Jersey v. T.L.O.., 
469 U.S. 325, 337 (1985) (Fourth Amendment protects 
personal luggage). 


Travelers have a privacy interest in luggage because 
“luggage is intended as a repository of personal effects.” 


United States v. Chadwick, 433 U.S. 1, 13 & n.8 (1977), 
abrogated on other grounds, California v. Acevedo, 500 
U.S. 565 (1991).® One’s “effects” are explictly protected 
by the text of the Fourth Amendment, and the Framers of 
the amendment would have understood the term “effects” 
to include personal property—property like the contents 
of Bond’s carry-on luggage. See Oliver v. United States, 
466 U.S. 170, 177 n.7 (1984) (“effects” in Fourth 
Amendment refers to personal, not real property); see also 
Cardwell v. Lewis, 417 U.S. 583, 591 (1974) (Fourth 
Amendment traditionally deemed to protect personal 
effects) .7 


Bond manifested his privacy expectation by carrying 
his closed luggage onto a Greyhound bus for his trip 
from California to Arkansas. Instead of checking his bag, 
Bond kept it in his control by placing it in the overhead 
bin directly above his seat. With his luggage close by, 
Bond had a reasonable expectation that its contents would 
remain private.

B. Agent Cantu’s Exploratory Tactile Examination of 
Bond’s Bag Was a Search Because It Infringed Bond’s 
Reasonable Expectation of Privacy in the Contents of 
His Luggage. 


Using his sense of touch, Agent Cantu examined Bond’s 
bag from the outside. Such examination constitutes a 
search when it is designed to reveal the presence of ob- 
jects that are hidden from view. See Minnesota v. Dicker- 
son, 508 U.S. 366, 378-79 (1993) (feeling outside of 
jacket pocket to investigate its comtents was search); 
Terry v. Ohio, 392 U.S. 1, 16 (1968) (frisk of outer 
clothing for weapons was search). With regard to a 
person’s clothing, this Court has observed that “it is noth- 
ing less than sheer torture of the English language to 
suggest that a careful exploration of the outer surfaces 

. in an attempt to find weapons is not a ‘search.’” 
Terry, 392 U.S. at 16. It would likewise torture the Eng- 
lish language to say that Agent Cantu’s manipulation of 
the outside of Bond’s luggage, in an attempt to find drugs, 
was not a search. Like the frisk at issue in Terry, Cantu’s 
feeling and squeezing of Bond’s bag was aimed at reveal- 
ing hidden objects; it was calculated to reveal items with 
a size, shape, density, or other characteristic that indi- 
cated the presence of drugs. Cantu’s exploratory manipu- 
lation was a search as that term is commonly understood.°

Amendment, because they infringed Bond’s reasonable

Agent Cantu’s exploratory tactile examination of Bond’s 
luggage was also a Fourth Amendment search because of 
the private information that it revealed. The “protection 
of privacy” is the “principal object of the Amendment.” 
Soldal v. Cook County, Ill., 506 U.S. 56, 64 (1992) 
(internal quotation omitted). Consequently, government 
action that reveals information about private matters often 
constitutes a search. See Hicks, 480 U.S. at 325 (moving 
stereo equipment revealed information about home); 
United States v. Karo, 468 U.S. 705, 715 (1984) (moni- 
toring of “beeper” revealed fact about interior of home); 
Katz, 389 U.S. at 352-53 (electronic eavesdropping re- 
vealed contents of private telephone conversation).

In Hicks, this Court held that a search occurred when 
a police officer moved a piece of stereo equipment in 
Hicks’s apartment. 480 U.S. at 324-25. Although the offi- 
cer was legitimately in the apartment, moving the equip- 
ment invaded Hicks’s protected privacy interest by reveal- 
ing hidden information about the apartment's contents 
—serial numbers that appeared only on the bottom of a 
turntable. Id. 


Like the moving of the turntable in Hicks, Agent 
Cantu’s manipulation of Bond’s luggage invaded a pro- 
tected privacy interest because it revealed information 
about a private matter—the contents of Bond’s luggage. 
The agent’s manipulation revealed detailed and specific 
information about an item contained in Bond’s luggage: 
a hard, brick-like object, with defined edges, and “some- 
thing of different mass inside of it.” (J.A. 11-12, Tr. 9, 
10-11.) Based on this specific information, Cantu thought 
he had found drugs. (J.A. 12-13; Tr. 10-11.) 


This Court has recognized that touch is an effective 
means of discovering specific information about hidden 
items: the “sense of touch is capable of revealing the 
nature of an object with sufficient reliability to support a 
seizure” of the object felt. Dickerson, 508 U.S. at 376. 
In this case, the Fifth Circuit found that Agent Cantu’s 
techniques were “calculated” to make precisely this sort 
of discovery. (J.A. 40.) 


Such techniques are typical when an officer manipulates 
the outside of luggage to reveal its contents. Using his 
sense of touch, the officer must feel his way through the 
various contents of personal luggage to determine whether 
any of those contents has a size, shape, or density indica- 
tive of packaged drugs. The officer’s “tactile inspection 
fis] . . . aimed at discovering the nature of the contents 
of the bag.” United States v. McDonald, 100 F.3d 1320, 


14 


1333 (7th Cir. 1996) (Ripple, J., dissenting) (rejecting 
court’s holding that manipulation of luggage not search). 
He must “work” a bag with his hands to “determine 
whether it contain[s] something of independent evidentiary 
value.” United States v. Nicholson, 144 F.3d 632, 639 
(10th Cir. 1998) (holding manipulation of luggage was 
search). The officer’s manual probing must be sufficiently 
intrusive to permit identification of the internal contents 
of the luggage. See McDonald, 100 F.3d at 1333 (Ripple, 
J., dissenting). While Cantu was not questioned about 
what other contents of Bond’s luggage were revealed by 
his manual inspection, a probing manipulation that is 
calculated to disclose seizable items is likewise capable 
of identifying any number of other objects that are com- 
monly carried in personal luggage. 


Of course, neither Agent Cantu’s nor any other officer’s 
subjective intent determines whether a Fourth Amendment 
search has occurred. See Ciraolo, 476 U.S. at 213-14 & 
n.2 (officer’s intent to look for marijuana irrelevant). 
It is not the officer’s purpose, but the means necessary 
to achieve that purpose which is subject to Fourth 
Amendment scrutiny. Cf. Maryland v. Buie, 494 US. 
325, 335 (1990) (considering “aim” of a prcetective 
sweep); Dickerson, 508 U.S. at 378 (considering officer’s 
purpose not to search for weapons, but to search for 
evidence); Terry, 392 U.S. at 16 (limited purpose of 
frisk was “to find weapons”). The manipulation neces- 
sary to reveal contraband packed in luggage is probing, 
physically intrusive, and revealing. Objiectively considered, 
it constitutes a search, just as the similar manipulation in 
Terry and in Dickerson was found to be a search. 


While Terry and Dickerson involved searches of a per- 
son, “persons” receive no more protection under the plain 
terms of the Fourth Amendment than the “effects” con- 


15 


tained in Bond’s luggage. Personal effects may be carried 
in one’s luggage as readily as they may be carried in one’s 
jacket pocket. It would be anomalous to find that the 
Fourth Amendment is implicated when a law enforcement 
officer “works” a pocket to discover its contents, but not 
when he “works” personal luggage for the same purpose. 
In either case, the officer’s action is a serious infringement 
of privacy interests and, consequently, a search. 


3. The physical intrusiveness of Agent Cantu’s explor- 
atory manipulation, and the information it revealed, 
distinguish his actions from the dog sniff in United 
States v. Place. 


In Place, this Court considered whether a search 
occurred when DEA agents permitted a drug-detecting 
dog to sniff a traveler’s luggage. 462 U.S. at 707. As the 
Court observed, the dog sniff was uniquely nonintrusive: 
“We are aware of no other investigative procedure that is 
so limited . . . in the manner in which the information is 
obtained[.]” Jd. The dog sniff was also uniquely limited 
in the information it revealed. It disclosed only the pres- 
ence or absence of contraband, and it was incapable of 
revealing anything else about the contents of Place’s lug- 
gage. Id. Because the dog sniff was not intrusive, and 
because it could reveal nothing about the noncontraband 
contents of Place’s luggage, this Court held that it was 
not a search. Id.; see also Jacobsen, 466 U.S. at 123-24 
& n.24 (chemical test of white powder not search because, 
like dog sniff, it “could reveal nothing about noncontra- 
band items”). 


The Court’s narrow decision in Place has no application 
to the far more intrusive and revealing investigation con- 
ducted here. Unlike the dog sniff in Place, an exploratory 
feeling and squeezing manipulation is physically intrusive, 
and it reveals more than just the presence or absence of 


16 


contraband in a traveler’s luggage. A “tactile examina- 
tion” of luggage is capable of “reveal[ing] almost as much 
information as opening the bag itself, such as information 
about the number, the shape, and character of items, per- 
haps very personal items, inside the bag.” United States 
v. Gwinn, 191 F.3d 874, 879 (8th Cir. 1999) (holding 
that manipulation of luggage is search). As illustrated 
by Agent Cantu’s testimony, it was this sort of probing 
and intrusive manual inspection that occurred in Bond’s 
case. Unlike the dog sniff in Place, an exploratory tactile 
examination like Cantu’s infringes a traveler’s reasonable 
expectation of privacy in the contents of his luggage. 


C. Bond Did Not Relinquish His Reasonable Expectation 
of Privacy by Placing His Bag in the Luggage Bin 
Directly Over His Seat. 


The Fifth Circuit Court of Appeals found that Bond 
had no reasonable expectation of privacy in his carry-on 
bag because he “knowingly exposed it to the public” by 
putting it in the overhead luggage bin on the Greyhound 
bus. (J.A. 39.) Observing that the bin was accessible to 
other bus passengers, the court reasoned that Bond could 
have expected his bag would be handled by others. /d. 


The Fifth Circuit’s ruling failed to recognize two crucial 
factors. First, luggage is designed to preserve the privacy 
and security of one’s personal effects while traveling; 
privacy expectations are not forfeited when a traveler tem- 
porarily stows his luggage nearby as he travels. Second, 
Agent Cantu’s feeling and squeezing of Bond’s bag ex- 
ceeded the incidental and casual contact that Bond would 
have expected from his fellow passengers. Bond could not 
have reasonably expected other passengers to feel and 
squeeze his luggage to discern its contents, thus he did not 
knowingly expose it to the type of intrusive tactile exami- 
nation Cantu used to inspect luggage. By placing his bag

in the overhead bin. Bond did not relinquish his privacy 
interest in its contents. 


1. Travelers like Bond do not relinquish their reason- 
able expectation of privacy in luggage by putting 
it in an overhead bin. 


The “Fourth Amendment protects people, not places.” 
Katz, 389 U.S. at 351. Thus, “[w]hat a person . . . seeks 
to preserve as private, even in an area accessible to the 
public, may be constitutionally protected.” Jd.; see also 
O’Connor v. Ortega, 480 U.S. 709, 730 (1987) (Scalia, 
J., concurring) (“It is privacy that is protected by the 
Fourth Amendment, not solitude.”). Travelers like Bond 
preserve the privacy of their personal effects by placing 
them in luggage when they leave their homes. Luggage, 
by definition, is designed to carry one’s personal effects 
while traveling. Traveling, a right protected by the Con- 
stitution, necessarily is done in places that are accessible 
to the public. 


Travelers take their luggage into public terminals when 
leaving or arriving on buses and trains. Those buses and 
trains carry passengers on public thoroughfares while they 
travel. Carrying luggage through places that are acces- 
sible to the public does not mean that the right to expect 
privacy in the contents of that luggage is forfeited. See 
Place, 462 U.S. at 707 (privacy expectation in luggage 
contents at airport); Chadwick, 433 U.S. at 4-6 (privacy 
expectation in luggage at train station). To the contrary, 
one places personal effects in luggage in order to secure 
their privacy and safety while traveling. See T.L.O., 469 
U.S. at 337-40 (personal luggage conceals contents from 
plain view). 


Putting luggage in a place accessible to others may 
affect one’s expectation of privacy “in the existence and 


18 


the outward appearance of the luggage.” Ortega, 480 
U.S. at 716 (plurality opinion) (taking closed luggage to 
workplace). But the “expectaiion of privacy in the con- 
tents of the luggage is not affected in the same way.” 
Id. That is so precisely because, as the repository of one’s 
“effects,” luggage is meant to keep personal effects private 
even in, and perhaps especially in, areas accessible to the 
public. 


In this case, Bond manifested his expectation of privacy 
in the contents of his luggage by putting it in the over- 
head bin directly above his seat. By stowing his bag close 
by, Bond kept control of it; he retained the ability to pre- 
vent other passengers from interfering with it. Agent 
Cantu, by his show of authority, did what other passengers 
would not have been expected—or permitted—to do: he 
examined and explored Bond’s carry-on luggage by feeling 
and squeezing it to determine its contents. By putting 
luggage in an overhead bin, neither Bond nor any other 
passenger relinquishes his right to be free from such an 
intrusive governmental invasion of protected privacy 
interests. 


2. Bond did not knowingly expose his bag to an in- 
trusive manipulation calculated to detect its 
contents. 


When he put his bag in the bin above his seat, Bond 
knowingly exposed the outward appearance of his luggage 
to the view of other passengers. He may even have ex- 
pected that other bus passengers would come into casual 
contact with his bag, pushing or moving it as they stored 
their own luggage in the overhead bin. See Gwinn, 191 
F.3d at 879 (passengers may push aside or briefly touch 
others’ bags); Nicholson, 144 F.3d at 639 (passengers 
may push or move others’ bags). But Agent Cantu’s 


19 


squeezing and feeling of Bond’s luggage far exceeded the 
casual contact Bond could have expected from other pas- 
sengers. Neither Bond nor any other person “traveling by 
bus or rail would expect, or permit, a fellow passenger to 
. . . Squeeze or manipulate his or her hand baggage in a 
concerted attempt to determine the contents.” McDonald, 
100 F.3d at 1334 (Ripple, J., dissenting); Gwinn, 191 
F.3d at 879 (majority of traveling public would not ex- 
pect overhead luggage “to be subject to a calculated and 
thorough squeezing and manipulation”). 


When government action exceeds what could reasonably 
be expected from others, it cannot be said that one has 
knowingly exposed his protected privacy interests to that 
action. The Court made this distinction in Lo-Ji Sales, 
Inc. v. New York, 442 U.S. 319 (1979). In that case, 
a local magistrate accompanied police to an adult book- 
store where he viewed films and examined books and 
magazines offered for sale to determine whether they were 
obscene. Id. at 322-23. Unlike a member of the public, 
the magistrate neither paid to view the films nor bought 
the publications he inspected. Instead, he directed the 
bookstore’s proprietor to disable the coin-operated film 
projectors before he viewed the films, and he had police 
remove cellophane wrappers from books and magazines, 
as well as take items from a glass-enclosed case. Id. 


The government contended that there was no expecta- 
tion of privacy in these items because the bookstore 
proprietor displayed the films and magazines in a place 
where the public was invited to enter. 442 U.S. at 329. 
This Court disagreed. Although the magistrate was in an 
area accessible to the public, he was not seeing the books 
and films “as a customer would ordinarily see them.” 7d. 
Consequently, the bookstore’s proprietor retained a reason- 
able expectation of privacy against government intrusion 


20 


into the property that he offered for sale. IJd.; see also 
Nicholson, 144 F.3d at 639 (police manipulation that 
exceeded what could reasonably be expected from others 
was search); United States v. Most, 876 F.2d 191, 198 
n.13 (D.C. Cir. 1989) (same) .** 


Agent Cantu did not handle Bond’s bag as other 
passengers would reasonably have been expected to handle 
it. Instead, Cantu squeezed and felt Bond’s luggage in a 
manner calculated to reveal its contents. Passengers travel- 
ing by common carrier do not engage in an exploratory 
examination of one another’s bags using their sense of 
touch. Neither do they squeeze one anothers’ personal 
luggage as Cantu did—they do not manipulate luggage in 
a manner calculated to reveal its contents. 


By placing his luggage in the overhead bin, Bond might 
have subjected it to fellow passengers’ casual contact. But 
he certainly did not knowingly expose it to the intrusive 
and exploratory manipulation that Agent Cantu used to 
inspect its contents. When Agent Cantu felt and squeezed 
Bond’s luggage, he infringed Bond’s reasonable expecta- 
tion of privacy.

“The basic purpose” of the Fourth Amendment is to 
“safeguard the privacy and security of individuals against 
arbitrary invasions by governmental officials.” Camara v. 
Municipal Court, 387 U.S. 523, 528 (1967). The amend- 
ment “gives concrete expression to the right of the people 
which is basic to a free society.” Id. (internal quotation 
and citation omitted). It guarantees “the right to be let 
alone—the most comprehensive of rights and the right 
most valued by civilized” society. Winston v. Lee, 470 
U.S. 753, 758 (1985) (quoting Olmstead v. United States, 
277 U.S. 438, 478 (1928) (Brandeis, J., dissenting) ). 
Agent Cantu’s “inspection” of bus passengers’ luggage for 
drugs was an arbitrary government invasion that violated 
the privacy and security the Fourth Amendment was 
meant to preserve. 


Acting without probable cause, reasonable suspicion, or 
consent, Cantu manipulated travelers’ personal carry-on 
luggage in an exploratory tactile examination calculated 
to reveal the private contents of their bags. Unless his 
conduct is ruled a search, the privacy interests of travelers 
everywhere—on buses, on trains, on subways, and even 
on the streets—will be severely compromised. People who 
put their jackets on a coat rack, their purses on a nearby 
chair, or their briefcases beside them on a bench, will 
subject those belongings to random, suspicionless han- 
dling by law enforcement officers, intent on discovering 
hidden items. Preventing this sort of widespread govern- 
ment intrusion into citizens’ personal effects was the 
reason the Fourth Amendment was enacted. To give effect 
to “the essential rights of a citizen of a Free State,” » 
the Court must rule that Agent Cantu’s conduct was a 
search.
