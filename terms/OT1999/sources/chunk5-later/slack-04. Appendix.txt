“Abuse of the writ doctrine examines petitioner's conduct: the question is whether petitioner 
possessed, or by reasonable means could have obtained, a sufficient basis to allege a claim in the first 
petition... .” McClesky, 499 U.S.at 498. An inmate's pro se status does not constitute "an objective 
external factor" which excuses a petitioner's failure to raise his new claim in prior habeas petitions. 

Saahir v. Collins, 956 F.2d 115, 118 (Sth Cir.1992); Hughes v. Idaho State Board, 800 F.2d 905, 
909 (9th Cir.1986). An inmate's pro se status does not excuse an inmate's ignorance of the law nor 
establish cause for the purpose of determining whether an inmate abused the writ by failing to raise 
new claims in prior habeas petitions: there is no constitutional right to counsel in post-conviction 
proceedings. Saahir, 956 F.2d at 118. To show actual prejudice, “petitioner bears ‘the burden of 
showing not merely that the errors at his trial constituted a possibility of prejudice, but that they 
worked to his actual and substantial disadvantage, infecting his entire trial with errors of constitutional 
dimension.’” White v. Lewis, 874 F.2d 599, 603 (9th Cir. 1989) (quoting United States v. Frady, 456 
U.S. 152, 170 (1982)). 

As an alternative to showing “cause and prejudice,” Slack may attempt to show that dismissal 
of his instant claim on abuse of the writ grounds will result in a “fundamental miscarriage of justice.” 
Such a showing, however, is very difficult to make. It requires Slack to demonstrate that he is 
“actually innocent” of the crime with which he was convicted. See also Smith v. Murray, 477 U.S. 
527, 537 (1986). 

Respondents have met their burden in alleging that Slack has abused the writ. Slack must now 
establish cause and prejudice for his failure to assert the instant claim in his first petition or he must 
demonstrate that a fundamental miscarriage of justice will result if the court dismisses the case for 
abuse of the writ. Given the extreme difficulty of showing a “fundamentally unjust incarceration,” 


this court recommends Slack disregard this alternative test and attempt to show cause and prejudice.

The Antiterrorism and Effective Death Penalty Act of 1996 (AEDPA), 110 Stat. 
1214, signed into law on April 24, 1996, enacted the present 28 U.S.C.A. § 2253 (Supp. 1997, 
requiring a “certificate of appealability” in order for an appeal to be taken from a final order in 
a habeas proceeding. See 28 U.S.C.A. § 2253(b) (requiring “ substantial showing of the denial 
of a constitutional right”). Prior to the enactment of the AEDPA, § 2253 required a “certificate 
of probable cause” in order for an appeal to be taken from a final order in a habeas proceeding. 
See 28 U.S.C.A. § 2253 (1994). 

The portions of the AEDPA which apply to non-capital cases have been held to 
be inapplicable to cases already pending when the Act was passed. Lindh v. Murphy, 117 S.Ct. 
2059, 138 L.Ed.2d 481 (1997) (the portions of § 2254(d) dealing with noncapital cases are 
inapplicable to cases already pending when the Act was passed); Jeffries v. Wood, 114 F.3d 
1484 (9th Cir. 1997) (the AEDPA does not apply retroactively to cases filed in federal courts in 
Ninth Circuit prior to Act’s effective date). 

Slack filed his petition on May 30, 1995, well before the date the AEDPA was 
passed (April 24, 1996). CR 4. Accordingly, his case is governed by the pre-Act version of § 
2253 (requiring a certificate of probable cause). The amendments to § 2253 promulgated by the 
AEDPA cannot apply retroactively to Slack’s cases. See Lindh, and Jeffries, see also Greenwalt 
v, Stewart, 105 F.3d 1268 (9th Cir. 1997) (applying “certificate of probable cause” standard to 
dismissal of petition filed April 23, 1996).

Petitioner’s conviction and sentence became final when the Nevada Supreme Court 
issued its remittitur on December 20, 1988, denying Petitioner’s direct appeal. A warrant for Petitioner's 
execution was issued setting March 31, 1989, as the execution date. Four days prior to the scheduled 
execution, Petitioner pro se filed a petition for writ of habeas corpus and motion for stay of execution 
in this court. A hearing was immediately held and the execution stayed to allow Petitioner an 
opportunity to pursue a state post-conviction proceeding. The federal proceeding was subsequently 
stayed and then disinissed a few months later. In total, the federal action was open only three and a half 
months. The acknowledged purpose of that action was to stop an execution that was going to occur 
before Petitioner could even pursue a state post-conviction proceeding; the goal of the prior federal 
action was not to have the validity of his state court conviction and death sentence reviewed on the 
merits. Under these circumstances, Petitioner’s conduct does not evidence a failure to act diligently in 
stating all grounds for relief in the first federal action. 

Abuse-of-the-wnit doctrine examines petitioner's conduct. The question is whether 

petitioner possessed, or by reasonable means could have obtained, a sufficient basis to 

allege a claim in the first petition and pursue the matter through the habeas process. 
Farmer, 98 F.3d at 1560, quoting McCleskey v. Zant, 499 U.S. 467, 498 (1991 )(emphasis original). 
To hold Petitioner bound to only those grounds stated in the prior action, an action that was begun solely 
so that he could commence state court post-conviction proceedings before being put to death, is absurd. 
The motion to dismiss shall be denied. 

Petitioner has filed a motion (#50) to hold in abeyance, alternatively to extend the time, 
for filing a motion for an evidentiary hearing. The scheduling order (#21) directed Petitioner to request 
an evidentiary hearing at the same time that he opposed a motion to dismiss or filed a traverse to the 
answer. The motion shall be granted to the extent that Petitioner shall file his motion for an evidentiary 
hearing at the same time but separately from the traverse.

Petitioner Antonio A. Slack has filed an amended petition for habeas relief pursuant to 28 
U.S.C. § 2254 (#65). Respondents move to dismiss claiming several grounds are unexhausted and 
abusive (#66). The Court ordered Slack to file points and authorities addressing whether he can 
overcome the abuse of the writ bar that is applicable to grounds two, four(C), five, six and seven 
(#72). Slack has filed a motion for reconsideration of the court order (#73). For the reasons set forth 
below, the Court finds grounds two, four(C), five, six and seven are abusive and ground three is 
unexhausted. Therefore the petition must be dismissed.

weapon. See #16, ex.12. Slack was sentenced to two consecutive life terms with the possibility of 
parole. See #16, ex.13. Slack filed a direct appeal with the Nevada Supreme Court which was 
dismissed. See #16, ex.22.

ABUSE OF THE WRIT 

A second petition may be dismissed for abuse of the writ if new grounds are alleged and the 
failure to allege them in the previous petition was an abuse of the writ. See Farmer v. McDaniel, 98 
F.3d 1548, 1555 (9th Cir.1996). The previous petition need not have been dismissed on the merits. 
See id. at 1557. The instant petition therefore may be subject to dismissal for abuse of the writ. 

In McCleskey v. Zant, 499 U.S. 467 (1991), the U.S. Supreme Court defined the respective 
burdens of pleading abuse of the writ. The State met that burden here by noting Slack’s previous writ 
history, identifying the grounds for relief that appear for the first time in the instant petition and 
alleging that Slack abused the writ. See id. The burden now is on Slack to disprove abuse of the writ 


by showing cause for failing to raise the new grounds for relief earlier and prejudice therefrom. See 


id. Slack does not meet his burden.

the malice aforethought instruction was unconstitutional; and (b) appellate counsel conceded two of the four issues raised

In reply to the court order instructing Slack to file points and authorities showing cause and 
prejudice to overcome the abuse of the writ bar, he filed a motion for reconsideration (#73). First the 
Court notes that Slack has incorrectly filed his motion for reconsideration pursuant to Fed.R.Civ.P.59. 
Under this rule a party may move for a new trial or an amendment of a judgment. Since there has nox 
been a trial and a judgment has not been entered in this case, Slack cannot bring a motion pursuant 
to this rule. Additionally even if Slack could bring a motion under Fed.R.Civ.P.59, the motion must 
be brought within ten days after the entry of judgment. See Fed.R.Civ.P. 59(e). Slack’s motion was 
untimely since it was filed two days after the ten day period expired. Nevertheless, the Court wil] 
address Slack’s arguments in his motion for reconsideration. ? 

In his motion for reconsideration, Slack claims the State has not established the habeas petition 
is abusive, the amended petition is not successive, and that the case is governed by the Antiterrorism 
and Effective Death Penalty Act (AEDPA). As previously noted, the State has met its burden under 
McClesky by specifying which grounds for relief in the amended habeas petition are abusive. Next, 
the instant petition is Slack’s second petition and thus successive. The Ninth Circuit has held that a 
judge may find a second petition is abusive when new grounds are alleged and a petitioner failed to 
allege them in the previous petition, even if the previous petition was dismissed for failing to exhaust 
state remedies. See Farmer, 98 F.3d at 1557. Lastly, Slack wrongly asserts the case is governed by 
the AEDPA because it was commenced after April 26, 1996. First, the instant case was commenced 
March 27, 1995. Second, the amendments to 28 U.S.C. §§ 2244(a) and 2255 resulting from the 
AEDPA are not retroactive. See Lindh v. Murphy, --- U.S. ----, ----, 117 S.Ct. 2059, 2063 (1997). 
Accordingly, the AEDPA is not applicable to this case. 

Thus, since Slack has failed to demonstrate cause and prejudice to overcome the abuse of the 


writ bar, the Court will dismiss grounds two, four(c), five, six and seven as abusive.

EXHAUSTION 

Although respondents argue several grounds are unexhausted, the court will only address 
ground three since all other grounds will be dismissed as abusive. Title 28 U.S.C. § 2254 (b) and (c) 
provide that a state prisoner must exhaust all available state court remedies before filing a petition for 
habeas corpus in federal court. The exhaustion of available state judicial remedies is ordinarily a 
prerequisite to obtaining federal habeas corpus relief. 28 U.S.C. § 2254(b) (1988); Rose v. Lundy, 
455 U.S. 509, 515 (1982). To demonstrate compliance with the exhaustion requirement, a habeas 
applicant must show that the claim included in the federal petition was “fairly presented” to the state's 
highest court, Picard v. Connor, 404 U.S. 270, 275 (1971), or demonstrate that no state remedy 
remains available. Harmon v. Ryan, 959 F.2d 1457, 1460 (9th Cir. 1992). A claim has been “fairly 
presented" to the state court if the petitioner has described the operative facts and the legal theory 
upon which he bases his constitutional claim. Anderson v. Harless, 459 U.S. 4, 6 (1982). 
Furthermore, a petitioner cannot merely allege exhaustion; the record must show that the petitioner 
sought review in the highest state court. Cartwright v. Cupp, 650 F.2d 1103, 1104 (9th Cir. 
1981) (per curiam), cert. denied, 455 U.S. 1023 (1982). In addition, the habeas petitioner must 
exhaust his state remedies as to each of his federal claims. If a habeas corpus petition contains both 
exhausted and non-exhausted claims, the petition must be dismissed. Rose, 455 U.S. at 520. 

In ground three, Slack claims his Fourteenth and Fifth Amendment right to due process was 
violated when the jury was prejudiced by evidence of sexual misconduct. Respondents contend the 
claim is unexhausted because although Slack presented the facts of this claim to the Nevada Supreme 


Court, he did not claim his Fourteenth and Fifth Amendment rights were violated. If a petitioner fails 


to alert the state court to the fact that he is raising a federal constitutional claim, his federal claim is 
unexhausted regardless of its similarity to the issues raised in state court. Duncan v. Henry, 513 U.S. 
364, 366 (1995); Johnson v. Zenon, 88 F.3d 828, 830 (1996); see also Ybarra v. Sumner, 678

CONCLUSION 

Since ground three of Slack’s third amended habeas petition is unexhausted, this action will 
be dismissed to allow Slack the opportunity to present the unexhausted claim to the state court. If 
Slack wishes to continue with this proceeding instead of returning to state court, he will need to re- 
open this proceeding and abandon the unexhausted claim as described below. See Reutter v. Crande, 
109 F.3d 575 (9th Cir. 1997). Alternatively, Slack may immediately commence a new federal action 
in which this court could review the merits of the unexhausted claim because the new action would 
be subject to the statutory changes effected by the Antiterrorism and Effective Death Penalty Act 
(AEDPA)*; however, relief could not be granted based on any unexhausted claim. 

Pursuant to the AEDPA, the dismissal of this action will trigger the commencement of the one 
year statute of limitations period for Slack to return to federal court with a new action. 28 U.S.C. 
§ 2244(d)(1). The time during which Slack’s post conviction proceeding is pending in state court will 
not count toward the one year period. 28 U.S.C. § 2244(d)(2). Therefore, if Slack waits three 
months after this dismissal to file a state post conviction petition, he will have only nine months from 
the disposition of that state proceeding in which to commence a new federal proceeding. The failure 
to timely commence a new federal proceeding will bar Slack from having the validity of his conviction 


and/or sentence reviewed in federal court. 


* Until recently, a federal court could not proceed with a mixed petition that contained both exhausted and 
unexhausted claims for habeas corpus relief. Rose, 455 U.S. at 521-22; Szeto v. Rusen, 709 F.2d 1340, 1341 (9th Cir. 
1983). However, in April 1996, Congress amended 28 U.S.C. § 2254 to give federal courts the authority to deny habeas 
relief based on unexhausted grounds. Thus, now a federal court can address a mixed petition, but can only deny relief 
regarding any unexhausted grounds.
