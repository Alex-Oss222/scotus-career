ORDER DISMISSING APPEAL 
(Filed Nov. 5 1991) 


This is an appeal from a judgment of conviction of 
second-degree murder with the use of a deadly weapon. 
Having reviewed the record and briefs and having heard 
the parties’ arguments, we conclude that Slack was prop- 
erly convicted. On direct examination, Slack denied that 
he had a girlfriend/boyfriend relationship with the 
twelve-year-old victim. The prosecution's reference to a 
sexual relationship between Slack and the victim was not 
improper. 


The prosecution, on cross-examination, asked Slack 
whether he had told the North Las Vegas Police Depart- 
ment that he had had sexual intercourse with the victim 
on Halloween, 1989. Slack did not remember making any 
such statement. Slack contends that this reference to an 
intimate relationship was improperly admitted because 
the jury was prejudiced by the thought of a nineteen- 
year-old man and a twelve-year-old girl having a sexual 
relationship. 


We conclude that the prosecution’s reference to the 
intimate relationship of Slack and the victim was prop- 
erly admitted as evidence of Slack’s credibility after he 
denied having a boyfriend/girlfriend relationship with 
the victim. The trial court has discretion over the limita- 
tions of cross-examination, Dyer v. State, 99 Nev. 422, 42°, 
663 P.2d 699, 701 (1983), and that discretion was not 
abused here. 


Furthermore, any error in allowing this line of cross- 
examination was harmless, considering the arount of 
evidence linking Slack to the shooting. The fact that the 
jury returned a verdict of second-degree murder, when it 
was also instructed on first-degree murder, indicates that 
the disputed evidence was not prejudicial. 


At oral argument, Slack’s counsel conceded that the 
other issues raised in the brief were clearly without merit. 


Accordingly, we hereby 


ORDER this appeal dismissed.

1038 (1989): 


1) Ineffective assistance of counsel during all pre- 
trial and trial proceedings as follows:

completed before his testimony was allowed;

ORDER 
DENYING PROPER PERSON PETITION FOR 
POST-CONVICTION RELIEF 


DATE OF HEARING: 9-2-92 
TIME OF HEARING: 9:00 A.M. 


The Proper Person Petition for Post-Conviction Relief 
of Antonio Tonton Slack having come before the above 
entitled Court on the 2nd day of September, 1992, this 
Court having duly considered the Proper Person Petition 
for Post-Conviction Relief and Respondent’s Answer in 


25 


Opposition to the Petition for Post-Conviction Relief and 
good cause appearing the Court does hereby enter the 
following Findings of Fact, Conclusions of Law and Deci- 
sion: 


FINDINGS OF FACT 


1. The trial of this matter occurred between Febru- 
ary 20 and February 22, 1990 in Department IV of the 
Eighth Judicial District Court. The trial jury returned a 
verdict of guilty of Murder of the Second Degree With 
Use of a Deadly Weapon. Thereafter, the court imposed 
consecutive sentences of life with the possibility of parole 
in the Nevada State Prison. Additionally, the Court stipu- 
lated that Slack should pay $2,413.50 in restitution. 


2. A direct appeal was pursued to the Nevada 
Supreme Court and by its Order Dismissing Appeal filed 
November 5, 1991 the conviction and sentence of Slack 
were affirmed. 


3. Defendant Slack did cause to be filed on July 10, 
1992 a Proper Person Petition for Post-Conviction Relief 
wherein he alleged that he had received ineffective repre- 
sentation of counsel at the trial and appellate levels and 
that the Court had committed judicial error during his 
trial proceedings. The Court does expressly find that all 
of the allegations raised by the Defendant are contra- 
dicted by the record in this case. Slack was represented 
by Michael Gardner of the Clark County Public 
Defender’s Office at the trial and appellate levels. The 
Court finds that his representation was effective, profes- 
sional, vigorous and thorough. The petition of Slack has 
failed to establish that the representation of Michael 


—_, 


26 


Gardner was deficient or that Slack was prejudiced by the 
manner in which he was defended by counsel. 


4. Slack has complained in his petition that his trial 
counsel failed to determine what type of agreement was 
reached with Kamal Bey, who was a principal State's 
witness. The Court finds that this contention is totally 
specious. The preliminary hearing and the trial record 
explicitly established the nature of the agreement 
between Bey and law enforcement. In return for Bey’s 
cooperation as a witness the State of Nevada agreed to 
withdraw its motion to have Kamal Bey certified to stand 
trial as an adult. However, the substantive charge of 
accessory to murder in the juvenile system was not 
affected by the agreement. Subsequently, Bey was com- 
mitted to Elko for six months as a result of the finding in 
Juvenile Court regarding the accessory to commit murder 
offense. Accordingly, this Court finds that Defendant 
Slack and his attorney were completely aware of the 
parameters of the agreement reached between Kamal Bey 
and the prosecution. Further, this Court finds that trial 
counsel was not ineffective in failing to file a pre-trial 
motion in limine to exclude the testimony of Kamal Bey. 
Nothing about the status of Kamal Bey made him incom- 
petent to be a prosecution witness. Effective trial repre- 
sentation does not require the filing of frivolous pre-trial 
motions. Slack argues that his trial counsel failed to inter- 
view Slack’s brother to refute the testimony of Kamal Bey. 
However, the testimony of a family member would have 
been inherently suspect and of questionable credibility in 
the eyes of the jury. Also, this contention is a naked 
allegation unsupported by any factual predicate. Slack 
contends that his counsel was ineffective in failing to file 


27 


a pre-trial motion in limine to exclude reference to prior 
sexual relations with the victim. This contention is frivo- 
lous. In its Order Dismissing Appeal filed November 5, 
1991 the Nevada Supreme Court ruled that the prosecu- 
tion’s reference to the intimate relationship between Slack 
and the victim was properly admitted as evidence of 
Slack’s credibility after he denied having a boyfriend / 
girlfriend association with the victim. Hence, this issue 
has already been addressed by the State Supreme Court 
and the Court finds that their previous ruling remains the 
law of this case. Slack submits that his counsel was inef- 
fective in not requesting an accomplice instruction. This 
Court finds no evidence that Kamal Bey was an accom- 
plice to murder. Consequently, the Court would not have 
given an accomplice instruction had one been proffered 
by trial counsel for Defendant Slack. Slack also argues 
that he was prejudiced by the failure of his counsel to 
present evidence to the jury that the only fingerprints 
found on the murder weapon were those of Kamal Bey. 
This assertion is a naked allegation unsupported by the 
record. Furthermore, Defendant Slack made various 
admissions to citizens and to representatives of the North 
Las Vegas Police Department accepting responsibility for 
the shooting of Alanna Holmes. Accordingly, the Court 
finds that the totality of the evidence introduced at the 
trial was overwhelming proof that Slack was the shooter 
of Alanna Holmes. 


5. Slack also argues that he received ineffective rep- 
resentation during his direct appeal. The Court finds that 
this contention is without merit. Slack contends that a 
Franklin violation should have been raised on direct 
appeal. However, there is no evidence that Kamal Bey 


28 


was an accomplice in the shooting of the victim. The only 
evidence involved Kamal Bey as an accessory to the 
murder of Alanna Holmes. The Franklin rule applies to 
accomplice testimony. Therefore, the Court finds that the 
Franklin issue was not applicable to this case. Likewise, 
since there is no evicence that Bey was an accomplice it 
was not incumbent upon appellate counsel to argue 
before the State Supreme Court that an accomplice 
instruction should have been given to the trial jury. Addi- 
tionally, this Court finds no evidence of prosecutorial 
misconduct in the record of the trial proceedings. So, the 
failure of appellate counsel to raise the issue of pros- 
ecutorial misconduct does not establish ineffective repre- 
sentation. 


6. The Court also rejects all arguments by Slack that 
judicial error occurred at his trial. This Court finds that 
the trial court properly i~structed the trial jury. Instruc- 
tions regarding accomplice testimony were not applicable 
in that no accomplice testified. The issue regarding pre- 
meditation is now academic. The trial jury convicted 
Slack of Murder of the Second Degree With Use of a 
Deadly Weapon and not murder of the first degree. 


CONCLUSIONS OF LAW 


1. Slack’s claim of ineffective assistance of counsel 
must be analyzed under the “reasonably effective assis- 
tance” standard articulated in Strickland v. Washington, 
466 U.S. 668 (1984), and adopted by the Nevada Supreme 
Court in Warden v. Lyons, 100 Nev. 430, 683 P.2d 504 
(1984), cert. denied, 471 U.S. 1004 (1985). The Strickland 
standard requires a two-prong analysis. First, a defendant 


29 


must show that his counsel's performance was deficient. 
This analysis requires a showing that counsel made errors 
so serious that a defendant's attorney was not function- 
ing as the “counsel” guaranteed by the Sixth Amend- 
ment. Second, a defendant must show that the deficient 
performance prejudiced his defense and caused him to be 
deprived of his right to a fair trial. See also Bejarano v. 
State, 106 Nev. 840, 842, 801 P.2d 1388 (1990). This Court 
concludes as a matter of law that Defendant Slack has 
failed to establish that his trial and appellate counsel 
provided ineffective representation and Slack has failed 
to show that he suffered any prejudice by the manner in 
which he was represented at the trial and appellate 
levels. Additionally, Defendant may not litigate in a peti- 
tion for post-conviction relief matters which have been 
decided on his direct appeal. Hall v. State, 91 Nev. 314, 535 
P.2d 797 (1975); Walker v. State, 85 Nev. 337, 343, 455 P.2d 
34 (1969) and State v. Loveless, 62 Nev. 312, 150 P.2d 1015 
(1944). 


2. Further, the Court concludes that Slack’s proper 
person petition consists entirely of factual claims which 
are belied or repelled by the record of these proceedings. 
A defendant seeking post-conviction relief is not entitled 
to an evidentiary hearing or to any of the remedies he 
seeks based upon factual allegations which are contra- 
dicted by the record. See Hargrove v. State, 100 Nev. 498, 
686 P.2d 222 (1984) and Grondin v. State, 97 Nev. 454, 634 
P.2d 456 (1981). 


30 


DECISION 


Accordingly, based on the foregoing Findings of Fact 
and Conclusions of Law, it is the Order of this Court that 
Defendant Slack’s Proper Person Petition for Post-Con- 
viction Relief is hereby denied.

ORDER DISMISSING APPEAL 
(Filed Dec. 30, 1993) 


This is a proper person appeal from an order of the 
district court denying a post-conviction petition for a writ 
of habeas corpus. 


On May 23, 1990, the district court convicted appel- 
lant of one count of second degree murder with use of a 
deadly weapon. The district court sentenced appellant to 
serve in the Nevada State Prison two consecutive terms of 
life with the possibility of parole. This court dismissed 
appellant's direct appeal. Slack v. State, Docket No. 21421 
(Order Dismissing Appeal, November 5, 1991). 


On July 10, 1992, appellant filed in the district court a 
petition for post-conviction relief. In his petition, appel- 
lant argued that he was denied his right to effective 
assistance of counsel at trial and that he was denied his 
right to effective assistance of counsel on direct appeal. 
Appellant further argued that the district court erred by 
failing to instruct the jury on the credibility of accomplice 
testimony and premeditation. Specifically, appellant 
stated the following about his trial attorney: 1) he failed 


— 


sind a6 San . 


© 


32 


to find out what type of agreement was made with Kamal 
Bey, the state’s key witness and appellant's co-defendant; 
2) he failed to file a motion in limine to prohibit the 
introduction of evidence concerning prior sexual rela- 
tions with the victim; 3) he failed to file a motion in 
limine to prohibit the testimony of Kamal Bey because 
Bey’s agreement with the state required specific testi- 
mony by Bey; 4) he failed to interview appellant's brother 
even though the brother's testimony would have rebutted 
Bey’s testimony; 5) he failed to examine Bey about Bey’s 
agreement with the state; 6) he failed to request an 
accomplice instruction; and 7) he failed to introduce evi- 
dence concerning fingerprints belonging to Bey which 
were found on the murder weapon. Further, appellant 
stated the following about his appellate attorney: 1) he 
failed to raise as an issue on appeal a Franklin violation 
which occurred during trial; 2) he failed to raise the issue 
that appellant’s trial counsel failed to request an accom- 
plice instruction; 3) he failed to raise the issue that Kamal 
Bey was a co-defendant and thus Bey’s testimony was 
questionable; and 4) he failed to raise the issue that there 
was prosecutorial misconduct in that the prosecutor 
failed to inform the jury that Bey was a co-defendant and 
that Bey’s fingerprints were the only fingerprints found 
on the murder weapon. 


The state opposed appellant’s petition. In its opposi- 
tion, the state claimed that appellant's allegations are 
repelled by the record. On February 11, 1993, the district 
court, without appointing counsel or conducting an evi- 
dentiary hearing, denied appellant’s petition for post- 
conviction relief. The district court concluded that appel- 
lant’s allegations are belied or repelled by the record and 


33 


that appellant was not entitled to an evidentiary hearing 
on his claims. This appeal followed. 


When a petition for post-conviction relief raises 
claims supported by specific factual allegations which, if 
true, would entitle the petitioner to relief, the petitioner is 
entitled to an evidentiary hearing unless those claims are 
repelled by the record. Hargrove v. State, 100 Nev. 498, 
686 P.2d 222 (1984). Our review of the record on appeal 
and specifically our review of the transcript of appellant's 
trial, reveals that appellant’s claims are repelled by the . 
record. The terms of the state’s agreement with Bey were 
fully disclosed to the jury. At trial, appellant’s theory of 
the case was that he accidentally shot the victim. Appel- 
lant testified that his finger was on the trigger when the 
gun fired. It is therefore irrelevant that Bey’s fingerprints 
were found on the weapon. Bey was neither an accom- 
plice nor a co-defendant. Appellant was not prejudiced 
by the lack of an accomplice testimony instruction. 
Appellant’s remaining claims are similarly repelled by 
the record. The district court properly denied appellant's 
petition for post-conviction relief. 


Having reviewed the record on appeal, and for the 
reasons set forth above, we conclude that appellant can- 
not demonstrate error in this appeal, and that briefing 
and oral argument are unwarranted. See Luckett v. War- 
den, 91 Nev. 681, 682, 541 P.2d 910, 911 (1975), cert. denied, 
423 U.S. 1077 (1976). Accordingly, we

This opposition is made and based on the attached points

ARGUMENT 


Petitioner Antonio T. Slack (hereinafter Slack) has 
filed a motion for the appointment of counsel. Slack 
claims that he has contacted an attorney who would 


62 


accept an appointment to represent him. Slack claims that 
access to the prison library is limited, that he has limited 
knowledge of the law, and that the issues that he presents 
in this case are complex and will require investigation. 
None of the representations made by Slack justify the 
appointment of counsel. 


18 U.S.C. § 3006A(a)(2)(B) gives the district court 
discretion to appoint counsel when it determines that the 
“interests of justice” require representation. Counsel must 
be appointed if an evidentiary hearing is required. Rule 8, 
28 U.S.C. foll. § 2254; Bashor v. Risley, 730 F.2d 1228 at 
1234 (9th Cir.), cert. denied, 469 U.S. 838 (1984). Otherwise, 
appointment of counsel rests with the district court's 
discretion. A district court abuses its discretion in declin- 
ing to appoint counsel if due process violations will occur 
absent representation. Bonin v. Vasquez, 999 F.2d 425, 429 
(9th Cir. 1993). 


Slack has no constitutional right to counsel in a fed- 
eral habeas corpus proceeding. See Evitts v. Lucey, 469 
U.S. 387, 105 S.Ct. 830 (1985); Knaubert v. Goldsmith, 791 
F.2d 722, 729 (9th Cir.), cert. denied, 479 U.S. 867 (1986). 


Whether or not the issues in this case will require 
investigation remains to be seen. Contrary to Slack’s 
assertion, the issues presented in this action are not com- 
plex. Slack does not aver that his limited access to the 
prison law library falls below constitutional requirements 
or that the materials contained therein fall below consti- 
tutional requirements. Slack has made no averment that 
any limitation prevented him from litigating this action. 
That Slack has limited knowledge of the law does not 
require this Court to appoint counsel for him. None of the 


63 


representations made by Slack, even if true, either singly 
or taken as a whole either justify or require this Court to 
appoint counsel for Slack. 


Based upon the foregoing, Respondents respectfully 
submit this Court should deny Slack’s motion for the 
appointment of counsel.

18. Ground Four: 


The trial court failed to properly instruct 
the jury in violation of the Fifth and Four- 
teenth Amendments to the U.S. Constitu- 
tion. 


A. The reasonable doubt instruction was uncon- 
stitutional. 

The reasonable doubt instruction was uncon- 
stitutional because it minimized the state’s burden of 
proof. The trial court gave the following jury instruction 
defining reasonable doubt: 


A reasonable doubt is one based on reason. It is 
not mere possible doubt but is such a doubt as 
would govern or control a person in the more 
weighty affairs of life. If the minds of the jurors, 


74 


after the entire comparison and consideration of 
all the evidence, are in such a condition that 
they can say they feel an abiding conviction of 
the truth of the charge, there is not a reasonable 
doubt. Doubt to be reasonable must be actual 
and substantial, nor merely possibility or spec- 
ulation. CR at Exhibit 11. 


The above instruction inflates the constitutional 
standard necessary for acquittal, and giving of this 
instruction created a reasonable likelihood that the jury 
would convict and sentence based on a lesser standard of 
proof than constitutionally required. 


The principle defect of the instruction is the sec- 
ond sentence: “reasonable doubt” is not mere possible 
doubt, but is such doubt as would govern or control a 
person in the more weighty affairs of life.” This language 
is an appropriate characterization of the degree of cer- 
tainty required to find proof beyond a reasonable doubt, 
rather than the standard of reasonable doubt itself. This 
language is also an historical anomaly; as far as can be 
determined, no other state currently uses the “govern or 
control” language in its reasonable doubt instruction, and 
the few states that previously used it have since disap- 
proved it. 


The final sentence of the instruction is also con- 
stitutionally infirm. That sentence states “doubt to be 
reasonable must be actual and substantial, not mere pos- 
sibility or speculation.” The language is functionally 
identical to language condemned by the United States 
Supreme Court and, when read in combination with the 
“govern or control” language, creates a reasonable likeli- 
hood that the jury would convict and sentence based on a 


75 


lesser standard of proof than the Constitution requires. 
This structural error is per se prejudicial and no showing 
of specific prejudice is required. 


Statement of Exhaustion: This claim was raised on 
direct appeal and in state post-conviction proceedings.

C. The malice aforethought instruction was 
unconstitutional. 


; The jury was given the following instruction on 
malice aforethought”: 


Express Malice is that deliberate intention 
unlawfully to take away the life of a fellow 
creature, which is manifested by external cir- 
cumstances capable of proof. 


Malice shall be implied when no considerable 
provocation appears, or when all the circum- 


stances of the killing show an abandoned or 
malignant heart. 


The giving of this instruction deprived Mr. Slack 
of his constitutional rights by creating a reasonable likeli- 
hood that the State would be relieved of its burden to 
prove each element of the offense beyond a reasonable 


78 


doubt. Such an instruction subverted the presumption of 
innocence afforded to accused persons and also invaded 
the truth-finding task of the jury. 


The primary failing of the “implied malice” 
instruction is that it created a mandatory presumption 
that malice shall be implied both in the absence of provo- 
cation and when the circumstances of the killing show 
“abandoned or malignant heart”. This explicit and 
unqualified command directly foreclosed independent 
jury consideration of whether the facts established “mal- 
ice aforethought” an essential element of the offenses 
with which Mr. Slack was charged. 


The instruction also poses substantial problems 
with respect to the specified “basic-facts” - lack of provo- 
cation or an “abandoned and malignant heart” - from 
which malice shall be implied. These supposed circum- 
stances are so cryptic and metaphysical that they are 
meaningless without further definition. Such language 
might also easily permit a jury to equate malignant heart 
with an evil disposition or despicable character, which 
could then lead the jury, especially in a close case, to find 
the existence of malice aforethought because it believes 
the accused to be a bad person. 


The giving of the unconstitutional “implied mal- 
ice” instruction substantially and injuriously affected the 
process to such an extent as to render Mr. Slack’s convic- 
tion and sentence fundamentally unfair. The State cannot 
show, beyond a reasonable doubt that this instruction did

A. SLACK’S THIRD AMENDED PETITION CONTAINS 


UNEXHAUSTED GROUNDS AND SHOULD BE DIS- 
MISSED. 


A federal court should not entertain a petition for 
habeas corpus relief unless the petitioner has exhausted 
available and adequate state court remedies with respect 
to each of the claims contained in the petition. Rose v. 
Lundy, 455 U.S. 509, 510 (1982); Lindquist v. Gardner, 770 
F.2d 876, 877 (9th Cir. 1985); Szeto v. Rushen, 709 F.2d 1340, 
1341 (9th Cir. 1983); Gutierrez v. Griggs, 695 F.2d 1195, 
1197 (9th Cir. 1983). In this regard, state court remedies 
have not been exhausted until the petitioner unsuc- 
cessfully presents his constitutional claims to the highest 
available state court through direct appeal or state collat- 
eral review proceedings. See Preiser v. Rodriguez, 411 US. 


475, 490-94 (1973); Garrison v. McCarthy, 653 F.2d 374, 376 
(9th Cir. 1981). 


The exhaustion doctrine is satisfied “if a petitioner 
presents his claim to the highest state court and that court 
disposes of the claim on the merits... . ” Hayes v. 
Kincheloe, 784 F.2d 1434, 1437 (9th Cir. 1986). The exhaus- 
tion requirement is also satisfied if a federal habeas peti- 
tioner demonstrates at the time he files his federal 
petition that “no state remedies are still available to the 
petitioner and the petitioner had not deliberately by- 
passed the state remedies.” Bronson v. Swinney, 648 
F. Supp. 1094, 1101 (D.Nev. 1986), quoting Batchelor v. 
Cupp, 693 F.2d 859, 862 (9th Cir. 1982), cert. denied, 463 
U.S. 1212 (1983). 


100 


Federal statutory law provides in relevant part that: 


An application for a writ of habeas corpus 
in behalf of a person in custody pursuant to the 
judgment of a State court shall not be granted 
unless it appears that the applicant has 
exhausted the remedies available in the courts 
of the State, or that there is either an absence of 
available State corrective process or the exis- 
tence of circumstances rendering such process 
ineffective to protect the rights of the prisoner. 
.. . An applicant shall not be deemed to have 
exhausted the remedies available in the courts 
of the State, within the meaning of this section, 
if he has the right under the law of the State to 
raise, by any available procedure, the question 
presented. 28 U.S.C. § 2254(b) & (c). 


This exhaustion requirement is a matter of comity 
designed to afford state courts the first opportunity to 
remedy a constitutional violation. Sweet v. Cupp, 640 F.2d 
233, 236 (9th Cir. 1981). Even a petition containing both 
exhausted and unexhausted claims must be dismissed 
“leaving the prisoner with the choice of returning to state 
court to exhaust his claims or of amending or resubmit- 
ting the habeas petition so as to present only exhausted 
claims to the district court.” Rose v. Lundy, 455 U.S. at 510. 
Both the federal district court and court of appeals may 
examine the issue of exhaustion sua sponte. Batchelor v. 
Cupp, 693 F.2d at 862, citing Campbell v. Crist, 647 F.2d 956, 
957 (9th Cir. 1981). 


The United States Supreme Court has held that, in 
order for a federal claim to be exhausted, the state courts 
must be alerted to the fact that the state prisoner is 


101 


raising claims under the United States Constitution. Dun- 
can v. Henry, 115 S.Ct. 887 (1995). The Ninth Circuit Court 
of Appeals has stated that q claim is “fairly presented” if 
the petitioner has described the operative facts and legal 
theory upon which his claim is based. Picard v. Connor, 
404 U.S. 270, 277-78 (1971). See also Pappageorge v. Sumner, 
688 F.2d 1294, 1295 (9th Cir. 1982), cert. denied, 459 US. 
1219 (1983). 


Further, the United [sic] Supreme Court has held that 
raising a claim for the first and only time in a procedural 
context in which its merits will not be considered, 
absence special circumstances, does not constitute fair 
presentation for exhaustion purposes. Castille v. Peoples, 
489 U.S. 346, 350, 109 S.Ct. 1056, 1060 (1989); See also, 
Roettgen v. Copeland, 33 F.3d 36 (9th Cir. 1994). (Claim was 
unexhausted when presented to State’s highest court in a 
procedural context in which its merits will not be consid- 
ered absent special circumstances). 


Additionally, the United States Supreme Court has 
adopted the principle of complete exhaustion and deter- 
mined that mixed petitions, those petitions containing 
both exhausted and unexhausted claims, must be dis- 
missed. Rose v. Lundy, 455 U.S. 509, 522 (1982).

9(b), of the Rules Governing Section 2254 Cases in the

The federal rules regarding habeas petitions state 
that a “second or successive petition may be dismissed if 
the judge finds that it fails to allege new or different 
grounds for relief and the prior determination was on the 
merits or, if new and different grounds are alleged, the 
judge finds that the failure of the petitioner to assert 
those grounds in a prior petition constituted an abuse of 
the writ.” Rules Governing Section 2254 Cases, Rule 9(b), 
28 U.S.C. foll. § 2254. Thus, the rule covers those situa- 
tions in which a claim previously addressed is again 
raised and those situations in which new claims are 
raised which were not raised in the first petition. 


The most recent and thorough case dealing with the 
issue of successive petitions and abuse of the writ is 
McCleskey v. Zant, 111 S.Ct. 1454 (1991). Although 
McCleskey primarily deals with the issue of good cause, 
the case contains a fairly thorough discussion and history 
on abuse of the writ. On a Rule 9(b) issue, it is the 
government’s burden to plead abuse of the writ. 
McCleskey at 1470; Sanders v. United States, 373 U.S. 1, 10 
(1963). A government meets its burden of pleading if it 
notes petitioner’s prior writ history, indicates the claims 
appearing for the first time in a successive petition and 
affirms its belief that petitioner is abusing the writ in the 
manner prescribed by Rule 9(b). McCleskey at 1470. Abuse 
of the 'vrit may be raised by the court sua sponte. Jones v. 
Estelle, 722 F.2d 159, 164 (5th Cir. 1983). 


107 


Once abuse of the writ has been alleged, petitioner 
has the burden of proving that he has not abused the 
writ. McCleskey at 1470; Price v. Johnston, 334 U.S. 266, 292 
(1948). He must demonstrate “cause” and “prejudice” as 
those terms are understood in the context of procedural 
bar, for not presenting the claim in a prior petition. The 
“cause” element may be excused only if the failure of the 
federal court to address the claim would constitute a 
“fundamental miscarriage of justice,” i.e., the claim 


would establish the petitioner’s factual innocence. 
McCleskey at 1470-71. 


“Cause” in this context means petitioner could not 
have presented the claim in a previous petition because 
“some objective factor external to the defense impeded 
counsel’s efforts” to raise the claim. McCleskey at 1470. 
For cause to exist, the external impediment must have 
actually prevented petitioner from raising the claim. 
McCleskey at 1470, 1472. Once cause has been established, 
petitioner must show “actual Prejudice” resulting from 
the errors of which he complains. McCleskey at 1470. 


To establish “actual prejudice” a habeas petitioner 
bears the burden of showing not merely that the errors 
created a possibility of Prejudice, but that they work to 
his actual and substantial disadvantage, “infecting his 
entire trial with errors of constitutional dimension.” Such 
a showing of pervasive actual prejudice can hardly be 
thought to constitute anything other than a showing that 
the petitioner was denied “fundamental fairness” at trial. 
Murray v. Carrier, 477 US. 478, 494 (1986); United States v. 
Frady, 456 U.S. 152, 168, 170 (1982); White v. Lewis, 874 
F.2d 599, 603 (9th Cir. 1989). 


108 


Petitioner's opportunity to meet the burden of cause 
and prejudice will not include an evidentiary hearing if the 
district court determines as a matter of law that petitioner 
cannot satisfy the standard of cause and prejudice. 


McCleskey at 1470. 


The doctrines of procedural default and abuse of the 
writ implicate nearly identical concerns flowing from the 
significant costs of federal habeas corpus review. To begin 
with, the writ strikes at finality. One of the law’s very 
objects is the finality of its judgments. When a habeas 
petitioner succeeds in obtaining a new trial the “erosion 
of memory and dispersion of witnesses that occur with 
the passage of time” prejudice the government diminish- 
ing the chances of a reliable criminal adjudication. 
McCleskey at 1468. Finality has special importance in the 
context of the federal attack on a state conviction. Reex- 
amination of state convictions on federal habeas frus- 
trates both the State’s sovereign power to punish 
offenders and their good faith attempts to honor constitu- 
tional rights. McCleskey at 1469. The federal system recog- 
nizes the independent power of a state to articulate 
societal norms with criminal laws, but the power of a 
State to pass law means little if the state cannot enforce 


them. McCleskey at 1469. 


Federal collateral litigation places a heavy burden on 
scarce federal judicial resources and threatens the capac- 
ity of the system to resolve primary disputes. Habeas 
review may give litigants incentives to withhold claims 
for manipulative purposes and may establish disincen- 
tives to present claims when evidence is fresh. McCleskey 
at 1469; Reed v. Ross, 468 U.S. 1, 13 (1984). 


109 


Far more severe are the disruptions attached to a 
successive petition. Perpetual disrespect for the finality of 
convictions disparages the entire criminal justice system. 
McCleskey at 1469. There comes a point where a pro- 
cedural system which leaves matters perpetually open no 
longer reflects humane concern but merely anxiety and a 
desire for immobility. McCleskey at 1469. 


If first round habeas stretches resources, examination 
of successor petitions spreads the resources thinner still. 
Likewise, if comity and federalism are offended by a first 
round habeas, the offense increases when a State must 
defend its conviction in a second or subsequent federal 
proceeding on grounds not even raised in the first peti- 
tion. McCleskey at 1469. 


However, the above considerations give way when a 
meritorious constitutional claim is presented in a proper 
manner in a federal petition. The abuse of the writ rules 
provide a forum for presentation of meritorious claims 
and at the same time serve to vindicate the State’s interest 
in finality of judgments. Accordingly, for a second or 
successive petition, the petitioner must demonstrate 
cause and prejudice to raise a new issue in such a peti- 
tion. McCleskey at 1469-70. 


The question to be answered in determining whether 
a habeas petitioner has abused the writ by failing to raise 
a claim in a prior habeas petition is whether the claim 
was withheld without legal excuse. Harris v. Pulley, 885 
F.2d 1354, 1369 (9th Cir. 1988). 


The abuse of the writ doctrine examines petitioner's 
conduct: the question is whether petitioner possessed, or 
by reasonable means could have obtained, a sufficient basis to 


110 


allege a claim in the first petition and pursue the matter 
in habeas corpus. McCleskey at 1472. The requirement of 
cause in the abuse of the writ context is based on the 
principle that petitioner must conduct a reasonable and 
diligent investigation aimed at including all relevant 
claims and grounds in a first federal habeas petition. If 
what petitioner knows or could discover upon reasonable 
investigation supports a claim for belief, what he does not 
know is irrelevant. Omission of the claim will not be 
excused merely because evidence discovered later might 
also have supported or strengthened the claim. McCleskey 
at 1472. 


In Delo v. Stokes, 110 S.Ct. 1880 (1990) (per curiam), 
the United States Supreme Court vacated a stay of execu- 
tion and expressly held it was an abuse of the writ for 
Stokes to raise a claim which “could have been raised in 
his first petition for federal habeas corpus.” Id. at 329. 
Thus, the Supreme Court has made it clear that presenta- 
tion of any claim which could have been presented in an 
earlier habeas petition constitutes an abuse of the writ.

1. The legal bases for petitioner's position. 


A federal court will not grant a state prisoner's peti- 
tion for relief until the prisoner has exhausted his avail- 
able state remedies for all claims raised. Rose v. Lundy, 455 
U.S. 509 (1982); 28 U.S.C. § 2254(b). As a general matter, 
state remedies have not been exhausted unless the claim 
has been fairly presented to the state courts and the 
highest state court has disposed of the claim on the 
merits. Carothers v. Rhay, 594 F.2d 225, 228 (9th Cir. 1979). 
Although a state prisoner seeking federal habeas relief 
must first exhaust the remedies available in the courts of 
the State. “It is the legal issues that are to be exhausted, not 
the petitioner.” Burkett v. Cunningham, 826 F.2d 1208, 1218 


115 


(3d Cir. 1987) (emphasis added) (quoting U.S. ex rel. 
Geisler v. Walters, 510 F.2d 887, 893 (3d Cir. 1975)). In the 
instant case, Slack has raised every claim in state court and 
has pursued every possible remedy; respondent's argu- 
ments in each case relate to the technical specifics con- 
cerning underlying facts or legal theories. 


The exhaustion requirement stems from the doctrine 
of comity which “teaches that one court should defer 
action on causes properly within its jurisdiction until the 
courts of another sovereignty with concurrent powers, 
and already cognizant of the litigation, have had an 
opportunity to pass upon the matter.” Carter v. Estelle, 677 
F.2d 427, 441 (5th Cir. 1982), reh'g denied 691 F.2d 777, cert. 
denied 460 U.S. 1056, 103 S.Ct. 1508 (1983) (citing Darr v. 
Burford, 339 U.S. 200, 204, 70 S.Ct. 587, 590, 94 L.Ed. 761 
(1950)). Thus, exhaustion requires only that the petitioner 
fairly present his federal claims to the State's highest 
court “either on direct review of the conviction or in a 
post-conviction attack” before a federal court may con- 
sider those claims. Id. at 443 (citations omitted). 


The Court in Castille v. Peoples, 489 U.S. 346, 109 S.Ct. 
1056, reh’g denied 490 U.S. 1076, 109 S.Ct. 2091 (1989) 
expressly made clear that, where further state proceed- 
ings are unavailable or would be useless, the exhaustion 
requirement is excused. Castille, 489 US. at 351; accord 
Carter, 691 F.2d at 445 (“[iJf state procedures do not 
Provide a bona fide forum for a petitioner's constitutional 
claims or merely delay and hinder ultimate resolution 
the foundations upon which exhaustion doctrine rests ore 
dissolved”). “Thus, in determining whether a remedy for 
4 particular constitutional claim is ‘available,’ the federal 


116 


courts are authorized, indeed required, to assess the like- 
lihood that a state court will accord the habeas petitioner 
a hearing on the merits of his claim.” Harris v. Reed, 489 
U.S. 255, 109 S.Ct. 1038, 1046, 103 L.2d.2d 308 (1989) 
(O’Connor, J. concurring, joined by The Chief Justice, and 
Scalia, J.) “The word “available” as used in 28 U.S.C. 
§ 2254(b) (requiring a petitioner first to exhaust available 
remedies in state court unless there exists an absence of 
corrective processes or ‘circumstances rendering such 
processes ineffective to protect the rights of the prisoner’) 
does not refer to an avenue that exists on a map but is closed 
permanently to the kind of vehicle being driven by the defen- 
dant. It does not include a door that has been forever sealed.” 
Russell v. Rolfs, 893 F.2d 1033, 1037-38 (9th Cir. 1989) 
(emphasis added). Although respondents contend that 
Slack has not exhausted his claims, they have failed to 
identify the available state remedies that would permit 
him to do so. 


Further, a habeas petitioner may reformulate claims 
already raised in state court; the test for exhaustion is

presented to the state court. Tamapua v. Shimoda, 796 F.2d 
261, 262 (9th Cir. 1986) (citing Picard v. Connor, 404 U.S. 
270, 278 (1971)). Allegations which merely lend specificity

record did not circumvent exhaustion requirement where

F.2d 1486, 1491 (8th Cir.), cert. denied, 113 S.Ct. 2950 (1993) 
(petitioner fairly presented claim to state court by refer-

B. PETITIONER HAS NOT ABUSED THE WRIT 


The amended petition does not violate Rule 9 of the 
rules governing § 2254 cases and does not constitute 
abuse of the writ. In Farmer v. Angelone, 98 F.3d 1548 (9th 
Cir 1996), the Ninth Circuit held that Rule 9(b) recognizes 
two types of abuse of the writ. One type of abuse occurs 
when a new petition - raising either new claims or claims 
previously raised — is filed after a previous petition has 
been addressed on the merits. This type of abuse may 
conveniently be termed McCleskey abuse: the Supreme 
Court, in McCleskey v. Zant, 499 U.S. 467 (1991), imposed 
the rule that a prima facie case of abuse of the writ was 
established as a matter of law when a new petition was 
filed after a previous petition had been adjudicated on 
the merits, and that, when those facts were pleaded, the 
burden shifted to the petitioner to establish an exception 
~ cause and prejudice or a miscarriage of justice — to the 
abuse doctrine. Id. at 477. 


124 


The Supreme Court relied upon the costs associated 
with a second reexamination of the validity of a state 
conviction by a federal court, after a claim has been 
“defaulted in the first round of federal habeas,” 
McCleskey, 499 U.S. at 490-492, 498, in imposing the strin- 
gent rules that actual knowledge of a claim at the time of 
the first petition is not necessary to a finding of default, 
when there has been a prior proceeding on the merits. 


The second type of abuse may arise under the cir- 
cumstances suggested by Sanders v. United States (upon 
which Rule 9(b) is based), and by former 28 U.S.C. 
§ 2244(b). This type of abuse requires a showing that a 
new claim in a petition was “deliberately withheld” from 
a previous petition, former 28 U.S.C. § 2244(b); Sanders, 
373 U.S. at 18, or that the petitioner “deliberately aban- 
don{ed]” the claim in a hearing on a previous petition, id., 
citing Wong Doo v. United States, 265 U.S. 239, 241 (1924); 
or that the “only purpose” of the petition is “to vex, 
harass or delay,” Sanders, 373 U.S. at 18; or that the 
petitioner is deliberately engaging in needless piecemeal 
litigation. Sanders, 373 U.S. at 18. 


The Farmer decision explicitly acknowledged the con- 
tinuing validity of these standards for non-McCleskey 
abuse: 


“A federal court need not consider habeas 
claims previously unlitigated in federal court if 
it determines that the petitioner (1) made a con- 
scious decision deliberately to withhold them 
from a prior petition, (2) is pursuing needless 
piecemeal litigation, or (3) has raised the claims 
only to vex, harass, or delay. Where there is no 


125 


affirmative indication in the record that the peti- 
tioner made a conscious decision deliberately to 
withhold his claim, proceed by piecemeal litiga- 
tion, to vex or harass or annoy the court or the 
state, a second petition raising a new claim is 
not an abuse of the writ. 


The state has presented no evidence that 
any of the factors outlined in Sanders are pre- 
sent. Moreover, there is nothing in the history of 
this case to indicate that Hamilton deliberately 
withheld his claim in the hope of being granted 
two hearings instead of one.” 


Farmer, 98 F.3d at 1559, quoting Hamilton v. Vasquez, 882 
F.2d 1469, 1473 (9th Cir. 1989) (citations omitted). 


This analysis of Sanders abuse is the only one that fits 
into the text and structure of the applicable statutes and 
rules. The Farmer decision emphasized the language of 
Rule 9(b) in concluding that abuse of the writ could be 
found, even if no prior proceeding on the merits of a 
previous petition had been conducted, relying on the 
language “[a] second or successive petition may be dis- 
missed . . . if new and different grounds are alleged, the 
judge finds that the failure of the petitioner to assert 
those grounds in a prior petition constituted an abuse of 
the writ.” Farmer, 98 F.3d at 1555. The Advisory Commit- 
tee Notes (1976) to Rule 9(b), quoted in Farmer, explicitly 
adopt the Sanders analysis on the standard of abuse under 
this theory: 


With reference to a successive application 
asserting a new ground or one not previously 
decided on the merits, the court in Sanders 
noted: 


126 


In either case, full consideration of the merits of 
the new application can be avoided [only] if 
there has been an abuse of the writ * * * and this 
the Government has the burden of plead- 


ing. * . * 


Thus, for example, if a prisoner deliberately 
withholds one of two grounds for federal collat- 
eral relief at the time of filing his first applica- 
tion, * * * he may be deemed to have waived his 


right to a hearing on a second application pre- 
senting the withheld ground. 


373 U.S. at 17-18 [83 S.Ct. at 1078] 


Farmer, 98 F.3d at 1555-1556. The Notes conclude that 
Rule 9b) will not “eliminate a remedy to which the 
petitioner is rightfully entitled,” and emphasize the lan- 
guage of Sanders holding that: 


Nothing in the traditions of habeas corpus 
requires the federal courts to tolerate needless 
piecemeal litigation, or to entertain collateral 
proceedings whose only purpose is to vex, 
harass, or delay. 


373 U.S. at 19 [83 S.Ct. at 1078]. 
Farmer, 98 F.3d at 1556. 


Thus the non-McCleskey prong of Rule 9(b) incorpo- 
rates the Sanders standards of abuse, as the Advisory 
Committee Notes clearly indicate. This conclusion is also 
the only one consistent with the settled principle of statu- 
tory construction that an enactment which employees a 
term of art (like “abuse of the writ”) has been judicially 
defined is presumed to have incorporated the judicial 
interpretation of the term. E.g., McDermott Intern., Inc. v. 


127 


Wilander, 498 U.S. 337, 342 (1991); Davis v. Michigan Dept. 
of Treasury, 489 U.S. 803, 813 (1989). 


Abuse of the writ is a defense which must be pleaded 
and proved by the state. McCleskey, 499 U.S. at 477; United 
States v. Kleinbart, 27 F.3d 586, 593 n.5 (D.C. Cir.), cert. 
denied 115 S.Ct. 456 (1994); see, Farmer, 98 F.3d at 1559. As 
with any other affirmative defense, the facts establishing 
the defense must be pleaded with specificity. Fed. R. Civ. 
P., Rules 8(b, c), 8(e)(1), 12(b); see, e.g., Heller Financial, Inc. 
v. Midwhey Power Co., Inc., 883 F.2d 1286, 1294-1295 (7th 
Cir. 1989) (rules of pleading require “ ‘short and plain 
Statement’ of the defense [citation],” and “bare bones 
conclusory allegations” of legal conclusions insufficient). 


In the case of McCleskey abuse, the requirement of 
specifically pleading the facts supporting the defense is 
simply met: all the state needs to allege is the petitioner's 
“writ history,” McCleskey, 499 U.S. at 494: and if there has 
been an adjudication on the merits of a previous petition, 
the current petition (whether it raises the same claims or 
new claims) is abusive as a matter of law and the burden 
shifts to the petitioner to allege that the abuse is excused. 


Non-McCleskey abuse, however, presents quite a dif- 
ferent pleading situation. Unlike McCleskey abuse, there 
can be no easy recitation of a prior adjudication on the 
merits to establish abuse as a matter of law. Rather, the 
substantive standard of Sanders abuse requires a showing 
that the petitioner deliberately withheld a claim in pre- 
vious litigation, or that the petitioner is engaging in a 
Pattern of needless piecemeal litigation, or is filing the 
petition solely to “vex, harass, or delay” the opposing 


128 


party. See Hamilton v. Vasquez, supra, 882 F.2d at 1473. This 
pleading burden is not onerous where actual abuse exists. 


For instance, in McCleskey itself, the petitioner had 
raised a claim in state collateral proceedings, thus clearly 
indicating the fact that the claim was known to the peti- 
tioner. In his first federal petition, petitioner did not raise 
that claim, but after the first petition was adjudicated he 
tried to raise it in a second petition. McCleskey, 499 U.S. at 
475-476. Under the Farmer analysis, those facts could be 
alleged as showing abuse by the deliberate withholding 
of the claim and needless piecemeal litigation: if the claim 
was unexhausted when it was introduced in the new 
petition, and it had been deliberately withheld in a first 
petition that was dismissed for exhaustion in order to 
induce a second round of exhaustion, an inference could 
arise that abuse was shown. See also Wong Doo v. United 
States, 265 U.S. at 240-241 (claim raised but not litigated 
in first federal petition raised in second federal petition); 
cf. Price v. Johnson, 334 U.S. 266, 291-292 (1948) (no abuse 
where petitioner ignorant of facts supporting claim or 
unaware of legal significance of relevant facts). Similarly, 
if the only unexhausted claims included in a petition are 
patently frivolous, the state could allege that an inference 
should arise that the purpose of including the unex- 
hausted claims in the petition was solely to “mix” the 
petition and to induce the necessity of exhaustion pro- 
ceedings. Thus the state could properly allege that the 
sole purpose of asserting the claims was “to vex or harass 
or annoy the court or the state,” Farmer, 98 F.3d at 1559, 
quoting Hamilton v. Vasquez, 882 F.2d at 1473, and thus 
that abusive conduct was shown. 


129 


By contrast, pleading simply the fact that a habeas 
corpus petition contains a new claim which was not 
raised in a previous petition which was dismissed “with- 
out prejudice” and was not adjudicated on the merits 
does not in any way establish that the petitioner was 
acting with an improper purpose, by deliberately with- 
holding the claim in the previous proceeding, nor does it 
otherwise suggest the existence of a subjective intent to 
“vex, harass, or delay.” See Hamilton v. Vasquez, supra, 882 
F.2d at 1473. To the contrary, the addition of new claims 
as the result of the current federal proceedings will nor-

The Respondent's contention that the petitioner has

for a Writ of Habeas Corpus pursuant to 28 U.S.C. § 2254 
attacking the validity of his conviction. Slack asserts 
seven grounds for relief: (1) insufficient evidence was 
adduced at trial to support his conviction; (2) he had 
insufficient notice of his charges since he was charged

the jury was prejudiced by evidence of sexual miscon- 
duct; (4) the trial court failed to properly instruct the jury;

Slack previously filed a petition for writ of habeas 
corpus, case number CV-N-91-561-HDM, in which he 
alleged: (1) insufficient evidence was adduced at trial to 
support his conviction; (2) the trial court improperly 
allowed prejudicial evidence of sexual misconduct; (3) the

and (4) the trial court failed to adequately define the

State Prisons, CV-N-91-561-HDM. In that action, Slack 
filed a motion to stay the proceedings so he could exhaust 
the following issues before continuing with his federal 
habeas petition: ( 1) ineffective assistance of counsel dur-

in allowing co-defendant to testify prior to state making a

ecutor failed to instruct jury that only fingerprints on the 
murder weapon were from co-defendant. The court dis-

Respondents have moved to dismiss the instant peti- 
tion alleging abuse of the writ (#67). Rule 9(b) of the 
Rules Governing Section 2254 Cases in the United States 
District Courts provides that: 


A second or successive petition may be dismiss- 
ed... if new and different grounds are alleged 
[and] the judge finds that the failure of the 
petitioner to assert those grounds in a prior 
petition constituted an abuse of the writ. 


In the instant petition, grounds two, four (C), five, six 
and seven were not raised in Slack’s previous petition. 
Therefore, the issue before the court is whether Slack’s 
failure to assert the new ground in his first petition 
constitutes an abuse of the writ. 


142 


In McCleskey v. Zant, 499 U.S. 467 (1991), the U.S. 
Supreme Court defined the respective burdens of plead- 
ing abuse of the writ. To meet their burden, the state must 
with clarity and particularity note the prior writ history, 
identify the claims that appear for the first time in the 
current petition, and allege that petitioner has abused the 
writ. If the state meets their burden, then petitioner must 
show (1) cause for not raising the claim the previous 
petition, e.g., that he was impeded by some objective 
factor external to the defense, such as governmental 
interference or the reasonable unavailability of the factual 
basis for the claim, and (2) actual prejudice resulting from 
the errors about which he complains. Bonin v. Calderon, 77 
F.3d 1155, 1158-59 (9th Cir. 1996); Moran v. McDaniel, 80 
F.3d 1261, 1270 (9th Cir. 1996). 


“Abuse of the writ doctrine examines petitioner's con- 
duct: the question is whether petitioner possessed, or by 
reasonable means could have obtained, a sufficient basis 
to allege a claim in the first petition. .. . ” McClesky, 499 
U.S. at 498. An inmate’s pro se status does not constitute 
“an objective external factor” which excuses a peti- 
tioner’s failure to raise his new claim in prior habeas 
petitions. Saahir v. Collins, 956 F.2d 115, 118 (5th Cir.1992); 
Hughes v. Idaho State Board, 800 F.2d 905, 909 (9th 
Cir.1986). An inmate’s pro se status does not excuse an 
inmate’s ignorance of the law nor establish cause for the 
purpose of determining whether an inmate abused the 
writ by failing to raise new claims in prior habeas peti- 
tions: there is no constitutional right to counsel in post- 
conviction proceedings. Saahir, 956 F.2d at 118. To show 
actual prejudice, “petitioner bears ‘the burden of showing 


143 


not merely that the errors at his trial constituted a Possi- 
bility of prejudice, but that they worked to his actual and 
substantial disadvantage, infecting his entire trial with 
errors of constitutional dimension.’ ” White v. Lewis, 874 
F.2d 599, 603 (9th Cir. 1989) (quoting United States v. F rady, 
456 U.S. 152, 170 (1982)). 


As an alternative to showing “cause and prejudice,” 
Slack may attempt to show that dismissal of his instant 
claim on abuse of the writ grounds will result in a “fun- 
damental miscarriage of justice.” Such a showing, how- 
ever, is very difficult to make. It requires Slack to 
demonstrate that he is “actually innocent” of the crime 
with which he was convicted. See also Smith v. Murray, 477 
U.S. 527, 537 (1986). 


Respondents have met their burden in alleging that 
Slack has abused the writ. Slack must now establish cause 
and prejudice for his failure to assert the instant claim in 
his first petition or he must demonstrate that a funda- 
mental miscarriage of justice will result if the court dis- 
misses the case for abuse of the writ. Given the extreme 
difficulty of showing a “fundamentally unjust incarcera- 
tion,” this court recommends Slack disregard this alterna- 
tive test and attempt to show cause and prejudice.

I. INTRODUCTION 


The court has entered an order determining that 
Respondents have met their burden in alleging that peti- 
tioner has abused the writ with regard to grounds two, 
four(C), five and seven of the instant amended petition. 
On the basis of this determination, the court has ordered 
petitioner to file points and authorities addressing 
whether he can overcome the abuse of the writ bar. This 
motion for reconsideration follows. The court’s order is 
premised upon an error of law which requires correction 
and reconsideration. 


A. The state has not alleged facts sufficient to estab- 
lish that an abuse of the writ has occurred. 


The state argues that Rule 9(b) of the Rules Govern- 
ing Section 2254 cases requires that the grounds asserted 
in the amended petition which are unexhausted or were 
not included in the previous federal petitions should be 
dismissed under the abuse of the writ doctrine. The 
state’s pleading does not furnish any ground for dismiss- 
ing any part of the amended petition. 


147 


B. The amended petition is not a “second or succes- 
sive” one under Rule 9(b). 


Initially, in order to preserve the question for review, 
petitioner submits that Rule 9(b) does not apply to his 
case at all, because the amended petition before the court 
is not a “second or successive” one within the meaning of 
the rule, since the previous federai petition was dis- 
missed without prejudice for exhaustion. Petitioner rec- 
ognizes that the Court of Appeals has adopted a contrary 
position. Farmer v. McDaniel, 98 F.3d at 1556-1557. Every 
other Court of Appeals that has addressed this issue, 
however, has held that a petition is not subject to Rule 
9(b) as a “second or successive” one if previous petitions 
have been dismissed without prejudice for exhaustion. 
McWilliams v. State of Colorado, 121 F.3d 573, 575 (10th Cir. 
1997); Benton v. Washington, 106 F.3d 162, 164 (7th Cir. 
1996); Camarano v. Irvin, 98 F.3d 44, 46-47 (3d Cir. 1996); In 
re Gasery, 116 F.3d 1051, 1052 (Sth Cir. 1997); Woods v. 
Whitley, 933 F.2d 321, 322 (5th Cir. 1991); Jones v. Estelle, 
722 F.2d 159, 168 (5th Cir. 1983) (en banc) cert. denied, 466 
U.S. 976 (1984); Dickson v. Maine, 101 F.3d 791, 791 (1st 
Cir. 1996) (per curiam). 


C. The A.E.D.P.A. has superseded Rule 9(b) of the 
Rules governing § 2254 cases. 


This case is governed by the A.E.D.P.A. because it 
was commenced after April 25, 1996. In Farmer the Ninth 
Circuit held that Rule 9(b) applies, in limited part, to 
§2254 actions that had previously been dismissed without 
Prejudice. The A.E.D.P.A. provides that a successor peti- 
tion cannot be filed with the district court unless a panel 


148 


of the Court of Appeals authorizes such an action. 28 
U.S.C. §2244(b)(3). In a case following the enactment of 
the A.E.D.P.A. the Ninth Circuit held that previous peti- 
tions which have been dismissed without prejudice on 
exhaustion grounds do not trigger §2244(b)(3). In re 
Turner, 101 F.3d 1323 (9th Cir. 1997). In other words, a 
dismissal without prejudice does not make the next §2254 
action a successor petition. 


The Court of Appeals for this circuit has created an 
intra-circuit conflict with Farmer by holding that a “sec- 
ond or successive” petition under the Anti-Terrorism and 
Effective Death Penalty Act of 1996 (AEDPA) - which 
uses the identical language in 28 U.S.C. §2244(b) that is 
used in, and was adopted from, Rule 9(b) — does not refer 
to a petition filed after a previous petition has been 
dismissed without prejudice for purposes of exhaustion. 
In re Turner, supra at p. 323. In order to preserve this issue 
for review, see Sup. Ct. Rule 10.1(a) (conflicting decisions 
of Court of Appeals as basis for review on certiorari); 
Fed. R. App. P., Rule 35(a)(1) (securing uniformity of 
decision as ground for hearing en banc); petitioner sub- 
mits that no part of the instant amended petition can be 
subject to dismissal under Rule 9(b). 


This position is also supported by the decision of the 
Court of Appeals in Calderon v. United States District Court 
(Taylor) F.3d __, 1998 WL 24138 (9th Cir. January 28, 


a ee 


1998), in which the court held that a district court has 
discretion to hold the exhausted part of a habeas petition 
in abeyance while dismissing the unexhausted claims for 
exhaustion. An amended petition, adding the claims fol- 
lowing exhaustion, could not rationally be considered a 
“second or successive” one under Rule 9(b) because there 


149 


would be no difference in substance between that situa- 
tion and the one presented here where the previous peti- 
tion was dismissed without prejudice in its entirety. 


The Turner decision makes the jurisprudence in this 
circuit on this issue fundamentally incoherent. It is clear 
that the AEDPA was intended to, and did, impose restric- 
tions on the filing of “second or successive” habeas 
corpus petitions in 28 U.S.C. §2244(b) that are more strin- 
gent than the preexisting caselaw on abuse of the writ. 
E.g., Felker v. Turpin, __ U.S. __, 116 S.Ct. 2333, 2340 
(1996); cf. Schlup v. Delo, 513 U.S. 298 (1995); McCleskey v. 
Zant, 499 U.S. 467 (1991). Under Turner, however, it does 
not matter how many previous federal petitions have 
been dismissed for exhaustion: a new petition under the 
AEDPA is not a “second or successive” one under 28 
U.S.C. §2244(b). Turner and Farmer can be harmonized if 
this court concludes that the Farmer decision is limited to 
cases pre-dating the enactment of the A.E.D.P.A. A con- 
trary conclusion ignores the holding in Turner a case more 
applicable to the instant action because it was controlled 
by the A.E.D.P.A. 


D. There is no basis for finding abuse as a result of 
presentation of unexhausted claims. 


As shown above, the amended petition before this 
court is fully exhausted. As a result, there is no basis for 
dismissal, with or without prejudice. The basis for the 
theory of abuse of the writ in the non-McCleskey context 
~ that is, when there has been no “first round of federal 


150 


habeas,” McCleskey v. Zant, 499 U.S. at 490 - is (as origi- 
nally articulated by the state in this case) that the peti- 
tioner is attempting to evade resolution of his 
constitutional claims by repeatedly filing mixed petitions, 
thus avoiding a decision on the merits due to the neces- 
sity of dismissal for exhaustion. Petitioner here was not, 
and has not been, seeking to evade such a resolution. 
There is accordingly no basis for the state to seek dis- 
missal either for exhaustion or on the basis of abuse. 


Petitioner submits that a hearing on that issue is 
necessary before final resolution of any question involv- 
ing petitioner’s substantial rights.

ABUSE OF THE WRIT 


A second petition may be dismissed for abuse of the 
writ if new grounds are alleged and the failure to allege 
them in the previous petition was an abuse of the writ. 
See Farmer v. McDaniel, 98 F.3d 1548, 1555 (9th Cir.1996). 
The previous petition need not have beer dismissed on 
the merits. See id. at 1557. The instant petition therefore 
may be subject to dismissal for abuse of the writ. 


In McCleskey v. Zant, 499 US. 467 (1991), the U.S. 
Supreme Court defined the respective burdens of plead- 
ing abuse of the writ. The State met that burden here by 
noting Slack’s previous writ history, identifying the 
grounds for relief that appear for the first time in the 
instant petition and alleging that Slack abused the writ. 
See id. The burden now is on Slack to disprove abuse of 
the writ by showing cause for failing to raise the new 
grounds for relief earlier and prejudice therefrom. See id. 
Slack does not meet his burden. 


| In reply to the court order instructing Slack to file 
points and authorities showing cause and prejudice to 
overcome the abuse of the writ bar, he filed a motion for 
reconsideration (#73). First the Court notes that Slack has 
incorrectly filed his motion for reconsideration pursuant 
to Fed.R.Civ.P.59. Under this rule a party may move for a 
new trial or an amendment of a judgment. Since there has 
not been a trial and a judgment has not been entered in 
this case, Slack cannot bring a motion pursuant to this 


156 


rule. Additionally even if Slack could bring motion under 
Fed.R.Civ.P.59, the motion must be brought within ten 
days after the entry of judgment. See Fed.R.Civ.P. 59(e). 
Slack’s motion was untimely since it was filed two days 
after the ten day period expired. Nevertheless, the Court 
will address Slack’s arguments in his motion for recon- 
sideration. 


In his motion for reconsideration, Slack claims the 
State has not established the habeas petition is abusive, 
the amended petition is not successive, and that the case 
is governed by the Antiterrorism and Effective Death 
Penalty Act (AEDPA). As previously noted, the State has 
met its burden under McClesky [sic] by specifying which 
grounds for relief in the amended habeas petition are 
abusive. Next, the instant petition is Slack’s second peti- 
tion and thus successive. The Ninth Circuit has held that 
a judge may find a second petition is abusive when new 
grounds are alleged and a petitioner failed to allege them 
in the previous petition, even if the previous petition was 
dismissed for failing to exhaust state remedies. See 
Farmer, 98 F.3d at 1557. Lastly, Slack wrongly asserts the 
case is governed by the AEDPA because it was com- 
menced after April 26, 1996. First, the instant case was 
commenced March 27, 1995. Second, the amendments to 
28 U.S.C. §§ 2244(a) and 2255 resulting from the AEDPA 
are not retroactive. See Lindh v. Murphy, __ U.S. __. __ 
117 S.Ct. 2059, 2063 (1997). Accordingly, the AEDPA is not 
applicable to this case. 


Thus, since Slack has failed to demonstrate cause and 
prejudice to overcome the abuse of the writ bar, the Court 
will dismiss grounds two, four(c), five, six and seven as 
abusive.

EXHAUSTION 


Although respondents argue several grounds are 
unexhausted, the court will only address ground three 
since all other grounds will be dismissed as abusive. Title 
28 U.S.C. § 2254(b) and (c) provide that a state prisoner 
must exhaust all available state court remedies before 
filing a petition for habeas corpus in federal court. The 
exhaustion of available state judicial remedies is ordi- 
narily a prerequisite to obtaining federal habeas corpus 
relief. 28 U.S.C. § 2254(b) (1988); Rose v. Lundy, 455 U.S. 
509, 515 (1982). To demonstrate compliance with the 
exhaustion requirement, a habeas applicant must show 
that the claim included in the federal petition was “fairly 
presented” to the state’s highest court, Picard v. Connor, 
404 U.S. 270, 275 (1971), or demonstrate that no state 
remedy remains available. Harmon v. Ryan, 959 F.2d 1457, 
1460 (9th Cir. 1992). A claim has been “fairly presented” 
to the state court if the petitioner has described the 
operative facts and the legal theory upon which he bases 
his constitutional claim. Anderson v. Harless, 459 U.S. 4, 6 
(1982). Furthermore, a petitioner has described the opera- 
tive facts and the legal theory upon which he bases his 
constitutional claim. Anderson v. Harless, 459 U.S. 4, 6 
(1982). Furthermore, a petitioner cannot merely allege 
exhaustion; the record must show that the petitioner 
sought review in the highest state court. Cartwright v. 
Cupp, 650 F.2d 1103, 1104 (9th Cir. 1981) (per curiam), cert. 
denied, 455 U.S. 1023 (1982). In addition, the habeas peti- 
tioner must exhaust his state remedies as to each of his 
federal claims. If a habeas corpus petition contains both 
exhausted and non-exhausted claims, the petition must 
be dismissed. Rose, 455 U.S. at 520. 


158 


In ground three, Slack claims his Fourteenth and 
Fifth Amendment right to due process was violated when 
the jury was prejudiced by evidence of sexual miscon- 
duct. Respondents contend the claim is unexhausted 
because although Slack presented the facts of this claim 
to the Nevada Supreme Court, he did not claim his Four- 
teenth and Fifth Amendment rights were violated. If a 
petitioner fails to alert the state court to the fact that he is 
raising a federal constitutional claim, his federal claim is 
unexhausted regardless of its similarity to the issues 
raised in state court. Duncan v. Henry, 513 U.S. 364, 366 
(1995); Johnson v. Zenon, 88 F.3d 828, 830 (1996); see also 
Ybarra v. Sumner, 678 F.Supp. 1480, 1481 (D.Nev. 1988) 
(citing Picard, 404 U.S. at 276). In his opening brief to the 
Nevada Supreme Court on direct appeal, Slack never 
argued his Fifth or Fourteenth Amendment right to due 
process was violated when prejudicial evidence was pre- 
sented to the jury. In his opening brief, Slack relied only 
upon state law to support his claim. See #16, ex.20, pp. 
15-18. Thus, the Court finds ground three is unexhausted. 


CONCLUSION 


Since ground three of Slack’s third amended habeas 
petition is unexhausted, this action will be dismissed to 
allow Slack the opportunity to present the unexhausted 
claim to the state court. If Slack wishes to continue with 
this proceeding instead of returning to state court, he will 
need to re-open this proceeding and abandon the unex- 
hausted claim as described below. See Reutter v. Crande, 
109 F.3d 575 (9th Cir. 1997). Alternatively, Slack may 
immediately commence a new federal action in which 
this court could review the merits of the unexhausted 


159 


claim because the new action would be subject to the 
statutory changes effected by the Antiterrorism and 
Effective Death Penalty Act (AEDPA);* however, relief 
could not be granted based on any unexhausted claim. 


Pursuant to the AEDPA, the dismissal of this action 
will trigger the commencement of the one year statute of 
limitations period for Slack to return to federal court with 
a new action. 28 U.S.C. § 2244(d)(1). The time during 
which Slack’s post conviction proceeding is pending in 
state court will not count toward the one year period. 28 
U.S.C. § 2244(d)(2). Therefore, if Slack waits three months 
after this dismissal to file a state post conviction petition, 
he will have only nine months from the disposition of 
that state proceeding in which to commence a new fed- 
eral proceeding. The failure to timely commence a new 
federal proceeding will bar Slack from having the validity 
of his conviction and/or sentence reviewed in federal 
court.

C. ABUSE OF THE WRIT APPLIES TO THIS CASE. 


SLACK next argues that abuse of the writ does not 
apply to his case because he has filed only one prior 
federal petition, his prior petition was litigated pro se, his 
prior petition was dismissed without prejudice within 
ninety days and finally, that SLACK was never admon-

claims could result in a bar of those claims in a later 
federal action. (Application pgs. 7-8). SLACK’s argument 
has no merit. 


189 


There is nothing in Rule 9(b) or Farmer which sup- 
ports SLACK’s contention that abuse of the writ only 
applies if there have been two or more prior petitions, the 
petitioner was represented by counsel on each of his prior 
petitions, the prior petitions resulted in a “significant 
expenditure of federal judicial resources” (Application, 
pg. 7), and the prior federal court issued a written order 
advising the petitioner that the failure to raise all of his 
claims may bar those claims in future proceedings. 


Indeed, such requirements would virtually eliminate Rule 
9(b). 


Abuse of the writ occurs when there is a successive 
petition which does not raise new claims and the former 
petition was adjudicated on the merits or when the suc- 
cessive petition does raise new claims and the failure of 
the petitioner to raise those claims previously constitutes 
abuse. Rule 9(b) of the Rules Governing Section 2254 
Cases in the United States District Court foll. 28 U.S.C. 
§ 2254; see also Farmer v. McDaniel, 98 F.3d 1548 (9th Cir. 
1996), cert. denied, 117 S.Ct. 1474 (1997). There are no 
other requirements in order for Rule 9(b) to apply. 
SLACK’s argument to the contrary has no merit. 


D. RESPONDENTS HAVE PROPERLY PLEAD 
ABUSE OF THE WRIT. 


The most recent and thorough case dealing with the 
issue of successive petitions and abuse of the writ is 
McCleskey v. Zant, 111 S.Ct. 1454 (1991). Although 
McCleskey primarily deals with the issue of good cause, 
the case contains a fairly thorough discussion and history 
on abuse of the writ. On a Rule 9(b) issue, it is the 


190 


government's burden to plead abuse of the writ. 
McCleskey at 1470; Sanders v. United States, 373 U.S. 1, 10 
(1963). A government meets its burden of pleading if it 
notes petitioner's prior writ history, indicates the claims 
appearing for the first time in a successive petition and 
affirms its belief that petitioner is abusing the writ in a 
manner prescribed by Rule 9(b). McCleskey at 1470. Abuse 
of the writ may be raised by the court sua sponte. Jones v. 
Estelle, 722 F.2d 159, 164 (5th Cir. 1983). 


Once abuse of the writ has been alleged, petitioner 
has the burden of proving that he has not abused the 
writ. McCleskey at 1470; Price v. Johnston, 334 U.S. 266, 292 
(1948). He must demonstrate “cause” and “prejudice” as 
those terms are understood in the context of procedural 
bar, for not presenting the claim in a prior petition. The 
“cause” element may be excused only if the failure of the 
federal court to address the claim would constitute a 
“fundamental miscarriage of justice,” i.e., the claim 
would establish the petitioner’s factual innocence. 
McCleskey at 1470-71. 


SLACK states in his application that Respondents 
have not demonstrated that he has abused the writ. How- 
ever, in their motion to dismiss Respondents properly 
plead abuse of the writ. The burden then shifted to 
SLACK to demonstrate that he did not abuse the writ. 
McCleskey at 1470. SLACK did not even attempt to meet 
his burden. Instead he filed a motion for reconsideration 
which this Court denied. 


In his application, SLACK argues that Rule %(b) of 
the rules governing section 2254 cases recognizes two 
types of abuse of the writ. Specifically, abuse occurs when 


19] 


a petitioner files a new petition, following the adjudica- 
tion of a former petition on the merits, which does not 
raise new claims. SLACK has elected to refer to this type 
of abuse as McCleskey abuse. The second type of abuse 
under 9(b) according to SLACK, requires a showing that a 
new claim was deliberately withheld from a previous 
petition, or that the petitioner deliberately abandoned his 
claim, or that the only purpose of the petition-is to vex, 
harass, or delay or that the petitioner is deliberately 
engaging in needless, piecemeal litigation. (Application, 
pgs. 9-11.) 


According to SLACK his two types of abuse have two 
different standards. With the so-called McCleskey abuse, 
the burden is on the petitioner to show cause and preju-
