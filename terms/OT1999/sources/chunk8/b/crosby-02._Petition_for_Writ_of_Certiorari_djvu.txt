QUESTIONS PRESENTED 


A Massachusetts statute limits the authority of state agencies 
(but not private persons) to buy goods and services from persons 
who do business with Burma. This type of law is known as a 
“selective purchasing law.” The questions presented are: 


1. Whether economic sanctions against Burma enacted 
by Congress in 1996 -- three months after enactment 
of the Massachusetts Burma Law -- implicitly permit, 
or preempt, state and local selective purchasing laws 
regarding Burma. 


2. Whether selective purchasing laws such as the 
Massachusetts Burma Law represent “market 
participation,” not regulation, and are therefore 
exempt from claims based on the Foreign Commerce 
Clause and the foreign affairs power of the federal 
government. 


3. Whether selective purchasing laws such as the 
Massachusetts Burma Law _ unconstitutionally 
interfere with the power of the federal government to 
conduct foreign affairs. 


4. Whether selective purchasing laws such as the 
Massachusetts Burma Law discriminate against 
foreign commerce in violation of the Foreign 
Commerce Clause.

STATEMENT OF THE CASE 


1. Procedural History 


Plaintiff National Foreign Trade Council (NFTC) filed its 
complaint on or about April 30, 1998. The parties filed a 
stipulation of facts, cross-motions for summary judgment, and 
uncontested affidavits. Pet. App. 13-14. No party argued that a 
material fact was in dispute. 

On November 4, 1998, the district court entered a 
memorandum and order granting summary judgment for the 
NFTC. Pet. App. 13-14, 75. The district court found that the 
Massachusetts Burma Law was not preempted by federal law, 
stating that the NFTC had “failed to carry [its] burden” to show 
preemption and that the “evidence does not establish sufficient 
actual conflict for th{e] court to find implied preemption.” Jd. at 


5 


85-86 (citation omitted). Nevertheless, the district court held that 
the Burma Law “impermissibly infringes on the federal 
government’s power to regulate foreign affairs.” Jd at 77. 
Quoting Zschernig v. Miller, 389 U.S. 429, 434-35 (1968), the 
court stated that the Massachusetts Burma Law “has more than an 
indirect or incidental effect in foreign countries” and a “great 
potential for disruption or embarrassment.” Pet. App. 81. The 
sole evidence of “disruption or embarrassment” cited by the 
district court was the withdrawal of three companies from Burma 
and protests against the law filed by the European Union, the 
Government of Japan, and the Association of South East Asian 
Nations. Pet. App. 81-82. The district court did not reach the 
claim by the NFTC under the Foreign Commerce Clause. /d at 
86. On November 17, 1998, the court entered final judgment. 

Petitioners timely appealed to the court of appeals, which 
affirmed. The court of appeals first noted that the parties 
“agreed” that “human rights conditions” in Burma “are 
deplorable.” Pet. App. 5. The court nevertheless held that (1) 
“the law interferes with the foreign affairs power of the federal 
government”; (2) the law represents regulation, not market 
participation, and violates the Foreign Commerce Clause; and (3) 
the law “violates the Supremacy Clause because it is preempted 
by federal sanctions against Burma.” Jd. 

The court summarized its decision as follows: “The passage 
of the Massachusetts Burma Law,” the court said, “has resulted 
in significant attention being brought to the Burmese 
government’s human rights record.” Pet. App. 73. “Indeed, it 
may be that the Massachusetts law was a catalyst for federal 
sanctions.” Jd “Nonetheless, the conduct of this nations’s 
foreign affairs cannot be effectively managed on behalf of all of 


6 


the nation’s citizens if each of the many state and local 
governments pursues its own foreign policy.” Jd “Absent 
express congressional authorization,” the court concluded, 
“Massachusetts cannot set the nation’s foreign policy.” Jd. 


2. Facts 
A. The Massachusetts Burma Law 


The Massachusetts Burma Law limits the procurement of 
goods and services by Massachusetts state agencies from persons 
who do business with Burma. The Burma Law was enacted in 
July, 1996, Mass. St. 1996, c. 130, and entitled “An Act 
Regulating State Contracts with Companies Doing Business with 
or in Burma (Myanmar).” The Act is codified at M.G.L.A. c. 7, 
§§ 22G-M. 

According to the United States Department of State, the 
“people of Burma continue to live under a highly authoritarian 
military regime that is widely condemned for its serious human 
rights abuses.” Ist Cir. App. 336. In the months following 
enactment of the Massachusetts Burma Law in 1996, the United 
States, the European Union, and other countries imposed 
economic sanctions on Burma. /d. at 338, 342-48. 

The Massachusetts Burma Law effectively requires state 
agencies to increase by 10 percent the price of offers to sell goods 
and services to the Commonwealth submitted by companies that 
do business with Burma. M.G.L.A. ch. 7, § 22H(d). The Law 
applies equally to state purchases of goods and services from 
persons domiciled in Massachusetts, other States, and foreign 
countries. The Burma Law does not apply to the purchase of 


7 


goods or services by private persons or prohibit contact between 
private persons and the Government of Burma. 

Subject to certain exceptions, the Burma Law provides that 
“a state agency, a state authority, the house of representatives or 
the state senate may not procure goods or services from any 
person listed on the restricted purchase list maintained by the 
secretary [of admini< tration and finance], or who is determined 
through affidavit or through other reliable methods to meet the 
criteria for so being listed.” M.G.L.A. ch. 7, § 22H(a). By its 
terms, the statute imposes “preference requirements” (§ 221) 
rather than an absolute prohibition on state contracting with 
companies doing business in Burma. Thus, exceptions are 
authorized when the procurement is “essential” and enforcement 
of the restriction would “eliminate the only bid or offer, or would 
result in inadequate competition[,]” § 22H(b)(1) and (2); when 
the State is purchasing certain kinds of medical supplies, § 221; 
or when there is “no comparable low bid or offer” by a bidder 
who is not on the list. § 22H(d). A “comparable low bid or 
offer” is defined as “a responsive and responsible bid or offer 
which is no more than ten percent greater than the lowest bid or 
offer submitted for goods or a service.” § 22G. In practice, the 
“comparable low bid or offer” provision is applied by adding 10 
percent to offers made by persons determined to be doing 
business with Burma. Pet. App. 7-8. 

Section 22J(a) provides that the “secretary shall establish and 
maintain a restricted purchase list . . . [which] shall contain the 
names of all persons currently doing business with Burma 
(Myanmar).” “Doing business with Burma” is defined by § 22G 
as: “(a) having a principal place of business, place of 
incorporation or its corporate headquarters in Burma (Myanmar) 


8 


or having any operations, leases, franchises, majority-owned 
subsidiaries, distribution agreements, or any other similar 
agreements in Burma (Myanmar), or being the majority-owned 
subsidiary licensee or franchise[e] of such a person; (b) providing 
financial services to the government of Burma (Myanmar), 
including providing direct loans, underwriting government 
securities, providing any consulting advice or assistance, 
providing brokerage services, acting as a trustee or escrow agent, 
or otherwise acting as an agent pursuant to a contractual 
agreement; (c) promoting the importation or sale of gems, timber, 
oil, gas or other related products, commerce in which is largely 
controlled by the government of Burma (Myanmar), from Burma 
(Myanmar); (d) providing any goods or services to the 
government of Burma (Myanmar).” Under sections 22H(e) and 
22I, persons with operations in Burma limited to the “reporting 
the news” or providing goods or services “for the provision of 
international telecommunications,” or “providing only medical 
supplies” are exempt from the restrictions of the Act. 

The Act directs the Secretary of Administration and Finance 
(the Secretary), “[iJn establishing the restricted purchase list,” “to 
consult United Nations reports, resources of the Investor 
Responsibility Research Center and the Associates to Develop 
Democratic Burma, and other reliable sources.” § 22J(b). “The 
Secretary shall also place the name of any person [on the list] 
who, in the statement [submitted to state agencies under section 
22H], declared that he meets the criteria for being so listed.” Jd.

Three months after Massachusetts enacted its Burma Law,

The Act further directs the President to “seek to develop, in

within Burma.” /d. at § 570(c). 

Finally, the Act requires the President to submit reports

“(1) progress toward democraticizationin Burma; (2) progress on

progress made in developing the [multilateral] strategy referred 
to in subsection (c) [ ‘to bring democracy to and improve human

certiffied] that, for purposes of [Section 570(b)], the Government

REASONS FOR GRANTING THE WRIT 


For more than two hundred years, citizens of Massachusetts 
and other States have used boycotts to support the “natural, 
essential, and unalienable rights” of people around the world. 
See Mass. Const. of 1780, Pt. 1, art. 1 (West 1997 ed.); Charles 
McLean Andrews, The Boston Merchants and the Non- 
Importation Movement 17, 33-34, 40-41, 43, 55 (1968). The 
Massachusetts Burma Law closely resembles private boycotts and 
the scores of divestment and selective purchasing laws enacted 
by state and local governments in the 1980s concerning South 


_ Africa. The Massachusetts law is also similar to many current 


state and local laws and resolutions concerning Northern Ireland, 


12 


Cuba, Switzerland, and Nigeria -- laws and resolutions now at 
risk in light of the broad ruling of the court below. 

Nothing in our federal Constitution denies to the States the 
right to apply a moral standard to their spending decisions. Not 
one constitutional grant, prohibition, or command requires the 
States to trade with dictators. In an era of global procurement and 
trade, state spending decisions will inevitably rile foreign 
countries and even risk embarrassment to the United States. 
These commonplaces, however, are not adequate grounds for 
constitutional judgments against selective purchasing laws. State 
laws that threaten national interests may be addressed by 
Congress, “whose voice, in th{e] area [of foreign affairs], is the 
Nation’s.” Barclays Bank v. Franchise Tax Board, 512 U.S. 298, 
331 (1994). Absent clear and valid preemption of state law by 
Congress, the States should be free to choose their trading 
partners and indirectly seek to “secure the blessings of liberty” 
around the world. U.S. Const., Preamble. 


I. THE CONSTITUTIONALITY OF SELECTIVE 
PURCHASING LAWS IS AN IMPORTANT 
QUESTION OF FEDERAL LAW THAT HAS

A. The Scores of Selective Purchasing and 
Similar Laws Enacted in the 1980s and 
1990s Show the National Importance of 
the Questions Presented. 


In this case “a United States court of appeals has decided an 


13 


important question of federal law that has not been, but should be, 
settled by this Court... .” United States Supreme Court Rule 
10(c). The court of appeals acknowledged the national interest 
in the question presented. According to the court below, “[a]t 
least nineteen municipal governments have enacted analogous 
laws restricting their purchases from companies that do business 
with Burma. In addition, local jurisdictions have enacted similar 
laws relating to China, Cuba, Nigeria, and other nations.” Pet. 
App. 10; see, e.g., New York City Admin. Code § 6-115.1 
(Northern Ireland); § 6-115 (Burma); Los Angeles Admin. Code 
§§ 10.38 et seg. (Burma); San Francisco Admin. Code, Ch. 12J 
(Burma); Philadelphia Code § 17-104(b) (Burma); Mass. Gen. 
Laws Ann. ch. 22C-22F (West 1996 ed.) (Northern Ireland); 
Florida Stat. Ann. §§ 215.471 and 215.472 (Cuba); Vermont 
J.H.R. 157 (Joint Resolution Supporting National League for 
Democracy in Burma); Vermont Acts of 1999, ch.-13 (requiring 
state pension funds to support certain shareholder resolutions 
concerning business done in Burma). The court below noted 
further that “Massachusetts is not alone in its views regarding 
Burma and there is great potential for the proliferation of similar 
statutes.” Pet. App. 24. 

Selective purchasing laws have a long pedigree. In the New 
England and other colonies, the founding generation considered 
the nonimportation boycott to be a wise alternative to war. See 
Andrews, supra; James Gray Pope, Republican Moments: The 
Role of Direct Popular Power in the American Constitutional 
Order, 139 Pa. L. Rev. 287, 330-35 (1990). In the 1980s, scores 
of state and local governments boycotted companies that did 
business with South Africa. Ultimately, twenty-three States and 
eighty cities enacted divestment or procuremert laws intended to 


if 


promote human rights in South Africa. See Richard B. Bilder, 
The Role of States and Cities in Foreign Relations, 83 Am. J. Int’! 
Law 821, 822 (1989); Board of Trustees v. Mayor and City 
Council of Baltimore, 317 Md. 72, 562 A.2d 720, 744-49 (1989) 
(upholding constitutionality of divestment ordinance), cert. 
denied, 493 U.S. 1093 (1990); 10 Op. Off. Legal Counsel 49 
(1986) (upholding state and local divestment and selective 
purchasing laws). During the same period, “Buy American” laws 
were enacted by approximately forty States, despite criticism by 
foreign countries and firms. See Trojan Technologies, Inc. v. 
Commonwealth of Pennsylvania, 916 F.2d 903 (3d Cir. 1990) 
(upholding Pennsylvania “Buy American” law), cert. denied, 501 
U.S. 1212 (1991). 

This long pedigree reflects the historic power of state and 
local governments to decide “with whom [they] will deal.” 
Reeves v. Stake, 447 U.S. 429, 439 (1980) (quoting United States 
v. Colgate & Co., 250 U.S. 300, 307 (1919)). The recurring use 
of selective purchasing and similar laws shows that this case 
should be resolved by this Court. 


B. The Recent Rise in Global Commerce 
and International Trade Agreements 
Heightens the Need for Review of 
Claims that State Laws 
Unconstitutionally Interfere with 
Foreign Commerce and Foreign 
Affairs. — 


The importance of this case is underscored by increasing 
global commerce and a new generation of trade agreements. 


15 


Foreign trade has increased dramatically in recent years; 
improvements in communications and transportation have 
married the States to foreign firms and countries, for better or 
worse. Countless state and local laws now significantly affect 
foreign traders and investors. See Louis Henkin, Foreign Affairs 
and the Constitution 150 (1996 ed.). 

Meanwhile, the United States has entered a new generation 
of agreements governing international trade and procurement. 
See North American Free Trade Agreement (NAFTA) 
Implementation Act, P.L. 103-182, 107 Stat. 2057 (1993), 19 
U.S.C. §§ 3301 et seq.; Uruguay Round Agreement Act of 1994 
(URAA), P.L. 103-465, 108 Stat. 4809, 19 U.S.C. § 3501 et seq. 
Under these agreements, Congress has purported to subject state 
laws to review by international bodies. Agreement Establishing 
the World Trade Organization(WTO), Annex 1B, art. 1, 33 1.LM. 
1144 (1994), 1169; 1994 U.S.C.A.ALN. at 4052; 19 U.S.C. § 
3312(NAFTA). Foreign countries have targeted dozens of state 
laws for attack. See European Commission, Report on Barriers 
to Trade and Investment in the United States (1997). The 
possibility of adverse action against the States under the GATT 
“impose(s] considerable new burdens and vulnerabilities” on the 
States. Laurence H. Tribe, Taking Text and Structure Seriously: 
Reflections on Free-Form Method in Constitutional 
Interpretation, 108 Harv. L. Rev. 1221, 1267 n.156 (1995). 
These burdens and vulnerabilities will increase dramatically if 
courts may rely on mere trade complaints as the basis for 
constitutional judgments against state laws. 

In this case, the European Union and Japan have challenged 
the Massachusetts Burma Law under the GATT at the WTO. Pet. 
App. 10, 20, 24. Despite the fact that Congress has denied a 


16 


private right of action to enforce GATT in the courts of the 
United States (see 19 U.S.C. §§ 3512(b)2MA), 3512(c)(1); 1994 
U.S.C.A.A.N. at 4055), and the United States Trade 
Representative has pledged a strong defense of the Massachusetts 
law at the WTO (Ist Cir. App. 425), the court below expressly 
based its constitutional holdings on the complaints made by the 
European Union and Japan. Pet. App. 24-26. The court’s 
reliance on trade complaints -- inevitable rows in an era of global 
procurement -- grants to foreign countries and firms a “heckler’s 
veto” against state laws. Absent review by this Court, foreign 
complaints under NAFTA and GATT will increasingly fuel 
constitutional claims against divestment and selective purchasing 
laws, “Buy American” laws, environmental regulations, and other 
state laws. These claims will sap the lawmaking powers of the 
States and unduly enlarge the role of the courts. The Court 
should grant certiorari to assess the effects of trade pacts and 
related federal laws on respondent’s constitutional claims. See 
Barclays, 512 U.S at 324 & n.22, 326 (federal law may show that 
Congress has “implicitly permitted” state laws that affect foreign 
commerce; complaints by foreign countries do not establish 
unconstitutional interference with foreign commerce); cf 
Kolovrat v. Oregon, 366 U.S. 187, 191 (1961) (certiorari granted 
“because the cases involve important rights asserted in reliance 
upon federal treaty obligations”). 
Cc. The Constitutionality of Selective 
Purchasing Laws Has Not Been Decided 
by This Court, and a Split in the 
Circuits Now Exists on Whether the 


Market ParticipationException Applies 
to the Foreign Commerce Clause.

The Court has never decided the constitutionalityof selective 
purchasing laws; the court below described the central issues in 
this case as “issues of first impression.” Pet. App. 20. Although 
such laws concerning South Africa existed throughout this 
country (and the world) in the 1980s, no one challenged them in 
court. This Court denied review in a case sustaining a local 
divestment law: Board of Trustees, 562 A.2d at 740-757. In that 
case, the Maryland Court of Appeals upheld the Baltimore 
divestment ordinance against claims under the Supremacy, 
Foreign Commerce, and Foreign Affairs clauses -- the same 
clauses at issue here. This Court denied certiorari. 493 U.S. 1093 
(1990). During the same period, the Court twice denied review 
in cases upholding so-called “Buy American” laws against 
similar claims. See Trojan Technologies, Inc., supra, cert. 
denied, 501 U.S. 1212 (1991); K.S.B. Technical Sales Corp. v. 
North Jersey District Water Supply Comm'n, 75 N.J. 272, 381 
A.2d 774 (1977), appeal dismissed, 435 U.S. 982 (1978). 

The case repeatedly cited by the courts below -- Zschernig 
v. Miller, 389 U.S. 429 (1968) - did not concern selective 
purchasing or divestment. In Zschernig, the Oregon statute at 
issue regulated the terms under which a nonresident alien could 
inherit property from an Oregon decedent. The state law 
prohibited inheritance by a nonresident alien unless three 
conditions were satisfied: (1) the alien’s government must accord 
Americans the right to inherit on equal terms with its citizens; (2) 
the alien’s government must give Americans the right to receive 
payment in the United States of funds from foreign estates; and 
(3) the nonresident alien must be able to receive “the benefit, use 
or control” of the proceeds of the Oregon estate “without 
confiscation” by his government. The Court concluded that this 


18 


type of probate law as enforced in the Oregon courts had “a 
direct impact on foreign relations and may well adversely affect 
the power of the central governmentto deal with those problems.” 
Id. at 441. The Court stressed that the extensive record of 
enforcement of the statute by the Oregon courts showed a “great 
potential for disruption or embarrassment” in the conduct of 
foreign relations: the Oregon courts had repeatedly criticized the 
administration of foreign laws by foreign officials, questioned 
their credibility, and denied foreign citizens private inheritances 
based on “foreign policy attitudes” toward particular 
governments. See 429 U.S. at 435 (emphasis added). 

Zschernig did not overrule Clark v. Allen, 331 U.S. 503 
(1947), which upheld the facial validity of a California statute 
similar to the first two sections of the Oregon law. Although the 
California statute in Clark was clearly designed to influence 
foreign countries to change their laws to allow Americans to 
inherit, the Court dismissed the constitutional claim against the 
law as “farfetched.” Jd at 517. Emphasizing that “rights of 
succession” were peculiarly a matter of local law, the Court 
agreed that “[w]Jhat California has done will have some incidental 
or indirect effect in foreign countries” but concluded “that is true 
of many state laws which none would claim cross the forbidden 
line.” Jd. 

Neither Zschernig nor Clark addressed the exercise of state 
spending powers -- only the regulation of private inheritances. 
The court below stated that the “precise boundaries” of Zschernig 
“are not clear[,]” and “subsequent Supreme Court holdings have 
done little to clarify the reach of the Court’s holding” in that case. 
Pet. App. 20, 30; see Recent Cases, 112 Harv. L. Rev. 2013, 2016 
(1999) (noting “two different and inconsistent approaches” used 


19 


by lower courts to “apply the Zschernig effects test”). No case of 
this Court addresses the intersection of foreign affairs, state 
spending, and international trade agreements. Compare Japan 
Lines, Lid. v. County of Los Angeles, 441 U.S. 434 (1979); 
Container Corp. of America v. Franchise Tax Board, 463 U.S. 
159 (1983); Barclays, 512 U.S. at 310-31 (all reviewing state 
taxation under the Foreign Commerce Clause). 

The Court has addressed state spending in cases applying the 
market participationexception to the domestic Commerce Clause. 
The Court has made clear that “when a state or local government 
enters the market as a participant it is not subject to the restraints 
of the Commerce Clause.” White v. Massachusetts Council of 
Construction Employers, Inc., 460 U.S. 204, 208 (1983). The 
Court has reserved, however, the question whether the market 
participation exception to the Commerce Clause applies to claims 
under the Foreign Commerce Clause (see Reeves v. Stake, 447 
U.S. at 438 n.9), and it has likewise never decided whether the 
exception applies to claims under the Foreign Affairs clauses. 
See 10 Op. Off. Legal Counsel at 50 n.4. (“Although the Court [in 
Reeves v. Stake] expressly reserved the question whether the 
market participation doctrine applies to the statutes that affect 
foreign, as opposed to interstate, commerce, we believe that the 
rationale for the distinction -- that the Commerce Clause was 
intended to restrict a state’s ability to regulate but not its ability 
to participate in markets -- applies equally to statutes that affect 
foreign commerce.”); 10 Op. Off. Legal Counsel at 63-64 (market 
participation doctrine should apply as well to claims under the 
federal foreign affairs power). 

The decision below creates a split between the First and 
Third Circuits on whether the market participation doctrine 


20 


applies to the Foreign Commerce Clause. Compare Pet. App. 48- 
50 with Trojan Technologies, Inc., 916 F.2d at. 909-13, Board of 
Trustees, 562 A.2d at 749-53 (quoting Laurence H. Tribe, 
American Constitutional Law § 6-21 (2d ed. 1988) at 469), and 
K.S.B. Technical Sales Corp., 381 A.2d at 784-89.' The court 
below further stated its “disagree[ment]” with the decision of the 
district court in Trojan Technologies, Inc., 742 F. Supp. 900, 902- 
03 (M.D. Pa.), aff'd, 916 F.2d 903 (3d Cir. 1990), “[t]o the extent 
that this case supports a market participant exception to the 
foreign affairs power” (Pet. App. 37n), and rejected the 1986 
opinion of the Department of Justice (quoted above) on the same 
point. Pet. App. 37-38. The Court should grant the petition to 
resolve these splits of authority on the application of the market 
participation exception to the Foreign Commerce and Foreign 
Affairs clauses.

“We deal here with the basic allocation of power between the 
States and the Nation.” Zschernig, 389 U.S. at 443 (Stewart, J.,

constitutionality of certain types of state laws alleged to interfere 
with foreign commerce and foreign affairs. These cases, 
however, addressed only state taxation and regulation, not a 
proprietary activity such as state spending. Meanwhile, in the 
period between Zschernig and Barclays, the Court applied the 
market participant exception to state spending and stressed its 
roots in principles of federalism. See Reeves v. Stake, 447 U.S. 
at 441; Hughes v. Alexandria Scrap Corp., 426 U.S. 794, 807-10

Zschernig, Barclays, and the market participationexception. The 
case is a unique oppoiiuniiy to join these lines in a decision that 
would clarify the authority of the States to take action that has 
indirect foreign effects. 

“Zschernig remains the only case in which the Supreme 
Court has struck down a state statute as violative of the foreign 
affairs power of the federal government.” Pet. App. 30. The case 
remains “a unique statement and a sole application of 
constitutional doctrine.” Louis Henkin, Foreign Affairs and the 
Constitution (1996 ed.) at 165. The doctrine has been questioned 
and the result in Zschernig attributed to Cold War tensions. See 
id. at 162 (“until 1968, there was no hint of such a principle”); id 
at 165 & n. (“relic of the Cold War”); Jack L. Goldsmith, Federal 
Courts, Foreign Affairs, and Federalism, 83 Va. L. Rev. 1617, 
1641-64 (1997). ! 

The decision in Barclays sparked new attention in 
Zschernig. In Barclays, the Court upheld the California 


22 


“worldwide unitary” method of taxing multinational corporations, 
relying on Congress’s failure to preempt and the “implicit 
permission” that failure represented. /d. at 324, 326, 329. The 
Court noted that the Constitution allocates to Congress the power 
to establish uniformity -- to “speak with one voice” in matters 
affecting foreign nations -- when it wishes to do so. /d. at 329. 
Where, however, Congress had failed to nullify the state tax at 
issue, despite vocal attacks by the Executive Branch, id. at 328- 
29, and a “battalion” of foreign countries, id. at 320, 324 n.22, the 
Court determined that Congress had implicitly permitted the law 
and thus protected it from a claim that it interfered with the 
conduct of foreign relations. 

Thus, under Barclays, express Congressional approval of a 
state law affecting foreign affairs is not required for the Court to 
hold a state law constitutional: while the federal government 
must have the “capacity” to “speak with one voice” on foreign 
affairs when the national interest so requires, “Congress may 
more passively indicate that certain state practices do not ‘impair 
federal uniformity in an area where federal uniformity is 
essential;’. . . it need not convey its intent with the unmistakable 
clarity required to permit state regulation that discriminated 
against interstate commerce ....” Jd. at 323 (emphasis added); 
see also Dames & Moore v. Regan, 453 U.S. 654, 682-83 & n.10 
(1981). 

Zschernig was not cited in Barclays, though it was cited in 
the briefs of both parties and in the oral argument of the United 
States as amicus curiae in that case. See Barclays, supra, 1993 
WL 639306 (trief for petitioner), 1994 WL 135983 (brief for 
respondent), and 1994 WL 664950 at *13 (argument for United 
States). Commentators have understandably asked “whether

14 (citing Goldsmith, supra, at 1700-01, and Harold Hongju Koh,

1848 (1998)). This petition squarely presents that question and 
an opportunity for this Court to address the interplay of 
Zschernig, Barclays, and the market participationexception. The 
Court should grant the petition and decide whether (1) the 
“implicit permission” found in Barclays may be shown by the 
later enactment of a non-preemptive federal law and (2) whether 
implicit Congressional permission forecloses constitutional 
claims under Zschernig. Finally, this selective purchasing case 
presents a unique opportunity to apply the market participant 
exception -- a doctrine firmly established by decisions of this 
Court -- to claims under the Foreign Commerce and Foreign 
Affairs Clauses.”

II. THE MARKET PARTICIPATION 
EXCEPTION APPLIES TO THE 
FOREIGN COMMERCE AND 
FOREIGN AFFAIRS CLAUSES. 


The Court should grant the petition and hold that the market 
participation exception applies to both the Foreign Commerce 
and Foreign Affairs clauses. 

“When a state or local gc-"ernment enters the market as a 
participant it is not subject to the restraints of the Commerce 
Clause.” White v. Massachusetts Council of Construction 
Employers, Inc., 460 U.S. at 208 (rejecting Commerce Clause 
challenge to City of Boston requirement that at least 50 percent 
of private workforce on all construction projects funded wholly 
by city must be Boston residents); see Reeves, Inc. v. Stake, 447 
U.S. at 436-37 (upholding state law restricting sale of state- 
produced cement to state residents); Hughes v. Alexandria Scrap 
Corp., 426 U.S. at 810 (upholding state “bounties” for scrap cars 
under law favoring state residents seeking bounties). The 
“market participant” doctrine stems from the fact that “[t}here is 
no indication of a constitutional plan to limit the ability of the 
States themselves to operate freely in the free market.” Reeves, 
Inc. v. Stake, 447 U.S. at 437. Indeed, in light of the “long 
recognized right of trader or manufacturer, engaged in an entirely 
private business, freely to exercise his own independent discretion 
as to parties with whom he will deal,” ic. at 438-439 (quotation 


26 


omitted), the Court has reasoned that “when acting as proprietors, 
States should similarly share existing freedoms from federal 
constraints, including the inherent limits of the Commerce 
Clause.” Jd. The doctrine rests on a “healthy regard for 
federalism and good government” rooted in the powers reserved 
to the States by the Tenth Amendment. /d. at 441. 

The court below “decline[d]” to follow the federal and state 
courts that have applied the market participant exception to 
claims against state and local selective purchasing laws under the 
Foreign Commerce Clause. See pp. 19-20, supra. This was 
error because the rationale for the exception -- that the Commerce 
Clause was intended to restrict a State’s ability to regulate but not 
its ability to participate in markets -- applies equally to statutes 
that affect foreign commerce. See id. 

The court below similarly erred in not applying the market 
participation exception to claims that state action interferes with 
the ability of the federal government to conduct foreign affairs. 
As the Maryland Court of Appeals noted, “[TJhe concerns which 
underlie the foreign Commerce Clause are closely related to the 
concerns underlying the limits on a state’s authority to affect 
foreign policy.” Board of Trustees, 562 A.2d at 752. The close 
relation between the clauses shows that the foreign affairs power 
-- no less than the foreign commerce power -- must be analyzed 
to determine whether it was intended by the Framers to constrain 
the proprietary power of the States. Cf Wisconsin Dep't of 
Industry v. Gould, 475 U.S. 282, 289-91 (1982)(market 
participant doctrine does not apply to preemption analysis under 
NLRA because comprehensive statutory scheme reflects intent to 
foreclose certain state action, but Commerce Clause does not 


27 


itself interfere with power of States to contract freely in market). 
In the absence of any such original intent, there is no 
constitutional basis for a judgment against a state’s exercise of 
proprietary power, absent valid preemption by Congress. 

The historical basis for the plenary federal power over 
foreign relations does not establish an intent to displace state 
proprietary power. The Framers were primarily concerned with 
congressional control over the exercise of state regulatory power 
affecting foreign affairs -- particularly tariffs. See The Federalist 
No. 22, at 144 (C. Rossiter ed. 1961); id. No. 23 at 153. They 
also barred state actions -- such as treaties or declarations of war 
-- long associated with international sovereignty. Each of these 
restrictions was adopted at a time when the law affirmed the right 
of a government to decide with whom it would deal. See Perkins 
v. Lukens Steel Co., 310 U.S. 113, 127 (1940). In light of the 
historical basis for the federal foreign affairs power and nature of 
selective purchasing laws, this petition presents a unique 
opportunity to apply the market participant doctrine to claims 
under both the foreign affairs and foreign commerce powers. See 
10 Op. Office of Legal Counsel at 63 (“We do not think the 
[Zschernig] principle should be extended to state proprietal 
action”).‘

Finally, the court below “reject[ed] Massachusetts’s claim 
that Congress has permitted the law” and found instead “that 
Congress has preempted the law.” Pet. App. 59. The Court 
should grant certiorari and reverse this judgment because it 
inverts the normal presumption of validity of state law and 
conflicts with Barclays. 

The court below conceded that the Federal Burma Law 
contains no express preemption clause. Pet. App. 64. However, 
citing Hines v. Davidowitz, 312 U.S. 52, 67 (1941), it ruled that 
when “Congress legislates in an area of foreign relations, there is 
a strong presumption that it intended to preempt the field, in 
particular where the federal legislation does not touch on a 
traditional area of state concern.” Pet. App. 69-70. This ruling 
inverts the presumption against preemption long applied by this 
Court and places on Massachusetts the burden of proving that 
Congress did not intend to preempt state and local laws on 
Burma.
