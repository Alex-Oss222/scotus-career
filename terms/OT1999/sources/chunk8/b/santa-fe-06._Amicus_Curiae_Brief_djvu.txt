STATEMENT OF AMICI] INTEREST 


The State of Texas, Texas Attorney General John Cornyn, 
Texas Governor George W. Bush, and the States of Alabama, 
Colorado, Kansas, Louisiana, Mississippi, Nebraska, South 
Carolina, and Tennessee, appear as amici curiae and urge the 
Court to grant this petition. Amici curiae have an interest in 
this case because the Fifth Circuit’s judgment will require 
governmental officials to review and essentially censor 
student-initiated and student-given speech even though there 
is no state involvement in the decision that the speech occur or 
in the selection of the speaker. Amici’s interest here is 
substantial and direct, because the holding will emanate far 
beyond public school sponsored events. Under this decision: 


* Officials will be required to abandon their roles as 
neutral observers of religious speech and instead take 
up their pens as editors and censors. 


* Officials must enforce and defend these unprecedented 
requirements. 


* Officials, in enforcing and defending the Santa Fe 
1.S.D. requirements, will be compelled to endorse 
certain types of religious speech resulting in deep state 
entanglement with religion. 


* Officials will be forced to discriminate against any 
religious viewpoint that is not nonsectarian and 
nonproselytizing. 

This decision places states in an untenable, unacceptable, 

and unconstitutional position, despite established precedent to 


the contrary. In addition, since the petition was filed, the 
Eleventh Circuit decided Chandler v. James, Nos. 97-6898, 


97-6953, 1999 WL 493495 (CAI1 July 13, 1999), which 
directly conflicts with the Fifth Circuit's decision in Santa Fe 
LS.D. \na factually similar case involving student-initiated 
speech at graduation and sporting events, the Eleventh Circuit 
held that “genuinely student-initiated religious speech is 
private speech endorsing religion” and is “fully protected by 
both the Free Exercise and the Free Speech Clauses of the 
Constitution.” Jd. at *6. The Court should grant certiorari to 
address these important issues and resolve this split in the 
circuit courts. 


The parties have given their consent to the filing of this 
brief, as shown in the letters of consent that accompany this 
brief. Sup. CT. R. 37.2(a).

B. The Santa Fe I.S.D. Test Requires School Districts 
to Engage in Viewpoint Discrimination. 


The Fifth Circuit’s holding that religious speech under the 
Santa Fe I.S.D. graduation policy must be nonsectarian and 
nonproselytizing requires school officials to engage in 
religious viewpoint discrimination. See Santa Fe 1.S.D., 168 
F.3d at 833-34 (Jolly, J., dissenting). The majority opinion 
permits one type of religious speech—ecumenical—to the 
exclusion of all others. But once ecumenical religious speech 
is permitted, the state must neutrally accommodate all 
religious viewpoints. See Lamb's Chapel v. Center Moriches 
Union Free Sch. Dist., 508 U.S. 384, 394 (1993) (quoting 
Cornelius v. NAACP Legal Defense & Educ. Fund, Inc., 473 
U.S. 788, 806 (1985)); Widmar v. Vincent, 454 U.S. 263 
(1981).

governmental review and approval of only certain kinds of 
prayer is impermissible viewpoint discrimination, reasoning 
that the graduation ceremony is not a limited public forum 
subject to viewpoint discrimination. Santa Fe 1.S.D., 168 F.3d 
at 818-21. Remarkably, the majority admitted that if the 
graduation program did create a limited public forum, then its 
proscription of sectarian, proselytizing prayer would constitute 
unconstitutional viewpoint discrimination. Jd. at 821.'° This

C. The Fifth Circuit’s Decision Requires School 
Officials to Become Excessively Entangled in 
Private Religious Speech. 


Instead of enforcing the Establishment Clause, the Fifth 
Circuit’s decision will actually force school districts to violate 
it. By requiring school officials to preview student messages 
to determine whether they are overly sectarian or proselytizing, 
the Fifth Circuit will force school officials to excessively 
entangle themselves in private religious speech in violation of 
the Establishment Clause. Not only would review of student 
prayers by school officials prove unwieldy and unmanageable, 
it would also cause officials to wade into the constitutional 
quagmire of editing students’ religious speech. 


There is no doubt that the Fifth Circuit's content-based

The Court has repeatedly recognized that government 
oversight of student-initiated religious speech entangles the 
State in religion. In Lemon v. Kurtzman, 403 U.S. 602, 619 
(1971), the Court held that a state statute providing aid to 
church-related elementary and secondary schools excessively 
entangled church and state because it would require 
“comprehensive, discriminating, and continuing state 
surveillance” to meet the requirements of the First 
Amendment. 


This kind of state inspection and evaluation of the 
religious content of a religious organization is fraught 
with the sort of entanglement that the Constitution 
forbids. It is a relationship pregnant with dangers of 
excessive government direction of church schools and 
hence of churches. 


Id. at 620. 


The Court recognized again in Widmar v. Vincent, 454 
U.S. 263, 277 (1981), that a university policy that required the 
content-based exclusion of religious speech violated the 
fundamental principle that state regulation of speech should be 
content-neutral. The Court observed that requiring the 
university and ultimately the courts “to inquire into the 
significance of words and practices to different religious faiths 
_... would tend inevitably to entangle the State with religion 
in a manner forbidden by our cases.” /d. at 270 n.6. 


And in Rosenberger v. Rector & Visitors of University of 
Virginia, 515 U.S. 819, 845-46 (1995), the Court held that a 
university policy excluding financial support to a student 
religious newspaper violated the First Amendment. The Court 
expressly disapproved the argument that the school was 
required, “in order to avoid a constitutional violation, to 
scrutinize the content of student speech, lest the expression in 
question—speech otherwise protected by the 
Constitution—contain too great a religious content.” /d. at 
844. “[O}fficial censorship would be far more inconsistent 
with the Establishment Clause,” the Court observed. /d. at 
845. 


Requiring school officials to censor student prayers is 
essentially the same as having an official compose the prayer 
in the first place. The Court long ago recognized that 
government “should stay out of the business of writing or 
sanctioning official prayers and leave that purely religious 
function to the people themselves and to those the people 
choose to look to for religious guidance.” Engel v. Vitale, 370 
U.S. 421, 435 (1962). Indeed, “[t}he First Amendment was 
added to the Constitution to stand as a guarantee that neither 
the power nor the prestige of the Federal Government would 
be used to control, support or influence the kinds of prayer the 
American people can say.” /d. at 429.

Instead of following this Court’s admonitions against state 
censorship of religious speech, the Fifth Circuit elevated the 
“nonsectarian,” “nonproselytizing” requirement of some 
school prayer policies to the level of a constitutional mandate. 
However, this Court has rejected the notion that nonsectarian 
prayers could resurrect an otherwise unconstitutional 
government-sanctioned prayer. See Lee v. Weisman, 505 U.S. 
577, 588-90 (1992); id. at 616 (Souter, J., concurring); Engel, 
370 U.S. at 430. 


The Court in Lee observed that “though the First 
Amendment does not allow the government to stifle prayers 
which aspire to [nonsectarian]} ends, neither does it permit the 
government to undertake that task for itself.” 505 U.S. at 589. 
By forcing school districts to police student speech to stamp 
out sectarian or proselytizing messages, the Fifth Circuit is 
requiring officials to undertake a sectarian agenda in violation 
of the Establishment Clause. 


The Fifth Circuit’s logic extends beyond student-initiated 
invocations and benedictions, and ultimately could apply to all 
speech at school-sponsored events. Will school officials have 
to preview the contents of a speech any time a speaker could 
potentially inject a sectarian or proselytizing comment into it? 
Speakers at graduations, awards ceremonies, speech classes. 
anti-drug programs, student plays. and football banquets will 
be potentially subject to the nonsectarian, nonproselytizing 
requirements imposed by the Fifth Circuit. 

A student could give a speech to his class discussing his 
belief or disbelief in God. A recovering drug addict might 
testify at a mandatory anti-drug program that he owes his 
recovery to Jesus Christ. Students could decide to put on a 
play celebrating religion, such as Jesus Christ Superstar or 
Godspell. All of these activities could potentiaily fal! under 
the ambit of the Fifth Circuit’s decision. If school officials do 


not censor this speech beforehand, will they have to decide 
whether to pull the plug on the microphone or use the 
proverbial cane to pull the offending speaker offstage? Either 
way, school districts are faced with an unconstitutional Catch- 
22. This Court should grant certiorari to correct the Fifth 
Circuit’s grievous error.

The Fifth Circuit’s condemnation of Santa Fe’s football

prior Supreme Court authority. The content of the speech is 
not the focus of the Court’s inquiry. Instead, it is whether

here, and this Court should hold that the policy is 
constitutional.
