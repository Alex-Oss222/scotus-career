## Presentation of the Arizona I defense

- In February 1979 the State parties responded to the United States' motion for modification of the decree. The published record identifies the response as *Response of the States of Arizona, California, and Nevada and the Other California Defendants to the Motion of the United States for Modification of Decree*, October Term 1978, No. 8, Original. The response did not assert that Arizona I precluded the Fort Yuma disputed-boundary water claim.
- The State parties did not present that particular defense during the 1982 briefing and argument leading to Arizona II. The later oral-argument record reports counsel's concession of that chronology and explanation that counsel perceived the argument after Arizona II and Nevada v. United States, 463 U.S. 110 (1983). These are litigation acts and counsel's explanation, not an adjudication of excuse or timeliness.
- The identified first presentation was the State parties' 1989 motion to reopen, page 6, footnote 2. The State parties here are Arizona, California, Coachella Valley Water District, and Metropolitan Water District of Southern California.
- The Court granted the 1989 reopening motion, 493 U.S. 886. Special Master McGarr addressed the Arizona I defense in Memorandum Opinion and Order No. 4, September 6, 1991, pages 6–7. He rejected that defense on a changed-circumstance theory concerning Interior's 1978 position. The State parties except to that recommendation; the United States and Tribe defend rejection of the defense on their own stated grounds. The Master's recommendation is the ruling presented for review, not a present holding of the Court.
- No original 1989 response briefs have been separately recovered. Do not infer a conceded waiver, a preserved objection to untimeliness, or the absence of such an objection from the dates alone. If the precise responsive objection becomes dispositive, obtain the original filing or identify the specific source-reconstruction premise.

## Prior decrees and earlier requests

The 1979 and 1984 supplemental decrees make the five reservations' water quantities subject to appropriate adjustment by agreement or decree if their boundaries are finally determined. The 1964 language concerned the Fort Mojave and Colorado River reservations; the later language includes all five. The condition, scope, and change in coverage must be preserved. The State parties stipulated to the 1979 decree's terms. The text does not itself decide what future defenses remain available.

Arizona II, 460 U.S. 605, 634, reports the State parties' request that the Master receive evidence and legal argument and decide boundary disputes for determining additional Indian water rights. Master Tuttle's October Term 1981 report, page 57, describes the State contention that the five reservations' boundaries were not finally determined and their request for a new determination. The States' 1988 brief in California v. United States, October Term 1987, No. 87-1165, page 49, requested a new Master and expedited boundary review. These requests are procedural conduct; their legal implications remain disputed.

Arizona II, 460 U.S. at 638, separately stated that if grounds for dismissal of the District Court suit were sustained and survived review, the Court could later determine whether the boundary issues were nevertheless open for litigation in the original action. Preserve that reservation alongside the supplemental-decree condition. Neither document should be paraphrased as an express adjudication of the currently asserted preclusion defense.

## Distinct contentions and consent instrument

The State parties contend that the United States possessed the relevant boundary facts in the original proceedings and could have asserted the larger Fort Yuma claim then. They compare the boundary claim with the omitted-acreage finality issue addressed in Arizona II. The United States and Tribe distinguish reserved boundary disputes from omitted acreage within accepted boundaries and challenge the timing of the defense. These are the positions presented, not findings resolving claim identity or preservation.

The State parties also advance a distinct Indian Claims Commission Act argument: payment of a judgment concerning Indian land claims can extinguish underlying title and bar dependent claims against third parties. Their cited lower-court authorities include United States v. Gemmill, 535 F.2d 1145 (9th Cir. 1976), United States v. Dann, 873 F.2d 1189 (9th Cir. 1989), and United States v. Pend Oreille Public Utility District No. 1, 926 F.2d 1502 (9th Cir. 1991). Do not merge this statutory contention with ordinary issue preclusion or assume that a release of claims against the United States expressly releases claims against all other persons.

Section 22 of the Act, 25 U.S.C. section 70u (1976 edition), distinguishes two events. Under subsection (a), the filed Commission recovery report has the effect of a final Court of Claims judgment and authorizes payment; payment discharges the United States from all claims and demands touching the controversy. Under subsection (b), a final determination against a claimant, made and reported under the Act, bars further claims or demands against the United States arising from that matter. The stated discharged party is the United States; the State parties contend that land-title consequences also affect claims against others. That extension is a litigant position for adjudication. Exact subsection text is mechanically extracted to `arizona-icca-section22-source.txt`.

The Docket 320 petition presented alternative invalid-cession/trespass and uncompensated-taking theories. The final compromise instrument, already reproduced in the December 1999 government exception, disposes of the parties' claims against one another and contains its own no-admission provision. Whether the instrument or the Act conclusively resolves the land-title predicate remains contested; alternative pleaded theories do not substitute for interpreting the instrument.

## Settlement posture

No party filed an exception to the Master's recommendation to approve the Fort Mojave and Colorado River Reservation settlements. This does not erase any amicus objection or automatically establish the Court's appropriate action.
