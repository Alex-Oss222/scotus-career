QUESTIONS PRESENTED 


A Massachusetts statute -- the “Massachusetts Burma Law” -- 
limits the authority of state agencies (but not private persons) to 
buy goods and services from persons who do business with 
Burma. This type of law is known as a “selective purchasing 
law.” The questions presented are: 


1. Whether economic sanctions against Burma enacted 
by Congress in 1996 -- three months after enactment 
of the Massachusetts Burma Law -- implicitly permit, 
or preempt, state and local selective purchasing laws 
regarding Burma. 


2. Whether selective purchasing laws such as the 
Massachusetts Burma Law represent “market 
participation,” not regulation, and are therefore 
exempt from claims based on the Foreign Commerce 
Clause and the foreign affairs power of the federal 
government. 


3. Whether selective purchasing laws such as the 
Massachusetts Burma Law _ unconstitutionally 
interfere with the power of the federal government to 
conduct foreign affairs. 


4. Whether selective purchasing laws such as the 
Massachusetts Burma Law discriminate against 
foreign commerce in violation of the Foreign 
Commerce Clause.

STATEMENT OF THE CASE 


1. Procedural History 


Plaintiff NFTC filed its complaint on or about April 30, 1998. 
Jt. App. 12. The parties filed a stipulation of facts (Jt. App. 209), 
cross-motions for summary judgment, and uncontested affidavits 
Pet. App. 13-14. No party argued that a material fact was in 
dispute. 

On November 4, 1998, the district court entered a 
memorandum and order granting summary judgment for the 
NFTC. Pet. App. 13-14, 75. The district court found that the 
Massachusetts Burma Law was not preempted by federal law, 
stating that the NFTC had “failed to carry [its] burden” to show 
preemption and that the “evidence does not establish sufficient 
actual conflict for th{e] court to find implied preemption.” /d. at 


4 


85-86 (citation omitted). Nevertheless, the district court held that 
the Burma Law “impermissibly infringes on the federal 
government's power to regulate foreign affairs.” Jd at 77. 
Quoting Zschernig v. Miller, 389 U.S. 429, 434-35 (1968), the 
court stated that the Massachusetts Burma Law “has more than an 
indirect or incidental effect in foreign countries” and a “great 
potential for disruption or embarrassment.” Pet. App. 81. The 
sole evidence of “disruption or embarrassment” cited by the 
district court was the withdrawal of three companies from Burma 
and protests against the law filed by the European Union, the 
Government of Japan, and the Association of South East Asian 
Nations. Pet. App. 81-82. The district court did not reach the 
claim by the NFTC under the Foreign Commerce Clause. /d. at 
86. On November 17, 1998, the court entered final judgment. 

Petitioners timely appealed to the court of appeals, which 
affirmed. The court of appeals first noted that the parties 
“agreed” that “human rights conditions” in Burma “are 
deplorable.” Pet. App. 5. The court acknowledged that the 
“passage of the Massachusetts Burma Law has resulted in 
significant attention being brought to the Burmese government's 
human rights record.” Pet. App. 73. “Indeed,” the court said, “it 
may be that the Massachusetts law was a catalyst for federal 
sanctions.” /d The court nevertheless held that (1) “the law 
interferes with the foreign affairs power of the federal 
government”; (2) the law represents regulation, not market 
participation, and violates the Foreign Commerce Clause; and (3) 
the law “violates the Supremacy Clause because it is preempted 
by federal sanctions against Burma.” Pet. App. 5. 


2. Facts 
A. The Massachusetts Burma Law 


The Massachusetts Burma Law was enacted in July, 1996, 
Mass. St. 1996, c. 130, and entitled “An Act Regulating State 
Contracts with Companies Doing Business with or in Burma 
(Myanmar).” M.G.L.A.c. 7, §§ 22G-M. According to the United 
States Department of State, the “people of Burma continue to live 
under a highly authoritarian military regime that is widely 
condemned for its serious human rights abuses.” Ist Cir App. 
(hereafter “R.””) 336. In the months following enactment of the 
Massachusetts Burma Law in 1996, the United States, the 
European Union, Japan, and other countries imposed economic 
sanctions on Burma. Jt. App. 74-75, 78-81; R. 338, 342-48. See 
also United States Department of State, Burma Country Report 
on Human Rights Practices for 1998 (February 26, 1999). 

The Burma Law limits the procurement of goods and 
services by Massachusetts state agencies from persons who do 
business with Burma. It does not prohibit private investment in 
Burma; it does not prohibit private trade in goods and services in 
Burma; it does not govern the distribution of private assets or 
estates; it does not restrict foreign travel. It expressly applies to 
all Massachusetts and other United States companies, as well as 
foreign companies, doing business in Burma and seeking 
procurement contracts from the Commonwealth of 
Massachusetts. 

The Burma Law effectively requires state agencies to 
increase by 10 percent the price of offers to sell goods and 
services to the Commonwealth submitted by companies that do 


6 


business with Burma. M.G.L.A. ch. 7, § 22H(d). Subject to 
certain exceptions, the Burma Law provides that “a state agency, 
a state authority. the house of representatives or the state senate 
may not procure goods or services from any person listed on the 
restricted purchase list maintained by the secretary [of 
administration and finance], or who is determined through 
affidavit or through other reliable methods to meet the criteria for 
so being listed.” M.G.L.A. ch. 7, § 22H(a). By its terms, the 
statute imposes “preference requirements” (§ 221) rather than an 
absolute prohibition on state contracting with companies doing 
business with Burma. Thus, exceptions are authorized when the 
procurement is “essential” and enforcement of the restriction 
would “eliminate the only bid or offer, or would result in 
inadequate competition{,]” § 22H(b)(1) and (2); when the State is 
purchasing certain kinds of medical supplies, § 221; or when there 
is “no comparable low bid or offer” by a bidder who is not on the 
list. § 22H(d). A “comparable low bid or offer” is defined as “a 
responsive and responsible bid or offer which is no more than ten 
percent greater than the lowest bid or offer submitted for goods 
or a service.” § 22G. In practice, the “comparable low bid or 
offer” provision is applied by adding 10 percent to offers made 
by persons determined to be doing business with Burma. Pet. 
App. 7-8. 

Section 22J(a) provides that the “secretary shall establish and 
maintain a restricted purchase list . . . [which] shall contain the 
names of all persons currently doing business with Burma 
(Myanmar).” “Doing business with Burma” is defined by § 22G 
as: “(a) having a principal place of business, place of 
incorporation or its corporate headquarters in Burma (Myanmar) 
or having any operations, leases, franchises, majority-owned 


7 


subsidiaries, distribution agreements, or any other similar 
agreements in Burma (Myanmar), or being the majority-owned 
subsidiary licensee or franchise[e] of such a person; (b) providing 
financial services to the government of Burma (Myanmar), 
including providing direct loans, underwriting government 
securities, providing any consulting advice or assistance, 
providing brokerage services, acting as a trustee or escrow agent, 
or otherwise acting as an agent pursuant to a contractual 
agreement; (c) promoting the importation or sale of gems, timber, 
oil, gas or other related products, commerce in which is largely 
controlled by the government of Burma (Myanmar), from Burma 
(Myanmar); (d) providing any goods or services to the 
government of Burma (Myanmar).” Under sections 22H(e) and 
221, persons with operations in Burma limited to the “reporting 
the news” or providing goods or services “for the provision of 
international telecommunications,” or “providing only medical 
supplies” are exempt from the restrictions of the Act. 

The Act directs the Secretary of Administrationand Finance 
(the Secretary), “[i)n establishing the restricted purchase list,” “to 
consult United Nations reports, resources of the Investor 
Responsibility Research Center and the Associates to Develop 
Democratic Burma, and other reliable sources.” § 22J(b). “The 
Secretary shall also place the name of any person [on the list] 
who, in the statement [submitted to state agencies under section 
22H], declared that he meets the criteria for being so listed.” /d. 


B. Federal Legislation Concerning Burma 


Three months after Massachusetts enacted its Burma Law, 
Congress authorized federal sanctions against Burma. See Pet. 


8 


App. 10-12. The Omnibus Consolidated Appropriations Act of 
1997 (the Federal Burma Law), Pub. L. No. 104-208, § 570, 110 
Stat. 3009-166 through 3009-167 (Section 570), approved 
September 30, 1996, imposed conditional sanctions on Burma 
“{uJntil such time as the President determines and certifies to 
Congress that Burma has made measurable and substantial 
progress in improving human rights practices and implementing 
democratic government. . . .” § 570(a). The sanctions include an 
end to most bilateral assistance (§ 570 (a)(1)) and the opposition 
by the United States to most multilateral aid. /d. at § 570(a)(2). 

Section 570 also authorizes and directs the President “to 
prohibit . . . United States persons from new investment in 
Burma, if [he] determines and certifies to Congress that, after the 
date of th{e] Act, the Government of Burma has physically 
harmed, rearrested for political acts, or exiled Daw Aung San Suu 
Kyi or has committed large-scale repression of or violence against 
the Democratic opposition.” Jd. at § 570(b). “New investment” 
is defined by the Act to include “the economical development of 
resources located in Burma” but does “not include the entry into, 
performance of, or financing of a contract to sell or purchase 
goods, services or technology.” /d. at § 570(f)(2). 

The Act further directs the President to “seek to develop, in 
coordination with members of the Association of Southeast Asian 
Nations (ASEAN) and other countries having major trading and 
investment interests in Burma, a comprehensive, multilateral 
strategy to bring democracy to and improve human rights 
practices and the quality of life in Burma, including the 
development of a dialogue between the State Law and Order 
Restoration Council (SLORC) and democratic opposition groups 
within Burma.” /d. at § 570(c). 


9 


Finally, the Act requires the President to submit reports 
every six months to certain Congressional committees on: 
“(1) progress toward democratization in Burma; (2) progress on 
improving the quality of life of the Burmese people . . .; and (3)

SUMMARY OF ARGUMENT 


Introduction. For more than two hundred years, citizens of 
Massachusetts and other States have used boycotts to support the 
“natural, essential, and unalienable rights” of people around the 
world. See Mass. Const. of 1780, Pt. 1, art. 1 (West 1997 ed.). 
The Massachusetts Burma Law closely resembles private 
boycotts and the scores of divestment and selective purchasing 
laws enacted by state and local governments in the 1980s 
concerning South Africa. The Massachusetts law is also similar 
to many recent state and local laws and resolutions concerning 
Northern Ireland, Cuba, and Switzerland -- laws and resolutions 
now at risk in light of the broad ruling of the court below. 


Nothing in our federal Constitution denies to the States the 


1] 


right to apply a moral standard to their spending decisions. Not 
one constitutional grant, prohibition, or command requires the 
States to trade with dictators. In an era of global procurement and 
trade, state spending decisions will inevitably rile foreign 
countries and even risk embarrassment to the United States. 
These commonplaces, however, do not support constitutional 
judgments against selective purchasing laws. State laws that 
threaten national interests may be addressed by Congress, “whose 
voice, in th[e] area [of foreign affairs], is the Nation’s.” Barclays 
Bank PLC v. Franchise Tax Board, 512 U.S. 298, 331 (1994). 
Absent clear and valid preemption of state law by Congress, the 
States should be -- and are -- free to choose their own vendors 
and indirectly seek to “secure the blessings of liberty” around the 
world. U.S. Const., Preamble. 

I. The Federal Burma Law does not preempt -- but rather 
implicitly permits -- state and local selective purchasing laws 
regarding Burma. The Court should presume that the state law is 
valid, notwithstanding the foreign subject of the federal law. The 
presumption of validity is supported by the cases of this Court. 
the historical importance of the state spending power, and the 
close similarity between the state law and indisputably valid 
private boycotts. [Pp.15-17] 

Whatever presumption applies, there is no basis for a finding 
of preemption. Recent actions and inaction by Congress show 
that the Federal Burma Law does not impliedly preempt state and 
local selective purchasing laws concerning Burma. Congress was 
aware of state and local Burma laws in 1996 but took no action to 
preempt them. The absence of a preemption clause is especially 
telling here in light of the scores of similar laws enacted in the 
1980s and Congress’s failure to preempt them. The same federal 


12 


legislative history shows that Congress has impliedly permitted 
state and local laws regarding Burma. While the court below 
noted that “it may be that the Massachusetts law was a catalyst 
for federal sanctions,” it failed to appreciate the significance of its 
statement: the strongest evidence of implicit permission present 
here and absent in Barclays is the fact that Congress later enacted 
a law on the same subject but did not expressly preempt state law. 
[Pp. 17-21] 

Even if Congress has not implicitly permitted the state law, 
there is no actual conflict between the state and federal laws that 
would support a finding of preemption. The state law is 
consistent with the federal purpose: to promote human rights in 
Burma. The federal law does not create a scheme so 
comprehensive as to infer conflict from the mere differences 
between state and federal law. Nor do these differences alter a 
“balance” struck by Congress or frustrate the purpose of the 
later-enacted federal law. [Pp. 21-24] 

Il. Whether or not Congress has implicitly permitted the 
Burma Law, it is constitutional because the law represents market 
participation, not regulation, and therefore is not subject to claims 
under the dormant foreign commerce or foreign affairs powers. 
The constitutional basis for the market participation exception 
applies equally to these powers. The express limits on state 
action affecting foreign affairs imposed by Article I, § 10, read in 
light of the Tenth Amendment, further show that the Framers did 
not intend to impose unstated limitations on proprietary powers 
that might have foreign effects. [Pp. 25-29] 

Selective purchasing laws such as the Burma Law represent 
market participation, not regulation. The laws represent the right 
to boycott exercised by private persons -- including NFTC 


13

federal and state Burma laws further show that the state law is 
proprietary and the federal law regulatory. Whatever its 
aspirations for change abroad, the state law seeks to disassociate 
the Massachusetts government and its tax dollars from the denial 
of human rights in Burma. This valid local purpose further shows 
that the procurement law at issue is market participation, not 
regulation. [Pp. 29-36] 

Ill. The court of appeals held that the Massachusetts law 
“interferes with the foreign affairs power of the federal 
government.” The Court need not reach this question: it may 
decide that selective purchasing is market participation and 
therefore not subject to a claim under the dormant foreign affairs

the dormant foreign affairs power and that Congress has 
implicitly permitted state and local selective purchasing laws.

If the Court reaches the merits of the claim under the foreign 
affairs power it should reject it. The structure and terms of the 
Constitution require the Court to read Zschernig v. Miller, 389 
U.S. 429 (1968), narrowly to permit the indirect effects of 
selective purchasing laws. In any event, the state law satisfies 
even Zschernig because its effects are indirect, not direct; remote, 
not immediate; and contingent, not inevitable. Whatever the 
aspirations of a selective purchasing law, its center of gravity lies 
at home, not abroad, and its effects are distinguishable from the 
direct effects of the regulation of foreign inheritances. The

A. The Market Participation Doctrine 
Applies to Claims under Both the 
Dormant Foreign Commerce and

Whether or not Congress has implicitly permitted the Burma

participation, not regulation, and is therefore not subject to claims 
under the dormant foreign commerce and foreign affairs powers. 

The Court has primarily applied the market participant 
exception to claims under the domestic Commerce Clause. See, 
e.g., White v. Massachusetts Council of Construction Employers, 
Inc., 460 U.S. 204, 214-15 (1983) (rejecting Commerce Clause 
challenge to City of Boston requirement that at least 50% of the 
private workforce on all construction projects funded wholly by 
the city must be Boston residents); Reeves, Inc. v. Stake, 447 U.S. 
at 436-37 (upholding state law prohibiting sale of state-produced 
cement to non-state residents); Hughes v. Alexandria Scrap

participation where there is no “direct state involvement in the

has reserved the question whether the exception applies to the 
Foreign Commerce Clause. See Reeves, 447 U.S. at 438 n.9. The 
court of appeals saw this question as one of policy, “weighing the 
risks inherent in state regulation of foreign commerce ....” Pet. 
App. 49-50. The Court should reject this approach and rule that 
the history and structure of the dormant foreign commerce and 
foreign affairs clauses do not nullify proprietary state action. 

“The historical evidence no more suggests that the 
Commerce Clause was intended to limit the ability of states to 
purchase goods (including securities) and services in the 
marketplace when their operations indirectly affect foreign 
commerce than it indicates such an intent when their operations 
affect domestic commerce.” 10 Op. Off. Legal Counsel at 72-73 
(summarizing historical evidence). The primary purpose of the 
Foreign Commerce Clause -- itself a grant of regulatory power -- 
was to enable Congress to address problems created by state 
regulation of foreign commerce, particularly state tariffs. Jd. 
There is nothing in the history of that clause that suggests that it 
was intended, sub silentio, to displace state proprietary activity. 
See Brief of North Dakota, ef al., amici curiae. 

This conclusion is supported by the other constitutional 
bases for the market participation exception. The market 
participation doctrine rests on a “healthy regard for federalism 
and good government” and the constitutional “restraint . . . 
counseled by considerations of state sovereignty ... .” 
Reeves, 447 U.S. at 437-38, 441. Indeed, in Reeves, the Court 
noted that it was no part of the “constitutional plan to limit the 
ability of states themselves to operate freely in the free market.”

commerce has brought the world to the States’ doorsteps, the 
Constitution -- through the market participation exception -- 
allows the States to turn the more suspect peddlers away. 

The market participation doctrine likewise applies to the 
claim that the Burma Law interferes with the ability of the federal 
government to conduct foreign affairs. As explained below in 


Argument III, Zschernig v. Miller, 389 U.S. 429 (1968), remains - 


the “only case in which the Supreme Court has struck down a 
state statute as violative of the foreign affairs power of the federal 
government.” Pet. App. 30. Zschernig applied the doctrine to an 
Oregon law that regulated the distribution of private estates to 
nonresidentaliens. Nothing in the opinion of the Court addressed 
State proprietary activity. 

The historical basis for the federal foreign affairs powers 
does not imply the displacement of state proprietary power. The 
Framers were primarily concerned with congressional control 
over the exercise of state regulatory power affecting foreign 
affairs -- particularly tariffs. See The Federalist No. 22, at 144 
(C. Rossiter ed. 1961); id. No. 23 at 153. They also expressly 
denied to the States the right to undertake actions -- such as 
treaties or acts of war -- long associated with international 
sovereignty. Article I, § 10. These express limitations on state 
action in Article I are significant. “In view of the Tenth 
Amendment’s reminder that the powers not delegated to the 
Federal Government nor prohibited to the States are reserved to 
the States or to the people, the existence of express limitations on 
State sovereignty may equally imply that caution should be 
exercised before concluding that unstated limitations on state 


29 


power were intended by the Framers.” Alden, 119 S. Ct. at 2259 
(quotation omitted). None of the actions expressly prohibited by 
Article I addresses proprietal action. In light of the historical 
basis of the foreign affairs and foreign commerce powers, the 
Court should apply the market participant doctrine to the claims 
under both powers in this case. See 10 Op. Off. of Legal Counsel 
at 84-86 (“We do not think the [Zschernig] principle should be 
extended to state proprietal action”); Trojan Technologies, Inc. v. 
Commonwealth of Pennsylvania, 742 F. Supp. 900, 903 (M.D. 
Pa.), aff'd, 916 F.2d 903, 909-13 (3d Cir. 1990) (applying market 
participation doctrine to foreign commerce [district and circuit 
courts] and foreign affairs [district court] claims against “Buy 
American” laws), cert. denied, 501 U.S. 1212 (1991). 


B. Selective Purchasing Is Market 
Participation: It Represents the Right 
to Boycott Enjoyed by Private Persons 
for Two Centuries. 


Selective purchasing laws such as the Burma Law represent 
market participation, not regulation. These laws represent the 
same right to boycott exercised by private persons long before 
and after the adoption of the federal Constitution in 1787. 

Selective purchasing laws have a long pedigree. In the New 
England and other colonies, the founding generation considered 
the nonimportation boycott to be a wise alternative to war. See 
Charles McLean Andrews, The Boston Merchants and the Non- 
Importation Movement 17, 33-34, 40-41, 43, 55 (1968); James 
Gray Pope, Republican Moments: The Role of Direct Popular 
Power in the American Constitutional Order, 139 Pa. L. Rev. 


30 


287, 330-35 (1990). More recently, the Court has reaffirmed its 
view that private boycotts have historical roots in rights protected 
by the First Amendment. See NAACP v. Claiborne Hardware 
Co., 458 U.S. at 907. Recent laws on South Africa, Burma, and 
Northern Ireland maintain these long private and public traditions 
See Pet. App. 10; Jt. App. 144-56; Board of Trustees, 562 A.2d 
at 749-53 (divestment ordinance is market participation). 
Meanwhile, private companies have repeatedly conducted their 
own “selective purchasing.” Voluntary “codes of corporate 
conduct” and corporate withdrawals from Burma -- many of 
which criticize the conduct of foreign governments or firms -- 
show that Massachusetts has acted as private actors can and do 
act. See Debora L. Spar, The Spotlight and the Bottom Line -- 
How Multinationals Export Human Rights, 77 Foreign Affairs 
(March/April 1998) at 7; United States Dep’t of Labor, The 
Apparel Industry Codes of Conduct: A Solution to the 
International Child Labor Problem? (1996) at 8, 13 n.16 
(“Clinton Administration [has] encouraged United States 
corporations and organizations to develop their own voluntary 
codes of conduct for their foreign operations”); 155,157 (NFTC 
members have established codes of conduct and withdrawn from 
Burma, citing human rights concerns) (cited excerpts at R. 389, 
391 n.16, 402-03, 411-12, 413-14); Jt. App. 225 § 48 (NFTC 
stipulates that its members have “directly encouraged legal 
reforms in foreign countries” and have “asked foreign 
governments to change their laws and practices concerning child 
labor’). 

The important differences between the federal and state 
Burma laws further show that the state law is proprietary and the 
federal law regulatory. The Federal Burma Law bans new 


31 


investment by private persons. It does not govern federal 
procurement. Private companies are not free to avoid the federal 
ban on new investment: the federal law represents the exercise of 
the sovereign power uniquely enjoyed by government to compel 
private action under the threat of punishment. 

In contrast, the Massachusetts law governs only the 
procurement of goods and services by the State -- and thus a 
market created solely by State purchasing. See College Savings 
Bank v. Florida Prepaid Postsecondary Education Expense 
Board, 119 S. Ct. 2219, 2230 (1999) (market participant 
exception applies where State is. “buying and selling in the 
market”). It does not prohibit or compel private action: every 
domestic and foreign company is free to continue to do business 
with Burma. The limits on public purchases imposed by the law 
are therefore proprietary activity exempt from the constitutional 
claims in this case. See Laurence H. Tribe, Constitutional Law 
(1988 ed.) § 6-22, p. 469 ( a state “opposed” to foreign regime 
may not -- absent congressional authorization -- bar businesses 
from its jurisdiction or ban trade by its citizens, but may adopt 
selective purchasing laws). 

Despite the obvious differences between the state and federal 
laws, the court below deemed the state law an attempt to regulate 
because it “pursue[s] goals that are not designed to favor its 
citizens or to secure local benefits.” Pet. App. 46. This ruling 
ignores an important purpose of the state law. While one purpose 
of the Burma Law is indirectly to encourage change in Burma, 
that is not its only purpose. The law is also intended to 
disassociate the Massachusetts government and its tax dollars 
from the denial of human rights in Burma. See Jt. App. 51 
(remarks of Sen. Walsh) (prior to enactment of selective 


32 


purchasing law on South Africa, Massachusetts had become a 
“party and unwitting conspirator” to human rights violations); 52 
(prior to enactment of Northern Ireland law, Massachusetts had 
“subsidiz[ed] bad practices through [its] contracts”): see also Jt. 
App. 197 (Secretary of State opines that “state and local 
officials” have “right” and “responsibility” to apply “moral 
considerations” to their “procurement policies”). There is no 
question on this record that the Massachusetts Legislature would 
have enacted the Burma Law even if it believed that it could not 
effect change in Burma. Even if the Court believes that State or 
local governments may not constitutionally encourage change in 
foreign countries, selective purchasing laws must be upheld 
against a facial attack because they are a valid exercise of the 
right to disassociate from a repugnant regime. In light of this 
valid local purpose, the Court should deem the Burma Law 
market participation immune from this facial attack. See City of 
Renton v. Playtime Theatres, Inc., 475 U.S. 41, 47-48 (1986) 
(“Court will not strike down an otherwise constitutional statute on 

the basis of an alleged illicit legislative motive”; “what motivates 

one legislator to make a speech about a statute is not necessarily 

what motivates scores of others to enact it”); see also South- 

Central Timber v. Wunnicke, 467 U.S. 82, 101-103 (1984) 

(Rehnquist, J., dissenting) (conditions on state sale of timber are 

market participation where state could accomplish same end by 

indisputably valid means). 

The state need not show an economic benefit to itself or its 
citizens in order to establish that a selective purchasing law is 
proprietary activity. A private boycott on purely altruistic 
grounds is no less private, and possibly more worthy of protection 
under the First Amendment, than a profit-motivated boycott. 


33 


Similarly, the Court has held state action to be proprietary even 
where the stated or obvious purpose of the state law -- or its effect 
-- was to influence private conduct and thus achieve goals 
unrelated to local economic self-interest. See Hughes, 426 U.S. 
at 814 (environmental concerns). Thus, in Hughes and Reeves, 
“{the States] did not enter a presumably unmanipulable market 
with the profit-maximizing goals implied in the phrase ‘market 
participation.’ Instead, the States intended their entrances to 
affect the flow of commerce so as to enhance public values -- 
improvement of the environment or increased supply of a needed 
product at the expense of the state as a business.” Laurence H. 
Tribe, Constitutional Choices (1985) at 144-46 (emphasis in 
original). Market participation may promote public values 
broader than local economic benefits because the doctrine rests on 
principles of federalism as well as an analogy to private conduct. 
See Reeves, 447 U.S. at 438. 

Nothing in Wunnicke suggests that the Burma Law is a 
regulatory measure. In Wunnicke, a plurality of the Court held 
that the market participant doctrine did not protect a 
discriminatory restriction imposed by Alaska, which required 
purchasers of state-owned timber to process the logs within the 
State. The plurality reasoned that this additional restriction on 
private activity reached beyond the immediate market in which 
the State was participating -- the sale of its timber -- and that for 
purposes of applying the market participant doctrine, the 
“market” must be “relatively narrowly defined.” 467 U.S. at 97- 
98. However, the plurality also emphasized that the State was 
attempting to impose an additional downstream restriction, going 
beyond what sellers, who in the ordinary course of commercial 
endeavor have no continuing interest in the product once it is 


34 


sold, traditionally do. /d. at 98-99. 

In contrast, the Massachusetts state government is the 
ultimate purchaser of all goods and services covered by the 
Burma Law, and purchasers traditionally do “decide with whom 
they will deal,” even if that decision indirectly affects secondary 
upstream markets. Indeed, given that White accepted an upstream 
restriction on whom private contractors can hire (460 U.S. at 
208), a selective purchasing law could not possibly be invalid 
simply because it might affect secondary upstream markets. Like 
the Baltimore divestment ordinance and the “Buy American” 
laws, the Burma Law places no “restriction on private economic 
activity [which] takes place after the completion of the parties’ 
direct commercial obligation.” Board of Trustees, 562 A.2d at 
752 (quoting South Central Timber, 467 U.S. at 99). Like the 
restriction in White, the Burma Law imposes a narrow condition 
precedent on companies who are competing for state contracts 
and thus “cover[s] a discrete, identifiable class of economic 
activity in which the State is a major participant.” 460 U.S. at 
211 n.7. Its terms do not “reach beyond the immediate parties” 
to state procurement, and its indirect effects, if any, do not 
“figure[]” in “equation” used to judge whether the law is 
regulation or market participation. /d. at 210. 

Nor do the cases decided under the National Labor Relations 
Act (NLRA), 29 U.S.C. § 51 ef seq., cited by the court below, 
restrict the scope of the market participationexception here. Each 
of the cases concerned the scope of the preemption of the NLRA, 
not the scope of the Constitution. “The market participant 
doctrine reflects the particular concerns underlying the Commerce 
Clause, not any general notion regarding the necessary extent of 
State power in areas where Congress has acted.” Wisconsin 


35 


Dep't of Industry v. Gould, 475 U.S. 282, 289 (1982)(emphasis 
added). Emphasizing that “what the Commerce Clause would 
permit States to do in the absence of the NLRA is thus an entirely 
different question from what the States may do with the Act in 
place,” the Court held that in enacting the NLRA, Congress 
intended to prohibit the States from interfering in any way with 
the “interrelated federal scheme of law, remedy, and 
administration.” Jd. (citations omitted). Eleven years later, the 
Court distinguished Gould, noting that a “State does not regulate 

. . simply by acting within [a federally] protected area{].” 
Building & Constr. Trades Council v. Associated Builders & 
Contractors, 507 U.S. 218, 227 (1993). 

The court below relied on a single sentence in Gould, which 
stated that “for all practical purposes, Wisconsin’s debarment 
scheme is tantamount to regulation.” /d. at 289; see Pet. App. 47. 
But this sentence merely shows that the Court viewed the 
Wisconsin statute as a “regulation” because it specifically linked 
the State’s decisions to violations of the broad regulatory 
scheme erected by the NLRA, and punished companies for past 
violations of the federal regulatory law. The Court viewed the 
law as “an attempt to compel conformity with the NLRA.” 
Building & Constr. Trades Council, 507 U.S. at 228. The 
Massachusetts law, in contrast, does not depend for its operation 
on reference to a federal regulatory scheme. Unlike the 
Wisconsin scheme, the Burma Law does not disqualify 
companies from eligibility for state contracts on the basis of past 
actions, but rather makes their continuing, unrestricted eligibility 
for contracts subject to certain conditions with which they can 
prospectively comply. Thus, the Burma Law does not operate 
like the statute in Gould, but rather like the condition on the 


36 


procurement of construction services in White. See Gould, 475 
U.S. at 289 (reaffirming White); 10 Op. Off. of Legal Counsel at 
77-79 (distinguishing Gould); 

For these reasons, the Court should hold that the Burma 


Law is not subject to review under the foreign commerce or 
foreign affairs powers. 


Ill. SELECTIVE PURCHASING LAWS DO NOT 
INTERFERE WITH THE CAPACITY OF THE 


UNITED STATES TO CONDUCT FOREIGN 
RELATIONS. 


A. The Court Need Not Reach the Foreign 
Affairs Power Claim If It Holds That 
the Burma Law Is Market Participation 
or Implicitly Permitted by Congress. 


The court of appeals held that the Massachusetts law 
“interferes with the foreign affairs power of the federal 
government.” Pet. App. 5, 14-41. Citing Zschernig v. Miller, the 
court said that the “Massachusetts law presents a threat of 
embarrassment to the country’s conduct of foreign relations 
regarding Burma” and therefore “has more than an “incidental 
effect” on foreign affairs. Pet. App. 27. 

The Court need not reach this question: it may decide that 
selective purchasing is market participation and therefore not 
subject to a claim under the dormant foreign affairs power. 
Alternatively, the Court may decide that Congress has implicitly 
permitted state and local selective purchasing laws and Barclays’ 
standard of implicit Congressional permission for action 


37 


challenged under the Foreign Commerce Clause applies equally 
to a claim under the dormant foreign affairs power. Under 
Barclays, express Congressional approval of a state law affecting 
foreign affairs is not required for the Court to hold a state law 
constitutional: while the federal government must have the 
“capacity” to “speak with one voice” on foreign affairs when the 
national interest so requires, “Congress may more passively 
indicate that certain state practices do not ‘impair federal 
uniformity in an area where federal uniformity is essential;’ . . . 
it need not convey its intent with the unmistakable clarity 
required to permit state regulation that discriminated against 
interstatecommerce....” Jd. at 323 (emphasis in original). This 
more deferential rule for state laws affecting foreign policy 
reflects the fact that the “nuances” of the “foreign policy of the 
United States are much more the province of the Executive 
Branch and Congress than of th{e] Court. . . .” Barclays, 512 
U.S. at 327, and the “judiciary is not “vested with power to 
decide how to balance” the effects of a state law on foreign 
relations. /d. at 328. Under this principle of judicial restraint in 
the area of foreign affairs, the Court “leave[s] it to Congress -- 
whose voice, in this area, is the Nation’s -- to evaluate whether 
the national interest is best served by uniformity, or state 
autonomy.” /d. at 331. 

This deferential standard should apply to claims under 
Zschernig as well. Claims under Zschernig -- like the claims 
under Barclays -- essentially allege that state or local action 
unconstitutionally interferes with the conduct of foreign policy. 
Regarding both dormant powers, Congress may authorize 
implicitly what the negative force of the grant of federal power 
might otherwise forbid. Accordingly, if the Court holds that 


38 


Congress has implicitly permitted the Burma Law, it should reject 
both the dormant foreign commerce and foreign affairs power 
claims. If, on the other hand, the Court reaches these claims, it 
Should hold that selective purchasing laws such as the 
Massachusetts Burma Law do not interfere with capacity of the 
United States to conduct foreign relations. 


B. The Structure and Terms of the 
Constitution Require the Court to 
Read Zschernig Narrowly to Permit 
the Indirect Effects of Selective 
Purchasing Laws. 


Zschernig remains “a unique statement and a sole 
application of constitutional doctrine.” Pet. App. 30 (quoting 
Louis Henkin, Foreign Affairs and the Constitution (1996 ed.) at 
165); see id. at 162 (“until 1968, there was no hint of such a 
principle”), 165 n.; Jack L. Goldsmith, Federal Courts, Foreign 
Affairs, and Federalism, 83 Va. L. Rev. 1617, 1641-64 (1997). 

Zschernig did not concern selective purchasing or 
divestment. In Zschernig, the Oregon statute at issue regulated 
the terms under which a nonresident alien could inherit property 
from an Oregon decedent. The state law prohibited inheritance 
by a nonresident alien unless three conditions were satisfied: (1) 
the alien’s government must accord Americans the right to inherit 
on equal terms with its citizens; (2) the alien’s government must 
give Americans the right to receive payment in the United States 
of funds from foreign estates; and (3) the nonresident alien must 
be able to receive “the benefit, use or control” of the proceeds of 
the Oregon estate “without confiscation” by his government. The 


39 


Court concluded that this type of probate law as enforced in the 
Oregon courts had “a direct impact on foreign relations and may 
well adversely affect the power of the central government to deal 
with those problems.” Jd at 441. The Court stressed that the 
extensive record of enforcement of the statute by the Oregon 
courts showed a “great potential for disruption or 
embarrassment’ in the conduct of foreign relations: the Oregon 
courts had repeatedly criticized the administration of foreign laws 
by foreign officials, questioned their credibility, and denied 
foreign citizens private inheritances based on “foreign policy 
attitudes” toward particular governments. See 429 U.S. at 435 
(emphasis added). 

Zschernig did not overrule Clark v. Allen, 331 U.S. 503 
(1947), which upheld the facial validity of a California statute 
similar to the first two sections of the Oregon law. Although the 
California statute in Clark was clearly designed to influence 
foreign countries to change their laws to allow Americans to 
inherit, the Court dismissed the constitutional claim against the 
law as “farfetched.” Jd at 517. Emphasizing that “rights of 
succession” were peculiarly a matter of local law, the Court 
agreed that “[w]hat California has done will have some incidental 
or indirect effect in foreign countries” but concluded “that is true 
of many state laws which none would claim cross the forbidden 
line.” Jd (emphasis added). Neither Zschernig nor Clark 
addressed the exercise of public spending powers -- only the 
regulation of private inheritances by foreign citizens.°

similarly concern the regulation of private activity, not public

The court below stated that the “precise boundaries” of

“some degree of state involvement in foreign affairs is inevitable”

and inevitably impinged on United States foreign relations.” Pet. 
App. 18 (quoting Henkin, supra, at 162). This trend is now a 
truism: increased foreign trade and global communication and 
transportation have married the States to foreign firms and 
countries, for better or worse, leaving “the line between national 
and state and local concerns . . . much less clear than when the 
Republic was founded.” Bilder, 83 Am. J. Int’] Law at 828. 

The court below asked whether the Massachusetts law fell 
within “the boundaries of permissible state activity in the foreign

as to threaten state and local divestment laws, resolutions, and

The structure and terms of the Constitution show why 
Zschernig must be cabined to its facts. The Constitution does not 
use the terms “foreign relations,” “foreign policy,” or “foreign 
affairs.” Instead, it grants certain powers to the federal 
government and denies certain powers to the States, reserving to 
the States in the Tenth Amendment the powers not granted or 
denied. Article II, § 2, makes the President Commander in Chief 
of the armed forces of the United States and authorizes him to 
enter into treaties and to appoint and receive ambassadors. 
Congress is given authority to regulate foreign commerce, raise 
and support armies, define offenses against the law of nations, 
and to declare war. Art.I, § 8 The powers granted to the federal 
government under Articles I and II, when exercised, are made 
plenary through the Supremacy Clause, Art. VI, § 2. 

Other parts of the Constitution affirmatively show that the 
Framers expected that state action would sometimes affect 
foreign affairs. U.S. Const. Art. I, § 10 (authorizing agreements 
between States and foreign countries and certain military actions 
with consent of Congress); Art. III, § 2 (jurisdictionto hear cases 
between States and foreign parties). Instead of a blanket 
prohibition on such contact, the Framers denied to the States 
specific powers, e.g., to engage in war, enter treaties, and levy 
duties on imports or exports. Art. I, § 10, cls. 1-3. The Framers 
likely prohibited these actions because they had been restricted by 
the Articles of Confederation, long associated with international 
sovereignty,and posed unacceptablerisks to the effective conduct 
of foreign affairs. See Goldsmith, 83 Va. L. Rev. at 1643-44 & 
n.121. None of the state actions prohibited by Art. I concerns a 
proprietary power such as the purchase of goods and services or 


42 


, the management of state investments. Rather, these proprietary 
powers are “reserved to the States, or the people” by the Tenth 
Amendment. 

“The most natural inference from these provisions and from 
the Constitution’ s enumerated powers structure is that all foreign 
relations matters not excluded by Article I, Section 10 fall within 
the concurrent power of the state and federal governments until 
preempted by federal statute or treaty.” Jd. at 1642. While the 
Court in Zschernig apparently did not draw the same inference, 
the structure and terms of the Constitution show that Zschernig 
must be read with “caution,” to invalidate only state actions that 
reflect acts of international sovereignty and create the same 
degree of risk to national security as the actions prohibited by 
Article I. See Alden, 119 S.Ct. at 2259. 

In this case, nothing in the Burma Law enters a treaty, 
engages in war, levies duties on imports or exports, or conducts 
direct relations with Burma. Like the statute in Clark and unlike 
the statute in Zschernig, the implementation of the Burma Law 
does not require repeated decisions by state officials about the 
operation of Burmese law or the credibility of Burmese officials. 
Jt. App. 217 (Stipulation § 21). The general statements critical of 
Burma made by Massachusetts supporters of the Burma Law do 
not render the law unconstitutional because Zschernig does not 
foreclose all “actions involving substantive judgments about 
foreign nations”; rather, “a single general decision” about a nation 
is “beyond the scope of Zschernig.” Board of Trustees, 562 A.2d 
at 745-46. In short, the procurement of goods by state or local 
government -- by its nature -- is not an act of international 
sovereignty that poses an unacceptable risk to the security of the 
United States. Cf United Building & Constr. Trades v. Mayor 


43 


and Council of City of Camden, 465 U.S. 208, 221 (1984)(city’s 
expenditure of “its own funds” may be “crucial factor” in 
defeating claim under Privileges and Immunities Clause). 

The Massachusetts law further satisfies Zschernig because 
its effects are indirect, not direct; remote, not immediate; and 
contingent, not inevitable. While some companies have 
withdrawn from Burma, citing the Massachusetts law, other 
companies have left because of the denial of human rights there. 
R. 362, 367. As for the NFTC’s own view of the effects of the 
law, it has elsewhere argued that “unilateral” governmental action 
has had “no success” in any country and “only makes] a 
statement without any impact.” Jt. App. 86. To be sure, selective 
purchasing laws are enacted in part in the hope that they -- when 
combined with similar efforts worldwide -- might prompt a 
foreign government to respect human rights. Whatever the 
aspirations of such laws, however, their center of gravity lies at 
home, not abroad, and their effects -- if any -- are indirect and 
distinguishable from the direct effects of the regulation of alien 
inheritances, let alone the effects of a treaty, tariff, or blockade. 
For these reasons, the Court should read Zschernig narrowly and 
hold that the Massachusetts Burma Law is constitutional. 


Cc. The Complaints of Foreign Countries 
under a Trade Agreement Do Not 
Establish Unconstitutional Interference 
with the Conduct of Foreign Relations. 


Nor do the complaints by foreign countries under trade 
agreements establish that the Burma Law creates “a great 
potential for disruption and embarrassment” to the conduct of the 


ae 


foreign relations of the United States. Pet. App. 20, 25, 27. 
Barclays rejected reliance on the “disapproval” of “many of our 
trading partners,” (512 U.S. at 324 n.22), for good reason: on the 
Stage of international politics, the drama never ceases. and each 
season is full of conflicts, conspiracies, protests, and threats. 
None of these perennials, however, should influence a United 
States court to nullify the democratic action of the citizens of a 
State. See Barclays, 512 U.S. at 320 (“battalion of foreign 
governments that has marched to [plaintiff's] aid, [through] 
diplomatic notes, amicus briefs, and even retaliatory legislation 

. [lacks] such force as to dictate thfe] Court’s Commerce 
Clause jurisprudence”); see id. at 327-328 (even state action 
“likely to provoke retaliatory action by foreign governments” is 
not unconstitutional); id at 324 n. 22 and 337 (O'Connor. J.. 
concurring in judgment). 

Judicial reliance on mere trade complaints poses a 
substantial threat to state legislative action. The United States has 
entered a new generation of agreements governing international 
trade and procurement. See North American Free Trade 
Agreement (NAFTA) Implementation Act, P.L. 103-182, 107 
Stat. 2057 (1993), 19 U.S.C. §§ 3301 et seq.; Uruguay Round 
Agreement Act of 1994 (URAA). P.L. 103-465, 108 Stat. 4809, 
19 U.S.C. § 3501 et seq. Under these agreements, Congress has 
purported to subject hundreds of state laws to review by 
international bodies. Agreement Establishing the World Trade 
Organization (WTO), Annex 1B, art. 1, 33 I.LM. 1144 (1994), 
1169; 1994 U.S.C.A.A.N. at 4052; 19 U.S.C. § 3312 (NAFTA). 
Foreign countries have targeted dozens of state laws -- including 
subsidies, health and safety standards, environmental standards. 
and taxation -- for attack. See European Commission, Report on 


45 


Barriers to Trade and Investment in the United States (1999). 
These attacks will multiply if United States courts continue to 
rely on mere trade complaints as the basis for constitutional 
judgments against state laws. 

In this case, the European Union and Japan have challenged 
the Massachusetts Burma Law under the GATT at the World 
Trade Organization (WTO). Pet. App. 10, 20, 24. The United 
States Trade Representative (USTR), however, has pledged a 
strong defense of the Massachusetts Burma Law at the WTO. R. 
425; Jt. App. 206, 82 (unanimous Massachusetts congressional 
delegation objects to Japanese complaint and urges USTR to 
defend law as “internal affair{] of Massachusetts”). In these 
circumstances, the risk of retaliation by other countries under 
GATT is speculative. If the Executive Branch wishes to avoid 
retaliation and end foreign complaints -- and the minimal 

“embarrassment” alleged in this case -- it may bring a judicial 
action against the State under the URRA. Since it has determined 
not to do so, the judgment of the court below actually undercuts 
the power of the Executive by relying on trade complaints that the 
Executive has determined to resist. 

Congressional action further shows that the foreign 
complaints should be ignored in this case. Congress expected 
that state laws could be challenged at the WTO under GATT, but 
it denied a private right of action to enforce GATT in the courts 
of the United States. See 19 U.S.C. §§ 3512(b(2)(A),3512(c (1): 
1994 U.S.C.A.A.N. at 4055; Arg. I.C, above. The same Congress 
would be surprised to learn that complaints under GATT now fuel 
constitutionalclaims. Furthermore,Congressin 1996 presumably 
discounted the risk of foreign retaliation when it enacted 
unilateral sanctions against Burma and left state and local laws 


46 


undisturbed. Thus the record of private and governmental actions 
in this case shows that there is no “great potential for disruption 
and embarrassment” found by the Court in Zschernig in 1968. 

Despite these factors, the court below expressly based its 
constitutional holdings on the complaints made by the European 
Union and Japan. Pet. App. 24-27. The court’s reliance on trade 
complaints -- inevitable rows in an era of global procurement -- 
grants to foreign countries and firms a “heckler’s veto” against 
state laws. Absent reversal by this Court, foreign complaints 
under NAFTA and GATT will increasingly fuel constitutional 
claims against divestment and selective purchasing laws, “Buy 
American” laws, environmental regulations, and other state laws. 
See Brief for Non-Profit Organizations, Amici Curiae; Brief for 
Comptroller of the City of New York, et al., Amici Curiae. These 
claims will sap the lawmaking powers of the States and unduly 
enlarge the role of the courts, casting the judiciary -- 
inappropriately -- as the “overseer of our government.” Barclays, 
512 U.S. at 330. The Court should reject this role and instead 
defer to the powers of Congress “to reduce to a minimum the 
frictions that are unavoidable in a world of sovereigns sensitive 
in matters touching their dignity and interests.” Perez v. 
Brownell, 356 U.S. 44, 57 (1958), overruled on other grounds, 
Afroym v. Rusk, 387 U.S. 253 (1967). 


IV. THE BURMA LAW DOES NOT 
VIOLATE THE FOREIGN 
COMMERCE CLAUSE. 


The court of appeals also ruled that the Burma Law violates 
the Foreign Commerce Clause, in that it “facially discriminates 


47 


against foreign commerce,” “interferes with the ability of the 
federal government to speak with one voice,” and “attempts to 
regulate conduct beyond its borders.” Pet. App. 50-57. The court 
also found that “Massachusetts has failed to put forth a legitimate 
local justification in support of its law.” Pet. App. 57-59. 

If the Court holds that the Burma Law represents market 
participation or that Congress has implicitly permitted the law, 
the Court must reject the claim under the Foreign Commerce 
Clause. Even if the Burma Law is subject to review under that 
clause, however, the Court should reject the claim on its merits. 

First, the Burma Law applies equally to firms domiciled in 
Massachusetts, other States, and foreign countries. Whether a 
firm is based in Burma or Boston, it is subject to the price 
preference imposed by the law. The law is _ therefore 
distinguishable from laws that discriminate against firms 
domiciled outside the United States. See, e.g, Kraft General 
Foods, Inc. v. lowa Dep't of Revenue, 505 U.S. 71, 73-77 (1992). 

The law is also distinguishable from state laws that “intend 
to secure economic advantages for local businesses at the expense 
of businesses situated elsewhere.” Board of Trustees, 562 A.2d 
at 754 (divestment ordinance concerning South Africa does not 
discriminate). Even a state law that singles out a foreign country 
does not violate the Foreign Commerce Clause unless it 
discriminates “in favor of in-state interests.” /d. at 754 n. 56 
(citations omitted). The Massachusetts law clearly does not. 

Even if protectionist intent need not be shown, however, a 
criterion based on commerce with a particular country is not 
unconstitutional because the “[Foreign Commerce] Clause 
protects the inter[national] market, not particular inter[national] 
firms” and does not prohibit a state law that “causes some 


48 


business to shift from one inter[national] supplier to another.” 
Exxon Corp. v. Governor of Maryland, 437 U.S. 117, 127 (1978) 
(interpreting domestic Commerce Clause). Even if the Burma 
Law causes some state business t _.ift from one international 
supplier to another, this effect would not show unconstitutional 
discrimination. Thus, for example, if Massachusetts were 
concerned that state purchases from a particular country might 
increase the smuggling of heroin onto Massachusetts streets, it 
could decline to purchase goods from that country. Since the 
Burma Law does not discriminate against foreign commerce as a 
class or favor in-state or United States interests, it does not 
discriminate in violation of the Foreign Commerce Clause. 

Nor does the law have an unconstitutional “extraterritorial 
effect.” See Pet. App. 55-56. As shown above in Arg. III.B, the 
effects of the Burma Law are indirect, not inevitable. It does not 
“impose economic sanctions for violations of its laws” or 
“regulate conduct wholly beyond its borders.” /d The 
deferential approach of Barclays counsels that the Court should 
not extend the “extraterritorial effect” principle of the domestic 
Commerce Clause to state laws touching foreign affairs. 

In any event, the Court should uphold the law because it has 
a legitimate local purpose: to disassociate Massachusetts from the 
human rights violations in Burma. The court below doubted 
whether an “expression of moral outrage” is a sufficient local 
interest to satisfy the Foreign Commerce Clause. Pet. App. 58. 
However, the long history of boycotts in aid of human rights 
shows that the moral statement made by the Burma Law is a 
product of tradition and reason and promotes values embodied in 
the First Amendment. See First National Bank v. Bellotti, 435 
U.S. 765, 776-77 (1978) (First Amendment promotes a 


49 


democratic society by protecting free debate of public issues); Vt. 
J.H.R. 157 (resolution on conditions in Burma); Vt. Acts of 1999, 
c. 13 (directions to public pension funds regarding shareholder 
resolutions on Burma); Ordinance of Oakland, CA (April 23, 
1996) (Jt.App. 156) (divestment ordinance concerning Burma). 
Accordingly, the Court should reject the NFTC’s view that state 
and local resolutions concerning foreign policy are 
unconstitutional (see Jt. App. 232-33) and hold that the moral 
statement embodied in the Massachusetts Burma Law satisfies the 
requirement of a legitimate local purpose under the Foreign 
Commerce Clause. 

This moral statement in support of freedom -- freedom from 
dictators and forced commerce with their vendors -- does not 
interfere with the ability of ihe federal government to “speak with 
one voice” in foreign affairs. See Barclays, 512 U.S. at 320 
(quoting Japan Line, Ltd. v. County of Los Angeles, 441 U.S. 434, 
453 (1979)). The court of appeals read this metaphor literally, to 
warrant the courts to stifle all dissent to national policy, and not 
merely local actions that are contrary to national policy. Barclays 
and Container Corp. of America v. Franchise Tax Board, 463 
U.S. 159 (1983), however, show that the “one voice” test never 
literally forbade multiple voices but rather certain types of state 
actions concerning foreign affairs. The statements embodied in 
selective purchasing and divestment laws “encourage[] ordinary 
citizens, through the government closest to them, to participate 
more meaningfully in the formation and carrying out of foreign 
policies that deeply affect their lives.” Bilder, 83 Am. J. Int’l 
Law at 831. Multiple voices are not per se unconstitutional 
because they do not affect the capacity of the Congress to 
establish uniformity when the national interest so requires. 


50 


Where “Congress is aware of state activity to restrict 
procurement. . . and yet has not yet imposed a policy of national 
uniformity . . . state procurement policy fits comfortably within 
the . .. Court's observation that nothing in the Foreign Commerce 
Clause insists the Federal Government speak with any particular 
voice.” Trojan Technologies, 916 F.2d at 912. Under Barclays, 
the Court has left it to Congress -- “whose voice, in this area, is 
the Nation's -- to evaluate whether the national interest is best 
served by . . . uniformity, or state autonomy.” /d. at 331. The 
court of appeals erred in making its own evaluation of the 
national interest here. 


CONCLUSION 


For the reasons stated above, the Court should reverse the 
judgment of the United States Court of Appeals for the First 
Circuit.
