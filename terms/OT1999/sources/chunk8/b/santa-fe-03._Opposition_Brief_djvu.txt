STATEMENT OF THE CASE 


This Statement of Case is somewhat restricted in that 
it essentially addresses misstatements found in the Peti- 
tioner’s Statement of the Case. The Respondents, Jane 
Does, each mothers of school age children, brought suit 
in the district court below complaining with respect to 
certain practices of the Petitioner school district. These 
practices included the Petitioner, Santa Fe Independent 
School District (“SFISD”), allowing students to read over- 
tly Christian prayers from the stage at graduation cere- 
monies and over the public address system at home 
football games. Jane Doe, et al. v. Santa Fe Indep. School 
Dist., 168 F.3d 806, 810 (5th Cir. 1999). The prayers were 
delivered as “invocations” or “benedictions” for these 
events, and typically were given by officers of the student 
council. Id. SFISD maintained complete control over the 
programs and facilities during the reading of the prayers, 
including the ability to mute the microphone or remove 
the speaker. Jd. Furthermore, the text of the graduation 
invocations and benedictions was screened by SFISD for 
content prior to the ceremony. /d. 


The first misstatement found in Petitioner's State- 
ment of the Case is seen in the following statement to this 
Court, “[t]he questions presented here arise from the 
Respondents’ complaints that two of Santa Fe ISD’s poli- 
cies permitted religious content in speech by students at 
certain school events.” Petition for Certiorari at p. 2. The 
statement is incorrect in that Respondents filed suit in the 
district court below with respect to the actual practices of 
the district. The school district developed the policies at 
issue only after the district court ordered the Petitioner to 
“finalize a unified First Amendment religion/expression 


policy addressing all issues.” Jane Doe, et al. v. Santa Fe 
Indep. School Dist., supra at 812. 


In response to the court’s order, SFISD presented 
graduation and football prayer policies that were two- 
tier: the first-tier was to allow prayer with no require- 
ment that the prayer be “nonsectarian and nonproselytiz- 
ing.” The second-tier, or the fall back position, was one 
that was to take effect if the district court was to reject 
their desire to have prayer that was nonsectarian and 
nonproselytizing. If the district court would reject the 
first-tier, then and only then, the district would insert the 
words “nonsectarian” and “nonproselytizing” in their 
graduation and football policies. It is clear that the dis- 
trict policies were directed at the Fifth Circuit’s previous 
ruling in Jones v. Clear Creek Indep. School Dist., 977 F.2d 
963 (5th Cir. 1992), cert. denied, 508 U.S. 967 (1993) (“Clear 
Creek II”). 


Curiously, the Petitioner omits from its Statement of 
Facts all of the Fifth Circuit’s findings as to the historical 
context in which the district adopted their policies and 
the district’s intent in developing those policies, when in 
fact many of the Fifth Circuit's holdings were based on 
these findings. For example, in analyzing the secular 
purpose prong of the Lemon test, the Fifth Circuit found 
the school district's true intent was to encourage sec- 
tarian and proselytizing prayers. The court correctly con- 
cluded that the district’s two-tier policies could only be 
found to be an “attempt to encourage sectarian and pros- 
elytizing prayers —- a purpose which is the antithesis of 
secular.” Jane Doe, et al. v. Santa Fe Indep. School Dist., 
supra at 816. The court also stated, “Our cynicism about 


the school board’s proffered secular purpose is gal- 
vanized by SFISD’s inclusion of the fall-back alternative 
that would re-insert the twin restrictions ipso facto 
should the district court invalidate the basic provision of 
the July policy.” Id. at 817. 


The Fifth Circuit also looked to the intent of the 
school district as it related to whether it had created a 
public forum at graduation or football games: 


SFISD attempts to evade the requirements of the 
Establishment Clause by running for the protec- 
tive cover of a designated public forum. We 
must, therefore, view skeptically SFISD’s own 
self-serving assertion of its intent and examine 
closely the relationship between the objective 
nature of the venue and its compatibility with 
expressive activity. 


Id. at 820. 


Similarly, in reviewing the practices of the district 
and the context in which their policies were developed, 
the court found that, “[t]he context of the evolutionary 
history in which SFISD developed its series of prayer 
policies further confirms the school district’s penumbral 
religious purpose.” Id. at 816. Furthermore, the Fifth Cir- 
cuit characterized the Respondents’ proof in the district 
court as: “evidence that the Does were able to accumulate 
covered a wide variety of disturbing incidents and prac- 
tices.” Id. at 809-810. The Fifth Circuit also took note of 
the attempts by SFISD administrators, teachers, and other 
employees “overtly or covertly to ferret out the identities 
of the Plaintiffs by means of bogus petitions, 
questionnaires, individual interrogation, or downright 
‘snooping.’ ” Id. at 809, n.1. 


The Petitioner also misstates what and why the Fifth 
Circuit rejected its attempt at structuring a football 
prayer policy. On page 5 of the Petitioner’s Petition for 
Certiorari the following is found: 


The Fifth Circuit held that this policy violates 
the Establishment Clause because it permits 
prayer at a school-sponsored athletic event 
before a “government-organized audience.” 


In fact, the Fifth Circuit was far more restrictive and quite 
clear in its rejection of the school district’s policies: 


Thus, as we indicated in Duncanville [70 F.3d 402 
(5th Cir. 1995)], our decision in Clear Creek II 
hinged on the singular context and singularly 
serious nature of a graduation ceremony. Out- 
side that nurturing context, a Clear Creek 
Prayer Policy cannot survive. We, therefore 
reverse the district court’s holding that SFISD’s 
alternative Clear Creek Prayer Policy can be 
extended to football games. 


Id. at 823. 


And finally the Respondent overstates the Fifth Cir- 
cuit’s ruling when it submits, “[t}he court further held, 
however, that no student prayers are permitted at other 
school-sponsored functions and that Santa Fe ISD’s foot- 
ball game student speech policy violated the Establish- 
ment Clause because it theoretically allowed prayer as 
one of the types of speech a student might deliver.” 
Petition for Certiorari at p. 3. The statement is incorrect in 
that this issue was never broached in the trial court 
below. The only policy that was presented by the Peti- 
tioner to the district court was that of football and gradu- 
ation ceremonies. The application of this distinction is 


seen when the Petitioner seeks to have this Court review 
“other school functions” in the Questions Presented 
(Question 1), when the record is silent as to any other 
policy being subjected to review.

ARGUMENT 
REASONS FOR DENYING THE WRIT 


The school district’s petition for certiorari in this case 
rests on two flawed premises. First, the school district 
argues that the decision below demonstrates a conflict 
among the circuits on the constitutional rules governing 
student-led and student-initiated prayer at public school 
graduation ceremonies and football games. Second, the 
school district argues that the Fifth Circuit’s reference to 
overtly sectarian and proselytizing prayer at either grad- 
uation or football games is inconsistent with this Court's 
Establishment Clause jurisprudence. Neither claim has 
merit; neither warrants plenary review. 


I. PETITIONER MISCHARACTERIZES THE RELE- 
VANT LOWER COURT DECISIONS. 


The school district’s assertion of a circuit conflict 
rests principally on the Eleventh Circuit's recent decision 
in Chandler v. James, 180 F.3d 1254 (11th Cir. 1999). While 
hardly a model of clarity, Chandler does not go nearly as 
far as Petitioner suggests in allowing school prayer so 
long as it is student run and student initiated. 


At the outset, it is important to note that the district 
court in Chandler struck down an Alabama statute permit- 
ting nonsectarian, nonproselytizing student-initiated 
prayer, invocations and benedictions during compulsory 
or non-compulsory school-related assemblies, sporting 
events, graduation ceremonies and other school-spon- 
sored events. Chandler, supra at 1256. The Eleventh Cir- 
cuit’s ruling on the constitutionality of the statute was 
not appealed and is entirely consistent with the Fifth 
Circuit's approach in football games and hardly supports 
the school district’s contention in the case that students 
should be allowed to engage even in sectarian and pros- 
elytizing prayer at graduation. 


The issue that was appealed to the Eleventh Circuit 
in Chandler was whether the district court’s injunction 
represented an appropriate remedy for the uncontested 
(on appeal) constitutional violations. The Eleventh Cir- 
cuit’s decision vacating the injunction was based on its 
highly debatable conclusion that the injunction prohib- 
ited all student prayer in school (including, for example, 
a student’s whispered prayer prior to a math exam). Even 
so, the Eleventh Circuit agreed with the Fifth Circuit that 
“[pjroselytizing speech is inherently coercive and, the 
Constitution prohibits it from the government's pulpit.” 
Id. at 1265. 


Petitioner also cites Adler v. Duval County School 
Board, 174 F.3d 1236 (11th Cir. 1999), where an Eleventh 
Circuit panel declared unconstitutional a school policy 
that permitted graduating seniors to select a class speaker 
who was then free to deliver a prayer at graduation. 
However, that decision has since been vacated and the 
case has been scheduled for rehearing en banc. Adler v. 


Duval County School Board, 1999 U.S. App. LEXIS 13190 
(11th Cir. June 3, 1999). Likewise, Petitioner relies on the 
Ninth Circuit decision in Doe v. Madison School District, 
147 F.3d 832 (9th Cir. 1998), which was vacated as moot 
following remand from this Court. Doe v. Madison School 
District, 177 F.3d 789 (9th Cir. 1999). Vacated decisions do 
not create a circuit conflict. 


Il. THE FIFTH CIRCUIT CORRECTLY REJECTED 
THE SCHOOL DISTRICT’S POSITION THAT THE 
CONSTITUTION PERMITS OVERTLY SEC- 
TARIAN PRAYER AT SCHOOL-SPONSORED 
FUNCTIONS. 


The school district in this case challenges the Fifth 
Circuit's holding that the use of the public address sys- 
tem for “student-initiated” prayers prior to football 
games is unconstitutional even if the prayer is nonsec- 
tarian and nonproselytizing, while student-initiated 
prayer at graduation ceremonies is constitutional only if 
it is nonsectarian and nonproselytizing. 


Petitioner describes the holding as inconsistent, and 
it may well be. Throughout this litigation, Respondents 
have made clear their disagreement with the Fifth Cir- 
cuit’s rule, first announced in Jones v. Clear Creek Indepen- 
dent School District, supra, that student-initiated 
graduation prayer is constitutional so long as it is nonsec- 
tarian and nonproselytizing. However, Petitioner does 
not seek a reaffirmation of Clear Creek II. Petitioner chal- 
lenges Clear Creek II because it does not go far enough, 
because it does not permit students to engage in overtly 


sectarian prayer at graduation ceremonies. The Fifth Cir- 
cuit properly rejected that view, which is plainly inconsis- 
tent with this Court’s Establishment Clause juris- 
prudence. 


In recent cases, the Court has emphasized that one of 
the important functions of the Establishment Clause is to 
ensure that individuals not be made to feel like outsiders 
in their own community because of their religious views. 
See County of Allegheny v. American Civil Liberties Union, 
Greater Pittsburgh Chapter, 492 U.S. 573, 628 (1989) 
(O’Connor, J., concurring). This Court has also recognized 
that school children are especially vulnerable to such 
feelings because they are inherently susceptible to peer 
pressure, and that it is inherently coercive to put graduat- 
ing seniors in the position of submitting to that pressure 
or skipping graduation. See Lee v. Weisman, 505 U.S. 577, 
593 (1992). The fact that graduation prayer (or prayer 
before football games) is led by students, does not dimin- 
ish the pressure to religious conformity, if anything, it 
may increase it. And the fact that a prayer is overtly 
sectarian only magnifies the message that there is one 
truth faith. 


As the Fifth Circuit correctly perceived, therefore, 
this case is not about speech in a genuine public forum. 
Jane Doe, et al. v. Santa Fe Indep. School Dist., supra at 
819-821. It is about a school policy deliberately designed 
to circumvent Lee through the use of student surrogates 
to deliver prayers before a captive audience that school 
officials can no longer deliver themselves. The effect of 
such a policy, at the very least, is to promote religion in 
violation of the Establishment Clause. Lemon v. Kurtzman, 
403 U.S. 602 (1971). As a result, it is not surprising that 


Petitioner cannot point to a single case that goes as far as 
they now ask this Court to go. 


There is no reason to grant certiorari in this matter.
