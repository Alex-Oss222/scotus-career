Dear Representative Hodgkins and Senator Tolman: 


I am writing you in support of H. 2833, an Act to Regulate 


30 


State Contracts With Companies Doing Business in Burma. Its 
passage would add our state to the various governments 
supporting the restoration of democracy in Burma. 


In the free elections in Burma in 1990, the National League for 
Democracy led by Nobel Peace Prize laureate, Aung San 
Suukyi, received 80 percent of the votes cast. Since that time 
Suukyi has been under house by the martial administration law 
known as “SLORC” (State Law and Order Restoration 
Council). This military junta has committed well-known 
human rights violations including the systematic murder, rape, 
and torture of civilians, and drug trafficking. According to the 
Burma Rights Movement for Action Group, “SLORC” has 
managed to survive through revenues generated by foreign 
investments and by a market for the export of the nation’s 
natural resources. Burton Levin, the Former U.S. Ambassador 
to Burma, explains that “foreign investment in most countries 
act as a catalyst to promote change, but the Burmese Regime is 
so single minded that whatever they might obtain from foreign 


sources they pour straight into the army while the rest of the 
country is collapsing.” 


H.2833 establishes a “selective purchase” program for state 
vendors doing business in Burma. It is based on the “selective 
purchase” program used so effectively by this state to put 
pressure on the pro-apartheid government of South Africa. The 
Secretary of Administration and Finance would compile a list 
of businesses that do business in Burma. Persons with 
operations in Burma for the sole purpose of reporting news, 
providing international telecommunications, and disseminating 


31 


medical supplies would be exempt. Otherwise companies on 
the list would be awarded a contract only if their bids are 10% 
or less than the bids of companies not doing business in Burma. 
Goods and services could also be procured from a company 
when a state agency certifies to the Secretary of Administration 
and Finance that the procurement is vital to the operations of 
the state agency. 


Considering the deterioration of human living conditions in 
Burma, where military generals have stolen an entire country’s 
resources and population through force, the Commonwealth of 
Massachusetts should not provide contracts to companies that 
do business with this dictatorship government. The 
Commonwealth has a history of assisting fledgling, democratic 
movements throughout the world. Burma calls on our support 
now. 


The new South Africa demonstrates that economic pressure can 
be effective in moving governments away from oppression. 
Continued pressure from Massachusetts is necessary to 
vigorously combat well documented repression and intolerance 
in Burma. 


I enclose recent news articles on the situation in Burma and on 
Reebok’s and Liz Claiborne’s decisions to cut ties with the 
SLORC government. 


Yours truly, 
/s/ 
Byron Rushing

WELD, CELLUCCI SIGN BURMA BILL 
First State in Nation to Impose Economic Sanctions 


Governor William Weld and Lieutenant Governor Paul 
Cellucci today signed the Burma Bil! placing Massachusetts in 
the lead of a growing nation-wide campaign to impose 
economic sanctions on Burma’s tyrannical regime. 


“One law passed by one state will not end the suffering and 
oppression of the people of Burma, but it is my hope that other 
states and the Congress will follow our example, and make a 
stand for the cause of freedom and democracy around the 
world,” said Weld. 


“Due to a steady flow of foreign investments, including those 
of some US companies, this brutal military regime has been 
able to supply itself with weapons and portray itself as the 
legitimate government of Burma. Today is the day that we call 
their bluff,” said Cellucci. 


The bill prohibits the Commonwealth from procuring goods or 
services from firms that do business in Burma. Under the new 
law, a list of restricted companies would be maintained by the 
Executive Office of Administration and Finance. 


“Enactment of this legislation is a major breakthrough in 


 Burma’s struggle for freedom,” said Zarni, a Burmese native 


and executive director of the Free Burma Coalition, responding 


58 


to Weld’s bill signing. “Federal legislation is currently being 
debated, and Governor Weld’s action at the state level sets an 
excellent example for other states and the Congress.” 


Since seizing control in 1988, the military regime of Burma has 
held the country in an authoritarian stranglehold. Abuses have 
included: arbitrary arrest and detention, forced labor, torture, 
and murder. In 1989, the regime arrested Nobel Peace prize 
winner Aung San Suu Kyi, an outspoken leader of the pro 
democracy movement and advocate of an economic boycott 
against Burma. In addition, the military party voided the 1990 
elections, after gaining only 2.1 percent of the legislative seats, 
and arrested hundreds of the democratically-elected officials. 


Massachusetts is the first state to enact a selective purchase law 
against Burma, but several cities have taken similar action, 
including: Berkeley, CA; Santa Monica, CA; San Francisco, 
CA; Oakland, CA; Madison, WI; and Ann Arbor, M I. 


“This bill gives hope to the people in Burma who are currently 
being oppressed and silenced,” added Zarni.

Reviewing Bidders For Burma Connections - The Burma 


Statute -Application Of The Restricted Purchase List To 
Procurements 


In passing the Burma Statute in 1996 (Massachusetts General 
Law Chapter 22 Sections 22G-M), the Commonwealth, 
through its Legislative and Executive Branches. expressed its 
clear preference against doing business with those entities that 
do business in Burma (also named Myanmar). This is due to 
the violations of human rights by the government of Burma. 


To accomplish this as it applies to our procurement activities, 
OSD has established and maintains a restricted purchase list as 
required by the Statute listing those entities which are doing 
business in Burma. This list is available on the OSD 
Homepage at http://wwwmagnet.state.ma.us/osd. This list is 
updated quarterly by OSD. 


In all procurements, the Procuring Department should check 
the list to determine which Bidders are on the list. The 
Responses (bids) of those Bidders that are on the list are then 
modified by adding a premium of 10% to their Response and 
the resulting modified Response is used as the basis for 
comparing Responses. Only if the Response with the 10% 
premium added still provides Best Value in comparison to 
other non-listed entities will it be considered for the contract 
award.

However, if the entity which is listed as doing business in 
Burma does get the contract award, the prices originally bid 
shall form the basis of the contract, not the premium-added 
Response. If any Bidder insists that it should not be on the 
restricted purchase list, they should be referred to OSD Legal 
staff for appropriate action.

The European Commission presents its compliments to the 
Department of State with reference to the Act Regulating State 
Contracts with companies doing business with or in Burma 
(Myanmar) enacted on 25 June 1996 by the Commonwealth of 
Massachusetts. [1996 Mass. ALS 130, 1996 Mass. Adv. Legis. 
Serv. 130; 1996 Mass. H.B. 2833]. 


This legislation forbids Massachusetts’ state agencies, state 
authorities, the House of Representatives or the State Senate to 
sign new contracts or renewals of existing contracts with 
companies doing business with or in Burma. 


The European Community is of the opinion that the above 
mentioned legislation is a breach of the rules for procurement 
procedures as stipulated in the Agreement on Government 
Procurement (GPA). Firstly, it violates Article VIII(b) of the 
GPA, given that it imposes conditions on a tendering company 
which are not essential to ensure the firms capability to fulfill 
the contract. Secondly, it infringes Article X(3) of the GPA, 
because it imposes qualification criteria based on political, 
rather than economic considerations. Thirdly, it is in 
contradiction to Article XIII (4) of the GPA to the extent that 
the statute allows the award of contracts to be based on 
political instead of economic considerations. 


When concluding the GPA, the US accepted to cover the state 
of Massachusetts under its offer of sub-federal agencies in 
order to balance the EC's offer to the US. By limiting access of 


77 


European suppliers to the procurement of Massachusetts’ state 
agencies, state authorities, the House of Representatives or the 
State Senate, the USA is reducing its GPA offer. 


Furthermore, it appears that several other sub-federal 
authorities have adopted or are adopting legislation or 
ordinances with a similar effect. The state of Massachusetts is 
also considering similar measures with regard to another 
country besides Burma. We underline that these types of 
sub-federal actions reduce the US offer under the GPA or 
affect the way special or exclusive rights are attributed. 


The European Community requests the US government to 
transmit its concern to the State of Massachusetts. The 
European Community also wishes to know what action, if any, 
the US Government intends to take in response to the 
Massachusetts measure. Furthermore, the European 
Community fully reserves its rights under the WTO dispute 
settlement procedure. 

The European Community avails itself of this opportunity to 
renew to the Department of State the assurances of its highest

Use and Effect of Unilateral Trade Sanctions 


TESTIMONY FOR 

UNDER SECRETARY EIZENSTAT 
BEFORE THE 

TRADE SUBCOMMITTEE 

HOUSE WAYS AND MEANS 
OCTOBER 23, 1997 


Mr. Chairman and Members of the Committee, thank you for 
this opportunity to testify before you on the use and effect of 
unilateral trade sanctions. 


Mr. Chairman, the view that I would like to lay before you 
today is that the record is clear. Properly designed, 
implemented, and applied as part of a coherent strategy, 
sanctions, including economic sanctions, can be and are a 
valuable tool for enforcing international norms and protecting 
our national interests. In this age of increasing globalization 
we have turned more frequently to sanctions to deal with 
inappropriate or unacceptable behavior by states. We should 
improve our ability to use them effectively. They should be 
used only when carefully considered by both the Executive and 
Legislative branches as to their likely effectiveness, after all 


108 


benefits, costs, and consequences are analyzed, and after the 
full range of diplomatic and political options has been 
attempted to change the conduct in question by the target 
country or group. 


Sanctions, especially multilateral sanctions, have been 
effective. Iraq, Libya, former Yugoslavia, Haiti and others 
provide examples. But at the same time, a realistic appraisal of 
our experience has taught that sanctions are not a panacea and 
they are not cost-free. Our object must be to learn from our 
growing experience, to draw the appropriate lessons so that we 
can find the best, the most effective way to employ this 
potentially powerful tool while minimizing the adverse costs. 


Some things are clear. We should resort to sanctions only 
after other appropriate diplomatic options have been 
aggressively pursued and have failed, or would be inadequate. 
Sanctions are much more likely to be effective when they have 
multilateral support and participation. Multilateral sanctions 
maximize international pressure on the offending state while 
minimizing damage to U.S. competitiveness and more 
equitably distributing the sanctions burden across the 
international community. There are times, however, when the 
stakes are high, when important national interests or core 
values are at issue, that we must also be prepared to act 
unilaterally. Before imposing unilateral sanctions, serious 
thought must be given to all the potential 

ramifications.

Mr. Chairman, the general concept of sanctions is not new; 
the idea is as old as society itself. In their work "Economic 
Sanctions Revisited" authors Hufbauer, Schott and Elliott cite 
the imposition of a trade embargo by the Athenian leader 
Pericles on neighboring Megara in approximately 432 BC in 
retaliation for Megara's attempted expropriation of territory and 
the kidnapping of three women. In 1812 the United States 
imposed an embargo on Great Britain in retaliation for British 
attempts to limit US trade with France. In 1917 President 
Wilson imposed an embargo on the sale of iron, steel and other 
war essentials to Japan. President Roosevelt also imposed 
economic sanctions on Japan in 1940. I was personally 
involved in the use of sanctions against the former Soviet 
Union following its invasion of Afghanistan during my service 
in the Carter White House. So the use of sanctions in pursuit of 
foreign policy objectives is not new. 


We, however, are living in an age of heightened political 
and economic global integration and growing interdependence 
in which most countries derive their prosperity, and even 


110 


power, from growing engagement in the international 
economy. In such a world, sanctions are an important and 
potentially effective, if problematic, tool for enforcing 
international norms and standards of behavior, mediating not 
only between differing countries, but in the relationship 
between individual states and their own citizens as well. 
Sanctions are also a tool, however, which bring with 
themselves real costs as well as potential benefits. 


The frequency with which we turn to sanctions has increased 
dramatically, particularly since 1990. The U.S. has applied 
sanctions for foreign policy purposes a total of 115 times since 
World War I, 104 times since World War II, and according to 
the count of the President's Export Council, 61 times since. 
1993. Thus more than half the sanctions imposed in the past 
80 years have been imposed in only the past four years. These 
figures do not include the increasing use of sanctions at the 
state and local levels. 


In the UN, the Security Council imposed sanctions only twice 
between 1945 and 1990: Rhodesia and South Africa. Since 
1990, however, the UN has imposed mandatory sanctions on 8 
countries: Iraq for its invasion of Kuwait; Serbia for its use of 
force in former Yugoslavia; Libya in reaction to the PanAm 
103 massacre; Somalia and Liberia in their civil strife; Rwanda 
for genocide; Haiti for abrogation of democracy; and Angola 
during a renewed insurrection. Just within the past few weeks 
the UN has threatened further tightening of existing sanctions 
in UNITA, and imposed sanctions on the revolutionary junta 
in Sierra Leone. 


111 


Others have also resorted to sanctions. The EU, Japan and 
Canada have joined us in imposing sanctions on Burma. The 
EU is withholding aid from Kenya until that country deals with 
problems of corruption and rigged elections. The IMF has also 
moved to condition its lending to Kenya on serious efforts to 
deal with problems of pervasive corruption. The East African 
States imposed sanctions on Burundi, the Economic 
Community of West African States (ECOWAS) on Sierra 
Leone, and the Association of South-East Asian Nations 
(ASEAN) on Cambodia. 


Sanctions are used for a variety of purposes: 
- to punish a country for unacceptable behavior: 
- to influence the behavior of a target country: 
- to signal disapproval of a government's behavior: 
- aS anecessary early reaction and as a warning that 
harsher measures--even military--could follow; 
- to limit a target state's freedom of action; to deny 
resources or technology; 
- to increase the cost of engaging in unacceptable 
behavior; to draw international attention to unacceptable 
behavior; 
- to challenge our allies to take more forceful action 
themselves in support of common objectives; 
- fF at times, simply to signal that a business-as-usual 
approach to a government that violates core values is not 


acceptable. 


We also use sanctions in pursuit of a large number of policy 
objectives. These include such things as support for human 
rights, including workers and religious rights, promotion of 


i12 


democracy, to combat terrorism or the scourge of narcotics, 
in support of weapons of mass destruction and conventional 
non- proliferation or protection of the environment. 


We have available a broad array of policy measures. These 
include not only punitive or coercive economic measures but 
political and military steps as well. These may range from 
simple measures such as a change in the level and size of 
diplomatic missions to the ultimate sanction, application of 
military force, with many possible stops in between. The 
Secretary's Advisory Committee on International Economic 
Policy has developed an "Illustrative Matrix of Foreign Policy 
Tools” including possible diplomatic, political, cultural, 
economic and military measures ranging from friendly 
persuasion, including positive inducements, to hostile or 
coercive steps. This matrix offers a response ramp of measures 
from which one could select appropriate tools for seeking to 
change the behavior of states that violate international norms. 


We must be frank to recognize that in today's 

interdependent, global economy, the ability of the US to 
unilaterally deny key economic benefits to a target country is 
limited. Nevertheless, there are some measures which are not 
subject to foreign substitution, such as denial of a US quota, 
withdrawal of port privileges or landing rights, and actions in 
international financial institutions, consistent with their own 
rules, to withhold loans and assistance. 


In any event, it is not always easy for us to agree whether 
specific sanctions have been effective. How we judge the 
effectiveness of sanctions depends on the desired results-- 


113 


overthrow of a government (in which case anything other than 
the application of military force is likely to be ineffective by 
itself); changes in a regime's behavioral patterns by increasing 
the cost of doing business (by deferring investment, deterring 
trade and the like where multilateral sanctions are likely to be 
most effective); or simply as a means of expressing moral or 
other outrage at what we consider unacceptable behavior. 


There is no single, common standard against which to measure 
success or failure. The standards proposed by a firm dependent 
on sales to Burma (where we imposed targeted unilateral 
sanctions) would certainly differ from those applied by a 
committed human rights activist who oppose any interaction 
that might legitimize or strengthen the position of the 
authorities. The lower the expectations set for what sanctions 
are intended to accomplish, of course, the more likely the 
judgment is to be positive and, perhaps, the greater the 
willingness to bear the costs imposed on the country imposing 
sanctions. 


One thing is clear: sanctions are an important potential 

tool but are not a panacea and they are not without costs. 
Sanctions do offer, however, important levers for behavior 
modification and will continue to do so. There is one common 
thread: the sanctions which are most likely to be successful are 
multilateral ones in which a significant number of countries 
with economic and political clout have agreed. These also 
impose fewer burdens on the US business community since the 
companies of a significant number of countries will be required 
to forswear doing business with the target country.

Unilateral sanctions in particular also have real costs to US

Most of these studies reach similar conclusions and criticisms

sanctions, particularly unilateral economic sanctions, are often 
seen as undisciplined, poorly targeted and/or ineffective. 
Measures may be overly broad--like applying a meat cleaver 
where laser surgery would be more appropriate--and the 
consequences are not well thought through. Decisions to 
impose sanctions may be made with little or no analysis of the 
actual impact the proposed sanctions are likely to have on 
various groups within the target country, or on how economic 
pressure is likely to be translated into political pressure. 
Alternatives such as diplomatic measures, sometimes more 
effective, may not always be considered. 


Some also argue that the cost to the US economy and US

The President's Export Council, for example, recently

reliability of US suppliers and providing a competitive 
advantage to foreign competitors. Other studies have also 
pointed to lost exports and lost jobs, to cases where US 
components are specifically designed out of products because 
the producers do not wish to face the prospect of eventual 
restrictions on exports to particular markets, and to reports that 
foreign firms have intentionally switched R&D away from the 
US to Europe because of a desire to avoid sanctions problems.

the Middle East?

First, as a general rule, we should resort to sanctions only after 
other appropriate diplomatic options have beer aggressively 
pursued and have failed or would be inadequate. Sanctions are, 
after all, only one of many measures available to us, from 
symbolic measures like withdrawing an Ambassador, reducing 
Embassy staff, to denying visas to target figures, entering into 
security arrangements with neighboring countries, to military 
intervention and everything in between. We should also not 
forget the power of positive inducements--rewarding desired 
behavior. 


Second, a major objective of sanctions is to change behavior. 
That implies that a primary consideration in considering a 
sanctions regime must be whether or not the sanctions 
measures are indeed likely to be-effective in achieving that 

goal and whether they are part of an integrated strategy. 
Sanctions that are ineffective, that are easy to evade or avoid,

pointless in achieving our objectives, but in the longer run 
debase and undermine the value of sanctions as a potent foreign 
policy tool.

leverage. Whether we act alone or in concert with others, we 
should analyze before we penalize, studying in advance the

decision to impose sanctions. 


Fourth, sanctions are much more likely to be effective when 
they have multilateral support and participation. Multilateral 
sanctions maximize international pressure on the offending

they are multilateral, these sanctions regimes are also more 
difficult to evade or avoid, while minimizing damage to U.S. 
competitiveness and more equitably distributing the sanctions

instances when sanctions are a viable option, and given a

In conclusion, as the world becomes more interdependeni. as 
we move forward to establishing codes of acceptable 
international behavior and the will to enforce them, the 
effective application of sanctions will be a real asset. But it is 
no panacea. Thus it is all the more important that we undertake 
a rigorous analysis of sanctions before we impose them. We 
need to know not only how they will affect the target 
governments, but also how they affect vulnerable groups within 
the target countries. We also need to know how proposed 
sanctions affect US interests in other areas, including US 
business and workers, and how they affect our international 
partners on whose cooperation we depend to advance key 
foreign policy goals. 


Sanctions can be an effective tool to punish states that 

violate international norms and threaten vital US interests. And 
we must keep this tool at our disposal. But we need to work to 
sharpen our ability to use it effectively. One lesson is that 
taking time to forge a consensus both at home with the 
American people and abroad with our allies can greatly 
increase the effectiveness of sanctions and reduce the cost to 
the United States. Building such a consensus in many cases 
does not come quickly. However, it can help to avoid problems 
down the road while demonstrating to the offending state that 
our nation and the international community are united in

interests.

It is great to be back in California, my home state. | 
would like to discuss with you our shared interest in promoting 
U.S. national security, strengthening protection of human rights 
and enhancing U.S. competitiveness, export markets and jobs. 

Let me begin by saying a few words about exports, 
jobs and competitiveness. 

The world is growing increasingly interdependent. In 
1970, U.S. imports and exports were equivalent to 13 percent 
of gross domestic product (GDP). Now that figure is 30 
percent, up from 25 percent in 1992. The U.S. is the largest 
exporter in the world, exporting over $780 billion worth of 
goods and services in 1995. Much of this growth in exports is 
due to the performance of states like California. In the first 6 
months of 1997, goods exports from California were running at 
a rate of $107 billion -- up 45% since 1992--making California 
America's largest exporting state. If California were a separate 
nation, it would be one of the largest exporting countries in the 
world, placing it ahead of Mexico, Switzerland and Brazil. 

Due in part to our export performance, the U.S. ranked number 
one in competitiveness for three years in a row and the 
American economy continues to produce more jobs and more 
growth than any other major industrialized country. Our 
prosperity as a nation depends on our ability to compete in 
international markets. 95 percent of the world's consumers live 
outside of the U.S., many of them in dynamic, emerging

132 


markets in Latin America and Asia that are growing much 
faster than our iraditional export markets. We can benefit from 
their growth--but only if their markets are open to our products 
since our markets are already open to theirs. 

Opening markets is critical for creating high wage job 
opportunities for U.S. workers. Exports support roughly 11 
million American jobs. One million of these jobs are generated 
from agricultural exports like California grapes, beef and 
almonds. California is America's largest agricultural exporting 
state, shipping 12% of total U.S. agricultural exports. Today, 
1.3 million jobs in California are supported by trade -- an 
increase of 17% or 187,200 jobs since 1992. These trade 
related jobs are good ones, paying Californians an average of 
15 percent more than non-trade related jobs. 

America benefits from free trade that is also fair trade, 
and this Administration has worked hard to ensure that other 
countries live up to their word. We have frequently used the 
World Trade Organization's (WTO) dispute settlement system 
to enforce our rights. We have filed and won more dispute 
settlement cases in the WTO than any country in the world. 
We have benefitted from clearer rules on government 
procurement which has opened billions of dollars of 
procurement opportunities for U.S. firms and workers. 

Working toward creating a level playing field has 
benefitted California. For example, the Government 
procurement Agreement has enhanced access for U.S. 
companies. California-based companies or companies with 
facilities in California have won: a $130 million contract to 
build a satellite for Telenor, Norway's telephone company, and 
a $30 million contract to bring on line Europe's largest

windmill park in the North of the Netherlands. These 

companies are also in contention for other projects. For 
example, Hughes and Lockheed-Martin have bid to sell 
Telenor an additional satellite worth over $200 million. 

Now let me turn to the issue of sanctions. Economic 
sanctions are an important foreign policy tool intermediate 
between diplomacy and the use of force. They are used when 
other measures have been insufficient and when their 
imposition is likely to change the behavior of the offending 
State. 

Sanctions are not a "one-size-fits-all" foreign policy 
tool, however. When applying sanctions, if it has the requisite 
flexibility, the Administration designs a sanctions regime to 
target the vulnerabilities of the sanctioned country and target 
effectively the offensive behavior, while minimizing the 
damage to U.S. interests. 

Sanctions are intended to highlight misconduct by 
rogue regimes, and alter the behavior which threatens our 
national interests and the stability of the international 
community. Sanctions address misconduct in the areas of 
human rights, terrorism, narcotics, weapons of mass 
destruction, and other areas where such conduct is considered 
unacceptable by world standards. 

To maximize their effectiveness, we prefer sanctions 
which have multilateral support and participation wherever 
possible. Multilateral economic sanctions are not only more 
effective vis-a-vis the target, but they also show unity of 
international purpose, minimize damage to U.S. 
competitiveness. and distribute the sanctions burden across 
responsible countries. 


134 


However, we are prepared to use unilateral sanctions 
when important national interests are at stake. The Clinton 
Administration has at times employed sanctions to promote 
human rights or isolate rogue regimes. The president has 
banned new U.S. investment in Burma to spur the repressive 
regime in Burma. He signed the Liberated Act to prevent 
Cuba, the only non-democratic nation in our hemisphere, from 
profiting from investment in properties confiscated from 
Americans. He signed the Iran-Libya Sanctions Act to punish 
Iran and Libya for state-sponsored terrorism. Simultaneously 
with these actions, the Administration has launched vigorous 
diplomatic initiatives to encourage other countries to pursue 
our shared goals. 

While we recognize and share state governments’ 
frustration and often outrage at certain countries’ unacceptable 
behavior, we are concerned about the growing number of state 
and local measures and the message they send to the rest of the 
world. The President has made it clear that this Administration 
will challenge the unacceptable behavior of rogue states 
wherever they are in the world. The Secretary of State has told 
other countries that the U.S. is prepared to pay the cost of 
leadership and, if necessary, to go it alone when it comes to 
delivering a clear message of condemnation to these regimes. 
Secretary Albright has not pulled any punches -- as diplomats 
often do -- in expressing the United States ou’ ~ge at egregious 
violations of human rights and international norms in countries 
like Cuba, Burma and Iran. 

Our first concern is over whether these sanctions are 
effective in pursuing the goals we all share. As | said before, to 
be most effective, sanctions should have multilateral support 


135 


and participation wherever possible. While we are the largest 
economy and most important country, sanctions by the U.S. 
alone -- or certain states alone -- may not have the desired 
impact because they can be circumvented. Sanctions often are 
ineffective unless they are supported by other key countries 
around the world. 

We are also concerned about the impact of sanctions on 
our domestic economy. While sanctions serve an important 
foreign policy purpose, they are not without cost. The cost is 
measured in foregone economic opportunities by countries that 
put these measures in place. One private sector group 
estimates foregone opportunities for the U.S. alone in 1996 at 
$15-19 billion dollars in unrealized exports and the loss of over 
200,000 jobs in export-related sectors of our economy. 

Second, from the Department of State's perspective, we 
are concerned that state and local sanctions may impair the 
President's ability to send a clear and unified message to the 
rest of the world. As the world's only economic and military 
super power, the United States has the obligation to project a 
coherent and consistent message. We would like to work with 
you to try to send a clear message to oppressive regimes. But 
ad hoc and scattered actions at various levels of government, 
however well-intentioned, can do more harm than good in 
achieving the desired objective and impede the President's and 
Secretary of State's conduct of foreign policy. They also can 
create conflicts with our allies with whom we need to work to 
achieve our common goals. 

For example, a variety of state and local officials -- 
including in California -- recently began considering the 
imposition of various economic sanctions on Swiss banks and 


136 


other Swiss institutions in order to force an accounting for 
assets of Holocaust victims. Our goal is to provide justice for 
Holocaust victims, not to punish the Swiss people or their 
government. The U.S. Government has already issued a very 
hard-hitting report on the facts in the issue, and the Swiss have 
taken strong steps to account for the past. But now is the time 
for cooperation with the Swiss, not confrontation. According 
to Stuart Eizenstat, Under Secretary of State and the President's 
point person on this project, state actions at this time are 
counterproductive, particularly given positive steps the 
Government of Switzerland is taking to address the issue. Such 
actions threaten to harden Swiss public opinion against a 
referendum to create a multibillion dollar humanitarian fund. 
These sanctions efforts also complicate our government's 
ability to develop a healing and reconciliation process that 
helps uncover the truth. 

Third, if states do want to express concerns or 
viewpoints on particular human rights abuses or other 
objectionable behavior by foreign regimes, we would like to 
work with them to ensure that these actions complement U.S. 
foreign policy objectives and are consistent with our 
international treaties and agreements. As, | mentioned before, 
as the world's largest exporter, no country does better than the 
United States when countries follow internationally developed 
rules. At the same time, the United States must also play by 
the rules. If state and local sanctions are perceived to violate 
the rules, they can cause counterproductive disagreement with 
nations that share the same values and are otherwise inclined to 
follow our lead in pursuing our foreign policy goals.

Often state and local sanctions can have unintended 
effects that are counterproductive or divert attention from their 
original goal. In June 1996, Massachusetts enacted a law 
applying a price penalty on procurement from companies that 
have business operations in Burma. The European Union and 
Japan have initiated dispute settlement consultations in the 
World Trade Organization over this law, alleging that it 
violates U.S. obligations under the WTO Government 
Procurement Agreement. 

Our trading partners claim that the Massachusetts law 
violates provisions of the Government procurement Agreement 
which, among other things, requires that government contracts 
be awarded on the basis of objective economic considerations 
and precludes the application of political criteria. 

We are working with the Commonwealth of 
Massachusetts on this issue, just as we continue to work with 
the European Union, Japan and others to improve the situation 
in Burma. Let me be clear. We will defend the rights of the 
United States in the WTO dispute settlement process. But we 
must be honest in saying that the threatened WTO case has 
been an irritant and has diverted the United States’ and Europe's 
attention from focussing where it should be -- on Burma. 

Finally, I know that questions have been raised about 
the constitutionality of state sanctions, and I'm not here to 
debate that issue, but I can say that state and local sanctions do 
have the practical effect of interfering with the president's 
ability to conduct our foreign policy. 

My main message today is one of partnership -- an offer 
to work closely with you to respond to egregious threats to our 
national security, our interest in enhancing human rights, or our 


138 


values. Just as we have reached out to you, we have also 
consulted with the National Governors’ Association, the 
Association of State Attorneys General, the National 
Conference of State Legislators, and the National Association 
of State Procurement Officers. 

| hope that we can work together to send the nght 
message to governments that do not respect the basic human 
rights of their citizens by only taking actions that will be 
effective and not ones which will strengthen the position of 
rogue states and divert the U.S. and other countries from our 
purpose. Our aim is not to try to halt state actions -- rather, we 
would like to work with you to encourage the judicious and 
appropriate use of such action. We aid pariah states when the 
United States does not speak with one voice. We also become 
embroiled in disputes that could easily be avoided by following 
a basic American value: living up to our commitments as 
individuals and as a nation. These avoidable disputes distract 
us and the nations whose cooperation we need to bring to bear 
the maximum pressure possible to bring about meaningful 
change in the lives of the citizens of Burma and elsewhere. 

Once again, thank you for the opportunity to speak on 
these important issues. | look forward to working with you as 
we move forward.

Testimony of 
Deputy Assistant Secretary David Marchick 
before the Maryland House of Delegates 
Committee on Commerce and Government Matters 
March 25, 1998 


Thank you for the opportunity to speak with you today. 
I would like to discuss with you OUT shared interest in 
promoting U.S. national security, strengthening protection of 
human rights and enhancing U.S. competitiveness. 

Let me begin by placing state sanctions in the context 
of exports, jobs and competitiveness. 

The world is growing increasingly interdependent. In 
1970, U.S. imports and exports were equivalent to 13 percent 
of gross domestic product (GDP). Now that figure is 30 
percent. The United States once again has become the world’s 
number one exporter, the world's largest manufacturer of 
automobiles, the world's premier agricultural exporter, and the 
world's leading producer of semiconductors. From coast to 
coast business is growing, real wages are rising and inflation is 
under control. Truly, the American economy has become the 
envy of countries around the globe. 

While this stunning success is attributable to many 
factors, American participation in an increasingly rule-based 
global trading system is among the most important. 
International trade is a vital component of our economic 
strength at home and abroad. Since 1993, more than a third of 
our economic growth has come directly from exports. Much of 
this growth in exports is due to the performance of states like 


161 


Maryland. 

The Port of Baltimore is the gateway to the global 
market place for exports and imports to and from Maryland and 
the entire country. From 1987 through 1996, Maryland goods 
exports increased 129.1% to $3.5 billion. In the first 6 months 
of 1997, Maryland's goods exports were running at a rate of 
$4.1 billion -- up 37% since 1992. Maryland's interests 
international trade translates into almost 50,000 jobs -- high 
paying jobs that pay 15% higher wages than other jobs in 
Maryland 

Our prosperity as a nation depends on our ability to 
compete in international markets. Ninety-six percent of the 
world's consumers live outside of the U.S., many of them in 
dynarnic, emerging markets in Latin America and Asia that are 
growing much faster than our traditional export markets. We 
can benefit from their growth-but only if their markets are open 
to our products, since our markets are already open to theirs. 

America benefits from free trade that is also fair trade, 
and this Administration has worked hard to ensure that other 
countries live up to their word. We have frequently used the 
World Trade Organization's (WTO) dispute settlement system 
to enforce our rights. We have filed and won more dispute 
settlement cases in the WTO than any country in the world for 
a wide spectrum of U.S. industries from agriculture, to 
manufacturing, services industries and intellectual property. 
We have benefitted from clearer rules on government 
procurement which has opened billions of dollars of 
procurement opportunities for U.S. firms and workers. 
Working toward creating a level playing field has benefitted 
Maryland. For example, the WTO's Government Procurement 


162 


Agreement has enhanced access for Maryland companies in 
key markets such as Japan and Europe. 

Now let me turn to the issue of sanctions. Economic 
sanctions are an important foreign policy tool that provides an 
option between diplomacy and the use of force. They are used 
when other measures have been insufficient and when their 
imposition is likely to change the behavior of the offending 
State. 

Sanctions are not a "one-size-fits-all" foreign policy 
tool, however. When the U.S. must apply sanctions, the 
Administration seeks to design a sanctions regime to target the 
specific vulnerabilities of the sanctioned country so as to 
ensure the best and fastest possible response to our concerts, 
while minimizing the damage to other important U.S. interests. 
Sanctions are intended to highlight misconduct by regimes, and 
alter the behavior which threatens our national interests. 
Sanctions can address misconduct in the areas of human rights, 
terrorism, narcotics, weapons of mass destruction, and other 
areas where such conduct is considered unacceptable by world 
standards. 

To maximize their effectiveness, we prefer sanctions 
that have multilateral support and participation. Multilateral 
economic sanctions are not only more effective vis-a-vis the 
target, but they also show unity of international purpose, 
minimize damage to U.S. competitiveness, and distribute the 
sanctions burden across responsible countries. 

However, we are prepared to use unilateral sanctions 
when important national interests are at stake. The Clinton 
Administration has at times employed sanctions to promote 
human rights, isolate rogue regimes and advance our economic 


163 


interests in the global economy. In response to extreme actions 
taken by the Nigerian government, the President has imposed a 
visa ban on Nigerian senior officials and has banned military 
sales and services to Nigeria. He has banned new U.S. 
investment in Burma to prevent the repressive regime in Burma 
from benefitting from its misdeeds. He signed the Iran-Libya 
Sanctions Act to send a strong message to Iran and Libya to 
halt state sponsored terrorism and the acquisition of weapons of 
mass destruction. Simultaneously with these actions, the 
Administration has launched vigorous diplomatic initiatives to 
encourage other countries to pursue our shared goals of 
changing the behavior of pariah regimes, improving the 
conditions under which their citizens live and working towards 
a world free from the threat of terrorism and weapons of mass 
destruction. 

While we recognize and share state governments’ 
frustration and often outrage at certain countries’ unacceptable 
behavior, we are concerned about the growing nu:nber of state 
and local measures and the message they send to the rest of the 
world. The President has made it clear that this Administration 
will challenge the unacceptable behavior of rogue states 
wherever they are in the world. The Secretary of State has told 
other countries that the U.S. is prepared to pay the cost of 
leadership and, if necessary, to go it alone when it comes to 
delivering a clear message of condemnation to these regimes. 
Secretary Albright has not pulled any punches -- as diplomats 
often do -- in expressing the United States’ outrage at egregious 
violations of human rights and international norms in countries 
like Cuba, Nigeria, Afghanistan, Burma and Iran. But state 
sanctions often can confuse the message the United States 


164 


sends and impede our ability to build coalitions to focus on the 
targeted regime. Let me outline a few concerns we have about 
State sanctions. 

Our first concern is whether sanctions are effective in 
pursuing the goals we all share. As I said before, to be most 
effective, sanctions should have multilateral support and 
participation wherever possible. While the U.S. is the largest 
economy and most important country, sanctions by the U.S. 
alone may not have the desired impact because they can be 
sometimes and often are circumvented. Sanctions often are 
ineffective unless they are supported by other key countries. 

Second, from the Department of State's perspective, we 
are concerned that state and local sanctions may impair the 
President's ability to send a clear and unified message to the 
rest of the world. As the nation to which the world looks for 
leadership, the United States has the obligation to project a 
coherent and consistent message. We would like to work with 
you to send a clear message to oppressive regimes. But ad hoc 
and scattered actions at various levels of government, however 
well-intentioned, can do more harm than good in achieving the 
desired objective. They can impede the President's and 
Secretary of State's conduct of foreign policy. They also can 
create conflicts with our allies with whom we need to work to 
achieve our common goals. 

For example, a variety of state and local officials 
recently began considering the imposition of various economic 
sanctions on Swiss banks and other Swiss institutions in order 
to force an accounting for assets of Holocaust victims. Our 
goal is to provide justice for Holocaust victims, not to punish 
the Swiss people or their government. The U.S. Government 


165 


has already issued a very hard-hitting report and the Swiss have 
taken strong steps to account for the past. But now is the time 
for cooperation with the Swiss, not confrontation. According 
to Stuart Eizenstat, Under Secretary of State and the President's 
point person on this project, state actions at this time are 
unwarranted and counterproductive, particularly given positive 
steps the Government of Switzerland is taking to address the 
issue. Such actions threaten to harden Swiss public opinion 
against staying this positive course. These sanctions efforts 
also complicate our government's ability to develop a healing 
and reconciliation process that helps uncover the truth. 

Third, if states do want to express concerns or 
viewpoints on particular human rights abuses or other 
objectionable behavior by foreign regimes, we would like to 
work with them to ensure that these actions complement U.S. 
foreign policy objectives and are consistent with our 
international treaties and agreements. As I mentioned before, 
as the world's largest exporter, no country does better than the 
United States when countries follow internationally developed 
trade rules. At the same time, the United States must also play 
by the rules. If state and local sanctions are perceived to 
violate the rules, they can cause counterproductive 
disagreements with nations that share the same values and are 
otherwise inclined to follow our lead in pursuing our foreign 
policy goals. 

Fourth, we are also concerned about the impact of 
sanctions on our domestic economy. While sanctions serve an 
important foreign policy purpose, they are not without cost. 
The cost is measured in foregone economic opportunities by 
countries that put these measures in place. One private sector 


166 


group estimates foregone opportunities for the U.S. alone in 
1996 at $15-$19 billion dollars in unrealized exports and the 
loss of over 200,000 jobs in export-related sectors of our 
economy. 

Often state and local sanctions have unintended effects 
that are counterproductive or divert attention from their 
original goal. In June 1996, Massachusetts enacted a law 
applying a price penalty, for purposes of bidding on 
procurement opportunities, on companies that have business 
operations in Burma. The European Union and Japan have 
initiated dispute settlement consultations in the World Trade 
Organization over this law. Our trading partners allege that the 
Massachusetts law violates certain provisions of the 
Government Procurement Agreement. 

We are working closely with the Commonwealth of 
Massachusetts on this issue, just as we continue to work with 
the European Union, Japan and others to improve the situation 
in Burma. We have made a lot of progress. Let me be clear. 
We will defend the rights of the United States in the WTO 
dispute settlement process. But we must be honest in saying 
that the threatened WTO case has been an irritant and has 
diverted the United States’ and Europe's attention from 
focussing where it should be -- on Burma. 


We share Maryland's concerns about human rights in 
Nigeria and the necessary democratic reforms which must take 
place to improve the lives of Nigerian citizens. In its 1997 
human rights report, the Administration issued a stinging 
account of the Nigerian Government's human rights abuses. 
The report holds, "The [Nigerian] Government's human rights 


167 


record remained dismal. Throughout the year, [Sani] Abacha' s 
Government relied regularly on arbitrary detention and 
harassment to silence its most outspoken critics.” The report 
goes on to note, "Security forces continued to commit 
extrajudicial killings arid use excessive force to quell 
antigovernment protests...". The report concludes that citizens 
of Nigeria do not have the right to change their government by 
peaceful means and that despite the announced timetable for 
transition from military to multiparty rule, there was little 
meaningful progress toward democracy. This scathing report 
demonstrates that we are in agreement with Maryland about the 
deplorable human rights and democratic situation in Nigeria 
and we welcome the voice of the people of Maryland 
demanding change. 

We do have concerns, however, about the way 
Maryland has chosen to respond to the Nigerian situation 
Specifically, certain provisions in the bill will make it difficult 
for us to continue our efforts to work with our trading partners 
to implement effective multilateral measures to influence 
reform in Nigeria. These provisions raise serious questions, 
much like the Massachusetts Burma law, and we would like to 
work with you to ensure that we don't expose ourselves~ to a 
potential WTO challenge. 

Finally, I know that questions have been raised about 
the constitutionality of state sanctions. I'm not here to debate 
those legal issues, but I can say that state and local sanctions 
that are inconsistent with our obligations to our political allies 
and trading partners can have the practical effect of interfering 
with the President's ability to conduct our foreign policy. Too 
often we find ourselves answering criticism about certain state 


168 


and local actions rather than focussing on the poor behavior of 
the object of those actions. 

My main message today is one of partnership -- an offer 
to work closely with you to respond to egregious threats to our 
national security, our interest in enhancing human rights, or in 
promoting other of our national values. We have had 
constructive private conversations with a number of Maryland 
legislators on this issue. We have made it clear to them and we 
want to make it clear to you that we fully support Maryland's 
desire to be heard, not silenced. Just as we have reached out to 
you, we have also consulted with the National Governors’ 
Association, the Association of State Attorneys General, the 
National Conference of State Legislators, and the National 
Association of State Procurement Officers. 


I hope we call work together to send the right message 
to governments that do not respect the basic human rights of 
their citizens. Our aim is not to try to halt state actions -- 
rather, we would like to work with you to encourage the 
judicious and appropriate use of sanctions. We can 
inadvertently aid pariah states when the United States does not 
speak with one voice. Measures by states that transgress our 
international obligations embroil us in disputes that could 
easily be avoided. Discord with our allies weakens opposition 
to rogue regimes and trade disputes can impair the cooperation 
that is essential bringing about meaningful change in the lives 
of the citizens of Nigeria and elsewhere. 


Once again, thank you for the opportunity to speak on 
these important issues. I look forward to working with you as 


we move forward.

THE SECRETARY OF STATE

Dear Colleagues and Friends, 


Welcome to Washington and the Department of State. The theme 
of your conference is "Are States Necessary?” As Secretary of 
State, my answer is an emphatic yes! State governments are our 
partners in keeping America competitive and secure. We count 
on the states to work with is to promote American exports and 
investment, to advance our democratic ideals, and to build new 
partnerships between our people and institutions and the people 
and institutions of nations, regions and cities all around the world. 
This kind of engagement in world affairs is essential to keep our 
economy growing, real wages rising, and inflation under control. 
The stunning economic success we have already experienced in 
the last few years is due in no small measure to the partnership 
between states and the federal government, at home and on the 
world stage. 


State and local governments are now more directly affected than 
ever by events overseas in areas such as migration, environmental 
protection, trade and investment. Many states have a particularly 
important stake in deepening our ties with Mexico and Canada, 
and are helping to improve cross-border cooperation. That State 
Department welcomes the role state and local leaders can play in 
helping to shape our engagement overseas. Indeed, as Secretary 
of State, I want to do all I can to ensure that Americans are 
interested in foreign policy, and I view the involvement of our 
State governments as a welcome expression of public concern. 


197 


At the same time, it is important that state and local actions 
strengthen the President's ability to run an effective and 
coordinated foreign policy. President Clinton and I recognize the 
authority of state and local officials to determine their own 
investment and procurement policies, and the right -- indeed their 
responsibility -- to take moral considerations into account as they 
do so. And we certainly share the concerns many stated have 
expressed about human rights abuses abroad. The Clinton 
Administration has worked vigorously to promote freedom in 
nations where it is denied; we have done so through diplomacy, 
through sanctions where we believe they can be effective, and 
even, where critical interests are at stake, through force. 


Our challenge is to ensure that America speaks with a single 
voice. That is why we want to work in partnership with you to 
ensure that state and local actions are effective and consistent 
with our international obligations, and that they complement the 
objectives of our foreign policy as well as our efforts to mobilize 
global support for the goals we share. 


Sincerely,

Statement by Stuart E. Eizenstat 

U.S. Under Secretary of State for Economic, Business 
and Agricultural Affairs 

Before the Hevesi Committee 

July 1, 1998 


I am grateful for the opportunity today to express once again the 
views of the U.S. Government on these important issues being 
considered by New York City Comptroller Alan Hevesi and his 
committee colleagues from state and local entities from across the 
country. We respect the Committee's process and we share their 
determination that justice be done. While we share this common 
purpose, the U.S. Government is also determined to see that 
justice be achieved promptly and in ways that maintain both the 
openness of our nation’s financial markets and a positive 
relationship between the United States and Switzerland. 


Since last December, at the request of both sides, I have 
convened intensive talks in an effort to facilitate a settlement of 
the class action suits by Holocaust victims against the three major 
Swiss banks. We have met on about a dozen separate occasions 
in formal discussions together with innumerable other efforts to 
find a just settlement. The parties have made significant progress 
in agreeing on a detailed structure for a potential settlement, in 
obtaining a coordinated negotiating position by the riaintiffs” 
attorneys and major NGOs, and in achieving some narrowing of 
major differences on the settlement amount. But they have not 
been able to bridge their remaining differences at this time. 


201 


No one is more disappointed than my U.S. Government 
colleagues and | that the parties were not able to reach an 
agreement given the amount of time, effort and energy all of us, 
including the parties, have put into this, and most importantly, the 
profound matters at stake in this litigation. We fully appreciate 
the important issues raised in these cases by the plaintiffs, many 
of whom are U.S. citizens. These talks presented an historic 
opportunity for resolving this matter that may not come again 
soon. The alternative of lengthy and contentious litigation serves 
no one’s interests. Given this, we call upon the parties to reflect 
on the consequences of protracted litigation: it means a delay in 
achieving a measure of justice for the unique class of aging and 
frail Holocaust survivors who would most benefit from a just and 
prompt settlement; and it represents a missed opportunity for a 
major step toward closure of a difficult chapter in Switzerland's 
history. 


Consistent with the terms of the confidentiality agreement 
governing these talks and my role as a neutral facilitator in this 
process, we will not comment further on the status or conduct of 
the talks. 


We hope that this missed opportunity will not lead to 
recriminations and hardening of positions on both sides. This 
matter is before a federal court and should be resolved there if it _ 
cannot be resolved through the mutual agreement of the parties. 


These developments also put added importance on the need for a 
thorough and prompt conclusion to the Volcker process of 
reviewing dormant accounts in Switzerland that may have 
belonged to Holocaust victims, to which Chairman Volcker and 
his Committee are committed. 


202 


The U.S. Government favors a just and prompt resolution of the 
issues at stake with the three major Swiss banks and other Swiss 
institutions. Sanctions - however well - intentioned or carefully 
calibrated - will not make it easier to achieve closure in this 
complex and sensitive matter. We believe that state and local 
sanctions in this matter are wrong both in principle and practice: 
wrong in principle because our nation should speak with one 
voice in matters of foreign policy and international commerce and 
because they are not warranted; wrong in practice because they 
may be counter-productive and fail to advance the goal of a 
prompt and just settlement. 


Far from compelling the banks to do more, the imposition of 
sanctions may lead to an opposite reaction which will delay still 
further the resolution of these issues. Even the threat of sanctions 
has reinforced an unfortunate climate of inflexibility in 
Switzerland. While Switzerland is undertaking a comprehensive 
series of steps to address Holocaust-era issues, it must continue 
on this positive course if it is to achieve closure. Imposing 
sanctions would make it more difficult for all Swiss institutions 
to do so. 


Moreover, we must safeguard the preeminence of the United 
States as the most open financial market in the world. By 
imposing sanctions such as those under consideration, New York 
City and other jurisdictions would risk endangering not only 
competitiveness in attracting and retaining world-class firms and 
the income they generate and the jobs they support, but also call 
into question the openness and attractiveness of all American 
financial markets.

European Union and Japan to formally chalienge the 
Massachusetts Burma selective purchasing law at the World 
Trade Organization (WTO).

Europe, Japan and the United States. However, we strongly 
believe that it is highly inappropriate for the European Union 
and Japan to involve themselves in the internal affairs of 
Massachusetts. It is not the place of the European Union and 
Japan to dictate how the Massachusetts legislature and state 
government should spend its own taxpayers’ money. The 
people of Massachusetts have long vested the power to levy 
taxes and spend public money with our elected representatives, 
not European and Japanese bureaucrats.

purchasing law is strongly supported by the legitimate leader of 
the Burmese democracy movement, Aung San Suu Kyi. The

San Suu Kyi remarked in a recent interview that selective 
purchasing laws are very effective means of restoring 
democracy in Burma. She also specifically praised the 
Massachusetts Burma law. 


We fear that, by placing European and Japanese corporate 
interests first, the European Union and Japan risk undermining 
their principled stand against the Burmese military junta. In 
March, the European Commission revoked Burma's tariff 
preferences because of the military junta's pervasive use of 
forced labor. The new British government recently cut its 
support for trade promotion in Burma and has "not ruled out 
the possibility of further measures, including economic 
sanctions" against the illegal military junta. Japan has called 
for dialogue between the military junta and the democracy 
movement. 


Opinion in Europe in particular towards business in Burma is 
best typified by the recent and unanimous resolution on Burma 
passed by the European Parliament. The resolution called on 
the European Union to enact economic sanctions on Burma. 
Moreover, in its resolution, the European Parliament 
specifically urged the European Commission not to take action 
against the Massachusetts Burma law under the WTO dispute 
settlement procedures. 


We ask the U.S. Trade Representative to weigh the support for

Japan, Massachusetts and, most importantly, Burma itself. We

20. The Massachusetts Burma Law restricts state 
agencies from purchasing goods or services from any company 
“doing business with Burma (Myanmar)” as those terms are 
defined by the Law. As described in Paragraph 9, above. these 
companies are placed on the restricted purchase list. The Law 
requires that “in establishing the restricted purchase list, the 
Secretary shall consult United Nations reports, resources of the 
Investor Responsibility Research Center, the Associates to 
Develop Democratic Burma, and other reliable sources.” This 
list is referred to as the “Restricted Purchase List.” 


21. In creating the Restricted Purchase List, the 
Commonwealth obtained a list of companies believed to be 
doing business in Burma (Myanmar) from the Investor 
Responsibility Research Center. Commonwealth personnel 
then compared the activities of each company on the list with 
the statutory definition of doing business with Burma 
(Myanmar), and made a preliminary assessment as to which 
companies should be placed on the list. The Commonwealth 
then sent a letter signed by defendant Anderson to each such 
company to inform the company that it would be placed on the 
list, and gave the company the opportunity to provide affidavits 
or other documentation to convince the Commonwealth that it 


217 


should not be placed on the list. In some cases, representatives 
of such companies met with Commonwealth personnel to 
discuss their proposed placement on the list. After receiving 
the company’s response, if any, the Commonwealth made a 
final decision as to whether to place an individual company on 
the Restricted Purchase List. As the list is updated, the 
Commonwealth follows this same procedure with respect to 
companies that it intends to add to the list. To date, no 
company has sought judicial review of a determination by the 
Commonwealth that it is doing business with Burma 
(Myanmar). In their review of the possible placement of 
companies on the list, Commonwealth officials have not 
reviewed Burmese law or judged the credibility of Burmese 
officials.

remain on the List, and though a number were either removed 
from, or never placed on, the List, a number of these objecting 
companies are still listed.

THE COURT: Let me ask you a question. It may be of 
absolutely no significance, but it comes to my mind. You 
mentioned something earlier in your argument, at least | think 
you did, that the state might even be barred from a resolution 
that touched on foreign affairs. Suppose that resolution was in 
support of the national policy -- in other words, praising the 
President or the congress for banning participation with Burma 
-- pick whatever subject you want -- is there a difference if the 
state is agreeing with the Federal Government, as opposed to 
prodding the Federal Government over disagreeing v th them? 


MR. DYK: I don't think it is so much a distinction 
between whether the state is agreeing or disagreeing with the 
Federal Government, because the key issue here is whether 
what the state does is attempting to make foreign policy, and | 
think, under some circumstances, a mere resolution could be 
making foreign policy. I think at least statements by Under 
Secretary Eizenstat and Deputy Assistant Marchick are clear 
that the whole issue of sanctions and how far to go and what to 


233 


say is a very critical element of foreign policy, and that you 
have to have one entity is the United States Government. 


So, even if you have a resolution with no sanctions 
attached to it, which, of course, is not this case, even if you had 
a resolution purported to agree with the position of the United 
States. There are nuances here, and foreign policy is a very 
delicate matter. It requires flexibility and expertise, which the 
state simply does not have. 


It does not have the foreign policy establishment. It 
requires the ability to change quickly, rather than to have a 
situation where the policy is written in stone. And, for all of 
those reasons, even if there is some parallelism between the 
state policy and the federal policy, that does not mean that it is 
not intruding into the area of foreign affairs. 


THE COURT: In any event, this is more than a 
resolution. 

MR. DYK: It is far more that a resolution. It is a 
traditional exercise of foreign policy making. It is condemning 
a foreign country and imposing sanctions.
