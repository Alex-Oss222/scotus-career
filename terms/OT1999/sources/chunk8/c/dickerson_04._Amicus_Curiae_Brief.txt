U.S.C. § 3501 to end the present uncertainty about whether 
Section 3501 governs the admissibility of custodial

Acting in the absence of legislation, the Court promulgated 
Miranda’s sweeping exclusionary rule as a prophylactic

Amendment violations. This extraconstitutional rule of

of confessions is a question of federal law on which the 
courts of appeals have adopted conflicting approaches and 
one that has not been, but should be, settled by this Court. 


The Court should also grant review because it is the 
proper authority to clarify the basis for the exclusionary 
rule devised by the Miranda Court, and the related question 
whether Congress properly may modify that rule. In 
particular, this Court should clarify the relationship 
between its own authority to devise remedies that assist in 
protecting (but are not required to protect) a Constitutional 
right, and Congress’s authority to modify those remedies in 
a way that fully protects the underlying right while 
reducing the serious social costs imposed by the judicially- 
devised regime sweeping more broadly than the 
Constitution requires. 


1. The court of appeals upheld Section 3501 against 
a constitutional challenge, ruling that Section 3501, rather 
than Miranda’s exclusionary rule, is the law governing the 
admission of custodial confessions into evidence at federal 
criminal trials. In two recent cases, United States v. Rivas-

circuits, which routinely decide the admissibility of 
custodial confessions under Miranda’s exclusionary rule 
without any reference to Section 3501’s superseding 
standard. 


Moreover, if Section 3501 is in fact the governing law, 
the lower courts’ failure to follow it rather than Miranda’s 
exclusionary rule has real and serious consequences. It 
“has caused the federal judiciary to confront a host of 
‘Miranda’ issues that might be entirely irrelevant under

our citizens.” Davis v. United States, 512 U.S. 452, 465- 
66 (1994) (Scalia, J., concurring). A decision by this 
Court is warranted in order to resolve this matter of 
recurring and first-rank importance.

The court below determined that Miranda’s

a. That Miranda’s exclusionary rule is not 
constitutionally required is both the better reading of the 
Miranda opinion and the only view of it consistent with the 
rest of this Court’s and the lower federal courts’ law on 
custodial confessions. The Miranda Court itself stated that 
it had no intention of “creat[ing] a constitutional straitjacket 
which will handicap sound efforts” in Congress and among 
the States to devise their own measures against compelled 
self-incrimination, “while promoting efficient enforcement 
of our criminal laws.” Miranda, 384 U.S. at 467. These 
statements are impossible to explain if the Court believed 
the specific rules it announced were ordained by the 
Constitution. 


7 


To the extent Miranda is ambiguous on the question, 
however, the Court’s later cases establish that the 
Constitution requires neither the Miranda warnings, nor the 
rule established there that unwarned confessions must be 
excluded even when they are voluntary. Rather, Miranda’s 
rules are “prophylactic,” put forth as “a series of 
recommended procedural safeguards,” Davis v. United 
States, 512 U.S. at 457-458, that are “not themselves 
protected by the Constitution” and are “not constitutional 
in character,” Withrow v. Williams, 507 U.S. 689, 690- 
691 (1993). The Court has also stated pointedly that 
Miranda’ exclusionary rule “sweeps more broadly than the 
Fifth Amendment” and that its “preventive medicine 
provides a remedy even to the defendant who has suffered 
no identifiable constitutional harm.” Elstad, 470 U.S. at 
306-07. 


Moreover, the Court has relied on the non- 
constitutional status of Miranda’s exclusionary rule in 
various contexts in refusing to suppress unwarned or 
imperfectly warned custodial confessions and their fruits.‘ 
Most important in this regard are a trio of cases in which 
the Court made an exception to Miranda’s exclusionary 
rule and allowed the admission into evidence of un- 
Mirandized but voluntary confessions. In New York v. 
Quarles, 467 U.S. 649, 654 (1984), the Court ruled that 
an incriminating statement obtained as a result of a police 
question, “Where’s the gun?,” asked of a rape suspect they

The competing theory of Miranda’s exclusionary rule 
— that un-Mirandized confessions are excluded because the 
failure to give the suspect his warnings means his 
confession is necessarily “compelled” as that term is used 
in the Fifth Amendment — cannot possibly be squared with 
these decisions. Under that reading, amy use of an un- 
Mirandized confession at trial, including the ones allowed 
in Quarles, Harris, and Hass, would also have to be 
barred, since those uses too would amount to “compelled” 
self-incriminating testimony. And, indeed, the Court has 
concluded that the Fifth and Fourteenth Amendments do 
require the exclusion of involuntary confessions even for 
impeachment purposes, distinguishing Harris and Hass as 
involving confessions obtained as a result of mere Miranda, 
rather than constitutional, violations. See New Jersey v. 


9 


Portash, 440 U.S. 450, 458-59 (1979); Mincey v. Arizona, 
437 U.S. 385, 397 (1978). Accordingly, the Court’s 
admission of un-Mirandized statements in Quarles, Harris, 
and Hass proves that Miranda’s rule excluding unwarned 
but voluntary confessions is mot required by the 
Constitution. That is the starting point from which to 
analyze Congress’s authority to adopt Section 3501. 


b. Miranda’s exclusionary rule is best understood 
not as a constitutional command, but as an exercise of this 
Court’s authority to improvise a remedy when it is 
presented with an issue implicating a constitutional right for 
which there is not at the time of the decision a 
constitutionally or legislatively specified remedy. See 
Bivens v. Six Unknown Agents, 403 U.S. 388 (1971); Mapp 
v. Ohio, 367 U.S. 643 (1961). The judicially devised 
remedy may sweep more broadly than is strictly necessary 
to vindicate a particular constitutional right. Bivens, 403 
U.S. at 397; cf. id. at 406-08 (Harlan, J., concurring). 
This devising of interstitial remedies not themselves 
required by the Constitution, but designed to assist in 
protecting constitutional rights, has become known as the 
Court’s power to create “constitutional common law.” 
See Henry P. Monaghan, Foreword: Constitutional 
Common Law, 89 HARV. L. REV. | (1975). Because the 
Court has crafted such remedies to protect constitutional 
rights against the States as well as against the federal 
government, see, ¢.g., Mapp, 367 U.S. 643, this 
understanding of Miranda’s exclusionary rule is entirely 
consistent with its application to the States. 


c. If, as the Court’s cases establish, Miranda’s 
exclusionary rule was not constitutionally compelled but 
was instead an exercise of the Court’s power to improvise 
a prophylactic measure to assist in protecting a 


10 


Constitutional right, it follows that Congress has power to 
modify the Court’s remedial scheme. Cf. Palermo v. 
United States, 360 U.S. 343, 353 n.11 (1959). The 
existence of such a residual power in Congress is, for 
example, presumed throughout Bivens, 403 U.S. at 397; id. 
at 406-08 (Harlan, J., concurring), and lies at the heart of 
this Court’s later holding in Bush v. Lucas, 462 U.S. 367 
(1983). In that case, the Court refused to allow a “Bivens” 
remedy for money damages in an action brought by a 
federal employee alleging a breach of his First Amendment 
rights, because Congress, by statute, had provided for a 
different remedy. Jd. at 377. Moreover, whatever the 
outer constitutional limits of Congress’s power may be in 
this context, it is plain that Congress acts within them so 
long as the remedial scheme it establishes provides 
“meaningful” relief for any actual constitutional violation. 
See Lucas, 462 U.S. at 386; id. at 372; Schweiker 
v. Chilicky, 487 U.S. 412, 425 (1988). 


d. Replacing Miranda’s automatic exclusionary 
rule with Section 3501’s regime was a proper exercise of 
such Congressional authority. Under Section 3501, 
compelled custodial statements remain inadmissible in all 
circumstances. Hence, Section 3501 leaves intact the core 
holding of Miranda: that tle prohibition against compelled 
self-incrimination extends beyond in-court statements to 
regulate any use at trial of a defendant’s pre-trial custodial 
Statements. Indeed, the relief the statute affords for an 
actual constitutional violation involving such statements is 
identical to the relief afforded by Miranda. That is 
sufficient per se to establish the statute’s propriety. 


But there is more. Section 3501 also preserves much 
of the prophylactic value of Miranda’s exclusionary rule, 
albeit through a slightly different and more refined 


11 


mechanism. As the Court below noted, Section 3501 
continues to provide a strong incentive for police to advise 
suspects of their “Miranda rights.” See Dickerson, 166 
F.3d at 692. The statute does this by making use of the 
police’s eventual need to secure a favorable judicial 
decision on admissibility concerning the fruits of their 
questioning — the same instrument the Miranda Court used 
— to create that incentive at the police station. 


Section 3501’s approach differs from Miranda’s on 
only one point. Instead of requiring automatic exclusion of 
a confession not preceded by warnings, Section 3501 
provides that the district court is to make the admissibility 
determinaiion by deciding whether the confession was, in 
fact, voluntary. In making this determination, the court is 
required by the statute to consider, not as the sole factor 
but as a prominent one, whether the warnings were given. 


Thus, Section 3501 is not only fully adequate to 
vindicate the actual constitutional right against compelled 
self-incrimination -- compelled statements remaining 
banned in all circumstances -- but, through its framework 
for determining voluntariness, preserves a significant 
portion of Miranda’s prophylactic protections for suspects 
in custody. In so doing, the statute does not simply restore 
the legal status quo ante but goes significantly beyond pre- 
Miranda voluntariness law. See FRED GRAHAM, THE SELF- 
INFLICTED WOUND 324 (1970) (“parts of [Section 3501] 
would have been a progressive expansion of suspect’s 
rights if Congress had passed it prior to Miranda”). 
Indeed, one of the Section 3501 factors goes beyond not 
only pre-Miranda case law, but beyond current Miranda 
and Fifth Amendment doctrines. The statute directs the 
district court to consider whether the “defendant knew the 
nature of the offense with which he was charged or of 


12 


which he was suspected at the time of the confession.” 18 
U.S.C. § 3501(b)(2). This requirement extends further 
than existing Miranda and Fifth Amendment case law, 
which makes clear that such inquiry by the trial court is not 
routinely required in order to assess the voluntariness of a 
suspect’s confession. See Colorado v. Spring, 479 U.S. 
564, 577 (1987) (suspect’s awareness of all the crimes 
about which he may be questioned is not relevant to 
determining the validity of his decision to waive the Fifth 
Amendment privilege). 


The unmistakable prominence that Miranda warnings 
enjoy in Section 3501 means that, if this Court were to 
uphold the statute, federal law enforcement officers would 
almost certainly continue to give them. That, indeed, is 
what amicus understands has been happening in the five 
States that comprise the Fourth Circuit since the Dickerson 
ruling earlier this year. Thus, the lower court’s opinion 
has produced an enviable “win-win” situation in that (1) 
federal law enforcement officers continue to deliver the 
Miranda warnings, while (2) in cases with technical 
disputes over Miranda compliance (this case being a prime 
example, see 166 F.3d at 673-77) the statement will be 
admitted — so long as it was voluntary — but excluded 
if it was not.°

For example, officers who forcibly extract confessions 
are now subject to criminal sanctions under 18 U.S.C. §§ 
241, 242 (provisions which were originally adopted during 
Reconstruction, but vastly expanded since Miranda through 
judicial interpretation); civil actions under Bivens (decided

employing federal agencies are subject to civil actions 
under the Federal Tort Claims Act (28 U.S.C. § 2680(h))

legal landscape today — if Section 3501 is implemented — 
allows voluntary confessions to be used in evidence (18 
U.S.C. § 3501). 


This is an entirely reasonable and, in many ways, a 
more effective approach to securing respect for the values 
of the Fifth Amendment than Miranda’s extraconstitutional 
exclusionary rule. Unlike Miranda, the criminal and civil 
sanctions adopted by Congress and the Bivens actions 
recognized by the Court focus on conduct squarely 
implicating the Fifth Amendment proscription against 
“compelled” self-incrimination. At the same time, they 
provide stronger remedies against federal agents who 
coerce confessions than does the Miranda exclusionary 
rule. The exclusion of evidence “does not apply any direct 
sanction to the individual official whose illegal conduct” is 
at issue. Bivens v. Six Unknown Named Agents, 403 U.S. 
388, 416 (1971) (Burger, C.J., dissenting). Civil suits and 
internal disciplinary actions constitute “sensible and 
reasonable steps to deter [Fifth] Amendment violations by 
[federal] officers, and this make the likely additional 
deterrent value of [the Miranda] exclusionary rule small.” 
INS v. Lopez-Mendoza, 468 U.S. 1032, 1050 (1984). 
Similarly, the FTCA remedy provides a strong incentive 


15 


for federal agencies to make sure that their agents hew to 
Fifth Amendment lines. As a result, the remedial regime 
that exists now, 33 years after Miranda -- including Section 
3501 -- is fully consistent with Miranda’s statement that 
“[w]e encourage Congress . . . to continue [its] laudable 
search for increasingly effective ways of protecting the 
rights of the individual while promoting efficient 
enforcement of our criminal laws.” 368 U.S. at 467. Cf. 
Bush v. Lucas, 462 U.S. 367, 388-90 (declining to create 


judicial remedy because of congressional action). 


f. Finally, given the substantial change in the 
legal and remedial landscape, and the equally substantial 
improvement in police professionalism, it is significantly 
less likely now than it was when Miranda was decided that 
unwamed custodial questioning will produce a “compelled” 
Statement. Moreover, there are probably few people in the 
country today who are unaware of the rights the Miranda 
Court thought they needed to be told about before police 
questioning. For the courts to continue in every case to 
apply an irrebuttable presumption assuming compulsion as 
a matter of fact, when the actual facts are extremely 
unlikely to mirror the presumed ones most of the time, 
seems difficult to justify. See Coleman v. Thompson, 501 
U.S. 722, 737 (1991) (“Per se rules should not be applied 
* * * in situations where the generalization is incorrect as 
an empirical matter; the justification for a conclusive 
presumption disappears when application of the 
presumption will not reach the correct result most of the 
time.”). Thus, putting entirely to ome side the question 
whether judicially improvised rules that extend beyond

3. A final reason for granting certiorari is the need to 
consider the interests of those who are too often “unnamed 
and unrepresented” in criminal cases: victims of crime. 
See Miranda, 384 U.S. at 543 (White, J., dissenting). The 
federal courts’ failure to implement Section 3501 “may 
have produced * * * the acquittal and the nonprosecution 
of many dangerous felons, enabling them to continue their 
depredations upon our citizens.” Davis v. United States, 
512 U.S. at 465-66 (Scalia, J., concurring). Similar 
concerns certainly animated the congressional decision to 
adopt Section 3501. After extensive hearings on Miranda’s 
real world effects, the Senate Judiciary Committee 
recommended adoption of Section 3501 because “the rigid 
and inflexible requirements of the majority opinion in the 
Miranda case are * * * extremely harmful to law 
enforcement.” S. REP. No. 1097, 90" Cong., 2d Sess. at 
46, reprinted in 1968 U.S.C.C.A.N. at 2132. Congress’ 
finding on this “essentially factual issue” are “of course 
entitled to a great deal of deference, inasmuch as Congress 
is an institution better equipped to amass and evaluate the 
vast amounts of data bearing on such an issue.” Walters v. 
Nat’l Ass’n of Radiation Survivors, 473 U.S. 305, 331 n.12 
(1985). 


More recent empirical scholarship also supports the 
congressional conclusion, despite what had long been the 
conventional academic wisdom that Miranda had done little 
to harm law enforcement. An econometric analysis of the 
precipitous drop in clearance rates (the rates at which 
police officers solve or “clear” crimes) immediately 
following Miranda found that tens of thousands of crimes 
go unsolved each year as a result of the decision. Paul G. 
Cassell & Richard Fowles, Handcuffing the Cops? A 
Thirty-Year Perspective on Miranda’s Harmful Effects on 


17 


Law Enforcement, 50 STAN. L REV. 1055 (1998).° 
Similarly, the “before-and-after” Miranda data on 
confession rates strongly suggest the Miranda rules have 
deterred many dangerous criminals from confessing. Paul 
G. Cassell, Miranda’s Social Costs: An Empirical 
Reassessment, 90 Nw. U.L. REV. 387 (1996); see also Paul 
G. Cassell & Bret S. Hayman, Police Interrogation in the 
1990s: An Empirical Study of the Effects of Miranda, 43 
UCLA L. REV. 839, 871-76 (1994) (similar findings). 
Like the congressional hearings, these studies consider 
harms beyond the ken of the judiciary; they review data on 
“lost” criminal cases from Miranda — that is, those that 
can never be prosecuted and thus never reach the courts. 


These real world effects lend urgency to reviewing the 
decision below. For if Congress’s factual finding is correct 
— as this Court must assume, see Turner Broadcasting 
System, Inc. v. FCC, 512 U.S. 622, 666 (1994) (plurality 
opinion) — many dangerous criminals are escaping justice 
in the teeth of a federal statute that would permit their 
successful prosecution. See, ¢.g., Dickerson, 166 F.3d at 
672. 


The Constitution does not require the country to pay 
this price. It requires the exclusion of compelled 
confessions, as the law of any civilized nation would 
exclude them. But it does not require the exclusion of a 
wrongdoer’s voluntary confession -- the most reliable and 
important evidence available to the criminal justice system. 
For the judicial branch to reject that evidence when the 
Constitution demands no such thing was, in the absence of
