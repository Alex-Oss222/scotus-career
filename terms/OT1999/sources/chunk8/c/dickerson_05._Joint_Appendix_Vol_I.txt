MANNER AND MEANS OF THE CONSPIRACY 


The purpose of the conspiracy was to obtain money 
belonging to and in the care, custody, and possession of 
banks. The manner and means by which the conspirators 
conducted the conspiracy included the following: 


1. It was part of the conspiracy that the defendant and 
his co-conspirators would survey or “case” a bank they 
intended to rob by having DICKERSON enter the bank 
and gather information about the size of the bank and its 
security features. 


2. It was further part of the conspiracy that, following 
DICKERSON’s surveillance of the bank, an unindicted 
co-conspirator or conspirators would enter the bank, ap- 
proach a bank teller, display a handgun, and demand 
money from the teller. 


3. It was further part of the conspiracy that an un- 
indicted co-conspirator would hand the teller a backpack 
and order the teller to fill the backpack with money. 


4. It was further part of the conspiracy that DICK- 
ERSON and his unindicted co-conspirators took money 
from federally insured banks. 


5. It was further part of the conspiracy that DICKER- 
SON and his unindicted co-conspirators would flee the 
scene of bank robberies in DICKERSON’s car. 


6. It was further part of the conspiracy that DICKER- 
SON would wash and attempt to wash currency stained 
by the dye from exploding bank security devices.

DEFENDANT'S MOTION TO SUPPRESS 


COMES NOW the Defendant, Charles Dickerson, 
through his counsel, and moves this Honorable Court to 
suppress the testimony of James Rochester and exclude 
him as a witness in this case. As grounds in support 
thereof, the Defendant states as follows: 

1. On July 1, 1997, the Honorable James C. Cacheris 
issued an Order together with a Memorandum Opinion 
granting in part and denying in part the Defendant’s Mo- 
tion to Suppress. The Court granted the motion as to 
the Defendant’s statements offered at the FBI Field Office 
on January 27, 1997, and as to items discovered during 
a search of the Defendant’s home that evening. The 
Court denied the motion as to the search of the Defend- 
ant’s Oldsmobile Ciera on March 28, 1997. 


2. The Court suppressed the Defendant’s statement 
given at the FBI Field Office on the grounds that the De- 
fendant was under arrest at the time he was removed 
from his home by armed FBI agents and driven to the 
Field Office, and that, once at the Field Office, the De- 
fendant was not properly advised of his Miranda rights 
until after he had completed his statement to the agents. 


3. The arrest of Mr. Dickerson on the evening of Jan- 
uary 27, 1997, was in violation of Mr. Dickerson’s Fourth 


76 


Amendment rights because it was not supported by prob- 
able cause. During this unconstitutional seizure of his 
person, Mr. Dickerson gave the aforementioned state- 
ment. Prior to obtaining this statement, the Government 
did not know the identity or whereabouts of James 
Rochester. 


4. Any and all information obtained by the Govern- 
ment from James Rochester was tainted by the Govern- 
ment’s unlawful arrest of Mr. Dickerson. Without Mr. 
Dickerson’s statement, the Government never could have 
located James Rochester or developed him as a witness 
against Mr. Dickerson. James Rochester’s admissions to 
the police incriminating Mr. Dickerson in the bank rob- 
beries and related charges pending before this Court are 
tainted by the unlawful arrest of Mr. Dickerson and 
should be excluded as “fruit of the poisonous tree.” Wong

C. The Fruits of the Warrant-Authorized Search Are 
Admissible 


The Court suppressed the fruits of the search of Dick- 
erson’s apartment ruling that the warrant was insuffi- 
ciently particular in describing the items to be seized, and 
that the good faith exception to the exclusionary rule was 
inapplicable because “[t]he officers executing the warrant 
acted in bad faith.” We believe the warrant was suffi- 
ciently particular, although by no great margin. See 
United States v. Fawole, 785 F. 2d 1141, 1144 (4th Cir. 
1986) (“The warrant confines the executing officers’ dis- 
cretion by allowing them to seize only evidence of a par- 
ticular crime. Consequently, we find that the warrant 
describes the items to be seized with sufficient particular- 
ity to satisfy the Fourth Amendment.”); United States 
y, Anthony, No. 92-5258 (4th Cir. August 8, 1993) (un- 
published) 1993 WL 321595. In addition, on this issue, 
we rely on our original pleadings (particularly Govern- 
ment’s Second Supplemental pleading, pp. 14-17). We 


believe the cases we cited are consistent with the prin- 


ciple that 


{t]he test for the necessary particularity is a prag- 
matic one. The degree of specificity required may 
necessarily vary according to the circumstances and 
type of items involved . . . There is a practical mar- 
gin of flexibility permitted by the constitutional re- 


quirement for particularity in the description of the 


items to be seized. 
United States v. Torch, 609 F. 2d 1088, 1090 (4th Cir.), 
cert. denied, 446 U.S. 957 (1979) (citations omitted). 


97 


As a practical matter, the instant warrant did not au- 
thorize an improper general search because “evidence of 
the crime of bank robbery” generally includes a distinct, 
readily identifiable class of items, as in Fawole. As re- 


‘flected in Agent Wenko’s affidavit, experienced FBI 


agents investigating bank robbery know exactly what to 
search for: “guns, money, bait bills, dye-stained money 
and dye-stained objects, burned money, clothing, shoes, 
disguises, carrying bags, gloves.” Wenko Affd. 4 8. 


This also distinguishes a case like this from those in- 
volving investigations of more complex, general, or docu- 
ment related offenses. For example, as Professor LaFave 
points out, “[s]Jearch warrants for documents are gener- 
ally deserving of somewhat closer scrutiny with respect 
to the particularity requirement because of the potential 
they carry for a-very serious intrusion into personal 
privacy.” W. LaFave, Search and Seizure, § 4.6(d) p. 
569 (3d ed. 1996). 


Even if the Court adheres to its view that the warrant 
was not sufficiently particular, we believe it should recon- 
sider its holding that the warrant was so facially deficient 
as to preclude reasonable reliance on it. As the Fourth 
Circuit recently explained, “{a] warrant that fails to par- 
ticularize the place to be searched or the items to be 
seized is so facially deficient that it precludes reasonable 
reliance only when ‘{o]fficers poised to conduct a search 
[would] be able to ascertain that [it] fails to offer suffi- 
ciently detailed instruction and instead leaves them guess- 
ing as to their task,’” United States v. Ruhe, 1997 WL 
269339 (4th Cir. May 22, 1997) (unpublished) (em- 
phasis added). Ruhe suggests that the standard for as- 
sessing the objective reasonableness of reliance on the 
warrant is whether it gave the searching officers “any 
guidance whatsoever as to the subject of the search,” tak- 


98 


ing care to consider as well “the knowledge of the search- 
ing officers.” 


Although the warrant certainly could have been more 
particular, one could not say that it failed to provide “any 
guidance whatsoever as to the subject of the search.” The 
warrant authorized a search for “evidence of the crime 
of bank robbery”—a distinct category of evidence such 
as guns, bait money, disguises and the like, that would 
easily be known to and identifiable by law enforcement 
officers. 


The Ruhe factor relating to “the knowledge of the 
searching officers” strongly supports our position that the 
search was undertaken in good faith. Thus, Agent 
Wenko, the lead agent in the search, states in his affi- 


davit: 


3. After the robbery of the First Virginia Bank, 
300 South Washington Street, Alexandria, Virginia, 
I was familiar with the specifics of that robbery, 
namely, that a semi-automatic handgun was used 
and the amount of loss in the robbery. 


7. At approximately 9:00 to 9:15 p.m., I received 
a call from SA Lawlor who informed me that he 
had obtained a telephonic search warrant for Dicker- 
son’s apartment from U.S. Magistrate Judge Kenkel. 
SA Lawlor told me that the warrant was signed. 


8. I knew from previous discussions with Agent 
Lawlor that we were applying for a search warrant 
to seize evidence of the robbery of the First Virginia 
Bank on January 24, 1997. More important, I have 
been investigating bank robberies for about seven 
years, and I know what evidence of bank robberies 
we search for: guns, money, bait bills, dye-stained 
money and dye-stained objects, burned money, cloth- 


99 


ing, shoes, disguises, carrying bags, gloves. These 
were the objects I and the other searching agent: 
were looking for in Dickerson’s apartment. I knew 
precisely what to search for and did not feel that I 
needed any other guidance. 


9. I relied in good faith on the warrant signed by 
Judge Kenkel in searching Charles Dickerson’s apart- 
ment and seizing evidence from that apartment. 


This shows that Agent Wenko knew the pu of 
the search, the specifics of the First Virginia a 
bery—including that a semi-automatic handgun was used 
and the amount of the loss—and knew from experience 
the particular items that typically constitute evidence of 


bank robbery. 


We have discussed, here and in our original pleading, 
the cases setting ..at a standard for the particularity re- 
quirement. The court also discussed some of these cases, 
as did defense counsel. In truth, the line dividing lan- 
guage found sufficiently particular from language found 
too general is a fine one indeed. The cases falling on one 
side are so similar to those falling on the other that only 
the most exacting eye could expect routinely to distin- 
guish the one from the other. But it is precisely that fact 
that tells the tale here. In order to overcome the good 
faith exception, the defense must show that the warrant 
was “so facially deficient” that no reasonable officer could 
believe it to be valid. United States v. Hyppolite, 65 F. 3d 
1151, 1156 (4th Cir. 1995) (citing United States v. 
Leon, 468 U.S. 897, 923 (1984)). While there may well 
be room to doubt the specificity of this warrant, that ex- 
acting test is one the defense has failed to meet. Accord- 
ingly, the good faith exception should apply, and the 
Court should reconsider its suppression order.

custody and subjected to interrogation prior to being ad- 
vised of his Miranda rights.

ments were obtained. 


In ruling on the Motion to Suppress, the Court held that 
Mr. Dickerson was in custody when he was taken from 
his residence. The Court made specific findings of fact 
in support of this ruling: 


In this case, several armed police officers and fed- 
eral agents entered Dickerson’s home after dark and 
asked him to accompany them to the station. They 
then took Dickerson across the state line, on a ten-

the interrogation described above, and without leav-

Under these circumstances, Dickerson’s “freedom 
of movement” was as restricted as if he had been 
arrested. . . . [T]he only circumstance of Dicker- 
son’s encounter with the agents that night which 
would have changed had he been arrested at the out- 
set rather than the end is that he would have been 
read his rights earlier. To allow law enforcemert 
officers to circumvent Miranda by merely delaying 
their inevitable arrest of a suspect until he or she 
offers an uncounseled and unwarned confession 
would ultimately gut the intent of Miranda and its

These conclusions are fully supported by the record 
before the Court. Even if considered, nothing in the new

affidavits offered by the Government contradicts the chain

2. The Defendant was not properly advised of his

Having found Mr. Dickerson to be in custody, the 
Court then ruled that he was not timely advised of his

relied upon the documents submitted by the Government

The Government contends that the times supplied by 
the officers is not relevant, only the sequence of events 
which they recite is important. This argument fails to 
account for the fact that Mr. Dickerson provided a dif- 
ferent sequence of events. The recorded times therefore 
are crucial to assess which version is correct. The recorded 
times corroborate Mr. Dickerson’s version and rebut the

Dickerson was not Mirandized before he gave his state-

In a last ditch effort to avoid suppression, the Govern- 
ment argues that, even if Mr. Dickerson’s statements were 
obtained through custodial interrogation without Miranda 
warnings, the statements are still admissible under 18 
U.S.C. § 3501. Miranda v. Arizona, 384 US. 436 
(1966), and its progeny clearly hold that statements ob- 
tained during custodial interrogation are not admissible 
unless the requisite warnings are given prior to question- 
ing. The Government cites no cases, and defense counsel 
is unaware of any, which hold that the passage of 18 
U.S.C. § 3501 in 1968 overruled the Miranda doctrine. 
18 U.S.C. § 3501 is a procedural statute which sets forth 
how and when a trial court is to assess the voluntariness 
of a confession. The fact that Miranda warnings have 
been given does not, of itself, lead to the conclusion that 
a confession was voluntary. Brown v_ Illinois, 422 U.S. 
590 (1975); U.S. v. Johnson, 495 F.2d 3,8 (4th Cir. 
1974). However. the failure to give Miranda warnings 
prior to custodial interrogation conclusively renders any 
statements involuntary and inadmissible.

B. The Court correctly ruled that the warrant was in- 
validly vague and that the officers executing it acted in 
bad faith. 


1. The search warrant did not state with particularity 
the items to be seized. 


Prior counsel for Mr. Dickerson provided the Court 
with a lengthy brief examining the law and cases govern- 
ing the particularity requirement for search warrants. See 
Defendant’s Second Supplemental Memorandum in Sup- 
port of Defendant’s Motion to Suppress Statements and 
Physical Evidence, pp. 7-14. In its Motion to Recon- 
sider, the Government has not provided the Court with 
any new materials in support of its position that the items 
to be seized were sufficiently described in the warrant. On 
this issue, the Government is relying on its original plead- 
ings. For the sake of brevity and judicial economy, the 
Defendant will do the same. 


The Government does offer to the Court the affidavit 
of Special Agent Wenko who executed the search warrant. 
In the affidavit, Agent Wenko explains that, given his 
years of experience and familiarity with this investigation, 
he knew what items to search for and limited his search 
accordingly. It is precisely this type of on the scene police 
discretion that the particularity requirement of the Fourth 
Amendment prohibits. This same use of discretion was 
addressed by the Court in United States v. Buck: 


While it can safely be said that the police here per- 
formed reasonably under the circumstances . . . they 
clearly did not insure that all the known facts were 
included in the warrant. The warrant only described 
the crimes—and gave no limitation whatsoever on 
the kind of evidence sought. In short, “the warrant 
left it entirely to the discretion of the officials con- 


ducting the search to decide what items were to be 
seized, and thus was not permissible under the Fourth 
Amendment. 


813 F.2d 588, 589 (2nd Cir. 1987). 


Warrants which fail to state as nearly as possible the 
distinguishing characteristics of the goods to be seized 
are conclusively invalid. United States v. Robertson, 21 
F.3d 1030, 1033 (10th Cir. 1994). The fact that the 
executing officers may have conducted a reasonably lim- 
ited search will not cure the problem. It is the lack of 
review and guidance from a neutral magistrate which ren- 
ders the warrant constitutionally invalid. 


2. The “good-faith” exception to the warrant require- 
ment is not applicable. 


The Fourth Amendment to the Constitution states that 
“no warrant shall issue but upon probable cause.” The 
Fourth Circuit has stated that “(p]robable cause to search 
exists when, at the time the magistrate issues the war- 
rant, there are reasonably trustworthy facts which, given 
the totality of the circumstances, are sufficient to lead a 
prudent person to believe that the items sought constitute 
fruits, instrumentalities, or evidence of crime and will be 
present at the time and place of the search.” United 
States v. Suarez, 906 F.2d 977, 984 (4th Cir. 1990). 
“The ultimate inquiry on a motion to suppress evidence 
seized pursuant to a warrant is . . . whether, putting aside 
all tainted allegations, the independent and lawful infor- 
mation stated in the affidavit suffices to show probable 
cause.” United States v. Giordano, 416 U.S. 505, 555 
(1974) (Powell, J. concurring in part and dissenting in 
part); United States v. Hawkins, 788 F.2d 200, 203 
(4th Cir. 1986). 


124 


Pursuant to the “good-faith” exception adopted in 
United States v. Leon, 463 U.S. 981 (1983), evidence 
obtained by law enforcement officers acting in reasonable 
reliance on a search warrant issued by a detached and 
neutral magistrate but ultimately found to be unsupported 
by probable cause can still be used in the prosecution’s 
case-in-chief. Even under this broad exception, however, 
suppression is still appropriate where the. warrant was 
based on an affidavit so lacking in indicia of probable 
cause as to render official belief in its existence entirely 
unreasonable. 


The search warrant issued in this case is based on just 
such an affidavit. The affidavit, a recorded oral statement 
by Special Agent Lawlor given under oath over the tele- 
phone, contains information regarding cash seen in Mr. 
Dickerson’s residence and statements made by Mr. Dick- 
erson while in police custody. This information is un- 
constitutionally tainted and must be redacted from the 
affidavit before determining the existence of probable 
cause. The affidavit does not contain any information 
supporting the detective’s assertion that relevant evidence 
would be located in the residence. This is especially true 
once the tainted information is removed. 

Similar affidavits have been characterized as “bare- 
boned” leading to the conclusion that the magistrate 
abandoned his detached and neutral role and acted merely 
as a “rubber stamp.” Under such circumstances, the 
good-faith exception of Leon cannot be applied and evi- 
dence obtained pursuant to the search warrant must be 
suppressed. 

{T]he good-faith exception to the exclusionary rule 
should not apply . . . due to the “bare bones” nature 
of the affidavit, and because the state magistrate 
could not have acted as other than a “rubber stamp” 


125 


in approving such an affidavit. See United States v. 
Laury, 985 F.2d 1293, 1311 n. 23 (Sth Cir. 1993) 
(defining “bare bones affidavit” as one that contains 
“wholly conclusory statements, which lack the facts 
and circumstances from which a magistrate can in- 
dependently determine probable cause.” ) 


United States v. Wilhelm, 80 F.3d 116 (4th Cir. 1996) 
(citing United States v. Leake, 998 F.2d 1359, 1367 
(6th Cir. 1993) and United States v. Barrington, 806 
F.2d 529, 532 (Sth Cir. 1986) ). 


Any belief that evidence related to robbery could be 
found in Mr. Dickerson’s residence was based on a mere 
hunch or suspicion, not known facts articulated in the 
affidavit. This lack of known facts is precisely why the 
warrant did not state with particularity what was to be 
seized during the search: Agent Lawlor did not know 
what might be found in the residence, therefore, he could 
not state it with particularity. The law is well settled that 
substantially more than this is required to support the 
issuance of a search warrant. 


Given the lack of facts in the affidavit upon which to 
conclude that evidence of robbery could be found in Mr. 
Dickerson’s residence as well as the lack of any state- 
ments limiting the scope of the search, any official reliance 
on the warrant by the executing officers was unreasonable. 
As in Wilhelm, supra., this is not a case of “objectively 
reasonable law enforcement activity.” The sworn state- 
ment by Special Agent Lawlor did not “provide the mag- 
istrate with a substantial basis for determining the exist- 
ence of probable cause.” United States v. Leon, 468 U.S. 
at 923. In addition, the magistrate acted as a rubber 
stamp in finding the evidence sufficient to establish prob- 
able cause to search. Officers will not be allowed to claim 
reliance on a warrant if it is “so facially deficient—i.e., 


126 


in failing to particularize the place to be searched or the 
things to be seized—that the executing officers cannot rea- 
sonably presume it to be valid.” United States v. Owens, 
842 F.2d 462 (4th Cir. 1988), citing United States v. 
Leon, 468 U.S. 897, 923 (1984). 


The warrant issued to search Mr. Dickerson’s home was 
overly broad, vague, and unsupported by probable cause. 
The search executed pursuant to this invalid warrant was 
unconstitutional because the warrant was so lacking in 
indicia of probable cause and particularity as to render 
official belief in its validity unreasonable.
