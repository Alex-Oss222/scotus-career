INTEREST OF AMICI 


The interest of the States in this case is obvious, even 
though 18 U.S.C. Section 3501 applies only in federal 
criminal proceedings. To the extent, if any, that the Court 
permits the admission of unwarned but voluntary 
confessions under Section 3501, some States undoubtedly 
would adopt procedures similar to any which this Court 


might approve. 


Even though the Court stated in Miranda that the 
procedures set forth therein should not be regarded as “a 
constitutional straitjacket which will handicap sound efforts 
at reform,” 384 U.S. at 467, the case has been regarded as 
just that for nearly thirty-four years. Subsequent cases have 
not approved alternatives to the Miranda procedures—they 
have only found instances where for one reason or another 
the procedures did not apply. The States thus have an 
abiding interest in seeing how they might develop 
“increasingly effective ways of protecting the rights of the 
individual while promoting efficient enforcement of [state] 
criminal laws.” 384 U.S. at 467. The effectuation of this 
promise made by the Miranda court is an event which is 
long overdue. 


The States also have an overpowering interest in 
seeing that the guilty are punished and the innocent set free. 
To the extent that it prevents voluntary confessions from 
occurring at all, Miranda increases the chances that the 
innocent will be punished; and to the extent that it prohibits 
the use of voluntary confessions once given, Miranda 
greatly increases the chances that the guilty will be set free.

SUMMARY OF ARGUMENT 


The holdings of Miranda and the provisions of 
Section 3501 may and should be read together harmoniously 
as creating a rule which expresses a strong preference for 
giving the Miranda warnings but without automatically 
requiring the suppression of confessions which are 
voluntary but unwarned. By permitting the admission of 
unwamed but voluntary confessions upon a strong showing 
of voluntariness, this proposed procedure would preserve 
the dual ends of preventing involuntary custodial 
confessions while at the same time preventing acquittals 
based on technicalities.’ 


Miranda’ s exclusionary rule for unwarned custodial 
confessions amounts to an irrebuttable presumption that 
such confessions are involuntary. The Court’s language in 
Miranda indicates that the rule thus created was intended by 
the Court to be a rule of procedure or evidence, subject to 
modification by Congress and the States. 


In the late 1960's, Congress conducted hearings and 
developed evidence showing the effects of Miranda on law 
enforcement. After weighing the competing policies in light 
of the facts it found, Congress enacted Section 3501. 


' Amici States are aware, of course, that the United States and 
others argue that if Section 350) is read as simply reinstating the pre- 
Miranda voluntariness test, then either the statute must be regarded as 
unconstitutional, or Mirand~ nust be substantially modified in order 
to accommodate the statut. Amici States submit that the two can be 
harmonized without the need for such a collision. 


3 


It is not necessary to overrule Miranda in order to 
hold Section 3501 constitutional. Section 3501 should be 
given a reading which will preserve its constitutionality and 
harmonize it with Miranda. Section 3501 thus should be 
read as substituting 2 rebuttable presumption for Miranda’s 
irrebuttable presumption, that is, as Congress’s mandate 
concerning the standards which need to be met if the 
confession is still to be admitted. Applying the statute in 
this manner is permissible in order to preserve its 
constitutionality. 


If the Court permits the use of the standards in 
Section 3501 as a means to rebut Miranda’s presumption of 
inadmissibility, the States would then be given the 
opportunity to craft rules of their own which would balance 
the need for voluntary confessions with the need to protect 
Fifth Amendment rights. Such state procedures could 
simply copy the approach of Section 3501, or they could be 
more innovative. For instance, a guarantee of voluntariness 
might be obtained through videotaping of custodial 
confessions, a measure which was not conveniently 
available in the late 1960's. - 


If the Court reaffirms that the Miranda warnings are 
not themselves constitutional rights, such a holding would 
preserve the existing rule that law enforcement officers are 
not liable under 42 U.S.C. § 1983 for not giving the 
warnings. 


Finally, the States submit that regardless of the 
outcome of this case, the Court should reiterate that the 
Miranda procedures were intended as provisional, 


4 


prophylactic rules which may be modified by appropriate 
state and federal rulemaking bodies.

A. In Enacting Section 3501, Congress In The 
Exercise Of Its Rulemaking Power 
Determined That Miranda’s Irrebuttable 
Presumption Rule Should Be Modified. 


One of the most durable principles of federal 
jurisprudence is that Congress has the power “to prescribe 
rules of evidence and standards of proof in the federal 
courts.” Vance v. Terrazas, 444 U.S. 252, 265 (1980). This 
“traditional” power, id., has been recognized “{f]rom almost 
the founding days of this country.” Willy v. Coastal Corp., 
503 U.S. 131, 136 (1992). The roots of this Congressional 
authority lie in the constitutional authority of Congress to 
create inferior federal courts. U.S. Const., Art. 1, § 8, cl. 9. 
Vance, supra, cites a number of other cases in which this 
“undoubted” power of Congress has been noted and 
sustained. 444 U.S. at 266, citing Usery v. Turner Elkhorn 
Mining Co., 428 U.S. 1, 31 (1976); Hawkins v. United 
States, 358 U.S. 74, 78 (1958); and Tot v. United States, 319 
U.S. 463 (1943). 


Section 3501 represents an exercise of this 
Congressional power to prescribe rules of evidence and 
standards of proof. The enactment of this legislation was 
explicitly undertaken pursuant to Congress’s “power to 
prescribe rules of evidence in Federal courts,” and in 
“respon[se] to the [Miranda] majority’s invitation to 
Congress” to do so. [Senate Comm. Report, 1968 
U.S.C.C.A.N. at 2137]. By enacting Section 3501, 
Congress concluded that Miranda’s irrebuttable 
presumption of inadmissibility failed to achieve the correct 
result in an unacceptably high number of cases and therefore 
was based on an unsound policy.

1. Section 3501 reflects’ the 
Congressional determination that 
the procedures established by 
Miranda did not achieve the correct 
result in many cases. 


In determining whether a conclusive presumption 
should be imposed, the issue is whether the “supposed 
injurious results” from admitting evidence are “of greater 
consequence than the predominance of truth over error. . . .” 
United States v. Provident Trust Co., 291 U.S. 272, 282 
(1934). The Miranda court undoubtedly believed that the 
“supposed injurious results” of permitting unwarned 
custodial questioning were harmful enough to require the 
exclusion of all unwarned custodial confessions. After 
reviewing several years’ experience with Miranda, however, 
Congress disagreed with both the factual and policy 
judgments of the Court on this issue. 


There was nothing in the record of Miranda which 
suggested that holding unwarned confessions to be 
involuntary “achieve[d] the correct result in almost all 
cases.” Coleman v. Thompson, 501 U.S. 722 (1991). To 
hold otherwise would be to suggest that practically all 
custodial confessions in the pre-warning era were 
involuntary. Moreover, even if a substantia! percentage of 
all unwarned custodial confessions were in fact involuntary 
(a fact as to which there is no evidence), the remaining 
cases, i.e., the ones in which the presumption is wrong, are 
not unimportant cases. See Continental T.V., Inc. v. GTE 
Sylvania, Inc., 433 U.S. 36, 50 n.16 (1977)(presumptions 
reflect a “judgment that [cases involving exceptions are not 
sufficiently common or important to justify the time and 


expense necessary to identify them”). To the contrary, in 
many cases the exclusion of an unwarned but voluntary 
confession precludes the successful prosecution of the 
person who confessed. Many, if not all, of the offices of 
Attorney General of the amici States have been directly 
involved in such cases. 


Even though Miranda’s irrebuttable presumption has 
undoubtedly led to the suppression of voluntary confessions 
in anumber of cases, the Court’s rule was, prior to action by 
Congress, the only procedure in place to effectuate the core 
holdings of the case. However, it is undeniable that 
Congress had before it a great deal more information than 
could be placed before the Court in a single case; in 
addition, Congress had the benefit of reviewing the results 
of several years of the Miranda rules being applied in 


practice. 


This Court has frequently recognized that Congress 
“is far better equipped than the judiciary to amass and 
evaluate the vast amounts of data bearing upon legislative 
questions.” Turner Broadcasting System, Inc. v. F.C.C., 
520 U.S. 180, 195 (1997). Indeed, 


[t]he process of making the determination of 
rationality [of presumptions] is, by its nature, 
highly empirical, and in matters not within 
specialized judicial competence or completely 
commonplace, significant weight should be 
accorded the capacity of Congress to amass 
the stuff of actual experience and cull 
conclusions from it.

United States v. Gainey, 380 U.S. 63, 67 (1965). This rule 
is appropriate for this case: the determination of the overall 
numbers and types of cases in which involuntary 
confessions occur, or in which guilty defendants go free, is 
not within “specialized judicial competence.” The courts 
can only adjudicate one case at a time, and individual 
criminal cases do not provide vehicles for the presentation 
and review of facts concerning Miranda’s effects.‘ 
Moreover, as Gainey, supra, indicates, Congress’s findings 
receive this deference even when the conclusive 
presumption establishes substantive criminal liability, rather 
than a mere evidentiary presumption.

are not given, Section 3501 should be construed as allowing 
the presumption of inadmissibility to be overcome by a 
showing that the confession was in fact voluntary, using the 
standards set forth in Section 3501 as a guideline.

such a decision will be to permit the States to craft standards 
of their own. Some States will no doubt continue to follow 
the Miranda procedures. See, e.g., State v. Jones, 205 Conn. 
638, 534 A.2d 1129, 1202 n.3 (1987) (recognizing that 
Miranda warnings have independent significance under the 
state constitution); Traylor v. State, 596 So.2d 957, 965-66 
(Fla. 1992) (result similar to Miranda under the Self- 
Incrimination Clause of Article I, Section 9, Florida 
Constitution); State v. Santiago, 53 Hawaii 254, 492 P.2d 
657, 664 (1971) (similar, applying Hawaii Constitution's 
privilege against self-incrimination); State in Interest of 
Dino, 359 So.2d 586, 589 (La. 1978), cert. denied, 439 U.S. 
1047 (discussing La. Const. Art. 1, § 13 and concluding 
“{b]y adoption of this provision Louisiana enhanced and 
incorporated the prophylactic rules of Miranda v. Arizona”); 
State v. Prevost, 141 N.H. 647, 690 A.2d 1029, 1032 (1997) 
(“In New Hampshire, before evidence obtained during 
custodial interrogation can be used against a defendant, the 
State must establish ‘beyond a reasonable doubt that the 
defendant was apprised of his constitutional rights and that 
his subsequent waiver was voluntary, knowing, and 
intelligent”); State v. Prickett, 324 Or. 489, 930 P.2d 221, 
223 (Ore. 1997) (“This Court has held that Article I, section 
12 [of the Oregon Constitution] requires that a police officer 
give Miranda-like warnings before questioning a person 


15 


who is in ‘full custody’”); Texas Code Crim. Proc. Ann. art. 
38.22 (codifying Miranda warnings and exceptions). 


Other States may experiment with approaches such as 
videotaping or audiotaping of interrogations. See Stephan 
v. State, 711 P.2d 1156, 1158 (Alaska 1985) (requiring 
electronic recording custodial interrogation under Alaska 
Constitution); State v. Scales, 518 N.W.2d 587, 591-93 
(Minn. 1994) (requiring electronic recording of custodial 
interrogation under court's supervisory power). Other States 
may move more in the direction of Section 3501. See Ariz. 
Rev. Stat. Ann. § 13-3988 (similar to 18 U.S.C. § 3501(a), 
-(b), and -(d)). 


Finally, some States may decide go beyond the 
Miranda rules and give even more protection to suspects. 
See, e.g., People v. Harris, 77 N.Y .2d 434, 568 N.Y.S.2d 
702, 570 N.E.2d 1051 (1991) (declining to follow New York 
v. Harris, 495 U.S. 14 (1990), on grounds of “indelible” 
right to counsel under New York law that cannot be waived 
outside presence of an attorney); People v. Hobson, 39 
N.Y.2d 479, 384 N.Y.S.2d 419, 348 N.E.2d 894 (N.Y. 
1976) (once police know or have reason to know that an 
attorney has become involved in the case of a suspect in 
custody, they many not obtain a waiver of the right to 
counsel in the absence of that attorney). 


Regardless of the precise response of the States to a 
decision upholding Section 3501, the result would be that 
“the laboratory of the States” would be directed toward “the 
. . . Challenging task of crafting appropriate procedures.” 
Cruzon v. Director, Mo. Dept. of Health, 497 U.S. 262, 292 
(O’Connor, J., concurring); Smith v. Robbins, supra, 120 


16 


S.Ct. at 758 (2000). No longer would Miranda’ s procedures 
stand as a judicially-imposed “categorical remedy [which] 
pretermit[s] other responsible solutions being considered in 
Congress and state legislatures.” Smith v. Robbins, supra, 
at 758 (quoting Kennedy, J., concurring, in Murray v. 
Giarratano, 492 U.S. 1, 14 (1989)). 


IV. A Holding By This Court That The Warnings 
Themselves Are Not Constitutionally Required 
Would Reinforce The Settled Conclusion That 
There Can Be No Section 1983 Liability For 
Failure To Provide The Warnings. 


A reaffirmance by this Court of the non- 
constitutional character of the Miranda rules would prevent 
the unnecessary expansion of civil liability against police 
agencies. Many federal courts of appeals have concluded 
that because the Miranda procedures are not required by the 
Constitution, departures from them are not actionable under 
42 U.S.C. § 1983. See, e.g., Mahan v. Plymouth County 
House of Corrections, 64 F.3d 14, 17 (1st Cir. 1995); 
DeShawn v. Safir, 156 F.3d 340, 346 (2d Cir. 1998); Giuffre 
v. Bissell, 31 F.3d 1241, 1256 (3d Cir. 1994); Clay v. 
Brown, 151 F.3d 1032 (7th Cir. 1998)(table); Winsett v. 
Washington, 130 F.3d 269, 274 (7th Cir. 1997); Warren v. 
City of Lincoln, 864 F.2d 1436, 1441-42 (8th Cir. en banc), 
cert. denied, 490 U.S. 1091 (1989); Lucero v. Gunter, 17 
F.3d 1347, 135-51 (10th Cir. 1994); Bennett v. Passic, 545 
F.2d 1260, 1263 (10th Cir. 1976). In the swirling and often 
harried circumstances in which police questioning must of 
necessity often place, it would be inappropriate to allow line 
law enforcement officers and their employing agencies to be 
sued whenever they deviated from the Miranda regime.

Vv. The Application Of Miranda’s Prophylactic Rules 
To The States Should Be Approached With 
Caution. 


In keeping with principles of federalism, this Court 
repeatedly has acknowledged that it has no supervisory 
powers over state-court proceedings beyond those necessary 
“to correct wrongs of constitutional dimension.” Smith v. 
Phillips, 455 U.S. 209, 221 (1982); Mu '’Min v. Virginia, 500 
U.S. 415, 422 (1991) (“Our cases dealing with the 
requirements of voir dire are of two kinds: those that were 
tried in federal courts, and are therefore subject to this 
Court's supervisory power * * * and those that were tried in 
state courts, with respect to which our authority is limited to 
enforcing the commands of the United States Constitution”); 
Victor v. Nebraska, 511 U.S. 1, 17 (1994) (“we have no 
supervisory power over the state courts”). 


Accordingly, even if Section 3501 should be declared 
invalid, amici States would urge that the Court proceed 
cautiously in describing the manner in which the Miranda 
warnings apply to the States. Because this Court has held 
that the warnings are merely prophylactic, the requirements 
should be regarded as, at most, interim procedures which 
protect constitutional rights only until other procedures can 
be adopted by state bodies with the power to set such rules 
for the courts of the States. Any suggestion that the, States 
cannot alter prophylactic rules, or that such rules may only 
be altered by federal authorities, creates a risk to our federal 
system of government, under which the “primary authority 
for defining and enforcing the criminal law” rests with the 
States, United States v. Lopez, 514 U.S. 549, 561 n. 3 
(1995). See also, Heath v. Alabama, 474 U.S. 82, 93 (1985) 


18 


(“The Constitution leaves in the possession of each State 
‘certain exclusive and very important portions of sovereign 
power.” * * * Foremost among the prerogatives of 
sovereignty is the power to create and enforce a criminal 
code”); Kelly v. Robinson, 479 U.S. 36, 47 (1986) (“This 
Court has emphasized repeatedly the fundamental policy 
against federal interference with state criminal 
prosecutions.”); Moran v. Burbine, 475 U.S. 412, 425 
(1986) (“Nothing in the Constitution vests in us the 
authority to mandate a code of behavior for state officials 
wholly unconnected to any federal right or privilege”). The 
States therefore respectfully submit that the Court should 
reaffirm the powers of Congress and the States, respectively, 
to make appropriate alterations to prophylactic rules.
