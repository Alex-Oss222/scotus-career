STATEMENT OF THE CASE 


In the early morning hours of December 22, 1994, 
petitioner Charles Apprendi, Jr., a white pharmacist, fired 
several rounds from a loaded rifle into a nearby single-family 
house, using as targets two black Santa Claus decorations 
hanging on the front door. The residents, Michael and Mattie 
Fowlkes and their three children, ages 9, 13 and 16, were the 
only black family living in petitioner's otherwise all-white 
neighborhood in Vineland, New Jersey. (Pa2-3, 100-02, 107- 
09).' 


Petitioner was charged by the Cumberland County, 
New Jersey, Grand Jury in a twenty-three count indictment 
with four counts of attempted murder in the first degree, four 
counts of aggravated assault in the second degree, four counts 
of possession of a firearm for an unlawful purpose in the 
second degree, four counts of unlawful possession of a loaded

REASONS FOR DENYING THE PETITION 


In determining that petitioner's racial bias motivating his 
shooting of a loaded rifle at the Fowlkes' house was a 
sentencing factor subject to the “preponderance of the 
evidence" standard, and not an essential element of the 
underlying crime of possession = a ra ~ an am 
purpose requiring a jury finding of guilt beyond a reasonable 
doubt, the Supreme Court of New Jersey faithfully applied the 
constitutional calculus formulated by this Court in a series of 
decisions addressing the so-called "sentencing factor versus 
element" debate. The statutory scheme created by the 
extended term provision of New Jersey's Hate Crime Statute, 
N.J.Stat.Ann. 2C:44-3e (West 1995), punishes motive, not 
criminal intent, and thereby guarantees petitioner's Sixth 


am 


9 


Amendment right to notice by indictment and trial by jury, 
and Fifth and Fourteenth Amendments right to due process of 
law. See State v. Apprendi, 159 N.J. 7, 731 A.2d 485 (1999) 
(Pal-28). This adherence to United States Supreme Court 
precedent means that the issue decided by the state court may 
implicate federal constitutional provisions, but ultimately fails 
to present "an important question of federal law that has not 
been, but should be settled by this Court," or "an important 
federal question in a way that conflicts with relevant decisions 
of this Court." Supreme Court Rule 10(c).* Simply put, there 
are no "compelling reasons" for this Court to grant this 
petition for a writ of certiorari. Supreme Court Rule 10. 


At the time petitioner entered his guilty plea to possession 
of a weapon for an unlawful purpose, N.J.Stat.Ann. 2C:39-4a 
(West 1995), the extended term provision of New Jersey's 
Hate Crime Statute required the sentencing court to impose an 
increased maximum term of imprisonment if, after a hearing, 
the State proved by a preponderance of the evidence that in 
committing the predicate offense the defendant acted "with a 
purpose to intimidate an individual or group of individuals 
because of race, color, religion, sexual orientation or 
ethnicity." Petitioner confuses motive (the sine gua non of 
New Jersey's Hate Crime Statute) with criminal intent (the 
sine qua non of New Jersey's unlawful possession of a 
weapon statute) to claim that the Hate Crime Statute 
unconstitutionally allows the sentencing court to decide, by a 
preponderance of the evidence, a defendant's mental state 
under a statute which can increase the maximum sentence. 
(Pet. at 3). 


. Certainly, the final resolution of the federal constitutional 


question invoked by New Jersey's Hate Crime Statute does not conflict 
with any decision of any other state court of last resort or of a United 
States court of appeals. Supreme Court Rule 10(b). 


10 


Petitioner's argument that N.J.Stat.Ann. 2C:44-3e 
punishes criminal intent stems from the statute's reference to 
the accused's "purpose to intimidate." The Apprendi court, 
however, equated “purpose to intimidate" with "motive," 
(Pa22), and this Court is “bound by a state court's 
construction of a state statute." Wisconsin v. Mitchell, 508 
U.S. 476, 483 (1993). Motive has been recognized by this 
Court to be a traditional sentencing factor. Jd. at 485; see 
also State v. Mortimer, 135 N.J. 517, 528, 641 A.2d 517, 
528, cert. denied, 513 U.S. 970 (1994). Motive, not criminal 
intent, is what New Jersey's Hate Crime Statute punishes. 


By petitioner's own admission at the plea hearing, his 
criminal intent or mens rea, that is, his unlawful purpose for 
shooting at the Fowlkes’ house, was to frighten or harass the 
family asleep inside. (T22-4 to 29-18). Petitioner's reason 
for wanting to scare the family, that is, his motive, was to 
send the Fowlkes family "a message" that blacks were 
unwelcome in petitioner's neighborhood. (Pal74-78, 299). 
While the prosecution bore the burden to prove petitioner's 
criminal intent (his unlawful purpose) behind the shooting (his 
conduct), it had no burden to prove that his conduct was 
influenced by racial hatred (his motive). Motive is relevant to 
the issue of intent but is not an essential element of any crime 
in New Jersey, State v. Beard, 16 N.J. 50, 60-61, 106 A.2d 
265, 270 (1954); State v. Cherry, 289 N.J. Super. 503, 529, 
674 A.2d 589, 602 (App Div. 1995), including possession of 
a weapon for an unlawful purpose. State v. Harmon, 104 
N.J. 189, 212, 516 A.2d 1047, 1059 (1986). 


New Jersey's Hate Crime Statute gives "no impression of 
having been tailored to permit the [racial animosity] finding to 
be a tail which wags the dog of the substantive offense." 
McMillan v. Pennsylvania, 477 U.S. 79, 88 (1986). 
N.J.Stat.Ann. 2C:44-3e does not create any impermissible 
presumptions nor relieve the prosecution of its burden of 
proving guilt; indeed, it is entirely inapplicable unless the 
prosecution first proves a defendant's guilt of the underlying 


11 


offense beyond a reasonable doubt. See McMillan, 477 U.S. 
at 87. The differential in sentencing is not between a nominal 
fine and mandatory life sentence, see Mullaney v. Wilbur, 421 
U.S. 684, 700 (1975), but merely increases the ordinary 
sentencing range for a second degree offense of between five 
to ten years, N.J.Stat.Ann. 2C:43-6a(2) (West 1995), to an 
extended range of between ten and twenty years, 
N.J.Stat.Ann. 2C:43-7a(3) (West 1995). The statute does not 
change the definition of any existing offense or create a 
separate offense calling for a separate penalty, and there is 
nothing to imply that the state legislature drafted this statute 
to avoid the prosecution's reasonable doubt standard. See 
McMillan, 477 U.S. at 89-90; see also Pa20-22. The New 
Jersey Legislature "simply took one factor that has always 
been considered by sentencing courts to bear on punishment 
... and dictated the weight to be given that factor...." 
McMillan, 477 U.S. at 89-90. 


Although the McMillan Court speculated in dicta that the 
claim that a sentencing factor was really an element of the 
offense "would have at least more superficial appeal" if a 
finding of such fact exposed the defendant to greater or 
additional punishment, id. at 88, that consideration was 
subsequently criticized in Almendarez-Torres v. United States, 
523 U.S. 224, 118 S.Ct. 1219 (1998), in which this Court 
rejected the argument that any factor triggering an increase in 
the maximum permissive sentence is an element of the offense 
which must be proven beyond a reasonable doubt. 


In Almendarez-Torres, this Court held that subsection 
(b)(2) of 8 U.S.C. § 1326 did not describe an element of an 
offense but was simply a sentence enhancer. Subsection (a) of 
this statute defines a crime by forbidding an alien who once 
was deported to return to the United States without special 
permission, and it authorizes the imposition of a prison term 
of up to, but no more than, twenty years for "an alien 
described" in subsection (a), if the initial "deportation was 
subsequent to a conviction for commission of an aggravated 
felony." 8 U.S.C. § 1326(b)(2). The validity of this 


12 


sentencing scheme was due, in part, to the “longstanding 
tradition of treating recidivism as ‘going to the 
only.'" Almendarez-Torres, 118 S.Ct. at 1230-31 (quoting 
Graham v. West Virginia, 224 U.S. 616, 629 (1912)). 


Recently revisiting the sentencing factor/element 

in Jones v. United States, 526 U.S. 227, 119 S.Ct. 1215 
(1999), this Court determined that a penalty provision based 
on a finding of serious bodily injury or death intertwined 
within the definition of the substantive crime of 

under the federal carjacking statute, 18 U.S.C. § 2119, was 
not a sentence enhancer that could be found by the sentencing 
court by a preponderance of the evidence, but was historically 
an element of the offense that had to be proven to the trier of 
fact beyond a reasonable doubt. The federal carjacking statute 
was structurally muddled in its treatment of injury as an 
element or penalty factor, thus prompting the Jones Court to 
consider the “traditional treatment of certain categories of 
important facts ... in relation to particular crimes." Jones, 
119 S.Ct. at 1220. Under this approach, the enhanced penalty 
provision of the federal carjacking statute could not be viewed 
aS a mere sentencing factor because the degree of injury to a 
victim has traditionally been treated, both by Congress and by 
state legislatures (including New Jersey, see State v. Federico, 
103 N.J. 169, 510 A.2d 1147 (1986)), as an element of the 
predicate offense, such as aggravated robbery. Jones, 119 
S.Ct. at 1220.° 


. This Court also took a structural approach in Almendarez- 
Torres, 118 S.Ct. at 1224-26, and in Jones, 119 S.Ct. at 1219-1222, to 
determine whether Congress intended to create new substantive offenses. 
Under this analysis, the extended term provision of New Jersey's Hate 
Crime Statute is clearly constitutional. The statute appears in a general 
sentencing provision of New Jersey's Code of Criminal Justice entitled 
"Authority of Court in Sentencing,” and was adopted long after the Code's 
provisions establishing the underlying crimes. State v. Apprendi, 304 NJ. 
Super. 147, 160, 698 A.2d 1265, 1272 (App. Div. 1997) (Stern, J.,

In reaching this result, this Court was careful not to 
overrule its prior decision in Almendarez-Torres that “not 
every fact expanding a penalty range must be stated in a 
felony indictment." Jones, 119 S.Ct. at 1226-27. Instead, the 
Jones Court acknowledged that recidivism, the factor in issue 
in Almendarez-Torres, historically is treated as a sentencing 
enhancer and is “potentially distinguishable for constitutional 
purposes from other facts that might extend the range of 
possible sentencing." Jones, 119 S.Ct. at 1227. Similarly, 
the instrumentality used in committing a violent felony "has 
always been considered by sentencing courts to bear on 
punishment." McMillan, 477 U.S. at 89-90 (this Court found 
constitutional a provision of Pennsylvania's Mandatory 
Minimum Sentencing Act, 42 Pa.Const.Stat.Ann. § 9712, 
authorizing judges to sentence anyone convicted of certain

of the offense).

concurring) (Pa79). The statute is expressly designated “Criteria for 
sentence of extended term of imprisonment." The plain language and 
structure of the Hate Crime Statute unquestionably establishes that the 
Statute does not add any new elements to any crime as defined in the Code, 
but was intended only as a sentencing enhancer. See Jones, 119 S.Ct. at 
1219 ("some statutes come with the benefit of provisions straightforwardly 
addressing the distinction between elements and sentencing factors”) and 
McMillan, 477 U.S at 85-86 (express identification of statutory provision 
as sentencing factor). 


The Supreme Court of New Jersey, however, chose not to rely upon 
this structural approach: “Merely because the Legislature has placed the 
hate-crimes enhancer within the sentencing provisions of the Code of 
Criminal Justice does not mean that the finding of a biased purpose to 
intimidate is not an essential element of the offense.” (Pal6). The court 
relied instead on the constitutional analysis employed by the Almendarez- 
Torres Court, thus removing all reservations about the constitutionality of 
the extended term provision of the Hate Crime Statute.

Any claim by petitioner that Jones leaves open the 
question whether any fact resulting in a substantial increase in 
the penalty range is necessarily an essential element that must 
be proven beyond a reasonable doubt is rebuffed by the Jones 
Court's outright approval of the challenged statute in 
Almendarez-Torres authorizing a sentencing court to increase 
the punishment for a recidivist deportee from two years 
imprisonment, 8 U.S.C. § 1326(a), to twenty years 
imprisonment, 8 U.S.C. § 1326(b)(2). Jones, 119 S.Ct. at 
1227. The Jones Court said, “our decision today does not 
‘announce any new principle of constitutional law, but merely 
interprets a particular federal statute in light of a set of 
constitutional concerns that have emerged through a series of 
our decisions over the past quarter century." /d. at 1228 
n.l1. The penalty enhancer embodied in N.J.Stat.Ann. 
2C:44-3e is based upon the traditional nature of motive as a 
sentencing factor; consequently, motive need not be charged 
in the indictment, Almendarez-Torres, nor must it be 
submitted to the jury and proven beyond a reasonable doubt, 
Jones. 


Significantly, N.J. Court Rule 3:21-4(e) requires the 
prosecution to give notice to the defendant of its intention to 
seek an extended term under N.J.Stat.Ann. 2C:44-3e within 
fourteen days of the entry of the defendant's guilty plea or of 
the return of the verdict or, where the defendant is pleading 
guilty pursuant to a negotiated disposition, at or prior to the 
plea. N.J.Stat.Ann. 2C:44-3e permits the sentencing court to 
impose an extended term only after finding racial bias by a 
preponderance of the evidence. The Hate Crime Statute thus 
affords criminal defendants the requisite procedural safeguards 
of notice and opportunity to be heard. Compare Specht v. 
Patterson, 386 U.S. 605, 608 (1967) (commitment 
proceedings under Colorado Sex Offenders Act did not permit 
defendant an opportunity to present his own evidence or 
confront witnesses against him). Petitioner here in fact knew 
of the State's intention to seek an extended term prior to the 
entry of his guilty plea (T9-20 to 10-8), and offered the

testimony of seven character witnesses and one expert witness 
at the sentencing hearing before Judge Ridgway. (Pal82- 
235). As such, N.J.Stat.Ann. 2C:44-3e comports with due 
process of law. 


One final point bears noting. The Supreme Court of New 
Jersey did not "invite" this Court to review the extended term 
provision of New Jersey's Hate Crime Statute; what the state 
court said was that at some future date this Court, in its 
supervisory capacity, would likely scrutinize "the recently 
enacted amendments to the federal sentencing guidelines 
[which] now include a hate crime penalty enhancement 
provision that applies to all federal crimes." (Pal7-18). 
Petitioner, of course, was not sentenced under the federal 
Statute and has no standing before this Court to challenge its 
constitutionality. The question presented by petitioner, 
“whether this Court should decline the invitation of the New 
Jersey Supreme Court" to review N.J.Stat.Ann. 2C:44-3e, 
should be answered with a resounding "yes." 


In sum, New Jersey's Hate Crime Statute creates a 
constitutionally-valid statutory scheme permitting the 
sentencing court to impose an extended term of imprisonment 
once it is satisfied by a preponderance of the evidence that the 
underlying crime was racially motivated. The New Jersey 
Legislature did not create a new element of any offense that 
must be proven beyond a reasonable doubt, but took "one 
factor that has always been considered by sentencing courts to 
bear on punishment and dictated the weight to be given that 
factor." (Pa22). The conclusion reached by the Supreme 
Court of New Jersey that the Hate Crime Statute does not 
transgress the constitutional limits erected by this Court is 
firmly supported by this Court's decisions addressing the 
"sentencing factors versus elements" debate and insures that 
petitioner's constitutional rights were fully protected.
