STATEMENT OF THE CASE 

Federal jurisdiction arose in the district court when, on January 28, 1999, the petitioner, 
Charles T. Dickerson, was arrested and charged by criminal complaint with one count of aiding 
and abetting a bank roovery in violation of 18 U.S.C. §§ 2 and 2113(a) and (d). On April 16, 
1997, Mr. Dickerson was indicted by a federal grand jury in the Eastern District of Virginia on 
one count of conspiracy to commit bank robbery in violation of 18 U.S.C. § 371, three counts of 
bank robbery in violation of 18 U.S.C. § 2113(a) and (d), and three counts of using a firearm 
during a bank robbery in violation of 18 U.S.C. § 924(c).’ 

The facts material to consideration of the questions presented in this Petition for a Writ of 
Certiorari are as follows: 

On January 24, 1997, a NationsBank in Alexandria, Virginia was robbed. Shortly 
thereafter, Special Agent Christopher Lawlor of the Federal Bureau of Investigation learned that 
the robbery had been committed with a large, silver, semi-automatic handgun, that the robber had 
placed the stolen money in a black leather backpack, that approximately $876 was taken in the 
robbery, and that no dye-pack or bait money was given to the robber. Witnesses had seen the 
robber, later identified as James Rochester, leave the bank and enter an Oldsmobile Cierra. Using 
the description of the getaway car, including its license plate number, the FBI determined that the 


petitioner, Charles Dickerson, was the car's owner. 


? A superseding indictment was issued on June 24, 1997. It charged the same offenses 
except it alleged that Mr. Dickerson acted as an aider and abettor to the bank robberies and 
firearm offenses in violation of 18 U.S.C. § 2. 


Agent Lawlor and several other law enforcement officers went to Mr. Dickerson's home at 
8313 Garland Avenue, Apartment 3, in Takoma Park, Maryland. They arrived at the residence at 
approximately 5:30 p.m. on the evening of January 27, 1997. An Oldsmobile Cierra registered to 
Mr. Dickerson was parked in the vicinity. The officers set-up surveillance. After waiting over 
one hour, they knocked on Mr. Dickerson's door. 


Approximately ten FBI agents and local police officers were outside of Mr. Dickerson's 


_apartment with their guns drawn when Mr. Dickerson answered the door. Three or four, 


including Special Agent Lawlor, entered Mr. Dickerson's apartment without a warrant and 
without the consent of Mr. Dickerson. The officers asked Mr. Dickerson to accompany them to 
the FBI field office in Washington, D.C. Given the circumstances, Mr. Dickerson felt he had no 
choice but to do as the officers requested. He asked to retrieve his coat, turned, and walked to 
his bedroom. Upon reaching the room, Mr. Dickerson realized that Agent Lawlor and another 
officer had followed him to his bedroom from his apartment's entrance. 

Mr. Dickerson picked up his coat. Under the coat was $552 in cash. Mr. Dickerson had 
won this money on a recent trip to Atlantic City. The agents forced Mr. Dickerson to count out 
this money on the bed before allowing him to put it in his pocket. No evidence linking Mr. 
Dickerson to the robbery of the NationsBank was observed by the officers in the apartment. Prior 
to leaving, Mr. Dickerson was asked to consent to a search of the residence. He refused and was 
driven by the officers to the FBI field office. 

Once at the station, Mr. Dickerson was interviewed by Agent Lawlor and another FBI 


agent. He was not advised of his Miranda rights prior to this interrogation. Initially, Mr. 


Dickerson denied any involvement in the robbery, admitting only that he had been in the vicinity 


of the bank at the time it was robbed. 

After obtaining this statement, Agent Lawlor left the interrogation room and placed a call 
to the Honorable James E. Kenkel, United States Magistrate Judge for the District of Maryland, 
in order to obtain a search warrant for Mr. Dickerson's home. As probable cause for the warrant, 
Agent Lawlor described the circumstances of the robbery and the fact that the getaway car had 
been traced to Mr. Dickerson. In addition, Agent Lawlor noted that Dickerson, who had been 
behind on his rent, had just paid his landlord $1,350. He told tine magistrate that earlier in the 
evening he and other officers had gone to Mr. Dickerson's home in Maryland and escorted him to 
the FBI Field Office in Washington, D.C. Agent Lawlor also mentioned the $552 he had seen in 
Mr. Dickerson's possession. Agent Lawlor then asserted that exigent circumstances necessitated 
an immediate search of the home because "Mr. Dickerson [was] not under arrest. . . and [was] 
free to go home." Agent Lawlor said he believed that if Mr. Dickerson went home, he would 
destroy evidence of the robbery under investigation. 

During the conversation between Judge Kenkel and Agent Lawlor, no discussion took 
place concerning what particular items were being sought under the warrant. The warrant stated 
only that there was probable cause to believe “evidence of the crime of bank robbery" would be 
found in Mr. Dickerson's home. Judge Kenkel approved the warrant at 8:50 p.m. At the time 
Agent Lawlor requested the warrant, he knew the $552 was no longer in the residence but was at 
the FBI field office. In fact, Mr. Dickerson had allowed Agent Lawlor to count and seize the 
money prior to the agent's call to Judge Kenkel. Agent Lawlor had seen no other incriminating 
evidence in the home and “had no way of knowing" what a search might uncover. After obtaining 


the warrant, Agent Lawlor dispatched other officers to Mr. Dickersou's home to conduct the 


search. The search uncovered a silver .45 caliber handgun, dye stained money, a bait bill from a 
separate bank robbery, and other evidence. 

After obtaining the warrant, Agent Lawlor returned to the interrogation room and told 
Mr. Dickerson his home was going to be searched. Mr. Dickerson then agreed to make another 
statement. He told the agents he had gone to Alexandria on January 24, 1997, with a man named 
Jimmy. Mr. Dickerson knew Jimmy because Jimmy was married to his cousin. While in 
Alexandria, Mr. Dickerson said he parked his car and went ir.to a bagel shop. Initially, Jimmy 
waited in the car, but as Mr. Dickerson walked away, he saw Jimmy get out of the car. When Mr. 
Dickerson returned to his car, Jimmy was already back in it. When Mr. Dickerson began to drive 
away, Jimmy told him to pull over. He complied, thinking Jimmy had to use the bathroom. 
Jimmy got out and returned after a few minutes. When Mr. Dickerson stopped at a stop sign, 
Jimmy got out of the car and put something in the trunk. When Mr. Dickerson stopped at a red 
light a short distance down the street, Jimmy told him to run it. It was at this point that Mr. 
Dickerson began to suspect that Jimmy may have committed a robbery. Mr. Dickerson knew that 
Jimmy had come to the Washington, D.C. area after committing a number of robberies in 
Georgia. 

After giving this statement, Mr. Dickerson was advised for the first time of his nghts 
under Miranda v. Arizona 384 U.S. 436 (1966). This was done using a rights waiver form which 
arrest. He then left the field office with the agents and showed them Jimmy's apartment in Oxon 
Hill, Maryland. Based on this information, the FBI obtained the arrest of James Rochester, an 
individual wanted in connection with a number of bank robberies along the east coast. Following 


his arrest, James Rochester admitted that he had robbed several banks, including the NationsBank 
in Alexandria on January 24, 1997. 

On May 19, 1997, the petitioner filed a Motion to Suppress Statements and Physical 
Evidence. On May 30, 1997, a hearing on the Motion to Suppress was held in the United States 
District Court for the Eastern District of Virginia, Alexandria Division, before the Honorable 
James C. Cacheris. The matter was taken advisement by the district court, and, at the court's 
request, the parties filed supplemental memoranda in support of their positions. 

On July 1, 1997, the district court issued an Order and Memorandum Opinion granting the 
defendant's Motion to Suppress as to his statements offered at the FBI field Office on January 27, 
1997, and as to items discovered during the se*rh of his home that same evening. The portion of 
the motion seeking suppression of items discovered during a search of the defendant's Oldsmobile 
Cierra was denied. 

On July 15, 1997, the United States filed a motion asking the district court to reconsider 
that portion of its Order granting suppression of the defendant's statement and the items 
discovered in the search of his home. A hearing on the Motion to Reconsider was held on August 
1, 1997. On August 4, 1997, the district court denied the United States’ Motion for 
Reconsideration. On August 6, 1997, the district court stayed trial of this matter pending appeal 
by the United States. On September 2, 1997, pursuant to 18 U.S.C. § 3731, the United States 
noticed its appeal. 

On February 8, 1999, a panel of the Fourth Circuit Court of Appeals, in a 2 to 1 decision, 
reversed the district court. United States v._ Dickerson, 166 F.3d 667 (4" Cir. 1999). As to Mr. 
Dickerson’s statements, the Court ruled that, although taken in violation of the requirements of 


Miranda, the statements were otherwise voluntarily made and therefore admissible pursuant to 18 
U.S.C. § 3501. As to the evidence obtained from Mr. Dickerson’s home, the Court ruled that the 
warrant’s sole directive to search for “evidence of the crime of bank robbery” was sufficiently 
particular to pass constitutional muster, and, in any event, was relied upon in good-faith by the 
executing officers. 

By Amended Order dated April 1, 1999, the full Fourth Circuit, by an eight to five vote, 


denied Mr. Dickerson’s Petition for Rehearing and Petition for Rehearing En Banc. 


ARGUMENT 

I. The Miranda warnings are constitutionally required; therefore, passage of 18 
U.S.C. § 3501 was an uncenstitutional attempt by Congress to legislatively overrule the 
Supreme Court. 

In enacting 18 U.S.C. § 3501, Congress attempted to secure the admissibility in federal 
courts of statements that would otherwise be suppressed under the Supreme Court’s decision in 
Miranda v. Arizona, 384 U.S. 436 (1966). The Court has recently emphasized, however, that 
Congress has no power to overrule its interpretations of the Constitution. City of Boerne v. 
Florida, 117 S. Ct. 2157, 2163-64 (1997). As the Fourth Circuit panel opinion in this case 
recognized, the question of whether § 3501 or Miranda governs the admissibility of Mr. 
Dickerson’s statements depends on whether Congress possesses the authority to overrule 
Miranda. Dickerson, 166 F. 3d at 672. If Miranda represents the Supreme Court’s 
interpretation of the Constitution, § 3501 is invalid. 

Contrary to the Fourth Circuit’s conclusion, the holding of Miranda was deemed 


constitutionally compelled by the Supreme Court. In deciding Miranda, the Court was not 
interpreting a statute, a federal rule of procedure, or a rule of evidence. Rather, the Court applied 
the Fifth Amendment’s privilege against self-incrimination in the context of custodial interrogation 
and constructed a rule as a result of that application. Thus, the Court was engaging in 
constitutional decision making. 

Proponents of § 3501 attempt to rely on Supreme Court cases referring to the Miranda 
warnings as “prophylactic” to argue that Miranda is not based on the Fifth Amendment and 
therefore can be overruled by statute. The fallacy of this position is revealed by three separate 
facets of Supreme Court precedent: (i) the language of Miranda itself, (ii) the Supreme Court’s 
continued application of Miranda in state court cases, and (iii) the Supreme Court’s recognition 
that an alleged Miranda violation is cognizable in federal habeas review. All three aspects of 
Miranda demonstrate that the decision is firmly based in the constitutional guarantees of the Fifth 
Amendment. 

The Miranda Court repeatedly and unequivocally stated throughout its opinion that its 
decision was based in the Constitution itself. The first paragraph of the opinion established this 
fundamental point: 

The cases before us raise questions which go to the roots of our concepts 

of American criminal jurisprudence: the restraints society must observe consistent 

with the Federal Constitution in prosecuting individuals for crime. More 

specifically, we deal with the admissibility of statements obtained from an 

individual who is subjected to custodial police interrogation and the necessity for 

procedures which assure that the individual is accorded his privilege under the 

Fifth Amendment to the Constitution not to be compelled to incriminate himself. 

384 U.S. at 439. Later in the opinion, the Court reiterated that the “constitutional issue we 


decide in each of these cases is the admissibility of statements obtained from a defendant 


10 


quest:oned while in custody or otherwise deprived of his freedom of action in any significant 
way.” Id. at 445. The holding of the Court was also described as unmistakably based on a 
constitutional violation. The confessions at issue were held inadmissible because each was 
obtained “under circumstances that did not meet constitutional standards for protection of the 
privilege.” Id. at 491. 

Although the Miranda Court invited Congress and the states to develop other effective 
ways to protect the constitutional rights of individuals subjected to custodial interrogation, it 
emphasized that “unless we are shown other procedures which are at least as effective in apprising 
accused persons of their right of silence and in assuring a continuous opportunity to exercise it, 
the following safeguards must be observed.” 384 U.S. at 467. This language clearly did not leave 
Congress free to eliminate the requisite warnings through legislation designed to overturn the 
decision. Indeed, the Court emphasized that “the issues presented are of constitutional dimension 
and must be determined by the courts. . . . Where rights secured by the Constitution are involved, 
there can be no rule making or legislation which would abroga*e them.” Id. at 490-91. 

An examination of the Supreme Court’s jurisprudence since Miranda further demonstrates 
that Miranda cannot properly be viewed as merely an exercise of the Court’s supervisory powers, 
Perhaps the most important indication that the Court does not regard Miranda as resting simply 
on its supervisory powers is the fact that the Court has continued to apply the Miranda rules to 
cases arising in state courts. See, e.g., Stansbury v. California, 511 U.S. 318 (1994); Minnick v. 
Mississippi, 498 U.S. 146 (1990); Arizona v. Roberson, 486 U.S. 675 (1988). Ir. ‘act, three of 
the four consolidated cases which comprise Miranda arose in state courts. Although the Court 


has the authority to announce rules of procedure and evidence binding on federal courts, it has 


11 


consistently disclaimed any such supervisory authority over the state courts. With respect to 
cases tried before state tribunals, the Court’s “authority is limited to enforcing the commands of 
the United States Constitution.” Mu’Min y. Virginia, 500 U.S. 415, 422 (1991). The federal 
judiciary “may not require the observance of any special procedures” in state courts “except when 
necessary to assure compliance with the dictates of the Federal Constitution.” Harris v. Rivera, 
454 U.S. 339, 344-45 (1981). As such, the Supreme Court’s continued application of Miranda's 
exclusionary rule in state cases leads to the inescapable conclusion that Miranda rests on 
constitutional underpinnings.’ 

Finally, the Supreme Court has held that claims that a conviction rests on statements 
obtained in violation of Miranda are cognizable on federal habeas review. Withrow v. Williams, 
507 U.S. 680, 690-95 (1993). Habeas review is only available for claims that a person “is in 
custody in violation of the Constitution or the laws or treaties of the United States.” 28 U.S.C. § 
2254(a). Miranda certainly does not involve laws or treaties. Thus, the Court’s holding in 
Withrow depends on the conclusion that the requirements of Miranda arise from and protect | 
constitutional rights. 

In attempting to prevent Withrow from extending federal habeas review, the government 


argued that since the Miranda rules “are not constitutional in character, but merely

constitutionally based decision, arose in state courts. Although both decisions narrowed the 
scope of Miranda's exclusionary rule (Tucker by declining to apply the holding retroactively to 
statements obtained prior to the Miranda decision, Elstad by declining to suppress a properly 
obtained confession after earlier statements were obtained in violation of Miranda), both 
decisions reaffirmed Miranda's basic conclusion that unwarned statements must be excluded from 
the government’s case-in-chief. Tucker, 417 U.S. at 445; Elstad, 479 U.S. at 307.

‘prophylactic,”” federal habeas review should not extend to claims based on violations of these 
rules. Withrow, 507 U.S. 690. The Court declined to reach this conclusion. Although Justice 
Souter, writing for the majority, did not deny that “we have sometimes called the Miranda 
safeguards ‘prophylactic’ in nature,” he concluded that “[p}rophylactic though it may be, in 
protecting a defendant's Fifth Amendment privilege against self-incrimination, Miranda 
safeguards ‘a fundamental trial right.” 507 U.S. at 691. The Court went on to state that Miranda 
“brace[s] against ‘the possibility of unreliable statements in every instance of in-custody 
interrogation,”” and thereby “serves to guard against ‘the use of unreliable statements at trial.” 
507 U.S. at 692. 

Miranda is hardly the sole example of a constitutionally based decision which contains a 
bright-line rule. For example, in North Carolina v. Pearce, 395 U.S. 711 (1969), the Court held 
that a “presumption of vindictiveness” would arise whenever a judge imposed a more sever 
sentence after a defendant had successfully overturned his original conviction on appeal. In such 
circumstances, the reason for the harsher sentence “must affirmatively appear [and] must be based 
upon objective information concerning identifiable conduct on the part of the defendant occurring 
after the time of the original sentencing proceeding.” Pearce, 395 U.S. at 726. Absent such a 
showing, vindictiveness against the defendant was to be presumed and the sentence he received 
on retrial struck down as a violation of due process. 

Likewise, in Edwards v. Arizona, 451 U.S. 477 (1981), the Court held that when a 
suspect effectively asserts his right to a lawyer (as opposed to his right to remain silent), the

Ill. Evidence obtained during a search of the Appellee's home should remain

A. The search was authorized by a facially deficient warrant which failed to 
state with particularity the items to be seized. 

The warrant requirement of the Fourth Amendment demands particularity. It prohibits the 
use of general warrants. Payton v. New York, 445 U.S. 573, 100 S.Ct. 1371 (1980); Andersen v. 
Maryland, 427 U.S. 463, 96 S.CT. 2737 (1976). The warrant soqelsement "makes general 
searches. . . impossible and prevents the seizure of one thing under a warrant describing another. 
As to what is to be taken, nothing is left to the discretion of the officer executing the warrant." 
Marron v. United States, 275 U.S. 192, 48 S.Ci. 74 (1927). 

The warrant approved by the magistrate judge to search Mr. Dickerson's home was 
drafted in the most general of terms. It authorized an intrusion into a private home for "evidence 
of the crime of bank robbery." Even if circumstances only permit a generic description of the 
items to be seized, “the Fourth Amendment requires that the government describe the items to be 
seized with as much specificity as the government's knowledge and circumstances allow, and 
‘warrants are conclusively invalidated by their substantial failure to specify as nearly as possible 
the distinguishing characteristics of the goods to be seized.” United States v. Robertson, 21 F.3d | 
1030, 1033 (10th Cir. 1994), cert. denied, 513 U.S. 891, 115 S.Ct. 238 (1994)(citations omitted). 

In the present case, Agent Lawlor, at a minimum, could have specified for the magistrate 
judge the amount of money taken during the bank robbery, the denominations and the serial 


numbers of the currency taken, the descriptions of the robber's clothing, and the descriptions of 


the gun and backpack used in the robbery. As Robertson notes, generic warrants such as this 


which do not contain any of the identifying characteristics known to the government violate the 
particularity requirement of the Fourth Amendment. 

The language in the warrant at issue here lacks any limitation whatsoever on the kind of 
evidence sought because the investigating officers had no idea what might be found. As Judge 
Michael correctly noted in dissenting to this portion of the Fourth Circuit’s majority opinion, 
““evidence of the crime of bank robbery’ is far too general. That description does nothing to 
confine the discretion of an executing officer... Under this interpretation, virtually no piece of 
paper or property would be beyond the bounds of a search.” Dickerson, 166 F.3d at 698. 

The fact that the officers executing the warrant used appropriate discretion while 
searching does not validate an otherwise insufficient warrant. It is precisely this type of on the 
scene police discretion that the particularity requirement of the Fourth Amendment prohibits. 
This same use of on the scene police discretion was addressed in United States v. Buck: 

While it can safely be said that the police here performed reasonably under 

the circumstances. . . they clearly did not insure that all the known facts were 

included in the warrant. The warrant only described the crimes -- and gave no 

limitation whatsoever on the kind of evidence sought. In short, “the warrant left it 

entirely to the discretion of the officials conducting the search to decide what items 

were to be seized, and thus was not permissible under the Fourth Amendment. 
813 F.2d 588, 589 (2nd Cir. 1987).

In response to the Supreme Court's decision in Miranda v. Arizona, 384 U.S. 436 (1966), the Congress of 
the United States enacted 18 U.S.C_A. § 3501 (West 1985), with the clear intent of restoring voluntariness 
as the test for admitting confessions in federai court. Although duly enacted by the United States Congress 
and signed into law by the President of the United States, the United States Department of Justice has 
steadfastly refused to enforce the provision. in fact, after initially “taking the Fifth” on the statute's 
constitutionality, the Department of Justice has now asserted, without explanation, that the provision is 
unconstitutional. With the issue squarely presented, we hoid that Congress, pursuaitt to its power to 
establish the rules of evidence and procedure in the federal courts, acted well within its authority in enacting 
§ 3501. As a consequence, § 3501, rather than Miranda, governs the admissibility of confessions in federal

On January 27, 1997, Charlies T. Dickerson confessed to robbing a series of banks in Maryland and 
Virginia. Dickerson was subsequently indicted by a federal grand jury on one count of conspiracy to commit 
bank robbery in violation of 18 U.S.C.A. § 371 (West Supp. 1998), three counts of bank robbery in violation 
of 18 U.S.C.A. [Page 3] § 2113(a) & (d) (West Supp. 1998), and three counts of using a firearm during and 
in relation to a crime of violence in violation of 18 U.S.C.A. § 924(c) (1) (West Supp. 1998). Shortly 
thereafter, Dickerson moved to suppress his confession. Although the district court specifically found that 
Dickerson's confession was voluntary for purposes of the Fifth Amendment, it nevertheless suppressed the 
confession because it was obtained in technical violation of Miranda. /1 


In ruling on the admissibility of Dickerson's confession, the district court failed to consider § 3501, which 
provides, in pertinent part, that "a confession . . . shall be admissible in evidence if it is voluntarily given.” 18 
U.S.C.A. § 3501(a). Based upon the statutory language, it is evident that Congress enacted § 3501 with the 
express purpose of legisiatively overruling Miranda and restoring voluntariness as the test for admitting 
confessions in federal court. Thus, if Congress possessed the authority to enact § 3501, Dickerson 
voluntary confession is admissible as substantive evidence in the Government's case-in-chief. 


Congress enacted § 3501 as a part of the Omnibus Crime Control Act of 1968, just two years after the 


Supreme Court decided Miranda. Although the Supreme Court has referred to § 3501 as “the statute 


governing the admissibility of confessions in federal prosecutions,” United States v. Alvarez-Sanchez, 511 
U.S. 350, 351 (1994), the Court has never considered whether the statute overruled Miranda, see Davis v. 
United States, 512 U.S. 452, 457 n.* (1994). indeed, although several lower courts have found that § 3501, 
rather than Miranda, governs the admissibility of confessions in federal court, see United States v. Crocker, 
510 F.2d 1129, 1137 (10th Cir. 1975); United States v. Rivas-Lopez, 988 F. Supp. 1424, 1430-36 (OD. Utah 
1997), no Administration since the provision's enactment has pressed the point, see Davis, 512 U.S. at 463- 
64 (Scalia, J., concurring) (noting [Page 4] that “the provision has been studiously avoided by every 
Administration . . . since its enactment more than 25 years ago"); see also U.S. Dep't of Justice, Report to 
Attorney General on Law of Pre-Trial interrogation 72-73 (1986) (discussing "[jhe abortive 

of § 3501" after its passage in 1968). in fact, after initially dectining to take a position on the applicability of § 
3501, see Davis, 512 U.S. at 457 n.", the current Administration has now asserted, without explanation, that 
the provision is unconstitutional, see Letter from Janet Reno, Attorney General, to Congress (Sept. 10, 


1997). 


Recently, Justice Scalia expressed his concern with the Department of Justice's failure to enforce § 3501. 
See Davis, 512 U.S. at 465 (Scalia, J., concurring). In addition to "caus{ing] the federal judiciary to confront 
a host of Miranda ‘issues that might be entirely irrelevant under federal law,” id., Justice Scalia noted that

Determining whether Congress possesses the authority to enact § 3501 is relatively straightforward. 
Congress has the power to overrule judicially created rules of evidence and procedure that are not required 
by the Constitution. See Cariisie v. United States, 517 U.S. 416, [Page 5] 426 (1996); Palermo v. United 
States, 360 U.S. 343, 345-48 (1959). Thus, whether Congress has the authority to enact § 3501 turns on 
whether the rule set forth by the Supreme Court in Miranda is required by the Constitution. Clearly it is not. 
At no point did the Supreme Court in Miranda refer to the warnings as constitutional rights. indeed, the 
Court acknowledged that the Constitution did not require the warnings, 384 U.S. at 467, disclaimed any 
intent to create a “constitutional straightjacket,” id., referred to the warnings as "procedural safeguards,” ic. 
at 444, and invited Congress and the States “to develop their own safeguards for [protecting] the privilege,” 
id. at 490. Since deciding Miranda, the Supreme Court has consistently referred to the Miranda warnings as 
“prophylactic,” New York v. Quarles, 467 U.S. 649, 654 (1984), and "not themselves rights protected by 
the Constitution,” Michigan v. Tucker, 417 U.S. 433, 444 (1974). We have little difficulty conciuding, 
therefore, that § 3501, enacted at the invitation of the Supreme Court and pursuant to Congress's 
unquestioned power to establish the rules of procedure and evidence in the federal courts, is constitutional. 
As a consequence, we hold that the admissibility of confessions in federal court is governed by § 3501, 
rather than the judicially created rule of Miranda.

in Bram v. United States, 168 U.S. 532 (1897), the Supreme Court asserted, for the first time, a 
constitutional basis for its requirement that a confession be made voluntarily. /d. at 542 (stating that whether 
a confession is voluntary “is controlled by that portion of the fifth amendment. . commanding that no 
person shall be compelled in any criminal case to be a witness against himself ™ (quoting U.S. Const.

crystallization” of the common law rule that only voluntary confessions are admissible as evidence. /d. 
Although the Supreme Court - prior to Miranda - would eventually place less reliance upon the approach 
taken in Bram, see, e.g., United States v. Carignan, 342 U.S. 36, 41 (1951) (expressing doubt about 
"[wjhether involuntary confessions are excluded from federal criminal trials on the ground of a violation of 
the Fifth Amendment's protection against self-incrimination, or from [Page 28] a rule that forced 
confessions are untrustworthy” (footnote omitted)), the Supreme Court in Brown v. Mississippi, 297 U.S. 
278 (1936), invoked another constitutional basis for its requirement that a confession be made voluntarily: 
the Due Process Clause. /d. at 285-86. Thereafter, a confession was admissible only if voluntary within the 
meaning of the Due Process Clause. See, e.g., Haynes v. Washington, 373 U.S. 503 (1963); Ashcraft v. 
Tennessee, 322 U.S. 143, 154 (1944); Chambers v. Florida, 309 U.S. 227 (1940). 


Thus, prior to Miranda, the rule governing the admissibility of confessions in federal court - if not the rule's 
justification - remained the same for nearly 180 years: confessions were admissibie at trial if made 
voluntarily. See, e.g., Davis v. United States, 512 U.S. 452, 464 (1994) (Scalia, J., concurring) (noting that 
prior to Miranda, “voluntariness ve/ non was the touchstone of admissibility of confessions”); Miranda v. 
Arizona, 384 U.S. 436, 506-07 (1966) (Harian, J., dissenting) (noting that voluntariness has been the test 
for admitting confessions since the earliest days of the Republic). indeed, in Lisenba v. California, 314 U.S. 
219 (1941), the Supreme Court specifically referred to “voiuntariness” as the federal test for determining 
the admissibility of confessions. /d. at 236. 


Such was the stage in 1966 when the Supreme Court decided Miranda v. Arizona, 384 U.S. 436 (1966). 
In Miranda, the Supreme Court announced a new analytical approach to the admissibility of confessions. 
Specifically, the Court rejected a case-by-case determination of whether a confession was voluntary.

Based on the statutory language alone, it is clear that Congress enacted § 3501 with the express purpose 
of returning to the pre- Miranda case-by-case determination of whether a confession was voluntary. We 
now turn to our next inquiry: Does Congress possess the authority to supersede the irrebuttabie

Interestingly, much of the scholarly literature on Miranda deals not with whether Congress has the 
legisiative authority to overrule the presumption created in Miranda, but whether it should. Miranda 's 
opponents, like Professor Paul Cassell, contend that thousands of violent criminals escape justice each 
year as a direct result of Miranda. See, e.g., Paul G. Cassell & Richard Fowles, Handcuffing the Cops? A 
Thirty- Year Perspective on Miranda's Harmful Effects on Law Enforcement, 50 Stan. L. Rev. 1055 (1998); 
Paul G. Cassell, Ail Benefits, No Costs: The Grand Iliusion of Miranda's Defenders, 90 Nw. U. L. Rev. 1084 
(1996); Paul G. Cassell, Miranda's Social Costs: An Empirical Reassessment, 90 Nw. U. L. Rev. 387 
(1996). In contrast, its proponents, like Professor Stephen Schulhofer, argue that Miranda has had littie 
impact on law enforcement's ability to obtain confessions. See, e.g., Stephen J. Schulhofer, Miranda's 
Practical Effect: Substantial Benefits and Vanishingly Small Social Costs, 90 Nw. U. L. Rev. S00 (1996); 
Stephen J. Schulhofer, Reconsidering Miranda, 54 U. Chi. L. Rev. 435 (1987). This debate, however, is one 
we need not enter. Whether Congress shouid overrule Miranda tells us nothing about whether it could. 
More importantly, it is not our role to answer that question. It is the province of the judiciary to determine 
what the [Page 33] law is, not what it should be. See Marbury v. Madison, 5 U.S. (1 Cranch) 137 (1803). 


in City of Boerne v. Flores, 117 S. Ct. 2157 (1997), the Court recently held that Congress does not 
possess the legisiative authority to supersede a Supreme Court decision construing the Constitution. See id. 
at 2172 (refusing to enforce federal statute establishing more narrow test for violation of the Free Exercise 
Clause than prior test established by Supreme Court). On the other hand, Congress possesses the 
legisiatve authority to overrule judicially created rules of evidence and procedure that are not required by 
the Constitution. See Palermo v. United States, 360 U.S. 343, 345-48 (1959) (upholding federal statute 
establishing more narrow disclosure of Jenks material than prior rule established by Supreme Court); see 
also Carlisle v. United States, 517 U.S. 416, 426 (1996) (noting that the federal courts may formulate rules 
of evidence and procedure so long as they do not conflict with an Act of Congress); Vance v. Terrazas, 
444 U.S. 252, 265 (1980) (upholding statute altering the evidentiary standard for expatriation proceedings 
established by the Supreme Court because prior standard created by the Court was not required by “the 
Constitution”). in fact, the power of the Supreme Court to prescribe nonconstitutional "rules of procedure 
and evidence for the federal courts exists only in the absence of a relevant Act of Congress.” Palermo, 360 
U.S. at 353 n.11 (citing Funk v. United States, 290 U.S. 371, 382 (1933), and Gordon v. United States, 344 
U.S. 414, 418 (1953)).

We begin our analysis then, with the Supreme Court's decision in Miranda. Several passages in Chief 
Justice Warren's opinion for the Court suggest that the warnings safeguard rights guaranteed by the [Page 
35] Constitution. See, e.g., Miranda, 384 U.S. at 490 (noting that the privilege against self-incrimination is 
guaranteed by the Constitution). Surprisingly, the sixty-page opinion does not specifically state the basis for 
its holding that a statement obtained from a suspect without the warnings would be presumed involuntary. 
The Court strongly suggested, however, that the basis for the rule was identical to that set forth in McNabb 
and Mallory. See id. at 463. in particular, just as the “supervisory” rule set forth in McNabb and Mallory 
permitted the Court to avoid the constitutional issues associated with federal interrogations, see id., the rule 
set forth in Miranda would allow the Court to avoid the constitutional issues associated with state 


interrogations, see id. 

Although the Court failed to specifically state the basis for its holding in Miranda, it did specifically state 
what the basis was not. At no point does the Court refer to the warnings as constitutional rights. Indeed, the 
Court acknowledged that the Constitution did not require the warnings, id. at 467, disclaimed any intent to 
create a “constitutional straightjacket,” id., repeatedly referred to the warnings as “procedural safeguards,” 
id. at 444, and invited Congress and the States “to develop their own safeguards for [protecting] the 
privilege,” id. at 490. 


Since deciding Miranda, the Supreme Court consistently (and repeatedly) has referred to the warnings as 
“prophylactic,” New York v. Quarles, 467 U.S. 649, 654 (1984), and “not themselves rights protected by 
the Constitution,” Michigan v. Tucker, 417 U.S. 433, 444 (1974); see also Davis v. United States, 512 U.S. 
452, 457-58 (1994) (referring to Miranda warnings as "a series of recommended procedural safeguards” 
(internal quotation marks omitted)); Withrow v. Williams, 507 U.S. 680, 690-91 (1993) (acknowledging that 
"Miranda 's safeguards are not constitutional in character”); Duckworth v. Eagan, 492 U.S. 195, 203 (1989) 
(noting that the Miranda wamings are not required by the Constitution); Connecticut v. Barrett, 479 U.S. 
523, 528 (1987) (noting that “the Miranda Court adopted prophylactic rules designed to insulate the 
exercise of Fifth Amendment rights”); Oregon v. Elstad, 470 U.S. 298, 306 (1985) (noting that the Miranda 
exclusionary rule "may be triggered even in the absence of a Fifth Amendment violation”); Edwards v. 
Arizona, 451 U.S. 477, 492 (1981) (Powell, J., concurring) (noting that the [Page 36] Court in Miranda 
"imposed a general prophylactic rule that is not manifestly required by anything in the text of the 
Constitution”).

As explained by the Second Circuit in George, a warrant authorizing a search for evidence relating to “a 
broad criminal statute or general criminal activity” such as “wire fraud,” “fraud,” “conspiracy,” or “tax

to what items to seize.” 975 F.2d at 76. in contrast, a warrant authorizing a search for evidence relating to “a 
specific illegal activity,” such as “narcotics,” or “theft of fur coats” is sufficiently particular. id. (citing cases).

particular crime: bank robbery. Bank robbery is a specific illegal activity that, as the Government notes, 
generates quite distinctive evidence. Though certainly broad in its description, we cannot say that the 
warrant failed to provide that degree of specificity required by the precedent of this Court.

suppression ruling.

In order to be valid under the Fourth Amendment, a search 
warrant must “particularly describle] the place to be searched and 
the persons or things to be seized.” U.S. Const. Amend. IV. This 
provision was crafted as a bulwark against general warrants 
employed by British colonial officials. Stanford v. Texas, 379

the items to be seized in such a manner that “nothing is left to

puzzle” incumbent on solving “white collar crime”).
