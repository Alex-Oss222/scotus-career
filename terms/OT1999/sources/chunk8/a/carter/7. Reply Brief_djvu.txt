ARGUMENT 


Respondent's admissions make this a simple case. 
Respondent admits, as it must, that “under common law, 
robbery contained all of the elements of larceny,” includ- 
ing the requirements of asportation and felonious intent. 
Resp. Br. 30 (emphasis added). Respondent also admits 
that, prior to the recodification of all federal criminal 
statutes in 1948, “[t]he comm-r. law mental element of 
larceny — ‘feloniously takes’ - was incorporated into bank 
robbery in 1934.” Resp. Br. 33. Finally, Respondent admits 
that, under this Court’s decisions in Morissette v. United 
States, 342 U.S. 246 (1952), and similar cases, “bank rob- 
bery must be presumed to contain a mental element 
despite Congress’s failure to provide expressly for one 
during the codification of the statute in 1948.” Resp. Br. 
39 (emphasis added); id. at 19 (“There is a presumption 
that any federal criminal offense requires a mental ele- 
ment. Accordingly, this Court has read a mental element 
into a criminal statute even where the statute did not 
expressly provide for one.”) (citation omitted). Petitioner 
and Respondent are thus on common ground as to all 
three points. See Pet. Br. 20-24, 27-29, 14-20. The parties’ 
dispute is largely reduced to one simple question: In 
1948, when Congress recodified the offense of bank rob- 
bery, did it eliminate the specific “intent to steal” element 
that had been part of the offense for the fourteen pre- 
vious years? The answer, equally simple, is “No.” 

The text, structure, and history of 18 U.S.C. § 2113, 
when viewed against the backdrop of common law prin- 
ciples about which there is no controversy, indicate that 
bank larceny has been defined as a lesser included 
offense of bank robbery, and Respondent offers nothing 
of substance to the contrary. At the outset, it is clear that 
a mechanical “plain text” approach is inadequate to con- 
strue the statute. Specifically, although Congress did not 
expressly include any intent element in the recodified 


2 


bank robbery provision, the parties agree that some men- 
tal element must be implied. The statute must therefore 
draw meaning from the common law tradition in which it 
is so firmly rooted. This Court’s decision in Prince v. 
United States, 352 U.S. 322 (1957), which explained the 
relationship between the robbery and unlawful entry 
offenses in § 2113, provides additional support for the 
conclusion that bank robbery, like bank larceny, requires 
proof of the specific intent to steal. Nor does the decision 
in Bell v. United States, 462 U.S. 356 (1983), as Respondent 
would have it, cut the definitions of bank robbery and 
bank larceny free from their common law roots. 


Petitioner was entitled to a lesser included offense 
instruction under a proper interpretation of the bank 
robbery and bank larceny provisions. He was prejudiced 
by the district court’s denial of the instruction and a 
remand is therefore required. 


1. The text of the statute and its common law ante- 
cedents compel the conclusion that bank larceny is a 
lesser included offense of bank robbery. This result is 
hardly surprising, inasmuch as larceny and robbery have 
been two closely linked members of a family of property 
crimes for centuries. See 4 William Blackstone, Commen- 
taries *229-43 (discussing larceny and robbery in chapter 
entitled “Of Offences Against Private Property”). 


Respondent offers a series of “plain text” arguments. 
Resp. Br. 12-17 (regarding intent element), 23-25 (regard- 
ing asportation requirement). The core of Respondents’ 
argument on intent is its observation that, “[w]Jhile bank 
larceny contains an express ‘intent to steal or purloin’ 
element, bank robbery has no such mental element in its 
text.” Resp. Br. 12. Similarly, with respect to the asporta- 
tion requirement, Respondent observes that the defini- 
tions for both bank robbery and bank larceny “use the 
word ‘take[ ]’ to describe the actus reus of the crime” but 
that the definition of larceny “also requires that the per- 
petrator ‘carries away’ the property.” Resp. Br. 23-24. In 


3 


the remainder of its brief, however, Respondent thor- 
oughly undermines whatever value such “plain text” 
arguments might otherwise have. 


a. On the question of intent, Respondent 
admits, mere moments after setting forth its plain text 
arguments, that the meaning of the bank robbery statute 
is necessarily beyond the reach of a “plain text” 
approach. According to Respondent, “[a]lthough bank 
robbery has no specific intent to steal element, that does 
not mean that it lacks a mental intent element alto- 
gether. . . . There is a presumption that any federal 
criminal offense requires a mental element.” Resp. Br. 19. 
This admission exposes the weakness of Respondent's 
plain text arguments. 


Respondent cannot have it both ways. If, as Respon- 
dent correctly admits, this Court’s federal criminal juris- 
prudence makes it proper to imply an intent element of 
some kind into the bank robbery statute (Resp. Br. 19-20, 
39-40), then the plain text of § 2113 alone cannot, as 
Respondent argues elsewhere (Resp. Br. 12-17), provide 
the answer to the “lesser included offense” question in 
this case. Indeed, because Petitioner and Respondent 
agree that some intent element must be implied in the 
definition of bank robbery, only one question remains - 
namely, which intent element should be implied, general 
intent or the specific intent to steal? 


Petitioner, by relying on the text, the common law 
and the statutory history of § 2113, as well as cases such 
as Morissette and Prince, has identified - on a principled 
basis — the specific intent to steal as the mens rea that 
Congress chose for the offense of bank robbery. Congress 
adopted this specific intent element as part of the defini- 
tion of bank robbery in the original federal bank robbery 
statute in 1934 (Pet. Br. 27), and has stated nothing to the 
contrary since that time (Pet. Br. 30-38). Respondent, by 
contrast, contends that general intent is the proper choice 
and offers little in the way of persuasive support. For 


4 


example, Respondent cites some “diminished capacity” 
cases from the courts of appeals. Resp. 20 & n.11. Not one 
of the cases Respondent cites, however, determined the 
requisite intent for bank robbery by thoroughly analyzing 
the common law antecedents and statutory history of 
§ 2113, as the Morissette decision requires. See Morissette, 
342 U.S. at 260-71. Instead, these cases stopped with a 
superficial contrast among the plain text of the defini- 
tions of bank robbery, unlawful entry and bank larceny, 
which plain text definitions Respondent concedes are 
inadequate to the interpretive task at hand. See, e.g., 
United States v. Johnston, 543 F.2d 55, 58 (8th Cir. 1976) (“A 
simple comparison of the language in the two paragraphs 
of § 2113(a) convinces us that Congress chose to define 
the second paragraph in terms of specific intent and 
consciously did not include language of specific intent in 
the first paragraph.”); United States v. DeLeo, 422 F.2d 487, 
490-91 (1st Cir. 1970) (same). These cases therefore pro- 
vide little, if any, support for Respondent. Respondent 
also offers a jumble of policies generally related to rob- 
bery as a basis for implying a general intent element in 
the bank robbery statute. Resp. Br. 21-23. These post hoc 
policy arguments, which Respondent makes no effort to 
anchor to the statutory text or the common law, should be 
rejected. 


Finally, Respondent also cites a host of other federal 
and state anti-robbery statutes, some of which purpor- 
tedly require proof of specific intent, and some of which 
purportedly do not. Resp. Br. at 14-19. This Court has 
squarely held, however, that the express presence of a 
given common law element in other, arguably similar, 
federal criminal statutes “cannot rebut the presumption 
that Congress intended to incorporate the common-law” 
element in a disputed statute that appears to lack the 
element but is generally framed in common law lan- 
guage. Neder v. United States, 119 S. Ct. 1827, 1840 n.7 
(1999) (emphasis added). “That rebuttal can only come 


5 


from the text or structure of the [disputed] statutes them- 
selves.” Id. Moreover, the statutes that Respondent cites 
show, at best, nothing more than that both Congress and 
the various state legislatures have used different verbal 
formulae to define different robbery-type crimes over the 
years. These other statutes do not, however, shed much 
light on precisely how Congress intended to define the 
crimes of federal bank robbery and bank larceny in 
§ 2113, the issue in this case. 


b. On asportation, Respondent relies exclu- 
sively on an apparent contrast between the plain text of 
the bank robbery and bank larceny definitions in § 2113. 
Resp. Br. 23-24 (bank robbery provision says “takes,” 
whereas bank larceny provisions says “takes and carries 
away”). This apparent textual difference between the 
bank robbery and bank larceny provisions, however, 
actually reproduces a difference in the common law defi- 
nitions of robbery and larceny. This textual difference 
thus supports Petitioner, not Respondent. 


Specifically, Respondent admits that the “carries 
away” portion of the bank larceny statute is rooted in the 
asportation requirement for common law larceny. Resp. 
Br. 24. Congress thus used common law language - and, 
by inference, common law meaning - to define bank 
larceny. See, e.g., Neder, 119 S. Ct. at 1839-41; Evans v. 
United States, 504 U.S. 255, 259 (1992); Morissette, 342 U.S. 
at 263. Respondent itself also notes that common law 
robbery, which plainly shared common law larceny’s 
asportation requirement, “was often defined in simple 
and undetailed language” that makes no mention of 
asportation, “such as ‘the felonious and violent taking of 
goods or money from the person of another by force or 
intimidation.’” Resp. Br. 30 quoting 2 LaFave & Scott, 
Substantive Criminal Law § 8.11 n.6 (1986 & Supp. 1999). 
Indeed, as LaFave and Scott observe, “American statutes 
do not generally spell out the eight elements [of robbery]; 
they define the crime of robbery in different ways, often 


6 


in the somewhat undetailed language used by Blackstone, 
Hawkins, Hale and East in defining common-law rob- 
bery.” 2 LaFave & Scott, Substantive Criminal Law § 8.11 
n.6 (1986 & Supp. 1999). At the same time, however, such 
robbery statutes nevertheless include an asportation 
requirement. See id. The elliptical definition of federal 
bank robbery in § 2113(a) thus conforms to a common 
feature of American robbery statutes generally. See also 
Pet. Br. 20-21 & n.16 (quoting robbery and larceny defini- 
tions from common law sources). In other words, Con- 
gress followed common law usage just as closely by 
leaving “carries away” implicit for bank robbery as it did 
by making “carries away” explicit for bank larceny. Com- 
mon law meaning therefore follows. 


Just as it did in the case of the specific intent require- 
ment, Respondent also points to a diverse practice among 
the States, some of which have retained an asportation 
requirement in their definitions of robbery and some of 
which have not. Resp. Br. 25 & n.15. Again, however, the 
presumption that a federal crime framed in common law 
terms tacitly includes common law elements cannot be 
rebutted by different language in other statutes. See Neder, 
119 S. Ct. 1840 n.7. And, in any event, the disparate 
practices of the states — some requiring proof of asporta- 
tion, others not — does little to advance the analysis of 
§ 2113, the specific federal statute at issue in this case. 


c. The various statutory construction cases 
Respondent cites to support its plain text arguments do 
not advance its position. For example, in Bates v. United 
States, which Respondent relies on at pages 6, 10, and 24 
of its brief, this Court held that the “specific intent to 
injure or defraud someone, whether the United States or 
another, is not an element of the misapplication of [stu- 
dent loan] funds proscribed by” 20 U.S.C. § 1097(a). 118 
S. Ct. 285, 288 (1997). In so holding, this Court contrasted 
20 U.S.C. § 1097(a) against both § 1097(d)’s express 


7 


“intent to defraud” element and 18 U.S.C. § 656’s prohibi- 
tion on the willful misapplication of bank funds, stating 
that “§ 1097(a) never contained, as § 656 did, an ‘intent to 
defraud’ requirement, a requirement present from the 
start and still contained in § 1097(d).” Id. at 290 (emphasis 
added). However, as Respondent admits (Resp. Br. 33), 
the bank robbery statute - in sharp contrast to the statute 
at issue in Bates - did expressly contain a specific intent 
element, at least until 1948. Bates is simply inapposite. 


Similarly, in United States v. Shabani, which Respon- 
dent relies on at page 16 of its brief, this Court held that 
the drug conspiracy provision at 21 U.S.C. § 846 does not 
require the government to prove an overt act in fur- 
therance of the conspiracy. 513 U.S. 10, 11 (1994). In so 
holding, the Court followed the common law understand- 
ing of conspiracy rather than implying an overt act 
requirement into § 846, the text of which is silent on the 
point.! Id. at 13-14 (“[A]bsent contrary indications, Con- 
gress intends to adopt the common law definition of 
statutory terms. We have consistently held that the com- 
mon law understanding of conspiracy does not make the 
doing of any act other than the act of conspiring a condi- 
tion of liability.”) (internal quotations and citations omit- 
ted; emphasis added); see also id. at 16 (“the criminal 
agreement itself is the actus reus and has been so viewed 
since Regina v. Bass, 11 Mod. 55, 88 Eng. Rep. 881, 882 
(K.B. 1705) (‘[T]he very assembling together was an overt 
act’)... ”). In United States v. Wells, which Respondent 
relies on at page 36 of its brief, the Court declined to 
imply a materiality element into the false statement pro- 
hibition of 18 U.S.C. § 1014 because, among other things,

2. The structure of § 2113 as a whole indicates that 
bank larceny is a lesser included offense of bank robbery. 
Specifically, the “receiving stolen property”-type crime 
defined in subsection (c) of § 2113 refers only to property 
“taken or stolen . . . in violation of subsection (b)” — the 
bank larceny offense. 18 U.S.C. § 2113(c). It would be 
strange, indeed, to conclude that bank larceny is other 
than a lesser included offense of bank robbery, for — on 
that construction — one who receives stolen property from 
a bank robber has not committed the offense, but one 
who receives the same property from a bank larcenist has 
committed the offense. Pet. Br. 11-13. The anomaly disap- 
pears if bank larceny is, as Petitioner contends, a lesser 
included offense of bank robbery: anyone who commits 
the offense of bank robbery necessarily commits the 
lesser offense of bank larceny, and thus one who know- 
ingly receives stolen property from a bank robber is no 
less guilty under subsection (c) than one who knowingly 
receives stolen property from a bank larcenist. 


Respondent promises an answer on this point and 
then fails to deliver. Specifically, Respondent baldly 
asserts “that in most cases receipt of the proceeds of bank 
robbery will be covered by Section 2113(c)” (Resp. Br. 14 
n.6), referring the reader to footnote 25 later in its brief. 
One will search footnote 25 in vain, however, for any

9 


support for Respondent’s contention that “in most cases 
receipt of the proceeds of bank robbery will be covered 
by Section 2113(c).” All that footnote 25 of Respondent's 
brief contains is a discussion urging a narrow view of the 
Court’s decision in United States v. Gaddis, 424 U.S. 544 
(1976), and a passing reference to Heflin v. United States, 
358 U.S. 415 (1959).2 Nowhere, however, does Respondent 
explain why subsection (c) of § 2113 proscribes knowing 
receipt of bank robbery proceeds “in most cases.” Peti- 
tioner has offered the only explanation of how the statute 
proscribes knowing receipt of bank robbery proceeds in 
all cases — namely, by defining bank larceny as a lesser 
included offense of bank robbery. 


3. Petitioner and Respondent agree on the basic 
events comprising the statutory history of § 2113. Com- 
pare Pet. Br. 27-33 with Resp. Br. 30-33. The parties dis- 
agree, however, about the proper conclusions to be drawn 
from that history. According to Respondent, “Congress’s

steal or purloin’ element in the bank larceny section of 
the statute, showed an ‘affirmative instruction’ to delete 
an intent to steal from the bank robbery statute.” Resp. 
Br. 41 (citing Morissette). The Morissette decision, how- 
ever, forecloses this faulty analysis because there has 
been no such affirmative instruction to eliminate the 
specific intent element from the definition of federal bank 
robbery. 


As was noted above, Respondent admits “that, under 
Morissette, supra, bank robbery must be presumed to con- 
ta.a a mental element despite Congress's failure to pro- 
vide expressly for one during the codification of the 
statute in 1948”. Resp. Br. at 39. Having done so, Respon- 
dent cannot by simple fiat ignore the thorough analysis of 
and reliance upon the common law antecedents of the 
federal crime in dispute that Morissette mandates. Pet. Br. 
17-19, 34-35. 


In Morissette, this Court carefully reviewed the sev- 
eral statutes that were drawn together and recodified at 
18 U.S.C. § 641, together with their common law anteced- 
ents. Despite Congress’s having trimmed the language of 
specific intent from the recodified statute, the Court con- 
cluded that “[t]he history of § 641 demonstrates that it 
was to apply to acts which constituted larceny or embez- 
zlement at common law and also acts which shade into 
those crimes but which, most strictly considered, might 
not be found to fit their fixed definitions.” Id. at 269 n.28. 
Along the way to reaching this conclusion, the Court 
rejected the government’s contention in that case — essen- 
tially identical to Respondent’s arguments here -— that it 
was sufficient under the statute to require proof of gen- 
eral intent alone, and that Congress intended as much by 
dropping express references to the specific intent to steal 
from the predecessor statutes. Id. at 264. The Court 
rejected the argument because the common law, not the 


11 


government’s current policy preference, remained the 
touchstone for interpreting the federal criminal statute. 
Id. at 263, 270-71. Congress’s omission of the modifier 
“feloniously” from the recodified definition of bank rob- 
bery was no more an affirmative instruction to disregard 
the common law meaning of the remaining words in the 
statute than it was in Morissette. 


Importantly, there is a simple and proven way for 
Congress to make its intent to break with the traditions of 
the common law in a given instance abundantly clear. For 
example, in 18 U.S.C. § 645 (1994), an anti-embezzlement 
statute aimed at court officers, Congress expressly pro- 
vided that “[i]t shall not be a defense that the accused 
person had any interest in [the] moneys or fund” at issue. 
At common law, such a good faith claim of right to the 
allegedly embezzled funds was a valid defense. 2 LaFave 
& Scott, Substantive Criminal Law § 8.6(f)(1) (1986 & Supp. 
1999) (“One who converts the property of another which 
is in his lawful possession is not guilty of embezzlement 
(for his conversion is not fraudulent) if, when he con- 
verts, he honestly believes the property is his own or is 
nobody’s, or he otherwise honestly believes he is autho- 
rized to convert it.”) (footnotes omitted). Congress first 
passed this particular anti-embezzlement statute, includ- 
ing its express negation of the “good faith claim of right” 
defense, in 1920 (Act of May 29, 1920, ch. 212, 41 Stat. 
630), i.e., 28 years before it recodified the bank robbery 
and bank larceny statutes and dropped the modifier 
“feloniously” from the definition of bank robbery. 


Congress employed the same technique more 
recently in 18 U.S.C. § 373 (1994), which was first enacted 
in 1984 (Pub. L. 98-473, Title II, § 1003(a), 98 Stat. 1837, 
2138) and criminalizes the solicitation of a crime of vio- 
lence. Subsection (c) of this anti-solicitation statute 
expressly provides that “[i]t is not a defense to a prosecu- 
tion under this section that the person solicited could not 
be convicted of the crime because,” inter alia, “he lacked 


12 


the state of mind required for its commission” or 
“because he was incompetent or irresponsible.” 18 U.S.C. 
§ 373(c) (1994). Without this provision, the solicitee’s lack 
of knowledge or intent could well be a defense for the 
solicitor. See, e.g., United States v. Higgins, 40 M.J. 67, 
68-70 (Ct. Mil. App. 1994) (reversing acceptance of guilty 
plea to solicitation of larceny under 10 U.S.C. § 934, and 
expressly distinguishing the contrary result that would 
be required under 18 U.S.C. § 373(c)). 


If, as Respondent argues, Congress had intended to 
eliminate the specific intent element from the definition 
of bank robbery when it recodified the provision in 1948, 
it would have done more than merely drop the modifier 
“feloniously” from that definition. Instead, Congress 
would have expressly provided within the bank robbery 
provision — in much the same way it had done 28 years 
earlier in the anti-embezzlement statute aimed at court 
officers — that “it shall not be a defense that the accused 
person lacked the specific intent to steal.” That Congress 
did not employ this already-familiar method reinforces 
the conclusion that Congress intended to hew to, not to 
break from, the common law. 


Alternatively, Congress can break with the common 
law meaning of a federal crime by defining the crime in 
such a way that it precludes, at a structural level, the 
importation of one or more common law elements. This 
approach is illustrated by the fraud statutes construed in 
the Neder case. There, on the strength of the presumption 
in favor of common law meaning for common law terms, 
this Court held that “materiality is an element of a 
‘scheme or artifice to defraud’ under the federal” mail, 
wire, and bank fraud statutes. 119 S. Ct. at 1839. At the 
same time, however, the Court explained that the basic 
structure of a “scheme to defraud” offense inherently 
precluded incorporation of the common law fraud ele- 
ments of justifiable reliance and damages: “By prohibit- 
ing the ‘scheme to defraud,’ rather than the completed

13 


fraud, the elements of reliance and damage would clearly 
be inconsistent with the statutes Congress enacted.” Id. at

8. Finally, according to Respondent, even if this 
Court concludes that bank larceny is a lesser included 
offense of bank robbery, the district court’s refusal to give 
the “lesser included offense” instruction that Petitioner 
requested would not constitute a reversible error. Resp. 
Br. 43-46. This argument cannot be squared with the 
record in this case. 


To be sure, the district court denied Petitioner’s 
motion for a judgment of acquittal, holding that there 


18 


was adequate evidence to support a conviction for rob- 
bery if the jury chose to return such a verdict. J.A. 32. 
According to the district court, “a reasonable jury could 
indeed find beyond a reasonable doubt, based upon the 
evidence which has been presented in this case,” that 
Petitioner was. guilty of bank robbery. J.A. 32. However, 
the district court did not in any way consider or deter- 
mine whether, based upon the evidence in this case, a 
jury could reasonably find him guilty of bank larceny and 
at the same time acquit him of bank robbery — the eviden- 
tiary standard for providing a lesser included offense 
instruction. See Keeble v. United States, 412 U.S. 205, 208 
(1973) (“it is now beyond dispute that the defendant is 
entitled to an instruction on a lesser included offense if 
the evidence would permit a jury rationally to find him 
guilty of the lesser offense and acquit him of the 
greater”). . 


The district court did not make a fact-based assess- 
ment about the propriety of a lesser included offense 
instruction on bank larceny because it viewed the issue as 
a purely legal matter. Specifically, the district court 
applied the Third Circuit’s holding in United States v. 
Mosley, 126 F.3d 200 (3d Cir. 1997), cert. dismissed, 525 U.S. 
120 (1998), that as a matter of law bank larceny can never 
be a lesser included offense of bank robbery. J.A. 14 
(citing Mosley); J.A. 30-31 (same). The Third Circuit's 
decision in this case also emphasized the purely legal 
nature of its ruling in Mosley. J.A. 84. Under the correct 
law, however, a fact-based evaluation should have been 
performed by the district court. The failure to conduct 
such an evaluation was not harmless, given the evidence 
that Petitioner did not use force, violence, or intimidation 
to take the money from the bank. Pet. Br. 2-7. 


Respondent attempts to short-circuit this straightfor- 
ward analysis, arguing that, by denying Petitioner’s 
motion for acquittal, the district court in essence granted 
a directed verdict against Petitioner. According to

No matter how strong the evidence may be in the 
prosecutor’s favor, the district court cannot direct a guilty 
verdict against the defendant. See United States v. Martin 
Linen Supply Co., 430 U.S. 564, 572-73 (1977); United Broth-

330 U.S. 395, 410 (1947). Moreover, a denial of the defen-

constitute a finding of guilt. It is therefore not surprising 
that, in this case, the district court did not apply the 
directed verdict-type standard that Respondent erro- 
neously invokes. Resp. Br. 45. Again, in denying Peti- 
tioner’s motion for acquittal, the district court decided

indeed find beyond a reasonable doubt” that all the ele-

different question that Respondent posits, i.e., whether a 
jury could reasonably fail to find that all the elements of 
bank robbery had been proved beyond a reasonable

Nor is it an answer to Petitioner’s request for a 
properly instructed jury to insist, as Respondent does, 
that the evidence against Petitioner was strong and that, 
if the jury had believed Petitioner’s account of the rele- 
vant facts, it would have acquitted him of the crime on 
which it did receive instructions. This Court rejected the 
same argument almost thirty years ago, in Keeble v. United 
States, 412 U.S. 205 (1973). According to Keeble, 


if the prosecution has not established beyond a 
reasonable doubt every element of the offense 


20 


charged, and if no lesser offense instruction is 
offered, the jury must, as a theoretical matter, 
return a verdict of acquittal. But a defendant is 
entitled to a lesser offense instruction —- in this 
context or in any other - precisely because he 
should not be exposed to the substantial risk 
that the jury’s practice will diverge from theory. 
Where one of the elements of the offense 
charged remains in doubt, but the defendant is 
plainly guilty of some offense, the jury is likely 
to resolve its doubts in favor of conviction. 


Id. at 212-13. Time has not diminished the force of this

denial of the requested “lesser included offense” instruc-

odds with both the reasoning and the result in Keeble. It
