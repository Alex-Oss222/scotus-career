STATEMENT OF THE CASE 

Last Term, this Court granted certiorari to determine the exact same issue presented by this 
case: namely, whether federal bank larceny constitutes a lesser included offense of federal bank 
robbery. See Mosley v. United States, 118 S. Ct. 1298, 1299 (1998) (granting certiorari). The Court 
was deprived of the opportunity to settle this issue, however, because Mr. Mosley died after the 
parties briefed and argued the case, but before this Court could render a decision on the merits. After 
Mr. Mosley’s death, the writ was dismissed. See Mosley v. United States, 119 S. Ct. 484 (1998) 
(dismissing certiorari). 

This case, which presents precisely the same question as Mosiley, offers the Court an 
opportunity to resolve the continuing split in the courts of appeal that prompted review in Mosley. 
Consistent with the text, structure, and history of the bank robbery and bank larceny statutes, several 
courts of appeal permit juries to be instructed on the lesser included offense of bank larceny. The 
Third and Ninth Circuits, which are in the minority, hold that, as a matter of law, the larceny 
instruction cannot be given in a bank robbery case, even where the evidence supports it and the 
defense requests it. Like Mosley, this case also raises fundamental questions about the role played 


by established common law meanings of a phrase or term when a court interprets a federal criminal 


3 


statute incorporating that phrase or term. This Court should therefore grant certiorari in this case to 
finish the task it began last Term. |

: REASONS FOR GRANTING THE PETITION 

There are three compelling reasons why this Court should grant certiorari in this case. First, 

the case presents an issue which this Court correctly deemed worthy of review just last Term, but 


which the Court lost the opportunity to decide through happenstance. Second, the Third Circuit’s 


consistently testified that petitioner made no demands of them or threats against them. 


*As in the instant case, the defendant in Mosley had been indicted for bank robbery in 
violation of 18 U.S.C. § 2113(2). He went to trial to challenge the application of the bank 
robbery statute to his conduct. He argued that, because he did not use force and violence or 
intimidation, he was guilty not of bank robbery but of bank larceny. The district court refused to 
instruct the jury on bank larceny, and the jury found Mosley guilty of bank robbery. On appeal, 


<-——— -the Third Circuit concluded that, as a matter of law, bank larceny is not a lesser included offense 


of bank robbery and thus established the precedent on which the decision in this case squarely 
rests. See Mosley, 126 F.3d at 206. 


holding in this case conflicts directly with the holdings of several other circuits. See United States 
v. Mosley, 126 F.3d 200, 206 (3d Cir. 1997) (collecting cases acknowledged to conflict), cert. 
dismissed, 119 S. Ct. 484 (1998). This case thus presents the same clear split among the circuit 
courts of appeal that prompted review of the same question last Term. Finally, this split among the 
circuits should be resolved in order to prevent the unfair administration of justice. Specifically, the 
conflict among the circuits results in the intolerable disparate treatment of those defendants who 
happen to be charged in a jurisdiction within the Third Circuit, rather than in virtually any other 
jurisdiction within the United States. 

1. Only last Term, this Court granted certiorari in Mosley v. United States, 118 S. Ct. 1298, 
1299 (1998), to review the Third Circuit’s decision that, as a matter of law, bank larceny in violation 
of 18 U.S.C. § 2113(b) is not a lesser included offense of bank robbery in violation of 18 U.S.C. § 
2113(a). After the case was briefed and argued before this Court, but before a decision on the merits 
had been rendered, Mr. Mosley died. His untimely death led this Court to dismiss the petition 
without rendering a decision on the merits. See Mosley v. United States, 119 S. Ct. 484 (1998). The 
reasons supporting this Court’s decision to grant review in Mosley - the decisional conflict among 
the circuits and the important problems of statutory interpretation presented when Congress defines 
federal crimes in terms that are rich with common law meaning and tradition - apply with equal 
force in this case. Certiorari, therefore, should be granted. 

2. The same clear split among the circuits that prompted this Court to grant review last Term 
in Mosley persists today. Most courts of appeals that have considered the question have concluded 
that bank larceny is a lesser included offense of bank robbery. Others, a minority, have reached the 
opposite conclusion. 


9 

Since Congress codified the bank robbery and incidental crimes provisions into law, only the 

Third and Ninth Circuits have refused to follow the common law tradition that bank larceny 
constitutes a lesser included offense of bank robbery. See Mosley, 126 F.3d at 206; United States 
v. Gregory, 891 F.2d 732, 734 (9th Cir. 1989). The Third Circuit itself has expressly acknowledged 
that its interpretation of the bank larceny statute conflicts directly with the majority of circuits, which 
have concluded that, just as with common law larceny and robbery, bank larceny is a lesser included 
offense of bank robbery. Compare Mosley, 126 F.3d at 206 ("Therefore, we reject the aie view 
that bank larceny is a lesser included offense of bank robbery.") with United States v. Henson, 945 
F.2d 430, 441 (1st Cir. 1991) ("failure to instruct on the lesser charge of bank larceny" was not plain 
aah United States v. Walker, 75 F.3d 178, 180 (4th Cir. 1996) ("It is undisputed that bank larceny 
is a lesser-included offense of bank robbery."); United States v. Amos, 566 F.2d 899, 901 (4th Cir. 
1977) (vacating concurrent bank larceny sentence on ground that "the convictions for less aggravated 
forms of bank theft merge into the conviction for the more aggravated form of bank robbery"); 
United States v. Flint, 534 F.2d 58, 60 (Sth Cir. 1976) (affirming district court’s refusal to give 
instruction on lesser included offense of bank larceny based on the evidence, but indicating that bank 
larceny is a lesser included offense of bank robbery); United States v. Cady, 495 F.2d 742, 747-48 
(8th Cir. 1974) (recognizing that bank larceny is a lesser included offense of bank robbery); United 
States v. Brittain, 41 F.3d 1409, 1410 (10th Cir. 1994) (holding bank larceny is a lesser included 
offense of bank robbery); and United States v. Cornillie, 92 F.3d 1108, 1109 (11th Cir. 1996) 
(reviewing district court’s refusal to charge jury "on the Jesser included offense of bank larceny"). 
It is clear that the Third Circuit and Ninth Circuit have erred in concluding that bank larceny 


is not a lesser included offense of bank robbery. First, applying the textual elements test adopted 


10 


by this Court in Schmuck v. United States, 489 U.S. 705, 716 (1989), it is evident that bank larceny 
represents a textual subset of bank robbery, with elements identical to bank robbery save for the 
"force and violence or intimidation" element unique to bank robbery. Under Schmuck, one offense 
is the lesser included of another if "the elements of the lesser offense are a subset of the elements of 
the charged offense." Jd. Although this Court emphasized in Schmuck that the analysis must be 
"textual," id. at 720, it did not hold that the exact same words that define an element in the lesser 
offense must appear in the definition of the element in the greater offense. The Third Circuit erred 
in Mosley, and thus in this case, by adopting the very sort of ‘magic words’ approach that Schmuck 
avoided. Thus, although the bank robbery provision (18 U.S.C. § 2113(a)) does not contain the 
phrase “intent to steal or purloin" found in the bank larceny provision (18 U.S.C. § 21 13(b)), the 
bank robbery provision does contain terms tantamount to "steal or purloin" - namely, the phrase 
"takes or attempts to take from the person of another." The terms employed in both provisions 
correspond closely to one another and are alternative means of defining the same intent element. 
Second, the premise that the bank robbery statute lacks an intent element - which element 
the Third Circuit concluded distinguishes federal bank larceny from the federal bank robbery - runs 
afoul of the common law understanding of robbery, on which the statute is based. Moreover, it runs 
afoul of the Court’s seminal decision in Morissette v. United States, 342 U.S. 246, 263 (1952)(mens 
rea or intent is impliedly contained in a statute codifying a common law crime). In fact, contrary

III. ©1948 Provision On Classification Of Offenses 
§ 1. Offenses Classified. 

Notwithstanding any Act of Congress to the contrary: 


* (1) Any offense punishable by death or imprisonment for a term exceeding one year is a 


felony. 
(2) Any other offense is a misdemeanor. 
(3) Any misdemeanor, the penalty for which does not exceed imprisonment for a period 


of six months or a fine of not more than $500, or both, is a petty offense.
