DISCUSSION 

Petitioner contends (Pet. 7-15) that the court of appeals 
erred in holding that a bank larceny instruction was not required 
on the ground that bank larceny is not a lesser-included offense 
of bank robbery. He notes that this Court had granted certiorari 
in Mosley, but that the Court ultimately dismissed the petition 
because Mosley died before the case was decided. Because the 
legal issue in this case is identical to that of Mosley. and 


because the Court granted the petition in that case to decide the 


question presented, the petition in this case should be granted. 


1. The court of appeals relied on Mosley, which correctly 
held that bank larceny is not a lesser-included offense of bank 
robbery. Under the test for determining greater and lesser 
offenses set forth in Schmuck v. United States, 489 U.S. 705 
(1989), the controlling question is whether the elements of one 
offense are a subset of the elements of the other offense. 
Because the putative lesser offense of bank larceny under 18 
U.S.C. 2113(b) (Supp. IV 1998) has statutory elements that are 
omitted from the bank robbery offense in 18 U.S.C. 2113(a), bank 
larceny is not a lesser-included offense. 

Section 2113(a) defines bank robbery, in pertinent part, as 


follows: 


Whoever, by force and violence, or by intimidation, 
takes, or attempts to take, from the person or presence 
of another, or obtains or attempts to obtain by 
extortion any property or money or any other thing of 
value belonging to, or in the care, custody, control, 
management, or possession of, any bank, credit union, 

or any savings and loan association. 

Section 2113(b) (Supp. IV 1998) defines bank larceny, in 


pertinent part, as follows: 

Whoever takes and carries away, with intent to steal or 

purloin, any property or money or any other thing of 

value exceeding $1,000 belonging to, or in the care, 

custody, control, management, or possession of any 

bank, credit union, or any savings and loan 

association. 

Under the "elements" test, bank larceny is not a lesser- 
included offense of bank robbery because bank larceny has three 
textual elements that are omitted from the bank robbery offense: 
(i) am intent to steal; (2) a “carries away" actus reus element; 
and (3) a requirement that the property taken have a monetary 
value. The text of a federal statute ordinarily defines its 


elements. See Bates v. United States, 522 U.S. 23 (1997) 


(specific intent to injure or defraud is not an element of the 


offense of misapplication of funds); United States v. Wells, 519 


U.S. 482 (1997) (materiality is not an element of false statement 


to a federal bank offense). And, when Congress includes certain 
language in one section of a statute and omits it in another, 


Congress is presumed to act deliberately in the disparate 
inclusion or exclusion. Russello v. United States, 464 U.S. 16, 


a 


7 


23 (1983). Despite the common law status of robbery and larceny 
as greater and lesser offenses, nothing in the legislative 
history of Section 2113 indicated that Congress intended to 
codify the common law requirement of intent to steal in the bank 
robbery statute. Nor is there justification for reading the 
“carries away" or valuation elements of bank larceny into the 
bank robbery offense. 

Nevertheless, the courts of appeals have reached conflicting 
conclusions on the question whether bank larceny is a lesser- 
included offense of bank robbery. The Third Circuit’s holding is 
supported by the Ninth Circuit. See United States v. Gregory, 
891 F.2d 732, 734 (9th Cir. 1989). In contrast, the First, 
Fourth, Fifth, Seventh, and Eighth Circuits have stated to the 
contrary. See, e.g., United States v. Sandles, 80 F.3d 1145, 
1147 n.1 (7th Cir. 1996); United States v. Walker, 75 F.3d 178, 
180-181 (4th Cir.), cert. denied, 517 U.S. 1250 (1996); United 
States v. Smith, 40 F.3d 933, 935 (8th Cir. 1994); United States 
v. Henson, 945 F.2d 430, 440-441 (1st Cir. 1991); United States 
v. Elint, 534 F.2d 58, 60 (Sth Cir.) (per curiam), cert. denied, 


429 U.S. 924 (1976) .’ 


? In United States v. Cornillie, 92 F.3d 1108, 1109 (11th 
Cir. 1996) (per curiam), the court affirmed the district court's 


ruling denying the requested lesser-included offense instruction 
on the ground that the evidence did not support the defendant's 
contention. The court did not address the issue whether, as a 
matter of law, bank larceny is a lesser-included offense of bank 
robbery. é; ; . 


In United States v. Brittain, 41 F.3d 1409 (1994), the Tenth 


Circuit ruled by a divided vote that bank larceny is a lesser- 


included offense of bank robbery. The lead opinion indicated 
that bank larceny was probably not a lesser-included offense of 
bank robbery under Schmuck, but expressed the view that the court 
was compelled to follow pre-Schmuck circuit precedent to the 
contrary. 41 F.3d at 1410-1416 (opinion of Brown; S.D.J). A 
concurring judge concluded that the two crimes are greater and 
lesser offenses under Schmuck. Id. at 1416 (Ebel, J., 
concurring). The dissenting judge opined that the two offenses 
are not greater and lesser offenses under Schmuck. Jd. at 1416- 
1417 (Baldock, J. dissenting). The split opinions in Brittain 


demonstrate the range of views on this issue.’ 


> We note that this case is also like Mosley in that, even 
if bank larceny is found to be a lesser-included offense of bank 
robbery, petitioner here would not be entitled to a bank larceny 
instruction on the facts presented. Federal Rule of Criminal 
Procedure 31(c) entitles a defendant to a lesser included offense 
instruction only if the evidence would permit a rational jury to 
convict him of the lesser offense while acquitting him of the 
greater one. Keeble v. United States, 412 U.S. 205, 208 (1973). 
In this case, petitioner asked for and received an instruction to 
the jury that "the taking occurred without the use of force, 
violence, or intimidation." C.A. App. A254. Because the jury 
rejected that finding, there is no factual dispute that, if 
resolved in the defendant's favor, would result in a conviction 
on the lesser offense. See Sansone v. United States, 380 U.S. 
343, 350-351 (1965). Thus, even if this Court were to conclude 
that bank larceny is a lesser-included offense of bank robbery as 
a matter of law, petitioner would not benefit from that ruling 
because he cannot establish the prerequisites under Federal Bule 
of Criminal Procedure 31(c) for the instruction.
