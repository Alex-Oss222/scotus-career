sion of the charge of bank larceny to the jury in this case.

@ Lesser Included Offense 


Bank Larceny, 18 U.S.C. §2213(b) is a lesser include 
[sic] offense of Bank Robbery, 18 U.S.C. §2213(b). Both 
provisions come under the title “Bank Robbery and Inci- 
dental Crimes.” An “elements” test was established in 
Schmuck v. United States, for determining whether an 
offense is “necessarily included in the offense charged, 


489 U.S. 705 (1989). Under the “elements” test, a lesser 
offense cannot contain an element not required for the 
greater offense. The elements of the lesser offense must 
be a “subset” of the offense charged. Id. At 714. Compari- 
son is appropriately conducted be [sic] reference to the 
statutory elements of the offense in question. Id. At 718. 
The comparison must be textual. Id. at 720. 


Undoubtedly the government will rely on United 
States v. Mosley (No. 96-5832 decided September 18, 1997, 
Third Circuit), to argue that bank larceny is not a lesser 
included offense of bank robbery (Exhibit 2). However, 
Mosley is in direct conflict with the holdings of the Court 
of Appeals in the First, Fourth, Fifth, Sixth, Eighth, Tenth 
and Eleventh Circuits. (Cases listed Mosely Pg 10-11). A 
petition for a writ of certiorari challenging the propriety 
of Mosley is pending. Mosley also is factually distinguish- 
able from the present case. 


In Mosley the first charged bank robbery, Mosley told 
the teller “This is a hold-up” and demanded all big bills. 
Mosley gestured to his coat as if he had a weapon. The 
teller wet her pants while responding to Mosley’s 
demand to “hurry”. In the second charged bank robbery 
Mosley handed over a note to the teller which said in 
bold print “HOLD UP”. He threw the “bait money” back 
at the teller and demanded more. In the present case no 
threats, gestures, demands or force are present. 


In the Mosley analysis, the Circuit stated that the 
definition of bank robbery does not include the element 
of an “intent to steal or purloin” as the bank larceny 
statute states (Mosley Pg. 7). However, “textually”, the 
bank robbery statute contains the dictionary definition of 


ee a 


“steal or purloin”. That is, 18 U.S.C. §2113(a) contains the 
element “takes or attempt [sic] to take - from the per- 
son...” and the word “steal” is commonly defined “to 
take the property of another”. See Webster’s New Collegiate 
Dictionary, G&C Merrian Co. (1974) pg. 1138. The word 
“purloin” is a synonym of “steal”. Id at 937. Therefore, 
the terms employed in the text of the bank robbery stat- 
ute and bank larceny statute are alternative means of 
defining the same element. - 


Ultimately, because the element “steal or purloin” 
contained in the bank larceny statute, 18 U.S.C. §2113(b), 
is by definition contained in the bank robbery statute, 18 
U.S.C. §2113(a), (. . . take or attempt to take from the 
person ... ) it is indeed a textual subset and therefore a 
lesser included offense within the -“elements” test 
adopted in Schmuck v. Unites States. 


@ Theory of the Case 


Every criminal defendant is entitled to have his the- 
ory of the case, if it could amount to a legally sufficient 
defense based upon the evidence presented at trial, fairly 
submitted to the jury. Keeble v. United States, 412 U.S. 205, 
208 (1995); United States v. Burke, 700 F.2d 70, 81 (2na Wir. 
1983). See also United States v. Blair, 456 F.2d 514, 520 (3d 
Cir. 1972). (A trial court is “bound to give the substance 
of a requested instruction relating to any defense theory 
for which there [is] any foundation in the evidence) 
(emphasis supplied). This is so even though the evidence 
may be weak, insufficient, inconsistent or of doubtful 
credibility. United States v. Sielaff, 655 F.2d 402, 403 (7th 


Cir. 1979). See also United States v. Escobar de Bright, 742 
F.2d 1196 (9th Cir. 1984). 


Failure to give a requested theory of defense instruc- 
tion is reversible error when the requested instruction is: 
1) correct; 2) was not substantially covered by the court's 
charge to the jury; and 3) deals with some point in the . 
trial so important that failure to give the requested 
instruction seriously impaired the defendant's ability to 
conduct his defense. United States v. Benz, 740 F.2d 903, 
910 (11th Cir. 1984). 


The evidence in this case, essentially indicating a 
theft or larceny from the bank warrants the larceny 
charge. It is a [sic] issue of importance in the defense. 
Fundamental fairness and the 6th Amendment right to a 
fair trial compel the instruction.

MEMORANDUM OPINION 


ROSENN, Circuit Judge. 


Appellant Floyd J. Carter and Terry Stillwell were 
charged in a one-count indictment with bank robbery in 
violation of 18 U.S.C. § 2113(a) in the United States Dis- 
trict Court for the District of New Jersey. Stillwell 
pleaded guilty and, on January 23, 1998, a jury found 
Carter guilty of bank robbery. On October 15, 1998, the 
district court sentenced the defendant, Carter, to 215 
months in prison.! Defendant appealed. We affirm. 


I. 


At approximately 2:00 p.m. on September 9, 1997, the 
defendant entered the Collective Federal Savings Bank in 
Hamilton Township, New Jersey, wearing several layers 
of clothing and a ski mask that covered his entire face 
except for his eyes and mouth. As he entered, a customer, 
Maria Zahn, was leaving. Ms. Zahn attempted to exit 
around him, but the defendant pushed her back inside. 


1 The district court had subject matter jurisdiction under 18 
U.S.C. § 3231 and this court has appellate jurisdiction under 28 
U.S.C. § 1291 and 18 U.S.C. § 3742(1) and (2). 


82 


Ms. Zahn was so terrified that she screamed, startling 
others within the bank. 


At that point, Carter ran toward the customer service 
counter, and with a single leap, vaulted over the counter 
and through one of the teller windows to the area behind 
the counter where tellers Anastasia Hairston, Christine 
Sentech, and Mildred Calacal were standing. Ms. Hair- 
ston ran to the office of the branch manager, Robert 
Phipps, yelling that a robber was after her. Meanwhile, 
the defendant opened Ms. Hairston’s teller drawer and 
emr tied money into‘a brown paper bag. He then quickly 
moved to within two feet of where Ms. Sentech and Ms. 
Calacal stood and proceeded to empty their teller 
drawers. Meanwhile, Ms. Zahn ran out of the bank, got 
into her car, laid down on the floor and prayed until the 
authorities arrived. 


After having removed almost $16,000 in currency 
from the teller drawers, the defendant hurdled back over 
the teller counter and warned that no one was to follow 
him. He ran to a getaway car operated by Terry Stillwell, 
climbed into the trunk, and closed the hatch. The car then 
sped off. A short while later, the police arrived. At that 
point, a description of the getaway car was broadcasted 
over police radio. Eventually, other officers stopped the 
getaway car and apprehended Carter and his disguise. 
The defendant subsequently waived his Miranda rights 
and confessed to FBI special agent James Maxwell that he 
and Stillwell had driven to Hamilton Township from New 
Brunswick to rob the bank. 


83 


II. 


The defendant’s primary issue on appeal is the dis- 
trict court’s decision to reject his request to instruct the 
jury on bank larceny. The district court's decision to reject 
his request is subject to plenary review. See United States 
v. Price, 13 F.3d 711, 724 (3d Cir. 1994). 


A. 


The defendant argues that, even though not charged 
in the indictment, bank larceny is “a lesser included 
offense” of bank robbery for purposes of Fed. R. Crim. P. 
31(c). He therefore contends that the jury should have 
been instructed on bank larceny because the theory of his 
defense at trial was that he took money from the bank 
without force, violence, or intimidation. 


The district court denied the defendant's request on 
the ground that this court in United States v. Mosley, 126 
F.3d 200 (3d Cir. 1997), issued shortly before Carter’s 
trial, held that bank larceny is not a lesser included 
offense of bank robbery. In Mosley this court remarked 
that under Section 2113(a) “the subjective intent of a bank 
robber to steal is irrelevant; ‘the crime is his resort to 
force and violence, or intimidation, in the presence of 
another person to accomplish his purposes.’ ” Mosley, 126 
F.3d at 204 (quoting United States v. Brown, 547 F.2d 36, 39 
(3d Cir. 1976)). Conversely, the Mosley court observed that 
under Section 2113(b), the perpetrator must have the 
“intent to steal or purloin to satisfy the elements of bank 
larceny.” Mosley, 126 F.3d at 204. 


84 


The defendant asserts that even under Mosley the 
district court still was obligated to instruct the jury on the 
defense’s theory that he committed bank larceny, not 
bank robbery. We disagree. Mosley rested solely on the 
legal interpretation of Sections 2113(a) and (b). See 126 
F.3d at 204. We conclude that the district court committed 
no error in refusing to instruct the jury on bank larceny 
because this court’s holding in Mosley appropriately 
rested solely on a legal interpretation of Sections 2113(a) 
and (b). See 126 F.3d at 204. 


Notwithstanding the holding in Mosley, the defen- 
dant contends that we should reconsider that decision. 
He argues that an examination of the language of the 
statute reveals that Congress employed the dictionary 
definition of the words “steal” and “purloin,” contained 
in the bank larceny provision, when it wrote the bank 
robbery provision. He notes that the word “purloin” is a 
synonym of “steal” and that the text of the statute with 
respect to these terms alternatively defines the same ele- 
ment. He also claims that absent a clear and specific 
statement by Congress that a particular criminal statute 
does not require a mens rea, this element of intent is 
presumed to exist. Consequently, he submits that omis- 
sion of the mens rea requirement in the text of the bank 
robbery statute raises a presumption that mens rea is a 
requisite element of bank robbery. Thus, he submits that 
the intent to steal is an element of both bank larceny and 
bank robbery. Finally, he contends that the Mosley deci- 
sion was based on a mistaken and incomplete consider- 
ation of the common law history of bank robbery and 
bank larceny, and an incorrect application of the relevant 
case law. We disagree with the defendant in all respects 


85 


because Congress created a statutory crime of robbery 
that is set forth fully in Sections 2113(a) and (b). 


First, in Brown, this court held that specific intent is 
not an essential element of a violation of Section 2113(a). 
Second, under the modern robbery statute, Congress did 
not use the language of the common law to define the 
offenses of bank robbery and bank larceny. See Bell v. 
United States, 462 U.S. at 360 (citing Turley, 352 US. at 
411-412). Finally, Section 2113(b) is broader than the com- 
mon law definition because common law larceny was 
limited to thefts of tangible personal property. Accord- 
ingly, we conclude that our decision in Mosley need not 
be disturbed. 


The defendant also contends that the district court 
misinterpreted and misapplied United States Sentencing 
Guideline Section 3E1.1 in denying him a reduction in the 
offense level because he accepted responsibility for the 
offense. He submits that he accepted responsibility for his 
criminal conduct for purposes of Section 3E1.1 because he 
conceded at trial that he committed bank larceny, but 
denied that his conduct amounted to the offense of bank 
robbery because he did not use force, violence, or intim- 
idation. 


The district court’s decision to deny the claim for 
acceptance of responsibility is reviewed for clear error. 
See United States v. Muhammad, 146 F.3d 161, 167 (3d Cir. 
1998). The reduction for acceptance of responsibility gen- 
erally does not apply to a defendant “who puts the gov- 
ernment to its burden of proof at trial by denying the 


86 


essential factual elements of guilt, is convicted and only 
then admits guilt and expresses remorse.” U.S.S.G. 
§ 3E1.1, Application Note 2. At trial, Carter challenged 
the testimony of Ms. Zahn, claiming that his physical 
contact with her was wholly accidental, and that she did 
not scream. He attacked the Government's evidence as to 
his threatening remark as he fled from the bank. Finally, 
in his summation, he argued that there was no implicit 
threat of violence or injury to discourage a pursuit. These 
challenges to the Government's case are inconsistent with 
an acceptance of responsibility. 


Accordingly, we conclude that the district court com- 
mitted no error in rejecting defendant’s request for a 
reduction in his offense level. See United States v. DeLeon 
Rodriguez, 70 F.3d 764, 767 (3d Cir. 1995) (district court 
properly denied acceptance reduction after appellant 
pleaded not guilty and contested his factual guilt beyond 
a mere legal challenge); United State v. Veksler, 62 F.3d 544, 
551 (3d Cir. 1995). 


Cc. 


Lastly, the defendant contends that the district court 
erroneously denied his motion for acquittal pursuant to 
Fed. R. Crim. P. 29 because the Government failed to 
establish that he used force, violation, or intimidation to 
perpetrate the predicate offense. When a jury returns a 
verdict, this court's review is plenary and, in employing 
that review, we must view the evidence in the light most 
favorable to the Government to determine if any rational 
trier of fact could have found the essential elements of 
the offense beyond a reasonable doubt. See Burks v. United 


87 


States, 437 U.S. 1, 17 (1978); United States v. Leon, 739 F.2d 
885, 890 (3d Cir. 1984). 


To obtain a conviction for unarmed bank robbery 
under Section 2113(a), the Government must prove that 
the defendant: (1) used force and violence or intimida- 
tion; (2) took or attempted to take from the person or 
presence of another, (3) money, (4) in the care, custody, or 
control of a federally insured bank. To establish “intim- 
idation” under Section 2113(a), the Government need 
show only that “an ordinary person in the teller’s posi- 
tion reasonably could infer a threat of bodily harm from 
the defendant's acts.” Untied [sic] States v. Woodrup, 86 
F.3d 359, 363 (4th Cir. 1996). The evidence shows that the 
defendant wore a ski mask that covered his entire face 
except for his eyes and mouth, and that he gained access 
to the teller cages by forcefully, speedily, and impermissi- 
bly vaulting the customer service counter. These abnor- 
mal activities plainly were acts of intimidation, as well as 
his exertion of force on Ms. Zahn. We see no error in the 
district court’s denial of the defendant’s motion for 


acquittal. 


For the foregoing reasons, the judgment of conviction 
and sentence of the court will be affirmed.
