JURISDICTION 


This Court has jurisdiction to review the decision of the 
United States Court of appeals for the Fifth Circuit entered 
on April 22, 1999, Petition for Rehearing En Banc denied 
June 18, 1999, by a Writ of Certiorari under 28 U.S.C. § 
1254 (1). 


STATUTORY PROVISION INVOLVED 


The statute construed is the Age Discrimination in 
Employment Act, 29 U.S.C.S. § 623 (a) which says: “It shall 
be unlawful for an employer -- (1) ... to discharge any 
individual or otherwise discriminate against any individual ... 
because of such individual's age .. . ” 


STATEMENT 


A jury in the United States District Court for the 
Northern District of Mississippi found that Petitioner, Roger 
Reeves, was fired because of his age. The United States 
Court of Appeals for the Fifth Circuit reversed, holding that 
Respondent was entitled to judgment as a matter of law 
under Fed. R. Civ. P. 50 because Petitioner failed to produce 
evidence sufficient to satisfy its “pretext plus” standard for 
submitting an age discrimination case to the jury. 


(FACTS) 


Petitioner, Roger Reeves, worked for Respondent, 
Sanderson Plumbing Products, Inc., for forty (40) years 
before he was fired at age fifty-seven (57). (R. at 1-4) Two 
(2) months before Reeves was fired, Powe Chestnut, 
Director of Manufacturing, and the “absolute power” at the 
company (R. at 80-86), told Reeves he was “too damn old to 
do the job.” (R. at 26, 59) By the time of trial, Respondent 
had utilized three (3) different replacements for Reeves. All 
were in their thirties. (R. at 101-102, 224) 

Respondent claimed Reeves was fired because an 
investigation revealed overpayment of hourly employees, 
and that Reeves failed to reprimand employees who were 
absent or tardy. (R. at 113, 115, 119) 


3 


In rebuttal, Reeves produced evidence that his 
subordinates were on time and that, in any event, his superior 
productivity records show that the company was not 
suffering any losses because of alleged absences or tardies of 
workers. (R. at 18-19, 73, 241-245, 246-249) Furthermore, 
failing to reprimand employees who were absent or tardy 
could not have been the reason for his discharge, since he 
was not the person responsible for giving reprimands. (R. at 
20-21) 

The United States Court of Appeals for the Fifth Circuit 
agreed Plaintiff produced sufficient evidence that the reasons 
given for Reeves’ firing and replacement by younger persons 
were pretextual. (Op. at 9). However, the Fifth Circuit 
concluded that: “Even so, whether Sanderson was forthright 
in its explanation for firing Reeves is not dispositive of a 
finding of liability under ADEA. We must, as an essential 
final step, determine whether Reeves presented sufficient 
evidence that his age motivated Sanderson's employment 
decision.” (Op. at 9). 

Applying this “pretext plus” test, the Fifth Circuit found 
the evidence insufficient because the age-based statements 
were made by only one of three (3) decision makers, and 
because Reeves did not otherwise prove “a violation of the 
Age Discrimination in Employment Act (“ADEA”) by a 
preponderance of the evidence.” (Op. at 1) In finding there 
was not substantial evidence of age discrimination, the Fifth 
Circuit relied upon the Respondent’s (movant’s) evidence, 
that two (2) of the decision makers were themselves over age 
fifty (SO) and that Respondent had also accused other 
workers of inaccurate record keeping, including a thirty-five 
(35) year old. Appellee’s brief, p. 5. 

In requiring direct evidence of age discrimination, and in 
relying upon evidence produced by the movant in deciding a 


4 


Rule 50 motion, the United States Court of Appeals for the 
Fifth Circuit differs from other circuits. 


I. THIS COURT SHOULD GRANT 
CERTIORARI SINCE THERE IS A SPLIT IN 
THE CIRCUITS WITH RESPECT TO 
WHETHER PROOF OF PREFERENTIAL 
TREATMENT FOR A MEMBER OF THE 
PREFERRED CLASS, FOR PRETEXTUAL 
REASONS, IS SUFFICIENT TO PRESENT 
AN EMPLOYMENT DISCRIMINATION 
CASE TO THE JURY. 


Regarding the evidence necessary to create a factual issue 
under Title VII of the Civil Rights Act of 1964, this Court 
has stated: “... [R]Jejection of defendant’s proffered reasons 
will permit the trier of fact to infer the ultimate fact of 
intentional discrimination, and the Court of Appeals was 
correct when it noted that, upon such rejection, ‘[n]o 
additional proof of discrimination is required.’” St. Mary’s 
Honor Ctr. v. Hicks, 509 U.S. 502, 511 (Emphasis added). 

Some circuits hold the above language from Hicks means 
that proof of a prima facie case coupled with proof of pretext 
is always sufficient to present a jury question. Other circuits 
disagree, and require direct evidence of discriminatory 
intent. 

The Fifth Circuit is one of the circuits using the “pretext 
plus” (direct evidence) test for determining the sufficiency of 
the evidence to present an employment discrimination case 
to the jury.' Walton v. Bisco Indust., Inc., 119 F.3d 368, 370 
(Sth Cir. 1997) (stating a reason cannot be proved to be a 


‘While Hicks was decided under Title VII of the Civil Rights Act of 1964, 42 
U.S.C. § 2000 et seq., all circuits agree Hicks governs age discrimination cases. 


5 


pretext for discrimination unless it is shown both that the 
reason was false and that discrimination was the real reason). 

Several circuits agree with the Fifth Circuit that there 
must be additional evidence, other than evidence of a prima 
facie case, and pretext evidence, in order to create a jury 
question. See Woods v. Friction Materials, Inc., 30 F.3d 255, 
260 (ist Cir. 1994) (stating the Supreme Court in Hicks did 
not mean prima facie case plus proof of pretext was always 
sufficient to present a jury question); Theard v. Glaxo, Inc., 
47 F.3d 676, 680 (4th Cir. 1995) (stating to avoid summary 
judgment, plaintiff had to prove not only that the reason the 
employer presented was false, but that discrimination was 
the real reason). 

Other circuits have held that the elements of a prima facie 
case, plus evidence of pretext, are sufficient to create a jury 
issue. See Gallo v. Prudential Residential Services, 22 F.3d 
1219, 1224-25 (2d Cir. 1994) (stating intentional age 
discrimination may be demonstrated by reliance on the 
evidence that established the prima facie case and proof of 
pretext without necessity for any additional evidence); 
Sheridan v. EJ. Dupoint de Nemours & Co., 100 F.3d 1061, 
1069 (3rd Cir. 1996) (stating evidence which supports a 
prima facie case, coupled with the jury’s determination that 
employer’s proffered explanations were pretextual is 
sufficient to support a verdict of discrimination); Fuentes \v. 
Perski, 32 F.3d 759, 761 (3d Cir. 1994) (stating to survive a 
motion for summary judgment, the plaintiff must introduce 
evidence which will cast doubt upon the reasons offered by 
the defendant so that the jury may infer that discrimination 
was the motivating factor); Anderson v. Baxter Health Care 
Corp., 13 F.3d 1120, 1122-23 (7th Cir. 1994) (stating fact- 
finder may find in the plaintiff’s favor when a prima facie 
case of discrimination is established and plaintiff shows the 
falsity of the employer’s proffered reasons); Courtney v. 


6 


Biosound, Inc., 42 F.3d 414. 418 (7th Cir. 1994) (stating 
plaintiff will defeat a motion for summary judgment by 
producing evidence from which a jury could infer that the 
company’s proffered reasons were pretextual); Washington v. 
Garrett, 10 F.3d 1421, 1433 (9th Cir. 1994) (stating once 
plaintiff establishes prima facie case, and raises genuine 
question as to whether the employer’s explanation is true, 
there will always be a question for the fact-finder); Combs v. 
Plantation Patterns, 106 F.3d 1519, 1529 (11th Cir. 1997) 
(stating plaintiff in a discrimination case can avoid judgment 
as a matter of law by proving the prima facie case and 
showing that the defendant's reasons are a pretext); and 
Barbour v. Merrill, 48 F.3d 1270, 1277 (D.C. Cir. 1995) 
(stating plaintiff only needs to establish a prima facie case 
and introduce evidence to discredit the defendant's proffered 
reasons in order to create a jury issue). 

This split in the circuits has been discussed by legal 
commentators. See Catherine J. Lanctot, Pretext-Plus After 
Hicks: The Circuit Split Remains, Wiley Employment Law 
Update 161 (1996); C. Pledger, Rhodes v. Guiberson Oil 
Tools: The Fifth Circuit’s Approach to Pretext Evidence in 
Employment Discrimination, 57 LA. L. REV. 1393; 
Catherine J. Lanctot, The Defendant Lies and the Plaintiff 
Loses: The Fallacy of the Pretext-Plus Rule in Employment 
Discrimination Cases, 43 Hastings L.J. 57 (1991). 

Besides the split in the circuits, this Court should also 
review this case since the approach of the Fifth Circuit is 
inconsistent with the indirect evidence method of proof 
mandated by this Court in McDonnell-Douglas Corp. v. 
Green, 411 U.S. 792, 804-05 (1973), and followed in Texas 
Dept. of Community Affairs v. Burdine, 450 U.S. 248, 256 
(1981), and Hicks, 509 U.S. at 502 (1993). The theory 
underlying McDonnell-Douglas and its progeny is that an 
employer in an employment discrimination case will not 


7 


ordinarily admit employment discrimination. Because of the 
unavailability of direct evidence, this Court adopted the 
indirect burden-shifting method of proof. Requiring direct 
evidence (i.e., “pretext plus”) cannot be reconciled with 
McDonnell-Douglas and its progeny, which hold direct 
evidence is not required. 


II. CERTIORARI SHOULD BE GRANTED SINCE 
THE FIFTH CIRCUIT’S WEIGHING OF ALL 
THE EVIDENCE IS INCONSISTENT WITH 
THE APPROACH OF OTHER CIRCUITS 
WHICH CONSIDER ONLY THE EVIDENCE 
FAVORING THE NON-MOVANT, AND 
SINCE THIS APPROACH FAILS TO TAKE 
INTO ACCOUNT THIS COURT’S TEACHING 
THAT THE STANDARDS FOR JUDGMENT 
AS A MATTER OF LAW UNDER RULE 50 
AND GRANTING SUMMARY JUDGMENT 
UNDER RULE 56 ARE THE SAME. 


In reversing the jury verdict, the Fifth Circuit applied the 
standard it adopted in its leading case, Boeing v. Shipman, 
411 F.2d 365, 374-75 (Sth Cir. 1969) (en banc): 


On motions for directed verdict and for judgment 

notwithstanding the verdict the court should 
consider all of the evidence - not just that 
evidence which supports the non-mover’s case 
... If the facts and inferences point so strongly 
and overwhelmingly in favor of one party that 
the court believes that reasonable men could not 
arrive at a contrary verdict, granting of the 
motions is proper ... there must be a conflict in 
substantial evidence to create a jury question ... 


The Fifth Circuit’s weighing of all the evidence, rather 
than determining only whether non-movant’s evidence 


8 


supports the verdict, is contrary to the rule in other circuits. 
Dissenting from this Court’s denial of certiorari, Justice 
White described the conflict in the circuits as follows: 


[I]t is the Second Circuit’s practice to examine 
all of the evidence in a manner most favorable 
to the non-moving party. This is also the 
position of at least the Fifth and Seventh 
Circuits. Boeing Co. v. Shipman, 411 F.2d 365, 
374-75 (CA 5 1969); Panter v. Marshall Field 
& Co., 646 F.2d 271, 281-282 (CA 7 1981). 
In the Eighth Circuit, however, it appears that 
only evidence which supports the verdict 
winner is to be considered. Simpson v. Skelly 
Oil Co., 371 F. 2d 563 (CA 8 1967). The First 
and Third Circuits follow a middle ground: the 
reviewing court may consider uncontradicted, 
unimpeached evidence from disinterested 
witnesses. Layne v. Vinzant, 657 F.2d 468, 
472 (CA 1 1981); Inventive Music Ltd. v. 
Cohen, 617 F.2d 29, 33 (CA 3 1980). Thus, 
the federal courts of appeals follow three 
different approaches to determining whether 
evidence is sufficient to create a jury issue. 


Venture Technology, Inc. v. Nat'l Fuel Gas Distribution 
Corp., 459 U.S. 1007, 1009 (1982). See also Dace v. ACF 
Indus., Inc., 722 F.2d 374, 376 (8th Cir. 1993) (stating 
“Generally, only evidence favoring the non-moving party 
should be considered”). 

The Court of Appeals’ decisions which have rejected the 
Fifth Circuit’s consideration of all of the evidence and 
consider only the evidence which supports the non-movant 
are: CPC International, Inc. v. Northbrook Excess & Surplus 
Ins. Co., 46 F.3d 1211, 1214 (1st Cir. 1995); Casko v. Elgin, 


EE 


9 


Joliet and Eastern Ry. Co., 361 F.2d 748, 749 (7th Cir. 
1966); Lyons v. J.C. Penney Co., 316 F.2d 753-54 (7th Cir. 
1963); Simpson v. Skelly Oil Co., 371 F.2d 563, 566-67 (8th 
Cir. 1967); Kingston v. McGrath, 232 F.2d 495, 497 (9th Cir. 
1956). See also Judge Rivers’ dissent in Shipman, 411 F.2d 
at 381-82 (Sth Cir. 1961) (Rivers, J., dissenting) 
(consideration of all the evidence is inconsistent with this 
Court’s precedents). 

The Fifth Circuit’s continued adherence to the Shipman 
standard is also inconsistent with Anderson v. Liberty Lobby, 
Inc., 477 U.S. 242, 251-253 (1986). Anderson holds that the 
standards for a directed verdict are the same as those for 
summary judgment under Rule 56: 


The Court has said that summary judgment 
should be granted where the evidence is such 
that it “would require a directed verdict for 
the moving party.” ... and we have noted that 
the “genuine issue” summary judgment 
standard is “very close” to the reasonable 
jury” directed verdict standard: “The primary 
difference between the two (2) motions is 
procedural; summary judgment motions are 
usually made before trial and decided on 
documentary evidence, while directed verdict 
motions are made at trial and decided on the 
evidence that has been admitted.” ... In 
essence, though, the inquiry under each is the 
same: whether the evidence presents a 
sufficient disagreement to require submission 
to a jury or whether it is so one-sided that one 
party must prevail as a matter of law. [internal 
citations omitted] 


10 
Anderson v. Liberty Lobby, 477 U.S. at 251-252. 


Contrary to the Fifth Circuit, some courts hold that the 
Rule 56 standard also applies under Rule 50. Advantor 
Capital Corp. v. Yeary, 136 F.3d 1259, 1263 (10th Cir. 1998) 
(stating standard for judgment as a matter of law is 
essentially identical to “genuine issue” requirement in 
summary judgment context); Deimer v. Cincinnati Sub-Zero 
Prodos, 58 F.3d 341, 343 (7th Cir. 1995) (stating we now 
adopt the federal reasonable person’s standard across the 
board: pre-trial, mid-trial, post-trial, and on appeal, for 
evaluating both the merits and the quantum of relief. 
Contrary decisions are no longer authoritative); 
Schoonejongen v. Curtis Wright Corp., 143 F.3d 120, 129 
(3rd Cir. 1998) (stating standard for summary judgment is 
the same standard as standard for motion for judgment as a 
matter of law). 

If the Fifth Circuit had applied the Anderson teaching that 
the Rule 56 standard also governs Rule 50 -- then the Court 
of Appeals would not have reversed the jury verdict. 
Statements of age bias by a decision maker would obviously 
have created “issues of material fact” as to whether age was 
the reason for the decision. 


CONCLUSION 


This case presents important questions of the role of the 
jury in employment discrimination cases. The courts of 
appeals have taken different approaches on this issue; 
therefore, review by this Court is warranted.

FACTS AND PROCEEDINGS 


Fifty-seven year old Reeves was employed for 40 years

supervised by Reeves, and a special line which was 
supervised by 35 year old Joe Oswalt. Forty-five year old 
Russell Caldwell was the manager of the department and he 
supervised both Reeves and Oswalt. 

At all times relevant to this case, a union represented 
Sanderson's production and maintenance employees. The 
union contract included general work rules, part of which 
dealt specifically with attendance. Pursuant to these rules, 
and employee who was absent from work in excess of five 
percent of his scheduled hours in a month, or who was late 
twice in a month, was subject to disciplinary action. 

As part of his essential duties as a supervisor, Reeves was 
required to keep daily, weekly, and monthly records of the 
attendance and tardiness of employees under his control. 
These records were reviewed by Reeves for accuracy before 
he passed them on to Caldwell, who then sent them on to 
data processing.

Control - under the direction of Powe Chesnut - conducted a

problems on Reeves’s regular line, stemming from a lax

B. TheADEA 


The ADEA makes it “unlawful for an employer... to 
discharge any individual . . . because of such individual’s 
age.”* To establish a violation of the ADEA, a plaintiff must 
prove, by a preponderance of the evidence, intentional 
discrimination on the part of his employer.’ As direct 
evidence of discrimination is rare, plaintiffs may rely on 
indirect evidence and reasonable inferences to establish an 
ADEA claim under the McDonnell-Douglas burden-shifting 
analysis."

facie case, thereby establishing a rebuttable presumption of 
age discrimination." If the plaintiff meets this burden, the

Considering all of the evidence in a light most favorable
