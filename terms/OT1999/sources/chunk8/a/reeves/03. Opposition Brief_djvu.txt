This Court in St. Mary’s reaffirmed that once a 
prima facie case is established, then the burden of 
production shifts to the defendant although the burden of 
persuasion always remains with the plaintiff. Id. at 507. 
Moreover, St. Mary’s held that once the employer meets 
this burden, then the presumption made by the prima facie 
showing “drops out of the picture”, Id. at 511, and, 
indeed, is “no longer relevant”. Id. at 510-11; Texas Dep't 
of Community Affairs v. Burdine, 450 U.S. 248, 255, n.10 
(1981). To be sure, as set forth in St. Mary’s, once a 
defendant sets forth the legitimate, nondiscriminatory 
reason (there is no issue in the case sub judice in this 
regard), a plaintiff must demonstrate “that the proffered 
reason was not the true reason for the employment 
decision and that race was”. St. Mary's at 508. Indeed, 
the court in St. Mary’s made it clear that one such as 


12 

Petitioner has the burden to show that the proffered 
reason was false and that the real reason was discrimination. 
Id. at 515. Your Honors have further reminded that “it is 
not enough ... to disbelieve the employer”.’” Id. at 519. 
Finally, St. Mary’s held that a reason cannot be proved to 
be a pretext for discrimination unless it is shown “both 
that the reason was false and that discrimination was the 
real reason”. Id. at 515.

BY FINDING THAT AGE-BASED COMMENTS

WHEREIN THIS COURT CAN CLEARLY 
DELINEATE WHAT A PLAINTIFF NEED DO 
TO PRESENT A JURY ISSUE IN AN AGE 
DISCRIMINATION CASE. 


In Rhodes v. Guiberson Oil & Tool, 39 F.3d 537 (S" 
Cir. 1996), this Court announced a “pretext plus” test. 
Under this test for age discrimination, it was necessary 
for Plaintiff to produce proof of both pretext and direct 
evidence of age discrimination. However, Rhodes was 
reversed en banc, with this Court holding that independent 
direct evidence of age discrimination is necessary only 
in some, not in all, cases. See Rhodes, en banc, 75 F.3d at 
994. 


This case was one of those cases where the evidence 
of “pretext” was held to be insufficient evidence of 
age discrimination. Thus, the panel determined some 
additional evidence of age discrimination was necessary. 
The only other evidence of age discrimination was 
Plaintiff's being treated differently from younger persons 


19a 


Appendix 


and the “age-based statements” by a decision maker. The 


panel held such evidence insufficient when compared to 
contrary evidence. 


In this regard, the panel’s decision cannot be squared 
with the decisions of other panels. Even where there has 
not been shown any evidence of pretext, and age-based 
statements are the only evidence of age discrimination, 
some panels have held this evidence is sufficient. 
Furthermore, where evidence of age-biased statements 
have existed, other panels have not “balanced” contrary 
evidence. These panels have acted consistently with the 
rule of Boeing v. Shipman, 411 F.2d 365, 374-375 (5" 
Cir. 1961), which requires the jury, not the court of 
appeals, to balance the evidence. 


In Weaver v. Amoco Prod. Co., 66 F.3d 35, 86-87 
(5" Cir. 1995), plaintiff, one of five (5) supervisors who 
had been laid off during a reduction in force, produced 
no evidence of age discrimination other than that a 
supervisor had agreed with plaintiff's statement that “a 
guy my age doesn’t have much future left”. This statement 
in Weaver is less direct than that involved in this case, 
and yet was held sufficient evidence to present a jury issue 
on the age discrimination issue. Furthermore, the Weaver 
panel did not balance the defense’s evidence against the 
age-based statement. 


In Woodhouse v. Magnolia Hosp., 92 F.3d 248 (5* 
Cir. 1996), the President of the board of trustees of the 
hospital had said that “old people” would be one group 
who would be chosen for a layoff. This Court held that 


20a 
Appendix 


such a statement could constitute “substantial evidence” 
of age discrimination, even though the president of the 
board was not involved in choosing plaintiff for the layoff. 


In Portis v. First National Bank of New Albany, 
Mississippi, 34 F.3d 325, 329 (S® Cir. 1994), plaintiff's 
supervisor's statement that plaintiff would be paid less 
because she was a woman was held as evidence of sex 
discrimination in a demotion case, even though this 
statement was unrelated to the demotion decision in issue. 


Smith v. Berry Co., 165 F.3d 390, 394 (5" Cir. 1999) 
held that a memorandum by a company president referring 
to older employees as a “very difficult group”, was 
evidence of age discrimination, although the memo 
predated the discharge decision by six (6) years, and there 
was no evidence that this statement related to plaintiff. 


Ray v. luka Municipal Sep. School Dist., 51 F.3d 
1246, 1250 (5" Cir. 1995) found substantial evidence of 
a retaliatory motive from a statement made by one member 
of a school board, even though the case contained no 
evidence of any such discriminatory statements by a 
majority of the board, which made the discharge decision. 


EEOC v. Manville Sales Corp., 27 F.3d 1089, 1094 
(5" Cir. 1994) held that various age-based comments by 
a district manager over a four (4) year period referring to 
plaintiff as an “old man was substantial evidence of age 
discrimination, even though the district manager, as did 
Chestnut, only recommended the discharge. This Court 
said that such statements could be “highly probative of 
discriminatory intent”. 27 F.3d 1089.

employer's retaining several employees [over fifty (50)} 
including “several employees in their late sixties”. Panel

retained many older employees ignores Cooper v. Federal

“A racially balanced work force cannot

81 L.Ed.2d at 729. 


By finding that evidence of the ages of other 
employees retained, overcame Plaintiff's evidence of age 
discrimination, the panel not only impermissibly 


“balanced” the evidence, but disregarded this applicable 
teaching of the Supreme Court. 


CONCLUSION 


The evidence in this case is impossible to distinguish 
from that relied upon by several other panels in upholding 
jury verdicts. There is little justice when plaintiffs 
under practically identical facts, may obtain a jury’s 
consideration but Reeves may not. To maintain

Amendment of a right to trial by jury, this case should be 
reheard en banc.
