CAPITAL CASE 
QUESTION PRESENTED 


Simmons v. South Carolina holds that when a prosecu- 
tor seeks the death sentence on the ground of the defen- 
dant’s future dangerousness, the defendant has a 
constitutional right to inform the jurors truthfully that if 
they spare his life, state law forbids him ever to be 
released from prison. Does the rule in Simmons turn on 
the actual operation of state law, or on its hypertechnical 
terms; and must a federal habeas court adjudicating a 
Simmons claim make its own analysis of the functional 
consequences of state law, or is it bound by the state 
courts’ characterization of state law for federal constitu- 
tional purposes?

STATEMENT OF THE CASE 
A. The Crimes and the Trials 


In the summer of 1992, just after being released on 
parole from a Virginia prison, petitioner Bobby Ramdass 
committed a series of violent robberies. These included 
the armed robbery of two customers at a Pizza Hut in 
Fairfax County, Virginia, on August 25, 1992; an armed 
robbery at a Domino’s Pizza in Alexandria, Virginia, on 
August 30, 1992; and an armed robbery at a 7-Eleven in 
Fairfax County on September 2, 1992, during which a 
store clerk, Mohammed Kayani, was shot and killed. At 
the time of these offenses, Ramdass was 20 years old. 


Ramdass was quickly apprehended, and a series of 
separate criminal proceedings followed. First, a Fairfax 
County jury found Ramdass guilty of the Pizza Hut rob- 
beries on December 15, 1992, and fixed his aggregate 
sentence at imprisonment for 76 years. Ramdass v. 
Angelone, 450 S.E.2d 360, 361 (Va. 1994) (J.A. 130, 281, 355, 


385). The court entered judgment and imposed the jury’s 
recommended sentence on January 22, 1993. Id. (J.A. 130). 


Meanwhile, on January 7, 1993, an Alexandria jury 
convicted Ramdass of the Domino’s Pizza robbery and 
fixed his sentence at 18 years imprisonment. Id. (J.A. 130, 
281). The court subsequently entered judgment and 
imposed the jury’s recommended sentence on February 
18, 1993. Id. (J.A. 130, 281). 


Three weeks after the Domino’s Pizza guilty verdict, 
on January 28, 1993, a third state court jury, sitting in 
Fairfax County, found Ramdass guilty of capital murder 
for the death of the 7-Eleven employee, Mr. Kayani. (J.A. 
4). The court conducted a capital sentencing trial before 
the jury the next day, January 29, 1993. (J.A. 7). At that 
proceeding, the prosecution’s case for the death penalty 
rested exclusively on the statutory aggravating factor that 
Ramdass “would commit criminal acts of violence [in the 
future] that would constitute a continuing serious threat 
to society.” Virginia Code § 19.2-264.4(C). 


In support of this future-dangerousness aggravating 
factor, the prosecutor relied on the juries’ previous ver- 
dicts in the Pizza Hut robbery (J.A. 9, 16-18, 53-55, 81), 
and the Domino’s Pizza robbery (J.A. 57-59, 81). The 
prosecutor also brought to the jury’s attention Ramdass’s 
involvement in several as-yet-unadjudicated offenses: a 
robbery of a hotel clerk (J.A. 9-10, 18-23, 55-56, 81), a 
robbery and shooting of a taxi driver (J.A. 23-46), and the 
shooting of a drug dealer (J.A. 62-64). In addition, the 
prosecutor presented records of Ramdass’s juvenile con- 
viction for purse snatching (J.A. 15, 80) and an unarmed 
robbery that occurred in 1988, for which Ramdass was 


tried as an adult and sentenced to seven years imprison- 
ment. (J.A. 9, 12-14, 81). " 


The prosecutor emphasized the issue of parole dur- 
ing the sentencing trial. He elicited testimony showing 
that Ramdass had been released on parole in 1992, very 
shortly before the commission of the instant crimes, after 
serving only four years of the seven-year sentence for 
unarmed robbery. (J.A. 46-47, 61-62). In eliciting this fact, 
the prosecutor was careful to distinguish between “man- 
datory” and “discretionary” parole, and he established 
that Ramdass’s parole release had been mandatory. (J.A. 
46-47, 51-52, 61-62). Later, during his cross-examination 
of Ramdass, the prosecutor established that Ramdass had 
also been released on parole from a state juvenile institu- 
tion (J.A. 60-62), that he was returned to the institution 
after violating that parole, and that despite his parole 
violation, he was nevertheless released again on a 30-day 
furlough from which he never returned. (J.A. 60-61). The 
prosecutor used his cross-examination of Ramdass to 
remind the jury that state prison authorities had “manda- 
torily released [Ramdass] on parole in May of ‘92” after 
he had served less than four years of a seven-year adult 
prison sentence. (J.A. 62). He returned to this subject in 
closing argument, stating: 


Finally in May of 1992, he’s back on the streets 
of Fairfax County. When he finally got paroled 
in May of 1992, it’s significant to note that this 
parole is for a robbery. The first time he was 
tried as an adult, he was tried for robbery in 
1988. He serves a little over four years total for 
that robbery, in May he’s released. 


(J.A. 81). After again enumerating Ramdass’s adjudicated 
and unadjudicated crimes, the prosecutor concluded: 


We submit to you members of the Jury, he is the 
epitome of dangerousness. He is a proven 
serious threat to society. The best indicator of 
future behavior is past behavior and he does it 
in spades. 


(J.A. 82). 


In an effort to rebut the future dangerousness aggra- 
vating factor and persuade the jury to vote for life 
imprisonment, defense counsel tallied the term-of-years 
sentences for Ramdass’s adjudicated offenses and argued 
that, if these sentences were tacked together, they would 
total at least 99 years. (J.A. 49, 85). In his opening state- 
ment at the penalty phase, defense counsel squarely con- 
ceded that Ramdass “should be locked up for the rest of 
his life” (J.A. 51), and in closing argument he went even 
further: 


If you believe that Bobbie Lee’s a wild, 
crazed animal, then society must do something. 
I agree with you, they must. We must. 


But I submit a wild animal removed from its 
environment is not merely destroyed. We cage 
it. We put it away for life and that is what you 
should do. 


*s 2 


How much does society need to exact? I 
agree he should be put away for [his crimes]. He 
should never see the light of day. But to take 
another human life, that’s not right. 


(J.A. 84-85). The core of defense counsel’s argument on 
future dangerousness was that his client would not — and 
should not — ever “see the light of day.” (J.A. 85). Counsel 
was barred by Virginia law, however, from saying any- 
thing about whether Ramdass’s present or future terms of 
imprisonment might be cut short by release on parole, as 
the jury knew his prior juvenile and adult prison sen- 
tences had been. (J.A. 89-90). See, e.g., Clark v. Common- 
wealth, 257 S.E.2d 784, 792 (Va. 1979) (jury may not be 
informed of defendant’s parole eligibility or ineligibility), 
cert. denied, 444 U.S. 1049 (1980); Hinton v. Commonwealth, 
247 S.E.2d 704, 706 (Va. 1978) (same). | 


In addition to arguing that society could adequately 
protect itself from Ramdass by imprisoning him for life, 
defense counsel presented mitigating evidence about 
Ramdass’s deprived background. (J.A. 48-50). This evi- 
dence showed that Ramdass was the first child of an 
interracial marriage between a former topless dancer and 
a U.S. Army soldier of Pakistani origin. (J.A. 67-68). Ram- 
dass’s mother, Peggy Ramdass, was one of 21 children, 
and her own childhood had been marked by neglect, 
abuse, and incest. (J.A. 65-66, 186-187). Her parents and 
siblings, who were white, referred to Peggy and her dark- 
skinned sons as “nigger lovers,” and refused to help them 
even when they became homeless and desperately poor. 
(J.A. 69). For his part, Ramdass’s father consistently 
denied that Ramdass was actually his child: he beat Ram- 
dass constantly, but spared Ramdass’s younger brother 
Mark, whom he acknowledged as his son. (J.A. 68). Ram- 
dass’s father deserted his family when the children were 


very young, and at the time of a 1988 presentence investi- 
gation, conducted when Ramdass was 17, the elder Ram- 
dass was reportedly AWOL from the Army, and his 
whereabouts were unknown. (J.A. 51-51a). 


After her husband’s disappearance, Peggy Ramdass 
and her sons moved from place to place, and for a time 
were forced to live in the furniture storage bin of an 
apartment complex. (J.A. 69). When the boys were about 
six and seven years old, she taught them to steal food 
from grocery stores in order to eat, and to beg for money 
by pretending that they were soliciting for charity. (J.A. 
69-70). She lost custody of her children twice. (J.A. 74-75). 
When the boys were about eight and nine years old, she 
began to live with a boyfriend identified as Sanchez. (J.A. 
75). Sanchez abused both Peggy and the children, locking 
Ramdass in the closet and beating him with a belt buckle. 
(J.A. 75-76). At one point, Sanchez held Mark Ramdass 
out of a fifteenth floor window by his legs and threatened 
to drop him. (J.A. 76). 


After the close of the evidence, the trial court 
instructed the jury that it could fix Ramdass’s punish- 
ment at death only if it found “unanimously and beyond 
a reasonable doubt that there is a probability that the 
Defendant would commit criminal acts of violence that 
would constitute a continuing, serious threat to society.” 
(J.A. 76a). The court also instructed the jury that it was 
permitted to fix Ramdass’s sentence at life imprisonment, 
even if it found the future dangerousness aggravating 
factor. After deliberating for nearly three hours, the jury 
sent out the following question to the court: 


If the Defendant is given life, is there a possi- 
bility of parole at some time before his natural 
death? 


(J.A. 88). Over defense counsel’s objection, the court gave 
the response then required by Virginia law: 


If you find the Defendant guilty, which you 
have in this case, you should impose such pun- 
ishment as you feel is just under the evidence 
and within the instructions of the Court. You are 
not to concern yourselves with what may hap- 
pen afterwards. 


(J.A. 91). The jurors deliberated for another hour and a 
half and then fixed Ramdass’s sentence at death.

been, ineligible for parole. (J.A. 93-94). Defense counsel 
then reported that he had attempted to contact members 
of the sentencing jury to determine whether information 
about the possibility of parole would have affected their 
verdict. He reported that he was able to reach six jurors, 
and three of them (two men and one woman) were will- 
ing to speak. (J.A. 94-95). Counsel reported that each of 
these jurors said they personally would have voted for

life imprisonment rather than death if they had known

B. Appeal, Certiorari, and Remand 


On direct appeal, the Virginia Supreme Court 
affirmed the conviction and death sentence. Ramdass v. 
Commonwealth, 437 S.E.2d 566 (Va. 1993) (J.A. 102-126). In 
response to Ramdass’s argument that his parole inel- 
igibility should have been disclosed to the jury in answer 


2 If even one juror declines to fix the sentence at death, the 
court is required as a matter of law to impose a life sentence. 
Virginia Code § 19.2-264.4(E). 


3 See Note, The Meaning of “Life” for Virginia Jurors and Its 
Effect on Reliability in Capital Sentencing, 75 Va. L. Rev. 1605 
(1989), also cited in Simmons v. South Carolina, 512 U.S. 154, 170, 
n.9 (1994). 


10 


to its question about parole, the court said, “We repeat- 
edly have held that a jury should not hear evidence of 
parole eligibility or ineligibility because it is not a relevant 
consideration in fixing the appropriate sentence.” Id. at 
573 (J.A. 117) (emphasis added). Shortly thereafter, this 
Court decided Simmons v. South Carolina, 512 U.S. 154 
(1994), and remanded Ramdass’s case for reconsideration 
in light of that case. Ramdass v. Virginia, 512 U.S. 1217 
(1994) (J.A. 121). 


On remand, Ramdass contended that Simmons had 
conclusively established that the trial court’s refusal to 
answer his jury’s inquiry about parole eligibility was 
federal constitutional error. (J.A. 123-127). Ramdass 
pointed out that he was not eligible for parole under 
Virginia’s “three strikes” law, which provided in relevant 
part that: 


{aJny person convicted of three separate felony 
offenses of (i) murder, (ii) rape or (iii) robbery 
by the presenting of firearms or other deadly 
weapon, or any combination of the offenses 
specified in subdivisions (i), (ii) or (iii) when 
such offenses were not part of a common act, 
transaction or scheme shall not be eligible for 
parole. 


Virginia Code § 53.1-151(B1) (App. 1-4). Ramdass identi- 
fied his three qualifying convictions as the Pizza Hut 
armed robberies, the Domino’s Pizza armed robbery, and 
the capital murder during the 7-Eleven robbery. (J.A. 
123-124). 


The Virginia Supreme Court set out its understand- 
ing of the federal constitutional right at issue in the 
following preliminary paragraph: 


11 


In Simmons, the Supreme Court held that when 
“future dangerousness” is an issue in the sen- 
tencing phase of a capital murder case, the jury 
is entitled to information concerning the defen- 
dant’s parole ineligibility. Simmons, 512 U.S. at 
__, 114 S. Ct. at 2198. Hence, Simmons applies 
only if Ramdass was ineligible for parole when 
the jury was considering his sentence. 


Ramdass v. Commonwealth, 450 S.E.2d 360, 361 (Va. 1994) 
(J.A. 129-130) (emphasis in original). The court then 
undertook to determine whether Ramdass was techni- 
cally ineligible for parole on the date of his capital sen- 
tencing trial. It acknowledged that the Pizza Hut 
robberies counted as Ramdass’s first “strike” under the 
statute, and it held that the jury’s verdict that Ramdass 
was guilty of capital murder counted as the second 
“strike.” Id. (J.A. 130). The court refused, however, to 
count the jury’s guilty verdict in the Domino’s Pizza 
robbery as the third “strike.” Noting that Virginia’s three- 
strikes statute requires three “convictions,” the court held 
that a jury verdict of guilty does not become a “convic- 
tion” until judgment is entered. Id. (J.A. 130-131) (citing 
Smith v. Commonwealth, 113 S.E. 707, 709 (Va. 1922) (inter- 
preting word “conviction” as requiring entry of judgment 
for purpose of determining whether candidate was dis- 
qualified from holding public office)). The court con- 
cluded that because the Domino's Pizza verdict was not 
reduced to judgment until 19 days after the capital sen- 
tencing trial, Ramdass was technically still eligible for 
parole under state law on the day that the jury considered 
his sentence for capital murder. Ramdass v. Commonwealth, 
450 S.E.2d at 361 (J.A. 131). Treating this analysis of the 


12 


word “conviction” as dispositive of Ramdass’s constitu- 
tional rights under Simmons, the Virginia Supreme Court 
again affirmed his death sentence. Id. (J.A. 131). This 
Court denied Ramdass’s ensuing petition for certiorari. 
Ramdass v. Virginia, 514 U.S. 1085 (1995). 


C. Collateral Proceedings 


Ramdass next filed a habeas corpus petition in the 
Virginia Supreme Court, alleging that his rights under the 
Fourteenth Amendment were violated by the trial court's 
refusal to inform the jury of his ineligibility for parole. 
(J.A. 145-149). The Virginia Supreme Court summarily 
dismissed the claim on the ground that it had already 
been addressed on the merits on direct appeal and would 
not be reconsidered on collateral review.* Ramdass v.

SUMMARY OF THE ARGUMENT 


The Virginia Supreme Court’s charge, on remand 
from this Court, was to determine whether Ramdass’s 
rights under the Due Process Clause were infringed when 
he was not permitted to inform. the jurors that if they 
sentenced him to life imprisonment, there was no possi- 
bility that he could be released on parole before his 
natural death. The federal question (was there a violation 
of due process) thus overlay a state-law question (was 
there a possibility of release on parole under state law). 


The Virginia Supreme Court begged the federal ques- 
tion. Its adjudication was limited to an examination of 
state law and a determination that on the date of the 
capital sentencing trial, Ramdass was eligible for parole 
because he did not yet have three “convictions” as the 
state court defined that word. This analytical approach 
was flawed for the same reason that the state court's 
analysis was flawed in Bakery and Pastry Drivers and 
Helpers Local 802 v. Wohl, 315 U.S. 769 (1942). When a 
right arises under federal law and overlays state law, a 
reviewing court errs if it looks only at subsidiary techni- 
cal definitions under state law and assumes that those 
definitions circumscribe the federal right. Id. at 774. 
Rather, when the federal question concerns the nature or 
duration of restraints on liberty, the Court has held that a 


20 


reviewing court should consider the functional realities 
of the situation rather than technical definitions. 


This Court’s precedents, going back more than a 
century, establish that when a federal question rests on 
top of a state-law question, the state court’s characteriza- 
tion of its own law is not dispositive of the federal 
question. For purposes of deciding the federal question, 
the reviewing court must look to the actual consequences 
of state law. This rule holds true regardless of the nature 
of the case - civil, criminal, or collateral. See, e.g., Memphis 
Steam Laundry Cleaner, Inc. v. Stone, 342 U.S. 389 (1952) 
(state can determine operating incidence of its own tax, 
but for purposes of deciding whether that tax offends 
Commerce Clause, court must look to consequences of 
the tax); Bailey v. Alabama, 219 U.S. 219 (1911) (state can 
characterize criminal statute as one that merely punishes 
fraud, but for purposes of deciding whether statute vio- 
lates Thirteenth Amendment by compelling involuntary 
servitude on pain of imprisonment, court must look to 
actual effect and operation of statute). 


Under this well-established rule, Virginia can define 
the incidence of its parole law however it wishes, and 
Ramdass will have no federal basis for complaint. But it 
is equally plain that the federal due process issue in 
Ramdass’s Simmons claim is governed by how Virginia’s 
parole law actually operates, not by the label affixed by 
state law. The Virginia Supreme Court did not consider 
how state law actually operated, and the Fourth Circuit 
did not correct that error. 


In Ramdass’s case, state law in actual operation fore- 
closed any possibility that he could be released on parole 


21 


before his natural death. He had two of the three 
“strikes” required for parole ineligibility by state law, and 
entry of judgment for the third “strike” was a ministerial 
act whose performance was foreseeable, imminent, and 
inexorable. 


The validity of Ramdass’s claim under Simmons is 
proved by this Court’s analysis in Simmons itself, which 
was based on the practical and foreseeable operation of 
South Carolina law rather than a technical determination 
of parole eligibility on a particular date. As the undis- 
puted record clearly shows, Simmons was technically still 
eligible for parole at the time of his capital sentencing 
trial, and he could not be declared ineligible for parole 
until the parole board later determined whether his prof- 
fered “strikes” were for unrelated crimes. See Respon- 
dent’s Brief in Simmons v. South Carolina, No. 92-9059, 
1993 WL 669003, at 95-96 (filed Dec. 14, 1993). The Court 
was clearly cognizant of that fact. Based on the pragmatic 
and foreseeable operation of state law, however, this 
Court considered Simmons’s ineligibility for parole to 
have been conceded. See, e.g., Simmons, 512 U.S. at 166 
(plurality opinion) (“Respondent admits that an instruc- 
tion informing the jury that petitioner is ineligible for 
parole is legally accurate.”) 


The fact that a Virginia trial court had not yet per- 
formed the ministerial act of entering judgment on Ram- 
dass’s third “strike” is of no more consequence than the 
fact that the South Carolina parole board had not yet 
formally determined whether Simmons’s prior convic- 
tions were for unrelated offenses. The only obstacle to 
either of these predictable formalities was the sort of 


22 


“hypothetical future development” that this Court dis- 
missed out of hand in Simmons. Everyone knew that the 
trial judge would enter the judgment and make Ramdass 
ineligible for parole, just as everyone knew that the South 
Carolina parole board would declare that Simmons’s 
prior crimes were unrelated and make him ineligible for 
parole. 


The Court’s interchangeable use of terms such as 
“parole ineligibility,” “imprisonment without possibility 
of parole,” and “never would be released on parole” 
underscores the pragmatic nature of this Court’s Simmons 
analysis. Just as Simmons had a due process right to 
inform the jury even though he was technically still eligi- 
ble for parole on the date of his capital sentencing trial, so 


did Ramdass. 


Even if the South Carolina parole board had formally 
declared that Simmons was ineligible for parole before 
the capital sentencing, however, Ramdass would be enti- 
tled to relief because there was (and is) no possibility that 
he could ever be released on parole before his natural 
death. This Court’s due process concern, expressed in 
both the plurality opinion and Justice O’Connor’s concur- 
ring opinion, was that jurors should be permitted to make 
a realistic assessment of the threat to society that the 
defendant would pose in the future if he was sentenced 
to life imprisonment. The threat that Ramdass posed to 
society depended on whether, as a pragmatic matter, 
there was any possibility that he could be released on 
parole in the future; it did not depend on whether he was 
technically eligible or ineligible for parole on the single 
day of his capital sentencing trial.

ARGUMENT 


The ultimate issue before the Virginia Supreme Court 
on remand was whether, in light of Simmons v. South 
Carolina, 512 U.S. 154 (1994), Ramdass’s rights under the 
Due Process Clause of the Fourteenth Amendment to the 
federal Constitution were infringed when the trial court 
refused to inform the jury that the only available alterna- 
tive to a sentence of death was life imprisonment without 
the possibility of parole. The federal question (whether 
there was a violation of the Fourteenth Amendment) thus 
rested on top of the state-law question (whether, under 
state law, Ramdass would ever have the opportunity to 
be released on parole). This case therefore involves a 
simple problem that the Court has encountered and 
resolved many times before: Where a federal right over- 
lays state law, does the federal question turn on the 
state’s technical characterization of its own law, or does it 
depend on the operational consequences of state law? 


Preliminarily, it is noteworthy that the Virginia 
Supreme Court begged the federal question presented. Its 
adjudication was limited to an examination of state law 
and a determination that on the date of the capital sen- 
tencing trial, Ramdass’s formal status under that law was 
not one of “parole ineligibility,” Ramdass v. Commonwealth, 
450 S.E.2d at 361 (Va. 1994) (j.A. 130) (emphasis in origi- 
nal), because he did not yet have three qualifying convic- 
tions. In restricting its analysis to the technical meaning 
of the word “conviction” under state law and deeming 
the answer to the state-law question to be dispositive of 
any federal question, the Virginia Supreme Court com- 
mitted the same analytical error this Court described in 


24 


Bakery and Pastry Drivers and Helpers Local 802 v. Wohl, 315 
U.S. 769 (1942). 


In Bakery and Pastry Drivers, the state courts had 
enjoined a union from engaging in informational picket- 
ing on the ground that this was a federally protected 
activity only if its purpose was “the dissemination of 
information concerning the facts of a labor dispute,” 
Thornhill v. Alabama, 310 U.S. 88, 102 (1940), and there was 
no “labor dispute” as that term was defined by New York 
law. This Court reversed, succinctly describing the state 
court’s conceptual error: - 


So far as we can ascertain from the opinions 
delivered by the state courts in this case, those 
courts were concerned only with the question 
whether there was involved a labor dispute 
within the meaning of the New York statutes 
and assumed that the legality of the injunction 
followed from a determination that such a dis- 
pute was not involved. Of course that does not 
follow: one need not be in a “labor dispute” as 
defined by state law to have a right under the 
Fourteenth Amendment to express a grievance 
in a labor matter. 


Bakery and Pastry Drivers, 315 U.S. at 774. 


Simmons similarly establishes that a capital defendant 
need not already be technically ineligible for parole on 
the date of his capital sentencing trial in order to have a 
right under the Fourteenth Amendment to inform the 
jurors that, in its actual operation, state law forecloses 
any possibility of his release on parole. See Section B infra. 
By looking only at the definition of the word “conviction” 
under state law, the Virginia Supreme Court disregarded 


25 


this federal right and undid indirectly what it was pro- 
hibited from undoing directly. 


A. The Lower Courts Engaged in the Wrong Analysis 


1. Questions Concerning the Nature or Duration 
of Restraints on Liberty Require a Functional, 
Rather Than a Technical, Mode of Analysis 


There can be no disagreement as to the practical and 
foreseeable reality of Ramdass’s situation: The trial judge 
in the Domino’s Pizza case was going to enter judgment 
19 days after the capital sentencing trial, and from that 
day forth until the day he died, Ramdass would have no 
possibility of release on parole.* No state or federal court 
judge has questioned that immutable fact. Nonetheless, 
because a ministerial requirement of state law had not yet 
been satisfied, the Virginia Supreme Court employed, and 
the Fourth Circuit majority ratified, a reading of Simmons 
that disregarded the functional reality of parole inel- 
igibility. 


Simmons falls within a line of cases from this Court 
that address the need to define category boundaries when

condition or status that is both a practical fact and a legal

duration of restraints on individual liberty, this Court has 
resolved the problem by rejecting solutions that “place[ ] 
form over substance, and labels over reality.” Bowen v. 
Gilliard, 483 U.S. 587, 606 (1987). 


For example, in applying the requirement under Mir-

2. Federal Questions Require a Mode of Analysis 
That Looks to the Operating Consequences of 
Relevant State Law, Rather Than to the State’s 
Characterization of That Law 


In focusing on a technical assessment of parole eligi- 
bility, the Virginia Supreme Court improperly limited its 
analysis to (i) characterizing Ramdass’s status as a matter 
of state law and (ii) treating the applicability of the fed- 
eral constitutional rule of Simmons as controlled by that 
characterization. See Bakery and Pastry Drivers, supra. The 
Fourth Circuit failed to correct this error, deeming the 
state’s characterization of its own law to be dispositive of 
the federal question. Such .n analytical approach is con- 
trary to this Court’s well-established precedents, For 
more than a century, the Court has held consistently and 
in a wide variety of contexts — civil, criminal, and collat- 
eral - that when feaeral law overlays state law, the 
proper resolution of federal questions depends on the 
operational consequences of state-law rules, not on the 
characterization that a state court or legislature puts on 
those rules. 


In Bailey v. Alabama, 219 U.S. 219 (1911), for example, 
the Court considered whether the Thirteenth Amendment 
was violated by a state statute that (i) made it a criminal 
offense for a laborer to enter into an employment contract 
with intent to injure or defraud and then, after obtaining 
a salary advance from the employer, to refuse to perform 
the promised service or return the advance; and (ii) made 
refusal or failure to perform the service or refund the 
money prima facie evidence of the requisite intent to injure 
or defraud. Although the state characterized the statute 
as one that merely punished fraud, this Court concluded 


29 


that, for purposes of the federal question presented, the 
consequence of the statute was to employ the threat of 
criminal prosecution as a means of compelling involun- 
tary servitude for mere breach of contract. Id. at 238. 


We cannot escape the conclusion that, although 
the statute in terms is to punish fraud, still its 
natural and inevitable effect is to expose to con- 
viction for crime those who simply fail or refuse 
to perform contracts for personal service in liq- 
uidation of a debt; and judging its purpose by 
its effect, that it seeks in this way to provide the 
means of compulsion through which perfor- 
mance of such service may be secured. 


Id. at 238. Because the statute, in actual operation, com- 
pelled the employee to perform the contracted-for service 
on pain of imprisonment, it violated the Thirteenth 
Amendment. Id. at 293-94. 


In Mullaney v. Wilbur, 421 U.S. 684 (1975), the Court 
considered whether the Due Process Clause was 
infringed by a Maine statute that required a defendant 
charged with murder to prove he acted without malice in 
order to reduce the homicide to manslaughter. Affirming 
Wilbur’s murder conviction, the Maine Supreme Judicial 
Court held that murder and manslaughter were not dis- 
tinct crimes but were different degrees of the single 
generic offense of felonious homicide, that malice was 
presumed if the killing was intentional, and that the 
severity of the sentence could be reduced if — but only if - 
the defendant proved an absence of malice. Id. at 687-88. 
In habeas corpus proceedings, the lower federal courts 
concluded that murder and manslaughter were distinct 


30 


offenses and that, under In re Winship, 397 U.S. 358 (1970), 
the Due Process Clause required the prosecution to prove 
malice in order to obtain a conviction for the greater 
offense. This Court preliminarily rejected the lower fed- 
eral courts’ analytical approach, reminding them that 
they were bound by the state court’s definition of the 
offense. For purposes of the federal question, however, 
the Court explained that the state court’s single-offense 
characterization was not dispositive: 


Winship is concerned with substance rather than 
this kind of formalism. The rationale of that case 
requires an analysis that looks to the “operation 
and effect of the law as applied and enforced by 
the State,” St. Louis S.W.R. Co. v. Arkansas, 235 
U.S. 350, 362 (1914). 


Wilbur, 421 U.S. at 699. The Court then held that because 
state law, in its actual operation, made malice an essential 
element of the single offense, Winship required the state 
to carry the burden of persuasion on that element. Id. at 
703-04.

considered whether the Due Process Clause was 
infringed when, in contempt proceedings for repeated 
failure to make child-support payments, the defendant 
had the burden of proving that he was financially unable 
to make the payments. Failing this burden, the defendant 
was sentenced to five days in jail for each count and 
ordered to repay his accumulated arrearages. The state 
court concluded that the proceedings were quasi-criminal 
in nature, that ability to pay was an element of the charge 
of contempt rather than an affirmative defense, and that 
California law unconstitutionally shifted the burden of

persuasion to the contemnor. This Court acknowledged

In Memphis Steam Laundry Cleaner, Inc. v. Stone, 342 
U.S. 389 (1952), the Court considered whether the

Commerce Clause was infringed by a privilege tax that 
Mississippi imposed on individuals soliciting orders for 
out-of-state laundries. The Mississippi Supreme Court 
had held that under state law, Memphis Steam Laundry’s 
truck drivers were “transient vendors or dealers” subject 
to the tax. It then characterized the levy as “a tax on a 
person soliciting business for a laundry not licensed in 
this state, a local activity which applies to residents and 
~nonresidents alike.” Id. at 391. Based on this characteriza- 
tion, it concluded that the application of the tax to Mem- 
phis’s drivers did not offend the Commerce Clause. Id. 
For purposes of deciding the federal question, however, 
this Court looked to the consequences of the tax, not the 
state court’s characterization of it. “The State may deter- 
mine for itself the operating incidence of its tax. But it is 
for this Court to determine whether the tax, as construed 
by the highest court of the State, is or is not ‘a tax on 
interstate commerce.’” Id. at 392. Concluding that the 
effect of the levy was to tax the privilege of doing inter- 
state business, the Court held that it violated the Com- 
merce Clause. Id. at 393-94. 


In Railway Express Agency, Inc. v. Virginia, 347 U.S. 359 
(1954), another tax case, the Court considered whether a 
tax that was measured by gross receipts violated the

property tax imposed on the difference between the

did not violate the federal Constitution. Id. at 363. This

While great respect is due these conclusions, it 
has long been held that . . . “neither the state 
courts nor the legislatures, by giving the tax a 
particular name or by the use of some form of 
words, can take away our duty to consider its 
nature and effect,” Galveston, H. & S.A.R. Co. v. 
State of Texas, 210 U.S. 217, 227 (1908), in which

In Henderson v. Mayor of New York, 92 U.S. (2 Otto) 259 
(1875), the Court considered whether the Commerce 
Clause was infringed by a New York statute that required 
the owner of every ship to give a $300 bond, with a 
covenant for four years, for each new immigrant who 
disembarked in New York, but allowed the bond require- 
ment to be commuted if the owner paid $1.50 for each 
immigrant within 24 hours of arrival. The New York 
Court of Errors agreed with the state legislature that the 
bond was an indemnification against each new immi- 
grant’s need for public support for the four years after his 
arrival. It therefore held that the bond did not unduly 
burden foreign commerce. This Court explained that the 


34 


state court’s characterization was not dispositive of the 
federal question: 


In whatever language a statute may be framed, 
its purpose must be determined by its natural 
and reasonable effect; and if it is apparent that 
the object of this statute, as judged by that crite- 
rion, is to compel the owners of vessels to pay a 
sum of money for every passenger brought by 
them from a foreign shore, and landed at the 
Port of New York, it is . . . a tax on passengers if 
collected from them, or a tax on the vessel or 
owners for the exercise of the right of landing 
their passengers in that city. 


Id. at 268. Looking at the consequence of the statute, the 
Court first found it inevitable that the owner of a ship 
would not give a sizable bond for each of the thousands 
of passengers he transported to New York each year, but 
instead would pay $1.50 per head and would collect that 
amount from each passenger before embarkation. Id. Not- 
withstanding the state’s characterization of its own law, 
the Court then concluded that the function of the statute 
was not to protect against the future consequences of 
pauperism, because the bond was as much required for 
wealthy and hard-working immigrants as it was for dis- 
eased persons or paupers who might quickly become 
objects of charity. Id. at 268-69. Because the state bond, in 
operation, charged foreign passengers for the right to 
make the voyage, it violated federal law. Id. at 274-75. 


In Collins v. New Hampshire, 171 U.S. 30 (1898), the 
Court considered whether the Commerce Clause was 
infringed by a New Hampshire statute that prohibited the 
sale of oleomargarine as a substitute for butter unless it 
was colored pink. This Court had previously held in 


35 


Schollenberger v. Pennsylvania, 171 U.S. 1 (1898), that a 
state statute altogether prohibiting the introduction of 
oleomargarine violated the Commerce Clause. Upholding 
Collins’s criminal conviction for selling oleomargarine 
that was not the proper color, the New Hampshire 
Supreme Court characterized its statute as one that 
merely prescribed the addition of a harmless coloring 
agent. The state court explained that the statute did not 
unduly burden interstate commerce because the manufac- 
turer could sell his product freely and at any price. This 
Court disagreed: 


The direct and necessary result of a statute must 
be taken into consideration when deciding as to 
its validity, even if that result is not in so many 
words either enacted or distinctly provided for. 
In whatever language a statute may be framed, 
its purpose must be determined by its natural 
and reasonable effect. 


Collins, 171 U.S. at 33-34. For purposes of the Commerce

the coloration requirement was to make the oleo- 
margarine repugnant to customers to the point where 
they would not purchase it at any price. Id. Because this 
condition, if complied with, would effectually prevent 
any sale, the statute, in actual operation, suffered from 
the same federal constitutional vice as the statute in 
Schollenberger.

3. The Rule in Simmons Would Apply to Ramdass 
Even If Jonathan Simmons Had Been Techni- 
cally Ineligible for Parole 


Ramdass’s entitlement to relief does not rest solely 
on the fact that, for all material purposes, his case is a 
clone of Simmons’s case. Even if Jonathan Dale Simmons 
had been declared legally and technically ineligible for 
parole before the date of his capital sentencing trial, as 
the Virginia Supreme Court assumed, Ramdass would 
come fully within the core rule in Simmons.

The holding in Simmons, expressed in the plurality 
opinion, is as follows: 


We hold that where the defendant's future dan- 
gerousness is at issue, and state law prohibits” 
the defendant’s release on parole, due process 
requires that the sentencing jury be informed 
that the defendant is parole ineligible. 


Simmons, 512 U.S. at 156. Notably, the words “parole 
ineligib[ility]” appear here only in the description of the 
phrase to be given to the jury. They are not used in this 
Court's description of the condition precedent — a finding 
that “state law prohibits the defendant’s release on 
parole.” In Ramdass’s case, Virginia law, in its actual and 
foreseeable operation, “prohibit[ed] the defendant’s 
release on parole.” From the date of his capital sentencing 
trial until the date of his death, there would never be a 
point where state law permitted him to be released on 
parole. That circumstance entitled Ramdass to inform the 
jury, in the language of the final clause of the Simmons 
holding, that he was “parole ineligible.” Parole inel- 
igibility in this context flowed from the fact that state law 
prohibited the defendant’s release on parole, not from a 
technical and formalistic determination of parole eligi- 
bility under state law. 


The careful wording of the Simmons holding is con- 
sistent with the Court’s due process concern, expressed in 
both the plurality and concurring opinions, that jurors be 
permitted to make an informed and realistic assessment

imprisonment. Id. at 171 (plurality opinion) (“The State

RULES OF SUPREME COURT OF VIRGINIA 
PART THREE A Criminal Practice and Procedure 


RULE 3A:15. Motion to Strike or to Set Aside Verdict; 
Judgment of Acquittal or New Trial. 


(a) Motion to Strike Evidence. - After the Common- 
wealth has rested its case or at the conclusion of all the 
evidence, the court on motion of the accused may strike 
the Commonwealth's evidence if the evidence is insuffi- 
cient as a matter of law to sustain a conviction. If the 
court overrules a motion to strike the evidence and there 
is a hung jury, the accused may renew the motion within 
the time specified in Rule 1:11 and the court may take the 
action authorized by the Rule. 


(b) Motion to Set Aside Verdict. — If the jury returns 
a verdict of guilty, the court may, on motion of the 
accused made not later than 21 days after entry of a final 
order, set aside the verdict for error committed during the 
trial or if the evidence is insufficient as a matter of law to 
sustain a conviction. 


(c) Judgment of Acquittal or New Trial. — The court 
shall enter a judgment of acquittal if it strikes the evi- 
dence or sets aside the verdict because the evidence is 
insufficient as a matter of law to sustain a conviction. The 
court shall grant a new trial if it sets aside the verdict for 
any other reason.
