Simmons v. South Carolina holds that when a prosecutor seeks the 
death sentence on the ground of the defendant’s future dangerousness, 
the defendant has a constitutional right to inform the jurors truthfully 
that if they spare his life, state law forbids him ever to be released from 
prison. Does the rule in Simmons turn on the actual operation of state 
law, or on its hypertechnical terms; and must a federal habeas court 
adjudicating a Simmons claim make its own analysis of the functional 
consequences of state law, or is it bound by the state courts’ 
characterization of state law for federal constitutional purposes? 


Ake v. Oklahoma holds that an indigent capital defendant has a right to 
court assistance in obtaining the services of a mental health expert as a 
defense consultant and possible expert witness when the prosecutor 
puts the defendant’s mental condition in issue in the course of seeking 
a death sentence on the ground of the defendant’s future 
dangerousness. Is the Ake rule limited to cases in which the prosecutor 
presents expert testimony going to future dangerousness, or does it 
apply whenever the defendant’s mental condition is to be a significant 
factor at sentencing? 


In addition to rejecting petitioner’s Simmons and Ake claims on the 
merits in an opinion presenting the two Questions set out above, the 
Court of Appeals held alternatively that 28 U.S.C. § 2254(d)(1) 
required it to accept the state-court resolution of those claims (and that 
Teague required it to accept the state-court rejection of petitioner’s 
Simmons claim) without independent federal adjudication. These 
alternative grounds invoked the same Fourth Circuit construction of 
§ 2254(d)(1) that this Court is reviewing on certiorari in Williams 
(Terry) v. Taylor. Is that construction erroneous?

STATEMENT OF THE CASE 
For unknown reasons, Bobby Lee Ramdass went on a sudden crime spree in the summer of 


1992. As relevant to this petition, his offenses included robbery at a Pizza Hut in Fairfax County, 
Virginia, on August 25, 1992; robbery at a Domino’s Pizza in Alexandria, Virginia, on August 30, 
1992; and robbery at a 7-Eleven in Fairfax County on September 2, 1992. During the 7-Eleven 
robbery, Ramdass shot and killed Mohammed Kayani, a store employee. 

Ramdass was quickly apprehended, and the various charges against him came on for trial in 
rapid succession. A jury found Ramdass guilty of the Pizza Hut robbery on December 15, 1992, and 
the trial court entered judgment on January 22, 1993. Another jury found him guilty of the Domino’s 
Pizza robbery on January 7, 1993, and the trial court entered judgment on February 18, 1993. A third 
jury found him guilty of capital murder for the death of Mohammed Kayani on January 28, 1993, and 
it fixed his sentence at death on January 30, 1993. The sole aggravating factor establishing Ramdass’ 
eligibility for death in the capital case was the jury’s finding that he likely “would commit criminal on 
of violence [in the future] that would constitute a continuing serious threat to society.” Virginia Code 
§ 19.2-264.4(C). 

This petition concerns two ways in which Ramdass was prevented from rebutting the evidence 


of future dangerousness and presenting the jury with reasons to impose a sentence less than death.

B. Federal Collateral Proceedings 

After exhausting state remedies, Ramdass filed a federal petition for writ of habeas corpus.’ 
As his first issue, Ramdass alleged that the trial court violated his rights under the Eighth and 
Fourteenth Amendments when it refused to provide the jurors with truthful information that if they 
sentenced the defendant to life imprisonment, there was no possibility of release on parole. Ramdass 
pointed to specific language in Simmons stating that the rule in that case applies when state law 
prohibits the possibility of release on parole, and he contended that the rule is not limited to cases 
where the defendant is technically ineligible for parole. The difference between these two 
formulations is subtle but important. In Ramdass’ view, Simmons directs courts to employ a 
pragmatic, functional test of the possibility of release on parole; it does not demand the use of a 
technical, legalistic test. 

The magistrate judge recommended that the writ be granted on the Simmons issue because 


the state court’s hypertechnical decision was “contrary to clearly established Federal law, as

A divided panel of the Fourth Circuit reversed. The majority held that Simmons applies only 


in the narrow circumstances where a defendant is technically and legally ineligible for parole on the 


date of the capital sentencing proceeding. The majority essentially advanced three reasons. First, it 
announced that Simmons turns solely on the state court’s determination of its own law, and that this 
determination is dispositive: 

[T]he fact that a defendant will have no possibility of parole if given a life sentence 

can only stem from the legal conclusion that state law bars eligibility for parole. 

Because parole eligibility is entirely a creature of state law, Ramdass’ conception of 

practical or functional parole eligibility must inevitably collapse into a determination 

of state law. 

App. 11 (emphasis omitted). 

Second, the majority said that conditioning the application of Simmons on a functional 
determination that the defendant will never be eligible for release on parole “is simply incompatible 
with the logic of Simmons itself.” App. 10. According to the majority, this logic is reflected by 
statements in Justice O’Connor’s concurrence such as, “We hold that where the defendant’s future 
dangerousness is at issue, and state law prohibits the defendant’s release on parole, due process 
requires that the sentencing jury be informed that the defendant is parole ineligible.” App. 10 
(quoting Simmons, 512 U.S. at 165) (Fourth Circuit emphasis omitted). 

Third, the panel majority held in the alternative that relief could not be granted under AEDPA. 
“Even were we persuaded by Ramdass’ theory that practical rather than legal parole ineligibility 
suffices to trigger Simmons, we would conclude that was not ‘clearly established’ as required by 28 
US.C. § 2254(d)(1).” App. 11. After discussing the similarities between Section 2254(d)(1) and . 
the antiretroactivity doctrine in Teague v. Lane, 489 U.S. 288 (1989), the majority added that 
Ramdass’ claim would fail “either under Teague or under § 2254(d)(1).” App. 11-12. 

Judge Murnaghan dissented on each of these points. First, he emphasized that “the scope and 


parameter of the Simmons right is a matter of federal constitutional law.” App. 18. “Admittedly, the 


[due process] right recognized in Simmons . . . only applies to those for whom state law has 
eliminated the possibility of parole. But, the right is a federal right.” App. 18. He explained that 
federal due process was denied because only a hypothetical future development of the sort rejected 
by Simmons could make Ramdass eligible for release, and he berated the majority for “hid[ing] . . . 
behind a state law shield.” App. 20. 

[A]t the time of the Kayani sentencing, only some hypothetical future development 

as remote as legislative reform, commutation, or clemency, could have affected entry 

of the Domino’s Pizza robbery conviction and therefore prevented Ramdass from 

being parole ineligible... . Formal entry of the conviction at that point was merely 

a ministerial act. Thus, for all realistic intents and purposes, it was a guarantee that 


Ramdass would be parole ineligible [under state law] when the Kayani conviction was 
formally entered. 


App. 20.‘

genesis of Simmons [as] merely an extension of the rule in Gardner v. Florida, 430 U.S. 349 (1977), 


and Skipper v. South Carolina, 476 U.S. 1 (1986),” and with the specific language in the Simmons

A. Questions Concerning the Nature or Duration of Restraints on Liberty Require 
a Functional, Rather Than a Technical, Mode of Analysis 


There has never been disagreement as to the practical reality of Ramdass’ situation: If he was 
sentenced to life imprisonment, he was never going to have the opportunity to be considered for 
release on parole. No state or federal court judge has questioned that immutable fact. Nonetheless, 
because a ministerial requirement of state law had not yet been satisfied, the Virginia Supreme Court 
employed, and the Fourth Circuit majority ratified, a reading of Simmons that gave effect to technical 
parole eligibility and disregarded the functional reality of parole ineligibility. The Court should grant 
certiorari to decide whether such questions of restraints on liberty should be analyzed using a 
technical or a functional mode of analysis. 

Whether application of Simmons turns on a “technical” or a “functional” analysis of eligibility 
for release from prison is a subspecies of a commonplace problem that has drawn this Court’s 
repeated attention. That problem is the need to define category boundaries when a rule of federal 
constitutional or statutory law refers to a condition or status that is both a practical fact and a legal 
term of art. In closely analogous areas of the law that involve constitutional questions concerning 
the nature or duration of restraints on individual liberty, this Court has resolved the problem by 
rejecting solutions that “place[] form over substance, and labels over reality.” Bowen v. Gilliard, 483 
U.S. 587, 606 (1987). 


In applying the requirement under Miranda v. Arizona, 384 U.S. 436 (1966), that warnings 


17 


must precede police interrogation when a person is “in custody,” for example, this Court has asked 
whether the determination of “custody” turns on a purely ‘echnical analysis of the legal rules 
governing police powers of arrest and detention, or if it turns on a functional analysis of whether the 
circumstances reasonably would lead the person being interrogated to believe that he or she is not free 
to leave the stationhouse at will. The answer is that it turns on a functional analysis. See, e.g., 
Stansbury v. California, 511 U.S. 318, 322 (1994) (“In determining whether an individual was in 
custody, a court must examine all of the circumstances surrounding the interrogation”); Oregon v. 
Mathiason, 429 U.S. 492, 495 (1977) (per curiam) (officer’s obligation to administer Miranda 
warnings attaches “only where there has been such a restriction on a person’s freedom as to render 
him ‘in custody”). 

In determining whether an individual is “in custody” for purposes of federal habeas corpus 
jurisdiction, the Court similarly has applied a functional rather than technical test. In Jones v. 
Cunningham, 371 U.S. 236 (1963), for example, the Court held that a state prisoner who has been 
released on parole is nonetheless functionally “in custody” because he or she is still subject to various 
conditions and restrictions.

Ramdass does not seek an “extension of Simmons.” His claim is based squarely on the core

predicated on an interpretation or application of relevant precedent that reasonable jurists would ail

D. The Simmons Court Itself Used a Functional Analysis, and the Facts 

Demonstrating This Also Show That Ramdass’ Case Is Indistinguishable from 
Simmons’ Case on Both the Facts and the Law 

Both the plurality and concurring opinions in Simmons are based on a functional, practical 
analysis that eschews state-law technicalities in favor of a realistic view of the probability of future 
release. Confronted with an argument from South Carolina that Simmons was not technically eligible 
for parole and that he could, in any event, be released by means other than parole, the plurality 
dismissed the state’s argument as having “little force.” Simmons, 512 U.S. at 166. 

The Virginia Supreme Court and the Fourth Circuit appear to have presumed that Jonathan 
Simmons was technically ineligible for parole, and that this factual distinction disentitled Ramdass to 
benefit from the rule in Simmons. In truth, however, Simmons had never been declared eligible for 
parole, and he was only functionally ineligible for parole at the time of his trial. Thus, the facts which 
demonstrate the Court’s use of a functional analysis also demonstrate that Ramdass’ case is 
indistinguishable from Simmons’ case on the facts and the law. 

At the time of Simmons’ trial, firmly established South Carolina law vested parole eligibility 
decisions solely in the Probation, Parole and Pardon Board. State v. McKay, 386 S.E.2d 623 (S.C. 
1989). Based on this authority, South Carolina represented to this Court that: 

at the time of the trial, no state agency had ever determined that Simmons was going

vt WHETHER THERE IS A DUE PROCESS RIGHT TO THE ASSISTANCE OF A 
PSYCHIATRIST IN THE SENTENCING PHASE OF A CAPITAL CASE FOR 
PURPOSES OTHER THAN REBUTTING PSYCHIATRIC EVIDENCE OF FUTURE 
DANGEROUSNESS IS A QUESTION OF NATIONAL IMPORTANCE ON WHICH 
THE COURTS OF APPEALS ARE SPLIT 
This Court has repeatedly held that the Fourteenth Amendment requires states to provide 

indigent criminal defendants with the “basic tools of an adequate defense or appeal, when those tools 

are available for a price to other prisoners.” Britt v. North Carolina, 404 U.S. 226, 227 (1971). See, 

e.g., Griffin v. Illinois, 351 U.S. 12 (1956) (right to trial transcript for appeal); Burns v. Ohio, 360 

U.S. 252 (1959) (right to appeal without paying filing fee); Gideon v. Wainwright, 372 U.S. 335 

(1963) (right to assistance of counsel at trial); Douglas v. California, 372 U.S. 353 (1963) (right to 

assistance of counsel on first aed as of right); see also Little v. Streater, 452 U.S. 1 (1981) (right 

in quasi-criminal proceeding to blood grouping tests). 

In Ake v. Oklahoma, 470 U.S. 68 (1985), the Court affirmed that a psychiatrist can be a “basic 
tool” within the meaning of the Fourteenth Amendment. It held that the state must assure access to 
a competent psychiatrist when an indigent defendant “demonstrates that his sanity at the time of the 
offense is to be a significant factor at trial, .. . [or] when the State presents psychiatric evidence of 
the defendant’s future dangerousness” at a capital sentencing proceeding. /d. at 83. Although this 
holding nominally reflects the particular facts and circumstances presented in Ake, the Court also 
made quite clear the breadth of its ruling by setting out a three-part balancing test for determining 
whether any “tool” is “important enough to preparation of a defense to require the State to provide” 
that assistance: “The first [inquiry] is the private interest that will be affected by the action of the 
State. The second is the governmental interest that will be affected if the safeguard is to be provided. 
The third is the probable value of the additional or substitute procedural safeguards that are sought, 


30 


and the risk of an erroneous deprivation of the affected interest if those safeguards are not provided.” 
Ake, 470 U.S. at 77. 

As shown below, most federal habeas courts to address the issue have concheded that Ake is 
not limited to its facts. The Fourth Circuit, however, as it did in Williams (Terry) v. Taylor, held here 
that 28 U.S.C. § 2254(d)(1) permits the grant of habeas corpus relief for a claim based on Ake only 
where the petition presents the identical facts and circumstances that were present in Ake. In the 
penalty phase, according to the Fourth Circuit, that means that there is a constitutional right to a 
mental health expert under Ake only when the prosecution has introduced psychiatric evidence of 
future dangerousness. App.14. In that court’s view, there is no “clearly established Federal law, as 
determined by the Supreme Court of the United States,” which secngniass an indigent’s right to the 
assistance of a psychiatrist in anything other this precise factual setting. Section 2254(d)(1). The 
Court should grant certiorari to clarify the well-grounded rule in Ake and to address the special and 
important question of the degree of factual identity required by Section 2254(d)(1).

and, on a national scale, to clarify its core hold in Ake. 


B. The Fourth Circuit Failed To Consider the Substance or the Function of the 
Balancing Test That Is a Key Component of the Rule in Ake 


The Fourth Circuit’s limited and literal reading of Ake wholly ignores the portion of this 
Court’s opinion which sets out the three-part balancing test for determining whether a particular form 
of assistance is a “basic tools) of an adequate defense.” Ake, 470 US. at 77. That test is an essential 
part of this Court’s opinion. By including this test, the Court reasonably signaled that it was 
. announcing “a rule designed for the specific purpose of evaluating a myriad of factual contexts,” 
Wright v. West, SOS U.S. 277, 309 (1992) (Kennedy, J., concurring in the judgment). Announcing 


a rule for decision-making, rather than merely rendering a ruling about a defendant’s access to one 


34 


specific kind of expert under very specific circumstances, is consistent with the Court’s important, 
overarching role in the judicial system and the Court’s limited resources. When a rule is designed to 
evaluate different factual contexts, “it will be the infrequent case that yields a result so novel that it 
forges a new rule, one not dictated by precedent.” Jd. 

Under the three-part balancing test, a defendant’s interest in presenting psychiatric testimony 
to rebut future dangerousness is compelling, regardless whether the state’s evidence of future 
dangerousness is psychiatric or nonpsychiatric. The defendant’s interest in presenting the jury with 
truthful information which supports a sentence less than death is equally compelling. No state interest 
weighs against this right except perhaps its economy — an interest this Court squarely rejected in 
Ake. The re value of the psychiatric assistance, and the risk of error if such assistance is not 
offered, also weigh in Ramdass’ favor. Psychiatric evidence can show that the defendant is not a 
future danger, even when the prosecution’s case for future dangerousness is not based on psychiatric 
testimony. It also can show dramatically why the ultimate sentence is not appropriate in a particular 
case. That is particularly true when half of the state’s statutory mitigating factors relate to mental 
condition. See Virginia Code § 19.2-264.3:1."”
