QUESTIONS PRESENTED 


In a case where it is undisputed that the petitioner would have been eligible 
for parole on a life sentence as a matter of state law at the time the jury 
sentenced him to death, and where the petitioner does not contest the fact that 
Simmons v. South Carolina does not apply unless the prisoner was ineligible 
for parole, should certiorari be granted to decide whether the Fourth Circuit 
erred in ruling that it was not free to second-guess the Virginia Supreme 
Court’s determination of a matter of state law? 


In a case where it is undisputed that the trial court appointed an independent 
mental health expert to assist the petitioner during the penalty phase of trial 
and where it is equally undisputed that, as a matter of Virginia statutory law, 
every capital murder defendant has an absolute right to such appointment 
regardless of whether the prosecutor intends to prove future dangerousness 
or to present expert evidence on that issue, should certiorari be granted to 
resolve a so-called conflict in the federal courts concerning the application of 
Ake v. Oklahoma to a situation that did not occur in Ramdass’ case and 
cannot occur in Virginia? 


Should this Court grant the application for a stay of execution simply to 
“hold” Ramdass’ case for the decision in Terry Williams v. Taylor regarding 
the Fourth Circuit’s interpretation of 28 U.S.C. § 2254 (d) even though, by 
Ramdass’ own admission, the Fourth Circuit's application of § 2254 (d) to the 
Simmons and Ake claims was merely an alternative ruling?

STATEMENT OF THE CASE 

On January 28, 1993, a jury in the Circuit Court of Fairfax County, Virginia, found Ramdass 
guilty of the capital murder of Mohammed Kayani during the commission of robbery and of the 
illegal use of a firearm. Ramdass pleaded guilty to a charge of robbery. After a separate sentencing 
proceeding, the jury found that Ramdass represented a continuing serious threat to society and 
sentenced him to death for the capital murder. See Va. Code § 19.2-264.4. The jury also sentenced 
him to four years in prison for the firearm offense. After conducting the sentencing hearing 
required by § 19.2-264.5 on April 6, 1993, the court imposed the jury’s sentences and also 
sentenced Ramdass to life in prison for the robbery. 

The convictions and sentences unanimously were affirmed by the Virginia Supreme Court 
on November 5, 1993. Ramdass v. Commonwealth, 437 S.E.2d 566 (Va. 1993). This Court 
subsequently granted certiorari and remanded the case for reconsideration in light of its 
intervening decision in Simmons v. South Carolina, 512 U.S. 154 (1994). Ramdass v. Virginia, 
512 U.S. 1217 (1994). On remand, the Virginia Supreme Court reaffirmed the trial court’s 
judgment. Ramdass v. Commonwealth, 450 S.E.2d 360 (Va. 1994). (JA 202).' This Court 
denied contiecari seview of the reaffirmance on April 24, 1995. Ramdass v. Virginia, 514 U.S. 
1085 (1995). 

On May 11, 1995, the trial court appointed counsel to represent Ramdass in a state habeas 
corpus proceeding. (JA 217). Pursuant to Ramdass’ request, a second lawyer was appointed and 
Ramdass filed a petition for a writ of habeas corpus in the Virginia Supreme Court on August 30,

petition for rehearing was denied on June 7, 1996. (JA 303). 

On June 28, 1996, the trial court scheduled Ramdass’ execution for August 21, 1996. (JA 
304). On August 15, 1996, the United States District Court for the Eastern District of Virginia in

Ramdass filed a § 2254 petition on February 14, 1997. On February 27, 1998, a

court granted relief on the Simmons claim and “remanded” the case to the state trial court for 
resentencing. (JA 869). Ramdass v. Angelone, 28 F. Supp.2d 343 (E.D. Va. 1998). 

The respondent appealed to the United States Court of Appeals for the Fourth Circuit. 
On August 3, 1999, a three-judge panel of the Fourth Circuit reversed the district court. 
Ramdass v. Angelone, 187 F.2d 396 (4th Cir. 1999). One member of the panel, Judge

STATEMENT OF THE FACTS 
1. The Crimes 


It is undisputed that Ramdass is the person who murdered Mohammed Kayani by shooting 
him in the head at point-blank range. The Virginia Supreme Court found the facts surrounding 
Ramdass’ capital offense to be as follows: 


During the night of September | and early morning of September 2, 
1992, Ramdass and Darrell Wilson, both armed with pistols, were 
returning home in a car with three other men, Shane Singh, Edward 
O'Connor, and Candelerio Ramirez, after abandoning a plan to rob 
persons at a Roy Rogers restaurant in Fairfax County. On the way, 
Ramdass suggested that they Jb persons at a 7-Eleven store on 
Buelah Street in Fairfax County. 


Accordingly, near one o'clock on the morning of September 2, the 
five men entered the 7-Fleven store. Ramdass entered first and 
“drew” his pistol on Kayani, a 7-Eleven clerk who was behind the 
cash register. Wilson, who also displayed his pistol, ordered all the 
customers to lie on the floor and not look at him. The other three 
men, who were unarmed, took the customers’ wallets, money from 
the cash register, and cigarettes and lottery tickets from the store’s 
stock. 


After Ramdass ordered Kayani to open the safe, Kayani knelt down 
next to the safe and unsuccessfully tried to open it. Raimdass 
squatted next to Kayani and yelled at him to open the safe “or I'll 
blow your f. head off.” Wilson fired his pistol at one of the 
customers on the floor. Immediately thereafter, Singh, standing 
behind Ramdass, saw Ramdass shoot Kayani in the head on his 
second attempt to get the weapon to fire. 


Just after Ramdass shot Kayani, Ramirez returned from a back room 
in the store. Ramirez saw Ramdass laughing as he stood over 
Kayani’s body. Later, Ramirez heard Ramdass say that he shot 
Kayani because he “took too long.” Shortly thereafter, Ramirez 
opened the front door, and Wilson, Singh, and O’Connor ran out. As 


Ramirez held the door open, he urged Ramdass to “[c]ome on.” 
However, Ramdass was “clicking the gun at the people on the floor” 
and told Ramirez to “[s}hut up or I'll put one in you.” One of the 
customers also heard the clicking of the gun as Ramdass left. 


When they got in the car, Ramirez heard Ramdass ask Wilson, “Why 
didn’t you get rid of the people on the floor?” After the men divided 
the robbery proceeds at Singh’s home, Ramdass told Ramirez, 
“Don’t tell anybody about this [or] Ill kill you and I'll kill your 
whole family.” 


Singh, a co-owner of the gun with Ramdass, testified that the gun 
would not fire unless held at a certain angle because the “bullets” in 
the chamber were not the right size for the gun. Julian Jay Mason, 
Jr., a forensic scientist specializing in firearms identification, later 
examined and test fired the gun. Mason testified that the 9 
millimeter cartridges Ramdass used in the gun were smaller than the 
38 caliber cartridges specified for the gun. Therefore, when the 
gun’s muzzle was pointed down, the 9 millimeter cartridges slid too 
far forward to be struck by the firing pin. Mason further testified that 
when the muzzle was pointed up, the cartridge slid back closer to the 
firing pin, and the gun could be fired. 


Ramdass, 437 S.E.2d at 568-569. See 28 U.S.C. § 2254(e)(1) (state court findings of fact must be 
presumed correct and presumption may be overcome only by clear and convincing evidence); 
Sumner v. Mata, 449 U.S. 539, 541-549 (1981) (statutory presumption of correctness applies to 
state appellate court’s rendition of historical facts). 

With respect to the overwhelming evidence warranting the death penalty, the Virginia 


Supreme Court found as follows: 


The record in this case shows that Ramdass, who was born October 
19, 1971, has been involved in criminal activity since he was 14 
years old. 


His first violent offense was that of “purse snatch[ing]” from an 
elderly woman in 1985. During the time Ramdass was on probation 
for that offense, he began to sell illegal drugs. Soon after, he was 
involved in the unauthorized use of a moped and in the statutory 
burglary of a dwelling with the intent to commit larceny. As a result, 
Ramdass was committed to state juvenile facilities in January of 
1986 and was paroled in September of that year. Within three 


months of that release, Ramdass had violated his parole and was 
recommitted. He remained in custody until November of 1987, 
when he was released for a 30-day home visit. Ramdass was 
classified as an escapee when he failed to return to the state juvenile 
facility at the end of his home visit. 


On February 20, 1988, Ramdass robbed Frederick Thompson. When 
the police arrested Ramdass for that offense, he was sleeping with a 
loaded revolver on his bedside table. Ramdass, treated as an adult 
for that offense, pled guilty and was sentenced to a term of seven 
years in the penitentiary. Ramdass was released on mandatory 
parole in May of 1992. 


Within three months of that release, while still on parole, Ramdass 
again acquired a gun. Shortly thereafter, he committed six armed 
robberies, some accompanied by physical violence to the victims. 


The first two robberies occurred on August 25, 1992, when Ramdass 
abducted Robina Khan, robbed Mark Hasfiz, and robbed and struck 
Richard Imlow in a Pizza Hut in Fairfax County. Ramdass’s third 
robbery was that of Christopher Allen Crissel, a clerk at Bragg 
Towers, an apartment-hotel in Alexandria, on August 29. Crissel 
testified that Ramdass struck Crissel in the jaw “as hard as he could, 
with a lot of impact [and] said “Give me the f money or I'll 
blow your head off.’” After Crissel gave Ramdass over $500 from a 
desk drawer, Ramdass hit the back of Crissel’s head with his gun “as 
hard as he could.” 


Ramdass committed his fourth and fifth robberies on the same day. 
On August 30 at about 6:00 p.m., Ramdass robbed Emmanuel 
Gilbert Selassie and shot him in the head at close range. Later that 
evening, he robbed a clerk at a Domino’s Pizza in Arlington. Then, 
on September 2, in the present case, Ramdass robbed Kayani in 
Ramdass’s sixth robbery in eight days: he also shot Kayani in the 
head at close range and caused his death. 


Finally, our review of the record discloses nothing to suggest that 
Ramdass’s death sentence was imposed under the influence of 
passion, prejudice, or any other arbitrary factor. 


Ramdass, 437 S.E.2d at 574. 


Il. The Simmons Claim 


The Fourth Circuit recounted the relevant facts as follows: 


At the sentencing phase of trial, the Commonwealth of Virginia 
sought the death penalty, arguing that Ramdass presented "a 
continuing serious threat to society" -- the "future dangerousness" 
predicate for imposition of the death penalty. See Va. ‘Code Ann. 
19.2-264.2. In support of this argument, the Commonwealth 
presented evidence of Ramdass’ history of theft-related crimes 
beginning at age 14 and his pattern of recidivism during periods of 
escape or probation. More specifically, the prosecution detailed 
how, within three months of his release on mandatory parole after 
serving four years of a seven-year sentence for robbery, Ramdass 
committed a series of at least six armed robberies. The first two 
robberies occurred on August 25, 1992, when Ramdass robbed a 
Pizza Hut in Fairfax County, abducting a woman and hitting a 
man. Four days later, he robbed a clerk at an apartment-hotel in 
Alexandria and struck him in the head with a gun. On August 30, 
1992, he shot and robbed a cab driver. Later that evening, he 
robbed a clerk at a Domino's Pizza in Arlington. Finally, Ramdass 
killed Mohammed Kayani on September 2, 1992, during the sixth 
robbery in this eight day spree. See Ramdass 1, 437 S.E.2d at 574. 
Ramdass'’ counsel responded to the prosecution's argument by 
asserting that "Ramdass will never be out of jail. Your sentence 
today will insure that if he lives to be a hundred and twenty two, he 
will spend the rest of his life in prison." 


During sentencing deliberations, the jury asked: "if the 
Defendant is given life, is there a possibility of parole at some time 
before his natural death?" While recognizing that Virginia law did 
not permit a sentencing jury to be informed of the defendant's 
parole eligibility, Ramdass' counsel nevertheless maintained that 
“the [jurors'] question itself implies that they have a perception that 
if they give a life sentence that he will be out in a very short period 
of time” and that it was necessary to inform the jury otherwise with 
"some kind of language that would balance out that perception." 
Over defense counsel's objection, the trial judge told the jurors that 
they “should impose such punishment as [they] feel is just under 
the evidence and within the instructions of the Court" and that they 
“are not to concern themselves with what may happen afterwards." 
The jury returned a verdict recommending death on the capital 
murder count, based upon Ramdass’ "future dangerousness," and 
recommending four years imprisonment on the firearm count. 


At the sentencing hearing in April 1993, Ramdass’ counsel 
urged the court to impose a sentence of !ife in prison instead of 
death in light of Ramdass’ ineligibility parole under Virginia's 
three strikes provision. nl See Va. ~ode Ann. 53.1-151(B1). 
Defense counsel proffered to the court that three jurors had told 
him that they would have imposed a life sentence rather than death 
if they had known that Ramdass would not be eligible for parole. 
Rejecting defense counsel's request, the court sentenced Ramdass 
to death. 


nl Ramdass'’ parole eligibility at the time of the 
sentencing hearing was governed by Virginia's 
three-strikes statute, which provides that an 
individual is ineligible for parole if he has been 
"convicted of three separate felony offenses" of 
murder, rape, or armed robbery, which "were not 
part of a common act, transaction or scheme." Va. 
Code Ann. 53.1-151(B1). At the time the jury was 
deliberating his sentence in this case, Ramdass had 
already been sentenced (the week before) to 76 
years imprisonment in connection with the Pizza 
Hut armed robbery. In addition, about three weeks 
earlier, on January 7, 1993, a jury had returned a 
verdict finding Ramdass guilty in the Domino's 
Pizza armed robbery, for which it recommended an 
18-year sentence. The court, however, had not yet 
entered judgment in that case. It did so nearly three 
weeks after the jury in this case concluded its 
sentencing deliberations. 


Ramdass, 187 F.3d at 400. 

On appeal to the Virginia Supreme Court, Ramdass claimed, among other things, that error 
had occurred when the trial court gave the model jury instruction regarding parole eligibility. (JA 
167). The Virginia Supreme Court affirmed Ramdass’ sentence on November 5, 1993. On the 
parole eligibility issue, the Court ruled that, under long-standing precedent, the trial court 
correctly had answered the jury’s question regarding parole eligibility. Ramdass, 437 S.E.2d at 


573. 


Simmons vy. South Carolina was not argued in this Court until January 18, 1994. 
Simmons, 512 U.S. at 154. -Ramdass filed his certiorari petition in this Court in February, 1994, 
arguing, among other things, that the trial court had erred in its response to the jury’s question 
regarding parole. This Court decided Simmons on June 17, 1994. On June 20, 1994, the: Court 
granted Ramdass’ certiorari petition and remanded the case to the Virginia Supreme Court for 
reconsideration in light of Simmons. Ramdass v. Virginia, 512 U.S. 1217 (1994). 

On remand in the Virginia Supreme Court, Ramdass argued that Simmons required 
vacatur of his sentence. He alleged in conclusory fashion that he was ineligible for parole. (JA 
184-185). The Commonwealth argued in response that, at the time the jury was considering 
what sentence to recommend, Ramdass could not have been rendered parole-ineligible by a life 
sentence on the capital crime because he had at that time only one of the three necessary 
qualifying convictions under Virginia Code § 53.1-151(B1).* (JA 193-195). 

Ramdass made no reply to the Commonwealth’s argument, even though the Virginia 
Supreme Court’s briefing order expressly permitted him to do so. (JA 182). In its opinion 
reaffirming Ramdass’ sentence, the Virginia Supreme Court held as follows: 

In Simmons, the Supreme Court held that when “future 
dangerousness” is an issue in the sentencing phase of a capital 


murder case, the jury is entitled to information concerning the 
defendant’s parole ineligibility. Simmons, 512 U.S. at 114 S.Ct. 


— | 


* As the Fourth Circuit observed, § 53.1-151(B1) required that, before a convicted felon could 
be classified as parole-ineligible, he must have been convicted of three offenses of murder, rape 
or robbery with a deadly weapon, and further that the three or more offenses must not have 
occurred as part of a common act, transaction or scheme. At the time the capital jury was 
considering his sentence, Ramdass had been convicted of three robberies, but one involved no 
deadly weapon and the other two occurred during the same restaurant robbery and, thus, clearly 
were part of a common act, transaction or scheme. Ramdass also had been tried and found guilty 
of the Domino’s Pizza robbery in Alexandria, but the trial court in that case did not enter 
judgment on the jury’s verdict until after Ramdass’ capital jury had sentenced him. Thus, under 
Virginia law, that guilty verdict could not have been counted as a qualifying “conviction” under 
§ 53.1-151 at the time Ramdass’ capital jury inquired about his potential parole status. 


(JA 202-204). 


at 2198. Hence, Simmons applies only if Ramdass was ineligible for 
parole when the jury was considering his sentence. 


Code § 53.i-151(B) establishes a person's ineligibility for parole as 
follows: 


Any person convicted of three separate felony 
offenses of (i) murder, (ii) rape or (iii) robbery by the 
presenting of firearms or other deadly weapon, or any 
combination of the offenses specified in subdivisions 
(i), (ii) or (iii) when such offenses were not part of a 
common act, transaction or scheme shall not be 
eligible for parole. 


At the time that the jury was considering Ramdass’ penalty on 
January 30, 1993, Ramdass was not ineligible for parole since he had 
only two “separate felony offenses” within the meaning of Code § 
53.1-151(B)(1). They were: 


(1) Ramdass’ January 22, 1993, convictions and sentences for the 
armed robbery of two persons on August 25, 1992, at a Pizza Hut in 
Fairfax County. Since both convictions arose from the same 
transaction, they are considered as one felony offense under the 
express provisions of Code § 53.1-151(B)(1). 


(2) Ramdass’s convictions for Kayani’s capital murder and robbery 
arising from the same transaction and, thus, considered as one felony 
offense under Code § 53.1-151(B)(1). 


We do not agree with Ramdass’s contention that his January 7, 1993, 
conviction by a jury of an armed robbery in Alexandria also made 
him ineligible for parole under the statute. Judgment had not been 
entered on that verdict; therefore, it cannot be considered as a 
conviction under Code § 53.1-151(B)(1). See Smith v. 
Commonwealth, 134 Va. 589, 592-593, 113 S.E. 707, 709 (1922) 
(jury’s verdict of conviction upon which no judgment had been 
entered no conviction within meaning of statute disqualifying person 
from holding public office). Since Ramdass was not ineligible for 
parole when the jury was considering his sentence, the Simmons 
decision does not affect his death sentence. Therefore, we will 
reaffirm the trial court’s judgment, and reinstate the defendant’s 
conviction and sentence to death. 


Ramdass did not petition the Virginia Supreme Court for rehearing. He petitioned this 
Court for certiorari review, however, and argued for the first time that the Virginia Supreme Court 
improperly had found him eligible for parole under state law. (JA 207). This Court denied 
certiorari on April 24, 1995. Ramadass v. Virginia, 514 U.S. 1085 (1995). 

Ramdass repeated his generalized direct appeal Simmons claim during his state habeas 
corpus proceeding. He did not challenge the Virginia Supreme Court’s finding of parole 
eligibility. (JA 251-253). The Virginia Supreme Court refused to relitigate the matter under its 
rule in Hawks y. Cox, 175 S.E.2d 271 (Va. 1970). (JA 283, allegation C(13)). When Ramdass 
filed his § 2254 petition in the federal district court, however, he greatly expanded and changed 
the Simmons complaint that had been presented to the Virginia Supreme Court. He argued that: 
(1) under state law he allegedly was ineligible for parole; (2) the Virginia Supreme Court had no 
authority to determine parole eligibility; (3) the Virginia Supreme Court’s decision was 
internally inconsistent; (4) the Virginia Supreme Court ignored a decision of the Virginia Court 
of Appeals; (5) the Virginia Supreme Court’s decision was at odds with the parole statute; (6) 
even if the finding of eligibility was correct, the Simmons instruction still should have been 
given; (7) the refusal to give the instruction violated the Eighth Amendment; (8) he was entitled 
to the instruction as a matter of mitigating evidence; (9) the trial judge ignored Ramdass’ parole 
ineligibility in imposing the death sentence; and (10) the Virginia Supreme Court was required 
under Virginia Code § 17-110.1 to consider his parole ineligibility. (JA 315-344).

constitutional theory and then raising the same claim in federal court under a different

B. The claim is not controlled by Ake and 
thus is barred by the “new rule” doctrine. 
In Ake v. Oklahoma, the issue was whether and when a State had a due process 


obligation to provide an indigent capital defendant with the services of a mental health expert. 
Ake in no way dealt with the issue of whether there is a cognizable constitutional claim of 
“ineffective assistance” of mental health expert, the precise claim that Ramdass has raised in his 
federal petition. Indeed, Ake not only did not address the quality of the mental health expert’s 
performance, but it expressly held that a defendant has no right to choose a particular expert. 
See Ake, 470 U.S. at 83. Ake, therefore, simply does not govern Ramdass’ complaint. 

Thus, to the extent Ramdass relies on Ake for relief, his claim undoubtedly is barred by 
the “new rule” doctrine. This Court has refused to extend the Strickland-type inquiry regarding 
effective assistance of trial counsel to claims of ineffective assistance of habeas counsel. See 
Coleman v. Thompson, 501 U.S. 722, 752 (1991). The Court never has suggested that there 
exists an even more remote right to the “effective assistance” of a mental health expert. Clearly, 
such a novel question, if ever addressed, must be tackled on direct review. There is no existing 
authority compelling acceptance of such a claim. Thus the claim is barred from review in federal 
collateral proceedings by the “new rule” doctrine. See Gray, 518 U.S. at 169.

barred by the new rule doctrine in this collateral case because it is a paradigmatic example of an 
issue that is “susceptible to debate among reasonable minds.” See O’Dell, 521 U.S. at 159, 
quoting Butler v. McKellar, 494 U.S. 407, 415 (1990). Thus, if the issue is to be resolved, it 
must be decided on direct review and not in this collateral case. 

The most glaring problem with Ramdass’ “conflict” argument, however, and the reason it 
really has no effect whatsoever on Ramdass’ case, is twofold: first, Ramdass never even 
presented his Ake complaint about Dr. Samenow to the state court except by way of a claim of 
ineffective assistance of trial counsel. Ramdass’ default only highlights the unfairness to the 
state courts of considering a claim that was found barred by the federal district court and that 
only the federal court of appeals reached. 


Second, the “conflict” is over when a state court must appoint an expert, a completely 
moot point in Ramdass’, or any Virginia, case. Virginia Code § 19.2-264.3:1 guarantees to all 
capital defendants - simply for the asking - what the federal courts of appeals supposedly are in 
conflict over. Ramdass received the benefit of Virginia’s extra-constitutional provision. Thus, 
even if there is a conflict in the federal courts over when Ake requires appointment of an expert. 
the resolution of that conflict can have absolutely no impact in Ramdass’ case. He already 
received what Ake requires, under amy circuit's interpretation. 

Finally, the cases themselves do not demonstrate a conflict with Ramdass’ case. First, in 
Terry v. Rees, 985 F.2d 283 (6th Cir. 1993), the court of appeals held that Ake required the 
appointment of a pathologist, the denial of which appointment was found to be harmless error. 
Obviously, that decision is irrelevant to the issues in Ramdass’ case and, if anything, is in 
conflict with this Court's “new rule” doctrine. 

Second, of the four remaining cases, three were cases in which the state trial courts had 
denied the defendants’ motions for appointment of any independent mental health expert. See 
Moore v. Reynolds, 153 F.3d 1086, 1108-09 (10th Cir. 1998) (defendant made proper showing 
that mental state at issue but denial of expert was harmless), cert. denied. 119 S.Ct. 1266 (1999); 
Starr v. Lockhart, 23 F.3d 1280, 1290 n.8 (8th Cir.) (denial of independent expert in case error 
where defendant was retarded and where reasonable probability expert would aid defendant in 
sentencing), cert. denied, 513 U.S. 995 (1994); Smith v. McCormick, 914 F.2d 1153, 1157-59 
(9th Cir. 1990) (trial court’s own expert appointed for a competency exam did not fulfil Ake’s 
requirement of an independent expert to assist the defense with sentencing issues), cert. denied, 


504 U.S. 901 (1992). 


27 


The Tenth and Eighth Circuits in Moore and Starr do note in passing that, although Ake 
seems to limit the right to an expert to cases where the prosecutor uses one, they see no reason to 
so limit the right and thus will apply Ake whenever the defendant makes a showing that his 
mental state will be at issue. The Ninth Circuit in Smith simply does not address Ake’s holding 
that a defendant is entitled to an expert when the prosecutor will be using an expert to prove 
future dangerousness. The most that these cases prove, however, is that there is a “debate among 
reasonable minds” over what Ake requires: a debate that, as shown above, only demonstrates the 
claim’s “new rule” character and its irrelevance to Ramdass’ case where an expert was appointed 
at his request. 

In the last case, Clisby v. Jones, 960 F.2d 925, 928 (11th Cir. 1992), the in banc court of 
appeals reversed the district court’s granting of habeas relief on the basis of Ake. The court of 
appeals noted that it did not believe Ake should be restricted to cases where the prosecutor had 
used expert testimony, found that the appointed expert’s performance should be examined for 
competency and then found the expert competent. Id. As demonstrated above, such an 
extension of Ake represents a conflict not with Ramdass’ case, but with this Court’s “new rule” 
jurisprudence. (See above at subsection B).

Ill. Ramdass Is Not Entitled To A Stay Based Upon 
The Grant Of Certiorari In Williams vy. Taylor. 


Ramdass briefly argues that this Court should “hold” his case for the decision in Terry 


Williams v. Taylor, No. 98-8384. In Williams, the Court will decide whether the Fourth 


Circuit’s interpretation of the deference standard in § 2254 (d) was correct. As Ramdass 
candidly admits (Pet. Quest. 3), however, the Fourth Circuit’s recitation of § 2254 (d) in his case 
represents an alternative holding. 

For example, on the Simmons claim, the Fourth Circuit addressed the claim on its merits, 
found that Simmons did not apply because Ramdass was not ineligible for parole and then also 
held that a contrary ruling would run afoul of either § 2254 (d)’s “clearly established” provision 
or the “new rule” doctrine. Ramdass, 187 F.3d at 406-407. On the Ake claim, the Fourth Circuit 
held that the Virginia Supreme Court’s rejection of the claim was not an unreasonable 
application of “clearly established federal law” under § 2254 (d) because the Ake claim itself 
had no merit. \|d. at 409. Thus, the Fourth Circuit’s judgment does not ultimately rest upon its 
interpretation or application of § 2254 (d): the judgment rests upon a rejection of the merits of 


the claims themselves.
