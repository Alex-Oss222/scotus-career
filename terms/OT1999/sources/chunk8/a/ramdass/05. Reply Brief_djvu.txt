iL. Respondent Concedes a Split in the Circuits on the Meaning of Ake, and It 
Advances No Cogent Arguments As to Why Certiorari Should Be Not Be Granted. 


In response to Ramdass’ second question presented, referring to the Fourth Circuit’s 
narrow and literal reading of Ake v. Oklahoma, 470 U.S. 68 (1985), Respondent offers four 
arguments, none of which has merit. 

First, Respondent argues that Ramdass’ Ake claim is defaulted. The Fourth Circuit, 
which is well known for its zealous enforcement of procedural defaults, expressly held that 
Ramdass fairly presented his Ake claim in the state habeas petition. App. 14. Respondent now 
argues that the Fourth Circuit was wrong, and that the claim was actually defaulted because it 
was not exhausted in state court.* Respondent is wrong. As the Fourth Circuit correctly stated, 
the standard is whether the petitioner “fairly presented” his federal claim to the state court. /d ; 
see, Picard v. Connor, 404 U.S. 270, 277 (1971). If a habeas petitioner has fairly presented the 
state court with the “substance” of his federal claim, he has satisfied this standard. Vasquez v. 
Hillery, 474 U.S. 254, 258 (1986). 

Respondent does not dispute that Ramdass alleged in his state habeas petition that he 
“was denied the reasonable assistance of a mental health expert.” (JA 229). Nor does 
respondent dispute that Ramdass specifically cited and summarized the holding in Ake v. 
Oklahoma, 470 U.S. 68, 83 (1985) and the Eighth and Fourteenth Amendments as federal bases
