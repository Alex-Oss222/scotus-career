ARGUMENT 


AN ANONYMOUS TIP WHICH STATES THAT 
A PERSON IS CARRYING A CONCEALED 
FIREARM AT A SPECIFIC LOCATION, WITH 
A DETAILED DESCRIPTION OF THE PERSON 
AND HIS ATTIRE, IS SUFFICIENTLY 
RELIABLE TO JUSTIFY AN INVESTIGATORY 
DETENTION AND FRISK WHERE THE POLICE 
IMMEDIATELY VERIFY THE ACCURACY OF 
THE TIP. 


A. A Terry stop and frisk is justified by the 
totality of the circumstances, where an anonymous 
tip relates both innocent facts, verified 
immediately, and the carrying of a concealed 
firearm. 


Florida’s position, as clearly stated in its initial brief, is that the 
Fourth Amendment does not totally proscribe a Terry' stop and frisk 
based on an anonymous tip that an individual is carrying a concealed 
firearm where only the innocent details of the tip are immediately 
verified. Such a stop and frisk is lawful if, based on the totality of the 
circumstances, reasonable articulable suspicion exists that the 
individual is carrying a concealed firearm. The existence of 
reasonable suspicion is established based on the quantity and quality of 
the verified innocent details, including the age of the individual who 
is alleged to be carrying a concealed firearm. 


The Respondent, in its answer brief, totally misconstrues 
Florida’s position. According to Respondent, Florida’s position is that 
whenever an anonymous innocent details tip states that a firearm is 
involved, reasonable suspicion always exists to support a Terry stop 
and frisk. After erecting the strawman “firearm exception” position,

Respondent then advocates for a bright line rule that reasonable 
suspicion can never be established by an anonymous tip that an 
individual is carrying a concealed firearm where only the innocent 
details are verified. 


Florida’s position is in accord with the Court’s Fourth 
Amendment jurisprudence that holds that bright line rules are 
antithetical to the concept of reasonable suspicion. See Illinois v. 
Wardlow, 120 S.Ct. 673 (2000). In Wardlow, Illinois asked the Court 
to announce a bright line rule authorizing the temporary detention of 
anyone who flees at the mere sight of a police officer. Wardlow, on 
the other hand, asked the Court to adopt the opposite per se rule that 
the fact that an individual flees upon seeing the police can never, by 
itself, be sufficient to justify a Terry stop. The Court rejected both 
positions and held the flight from a police officer is just one 
circumstance in the totality of circumstances that determines the 
existence of reasonable suspicion. The Court then held that neither 
presence in a high crime area standing alone and nor flight from a 
police officer standing alone establishes reasonable suspicion. 
However when these two circumstances are present at the same time 
reasonable suspicion is established. 


Florida urges that reasonable suspicion is established based on 
the quantity and quality of the verified innocent details of an 
anonymous tip that an individual is carrying a concealed firearm. 
Therefore, the Court should quash the Supreme Court of Florida’s 
holding that such a tip can never provide the necessary reasonable 
suspicion to justify a Terry stop as a misapprehension of the law. 


B. The anonymous tip was sufficiently reliable to 
justify the investigatory detention and frisk. 


The totality of the circumstances herein establishes the 
requisite reasonable suspicion. The anonymous tip stated that three 
black youths were standing at a bus stop in front of a pawn shop. The 
three youths were described with particularity. Within six minutes of 
receiving the tip, Officer Anderson verified the tip’s innocent details.

Since empirical studies dealing with how many Florida 
juveniles carry concealed firearms do not exist, they can not be utilized 
in determining the propriety of Officer Anderson’s conduct. Rather 
based on the facts and the inferences drawn therefrom, the propriety 
of Officer Anderson’s conduct and thus the determination of reasonable 
suspicion herein must be based on commonsense judgments about 
human behavior. Officer Anderson’s determination that reasonable 
suspicion existed to stop and frisk Respondent requires the Court's 
approval, since after she verified that the particularly described youths 
were at the particularly described location, she utilized her 
commonsense judgment that in Florida it was more likely than not that 
the Respondent was carrying a concealed firearm in effectuating the 
stop. Officer Anderson’s use of commonsense judgment in ferreting 
out crime has an important place in the reasonable suspicion equation. 
Thus, its consideration herein, along with the objective facts as 
established by the innocent detail tip, establishes the necessary 
reasonable suspicion to support the instant stop and frisk.
