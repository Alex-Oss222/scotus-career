ABUSE OF THE WRIT 

A second petition may be dismissed for abuse of the writ if new grounds are alleged and the 
failure to allege them in the previous petition was an abuse of the writ. See Farmer v. McDaniel, 98 
F.3d 1548, 1555 (9th Cir.1996). The previous petition need not have been dismissed on the merits. 
See id. at 1557. The instant petition therefore may be subject to dismissal for abuse of the writ. 

In McCleskey v. Zant, 499 U.S. 467 (1991), the U.S. Supreme Court defined the respective 
burdens of pleading abuse of the writ. The State met that burden here by noting Slack’s previous writ 
history, identifying the grounds for relief that appear for the first time in the instant petition and 
alleging that Slack abused the writ. See id. The burden now is on Slack to disprove abuse of the writ 
by showing cause for failing to raise the new grounds for relief earlier and prejudice therefrom. See 
id. Slack does not meet his burden.

In reply to the court order instructing Slack to file points and authorities showing cause and 
prejudice to overcome the abuse of the writ bar, he filed a motion for reconsideration (#73). First the 
Court notes that Slack has incorrectly filed his motion for reconsideration pursuant to Fed.R.Civ.P.59. 
Under this rule a party may move for a new trial or an amendment of a judgment. Since there has not 
been a trial and a judgment has not been entered in this case, Slack cannot bring a motion pursuant 
to this rule. Additionally even if Slack could bring a motion under Fed.R.Civ.P.59, the motion must 
be brought within ten days after the entry of judgment. See Fed.R.Civ.P. 59(e). Slack’s motion was 
untimely since it was filed two days after the ten day period expired. Nevertheless, the Court will 
address Slack’s arguments in his motion for reconsideration. 

In his motion for reconsideration, Slack claims the State has not established the habeas petition 
is abusive, the amended petition is not successive, and that the case is governed by the Antiterrorism 
and Effective Death Penalty Act (AEDPA). As previously noted, the State has met its burden under 
McClesky by specifying which grounds for relief in the amended habeas petition are abusive. Next, 
the instant petition is Slack’s second petition and thus successive. The Ninth Circuit has held that a 
judge may find a second petition is abusive when new grounds are alleged and a petitioner failed to 
allege them in the previous petition, even if the previous petition was dismissed for failing to exhaust 
state remedies. See Farmer, 98 F.3d at 1557. Lastly, Slack wrongly asserts the case is governed by 
the AEDPA because it was commenced after April 26, 1996. First, the instant case was commenced 
March 27, 1995. Second, the amendments to 28 U.S.C. §§ 2244(a) and 2255 resulting from the 
AEDPA are not retroactive. See Lindh v. Murphy, --- U.S. --, -—, 117 S.Ct. 2059, 2063 (1997). 
Accordingly, the AEDPA is not applicable to this case. 

Thus, since Slack has failed to demonstrate cause and prejudice to overcome the abuse of the 
writ bar, the Court will dismiss grounds two, four(c), five, six and seven as abusive.

In ground three, Slack claims his Fourteenth and Fifth Amendment right to due process was 
violated when the jury was prejudiced by evidence of sexual misconduct. Respondents contend the 
claim is unexhausted because although Slack presented the facts of this claim to the Nevada Supreme 
Court, he did not claim his Fourteenth and Fifth Amendment rights were violated. If a petitioner fails 
to alert the state court to the fact that he is raising a federal constitutional claim, his federal claim is 
unexhausted regardless of its similarity to the issues raised in state court. Duncan v. Henry, 513 U.S. 
364, 366 (1995); Johnson v. Zenon, 88 F.3d 828, 830 (1996); see also Ybarra v. Sumner, 678

CONCLUSION 

Since ground three of Slack’s third amended habeas petition is unexhausted, this action will 
be dismissed to allow Slack the opportunity to present the unexhausted claim to the state court. If 
Slack wishes to continue with this proceeding instead of returning to state court, he will need to re- 
open this proceeding and abandon the unexhausted claim as described below. See Reutter v. Crande, 
109 F.3d 575 (9th Cir. 1997). Alternatively, Slack may immediately commence a new federal action 
in which this court could review the merits of the unexhausted claim because the new action would 
be subject to the statutory changes effected by the Antiterrorism and Effective Death Penalty Act 
(AEDPA)*; however, relief could not be granted based on any unexhausted claim.

ORDER DISMISSING APPEAL —_— 


This is an appeal from a judgment of conviction of 
second-degree murder with the use of a deadly weapon. Having 
reviewed the record and briefs and having heard the parties’ 
arguments, we conclude that Slack was properly convicted. On 
direct examination, Slack denied that he had a girlfriend/ 
boyfriend relationship with the twelve-year-old victim. The 
prosecution’s reference to a sexual relationship between Slack 
and the victim was not improper. 

The prosecution, on cross-examination, asked Slack 
whether he had told the North Las Vegas Police Department that 
he had had sexual intercourse with the victim on Halloween, 
1989. Slack did not remember making any such statement. Slack 
contends that this reference to an intimate relationship was 
improperly admitted because the jury was prejudiced by the 
thought of a nineteen-year-old man and a twelve-year-old girl 
having a sexual relationship. 

We conclude that the prosecution’s reference to the 
intimate relationship of Slack and the victim was properly 
admitted as evidence of Slack’s credibility after he denied 


having a boyfriend/girlfriend relationship with the victim. The

examination, Dyer v. State, 99 Nev. 422, 425, 663 P.2d 699, 701 
(1983), and that discretion was not abused here. 

Furthermore, any error in allowing this line of cross- 
examination was harmless, considering the amount of evidence 
linking Slack to the shooting. The fact that me Susy returned 
a verdict of second-degree murder, when it was also instructed 
on first-degree murder, indicates that the disputed evidence was 
not prejudicial. 

At oral argument, Slack’s counsel conceded that the 
other issues raised in the brief were Clearly without merit. 

Accordingly, we hereby 


ORDER this appeal dismissed.
