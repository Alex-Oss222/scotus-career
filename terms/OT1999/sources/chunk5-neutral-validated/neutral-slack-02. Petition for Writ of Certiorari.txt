REASONS WHY THE WRIT SHOULD BE GRANTED 

What should not be lost amidst the procedural history of this case is this simple reality: Mr. 
Slack has never received an adjudication by the federal courts of any of his claims, and yet, the lower 
courts have ruled that his initial, uncounseled foray into federal court -- which lasted less than 3 
months, and which was expressly dismissed “without prejudice” to allow for exhaustion -- has 
essentially foreclosed him from raising all available claims during this initial round of federal habeas 
proceedings. The lower courts’ ruling substantially departs from the spirit of Rule 9(b) and the 
language of McCleskey v. Zant, 499 U.S. 467 (1991), which established strict abuse of the writ 
standards for successive petitions, under the express theory that a petitioner had already received a 
full and fair round of habeas corpus adjudication. 

The basis for this eccentric ruling preventing Mr. Slack from litigating his claims in federal 


court traces directly to iel, 98 F.3d 1548, 1555-57 (9th Cir. 1996), cert. denied, _ 


U.S. _, 117 S.Ct. 1474 (1997), where a divided panel of the Ninth Circuit ruled that a petition can 
properly be labeled “second or successive” -- and therefore abusive under Rule 9(b) -- even though 
all previous petitions had been dismissed “without prejudice” for lack of complete exhaustion.

Alternatively, this Court should grant plenary review to resolve the split of authority among 
the United States Courts of Appeals regarding whether the provisions of Rule 9(b) governing 
“second or successive” petitions may properly be invoked even though the petitioner has never 
received a “first full round” of habeas corpus proceedings. To petitioner's knowledge, no other 
appellate court has so held. At least three Courts of Appeals squarely disagree with Farmer (and the 


consequent decision here), instead holding that a petition cannot, as a matter of law, trigger abuse-of-

In fact, even the Ninth Circuit has held, in an opinion issued just three months after the 
decision in Farmer, that a petition filed after a previous petition has been dismissed for lack of 
exhaustion is not “second or successive” under the AEDPA. In re Turner, 101 F.3d 1323 (9th Cir. 
1997). The ruling below accordingly creates an extremely anomalous and confusing scenario within 
the Ninth Circuit: As the law in that Circuit now stands, a litigant who has had previous petitions 
dismissed for exhaustion and who returns to federal court after the effective date of the AEDPA is 
clearly entitled to have his claims adjudicated under the same standards as those that would govern 
in any first petition, whereas a litigant like Mr. Slack, whose case is governed by the supposedly 
more lenient pre-AEDPA standards can, under Farmer, have his claims dismissed as abusive 
pursuant to Rule 9(b) despite never having completed any round of federal habeas review.

decision, conflict with those in other courts of appeals, and create a great potential for confusion and 
unfairness. Because the issue presented by this petition is currently the subject of debate among 


judges in the various circuits, it was not only worthy of the Court of Appeals’ attention, Lozada v.

adjudicate Mr. Slack’s claims “under the same standard as would govern those made in any other
