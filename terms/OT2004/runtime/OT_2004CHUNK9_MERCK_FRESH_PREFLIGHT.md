**Earlier authority correction.** Eli Lilly & Co. v. Medtronic, 496 U.S. 661 (1990), is Scalia's opinion, joined by Rehnquist, Brennan, Marshall, Blackmun and Stevens. Kennedy dissented with White. O'Connor did not participate. The case construed the federal-law reference to encompass the FDCA regulatory scheme and qualifying device uses; it did not decide this later preclinical-use question. Kennedy's actual dissent preferred a reference to provisions regulating drugs rather than an entire Act regulating additional products. That objection does not establish opposition to the exemption for qualifying experiments involving drug candidates. O'Connor has no earlier vote in Eli Lilly to carry forward.

**Verified trial and appellate posture.** The United States' merits brief reports that the district court instructed Merck to prove by a preponderance an objectively reasonable prospect that the accused uses would contribute relatively directly to kinds of information likely relevant to the FDA approval process. The jury rejected the defense. The district court denied Merck's motion for judgment as a matter of law. The Federal Circuit sustained liability through its restrictive regulatory relationship analysis, reversed the $15 million damages award and directed further damages proceedings. A correction of the appellate standard therefore requires evaluation of the actual record and preserved verdict challenge; it does not establish that the jury received a categorical clinical-only instruction or require automatic judgment for every experiment.

The same source reports the pre-August 1994 experimental-use treatment as part of the procedural history. The source's footnote 1 also reports that, on September 7, 2004, the district court awarded $6.375 million after the damages remand, using a prorated $1.5 million annual royalty for August 1994 through November 1998. **Supported development facts.** The research involved RGD peptide candidates and angiogenesis. Early work identified a biological mechanism; the 1995 agreement contemplated experiments addressing biological and regulatory requirements before clinical trials, including toxicity, efficacy and related studies. Work compared multiple candidates, with EMD 121974 later selected for further development; some research was included in an IND and some was not. These descriptions establish why a prospective reasonable relationship is a live issue, but neither project funding nor later submission conclusively establishes the defense for each challenged use. The United States' assessment that many or all uses appear protected remains advocacy.

**Statutory distinctions.** Section 271(e)(1) protects only the enumerated acts solely for uses reasonably related to developing and submitting information under the covered federal regulatory law. The text does not say clinical research only, successful candidates only, information actually submitted only, or generic products only. The animal-drug/veterinary-product biotechnology exception remains distinct and is not implicated by these human-drug candidates. Ordinary infringement, the statutory safe harbor, common-law experimental use, patent validity and damages each retain their own work.

The FDCA's IND and NDA processes use preclinical pharmacological and toxicological information, including mechanisms, safety and efficacy-related information pertinent to the regulatory assessment. A reasonable prospective regulatory connection can therefore precede an application and can concern a candidate later abandoned. A remote hope that any scientific discovery could eventually lead to an approved product is not the required connection. Patent use as a research tool to study other substances presents a distinct question not established merely by the use of patented candidate compounds here.

**Competing lawful paths.** One path reads the statutory relationship broadly enough to include these categories of preclinical work, while returning use-specific application and the preserved verdict challenge. Another excludes early discovery and unsuccessful or unsubmitted work as too attenuated from submission, and would preserve liability. The latter protects patent value but must answer the express development language and FDA's own preclinical informational requirements. A general exemption for all useful science and blanket judgment that every funded use qualifies lack support. The actual trial posture precludes treating correction of the legal standard as conclusive infringement immunity.
