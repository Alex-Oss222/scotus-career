## 18. Jama v. Immigration and Customs Enforcement

### Reconciliation conclusion and statutory scope

The independent four-to-four Associate division stands. Historical comparison confirms every Associate's result. O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy and Thomas support affirmance under the distinct statutory conditions. Stevens, Souter, Ginsburg and Breyer support reversal under the connected-country provision's text, structure and receiving-country condition. The latter position must not be described as an absolute acceptance requirement at every stage: subparagraph (C) permits, rather than commands, disregarding the person's designation when the stated conditions occur. Both the frozen text and the historical dissent acknowledge that distinction.

Section 1231(b)(2)(A) ordinarily commands removal to the person's designated country, subject to (B)'s territorial limitation and the actual (C) grounds. Under (C), the decisionmaker may disregard the designation for failure to designate promptly; failure of the foreign government to respond finally within 30 days of inquiry; unwillingness to accept; or prejudice to the United States. Under (D), the nationality destination is commanded unless its government fails to answer within 30 days or another period considered reasonable, or is unwilling to accept. Under (E), the connected-country alternatives precede (vii)'s fallback, which requires another accepting country when removal to every earlier listed country is impracticable, inadvisable or impossible. These conditions are not interchangeable.

The affirmance group reads (E)(iv), birthplace, without an advance-acceptance requirement because that clause has none and (vii) expressly does. Barnhart's last-antecedent inference supports that reading but does not replace the whole-enactment analysis. The removal sequence's different conditions and (C)'s permissive wording defeat an assumed universal condition. The rejection of an absolute acceptance rule supplies no independent foreign-affairs exception to the enacted sequence. Whether (D)'s exceptions leave residual discretionary authority need not be resolved to decide (E)(iv).

The reversal group reads the phrase identifying another accepting country in (E)(vii) as identifying a common characteristic of the connected-country destinations as well. Its structural objection is specifically that the Government could bypass (D)'s reception condition by choosing the same country under (E), and that a clause-by-clause reading must preserve the provision's integrated destination scheme. That group accepts the distinct discretionary wording of (C); it is not claiming that (C) and (D) are identical. The historical Souter dissent confirms this precise ground and thus corrects any overbroad implication of the provisional phrase about reception preceding every destination. No new fact, waiver or claim is added.

### Complete rule, contrary path and relief

The seven apply the same operative words in §1231(a)(6) to every category they cover. Once the Court construes that detention authorization to last only as long as reasonably necessary to accomplish removal, a different constitutional concern for a different category cannot give those words a second meaning. Constitutional avoidance chooses between plausible statutory meanings; it does not establish a variable license to detain until an individual constitutional boundary is approached. The prior express reservation means that this extension requires a present holding, not that the initial construction already adjudicated these petitioners. This statutory conclusion leaves the constitutional status distinction intact where it actually matters.

The six-month presumption operates within the removal process, not as an extra period after the ordinary ninety days. After six months, a detainee's good reason to believe there is no significant likelihood of removal within the reasonably foreseeable future requires sufficient government rebuttal. The foreseeable horizon shortens as detention continues. Six months alone does not mandate release, and supported reasons for a genuinely longer necessary removal period can be considered. Here the accepted lower findings establish lack of foreseeable Cuban removal and the frozen record supplies no sufficient rebuttal or independent custody ground. Section 1231(a)(6) therefore does not authorize continued or resumed custody on the rejected premise. Martinez's protection stands, and Benitez receives effective protection despite temporary parole; both matters return for proceedings consistent with that result.

The contrary path argues that Zadvydas's construction followed constitutional concerns peculiar to admitted persons, and that its express reservation permits a different result for the unadmitted class. Thomas treats that as the actual scope of the earlier decision; he also considers its original implied limit incorrect. The seven answer that the prior construction selected a statutory meaning, while the new category is governed by the same undifferentiated operative words. They do not need to find identical constitutional rights or to convert constitutional avoidance into adjudication of another person's rights.

Parole under §1182(d)(5) is not admission and supplies no independent perpetual detention authority after the removal period. The paragraph (1)(C) extension remains confined to failure or refusal to make a timely good-faith application for needed documents, or conspiracy or action preventing removal; a foreign government's refusal alone proves none of those predicates. Supervision under paragraph (3) retains periodic identification, necessary government-funded medical or psychiatric examination, sworn information and reasonable written conduct restrictions. A supervision violation or renewed removal prospect requires actual lawful authority and procedure before custody resumes. Section 1226a detention requires its distinct certification, specified activity and danger predicates; none is established here. Independent criminal or lawful civil-commitment authority likewise requires its own predicates.

### Complete remand and speech boundaries

Love and Hale establish the personal holder and state application and distinguish coverage from permissible regulation. Wright directs two inquiries: identify protected arms and conduct through text, history and precedent, then determine the practical burden and required justification. Incidental, nonobstructive regulations receive reasonableness review, retaining the limits against arbitrariness, discrimination and cumulative destruction.
