## Reconciled tax-immunity commitments

O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer reverse the property-tax-immunity ruling and remand at the component limits stated below. Stevens affirms that ruling. This corrects seven provisional affirmance commitments after the complete historical comparison. It does not change them merely to reproduce a count: the sources do not establish a concrete changed premise displacing those seven Justices' independent equity judgment.

The seven support this operative rule: where the Nation and its members left these parcels about two centuries ago, state and local governments continuously exercised ordinary territorial governance and taxation, and the land and surrounding community developed under that settled arrangement, ordinary market repurchase does not permit the Nation to reassert sovereign immunity from these current property taxes. Federal Indian-law equity, informed by extraordinary delay, territorial acquiescence and the disruptive restoration of dormant sovereign authority, bars that assertion both in an affirmative tax suit and as a tax-immunity defense to the City's enforcement claim. This is a substantive limit on the presently assertable sovereign prerogative, not merely a refusal to issue an injunction while preserving the identical tax-immunity defense.

The holding does not rest on a finding that Congress disposed of these parcels, that fee alienability itself authorizes taxation, or that local practice diminished a reservation. It also does not extinguish the Nation's federal recognition or settle every territorial or regulatory right. The particular present claim is unavailable despite assuming the underlying reservation and historical title predicates in the Nation's favor. Ordinary local tax law therefore is not defeated by this asserted restoration of sovereign tax immunity. Independent restrictions on a collection action remain separate.

The relevant prejudice is the revival of sovereign exclusion after generations of contrary territorial governance, not a finding that the City will become insolvent. The existing preliminary findings of budget surpluses and no identified service failure defeat an assertion of proved imminent fiscal collapse; they do not erase the undisputed long governmental arrangement. A purchased parcel's narrow tax decree and the protection of current owners from ejectment are substantial contrary considerations. The seven nevertheless regard parcel-by-parcel sovereign restoration itself as disruptive. They do not require the Court to first award all regional regulatory power before it can address that consequence of the tax claim.

Former section 465 provides the federal trust-acquisition route through which the Secretary can evaluate land use, need, tax-roll consequences and jurisdictional conflicts before restoring protection. It supports the availability of an orderly route in this setting; it is not a universal requirement for enforcing every retained tribal right, an automatic trust approval or a new exclusive personal judicial remedy of the sort examined in Rancho Palos Verdes.

Stevens's contrary sufficient ground remains complete. Canandaigua recognizes the particular retained reservation and protects its use and enjoyment; the federal supervision provisions refute the City's claim that a reservation originating with the State necessarily lacks a federal land relationship. Venetie's section 1151(b) rule does not collapse the distinct reservation category. Buffalo Creek's Articles 1–2 exchange Wisconsin interests for western lands; Article 3 conditions the new western interest, and Article 13 conditions Oneida removal on satisfactory New York purchase arrangements. The separate Seneca and Tuscarora transactions do not complete an Oneida cession. The record establishes no operative federal removal of the present protection or withdrawal of current recognition and succession. Yakima/Cass therefore provide no tax authorization. Section 233 preserves existing tax protection; its separate execution exception for one tribal member's suit against another over land use or possession is not a tax exception. Stevens rejects both substantive nonrevival and complete remedial foreclosure because the Nation's current ownership and new assessments distinguish the ancient recovery opposed in his Oneida II dissent.

## What history establishes, and what current law changes

The historical seven Associates joined an independent equity rationale. The full opinion expressly left Buffalo Creek diminishment undecided, distinguished continuously occupied Indian lands, rejected both affirmative and defensive assertions of revived tax immunity, and relied on two centuries of changed territorial governance. Souter separately treated long absence and failure to assert authority as part of the present sovereign-status claim itself. Stevens rejected that move because the Nation already owned the parcels and sought protection against a new tax, not return of another owner's property.

The frozen model identified the same voluntary purchases, prospective assessments and limited decree. Those facts were already central to the historical dispute and were expressly addressed in the opposing writings. The historical majority did not assume a pending ejectment demand against private sellers or a decree already awarding regional regulation. Treating the modern tax posture as newly discovered therefore cannot support seven departures.

The procedural disagreement also receives its actual weight. Stevens's historical dissent objected that the new equity formulation had not been discretely presented; the historical seven treated it as fairly included in the tax-immunity question, and Souter explained that both sides had addressed the underlying issue at argument. The clean packet already develops the independent equity path and its strongest response, while the source facts identify the City's governmental-reliance argument and the Nation's distinction between ejectment and land already purchased. The seven accept reaching that subsidiary ground; Stevens retains his objection to the Court's introducing it. No separate waiver holding or invented additional briefing event is necessary to these commitments. Current Ballard supplies its actual Rule 183 holding, not the historical opinion's unrelated procedural citation.

Current Yakima and Cass County do materially narrow the statutory tax reasoning available in this timeline. Yakima requires qualifying General Allotment Act/Burke Act fee provenance and the actual tax incidence; it rejects Brendale-style governmental impact as a substitute for that source. Cass requires a completed qualifying Nelson Act disposal, a parcel-specific chain and preservation of independent federal protections. Those holdings preclude a direct argument that these parcels are taxable simply because they were alienable or repurchased. The seven retain those holdings and do not pretend the City proved the missing disposal chain.

That statutory restriction does not answer the independent equity question. Cass did not decide a sovereign claim dormant for two centuries after unauthorized conveyances; neither case declared every otherwise supported tribal claim immune from federal equitable limitations. The present rule newly limits assertion of this dormant sovereign prerogative. It does not reinterpret the earlier statutory holdings as freestanding alienability rules or use financial impact to find congressional authorization. The seven must state this new independent qualification candidly; they may not describe the outcome as a routine Cass application. Stevens's objection is precisely that such a qualification itself permits courts to defeat protection that only Congress may remove.

Hagen, Yankton and Mille Lacs preserve the need for legally sufficient federal action when the question is reservation diminishment or treaty abrogation. The new equity rule supplies neither. Their exact instrument holdings and each Justice's broader or narrower joins remain intact. Venetie's dependent-community criteria likewise do not erase section 1151(a)'s distinct reservation category. The seven need not adopt the City's different reservation, treaty or lapse theories to resolve the tax claim on the sufficient equity ground.

Coeur d'Alene provides a route for prospective claims against responsible officers and requires adjudication of the asserted federal right and lawful remedy. Its actual majority did not decide ownership or guarantee every requested injunction. Its dissent's functional-title concern also addressed the officer route. Neither position supplies a merits holding forbidding the present independent equity qualification. The neutral packet's suggestion that every equitable limit must leave an effective response to an established ongoing violation cannot be expanded into a holding that the asserted tax protection necessarily survives the merits inquiry. Here the seven deny that this dormant sovereign immunity can presently be asserted; Stevens finds a continuing violation and would protect against it.

Oneida II retains its recognized federal common-law claim and expressly reserved equitable limits. The present result does not reopen its damages judgment. Felix and the territorial-acquiescence cases supply analogies for the consequences of delay and settled governance; they do not establish that a tribe is a State or mechanically transfer a state boundary. The changed character of the territory and the difficulty of restoring a former sovereign arrangement give those analogies their work here. Stevens distinguishes the same analogies on the concrete ground that title has already returned by purchase.

Neither the present financial findings nor access to fuller source text is a changed historical circumstance. The findings were part of the existing litigation and the rule does not require impending municipal bankruptcy. Current March 7 and March 22 decisions supply no contrary Indian-tax holding. Wilkinson's custody classification is not a general guarantee of equitable relief; Rancho's specific incompatible remedial route does not make trust acquisition exclusive or abolish other equitable grounds. No generic current-law, preference, record-completeness or desired-margin premise supports the seven provisional departures.
