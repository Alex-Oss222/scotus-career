## Reconciled common ground

Section 1956(h) independently punishes an agreement to commit an offense defined in section 1956 or section 1957. It requires neither allegation nor proof of a further overt act. Actual unlawful agreement, knowing participation and the charged unlawful object remain subject to proof beyond a reasonable doubt. The requested instruction adding an overt-act element was therefore unnecessary in both dockets, and both reviewed judgments are affirmed at that scope.

Shabani's enacted rule concerns section 846; it does not already decide section 1956(h) or every conspiracy statute. Its reasoning supplies a close analogy because the provisions use materially equivalent agreement-and-penalty language. Nash and Singer supply the traditional meaning of conspiracy; Congress expressly requires an additional act in section 371, while section 1956(h) contains neither that requirement nor incorporation of section 371. A sentence may define a crime and prescribe its punishment at once. The same-penalties language, placement outside the substantive-offense subsection and penalty-oriented title do not turn this complete agreement offense into an unstated section 371 enhancement. This remains a statute-specific construction rather than the historical opinion's broadest possible description of a universal formulary.

The strongest contrary path treats section 1956(h) as only increasing punishment for a section 371 conspiracy. It fails because the actual offense language identifies the conspirators and unlawful objects directly and does not incorporate the general conspiracy provision. Legislative silence about eliminating an act requirement cannot supply the missing incorporation. The later venue provision identifies geographic connections for prosecution; a reference to an act as a venue connection does not itself make an act a guilt element. No separate venue holding is needed. The frozen ground does not depend on the historical opinion's attribution of a concession to petitioners, and no such concession is imported.

Ratzlaf preserves lenity for genuine ambiguity remaining after ordinary construction. The competing interpretation here leaves no such ambiguity after the operative text, structure and traditional agreement meaning are considered. Its distinct structuring willfulness rule remains separate. The Court need not undertake harmless-error review of the omission of an instruction that the offense did not require.
