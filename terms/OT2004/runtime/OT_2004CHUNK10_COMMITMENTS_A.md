## Three rules tested separately

**M1 — Defined federal-registration subject.** Read subsection (b)'s registration category through subsection (a)'s defined standards and subsection (c)'s federal operating-authority, insurance, agent and allocated-fee system. A separate state program does not become a covered federal-registration obligation merely because it registers vehicles, requires a decal or waives a permitted federal-system fee. This supplied $100 obligation falls outside that defined subject; the record supplies no legal condition tying federal registration to payment, and its described requirements do not duplicate the listed federal proof. Affirm the judgment rejecting this statutory-preemption challenge.

M1's strongest objection is now M2, not only the broadest all-registration rule. Subsection (b)'s second sentence can be read to prohibit additional registration obligations imposed because the carrier operates interstate, even when their subject differs from the federal proofs. M1 answers that both sentences invoke the Secretary's standards; the carrier description identifies the regulated transportation field, while subsection (a) and the detailed system identify the registration subject. “In excess” gives the second sentence real work by barring additional obligations within that subject. It need not be converted into a separately inferred reason-for-registration test. This is a textual scope conclusion, not immunity supplied by a safety label, enactment age or lack of discriminatory motive.

**M2 — Registration imposed by reason of interstate-carrier status.** Subsection (b) reaches a state demand that the carrier register because it provides the specified interstate transportation in the State. Its second sentence prevents the State from imposing additional obligations in that category beyond the Secretary's standards. A genuinely general registration system whose legal operation does not select interstate-carrier status is outside that subsection (b) category, but remains subject to subsection (c)'s independent restrictions on its listed demands. This is an objective characterization of the obligation's legal trigger and operation; it does not ask whether officials subjectively disliked interstate commerce.

M2's strongest objection is that subsection (b) describes the carrier and its transportation rather than expressly adding the words “because of” or creating a discriminatory-trigger test. M2 responds that the description bounds the class of registration Congress addresses, while the excess-obligation clause ensures the federal standards provide a ceiling rather than merely regulate familiar proof forms. Subsection (a) defines what the federal standards prescribe; it does not necessarily limit the different obligations that subsection (b) forbids a State to add to an interstate-registration requirement. The competing neutral-program reading preserves ordinary registration without treating a narrower federal-proof subject as an implied exemption for every additional interstate-specific requirement.

On M2, the lower court's regulatory-purpose explanation does not settle the statutory characterization. Section 478.2(2) expressly addresses wholly interstate trucks, but Michigan's argument about the combined provisions cannot simply be disregarded. Their actual coverage and operation remain disputed on the supplied record. Vacate and remand for resolution of that characterization under M2, preserving the independent subsection (c) inquiry. The remand does not depend on a missing payment condition for completion of federal registration. It directs a legal and operational inquiry into existing provisions, not discovery of an imagined federal linkage or a presumption that the State violated the statute.

**M3 — Every registration imposed on an interstate carrier.** This reading gives subsection (b)'s words their broadest reach whenever the entity operates interstate in the State. It would potentially displace neutral state licensing and tax registration along with interstate-specific registration. Its strongest support is the absence of an express federal-proof-only or because-of-status qualifier. Its strongest objection is the integrated definition and detailed federal system, including the express limitation of prohibited fees to this section. The broad category cannot be derived from burden reduction alone. No Associate selects M3. Rejecting it does not dispose of M2, which has narrower coverage and a separate statutory account.

## Independent subsection commands retained by both supported coalitions

Subsection (c)(2)(A) permits only a State acting in its capacity as registration State to demand its listed federal-registration evidence, insurance proof, direct fees within the allocated system and local-agent information. That restriction is not confined to protecting the carrier against other participating States. Participation has its distinct roles in fee allocation and eligibility. Neither M1's separate-program category nor M2's general-neutral category licenses violation of those commands.

Subsection (c)(2)(B)(iii) limits what the Secretary's standards may require for identifying particular vehicles. It does not alone outlaw every state decal. Subsection (B)(iv) provides the insurance-proof fee system with its November 15, 1991 benchmark and $10 ceiling; subsection (B)(v) independently forbids authorizing a fee for filing or maintaining federal-registration evidence. Subsection (C) condemns fees under this section inconsistent with the fee system. No coalition merges them into a universal $10 cap. The apparent waiver and decal inform characterization but do not decide it. The lawfulness of a fee under unrelated constitutional doctrines is outside this granted statutory question.

## Separable components and competing paths

**E1 — Parents' own equal-protection claim.** Reach this distinct claim and affirm its rejection as applied to the ordinary integrated tuition requested. A State's purchase of educational services does not require it to buy demonstrated devotional instruction integrated into those services merely because it buys secular services from private alternatives. The distinction rests on the actual financed function, not the school's religious identity, presumed diversion or a supposed lack of sincere religious compulsion. The parents specifically seek Catholic instruction and viewpoint through the school's ordinary tuition; no separate controlled secular-service request is supplied. The judicial rule leaves religious providers equally eligible for independently identifiable secular work under lawful neutral program requirements and effective use controls.

The strongest contrary path treats the sectarian exclusion as a religious classification denying an otherwise equal private benefit. *Columbia Union*, *K.D.M.*, the preserved *Witters* relationship and the *Locke* dissent all forbid using status as a substitute for identifying what the public actually finances. That path would support fitted relief for eligible secular work or a genuinely independent individual benefit. It does not change the supplied payment relationship: ordinary tuition purchases the integrated instruction, and the parents identify its devotional component as the reason for their request. *Strout*'s earlier Religion Clause judgment does not adjudicate these parents' equality claim by preclusion, but its financed-function rule defeats the requested equation of the two kinds of purchase. Their equality theory supplies no basis to overrule that rule.

A broader affirmance of every application of Maine's sectarian-school wording is not joined. The specific exclusion cannot bar qualifying secular work solely by identity. Conversely, futility of applying under the religious exclusion does not establish that every lawful needs, approval or payment condition is satisfied. The decision reaches the actual constitutional claim without inventing an alternative denial under an untested neutral criterion. The local educational-needs condition, school approval rules and religious exclusion are distinct requirements; the parents' request for Catholic instruction establishes the financed-function issue here, not a universal definition of educational need.

**E2 — School's separate institutional claim.** Affirm rejection of the parents' third-party assertion. Their existing relationship with St. Dominic's is concrete; *Kowalski*'s missing-future-client relationship is not this case. But no genuine obstacle to the school's own suit is established. A close relationship and financial effect alone do not dispense with the independent hindrance requirement. This adjudicates entitlement to assert the institutional claim, not its merits. The parents' own injury and their personal claim remain separately adjudicated under E1.

The competing path stresses practical representation and aligned interests. It would matter if the record established a practical legal or factual obstacle to the school presenting its rights. Unlike the particular access barriers discussed in the *Kowalski* dissent, no institutional obstacle is supplied here. None may be manufactured from the ordinary parent-school relationship or from litigation expense in the abstract.

# OT2004 chunk 10 — independent commitments: Kelo

## Competing rules and their strongest objections

**K1 — Genuine public redevelopment under existing public-purpose review.** A comprehensive economic redevelopment plan may satisfy public use despite private implementation and inclusion of homes not individually blighted, when the actual plan serves a rationally supported public objective. The public-purpose inquiry addresses the undertaking as a whole; neither public access to every parcel nor individual blight is required. The plan here responds to prolonged economic distress through coordinated commercial, residential and public development. Its supported objectives permit affirmance. A transfer whose actual object is conferring a private benefit cannot be saved by reciting public benefits; compensation does not cure that defect. Judicial review of that objection remains real, but no new heightened burden, checklist or guaranteed-result requirement follows from the precedents.

The strongest objection is that every private owner can be replaced by a wealthier or more productive one, making hoped-for jobs and revenue an unlimited justification. The response is that the case presents an adopted comprehensive undertaking directed at a public condition, not a finding that any private replacement producing more taxes necessarily qualifies. *Berman* rejects atomizing a legitimate project parcel by parcel. *Midkiff* makes the public end, rather than private conveyancing mechanics, decisive, and *Boston & Maine* expressly carries that principle into this timeline. Judicial examination of actual purpose prevents a sham, while federal courts do not choose the best economic policy or demand proof that forecasts will come true. A categorical exclusion of economic redevelopment would add a boundary the existing rationale does not contain.

**K2 — A substantive boundary against successor-benefit takings.** Taking ordinary property for transfer to new private owners cannot be justified solely by the anticipated economic benefits of the successors' ordinary private uses. Public ownership or actual public service remains sufficient; *Berman*'s removal of a harmful area condition and *Midkiff*'s destruction of a harmful ownership structure also remain sufficient without public ownership. Those holdings do not authorize displacement merely to pursue more productive private successors. Under that rule, reverse the judgment authorizing the challenged homes' condemnation and remand for relief implementing the federal limit.

The strongest objection is that *Midkiff* explicitly equates public use with the scope of police power and *Boston & Maine* adopts a broad rational-public-purpose explanation. K2 therefore must candidly confine that general language to condemnations that themselves supply a public service or remove an established harmful property or ownership condition; it cannot claim that the precedents already state a categorical economic-development exclusion. Economic distress is real here, but on this path the alleged cure depends on the private successor's future activities rather than the removal of an existing harmful use or ownership structure. Plan size and sincerity do not cure that substantive defect. This is a limit on the reach of the earlier rationale while preserving its actual results, not a finding that this City acted corruptly.

**K3 — Remand for pretext facts.** This could be lawful if the supplied record identified a material private-favor issue wrongly excluded or unresolved below. The packet supplies neither such finding nor such procedural omission. Disagreement over what counts as public use is a legal dispute, not a reason to manufacture a pretext dispute. No Associate adopts this path on the supplied record.

## Competing statutory paths

**A1 — The general grant and actual exceptions control.** When complete diversity exists and at least one plaintiff's claim independently satisfies section 1332's amount requirement, section 1367(a) supplies jurisdiction over other claims in the same Article III case, including claims of additional parties. Subsection (b) does not exclude the smaller Rule 23 claims or claims by the additional Rule 20 plaintiffs before the Court. Its first listed category concerns claims by plaintiffs against persons added under Rules 14, 19, 20 or 24. Its other categories concern persons proposed as plaintiffs under Rule 19 or seeking intervention under Rule 24. Courts cannot add Rule 23 or Rule 20 plaintiffs to those lists merely because Congress might have intended a narrower change.

On this reading, affirm the jurisdictional judgment in No. 04-70, reverse the exclusion of the relatives' related claims in No. 04-79 and remand that matter for proceedings under section 1367. The rule abrogates the independent-amount requirements of *Zahn* and *Clark* only within this covered category. It preserves complete diversity, an independently sufficient original claim, same-case relatedness, the enacted exclusions and lawful subsection (c) discretion. Additional small claims neither destroy the sufficient claim nor become aggregated with it. Citizenship determines whether the opposing sides form the diverse controversy that section 1332 requires; amount is assessed claim by claim, leaving an adequate anchor when another related diverse claim is smaller. *Grupo Dataflux* resolves the former requirement and does not decide that every amount deficiency infects the entire action.

The strongest contrary argument is that the original-jurisdiction prerequisite refers to a civil action already satisfying the established separate-amount rule, so no supplemental power arises until every plaintiff qualifies. A1 answers that the statute expressly distinguishes claims within original jurisdiction from all other same-case claims and expressly includes additional parties. Treating every added amount deficiency as destroying the original action would make the new grant and selective plaintiff exclusions operate as if Congress had inserted an additional exception. Unlike *Finley*'s older statute, section 1367 expressly authorizes the additional-party relationship. Legislative expectations cannot replace the enacted grant.

**A2 — Preserve the established diversity original-action requirement.** Section 1367 supplements an action of which the district court already has original jurisdiction; it does not expressly alter section 1332's established requirement that separate plaintiffs each meet the amount. On this reading, *Zahn* and *Clark* remain controlling for the claims here, and the supplemental grant cannot create its own antecedent jurisdictional predicate. Reverse the Eleventh Circuit's jurisdictional ruling in No. 04-70 insofar as it permits the deficient class claims, and affirm the First Circuit's exclusion of the deficient relatives' claims in No. 04-79. Qualifying claims retain their own lawful disposition, with the class case returned for the consequences of removing claims lacking jurisdiction.

The strongest objection is the express inclusion of additional parties and the omission of Rule 23 claimants and Rule 20 plaintiffs from subsection (b). A2 answers that subsection (a)'s party sentence does important work for actions whose original basis is already established, particularly the federal-question and federal-party contexts that gave rise to *Finley*. Subsection (b)'s targeted exclusions address specified attempts to use an existing diversity action to circumvent its constraints; they are not an affirmative redefinition of the antecedent amount requirement. This is an interpretation of the action prerequisite against its established statutory meaning, not an assertion that every claim defect has the same consequences or that the words listing exceptions include rules they omit. Its proponents must acknowledge the broad-grant reading's textual force and explain why the prerequisite remains decisive.

Under either path, section 1367(c) permits declining an otherwise covered claim only for a novel or complex state-law issue, substantial predominance, dismissal of all original claims, or exceptional circumstances supplying other compelling reasons. Discretion cannot create power, and power does not command retention regardless of those grounds. No Associate uses litigation expense or the jury verdict to supply jurisdiction. No mixed class-versus-joined-plaintiff rule is adopted: the same underlying amount interpretation resolves both settings, while their distinct text must be examined separately.
