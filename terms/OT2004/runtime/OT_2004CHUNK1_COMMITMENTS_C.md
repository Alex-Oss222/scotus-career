## 1. Leocal v. Ashcroft, No. 03-583 — 2004-11-09

**Provisional disposition:** All eight Associates reach the classification question, reject both asserted section 16 routes, reverse the Eleventh Circuit's dismissal, and remand for proceedings consistent with the absence of the charged aggravated-felony predicate. This does not adjudicate a different removal ground or prescribe the administrative means of undoing completed removal.

### Threshold, entering law and common operative propositions

All nine Justices are seated and participating at submission and decision under the supplied institutional premises; the merits threshold is five votes. Review under section 1254(1) is proper. The Court may decide whether the conviction is within the criminal-alien jurisdictional bar. Calcano-Martinez, June 25, 2001, Part I, unanimously addresses conceded qualifying convictions and expressly leaves disputed offense classification open. Leocal disputes that predicate. Continuing immigration consequences preserve the controversy despite his removal. Neither the agency's adverse classification nor the seriousness of the accident establishes the jurisdictional predicate by itself.

The crime's statutory elements govern. Section 16(a) requires an element of using, attempting to use, or threatening to use physical force against another's person or property. Section 16(b) requires a felony that by its nature presents a substantial risk that such force may be used in committing it. Florida's supplied offense permits conviction for injury caused without an intentional employment of force, including negligent or accidental injury. The one-year sentence requirement is separately satisfied; it cannot supply section 16's missing classification.

Bailey, December 6, 1995, Part I, unanimously requires active employment for the different firearm-use statute and separates use from the connection to the underlying crime. It supplies a contextual analogy, not a holding that mechanically resolves section 16. Smith, June 1, 1993, permits active barter use and preserves a distinct relation requirement; it does not equate every physical causal process with using force against another. Taylor, 495 U.S. 575 (1990), supplies the offense-definition approach. The operative Standards and Tests entry **Firearms offered as consideration in a drug transaction**, effective June 1, 1993, retains Smith's separate use-and-relation requirements; it creates no freestanding section 16 test. **Immigration review channels and protection of former waiver eligibility**, including Calcano-Martinez and St. Cyr, June 25, 2001, preserves the channel/predicate distinction without awarding discretion or relief automatically.

### Alternative paths

**Exclude this predicate.** The common force-use language calls for more than accidental or negligent causation. In subsection (b), the risk must concern force being used during the crime, not simply a risk of injury. The strongest objection is that operating a vehicle actively employs an instrument and serious injury ordinarily involves physical force. The answer is that the statute identifies the employment of force against another, not merely the volitional act of driving followed by unintended injury. Reading (b) as any substantial risk of injury changes Congress's specified risk.

**Sustain under subsection (a) or (b).** The Government's strongest route combines active vehicle operation, injury causation and the danger inherent in impaired driving. Bailey does not itself foreclose that argument because its statute differs. The obstacle is the supplied minimum offense: force against another need neither be used deliberately nor be risked as a means of committing the offense. Treating injury causation as sufficient also collapses the distinction between the two statutory routes. Each Associate rejects this path for the reasons and limits below.

## 4. Koons Buick Pontiac GMC, Inc. v. Nigh, No. 03-377 — 2004-11-30

**Provisional disposition:** Stevens, O'Connor, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer would reverse the excess TILA statutory-damages award and remand for application of the $1,000 ceiling. Scalia would affirm the reviewed twice-finance-charge statutory award. All eight reach the preserved statutory question and preserve independent judgment components; no constitutional damages issue is decided.

### Threshold, entering law and precise statutory alternatives

The supplied final judgment and preservation premises permit merits review. The nine-Justice participation premise remains applicable. In the transaction's operative section 1640(a)(2)(A), clause (i) supplies twice the finance charge; clause (ii) supplies 25 percent of total monthly lease payments followed by a $100 minimum and $1,000 maximum for liability under the subparagraph; clause (iii) supplies $200 to $2,000 for an individual action concerning credit that is not under an open-end plan and is secured by real property or a dwelling. These are statutory damages. Actual damages under (a)(1), costs and reasonable fees under (a)(3), and independent claims remain distinct. Class recovery is governed separately.

Lamie, January 26, 2004, requires enforcement of the enacted eligibility and structural language; its recipient-list rule has nine direct supporters and Thomas's full explanation has eight, with Stevens separately concurring and Souter and Breyer joining both writings. Hartford Underwriters, May 30, 2000, Thomas for the unanimous Court, requires a statutory entitlement before a remedy. Neither case answers the semantic scope of subparagraph in this different law. The Standards and Tests entries **Estate compensation and authorized professional status**, effective January 26, 2004, and **Independent recovery of collateral-preservation expenses under section 506(c)**, effective May 30, 2000, preserve those actual entitlement rules, including their stated bankruptcy exceptions and reservations. They create no TILA-specific ceiling or general command to ignore context.

**Retained general bracket, specific real-property exception.** Reading subparagraph as (A) preserves the general $100-to-$1,000 range for clause (i), while the expressly different range controls the defined clause (iii) category. The strongest objection is the old bracket's placement inside the lease clause and the apparent inconsistency of applying two ranges to (iii). A general rule qualified by a more specific rule resolves the ranges without changing the retained noun or deleting either bracket. Successive enactments support, rather than replace, this structural reading.

**Independent clauses, uncapped clause (i).** The alternative reads the adjacent bracket as confined to clause (ii), taking the new alternative (iii) and its separate range as confirmation that each clause now supplies its own measure. The strongest objection is that this treats the retained word subparagraph as though it identified only a clause and abandons the previously general ceiling without express deletion. Its proponents answer that the full amended syntax, rather than the earlier arrangement, determines scope and that uncapped twice-finance-charge recovery is coherent rather than absurd. This path changes only the reviewed statutory amount; it supplies no findings about independent damages or fees.
