The provisions perform different jobs:

- Section 1396a(a)(8) requires an application opportunity and reasonable promptness in furnishing medical assistance to eligible persons. The present claim concerns the assistance and timing obligation, not an alleged refusal to accept an application.
- Section 1396a(a)(10) identifies required coverage and the covered categories, with distinct optional groups and numerous qualifications. Intermediate-care assistance under section 1396d(a)(15) is not in paragraph (a)(10)(A)'s minimum list that every participating State must provide. The accepted premise here is Pennsylvania's inclusion of that benefit and these claimants' eligibility. There is no holding that every State must adopt every optional service or that every person with a disability meets every statutory condition.
- Section 1396d(a)(15) identifies services in a qualifying intermediate-care facility, excludes an institution for mental diseases, and requires a determination of need in accordance with section 1396a(a)(31). It is a definition connected to the operative coverage and promptness commands, not an independent universal entitlement.
- Section 1396a(a)(31), when the plan covers these services, requires a written plan of care before admission or authorization of benefits and periodic independent professional review, including medical evaluation, of continued need. Section 1396d(d) supplies facility purpose and standards and active-treatment conditions. Its public-institution maintenance-of-expenditure provision expressly concerns quarters before January 1, 1975; it supplies no newly invented 2005 waiting-list condition.
- Section 1396c provides federal oversight after notice and an opportunity for a state-agency hearing. A noncompliant plan or substantial administrative noncompliance triggers withholding until the Secretary is satisfied the failure will end, with discretion to limit withholding to affected categories or portions of the plan. The substantial-compliance trigger for that federal remedy does not rewrite the individual promptness command.
- Section 1396a(a)(3) provides an individual state fair hearing when a claim is denied or not acted upon with reasonable promptness. That is more than pure federal auditing, and must actually be considered in the foreclosure inquiry. It does not itself provide a congressionally tailored exclusive federal judicial action, a special judicial filing period, or an express exclusion of section 1983.
- Sections 1320a-2 and 1320a-10 defeat state-plan placement as a categorical ground of nonenforceability, preserve other entitlement inquiries, and expressly preserve Suter's section 671(a)(15) result. They do not create the Medicaid rights on their own.
- Section 1983 supplies an express action for deprivation of a federal right under color of state law. Its special judicial-officer injunction qualification does not concern the official defendant here. No separate implied Medicaid action needs to be manufactured.

Four components are kept distinct:

**A. Covered individual assistance.** Whether section 1396a(a)(10), with the selected covered category and the operative payment definition in section 1396d(a), gives these eligible persons a sufficiently definite individual right. ## Common legal and factual premises

Blessing, April 21, 1997, unanimously requires a particular intended beneficiary, a binding duty, and an individual entitlement sufficiently definite for judicial enforcement. A beneficial statute or mandatory administrative duty alone is inadequate. Qualitative terms are not categorically unmanageable. State-plan location alone does not defeat a right; sections 1320a-2 and 1320a-10 also do not create one. A proven right is presumptively enforceable under section 1983, subject to express exclusion or a sufficiently comprehensive incompatible remedial arrangement. Defendant, violation, causation and relief remain separate inquiries.

Gonzaga, June 20, 2002, holds 5–4, in Stevens's opinion for the Court, that FERPA's nondisclosure provision secures an individual right enforceable through section 1983; it restates Blessing's factors, treats grammatical form as relevant but not controlling and places on the defendant the showing that Congress excluded section 1983. Thomas's four-Justice dissent finds no right in the institution-directed funding condition. Neither the Court, the Breyer-Souter concurrence nor the dissent categorically excludes funding statutes, makes an independently implied action necessary, or automatically displaces section 1983 through agency oversight. Bruneau, June 3, 1999, likewise distinguishes an established personal statutory right from its enforcement channel. No historical version of these cases replaces their supplied public law.

The present statutory commands identify medical assistance, eligible persons, and prompt performance. The definition then supplies a specified category of covered costs. Pennsylvania's adoption of the intermediate-care benefit and these plaintiffs' eligibility are accepted at this stage. The category is optional at the plan-selection level; this analysis does not require every State to adopt it or suspend its conditions. Applicable conditions include a qualifying facility other than an institution for mental diseases, the need determination connected to section 1396a(a)(31), a written plan before admission or authorization of benefits, periodic independent review, and section 1396d(d)'s facility and active-treatment requirements. Accepted eligibility is not reopened by inventing a new factual contest about those conditions.

Medical assistance at the relevant time means payment of part or all of covered costs. Recognition of a financing entitlement does not settle whether, on an actual violation record, effective prospective compliance requires an arrangement beyond merely announcing that payment is available. Conversely, the practical need for care does not itself authorize an order creating beds, choosing a facility, or directing unlimited home services. The Court can answer the pleaded enforceability questions fully and leave the distinct reserved remedy issue to the District Court.

The strongest common adverse path is to characterize the cited provisions as directions for a federally approved financing plan, with undefined timing and specialized clinical conditions that leave only a federal-state compliance obligation. A second adverse path accepts personal rights but treats the fair hearing and federal funding mechanisms together as an intentionally exclusive remedial system. Both require close statutory analysis; neither follows merely from the Spending Clause or from the absence of an additional implied action. A narrower adverse conclusion concerning an asserted right to immediate physical placement would not itself justify dismissing the actual assistance claim.

## Stevens

Stevens would answer that the operative right comes from paragraph (a)(10) read with the definition, not from the definition alone; the remaining payment-versus-service question limits relief rather than erasing the financing right. **B — provisional commitment: recognize the timing right.** The promptness obligation runs to eligible individuals and the assistance just identified. **C — provisional commitment: no foreclosure under existing law, subject to Rancho refresh.** His Wright and Wilder joins and Blessing opinion reject the inference that funding withdrawal alone is exclusive. **D — provisional commitment: affirm and return for ordinary further proceedings.** His Blessing mandate requires identification of breach, responsible official, causation, affected persons and fitted relief. The strongest argument for resolving placement now is that a purely nominal right could leave years of delay untouched. A legally sufficient remedy for a proved violation must be effective, but this enforceability appeal lacks an adjudicated basis to select one.
