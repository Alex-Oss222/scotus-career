## Common scope determination and operative effect

The two questions presented ask whether the specified questioning and handcuff detention violated the Fourth Amendment. The officers seek a further holding that, even if their conduct violated that Amendment, the February 1998 law did not clearly prohibit it. That independent conduct-date defense is not fairly included in these constitutional questions as framed. The Court should decide the presented constitutional issues and their judgment consequences without independently granting or denying qualified immunity.

This is a case-specific application of the question limit, not a holding that immunity must always appear by name or can never be fairly included in a broader section 1983 question. The officers' actual argument is substantial: they identify the same conduct, overlapping precedents, treatment below, presentation in the petition, and merits briefing; they invoke Kolstad and Procunier. Those features address notice and practical readiness, but do not alone make the separate legal defense necessary to answer these particular constitutional questions. In particular, referring to earlier precedents to ask whether conduct was constitutional does not also ask whether an unconstitutional act was insufficiently clearly forbidden at a different legal cutoff. Mena's alternative answer to the defense is not consent to enlargement.

The original merits commitments already provide complete answers at their supported level. All eight reject a violation based solely on the topic of questions absent added restraint or protected intrusion. Four resolve the identified search-period force issue for the officers; four require proper appellate application of the force rule. All preserve the independently presented duration ground and the actual verdict's remedial effect. These dispositions do not require the Court to decide the separate personal defense. This practical point reinforces the question-specific scope conclusion; it is not a free-standing power to leave a properly reached question incomplete.

Accordingly:

- None of the eight supplies a present independent qualified-immunity holding or a separate judgment vote granting that defense.
- O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, and Thomas retain their original substantive view that the identified search-period conduct would receive immunity if the issue were properly reached. That view is now expressly conditional and cannot be counted as an alternative holding of this decision.
- Stevens, Souter, Ginsburg, and Breyer retain their original conduct-specific approach. They do not obtain a present immunity denial from their refusal to decide the force issue categorically.
- On remand, any properly preserved independent immunity defense remains available under the existing February 1998 notice rule, on the conduct and factual account actually at issue and subject to ordinary preservation, finality, and mandate principles. This does not guarantee a new opportunity to litigate a forfeited or conclusively resolved matter.
- The existing distinction between current constitutional rules and conduct-date notice remains law. Explaining the standard governing a genuinely surviving defense on remand is not an adjudication of that defense in the officers' favor.

## Individual scope commitments

**Stevens — confines review; no independent immunity ruling.** His Summers opinion resolves execution-related detention authority; his complete Holland join separately addresses force and the particular immunity questions properly before the Court there. His Brosseau dissent treats an obvious constitutional prohibition as capable of supplying notice, but still addresses notice as a distinct inquiry. His unanimous Back join preserves the same division. Those positions support recognizing the difference between constitutional legality and the separate defense, rather than treating shared precedents as sufficient inclusion. Their application to this petition's wording is a reasoned case-specific inference, not an earlier Stevens holding on these questions.

The strongest alternative is to accept the officers' Procunier-based submission that an otherwise unresolved defense to the same section 1983 judgment is fairly subsumed. Stevens accepts that such inclusion is legally possible in another framing, but not that requested immunity relief and briefing alone expand these two constitutional questions. He therefore keeps the original F remand and all D, Q, P, and R protections, while changing I into preservation of a properly available lower-court defense. He will not join a present grant or denial of immunity, an assertion that Mena conceded scope, or a suggestion that the scope limit establishes a waiver below.

**O'Connor — confines review; retains immunity only as a conditional substantive view.** Her Holland Parts I and III join and join in Kennedy's partial dissent distinguish an actual constitutional force issue from a separate notice ground. Her Brosseau join and Back join require a defined conduct-date inquiry. Her Caballes opinion resolves the presented constitutional classification and restraint premises without making every related liability defense part of that question. These own positions support a case-specific separation here; they do not establish an automatic Rule 14 rule.

The strongest alternative is the officers' argument that the same Summers authorities answer both the merits and notice questions and that an independent legal immunity judgment would avoid more proceedings. O'Connor accepts that substantive efficiency but concludes it does not make notice part of the expressly framed constitutional question. Her original F conclusion already removes the identified search-period force ground. The original statement that she would independently grant immunity is therefore qualified: she holds that view if reached, but does not reach it here. She preserves P and the mixed-verdict remedy and refuses any mandate treating immunity as already established for other conduct or post-search detention.

**Scalia — confines review; no independent notice holding.** His Holland Part I/III joins and join in Kennedy's partial dissent recognize force and immunity as separable grounds. His Brosseau and Back joins apply the notice defense when that issue is presented; neither makes immunity an element of the constitutional prohibition itself. His original Muehler F commitment remains a legal reasonableness conclusion on the described weapons-search circumstances, which answers the second question without deciding the independent defense.

The strongest opposing path reads the questions' references to earlier holdings as encompassing what reasonable officers should have known. Scalia rejects that construction here: asking what the Fourth Amendment permitted is different from asking whether an established violation nevertheless avoids damages because the governing prohibition lacked sufficient clarity. Fair inclusion cannot follow from every reference to precedent. He retains his conditional immunity assessment but supplies no present alternative immunity ground. He can join a remand preserving an actually available defense under ordinary law; he refuses a new waiver finding, an extension of that conditional assessment to disputed later intervals, or a blanket denial of the defense.

**Kennedy — confines review despite his substantive immunity position.** His own Holland partial dissent supplies a notice ground distinct from constitutional reasonableness, while his joins in Parts I and III preserve actual-force review. His Brosseau opinion and Back join maintain that distinction and insist on a properly specified factual premise. The two questions here can be answered on constitutional grounds; their wording does not itself submit the separate conduct-date defense. That is the limited scope inference drawn from his own jurisprudence and the source text.

The strongest alternative is to regard immunity as inseparable from requested reversal of the personal damages judgment, especially because it was decided below and briefed. Kennedy concludes that those features preserve a defense for the appropriate tribunal but do not make it part of every constitutional question about the same conduct. His original F merits conclusion and post-search reservation remain. His original independent immunity preference becomes a conditional view rather than an alternative holding. He will join neither a present immunity denial nor a mandate announcing that the officers conclusively prevail on immunity; remaining conduct must receive its proper lower-court treatment.

**Souter — confines review and preserves ordinary defense adjudication.** His full Holland join requires the actual force and responsibility to be identified before notice is assessed; his Brosseau join accepts the separate defense; his Back join preserves both supported factual assumptions and preconduct notice. These positions support addressing the defense on its own proper procedural footing, rather than using a constitutional question to decide an additional legal issue automatically.

The strongest counterargument is that remand leaves a question already developed by both sides and closely related to the merits. Souter accepts readiness as relevant but not decisive under Rule 14. He distinguishes an argument needed to answer a presented question from a separate sufficient ground that defeats liability even after an opposite constitutional answer. His original F and P remand positions remain, as does the existing notice standard for any surviving defense. He will not join a categorical holding that immunity is unavailable, an automatic reopening of matters barred by ordinary procedural rules, or treating the absence of a present immunity adjudication as a new merits rule.

**Thomas — confines review; retains the original immunity assessment conditionally.** His Holland Part I/III joins and join in the partial dissent, followed by his Brosseau and Back joins, recognize that constitutional wrong and individual notice are separate legal inquiries. The text of these questions identifies the former. A personal defense may be fairly included in a differently framed section 1983 question, but its relationship to the same underlying conduct is insufficient to settle inclusion here.

His strongest alternative is the officers' reliance on prior fair-inclusion decisions and the completed briefing. Thomas accepts the possibility those authorities describe, while declining the automatic inference from a constitutional question to every defense against damages. His original F position removes the specified search-period force theory on its merits. His original favorable immunity assessment remains a conditional position and supplies no independent part of this Court's judgment. He preserves existing defenses to any surviving claim under ordinary law and refuses an inference either of forfeiture or of conclusively established immunity on the post-search account.

**Ginsburg — confines review; no present defense adjudication in either direction.** Her Holland opinion separately identifies the force question, the conduct-date notice question, and the factual predicates proper for appellate resolution. Her Brosseau and Back joins preserve the defense and its concrete notice requirement. Those own positions make the distinction legally consequential; the present question wording does not erase it merely because petitioners seek reversal of the same judgment.

The strongest alternative is that immunity is fully briefed and intimately related to the constitutional analysis, so no unfair surprise would follow from reaching it. Ginsburg accepts that this answers an objection based on briefing alone. It does not answer the distinct question limit, and Mena expressly preserved that objection while responding alternatively. She therefore retains the original F remand, Q limitation, P review, and verdict-sensitive R, with I reserved to properly available lower-court adjudication. She refuses a current immunity grant, a current immunity denial, or treatment of a party's alternative answer as consent to an expanded question.

**Breyer — confines review while preserving a practically complete remand.** His full Holland join separates actual-force analysis from notice; his Brosseau and Back joins preserve the defense's independent legal work. These positions support resolving the actual constitutional questions and leaving the distinct defense available where ordinary lower-court procedure permits. His original demand for a specified factual account before conclusively disposing of force or immunity remains intact.

The strongest alternative is practical: the same legal research and established record might permit an immediate immunity disposition, avoiding another appellate step. Breyer accepts that as a reason to consider fair inclusion, not a reason to presume it. The express question framing, the respondent's preserved scope objection, and the availability of a complete constitutional disposition favor the bounded route here. His original F and P positions do not change. He will join remand language that states existing notice principles and preserves a properly available defense, but not language automatically granting immunity, requiring redundant litigation already foreclosed, or converting this scope choice into a new limit on the defense itself.
