## Disposition and complete operative rule

All eight Associates vote to affirm the Eleventh Circuit in both consolidated dockets, Whitfield, No. 03-1293, and Hall, No. 03-1294, on the preserved overt-act instruction question. Section 1956(h) independently makes the agreement to commit an offense defined in section 1956 or section 1957 a conspiracy crime. It requires neither allegation nor proof of an additional overt act. The Government still must prove beyond a reasonable doubt the actual unlawful agreement, the defendant's knowing participation and the charged unlawful object; the absence of an overt-act element does not make mere association or knowledge of another's plan sufficient.

The requested additional-element instruction therefore was not required. Affirmance rejects that legal objection in both cases and preserves the judgments at the scope reviewed. The Court does not adjudicate a separate venue claim, determine evidentiary sufficiency, find other elements established afresh, prescribe a sentence or resolve a separately preserved issue absent from the grant. No remand to obtain an unnecessary overt-act finding, new trial, acquittal or resentencing is ordered.

## Competing lawful readings and their consequences

**Independent agreement offense.** The provision identifies the prohibited conspiracy, its specified unlawful objects and the resulting punishment. The ordinary agreement baseline described in Nash and Singer supplies meaning to the chosen term; section 371 demonstrates that Congress knows how to demand a further act when it wishes. Shabani is a close and unanimously accepted analogy, rather than a substitute for reading this statute. The strongest contrary argument is that a clause phrased in terms of the same penalties primarily enhances an already-defined section 371 offense. But a provision can define liability and prescribe punishment in the same sentence; its direct identification of conspirators is operative, and no cross-reference incorporates the general statute's additional condition.

**Incorporated section 371 offense.** This reading would retain an overt-act safeguard and make the penalty provision apply to a conspiracy otherwise defined by section 371. Its strongest support is the defendants' placement, history and venue argument. Its decisive weakness is the absence of an operative incorporation and the meaningful textual contrast with Congress's explicit overt-act statutes. A later venue rule may use conduct as a geographic connector without making conduct an additional guilt element or retroactively altering the earlier enactment. The Court does not decide that venue rule's entire reach. All eight reject the proposed extra element; no harmless-error adjudication is needed for omitting an instruction the law did not require.

**Lenity.** Ratzlaf preserves lenity for genuine ambiguity remaining after ordinary construction; it does not require an added element whenever litigants offer competing readings. The statute-specific analysis resolves the present choice. Ratzlaf's distinct knowledge-of-illegality holding for willful structuring does not migrate into this laundering-conspiracy ruling.

## Precedent treatment and resulting law

- **United States v. Shabani, November 1, 1994:** Applied as the close section 846 textual analogy and extended to the independently construed section 1956(h); its holding remains offense-specific and knowing agreement remains required.
- **Nash v. United States, 229 U.S. 373 (1913):** Applied for the traditional agreement baseline where Congress does not add an overt-act requirement; no universal exemption from statutory proof follows.
- **Singer v. United States, 323 U.S. 338 (1945):** Applied for the significance of the statutory selection of the conspiracy term without an additional act condition.
- **Ratzlaf v. United States, January 11, 1994:** Preserved as requiring residual ambiguity for lenity; that trigger is absent here. Its separate structuring mens rea is unchanged.
- **United States v. Felix, March 25, 1992, as described in Shabani:** Its agreement/substantive-offense distinction does not supply an overt-act element and no double-jeopardy issue is decided.

The resulting rule is confined to section 1956(h): allegation and proof of a further overt act are unnecessary, but actual agreement, knowing participation and the charged laundering object must still be established beyond a reasonable doubt. Venue, other statutes' express elements, separate sufficiency arguments and sentencing remain outside this determination. There is one common eight-Associate rationale and no part-specific join difference; no fractured-opinion aggregation is needed.
