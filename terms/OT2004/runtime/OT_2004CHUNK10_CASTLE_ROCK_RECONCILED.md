## Controlling questions and common factual qualifications

The first question is whether Colorado's scheme and individualized protective order secure a substantive property interest in the requested enforcement. The second is whether the pleaded municipal conduct deprived that interest without adequate process. The latter requires a substantive interest first; municipal policy, attribution, fault where required, causation and remedy remain separate inquiries.

The statutory core directs every reasonable enforcement means and arrest, or pursuit of a warrant when immediate arrest is impractical, upon probable cause of violation and service or actual notice. Means, practical circumstances and success remain distinct. The permanent order allowed the specified parenting time and pickup, including a midweek dinner arranged on reasonable notice. The alleged unannounced taking is assessed against those permissions; the order did not forbid every contact. The reverse-side notice reports enforcement law rather than creating a decree against nonparty police. No qualification of the later printed compilation is silently treated as a complete conduct-time code.

Complaint allegations are assumed in assessing dismissal, not converted into findings of municipal policy, feasible rescue, culpability or causation. The substantive-due-process and individual-immunity dispositions lie outside the question under review.

## Souter and Breyer: tested departures and retained premises

Both provisional affirmances rest on simulated prior positions that differ from the historical record. Each was tested against the strongest historical evidence, the Souter concurrence that Breyer joined, rather than treated as controlling because it differs from history or set aside because it does.

The historical Souter concurrence, joined by Breyer, contends that even absolute commands to arrest or seek a warrant do not supply the independent substantive guarantee Roth requires. It distinguishes ordinary services such as utilities, public education and benefits from an interest whose object is the same set of executive steps for which federal process is requested. Stevens's historical response calls enforcement itself the tangible service, distinguishes refusal from incorrect assessment, and separates the right to insist on a service from the right to decline it.

In this timeline the governing entitlement law differs. Breyer's Sandin opinion of the Court, joined by Souter, holds that a State creates a protected interest when substantive law meaningfully restricts officials' authority, examined across the whole scheme rather than by mandatory words; Breyer's whole-scheme concurrence, also joined by Souter, would have found the interest on Hawaii's scheme. Breyer's unanimous Wilkinson v. Austin opinion, decided June 13, 2005, applies that rule and approves designed safeguards only as they work meaningfully. The historical concurrence did not engage a controlling meaningful-constraint rule that both Justices established.

This timeline's Doe v. District of Columbia, whose procedural holding both joined, distinguishes investigative steps from a protective disposition that local law commanded only upon findings not established there, and it leaves independently created entitlements available in principle. Colorado's scheme falls on the reserved side: the protected person, the objective violation-and-notice predicates and the arrest-or-warrant command define the protective assistance itself. The procedures by which officers decide whether those predicates exist are distinct from that assistance, as procedures for terminating utility service or benefits are distinct from the service or benefit. That separation, supplied by the simulated Doe reservation and Sandin rule, answers the historical objection that the claimed interest is the enforcement procedure itself.

Breyer's Saenz opinion of the Court, joined by Souter, keeps DeShaney's rule against a general duty to protect while recognizing that actual interference with an available protective response can be a deprivation, and it keeps municipal attribution separate from individual defenses. Souter also joined Ginsburg's Pinder dissent on bounded affirmative deprivation and the Baby Richard protected-interest opinion. None of these establishes property in police enforcement, and neither Justice treats them as doing so; they confirm that DeShaney's limit, which both preserve, does not by itself answer a claim to a defined state-created protective service.

The statutory positions remain distinct. Breyer's CAPTA dissent join and both Justices' Gonzaga positions concern federal statutory rights enforceable through section 1983, and Breyer's same CAPTA writing joined Doe's rejection of the investigative-procedure due-process theory. Neither Justice converts a statutory-right position into constitutional property. Their property conclusion rests on the Sandin, Wilkinson and Saenz premises and Doe's reservation, applied to a scheme that commands protection rather than investigation.

The present protective order, statutory triggers, complaint posture and bounded request are the facts that were before the historical Court; the difference lies in the controlling entitlement law each Justice has established or joined in this timeline. Both changed premises are concrete and Justice-specific, and both provisional affirmances are retained. This is not a general presumption against historical outcomes.

**Historical departure:** Souter supports affirmance rather than the historical reversal and does not carry forward his historical concurrence's property-in-procedure objection. In this timeline he joined Breyer's Sandin meaningful-constraint rule and whole-scheme concurrence and Doe v. District of Columbia's reservation of independently created entitlements; Colorado's scheme commands protective assistance once objective conditions exist, so the protective service, not the procedure for deciding whether it is owed, is the protected benefit. He joins only that formulation, with Doe's investigative-procedure holding preserved and no outcome guaranteed.

**Historical departure:** Breyer supports affirmance rather than his historical joins in the Court's opinion and in Souter's concurrence. His own Sandin opinion and whole-scheme concurrence, his unanimous Wilkinson v. Austin opinion and his Saenz opinion establish in this timeline that a meaningful substantive constraint on withholding a defined benefit creates a protected interest, that DeShaney's limit does not answer a state-created protective claim, and that designed safeguards must work meaningfully. His statutory CAPTA and Gonzaga positions remain distinct and do not supply the conclusion.

The resulting final judgment alignment is four Associates for survival and remand—Stevens, Souter, Ginsburg and Breyer—and four for reversal—O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy and Thomas. Souter and Breyer depart from their historical judgments on the changed premises stated above; no other Associate judgment departs from history.

## Proposition joins, writing functions and remedies

Stevens, Souter, Ginsburg and Breyer support a common ground: Colorado's statute and the individualized order secure to the identified beneficiary a defined protective service, distinct from the procedures for deciding whether it is owed; discretion over practicable means and the probable-cause and practicability judgments remain; and the service is the required response, not a guaranteed outcome. An opinion confined to that ground, leaving process to a Mathews inquiry fitted to emergency conditions and requiring municipal attribution and injury to be proved separately, can receive all four complete joins. Souter's join requires that the opinion identify the protected object in that way, preserve Doe's investigative-procedure holding and exclude general investigative duties; a formulation treating the enforcement procedure itself as property would lose his join. Breyer's join requires attention to the governmental burden, without prescribed response times or a hearing that delays emergency work.

Stevens and Ginsburg's broader account, that state law creates and defines the protected interest and that authoritative state construction governs genuinely disputed state-law details, has a genuine concurring function. Souter's explanation of why the claim is not process to obtain process has a separate concurring function. Neither enlarges the common ground.

O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy and Thomas support a common opposing ground: these mandated enforcement steps do not secure the independent substantive property asserted, even giving the statutory commands their force. Their writing has the genuine function of stating that objection without resting on unlimited discretion, transferability or market value, and of answering the service-versus-procedure distinction. They adopt no independent adequate-process ruling.

The United States' adequate-process alternative remains a separate route, not a second ground for the four reversing Associates. Connecticut Doe asks whether additional process concerns facts legally material to the deprivation; on the pleaded nonconsideration policy the statutory conditions are such facts, and repeated contacts do not establish meaningful consideration as a complaint-stage fact.

Affirmance leaves the claim pending for proof of qualifying statutory circumstances, actual deprivation and usable process, an attributable policy or custom, fault where applicable, actual causal injury and fitted relief. Nominal relief for established denial of process and compensation for the deaths have distinct predicates. The separate officer and substantive-due-process dispositions remain outside review. The supported conduct-time statutory core and the order resolve the entitlement; no dispositive unresolved Colorado construction requires certification.

No general constitutional duty to protect, police timetable, nationwide hearing procedure, guaranteed rescue or damages finding is adopted. DeShaney's distinct no-general-protection rule, Doe's procedural-interest limit and its independent-entitlement reservation, Mathews, Zinermon and municipal-liability requirements retain their separate work.
