## Controlling questions and common factual qualifications

The first question is whether Colorado's scheme and individualized protective order secure a substantive property interest in the requested enforcement. The second is whether the pleaded municipal conduct deprived that interest without adequate process. The latter requires a substantive interest first; municipal policy, attribution, fault where required, causation and remedy remain separate inquiries.

The statutory core directs every reasonable enforcement means and arrest, or pursuit of a warrant when immediate arrest is impractical, upon probable cause of violation and service or actual notice. Means, practical circumstances and success remain distinct. The permanent order allowed the specified parenting time and pickup, including a midweek dinner arranged on reasonable notice. The alleged unannounced taking is assessed against those permissions; the order did not forbid every contact. The reverse-side notice reports enforcement law rather than creating a decree against nonparty police. No qualification of the later printed compilation is silently treated as a complete conduct-time code.

Complaint allegations are assumed in assessing dismissal, not converted into findings of municipal policy, feasible rescue, culpability or causation. The substantive-due-process and individual-immunity dispositions lie outside the question under review.

## Breyer: tested departure and resulting revision

The provisional departure relied on two genuinely new prior positions: Breyer authored the simulated Sandin substantive-constraints rule and joined the simulated Doe dissent recognizing a report-triggered CAPTA investigation right. Both were tested against the strongest historical evidence, not treated as irrelevant because they differ from history.

Sandin addresses state restrictions on imposing a meaningful deprivation of prison liberty. It expressly distinguishes substantive constraints from mandatory procedures and does not make every executive direction property. Breyer's whole-scheme concurrence, joined by Souter, finds a substantive restraint on punishment, not property in the prescribed process by itself. This premise supports serious examination of Colorado's scheme, but it does not answer whether the object claimed here is a substantive benefit rather than the enforcement mechanism.

The CAPTA dissent treats the identified beneficiary, mandatory trigger and concrete investigation as a federal statutory right enforceable through section 1983. Crucially, that same writing expressly joins the Court's separate rejection of the investigative-procedure due-process theory. A directly enforceable statutory service and constitutional property are therefore distinct in Breyer's actual simulated record. The new statutory-right position cannot silently be converted into the property conclusion it expressly withheld.

The historical Souter concurrence, joined by Breyer, addresses precisely the proposed rebuttal: even absolute commands to arrest or seek a warrant do not supply the independent substantive guarantee needed for Roth property. It distinguishes ordinary services such as utilities, public education and benefits from an interest whose object is the same set of executive steps for which federal process is requested. Stevens's historical response calls enforcement itself the tangible service, distinguishes refusal from incorrect assessment, and separates the right to insist on a service from the right to decline it. Those arguments were already before Breyer when he joined Souter; the present protective order, statutory triggers, complaint posture and bounded request do not materially change them.

There is thus no supported changed premise sufficient to retain this particular departure. Reconciliation revises Breyer to reversal because the independently sufficient property/procedure objection survives both new priors and the unchanged case-specific predicates. This is not a general presumption that historical outcomes prevail, nor a mechanical consequence of Sandin's prison setting. His practical concern with the value and burden of process remains, but cannot supply its own underlying property interest.

The resulting final judgment alignment is six Associates for reversal—O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas and Breyer—and two for survival and remand—Stevens and Ginsburg. No material historical departure remains in an Associate's judgment. Breyer's original independent row is preserved, and this document records the reason for its revision.

## Proposition joins, writing functions and remedies

All six reversing Associates support the sufficient ground that these mandated enforcement steps do not secure the independent substantive property asserted. An opinion confined to that ground and the actual scheme can receive all six complete joins. It may acknowledge practical judgment without deciding that officers have complete discretion to ignore the statute. Souter and Breyer's ground works even on the claimant's mandatory-duty premise; the common opinion must preserve that logic. They do not become judgment-only votes merely because they emphasize this independent ground. If assembly instead makes unrestricted enforcement discretion its exclusive explanation, their substantive ground needs a separate concurrence; that would not establish their join in the broader premise.

Stevens and Ginsburg's opposing writing has the genuine function of treating the specified enforcement as a substantive service and explaining why the alleged policy of nonconsideration permits a process claim to proceed. They do not propose guaranteed rescue or automatic municipal compensation. Their strongest answer to the majority is that operational discretion and inability to guarantee success do not dissolve a duty to provide a defined service. The reversing coalition answers that constraint is necessary but does not itself establish that the protected object is substantive property, distinct from enforcement procedure against another person.

The United States' adequate-process alternative remains a separate route, not a second holding for the six reversing Associates. Connecticut Doe asks whether additional process concerns facts legally material to the deprivation; it does not establish meaningful consideration here as a complaint-stage fact. No majority conclusion about the adequacy of actual police consideration follows from rejection of property.

Reversal restores dismissal of the reviewed federal municipal procedural-due-process theory. Stevens and Ginsburg would leave the claim pending for proof of qualifying statutory circumstances, actual deprivation and usable process, an attributable policy or custom, fault where applicable, actual causal injury and fitted relief. Nominal relief for established denial of process and compensation for the deaths have distinct predicates. The separate officer and substantive-due-process dispositions remain outside review.

No categorical immunity for all state-created protective services, universal economic-value requirement, police timetable, nationwide hearing procedure or damages finding is adopted. Roth's independent-entitlement principle, Doe's procedural-interest limit and its independent-entitlement reservation, DeShaney's distinct no-general-protection rule, Mathews, Zinermon and municipal-liability requirements retain their separate work.
