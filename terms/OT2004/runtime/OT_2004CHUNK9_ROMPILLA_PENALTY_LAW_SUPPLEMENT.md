# OT2004 chunk 9 — Rompilla objective Pennsylvania sentencing-law supplement

Scope: statutory unanimity and individual mitigation under the Pennsylvania sentencing scheme. This is an additive neutral verification artifact. It assigns no vote, authorship or probability finding and does not alter an earlier frozen packet. Its limited proposition is objective state sentencing law, not a new federal constitutional standard.

The complete principal opinion in Commonwealth v. Frey, 520 Pa. 338, 554 A.2d 27 (February 3, 1989), was read. It applies the statutory unanimity scheme to a 1980 trial and expressly states the individual-juror mitigation implication. The 1995 Rompilla direct appeal independently identifies §9711(c)(1)(iv) as the provision governing Rompilla’s 1988 penalty verdict. The official text’s amendment history and the statutory recitation in Sattazahn, 537 U.S. 101, 104 (2003), confirm the operative language; Sattazahn is used solely as a statutory-text cross-check, with no imported federal holding or vote. The current official page is a text-verification source, not later substantive law.

The legal distinction is between the unanimous statutory agreement required for a death verdict and each juror’s individual assessment of mitigation. Paragraph (v) permits the trial court, in its discretion, to discharge a jury when it concludes further deliberations will not yield unanimity; upon that discharge, the court must impose life. The permission to discharge and obligation after discharge remain distinct. This source verification does not itself decide Strickland prejudice or impose a numerical burden beyond its reasonable-probability standard.

## Exact official statutory text, copied mechanically

Source: [42 Pa. Cons. Stat. §9711(c)(1)(iii)–(v)](https://www.legis.state.pa.us/WU01/LI/LI/CT/HTM/42/00.097.011.000..HTM); retained source: sources/chunk9/pa_9711_official.html.

(iii) Aggravating circumstances must be proved by the Commonwealth beyond a reasonable doubt; mitigating circumstances must be proved by the defendant by a preponderance of the evidence. (iv) The verdict must be a sentence of death if the jury unanimously finds at least one aggravating circumstance specified in subsection (d) and no mitigating circumstance or if the jury unanimously finds one or more aggravating circumstances which outweigh any mitigating circumstances. The verdict must be a sentence of life imprisonment in all other cases. (v) The court may, in its discretion, discharge the jury if it is of the opinion that further deliberation will not result in a unanimous agreement as to the sentence, in which case the court shall sentence the defendant to life imprisonment.

## Exact date-eligible state construction, copied mechanically

Source: [Commonwealth v. Frey, 520 Pa.338](https://static.case.law/pa/520/cases/0338-01.json); retained source: sources/chunk9/frey_state_1989.json.

The present instructions did not express a need for unanimity in determining the existence of mitigating circumstances. (N.T. 1388-91). Nor did the form of the verdict slip infer a need for such unanimity. The jury instructions and verdict slip closely followed language in the sentencing statute stating that a unanimous verdict is necessary as to the ultimate decision to impose a sentence of death, 42 Pa.C.S. § 9711(c)(l)(v), but did not state or infer a requirement that any given mitigating circumstance must be unanimously recognized before it can be weighed against aggravating circumstances in reaching a verdict. See 42 Pa.C.S. § 9711(c)(l)(iv) (“[T]he verdict must be a sentence of death if the jury unanimously finds at least one aggravating circumstance specified in subsection (d) and no mitigating circumstance or if the jury unanimously finds one or more aggravating circumstances which outweigh any mitigating circumstances.”). Thus, individual jurors were free to weigh whatever mitigating circumstances they perceived, regardless of whether other jurors agreed that those circumstances were established by the evidence. The perceptions of even one juror, alone, with regard to mitigating circumstances, would be sufficient to deny the unanimity required for a sentence of death.
