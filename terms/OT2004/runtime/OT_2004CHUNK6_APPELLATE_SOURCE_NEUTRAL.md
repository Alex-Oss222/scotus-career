## California Public Employees' Retirement System v. WorldCom, Inc., 368 F.3d 86

**Identification and posture.** Second Circuit No. 04-0219; argued February 26, 2004; decided May 11, 2004. The appeal was an accepted interlocutory appeal under 28 U.S.C. §1292(b) from the Southern District of New York's May 5 and May 20, 2003 orders denying remand and reconsideration. A prior mandamus petition had been denied because interlocutory appeal remained an adequate route and the asserted right was not clear and indisputable. The operative certified issue was whether individual Securities Act of 1933 claims could be removed under §1452(a) notwithstanding Securities Act §22(a), 15 U.S.C. §77v(a). The Second Circuit affirmed the district court on that statutory conflict. It did not adjudicate the securities claims themselves. (Opinion paragraphs 13–20, 80.)

**Parties and removal basis.** The appellants were state and private pension funds that bought WorldCom bonds and brought individual state-court actions asserting 1933 Act claims, rather than joining the securities class action or alleging 1934 Act claims. After WorldCom sought Chapter 11 protection on July 21, 2002, the automatic stay prevented litigation against WorldCom from proceeding; the actions proceeded against nondebtor defendants. Underwriters and directors invoked contribution, indemnification, and contractual reimbursement rights against the estate as grounds for related-to bankruptcy jurisdiction. The removed cases were centralized for pretrial proceedings with the securities litigation before Judge Cote. NYCERS's separate action also contained New York-law claims; that action furnished the initial remand decision, and the Bondholders were allowed to intervene on remand. (Paragraphs 4–12; notes 4, 6, 7.)

**Express jurisdictional assumption.** The Second Circuit did not hold that these particular securities claims actually satisfied related-to jurisdiction. The district court declined to certify that question; the appellate court recognized its power to address issues fairly included in a certified order under Yamaha Motor Corp. v. Calhoun, but declined to exercise it here. The contribution issue might not control because directors' indemnification and payment of their legal fees could supply alternative grounds; disputes about near-zero valuation of estate claims were also substantially factual. The court assumed the relationship solely for the statutory-removability appeal and expressly intimated no view on it. The district court's application of the conceivable-effect test is a lower-court finding, not an appellate resolution of the predicate. (Paragraphs 19–24; note 11.)

**Grounds of the judgment below.** The court treated §22(a) and §1452(a) as containing conflicting categorical language. Enumeration of exceptions alone did not decide the conflict because each statute had express exceptions. Neither statute covered merely a subset of the other's claims, so the specific-over-general canon did not establish §22(a)'s priority. The 1998 SLUSA amendments concerned covered class actions and did not establish a new absolute choice of state forum for individual claims. The decisive structural comparison was with §1441(a), which begins with an express reservation for other Acts of Congress; §1452(a) lacks that reservation. The court reasoned that importing it would erase an intentional difference and make the reservation in §1441(a) unnecessary. It also invoked the centralization and efficiency purposes of bankruptcy jurisdiction. Gonsalves supplied an analogy concerning additional removal authority, but its relevant proposition was expressly acknowledged to be dictum; its plaintiff's voluntary joinder of removable claims was not attributed to these Bondholders. (Paragraphs 52–79; notes 13, 18, 19.)

**Exceptions and qualifications to preserve.** Section 1452(a) excludes a proceeding before the United States Tax Court and a civil action by a governmental unit to enforce that unit's police or regulatory power. It does not exclude every action involving government. Removal also requires district-court jurisdiction over the claim under §1334 and must be to the district court for the district where the civil action is pending. Section 22(a)'s exception refers to §77p(c); the exact covered-class-action, covered-security and state-law requirements are supplied by the official statutory sources, and the shorthand used in parts of the lower opinion must not broaden them. Neither permission to remove nor the court's assumed related-to predicate commands removal in every case. The opinion does not decide equitable remand, mandatory abstention, the merits of contribution or indemnity, or ultimate securities liability.

**Remedial and procedural boundary.** The appellate affirmance sustains rejection of the §22(a) objection to removal on the certified question. It is not a judgment that all other grounds for remand fail. An earlier February 3, 2004 appellate order had extended the class opt-out deadline and the accompanying deadline for voluntary dismissal of individual actions until at least 30 days after issuance of the appellate mandate. The opinion recounts that order without converting it into a new merits remedy. (Paragraph 18.)

## Consumers Energy Co. v. FERC, 367 F.3d 915

**Identification and posture.** D.C. Circuit No. 03-1162; argued March 8, 2004; decided May 14, 2004. Consumers petitioned for review of FERC's April 2002 approval, September 2002 rehearing order, and April 2003 rehearing order concerning Ontario Energy Trading International's application under Federal Power Act §205, 16 U.S.C. §824d, to sell energy, capacity and ancillary services and resell transmission capacity at market-based rates. The court denied the petition. It did not direct Ontario's regulator to adopt a United States tariff. (Official opinion pages 3, 9–11, 16.)

**Corporate and market facts.** Ontario's restructuring separated generation in Ontario Power Generation, ownership of transmission in Hydro One, and operation of the bulk system and market in the Independent Electricity Market Operator. Ontario Energy was OPG's wholly owned marketing subsidiary and owned no generation or transmission assets itself. Provincial ownership and director appointments supported the affiliation dispute. FERC initially found no relevant affiliate, but on rehearing agreed with Consumers that Ontario Energy and the IMO were affiliated. That favorable affiliation finding made examination of transmission comparability necessary; it did not resolve comparability adversely to the applicant. (Pages 4–6, 10.)

**Concrete dispute.** Consumers requested a firm 50 MW reservation for May 2002 to move power bought in New York through Ontario to Michigan. The IMO refused the reservation because its market did not offer separate transmission reservations; a participant instead sells power into one intertie and buys power out at another. Without intertie congestion, the two energy transactions offset at the common market price, leaving an export fee. With congestion, an intertie zone price can differ from that market price. Financial transmission rights, purchased separately for import and export at the relevant interties and auctioned for monthly or yearly periods, provide payments equal to those price differences. They are financial hedges, not physical reservations of transmission capacity. (Pages 6–10.)

**Grounds of the judgment below.** The Federal Power Act requires just and reasonable rates, and FERC may permit market-based rates where the seller and affiliates lack market power or have adequately mitigated it and cannot erect entry barriers. FERC requires foreign affiliates to provide comparable nondiscriminatory access to United States markets, while assessing comparability case by case rather than demanding identical pro forma tariffs from utilities outside its direct jurisdiction. The court reviewed the decision for arbitrariness or other legal error and treated supported factual findings as conclusive under §825l(b). It accepted FERC's explanation that paired market transactions and FTRs could provide through-and-out service at a price known in advance, despite a different institutional mechanism. All users had to undertake the same sell-in/buy-out transaction. (Pages 11–14.)

**Evidence and express limits.** Consumers conceded absence of evidence that the IMO operated discriminatorily. FERC found that at least 12 United States marketers had traded successfully through and out of Ontario, that OPG had no generation market power outside Ontario, and that the alleged manipulation of export congestion was speculative and unsupported by identified higher costs, identified instances, or discriminatory episodes. The court also reasoned that FTRs could offset congestion price effects and that increasing export costs would harm OPG's own ability to compete outside Ontario. These are conclusions based on the agency record in this case, not a statutory command that every foreign bid-based market be deemed comparable or a finding that discrimination could never occur. The statutory obligation of just and reasonable rates must remain distinct from the agency's findings about how this market operated. (Pages 14–15.)

**Unpreserved component.** Consumers also invoked Order No. 2000's independence requirements to attack comparability. Its agency submissions had used independence to establish affiliation, a point on which it won. They did not separately argue that a lack of independence prevented comparable service. Because FERC never had an opportunity to address that distinct theory on rehearing, the court declined to consider it under §825l(b), characterizing the limit as jurisdictional. The full statutory sentence preserves an exception when there is reasonable ground for failure to raise the objection on rehearing; the lower opinion's abbreviated quotation does not erase that exception. The opinion does not identify a successful reasonable-ground showing here. (Page 16; official 2004 §825l(b).)

**Statutory details and remedy.** Under §825l(a), an aggrieved party may seek rehearing within 30 days, specifying its grounds; agency inaction for 30 days permits the application to be deemed denied. Section 825l(b) permits judicial review within 60 days of the rehearing order, authorizes affirmance, modification or setting aside, and makes facts supported by substantial evidence conclusive. Its separate additional-evidence provision requires material evidence and reasonable grounds for failure to adduce it before the agency, and permits the court to order the evidence taken before the Commission; this is distinct from the exception for an unpreserved objection. Section 825l(c) provides that rehearing and judicial-review proceedings do not themselves stay an order absent a specific agency or court order. The denial of review left the challenged market-rate authorization in place; no refund, damages award, compelled reservation, or replacement tariff was ordered.

## Monsanto Co. v. McFarling, 363 F.3d 1336

**Identification and precise judgment.** Federal Circuit Nos. 03-1177 and 03-1228; decided April 9, 2004. The Eastern District of Missouri entered a partial final judgment under Rule 54(b) on Monsanto's contract claim and against McFarling on counterclaims. The appeal upheld contract liability and rejection of the asserted misuse, antitrust and PVPA defenses/counterclaim; vacated the $780,000 contractual damages award; and remanded for actual damages and consistent further proceedings, with no costs. The patent claims were not themselves final claims in this appeal. The district court had granted liability summary judgment on the '605 patent, leaving its damages for trial; Monsanto had not moved for summary judgment on the '435 patent. (Paragraphs 1, 14–19, 62–65; note 1.)

**Agreed conduct and contract.** McFarling operated a 5,000-acre Mississippi farm, signed the 1998 Technology Agreement and purchased 1,000 bags of Roundup Ready soybean seed. He conceded saving 1,500 bushels from the 1998 crop and replanting them in 1999; he then saved 3,075 bags from the 1999 crop and replanted them in 2000. The agreement permitted one-season commercial planting, prohibited supplying seed to others for planting and saving the harvested crop for replanting, and also restricted breeding, research, herbicide-registration-data generation and seed production. The damages clause applied a 120 multiplier to the applicable technology fee for saving, supplying, selling or acquiring seed for replant contrary to the agreement. The district court interpreted the fee base as purchased bags, giving 120 × $6.50 × 1,000 = $780,000. It had rejected Monsanto's proposed base of replanted bags as an unlawful penalty. (Paragraphs 5–17.)

**Misuse and tying as framed below.** McFarling sought to replant genetically modified second-generation seeds while paying Monsanto a technology fee, rather than purchasing new seed annually. He did not claim inability to buy unmodified soybeans or seek the seed-company license for inserting the genetic trait into seeds. The court treated his theory as a demand for a license to use the patented second-generation product. It rejected Monsanto's characterization of the restriction simply as a field-of-use limit on first-generation seeds: the seeds were planted identically whether their harvest was sold for consumption or replanted. The decisive ground was that the '435 patent was presumed to cover the nearly identical first and subsequent generations; restricting use of the patented second-generation seed therefore did not enlarge the patent's scope. The opinion expressly did not establish a general rule for goods produced by a licensed product that do not incorporate the patented invention. (Paragraphs 20–26.)

**Antitrust and statutory defenses.** The tying counterclaim failed on the same asserted conduct within the patent grant. The appellate court agreed that distinct-product analysis ordinarily turns on consumer demand and that the district court had not examined that demand, but expressly declined to review the district court's single-market finding because it was unnecessary to the appellate ground. The PVPA argument was that its saved-seed protection invalidated restrictions in utility-patent licenses; the lower court rejected that premise based on the separate statutory schemes and its earlier ruling. Those lower-court grounds are reported here as the grounds under review, not as a resolution of any issue under the simulator's entering law. (Paragraphs 27–30.)

**Preservation boundary.** Patent validity had not been challenged in the answer as described by the opinion. A post-argument letter argued that the '435 patent might later be attacked in the unfinished infringement claim. The court held the validity challenge waived for this appeal because McFarling had not raised it as a contract or misuse defense in the partial-final-judgment proceedings. This is not an affirmative validity adjudication for every later proceeding. (Paragraph 14; note 1.)

**Missouri damages test, including the alternative.** A valid liquidated sum must reasonably forecast compensable harm and address harm difficult to estimate accurately. The forecast is ordinarily assessed at contracting; the anticipated difficulty is difficulty proving the loss when breach is discovered, not merely uncertainty at contracting about whether or how breach will occur. The opinion acknowledges Missouri decisions allowing reasonableness by comparison either to anticipated loss or to actual loss. It does not reject that alternative: it says the $780,000 award was grossly disproportionate to actual lost technology fees and Monsanto did not defend the award on actual-loss reasonableness, leaving the forecast route as the only possible support in this case. (Paragraphs 31–40; note 2.)

**Reasons the multiplier failed.** The uniform formula applied to several crops with differing replication rates and to breaches with differing expected harms. The record gave soybean replication as 36-to-1 and cotton as 120-to-1, but no corn replication rate. Saving seed for replanting on a finite farm was distinguishable from distributing seed to unidentified third parties: the former permitted easier measurement and injunctive control, while the latter could spread over much wider acreage and be harder to trace. Measuring the award by purchased rather than replanted or transferred bags further broke its connection to harm. Brand harm from knowingly replanting was not shown to justify this multiplier; deterring other growers was punitive rather than compensatory; ordinary lost technology fees alone supported no 120-fold increase. (Paragraphs 41–61; note 3.)

**Remedy limits and reserved questions.** The appellate court remanded calculation of actual damages based on saved and replanted bags. It did not fix the ultimate actual-damages amount or approve another multiplier. It expressed no opinion whether a clause could reasonably forecast loss from supplying or selling saved seed to third parties, and did not need to decide the separate acquiring-seed breach. Monsanto retained its request for injunctive relief; its explanation at argument limited the claimed liquidated sum to past harm through detection and judicial relief rather than perpetual future use. The court noted Missouri's bar on double recovery for the same injury. Neither the vacatur of the penalty nor the affirmation of contract liability resolves the unfinished patent damages claim. (Paragraphs 50, 52, 55, 62–65; note 4.)
