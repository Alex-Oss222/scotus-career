## Reconciled common disposition and statutory rule

Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer would vacate the Federal Circuit's judgment on the reviewed statutory-defense determination and remand for review of the preserved verdict challenge under the correct construction and admissible trial record. No Associate changes the fresh judgment commitment.

Section 271(e)(1) protects the enumerated making, using, offering to sell or selling within the United States, and importation into the United States, of a patented invention solely for uses reasonably related to developing and submitting information under the covered federal regulatory law. Its animal-product exception concerns a new animal drug or veterinary biological product primarily manufactured through recombinant DNA, recombinant RNA, hybridoma technology or other processes involving site-specific genetic manipulation. That distinct exception is not implicated by the human-drug candidates presented here.

The reasonable-relationship condition includes qualifying preclinical work on drug safety, efficacy, mechanism of action, pharmacology and pharmacokinetics. At least where the drugmaker reasonably believes that a patented compound may act through an identified biological process to produce the intended physiological effect, and uses it in experiments that, if successful, would yield information appropriate for FDA submission, the use meets the statutory relationship. The assessment concerns what was reasonably supported when the use occurred. A generic-drug limitation, clinical-trial prerequisite, successful-candidate requirement or requirement that every experiment ultimately appear in an IND or NDA would exclude information development that Congress's terms encompass.

The statute does not shelter basic inquiry resting only on the speculative prospect that scientific knowledge might someday aid drug approval. Nor does a funded regulatory project establish the exemption for every use made within it. Each challenged activity must satisfy the statutory relationship on its own evidence. Commercial aims do not themselves defeat a qualifying regulatory use; they do not excuse the absence of one. These propositions give operative content to the enacted condition, rather than creating a mandatory research-phase sequence or a general judicial research privilege.

## Regulatory explanation and preserved qualifications

An IND precedes human trials and includes preclinical information bearing on whether the proposed trial creates an unreasonable risk. That inquiry can consider expected therapeutic benefit as well as safety. An NDA also includes preclinical information relevant to safety and effectiveness. Accordingly, efficacy, mechanism and related preclinical work cannot be excluded merely because it is not a direct human-safety test. Development involves trial and error; abandonment of a candidate or exclusion of an experiment's results from the ultimate submission does not retrospectively make a reasonably connected use infringing.

Integra's strongest objection is that an unlimited development exemption would consume commercially valuable patent rights because almost any scientific work might eventually help a regulated product. The objective, use-specific relationship answers that objection. Its good-laboratory-practice objection also does not establish a categorical bar. The applicable good-laboratory-practice rules concern safety studies, not preclinical efficacy, mechanism, pharmacology or pharmacokinetics as such. For a safety study subject to those rules, the IND provision permits submission accompanied by a brief explanation of noncompliance. These qualifications appear in the previously supplied clean merits source, footnote 9, and are confirmed by the official opinion at 204–205; they are not newly reconstructed facts or a finding that a particular experiment qualifies.

The Federal Circuit's error must be stated precisely. Although some language contrasted clinical testing with general biomedical research, it recognized that information for an IND could qualify and therefore did not exclude every preclinical study. Its restrictive treatment of candidate selection and information not ultimately submitted failed to give the reasonable-development relationship its statutory scope. Correcting that reasoning does not establish that the jury received a categorical clinical-only instruction.

## Verdict posture and complete remedy

The consented-to instruction required Merck to prove by a preponderance an objectively reasonable prospect that the accused activities would contribute relatively directly to kinds of information likely relevant to FDA approval. It required separate evaluation of the accused activities and expressly made actual submission unnecessary. That instruction is consistent with the common construction, though less detailed. The jury rejected the defense and the District Court denied judgment as a matter of law. The Federal Circuit must review the preserved sufficiency challenge under the correct construction rather than the restrictive premises rejected here. The Court does not conduct that first appellate review itself.
