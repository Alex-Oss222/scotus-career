## California Public Employees' Retirement System v. WorldCom, Inc.

### Judgment, form and sufficient rule

Each Associate votes to reverse the Second Circuit's rejection of the Securities Act nonremoval objection and remand with instructions to effect return of the covered individual Securities Act actions to the state courts. The agreed form is a signed opinion resolving the statutory forum question after plenary merits submission. The author is for assembly. Eight Associates support the following complete common ground.

Section 22(a), 15 U.S.C. section 77v(a), prohibits removal of a Securities Act case brought in a state court of competent jurisdiction except as section 77p(c) provides. Section 1452(a)'s permission to remove a claim within section 1334 jurisdiction does not displace that express prohibition. Accordingly, the individual Securities Act actions here cannot be removed under section 1452(a), even accepting for this certified question that the asserted indemnification, contribution or reimbursement connection would otherwise satisfy section 1334.

The provisions can operate together. Section 1452 makes bankruptcy-related removal available over a broad range of civil claims; section 22(a) withdraws removal for an identified category in specified circumstances. Giving effect to the withdrawal does not repeal section 1452 or undermine its jurisdictional grant. Conversely, interpreting the general permission to override the prohibition would deprive section 22(a)'s words of effect in the overlap without an enacted bankruptcy exception. This conclusion rests on the interaction of a permission and an express prohibition, not on a claim that one statute is more specific along every possible axis. Securities subject matter and bankruptcy connection crosscut; that observation does not resolve the operative words.

The absence from section 1452(a) of section 1441(a)'s introductory reservation for other Acts is the strongest contrary textual argument. It does establish that the removal provisions differ. It does not itself enact the proposition that every other congressional prohibition ceases to apply. Section 1452 already differs in who may remove, what is removable, and its jurisdictional condition and stated exclusions. Section 22(a) speaks to removal to any federal court and is not textually limited to removal under section 1441. The 1998 enactment expressly identified section 77p(c) as an exception while preserving the nonremoval command. The Court need not infer a new congressional guarantee from legislative silence; the enacted command remains the source of the result.

The class-action exception retains all its actual conditions. Section 77p(c) concerns covered class actions involving covered securities as specified in section 77p(b), including its state-law predicate. A state or political subdivision or state pension plan may bring its own protected action under section 77p(d)(2); the entity-counting rule treats a qualifying entity as one person unless it was established to participate in the action. The identified individual actions cannot be reclassified from their purchasers' aggregate beneficiaries alone. The result here follows the supplied individual-action status. It does not require a general construction of every section 77p exception.

The certified appellate ruling assumed actual related-to jurisdiction and expressly declined to decide it. The district court's finding remains a lower-court determination, not a Second Circuit holding of actual nexus. The statutory prohibition supplies the complete resolution even on the assumed nexus. The appellate route concerns legal removal authority under section 1292(b), not an exercise of equitable remand or an equitable refusal to remand under section 1452(b). The latter subsection's no-review command covers both kinds of equitable decision; an opinion must not say that every denial of remand is reviewable.

## Exxon Mobil Corp. v. Saudi Basic Industries Corp.

### Judgment, common rule and application

Each Associate votes to reverse the Third Circuit's jurisdictional dismissal and remand. A signed opinion after plenary submission is appropriate; author unassigned. All eight join the following rule and application in full.

Rooker and Feldman preclude district-court appellate reassessment when a state-court loser brings a federal action after the state judgment, complains of an injury caused by that judgment, and seeks its review and rejection. An original federal action asserting an independently actionable wrong does not lose jurisdiction merely because a parallel state proceeding later reaches judgment. A qualifying state judgment instead receives the preclusive effect required by section 1738, under the State's applicable preclusion rules; abstention and any other jurisdictional defense retain their own requirements.

Exxon filed its federal action before the Delaware judgment, sought relief for the underlying royalty conduct, and prevailed in the state trial. The later judgment did not turn that already pending original suit into an appeal from a state-court loss. Similar subject matter, potentially overlapping relief and a possible preclusion consequence do not establish the missing appellate character. Section 1257 allocates review of state judgments; it does not erase an originally filed district-court action whenever state litigation wins a race to judgment.

The federal complaint invoked section 1330 and the case included SABIC's immunity defense. Rejecting the state-judgment ground therefore requires return for the actual remaining immunity and procedural questions, not a declaration that every jurisdictional prerequisite has been conclusively established. The lower court must consider any live immunity, preclusion and mootness issues under their proper standards and actual pleadings and judgments. The neutral packet does not provide the record needed to determine them conclusively. This is factual and issue-specific remand work, not an unresolved legal question about the state-judgment bar.

### Alternative paths and individual commitments

Path A reverses the jurisdictional dismissal and directs ordinary adjudication of surviving defenses. Its strongest objection is the State's interest in finality and avoidance of duplicative proceedings. Section 1738 and the established abstention rules answer that concern without transforming a district court into an appellate court or changing the nature of a complaint retroactively.

Path B treats the state judgment as extinguishing federal subject-matter jurisdiction because a later federal ruling would revisit the same controversy. Its strongest support is an expansive reading of Feldman's interconnected-claim language. It fails here because it discards the source of the injury, the nature of the requested relief and the commencement sequence, contrary to Jordahl's adopted claim-specific boundary. A genuinely pleaded appellate attack would require a different application of the same rule; this supplied complaint is an independent conduct-based action.

Rooker and Feldman retain their district-court appellate-review boundary. The jurisdictional dismissal is reversed; the federal proceedings return for the properly presented remaining defenses and live claims. It should not list absent damages, possible future appellate attacks, or other unrequested relief. This disposition fully answers the question presented and supplies the legal rule for the remand.

## Rhines v. Weber

### Judgment and adopted operative rule

All eight Associates vote to vacate the Eighth Circuit's restrictive stay ruling and remand for application of a bounded stay standard. They favor a signed opinion after plenary submission, author unassigned. They support the existence of stay authority, the conditions below, and the remand; the clarification recorded below for Stevens, Souter, Ginsburg and Breyer is part of their scope of assent.

A district court may retain a timely mixed section 2254 petition and stay federal proceedings while the petitioner exhausts available state remedies. The court must exercise that power consistently with AEDPA's filing limit, state exhaustion and prompt resolution. The petitioner must give good cause for failing to exhaust before the federal filing; the unexhausted claims must not be plainly meritless; and the petitioner must not have engaged in intentionally dilatory litigation tactics or abusive delay. The stay must include reasonable definite limits for initiating state exhaustion and returning to federal court after its completion. Good cause requires a reasonable explanation assessed in the actual circumstances, rather than the extraordinary-circumstances threshold governing some distinct forms of relief; the mere existence of a mixed petition or expiration of time does not establish it.

When those conditions are satisfied and dismissal would jeopardize timely federal review, denying a stay and dismissing the entire mixed petition abuses discretion. When a stay is unavailable, the petitioner must have an opportunity to delete the unexhausted claims and proceed with the exhausted claims if dismissal of the entire petition would unreasonably impair the right to obtain federal relief. Section 2254(b)(2) separately permits denial of an unexhausted claim on its merits; it does not make denial compulsory.

The state-remedy duty remains operative: section 2254(b) permits a grant without exhaustion only when no state corrective process is available or circumstances render that process ineffective to protect the applicant's rights, and counsel must expressly waive exhaustion for the State. A stay directs exhaustion where remedies remain available; it is not a decision granting relief on unexhausted claims. The federal petition's timely filing and continued pendency matter. Under Duncan, the period spent in federal court is not excluded by section 2244(d)(2); retaining this pending action is a different mechanism from making a later application timely.

Rhines filed with more than eleven months remaining, and the limitations period expired before the court identified eight unexhausted claims. The existing order required initiation of state proceedings and federal return within sixty days at each end. Those conditions demonstrate a bounded procedural device, but the supplied record does not conclusively establish cause, potential merit or absence of intentional delay for all eight claims. The district court must make those determinations and retain, modify or decline a stay under the rule. Its deadlines must remain reasonable in the actual exhaustion process. The Court does not replace them with a nationally fixed number of days.

### Enhanced alternatives

Path A requires dismissal or deletion under a near-categorical no-stay reading of Rose and AEDPA, subject only to separately established equitable remedies. Its strongest argument is that routine retention rewards premature filing and bypasses the incentives Congress created. Its decisive weakness is that neither the statutory exhaustion rule nor the limitation provision commands terminating every timely pending action; permanent loss of otherwise available federal review can result from ordinary judicial processing after timely filing.

Path B recognizes retention subject to cause, potential merit, prompt exhaustion and rejection of abuse. Its strongest objection is the risk of discretionary conditions becoming either automatic stays or an undefined second procedural default rule. The stated reasonable-explanation standard and definite deadlines answer those objections. The requirements perform separate work: explanation concerns premature filing; potential merit prevents holding a useless claim; delay controls abuse and finality; deadlines supervise the exhaustion interval.

Path C would generally retain a timely petition whenever a claim is not plainly meritless and there is no abusive delay, without a separate cause requirement. Stevens, Souter, Ginsburg and Breyer regard this as a lawful broader position supported by their prior concern for preserving first federal review. They accept B with the express reasonable-explanation formulation; it gives the court a concrete assessment without importing the demanding external-impediment showing of procedural default. A draft replacing that formulation with extraordinary circumstances or a rigid cause-and-prejudice requirement would exceed their joins and require fresh compatibility review. O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy and Thomas support B's independent cause requirement and do not join automatic or presumptive retention merely from timely filing.

## Smith v. City of Jackson

### Component dispositions and common ground

All eight Associates vote to affirm the judgment on the reviewed disparate-impact branch. They divide on the existence of impact liability. Stevens, Scalia, Souter, Ginsburg and Breyer recognize section 623(a)(2) impact liability and affirm because the supplied pay-plan differentiation is based on reasonable factors other than age. O'Connor, Kennedy and Thomas affirm on the categorical ground that the ADEA does not authorize the asserted impact theory. The latter three do not join recognition or the ensuing construction as an alternative Court holding merely because the judgment agrees.

The appropriate form is a signed lead opinion with the actual component joins and a substantive separate writing explaining the no-impact ground. No author is selected. A Scalia concurrence has the distinct function described below. The surviving intentional-treatment branch remains on the appellate court's separate remand for discovery; an impact affirmance must not be expressed as final dismissal of all employment claims.

### Recognition coalition: complete operative rule and application

Section 623(a)(2) permits employees to challenge a facially neutral employment practice that causes a disadvantage to older workers of the kind described in that provision, without proof that age motivated the practice. The employee must identify the particular practice causing the disparate effect and establish that causal connection; an age imbalance standing alone does not identify its cause. Section 623(f)(1) excludes a differentiation based on reasonable factors other than age. The statutory standard is reasonableness of the actual nonage factors, not the amended Title VII requirement of business necessity or a duty to choose the least disparate alternative.

The recognition ground rests on the effects-oriented language concerning limitation, segregation or classification adversely affecting employees' opportunities or status, its parallel to the language construed in Griggs, and the independent work performed by the express reasonable-factors qualification. The words because of age require the age-linked adverse effect and do not erase the distinct effects formulation. Hazen Paper requires actual age motivation for disparate treatment and expressly reserved impact; it is neither an impact holding nor an obstacle to deciding the reserved question now. Cline determines the direction of statutory protection and does not settle the mechanism of liability.

The qualification matters precisely because age can correlate with reasonable employment factors such as experience and service. Griggs supplies the analogy for effects-based liability, but it does not authorize deleting this statute's different qualification. Wards Cove supplies the need to identify the actual challenged practice and causal disparity; the later Title VII amendments did not amend the ADEA. The current section 1625.7 regulation supports recognition of impact as the agency's considered interpretation. Its business-necessity phrase cannot replace Congress's reasonable-factors text. Christensen and Mead require attention to actual interpretive authority; neither makes every agency sentence controlling or forecloses persuasive consideration.

On the supplied facts, the City's regional-market adjustment and allocation through job rank, existing salary and tenure are reasonable nonage compensation factors. The City sought to bring starting police salaries to the regional average, used wage ranges derived from comparable southeastern communities, and placed each officer at the lowest step providing at least a two-percent raise. Lower existing salaries naturally produce larger percentage increases for a comparable adjustment. The highest ranks, occupied only by officers over forty, received larger dollar increases despite smaller percentages. Some over-forty officers were within the shorter-tenure group. These features substantiate a market-and-compensation structure rather than a rule that any asserted cost saving automatically qualifies.

For purposes of the impact disposition, the Court may assume that the challenged allocation caused the reported statistical disparity. The figures showing more under-forty officers above a ten-percent increase do not make the actual regional adjustment unreasonable. The officers' demand for more equal percentages or a different workable distribution does not turn a reasonable nonage method into an ADEA violation. The City therefore prevails on the reasonable-factors ground on these supplied undisputed mechanics, without requiring a finding that the employees failed to identify any practice.

This is a decision on a ground the neutral packet identifies as the City's actual alternative request, tested against the supplied plan facts and the officers' challenge. The absence of original papers in the research files is not evidence that the officers lacked an opportunity to contest that position. No concession, waiver, service event or adverse credibility determination is invented. The source limitation concerning practice identification is honored by declining to use an unverified judicial characterization as a second dispositive fact. The proof allocation of every future contested reasonable-factors case is not needed to explain this case's sufficiently established nonage justification and should not appear as a gratuitous reservation.

### No-impact coalition: sufficient competing rule

O'Connor, Kennedy and Thomas construe the ADEA's discrimination-because-of-age language as requiring an age-based employment decision, with section 623(a)(2) describing prohibited forms and consequences of that discrimination rather than creating a separate unintentional-effects cause. They regard the reasonable-factors language as confirming permission to use nonage criteria and distinguishing age from correlated characteristics. They reject the inference that this language would otherwise have no work: it can clarify the scope of forbidden age distinctions and the legal significance of legitimate nonage differentiation. Griggs's Title VII interpretation does not settle a statute with distinct text, structure and age-related employment concerns. They would affirm the impact branch categorically while preserving the separately pending treatment claim.

The recognition coalition's answer is that the effects-oriented subsection and reasonable-factors qualification must be construed together, rather than reducing both subsections to a single motivation prohibition. That answer should appear concisely in the controlling explanation because it meets the separate writing's principal legal ground.
