## 2004-12-13 — Brosseau v. Haugen; No. 03-1261.

Justice Stevens dissents. He would affirm the appellate reversal of summary judgment and return the claim for ordinary proceedings under existing law. Garner already gives the governing rule: escape alone does not justify deadly force without the requisite serious-threat basis. On the assumed clear route, stationary Jeep and absence of an identified immediate threat, the proposed future danger does not make that boundary uncertain. Smith and Cole involved materially more dangerous driving and therefore do not erase Garner's notice here. He regards the officer's reasonable assessment of the possible accident and the surrounding circumstances as matters for lawful adjudication, not a reason to end the claim on this assumed account. His position applies the existing defense; it does not make every historical-fact dispute defeat a separable legal immunity ruling.

Chief Justice Stone-Zsela separately dissents. He would expressly replace the broad objective legal-notice defense with a defense of actual reasonable reliance on authoritative law and affirm the removal of summary judgment. An official invoking his proposed defense would have to prove, by a preponderance, identified binding precedent or enacted authority directly authorizing the conduct, genuine reliance on it, and a reasonable basis for treating it as valid and applicable. Legal silence, a department's practice and the absence of a materially identical case would not suffice. A policy or supervisor's instruction would qualify only insofar as it accurately conveyed such identified authority and the official reasonably relied on it.

He reasons that Graham's complete force standard already accommodates urgency, reasonable factual mistakes and lawful discretion. An additional defense that can defeat an established violation without actual reliance on law authorizing the conduct is disconnected from the justification for protecting legal reliance. His proposal would therefore replace the broader Harlow–Anderson rule, preserve Graham and Garner in the force inquiry, and retain Hope's rejection of factual identity. Saucier did not previously adopt that replacement. Brosseau has shown neither directly authorizing authority nor actual reasonable reliance on this record. Further proceedings would apply the complete force rule, resolve genuinely material facts and any supported reliance defense, and require the remaining causation, injury and damages predicates. The proposal concerns the personal defense within the existing cause of action; absolute functional immunity, sovereign immunity and independently sourced institutional liability are outside it. The ultimate force merits remain outside the limited grant.

## 2004-12-13 — Kowalski v. Tesmer, No. 03-407.

GINSBURG, J., dissenting, joined by STEVENS and SOUTER, JJ. The dissent would permit these attorneys' third-party route and return only for genuinely unresolved preserved matters within the limited review. A present-versus-future-client distinction does not adequately examine a law that prevents the very representation on which a claim depends. The attorneys regularly accept appellate appointments through strict-rotation rosters, intend to continue that practice, and allege reduced paid assignments caused by the challenged restriction. Taken under the governing pleading standard, those allegations support a concrete personal economic stake as well as the setting in which the prospective representation arises.

The dissent reads *Craig v. Boren* and *Singleton v. Wulff*, which permit sufficiently direct prospective relationships, together with *Campbell v. Louisiana* and *Miller v. Albright*, which require attention to the actual claimant connection and enforcement obstacle. Here the intended assistance and the constitutional right are directly linked: indigent applicants must assert a right to appellate counsel while proceeding without that counsel. That practical barrier gives the attorneys' recurring appointment relationship legal significance beyond generalized professional concern. The dissent would not require new injury allegations merely because future clients remain unidentified. It would leave genuinely unresolved Article III or other preserved matters to their actual procedural standards and preserve the individual plaintiffs' separate abstention disposition. It does not decide the constitutional appointment entitlement.

## 2005-01-12 — United States v. Booker; No. 04-104; consolidated with United States v. Fanfan, No. 04-105.

O'Connor, joined by Kennedy and Breyer, dissents from the constitutional holding. Their earlier Angle positions distinguish the statutory offense limits from rules regulating judicial selection within them. Mistretta's institutional sentencing arrangement and the established judicial sentencing role support reconsidering the extension of element protections to those internal constraints. They would permit the challenged judicial findings, reverse Booker's constitutional ruling and return Fanfan for ordinary mandatory-Guidelines sentencing under that preferred rule. They recognize that Angle governs unless reconsidered; they do not treat the federal question as previously unanswered.

Breyer's separate Part I explains his additional constitutional position. He finds no coherent logical distinction between a fact raising a mandatory minimum and one raising a maximum, yet opposes extending the element requirement to the sentencing system in either form. That disagreement concerns the constitutional allocation itself rather than an exception consistent with Angle or Harris.

In Part II, Breyer is joined by O'Connor, Kennedy and Ginsburg. Given the Court's constitutional holding, they would sever §3553(b)(1), §3742(e) and necessarily incompatible cross-references, preserving guided advisory sentencing. Courts would remain required to consider the Guidelines and policy statements, offense and offender circumstances, lawful alternatives, seriousness, respect for law, just punishment, deterrence, public protection, effective correctional needs, avoidance of unwarranted disparities and restitution. The sentence would have to be sufficient but no greater than necessary for the applicable statutory purposes, accompanied by reasons and reasonableness review. They would affirm the setting aside of Booker's sentence and resentence him under that system, but vacate Fanfan's sentence for advisory reconsideration even though he suffered no constitutional enhancement error. Ginsburg joins the Court's constitutional rule and this remedial position; the other three retain their constitutional objections.

## 2005-01-24 — Illinois v. Caballes, No. 03-923.

**Souter, dissenting:** The dog was used to investigate concealed effects and to establish grounds for opening the trunk. Souter would treat that investigative examination as a search requiring an independent lawful basis beyond the speeding detention. *Place* supplies a special privacy distinction only when the method is genuinely confined to contraband; sufficient reliability for probable cause does not establish the absolute informational exemption asserted here. *Jacobsen* concerned a test of material already lawfully obtained, whereas this examination began an investigative process directed at a closed enclosure. *Kyllo* supports scrutiny of acquisition of concealed information, and *Edmond* requires authority for the actual intrusion. Souter accepts the individual reliability finding but rejects its use to settle constitutional classification. He would affirm suppression and also joins Ginsburg's separate seizure ground.

**Ginsburg, dissenting, joined by Souter:** A traffic seizure is limited in scope as well as time. The compulsory canine investigation materially expands that seizure from traffic enforcement to a drug inquiry, even though the accepted duration remains lawful. *Robinette* preserves the limited authority conferred by the traffic encounter, *Edmond* distinguishes ordinary crime investigation from an independently justified seizure, and *Knowles* rejects treating a citation as general search authority. Ginsburg agrees that probable-cause reliability does not by itself establish an all-purpose contraband-only exemption, but her sufficient ground concerns the scope of this seizure. She would require articulable drug suspicion for the added investigation and affirm the Illinois judgment. Her agreement with the limited privacy objection is not a whole-opinion join in Souter's separate search rationale.

## 2005-02-22 — Smith v. Massachusetts; No. 03-8661.

**Breyer, dissenting, joined by Kennedy, Ginsburg.** The dissent would affirm the firearm conviction because the trial judge's midtrial ruling remained subject to Massachusetts's general common-law reconsideration power. It accepts Martin Linen's substantive acquittal inquiry, Fong Foo's protection despite legal error, and Price's rejection of a signature or jury-announcement prerequisite. Its disagreement concerns whether this proceeding had reached a completed acquittal at all.

Rule 25(a) required an immediate ruling, but the dissent reads the state court's account of Haskell as permitting reconsideration of that partial ruling while the trial remained pending. It would not demand an additional rule specifically naming insufficiency determinations or an express reservation by the trial judge. The same jury remained empaneled, correction preceded closing arguments and instructions, and the prosecutor relied on evidence already presented. The Appeals Court reported no asserted change in defense strategy, and Smith did not request reopening after correction. In the dissent's view those features show a continued original adjudication with an opportunity to defend.

The unqualified allowance, docket implementation and ensuing defense proceedings are the strongest contrary evidence. The dissent nevertheless distinguishes a conclusive statement of the judge's present assessment from an irrevocable disposition under the governing procedure. It reads Smalis as addressing further proceedings after appeal from a final acquittal. It does not permit reconsideration of an acknowledged completed acquittal merely because the ruling was wrong or no prejudice was shown; it finds the antecedent finality absent here.

## 2005-02-23 — Johnson v. California; No. 03-636.

Stevens agrees that the common standard governs and that personal immunity and prospective relief are distinct. He would hold the categorical policy invalid on this record because its racial proxy and repeated application to known transferees remain insufficiently justified even under Turner. Integrated activities and available individual information expose the inadequacy of the asserted blanket need. Adarand and Oneonta strengthen that conclusion. A changed review standard does not itself entitle the State to another merits opportunity. He distinguishes genuine emergency precautions and would remand only for personal defenses and appropriate relief, rather than enter an immediate damages award or an unsupported decree.

Thomas, joined by Scalia, would affirm under Turner. Confined reception cells, limited monitoring, changing gang affiliations and incomplete information make this temporary classification an operational security judgment reasonably related to protecting inmates and staff. General racial-classification decisions outside confinement do not silently displace the specific prison line. The integrated activities and existing information about transferees make the application closer but do not negate the particular risk in cells. Their argument does not validate racial allocation for its own sake or permanent system-wide segregation. If the Court imposes the common standard, they would permit genuine factual application rather than invalidate the policy immediately, and would apply the ordinary independent remedy restrictions. That alternative position does not change their primary affirmance vote.

## 2005-03-01 — Roper v. Simmons; No. 03-633.

O'Connor accepts evolving standards and independent proportionality review, as she did in Atkins, but finds this extension insufficiently supported. The changes since Stanford are fewer and slower, continuing authorization remains substantial, and older adolescents vary in maturity. In her view, the evidence does not establish that individualized consideration cannot identify an offender sufficiently culpable for death. Atkins's distinct disability category and its reliable-status inquiry therefore do not decide this age question. She would preserve Stanford's eligibility rule, give youth its substantial mitigating force and return the case for lawful state proceedings. Her position does not reject independent proportionality judgment or treat legislatures as its exclusive source.

Scalia, joined by Thomas, objects both to the Court's method and to its account of domestic evidence. Stanford leaves the disputed eligibility rule in force unless the Constitution supplies a sound basis for change. The twelve States rejecting all capital punishment cannot be counted as twelve age-specific enactments; limited movement and continuing permission among capital States do not establish the claimed rejection. Rare use may reflect careful selection rather than repudiation, and grave planned crimes challenge a universal account of insufficient culpability. They oppose using independent judicial penal judgment to create the category and regard the showing as inadequate even within the prevailing evolving-standards inquiry. Atkins remains binding for its actual disability class, and youth must still receive its lawful mitigating consideration. They would reverse the federal categorical ruling and remand for lawful state proceedings, rather than direct execution.

## 2005-03-07 — Ballard v. Commissioner; No. 03-184; consolidated with Estate of Kanter v. Commissioner, No. 03-1034.

**Thomas, dissenting.** Thomas would affirm both judgments. The 1983 amendment deliberately removed automatic party service and changed filing to submission. In his view, the Tax Court reasonably reads the amended rule to permit a special trial judge to reconsider a report before the regular judge adopts the final endorsed version. The rule still requires deference to that judge's findings, but it does not expressly require disclosure of every earlier internal version.

Bowles v. Seminole Rock & Sand Co. supports respect for a reasonable interpretation of the institution's own rule. Freytag preserves the special trial judge's work and the regular judge's final authority; neither role proves that an initial report became immutable on submission. Full adoption of the special trial judge's endorsed findings can satisfy the required weight. Thomas also regards ordinary evidentiary adjudication by the authorized judge as adequate against the confined due-process objection absent a demonstrated hearing defect. He rejects treating an unproved account of internal revision as proof that the prescribed review failed. The Court answers that access is required to test the retained legal weight, regardless of whether misconduct or a changed tax result can yet be established.

## 2005-03-22 — Brown v. Payton; No. 03-1039; 544 U.S. 133.

**Souter, joined by Stevens and Ginsburg, concurring in Part I and dissenting from Part II and the judgment; Stone-Zsela joins Part I of this separate opinion.** Part I finds both constitutional error and an objectively unreasonable state application. Skipper made relevant postcrime adjustment legally available, while Eddings distinguished exclusion from weight. The prosecutor repeatedly told this jury that the defense's principal mitigating case could not count. The court declined to correct that legal statement, and the instruction to consider all evidence was qualified by later directions that included factor (k)'s crime-centered wording. Defense advocacy was not authoritative legal authorization. The alternative attack on sincerity did not withdraw the exclusion claim. These features distinguish Boyde and, in the dissent's view, exceed the range of reasonable application rather than merely warrant a different independent answer.

Parts II and III, joined only by Stevens and Ginsburg, separately determine actual effect and would affirm sentence-only habeas relief. Under Brecht, O'Neal, Coleman and Penry II, the State must prove beyond a reasonable doubt that the preserved error did not contribute to the actual death sentence. The dissent considers the murder, sexual violence, other attacks, earlier violence and incriminating statements alongside the eight witnesses' conversion and conduct evidence and both sides' arguments. That aggravation could justify death in a lawful proceeding, but it does not establish what this jury did with its principal reason for choosing life after being told that reason was legally unavailable. Neither the admission of evidence nor the alternative weight argument establishes that the jurors instead weighed it. The State therefore fails its burden on the existing record. This is a separate actual-effect determination, not a structural-error rule or automatic relief from every Boyde violation. The dissent would preserve conditional sentence relief allowing a reasonable court-fixed opportunity for a constitutionally adequate penalty proceeding or a lawful noncapital sentence.

**Stone-Zsela, concurring in Part I and dissenting from Part II and the judgment.** He joins Souter's Part I because the uncorrected legal exclusion of the only affirmative mitigation makes the state application objectively unreasonable and creates a reasonable likelihood that jurors treated the evidence as legally unavailable. The qualified general direction to consider evidence and the alternative prosecution argument about weight do not resolve that legal-use problem. He withholds Souter's completed harmlessness determination and proposed affirmance of final relief. He would affirm the statutory-gateway and constitutional-error determinations, vacate the final remedial component, and require a reasoned whole-record harmlessness adjudication with the State bearing the beyond-reasonable-doubt burden. The reviewing court must obtain and examine the full existing sentencing record and permit focused submissions; strong aggravation does not shift the burden or replace inquiry into the actual sentence. If the State fails, a conditional sentence writ must set and enforce a reasonable court-fixed period for a constitutionally adequate penalty proceeding or replacement of death with a lawful noncapital sentence. The convictions and unrelated habeas claims remain outside that relief.

## 2005-03-22 — Muehler v. Mena; No. 03-1423.

Justice Kennedy, joined by Justices O'Connor, Scalia and Thomas, concurs in Parts I and III and dissents from Part II's continued-force disposition. They would hold the identified two-to-three-hour search-period restraint reasonable given the weapons search, four adults and one or two guards, even accepting the supported unfavorable factual account. Summers permits practical control during execution, and Graham does not require the best possible alternative staffing or restraint arrangement. Those circumstances, in their view, resolve this force ground for the officers.

Their conclusion carries concrete limits. Real pain or serious discomfort from prolonged cuffs requires officers to alter the conditions sufficiently to attend to the detainee's needs. Two to three hours calls for serious attention to continuing justification; it is not a categorical safe period. They join review of the separate post-search custody and damages issues, and the Court's present question-scope limit.

## 2005-03-29 — City of Sherrill v. Oneida Indian Nation of New York; No. 03-855; the Lead, Eviction and Members cases at their reviewed scope.

**Stevens, concurring in Part II and dissenting from Part I; Stone-Zsela joins Part I of the dissent.** The two Justices would affirm effective protection of the identified Sherrill parcels against the challenged taxes and dependent enforcement. They require the Nation to establish the retained reservation and parcel connection and the City to identify a legally sufficient federal alteration or taxable disposal. The Treaty of Canandaigua recognizes the particular retained reservation and federal protection. Venetie's separate dependent-community criteria do not defeat that reservation status merely because the original reservation arose under state law.

The actual Buffalo Creek instrument supplies no completed Oneida surrender here. Articles 1 and 2 exchange Wisconsin interests for western lands; Article 3 conditions that new western interest, and Article 13 conditions Oneida removal on satisfactory arrangements for New York purchases. The separate Seneca and Tuscarora transactions cannot be attributed to the Oneidas. Existing recognition and succession likewise cannot be displaced by demographic dispersal alone. Yakima and Cass require the actual federal source, which this parcel chain lacks. Section 233 preserves existing tax protection; its distinct execution qualification for a tribal member's land-use or possession suit against another member is not a tax exception.

They reject the majority's independent qualification for the same reason. The Nation bought from willing sellers and resists a new governmental exaction on land it now owns. Effective prospective protection neither recovers ancient rents nor removes other private owners. Stevens's Oneida II concern about disruptive ancient recovery therefore does not decide this case. ## 2005-06-20 — Graham County Soil & Water Conservation District v. United States ex rel. Wilson, No. 04-169

**Stevens, concurring in the judgment.** Stevens regards the broad action-under-§3730 language as naturally inclusive of retaliation and recognizes the value of federal uniformity. The decisive contrary consideration is that the fraud-based trigger could exhaust the entire period before an actionable retaliation claim exists. He treats the ordinary accrual principle as sufficient to reject that result without requiring another feature first to establish textual ambiguity. He therefore supports state borrowing and remand without joining the Court’s principal interpretive reasoning in full.

**Breyer, joined by Ginsburg, dissenting.** The dissent would apply six years from the alleged or suspected §3729 violation. Retaliation is an action under §3730, and limitations provisions routinely measure time from alleged events without making ultimate proof of that event a condition of timeliness. This reading preserves independent retaliation liability; it does not require a successful fraud claim or move the federal starting date to the retaliation itself.

The government-burden provision’s own subject, the United States, limits its operation, while Congress expressly named subsections (a) and (b) elsewhere when it wished to narrow coverage. Congress could reasonably select one long period for related fraud and retaliation litigation. That clock can start before retaliation and could expire first, but the dissent considers the resulting risk less consequential than the short, varied periods produced by borrowing. Donnelley’s discussion of the difficulties of varied state periods reinforces the practical value of a uniform period. The dissent would affirm the Fourth Circuit’s six-year ruling.

## 2005-06-23 — Halbert v. Michigan, No. 03-10198.

**Justice Thomas, dissenting, joined by Justice Scalia.** The dissent would sustain the judgment on two independently sufficient grounds. First, Douglas governs an appeal as of right, while Ross permits a State to limit counsel at a genuinely selective stage. Michigan affords a leave application after a plea has narrowed the possible issues and supplies appointment in defined circumstances, including after leave is granted. The absence of an earlier counseled appellate presentation is significant, but the dissent does not regard it as sufficient to extend Douglas to this different procedure. Robbins governs the required assistance once that entitlement exists; it does not itself settle this classification.

Second, assuming an entitlement, the dissent finds an actual waiver from the reported record. Halbert repeatedly confirmed the changed appellate rights and finite appointment categories. Counsel's compliance response and the trial court's understanding-and-voluntariness finding reinforce the inference that he understood and accepted the ordinary absence of appointed assistance. The statutory duty supplies the legal context, rather than proof of unreported advice.

The dissent recognizes that surrender of an appeal as of right differs from surrender of help preparing an available application. It nevertheless reads the cumulative advice and confirmations as sufficiently conveying that residual consequence. Under Tovar and Stumpf, demonstrated understanding may be inferred without an express residual exclusion or a prescribed waiver formula. The subsequent requests do not negate the knowing choice the dissent finds. It would affirm on either ground.

## 2005-06-23 — Kelo v. City of New London, No. 04-108.

**Justice Kennedy, concurring.** Kennedy joins the Court's opinion in full. Ordinary deference must still examine a supported allegation that private favoritism supplies the actual object of a taking. The rational-basis inquiries in Cleburne and Moreno illustrate that judicial respect for governmental choices can coexist with a genuine examination of an impermissible object. The plan, decision process and beneficiary relationships matter to that inquiry; merely identifying incidental private gain does not establish invalidity.

The record supports this comprehensive plan under that standard. Kennedy leaves open whether a narrowly drawn, exceptionally suspicious class of transfers could warrant stronger review. He identifies no governing heightened standard or set of triggers for that different category in this case.

**Justice O'Connor, dissenting, joined by Justices Scalia and Thomas.** The dissent would prohibit taking ordinary private property for new private users solely because their normal activities are expected to produce greater economic benefits. Public ownership and actual public service remain valid. Berman addressed a harmful area, and Midkiff a harmful concentration of ownership; the acquisition itself removed the identified condition. The petitioners' ordinary homes present a materially different case.

The precedents' broad public-purpose language favors the City's position, but the dissent would confine that language at this economic-successor boundary. A sincere comprehensive plan does not cure the substantive problem because virtually every private activity yields incidental public gains. Meaningful pretext review addresses bad purpose but leaves the lawful-purpose rule too broad. Compensation and possible state-law protection cannot replace an independent federal public-use limit. The dissent would reverse and remand for relief preventing these condemnations.

**Justice Thomas, dissenting.** Thomas also joins the common dissent as an alternative under existing precedents, while proposing a further change to their constitutional rationale. The public-use condition should require governmental ownership or use, or a legally enforceable entitlement of the public to use the property. Common-carrier property may satisfy that rule despite private title; projected jobs and taxes from ordinary private premises do not.

The constitutional text and structure distinguish public use from the independent duty to compensate. Cole and Kohl illustrate public functions and the private-use prohibition without proving an exhaustive historical catalogue. Thomas would reconsider Berman's and Midkiff's broader public-purpose propositions and incompatible private-transfer permission. He would replace Boston & Maine's broader rationale while recognizing that secured passenger service could satisfy the narrower use rule. His alternative reliance on the common dissent does not amount to approval of those precedents' permanent constitutional correctness.

## 2005-06-27 — Metro-Goldwyn-Mayer Studios Inc. v. Grokster, Ltd., No. 04-480.

**Justice Ginsburg, concurring, joined by Justice Kennedy.** The concurrence joins the inducement opinion and would also reject the distributors' summary judgment under Sony. The relevant inquiry concerns these products' reasonable capability for commercially significant lawful use, rather than benefits attributed to peer-to-peer technology in general. Particularized evidence matters even though lawful use need not be predominant and genuine future markets count.

The declarations and anecdotal examples do not, in this view, establish that capability beyond genuine dispute. General statements about authorized distribution, public-domain works or possible future uses may describe real opportunities without proving the commercially significant prospect asserted for these products. The available-file survey does not establish actual-download proportions; its residual is unknown, and the owners continued to dispute the lawful-use showing. **Justice Breyer, concurring, joined by Justices Stevens and O'Connor.** This concurrence joins vacatur on inducement while accepting the lower court's favorable Sony capability determination. The survey's unknown residual is not a proved lawful percentage, and the position requires no precise numerical finding from it.

The concurrence evaluates the proposed change against Sony's protection of innovation, the strength of the demonstrated enforcement need and the comparative costs of alteration. That assessment is a reason to retain Sony, not a new three-step liability test. Inducement and direct-infringement enforcement provide protection for copyright; Napster's distinct knowing-assistance route remains available. ## 2005-06-27 — National Cable & Telecommunications Association v. Brand X Internet Services, No. 04-277; FCC v. Brand X Internet Services, No. 04-281.

**Justice Breyer, concurring.** Breyer joins the Court's opinion in full. Congress's assignment, not expertise or complexity, supplies an agency's interpretive authority; formal procedure is neither necessary nor sufficient to establish it, and the particular question must be one Congress left to the agency. The definitions turn on what the provider actually offers. This classification lies close to the boundary, but subscribers are offered transmission of their chosen information for a fee, and the Commission's account of integration does not move that function outside the definition. Within the statutory category the Commission keeps broad discretion: it decides which duties apply and how they operate, makes the technical and market findings section 160 requires, and must forbear when it makes them. Part II respects actual delegation because an earlier decision that left a choice open does not constrain it. The opinion adopts no exception based on describing a question as important and no rule that an agency loses authority whenever a court speaks first.

**Justice Scalia, concurring in part.** Scalia joins Parts II, III and IV and the judgment, but not Part I. As his Mead dissent explained, he would give controlling reasonable-choice treatment to an agency's authoritative official interpretation within an assigned statutory field rather than ask whether Congress authorized the particular mechanism to carry the force of law. That difference does not affect this case: the Commission's nationwide declaratory ruling is its authoritative position on any view, and the statutory definitions, which bind an agency even within an assigned field, exclude the classification. He adds that executive displacement of a judicially fixed statutory meaning would raise the judicial-finality concern illustrated by Chicago & Southern Air Lines v. Waterman: a completed construction by an Article III court may not be overturned by the Executive, and its force rests on judicial power as well as on precedent. Neal's particular holding does not itself dictate a universal prohibition, and he joins Part II's bounded rule; the finality ground is his alone.

**Justice Thomas, dissenting, joined by Justices Stevens, O'Connor and Kennedy.** The dissenters join Part I and would reverse in both dockets. An earlier court of appeals' selection of a preferred statutory reading does not, in their view, foreclose a different reasonable agency interpretation within a choice Congress actually delegated when the earlier holding and the statute leave that choice open. A binding determination that the statute requires a particular answer remains a legal boundary, and which kind of holding exists depends on its substance rather than on an express ambiguity formula. Portland construed the transmission component without deciding the offering question as one with a single lawful answer, so it did not foreclose the Commission's construction; placing the burden on the agency gives the first circuit to rule a choice Congress assigned to the Commission. The dissent's position concerns circuit precedent; the different force of this Court's constructions, which may settle statutory meaning and remove ambiguity, is not at issue.

On classification, the statute contemplates information service provided via telecommunications without making every transmission input a separate public offering. The Commission's paragraphs 38–41 describe genuine retrieval, storage, email and access functions integrated with cable transmission and going beyond internal network management; on that account the finished offering is an information service within the statutory range. A telephone service packaged with voicemail would keep its separate identity, but this offering is functionally integrated, and Gulf Power left the question open. The dissenters would also reject the cable/DSL arbitrariness objection: the Commission's paragraphs 42–44 rely on the Computer II framework for telephone-company facilities, those carriers' bottleneck history, emerging broadband alternatives and investment objectives, and Smiley permits that explained distinction within lawful authority. They would sustain the classification as permissible, not compelled, and adopt no general bundling exemption.

## 2005-06-27 — Town of Castle Rock v. Gonzales, No. 04-278.

**Chief Justice Stone-Zsela, concurring.** The Chief Justice joins the Court's opinion in full. He would further specify that a beneficiary plausibly denied the secured protective service by municipal policy states a procedural claim when officials fail to provide a practicable, timely opportunity to establish the statutory conditions and receive an intelligible determination. Receiving the order and material information, genuinely assessing the conditions and communicating a reason for refusal can allow a mistaken refusal to be reconsidered in time without delaying emergency action through a formal hearing. Monell still requires proof that the Town's policy caused the procedural deprivation. Any compensatory recovery must correspond to proved causal injury; the children's deaths cannot automatically measure damages merely because an enforcement duty existed.

**Justice Stevens, joined by Justice Ginsburg, concurring.** The concurrence joins the Court's opinion in full and adds that property interests protected by due process are created and defined by state law. A State that deliberately replaces general nonenforcement discretion with a specific protective duty owed to named beneficiaries creates the kind of entitlement Roth protects. Enforcement is itself the tangible service, so a beneficiary who insists on it does not claim a right to procedure alone, and refusal of the service differs from an incorrect assessment of its conditions.

The Colorado courts have the final word on the meaning of Colorado law. When a dispositive state-law question is genuinely unresolved, federal courts should obtain or await the State's authoritative construction rather than supply one of their own. No such question is dispositive here: the statute's commands and the order supply the entitlement, and a later compilation label creates no uncertainty requiring certification.

**Justice Souter, concurring.** Justice Souter joins the Court's opinion in full and writes to explain why the claim does not seek process to obtain process. A procedural due-process claim cannot rest on an interest consisting only of the procedure itself, and Doe v. District of Columbia's investigative-procedure holding rests on that principle. The benefit here is different. Colorado secures to the protected person the protective assistance itself, enforcement of the order when its conditions exist, much as state law may secure utility service, public education or benefits. The procedures by which officers determine whether those conditions exist are distinct from that assistance, and the federal process claimed concerns those determinations. Because the Court's opinion identifies the protected object in that way, preserves the exclusion of general investigative duties and promises no outcome, its rule does not convert enforcement procedure into property.

**Justice Scalia, joined by Justices O'Connor, Kennedy and Thomas, dissenting.** The dissent would reverse and restore dismissal of the reviewed municipal procedural-due-process theory. In its view, a state direction that police take specified enforcement steps upon qualifying violations does not create a beneficiary's constitutional property interest without an independently secured substantive benefit distinct from that enforcement procedure. Colorado's commands have legal force, but their object is the executive enforcement mechanism against another person. An identified beneficiary and defined triggers do not transform the prescribed steps into a separately secured benefit, and the reverse-side notice does not make the police parties to a decree. Doe v. District of Columbia distinguishes such an entitlement from mandatory investigation or enforcement procedures, and Sandin's attention to substantive constraints supplies no rule for property in police action. The order's parenting permissions also show that the claimed interest cannot be an unconditional right to the children's return.

The dissent assumes that the statutory duties are mandatory and does not rest on unlimited police discretion or on whether the interest can be sold or assigned a monetary value. It concludes that a binding direction can regulate officials without creating constitutional property in the process they must follow, so the pleaded policy cannot supply the missing interest, and it does not reach the adequacy of the process provided.
