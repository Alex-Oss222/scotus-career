## Alaska v. United States — June 6, 2005

### Components, supported alternatives and sufficient grounds

**A1, historic waters.** Overrule Alaska's count I exception. Historic inland status requires the demonstrated exercise of authority of the kind asserted, continuity and the requisite foreign acquiescence; control of fishing or coastal commerce alone does not establish a historic authority to exclude innocent navigation throughout the claimed pockets. The identified treaty permissions, isolated seizure met with protest, inconsistent boundary assertions and later acknowledgment of permissible Canadian fishing support the Master's rejection of the required continuous exclusion claim. The strongest contrary path treats those events cumulatively as recognized national dominion and invokes the United States' prior positions. That path fails on this record because it does not bridge the distinction between limited fishing/trading control and the broader continuous sovereign claim necessary for the requested inland classification. The national-interest submission cannot replace proof of that claim. United States v. Alaska (1997) leaves specific historic title available while rejecting a general fringe rule; United States v. Louisiana (Alabama and Mississippi Boundary Case), 470 U.S. 93 (1985), illustrates the particularized proof necessary. No new historical evidence is found by the model.

**A2, juridical bays.** Overrule the count II exception on the Master's independent Article 7(2) well-marked-indentation ground. Even with the proposed constructive peninsula assumed, the claimed North Bay and South Bay fail the rule requiring penetration relative to mouth width that creates landlocked waters and more than coastal curvature. Area sufficient to satisfy a semicircle does not dispense with that independent condition. The strongest contrary path invokes island assimilation and generous identification of mainland headlands; its strongest answer is that a properly assimilated coast could produce different mouth geography. The assumed-assimilation analysis nevertheless preserves Alaska's proposed geography and rejects its classification on the independent Convention condition. United States v. Louisiana, 394 U.S. 11 (1969), and United States v. Maine, 469 U.S. 504 (1985), permit attention to actual geography; they do not replace Article 7(2). The result requires no categorical rule barring every island assimilation and no unpresented State-wide coastal redrawing.

**A3, Glacier Bay retention.** The two lawful paths are federal retention through §6(e)'s wildlife-reservation exclusion, read with the actual reservation and §§5 and 6(m), and state title because the proviso does not extend beyond the initial transfer of property under the three named fisheries/game enactments. Inclusion of the beds is the unexcepted predicate; the disputed question is timely congressional retention. The federal path treats the proviso's own description of withdrawn or set-apart wildlife reservations as operative protection of those reservations, including this actual marine reservation, rather than a limitation confined to a distinct initial property-transfer program. The state path reads such transfer as a confined cross-reference and invokes the demanding post-1953 statutory retention requirement. Both preserve the separate clear-intent inquiry. The six supporters below find the actual prestatehood reservation and express wildlife-reservation description sufficient; Scalia and Thomas find the statutory bridge insufficient at this scope.

The retention coalition relies on United States v. Alaska's actual §6(e) legal-effect holding, not a claim that every monument retains every submerged parcel. Glacier Bay's wildlife protection is an actual reservation purpose; the proviso does not repeat the initial clause's sole-purpose or named-enactment conditions. Idaho v. United States reinforces timely enacted treatment of an identified estate and does not make purpose alone enough. The opposing path's strongest argument is the normal limiting work of a proviso and the words such transfer. The retention path answers through the Act's coordinated retention and transfer scheme and the reservation-specific description already given operative force in Alaska. No poststatehood act supplies an otherwise missing prestatehood intention.

**A4, disposition and implementation.** Count III follows the actual federal disclaimer. All eight accept resolution of the live exceptions and a direction that the parties submit a conforming proposed decree using the established report descriptions and disclaimer, with jurisdiction retained to implement the title determination. The decree must reflect the actual adopted title outcomes. No coordinates are fabricated and the earlier Arctic decree is not transferred to this geography.

## Spector v. Norwegian Cruise Line Ltd. — June 6, 2005

### Paths and proposition boundaries

**S1, domestic coverage and nonstructural obligations.** The broad exclusion path treats Title III as a comprehensive regime with structural duties affecting the internal governance of foreign vessels, requiring a clear statement before any application. Its strongest support is Benz and McCulloch's protection against pervasive ship regulation, plus uniform statutory construction. The competing path applies the enacted public-accommodation and transportation categories to domestic passenger services, while evaluating particular duties under the statutory exceptions and the maritime rule. Its strongest response is Cunard and Ariadne's domestic-contact distinction: American passenger transactions cannot be equated with governance of a foreign crew simply because they occur aboard a ship. The six reversal supporters adopt that distinction; O'Connor and Scalia regard the proposed division as an impermissible partial application of an internally regulating statutory scheme.

**S2, permanent structural modification and maritime limits.** Statutory coverage does not itself decide which alteration is required. All reversal supporters preserve the readily-achievable standard, the distinct reasonable-modification/fundamental-alteration inquiry, genuine safety restrictions and treaty compatibility. A change conflicting with the United States' actual treaty obligations is not readily achievable. The proposed design alteration must be identified before those conditions are applied. The narrower internal-affairs question produces a genuine proposition-level difference: Stevens, Kennedy, Souter and Thomas regard a substantial permanent structural alteration of the vessel as an intrusion into internal maritime affairs requiring a clear congressional instruction absent here; Ginsburg and Breyer reject permanence by itself as a sufficient legal classification and require actual interference with protected internal governance or an actual governing conflict. Those two would apply Title III's ordinary duty-specific limits to structural changes that satisfy them. Their agreement on coverage and remand must not be represented as agreement with the four's structural exclusion.

**S3, the actual remaining lower-court issue.** The district court dismissed barrier claims on the separate no-guidelines ground; the Fifth Circuit did not decide it. The reversal supporters return that ground for appellate consideration under the coverage holding, alongside the requested duties and applicable safety/treaty proof. This is an actual remaining defense, not a generic invitation to invent new thresholds. Nonstructural claims also return for ordinary merits and equitable adjudication. The granted question and lack of first-instance determinations do not support a comprehensive alteration injunction. The opposing two would leave dismissal in place on the categorical maritime construction.

Clark v. Martinez is applicable current law. A uniform meaning of the enacted category does not itself preclude a rule limiting an application because it would intrude into foreign ship governance, in the six supporters' view; the two opponents view the distinction as changing the statutory regime's coverage in precisely the manner Clark disfavors. Thomas's public Clark dissent supplies no commitment to that uniform-avoidance extension. No new severability statute or fabricated concession supplies the division.

## Dodd v. United States — June 20, 2005, conditional on chronology refresh

### Two supported statutory paths

**D1, initial recognition.** Affirm the statutory limitations dismissal. Paragraph 6(3) names the initial Supreme Court recognition date; the following if-clause restricts qualifying rights rather than naming another date. Richardson's June 1, 1999 recognition precedes April 4, 2001 by more than one year. Assume retroactive qualification in Dodd's favor for this holding. The strongest objection is that a successive movant may await a retroactivity holding until after that year expires. The response is that the limitations and successive-motion provisions impose distinct conditions, and courts cannot replace the express date because their combination can be restrictive. Clay and current Johnson require fidelity to the particular trigger; Tyler's logically compelled retroactivity route does not create a new statutory clock.

**D2, completed retroactivity condition.** Reverse the initial-recognition-only ruling and remand. Read the paragraph as granting a year for an asserted newly recognized right once the retroactivity condition makes that special collateral route operative; a later authoritative retroactivity determination begins the usable period rather than causing it to expire before qualification. The strongest objection is the explicit identification of initial recognition as the date. The response is contextual: the if-clause is an integrated condition, and the limitation should not consume the period while the condition permitting use of the newly recognized right remains incomplete. This approach retains initial Supreme Court recognition as indispensable identification of the right and does not transform any later application into a newly recognized right.

The D2 supporters do not announce that the Eleventh Circuit necessarily possesses the sole authority to satisfy every retroactivity condition or that Dodd wins the underlying Richardson claim. The motion preceded the later circuit ruling. Their disposition restores consideration under the later-condition construction and returns any remaining proper qualification and merits questions. The scope of who can satisfy paragraph 6(3)'s retroactivity clause is not resolved by importing Tyler's expressly Supreme-Court-specific successive-application text. That genuine legal issue is outside the granted timing question as supplied and unnecessary to D1's assumption; D2 likewise leaves any disputed qualification for the proper lower-court proceedings.

## Graham County Soil & Water Conservation District v. United States ex rel. Wilson — June 20, 2005, conditional on chronology refresh

### Competing readings and remedy

**G1, fraud-clock limitation and state borrowing.** Reverse the Fourth Circuit's six-year ruling and remand. Section 3731(b)(1)'s reference to civil actions under §3730 must be read with its express starting event: a violation of §3729. Retaliation under §3730(h) may occur before, after or without an actual false claim; McKenzie's independent retaliation conditions make that difference controlling. Reading the six-year provision as a fraud-enforcement limitation gives both the reference and trigger work. A new retaliation-accrual date cannot simply be substituted for the cross-referenced violation. Section 3731(c)'s United-States burden provision also confirms that broad under-§3730 wording receives context rather than identical reach wherever used.

The strongest contrary argument is that Congress added retaliation and changed the limitations text in the same 1986 Act, while retaining a broad §3730 reference. The G1 response is that the legislature retained the particular §3729 trigger and elsewhere regulates employee retaliation separately; simultaneity alone cannot supply an unexpressed change in that trigger. North Star Steel then supplies ordinary appropriate-state borrowing. The same-Act fraud period has not been shown both a clearly closer analogy and significantly more suitable than a proper employment/retaliation analogue; a general preference for uniformity is insufficient. Jones v. R. R. Donnelley does not bring the 1986 claim within §1658.

**G2, federal six-year protection for retaliation.** Affirm the use of a six-year period and remand any remaining issues. The broad cross-reference includes subsection (h), and the simultaneous creation of its independent remedy supports a single federal period. The six-year duration applies to retaliation from accrual of the retaliatory injury; the reference to a §3729 violation is read in the context of the fraud remedy for which it was drafted rather than used to delete the newly included employee action. The strongest objection is that this substitutes a trigger Congress did not state. The G2 answer treats the mismatch as a confined drafting/fit problem within an expressly broad coverage clause and preserves the ordinary principle that a claimant's period begins when the claimant can sue for the asserted injury. It does not make successful fraud a retaliation element or run time from an unrelated suspected false claim. This is a genuine alternative construction, not an assumption that §3731(b)(2) independently supplies a retaliation clock.

The G1 remedy does not reinstate dismissal automatically. The Fourth Circuit must select the proper analogous state period and address actual accrual, tolling and timeliness under it. The district court's three-year choice has not been conclusively validated. Both paths leave the separate qui tam matter outside this granted limitations decision.
