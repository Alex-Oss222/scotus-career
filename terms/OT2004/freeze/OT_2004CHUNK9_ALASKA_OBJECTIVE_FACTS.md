## Date and scope

The argument occurred January 10, 2005. The packet's February 28 date is a clerical error. Source: official reporter case heading, 545 U.S. 75. Decision placement remains June 6, 2005.

Counts I and II concern pockets and enclaves between or fringing the Alexander Archipelago islands, all more than three nautical miles from the mainland or any individual island. Count I asserts historic inland waters; count II asserts juridical bays. The United States accepts that, if these waters qualify as inland, it cannot rebut the resulting state-title presumption for these pockets. Count IV concerns Glacier Bay, whose waters are accepted as inland. The Tongass claim was removed by the federal disclaimer. Sources: objective case description, 545 U.S. 79–81; the supplied neutral packet for the disclaimer.

## Special Master's historical-record treatment and the parties' dispute

The Master examined Russian sovereignty, early American sovereignty, the 1903 boundary arbitration, the later prestatehood period and the poststatehood era. His recommendation on count I rests on insufficient proof of a continuous assertion of authority to exclude innocent passage. Alaska disputes his weighting of evidence and claims both foreign acquiescence and support from national interests. Source: recitation of Report 23–129 and Alaska's exceptions, 545 U.S. 81–83.

The Russian treaty invoked by Alaska allowed American fishing and trading during a ten-year period. The parties dispute whether the associated vessel-interception events establish a broader exclusion of innocent navigation. The record includes the 1903 United States boundary-arbitration assertion, the 1924 seizure of the Marguerite and the British protest, and 1934 State and Commerce Department correspondence treating Canadian fishing outside the three-mile limit as permissible. These are record items to assess, rather than an instruction about the legal inference to draw. Sources: record recitations, 545 U.S. 83–91.

## Juridical-bay alternative

The proposed constructive peninsula joins Kuiu Island, Kupreanof Island, Mitkof Island and Dry Island to each other and the mainland, dividing the claimed waters into the proposed North Bay and South Bay. The Master's primary recommendation rejected the proposed assimilation except for two channels that would not create the claimed bays. His independent alternative recommendation found that even with assimilation the proposed waters fail the Convention's well-marked-indentation criterion. Alaska contests both recommendations and argues that the resulting waters satisfy the Convention. Sources: Report 147–222 as recited at 545 U.S. 92–94.

Article 7(2) of the Convention requires a well-marked indentation whose penetration relative to mouth width creates landlocked waters and more than a mere coastal curvature, together with the semicircle area condition. United States v. Maine, 469 U.S. 504 (1985), and United States v. Louisiana, 394 U.S. 11 (1969), are date-eligible authority relevant to assimilation; their application must respect the actual simulated United States v. Alaska holding. The source's statement of the Master's alternative supplies a live ground; it is not the later Court's judgment.

## Glacier Bay reservation and congressional treatment

The Master's report found that the reservation included the submerged lands. Alaska filed no formal exception to that finding, although a four-sentence footnote in its brief expressed disagreement. The remaining exception contests the intention to retain federal title through statehood. Source: procedural description of the filed exception and brief, 545 U.S. 102.

The statutory dispute concerns the Alaska Statehood Act, especially sections 5, 6(e) and 6(m). Section 5 preserves territorial property for the State and otherwise retains federal property subject to section 6. Section 6(m) applies the Submerged Lands Act. Section 6(e)'s initial transfer provision concerns federal property specifically used solely for conservation and protection of Alaska fisheries and wildlife under the three named game and fisheries enactments. Its reservation proviso excludes lands set apart as wildlife refuges or reservations, associated facilities, and facilities connected with general fisheries or wildlife research. The proviso and the initial transfer clause have distinct terms.

Alaska argues that the proviso is confined to property within that initial clause; Glacier Bay was created under the Antiquities Act, rather than those three named enactments. It alternatively argues that Glacier Bay was not set apart for wildlife protection. The United States treats the proviso as expressing retention for the described reservations regardless of which authorizing statute established them and invokes the reservation's marine and wildlife purposes. The Master accepted the federal construction. These are the parties' competing constructions, not a supplied resolution. Source: statutory and party-position recitations, 545 U.S. 104–106.
