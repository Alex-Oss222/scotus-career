## Doe v. Porter — April 26, 2005

**Disposition of the eight Associates:** Affirm the constitutional judgment and injunction against the Bible Education Ministry program as operated; affirm the protective order, family standing determination and challenged fee award. The common merits ground is the demonstrated adoption of devotional instruction within the public elementary schools' own instructional program. All eight join that ground and its fitted relief. No separate writing is necessary to preserve a difference that the common formulation does not adopt.

### Component rules and supported alternatives

**Instruction and relief.** Public schools may not use their ordinary instructional program to teach religious doctrine as truth or prescribe religious observance. Supplying the regular classroom period and delegating lesson content to religious volunteers does not turn that governmental instructional function into a private forum. Agostini's modified Lemon inquiry reaches that specific prohibition directly; McCollum and Schempp supply the established school-instruction boundary. The operative record contains actual lessons about God's commands, divine creation, salvation and devotional conduct. A secular character-education objective and some secular lessons do not change the function of the devotional program proved here.

The injunction is sustained against that program, with its scope understood to prohibit the demonstrated school adoption of devotional instruction. The Board is not entitled to leave that program operating while requesting approval of a replacement. A court may explain the boundary necessary to its decree, but no unsubmitted curriculum is before this Court for advance approval. Objective academic study and genuinely private religious expression remain distinct from the adjudicated governmental function; the decision does not exclude instructors because they are religious.

The strongest reversal/remand path invokes Agostini's protection of separately controlled secular teaching and genuine private access: the college, rather than school employees, selects and delivers the content. That path fails on the actual curricular arrangement and documented devotional content. This is not merely a religious speaker's presence, a religious location, an unimplemented policy or a dispute requiring inferred future misconduct. No genuine material factual dispute identified in the record prevents summary judgment on the program as operated. A remand to address a genuinely broader injunction would require the broader language to be established; none is supplied as a necessary premise of this affirmance.

**Standing and confidentiality.** The sealed sworn affidavits establish actual classroom exposure of the families' children, caused by the challenged program and redressable by its cessation. That ground suffices for the requested common injunction. Roe II's unanimous Rules 10(a)/26(c) rule permits tailored public pseudonymity where substantial intimate privacy outweighs the public-identification interest and meaningful confidential defense access remains. Here the children's vulnerability, supported threats and religious privacy favor protection, while defense counsel's discovery remained unrestricted. The strongest objection, that anonymity prevents testing injury or defending the program, fails on the order's actual scope. No independent organizational-standing ruling is required to sustain identical relief.

**Fees.** The narrow fee objection fails under abuse-of-discretion review. The district court removed $9,500 for unnecessary travel and another five percent for duplication; the particular remaining $4,600 objection partly overlapped the already removed amount by the Board's own account. Those facts do not establish clear error or a legally inadequate reduction. This conclusion is about the presented objection, not an automatic entitlement to any amount requested by prevailing plaintiffs.

## Pasquantino v. United States — April 26, 2005

**Disposition of the eight Associates:** Stevens, O'Connor, Kennedy and Thomas affirm the wire-fraud convictions on the presented property/revenue-rule question. Scalia, Souter, Ginsburg and Breyer reverse the convictions resting on the foreign-revenue scheme. These are four votes on each side among the Associates; the Chief Justice's position is absent. The two groups disagree about the statutory effect of the revenue rule, not about whether every foreign-law violation is a federal offense.

### Separable propositions and competing rules

**Property.** An accrued entitlement to money is different from the sovereign permission excluded by Cleveland. The affirming four hold that Canada's actual entitlement to import taxes is money or property within §1343 and that a domestic scheme aimed at depriving it of that entitlement satisfies the property category. The reversing four can accept that unpaid taxes are proprietary in the ordinary sense, but do not treat that classification as answering whether the federal statute reaches the foreign-revenue scheme against the common-law background. No vote for reversal rests on the proposition that public monetary entitlements are invariably nonproperty.

**Affirmance rule.** Section 1343 reaches the use of domestic interstate wires to execute an intentional, material scheme to deprive a foreign sovereign of an actual entitlement to money, including accrued taxes. The received revenue rule does not require exemption of this domestic criminal prosecution: the United States prosecutes its own statutory offense rather than an action by Canada to collect its tax claim. The foreign entitlement must actually exist; neither dishonest intent alone nor abstract sovereign regulatory power supplies the property element. Neder preserves materiality and the distinction between an intended scheme and completed loss. Foreign law's content is determined through the applicable law-determination procedure, while the charged scheme and wire use retain the criminal proof requirements.

The strongest objection is that recognizing the tax obligation and measuring the wrong inevitably enforce Canadian revenue policy indirectly. The affirmative answer is that reference to foreign law to identify the victim's property does not turn the United States' prosecution of domestic conduct into Canada's collection action. The traditional refusal to adjudicate foreign tax claims supplies no established categorical immunity for domestic fraud. General territorial restraint does not eliminate the concrete American conduct Congress prohibited. This ground does not depend on the Executive having power to waive statutory limits; statutory coverage must be established first.

**Reversal rule.** Against the established revenue-rule background, §1343's general fraud language does not authorize this prosecution where the charged property is solely a foreign tax entitlement and the criminal wrong requires enforcing the foreign fiscal obligation. Congress must supply a sufficiently clear basis for that extension; domestic wire use identifies the federal instrument but does not itself answer which foreign sovereign claims Congress made enforceable through punishment. The resulting remedy removes the convictions on this sole charged theory and their dependent sentences.

The strongest objection is the statute's broad money/property language and the domestic location of the calls. The reversing answer is that statutory fraud terms carry legal background, as Neder demonstrates, and statutory coverage must account for the particular sovereign revenue rule rather than assume every entitlement is treated like ordinary private commercial property. Empagran preserves territorial statutory construction; Hartford Fire does not erase it. Their rule is about this foreign fiscal object, not immunity for all fraud with a foreign victim. The government's initiation of the case does not itself supply Congress's missing authorization. No reversal vote requires the Court to adjudicate Canadian criminal liability or compel extradition.

**New sentencing request.** All eight decline to add the Blakely claim, presented only in a merits-brief footnote and not raised before the court of appeals or in the petition, to the question decided here. Booker is already effective and retains binding Guidelines based on constitutionally established facts. That law would govern an otherwise properly reached sentencing issue; it does not automatically enlarge this review. Hilts's prior loss-calculation objection is not equated with preservation of the new constitutional claim. For the reversing four, dependent sentences fall with the convictions on the merits ground. For the affirming four, no independent sentence correction is ordered on this presentation. This position does not convert lack of presentation in this review into a ruling extinguishing an independently available claim in another proper proceeding.

**Restitution.** No actual restitution award is established. The convictions do not by themselves adjudicate a separate statutory restitution entitlement. The model makes no disposition of an invented award and does not use a hypothetical collection remedy to supply a missing judgment component.

## Small v. United States — April 26, 2005

**Disposition of the eight Associates:** Stevens, O'Connor, Souter, Ginsburg and Breyer reverse and remand because the Japanese judgment falls outside the domestic conviction category in §§922(g)(1) and 921(a)(20). Scalia, Kennedy and Thomas affirm the presented statutory ruling because “any court” includes foreign courts. None decides an unpresented constitutional firearms claim. All accept Beecham and Caron's separate statutory jobs; neither earlier holding already decides the foreign-predicate category.

### Competing statutory rules and remedy

**Domestic-category rule.** “Convicted in any court” refers to convictions of domestic courts in the statutory scheme. Its breadth operates within the category supplied by the surrounding provisions. Section 921(a)(20)'s federal-or-state business-regulation exclusions, separate state-misdemeanor treatment and convicting-jurisdiction restoration structure provide a domestic frame. They cannot be sensibly treated as an enacted worldwide system merely because “any” is broad. Foreign offenses, penalties and proceedings may differ materially; Congress supplied no general case-by-case screening regime for deciding which foreign convictions deserve recognition. The rule is categorical and does not depend on finding this Japanese judgment unfair.

The strongest objection is that the firearm possession occurs in the United States and “any court” contains no express territorial adjective. The domestic-category answer does not apply a mechanical extraterritoriality bar to domestic possession. It uses the ordinary domestic statutory setting and the actual definition to identify which judgments Congress selected. Beecham chooses the convicting sovereign's restoration law after the conviction is within the relevant category; it does not answer that anterior question. Caron preserves the federal consequences of a qualifying domestic conviction despite partial restoration; its protective purpose cannot create an additional predicate. Reversal removes the conviction's sole established predicate and requires proceedings consistent with that statutory conclusion.

**Inclusive rule.** “Any court” includes a foreign court that entered a conviction for a crime punishable by more than one year. Congress's express domestic limitations in particular exclusions do not silently insert the same limitation into the unqualified predicate phrase. Section 922(g)(1) regulates Small's later domestic firearm possession; the statute does not punish his conduct in Japan or direct Japan how to administer its law. Beecham's selector can operate by reference to the actual convicting jurisdiction without rewriting the category.

The strongest objection is the asymmetry between foreign convictions and domestic-specific exemptions, plus uncertainty about foreign procedural fairness. The inclusive answer is that a difference in the exemptions' reach does not make the unqualified predicate language incoherent; Congress can impose a federal consequence without reproducing every foreign system's restoration process. A general desire for a more symmetrical statute cannot replace the language. Whether the Constitution forbids using a particular fundamentally unfair conviction is analytically distinct from categorical statutory inclusion. This statutory affirmance does not adopt the lower court's entire Restatement screening regime or turn its discretionary grounds into mandatory ones.

The lower court's actual recognition screen contains two mandatory grounds for nonrecognition and six discretionary grounds. The fresh preflight states them accurately. The Supreme Court question here is the category; the model does not manufacture a fresh finding about the Japanese proceeding or decide an additional hearing claim beyond that question. The lower judgment is affirmed by the inclusive three on the presented statutory ground, with its actual fairness determination left in its existing procedural posture.
