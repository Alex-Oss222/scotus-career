## Johnson v. California, No. 03-636

**Historical action:** Argued November 2, 2004; decided February 23, 2005. The Ninth Circuit's judgment, 321 F.3d 791, was reversed and the case remanded. Five Justices supported that disposition: O'Connor, Kennedy, Souter, Ginsburg, and Breyer. Stevens dissented separately; Thomas dissented with Scalia. Rehnquist took no part in the decision. Participation at decision is the verified historical fact; this does not establish a separate simulation participation rule.

**O'Connor for the Court:** The government must justify its express racial classification through strict scrutiny: a compelling governmental interest and means narrowly tailored to it, with the burden on the government. Equal segregation of racial groups is not neutral treatment exempt from that scrutiny. Adarand, Croson, Shaw, Brown, and Powers supply the rule and the reasons for examining all express racial classifications. Lee supplies the prison-specific precedent and recognition of particular security necessities. Turner does not govern this racial classification: equal racial treatment need not be compromised as an incident of lawful imprisonment, and Turner's reasonable-relationship test would permit practices without demonstrated necessity or narrow tailoring. Prison security and discipline are compelling interests, and the prison setting matters when strict scrutiny is applied; strict scrutiny does not automatically invalidate a policy.

**Scope and remedy:** The Court decided the standard, not the policy's ultimate constitutionality, and left application to the Ninth Circuit or District Court. Both newly admitted prisoners and transferees were within the challenge and remand. The underlying action sought damages from former officials and injunctive relief; the lower court's qualified-immunity disposition does not become a Supreme Court merits determination of immunity. The disputed practice concerns reception-center double cells for up to 60 days, not a finding that all California prison life was segregated. The Court's statement about other States is qualified in note 2: it could not establish whether Oklahoma and Texas operated in practice like California.

**Ginsburg, joined by Souter and Breyer:** Joins the Court's opinion but expressly reserves her disagreement with imposing the identical standard on every governmental use of race. Measures burdening historically excluded groups differ from measures designed to remedy entrenched discrimination. This policy had no remedial purpose; its stereotyped racial classification warranted rigorous scrutiny. Her doubts about necessity, based on other prison systems' experience, are not a separate adjudication that the policy failed strict scrutiny.

**Stevens alone:** Would hold the policy unconstitutional on the existing record under either strict scrutiny or Turner and remand only to resolve qualified immunity. The conclusive racial proxy lacked sufficient empirical support; accounts of gang riots did not establish the need for universal cell segregation at every reception center; and individualized screening using presentence reports and existing prison records offered alternatives. Administrative delay in obtaining those records did not justify a system-wide policy. He regarded further factual development as needless because California had already had ample opportunity to justify the policy. His dissent is opposition to postponing a constitutional merits decision, not support for California's policy.

**Thomas, joined by Scalia:** Would uphold the policy under Turner. Prison administration calls for a uniform deferential standard; Lee condemned wholesale permanent segregation without specifying strict scrutiny and allowed good-faith measures responding to particularized security needs. Thomas considered this policy limited by place, inmates covered, double-cell conditions, and time, and thought all four Turner factors supported it. He credited the risk of race-based gang violence, monitoring limits, the integration of other prison activities, and information deficits on arrival; transferees presented a closer question, but their information could be outdated. Adarand's general language did not silently displace prison precedents. Alternatively, if strict scrutiny controlled, California should be permitted to offer narrow-tailoring evidence because the pleadings and earlier proceedings had not required it. Note 13 recognizes the logic of prison-specific deference in applying that scrutiny, while retaining his general objection to deference in ordinary race cases. Note 1 flags an unaddressed exhaustion question; it is not an exhaustion holding.

**Record-characterization caution:** The majority identifies Folsom as Johnson's 1987 reception site, whereas Thomas identifies the California Institution for Men at Chino. That discrepancy is immaterial to the verified standard-of-review holding but should not be converted into an established neutral fact without the underlying record. The opinions also disagree about the policy's practical flexibility, available individualized information, and the evidentiary significance of other prison systems; those are attributed judicial characterizations, not interchangeable findings.

## Roper v. Simmons, No. 03-633

**Historical action:** Argued October 13, 2004; decided March 1, 2005. The Missouri Supreme Court's judgment, 112 S.W.3d 397, was affirmed, 5–4. Kennedy wrote for Stevens, Souter, Ginsburg, and Breyer. Stevens concurred with Ginsburg. O'Connor dissented alone. Scalia dissented with Rehnquist and Thomas.

**Kennedy for the Court:** The Eighth and Fourteenth Amendments prohibit death sentences for crimes committed before age eighteen. The framework uses objective evidence of evolving standards together with the Court's independent proportionality judgment. Thirty States prohibited juvenile executions: twelve prohibited death sentences altogether and eighteen excluded juveniles from an otherwise available penalty. Actual executions were infrequent, and change moved toward abolition. The Court acknowledged that change was slower than in Atkins but found the direction and earlier recognition of the juvenile distinction significant. It identified immaturity and irresponsibility, vulnerability to pressure and reduced environmental control, and less fixed character as reasons juveniles could not reliably be classified among the worst offenders. Retribution and deterrence therefore did not justify the penalty for the class. Individual sentencing could not adequately guard against a crime's brutality overwhelming youth's mitigating force or reliably identify irreparable corruption. Eighteen supplies an administrable line despite variation in individual maturity. Stanford no longer controlled this issue. International law and practice confirmed the domestic conclusion; they were not controlling authority.

**Scope and remedy:** Age is measured when the crime was committed, not when sentence or execution occurs. The case arose on renewed state postconviction review following Atkins. Affirmance preserved Missouri's replacement sentence of life imprisonment without eligibility for probation, parole, or release except by gubernatorial action. It did not reopen the guilt judgment or independently adjudicate every possible challenge to that replacement sentence. The Court recognized reliance on Stanford but declined to retain it for pending cases. Its appendices supply historical state-law surveys, with express qualifications about Kansas and New York's capital statutes; those surveys are historical comparator material, not an automatic certification of alternate-timeline law.

**Stevens, joined by Ginsburg:** Joins the Court fully and emphasizes that the Eighth Amendment's meaning is informed by evolving standards rather than frozen at adoption. The speed and application of that evolution remain debatable, but the interpretive principle is established.

**O'Connor alone:** Accepts evolving standards, objective legislative and sentencing evidence, and independent proportionality judgment as the governing method. Finds the consensus evidence weaker than Atkins: affirmative minimum-age statutes persisted, relatively few States had changed position, and juvenile sentences remained in use. Unlike the disability category in Atkins, chronological age did not prove the impairments that make every individual's execution disproportionate. Some seventeen-year-olds could possess sufficient maturity and culpability, and the record did not establish that individualized sentencing could not assess them reliably. Simmons's deliberation and expectation of avoiding punishment were relevant to those concerns. Youth remains substantial mitigating evidence; the prosecutor's treatment of it as aggravating was troubling but not specifically preserved or directly presented here, and any remedy should be appropriately confined. Foreign law may confirm a genuine American consensus, but cannot supply the missing domestic showing. Separately faults Missouri for failing to follow Stanford until the Supreme Court itself overruled it. Her footnote distinguishes explicit minimum-age enactments from statutes that merely transfer juveniles to adult court.

**Scalia, joined by Rehnquist and Thomas:** Would retain Stanford. The original understanding permitted the punishment, and even under evolving-standards doctrine there was no sufficient national consensus. States abolishing death altogether did not answer whether juveniles deserved a particular exemption; limited legislative changes, continuing authorizations, and rare executions did not establish the asserted transformation. Independent judicial moral judgment could not replace the elected branches' standards. He challenged the scientific materials' evidentiary footing, their application to all offenders, the analogy to civil age limits, and the claim that sentencing juries could not weigh youth. Individual culpability and deterrence remained possible in exceptional cases. Foreign practice and unratified or reserved treaty provisions could not determine the American constitutional rule; selective consultation was methodologically unsound. He also objected to Missouri's disregard of controlling precedent. Notes 4–6 distinguish legislative changes from judicial interpretation and discuss continuing state support; the other notes develop the original-meaning, proportionality, and foreign-law objections.

## Cherokee Nation of Oklahoma v. Leavitt, Nos. 02-1472 and 03-853

**Historical action:** Argued November 9, 2004; decided March 1, 2005. No. 02-1472: Tenth Circuit judgment, 311 F.3d 1054, reversed. No. 03-853: Federal Circuit judgment, 334 F.3d 1075, affirmed. Both cases remanded. All eight participating Justices supported the judgment. Breyer's entire opinion was joined by Stevens, O'Connor, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, and Ginsburg; Scalia joined except the specified Senate-report reliance. Rehnquist took no part in the decision.

**Breyer for the Court:** The Government's admitted support-cost promises were enforceable where Congress supplied sufficient unrestricted appropriations to pay these contracts. Ordinary contract principles controlled; the Act's self-determination purposes and exclusion from procurement procedures did not make its promises unenforceable or transfer the Government's allocation risk to the tribes. The statutory prohibition on requiring reductions to programs serving other tribes did not excuse nonpayment where other unrestricted funds sufficed. Using those funds for important federal functions remained an allocation choice, not a legal earmark. The separate availability-of-appropriations proviso conditions binding commitments on appropriations; it did not confer discretion to repudiate commitments after adequate appropriations existed. Committee-report allocations could not legally restrict unambiguous lump sums. Section 314 of the later 1999 law was read to prevent use of unspent earlier appropriations, rather than retroactively extinguish existing contract claims, because repudiation would raise serious constitutional questions.

**Scalia alone:** Joins the Court's opinion except its reliance on a Senate Committee Report to explain the procurement-contract exclusion, at slip opinion page 8. The meaning of enacted text, not a committee's incompletely expressed intention, controls. This is a narrow reasoning exclusion, not judgment-only agreement or a refusal to join the other statutory and contract holdings.

**Scope and remedy:** The claims involved fiscal years 1994–1997, two tribes, and two procedural routes. The Government conceded the promises, nonpayment, and ordinary procurement-contract rules; sufficient unrestricted funds for the claims were found below and not denied here. The decision does not establish a promise binding regardless of whether any relevant appropriation exists. It also does not hold all later funding caps or all tribal contracts invalid or enforceable irrespective of their terms. The Court did not declare section 314 unconstitutional; it adopted an interpretation avoiding retroactive repudiation. The Federal Circuit's affirmed disposition concerned the Board's damages determination, while the Tenth Circuit branch required further proceedings after reversal.

## Tenet v. Doe, No. 03-1395

**Historical action:** Argued January 11, 2005; decided March 2, 2005. The Ninth Circuit's judgment, 329 F.3d 1135, was reversed unanimously. Rehnquist wrote for all nine Justices. Stevens concurred with Ginsburg; Scalia concurred separately. The formal concluding disposition says reversed, without an express remand clause.

**Rehnquist for the Court:** Totten bars a suit whose success depends on establishing a secret espionage relationship with the Government, including estoppel and constitutional formulations of claims arising from that relationship. It is not merely an ordinary contract defense or an evidentiary privilege to be tested after discovery. Reynolds preserved rather than displaced Totten's categorical treatment of that distinct class of cases; Catholic Action confirmed its continuing force. Webster concerned an acknowledged employment relationship despite the employee's concealed identity and therefore did not control a relationship the Government neither confirmed nor denied. In camera process and case-specific privilege assertions could not provide the necessary protection against exposure, pressure to settle, or the appearance of acknowledging particular agents.

**Footnotes and reach:** All espionage, promises, benefits, and administrative contacts recounted in the opinion remained the Does' allegations; the Government neither admitted nor denied them. The immigration admission statute, 50 U.S.C. §403h, and identified regulations did not establish an enforceable lifetime-support commitment. Footnote 4 allowed the unique threshold Totten dismissal before resolving the possible Tucker Act jurisdictional objection, because the bar precludes judicial inquiry itself. The majority did not decide that Totten is categorically jurisdictional in the terms later supplied by Scalia, nor did it authorize resolution of ordinary merits issues before jurisdiction. The asserted need to review administrative procedures did not remove dependence on the alleged secret relationship. No breach, actual covert service, damages amount, or entitlement to benefits was established.

**Stevens, joined by Ginsburg:** Joins the decision on stare decisis grounds. Totten's rule is federal common law that Congress may modify if an enforceable procedure would serve the national interest. Treats the absence of an enforceable agreement as sufficient to dismiss these claims before the arguably antecedent jurisdictional issue and links that result to his Steel Co. concurrence.

**Scalia alone:** Joins the Court but rejects Stevens's implication that ordinary merits questions may precede jurisdiction. Regards Totten's categorical preclusion of inquiry as jurisdictional as applied here. Alternatively, directly governing Totten makes the claims frivolous, supplying a separate jurisdictional dismissal ground recognized by Steel Co. These characterizations must remain attributed to his concurrence unless independently adopted in a later authorized stage.
