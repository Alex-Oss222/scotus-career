## 1. Complete official historical provision

Source: https://codes.ohio.gov/ohio-revised-code/section-2919.151/8-18-2000 . Saved source: `ohio-2919.151-2000.html`; deterministic text extraction: `ohio-2919.151-2000.txt`. Its (A)(6) cross-reference is retained; it is not silently expanded using current statutory text.

```text
(A) As used in this section:
(1) "Dilation and evacuation procedure of abortion" does not include the dilation and extraction procedure of abortion.
(2) "From the body of the mother" means that the portion of the fetus' body in question is beyond the mother's vaginal introitus in a vaginal delivery.
(3) "Partial birth procedure" means the medical procedure that includes all of the following elements in sequence:
(a) Intentional dilation of the cervix of a pregnant woman, usually over a sequence of days;
(b) In a breech presentation, intentional extraction of at least the lower torso to the navel, but not the entire body, of an intact fetus from the body of the mother, or in a cephalic presentation, intentional extraction of at least the complete head, but not the entire body, of an intact fetus from the body of the mother;
(c) Intentional partial evacuation of the intracranial contents of the fetus, which procedure the person performing the procedure knows will cause the death of the fetus, intentional compression of the head of the fetus, which procedure the person performing the procedure knows will cause the death of the fetus, or performance of another intentional act that the person performing the procedure knows will cause the death of the fetus;
(d) Completion of the vaginal delivery of the fetus.
(4) "Partially born" means that the portion of the body of an intact fetus described in division (A)(3)(b) of this section has been intentionally extracted from the body of the mother.
(5) "Serious risk of the substantial and irreversible impairment of a major bodily function" means any medically diagnosed condition that so complicates the pregnancy of the woman as to directly or indirectly cause the substantial and irreversible impairment of a major bodily function.
(6) "Viable" has the same meaning as in section 2901.01 of the Revised Code.
(B) When the fetus that is the subject of the procedure is viable, no person shall knowingly perform a partial birth procedure on a pregnant woman when the procedure is not necessary, in reasonable medical judgment, to preserve the life or health of the mother as a result of the mother's life or health being endangered by a serious risk of the substantial and irreversible impairment of a major bodily function.
(C) When the fetus that is the subject of the procedure is not viable, no person shall knowingly perform a partial birth procedure on a pregnant woman when the procedure is not necessary, in reasonable medical judgment, to preserve the life or health of the mother as a result of the mother's life or health being endangered by a serious risk of the substantial and irreversible impairment of a major bodily function.
(D) Whoever violates division (B) or (C) of this section is guilty of partial birth feticide, a felony of the second degree.
(E) A pregnant woman upon whom a partial birth procedure is performed in violation of division (B) or (C) of this section is not guilty of committing, attempting to commit, complicity in the commission of, or conspiracy in the commission of a violation of those divisions.
(F) This section does not prohibit the suction curettage procedure of abortion, the suction aspiration procedure of abortion, or the dilation and evacuation procedure of abortion.
(G) This section does not apply to any person who performs or attempts to perform a legal abortion if the act that causes the death of the fetus is performed prior to the fetus being partially born even though the death of the fetus occurs after it is partially born.
```

## Smith v. Massachusetts — trial episode and procedure

The indictment charged armed assault with intent to murder, assault and battery by means of a dangerous weapon, and unlawful firearm possession. The last count included the statutory requirement that the barrel be less than sixteen inches. Smith's girlfriend was tried with him as an accessory after the fact.

The victim described a pistol or revolver that appeared to be a .32 or .38. No other evidence concerning the weapon was introduced by the prosecution. At the close of that case, Smith moved under Massachusetts Rule of Criminal Procedure 25(a) for a required finding of not guilty on the firearm count, including an insufficiency objection about barrel length. At sidebar the judge granted the motion. The allowance was endorsed on the written motion and entered on the docket. The jury was not notified.

Before the ruling, the prosecutor had said he would request reopening so that the victim could testify about barrel length. He made no reopening motion before resting. The sidebar ended and the prosecution rested. Smith's codefendant called one witness; both defendants then rested. The source does not say that Smith personally presented testimony. During the recess before closing arguments, the prosecutor supplied Commonwealth v. Sperrazza, 372 Mass. 667, 363 N.E.2d 673 (1977), concerning an inference of barrel length from testimony describing a handgun. The judge retracted the allowance and sent the firearm count to the jury. Both the motion and the docket received corresponding notations. There was no intervening new jury or new trial. Smith did not move to reopen his defense after that retraction.

The jury found Smith guilty on all three counts and acquitted the codefendant. A subsequent bench proceeding found the additional repeat-offender element of the firearm charge. The firearm sentence was ten to twelve years, concurrent with the sentences on the other counts. The firearm count's independent punishment and any dependent sentencing effect must therefore be distinguished from the merits of the two assault convictions.

Massachusetts Rule 25(a), as quoted in the contemporaneous record, requires a finding of not guilty if the evidence is insufficient as a matter of law. A motion made at the close of the prosecution's evidence must be ruled on at that time. The rule does not expressly address reconsideration. Rule 42 permits correction of clerical mistakes and errors from oversight or omission. The Commonwealth relied on its general decisional rule allowing reconsideration of interlocutory rulings; Commonwealth v. Haskell, 438 Mass. 790, 792, 784 N.E.2d 625, 628 (2003), describes a common-law power to reconsider during the pendency of a case. The Massachusetts Appeals Court read Rule 25 as not prohibiting correction of this ruling within the ongoing trial. The source identifies no statute or rule expressly making this category of midtrial insufficiency allowance provisional. Whether the general common-law rule supplies the necessary legal status is an issue for adjudication, not an omitted historical fact.

The Appeals Court's stated prejudice premise was that Smith did not claim the first allowance altered his strategy on the other charges. That is a lower-court characterization of the litigation; it is not a newly found fact about every possible strategic effect. Any broader rule about whether actual prejudice matters belongs to legal analysis. No fresh concession is attributed to Smith based on oral argument.

## Stewart v. Dutra Construction Co. — craft, claims, and two summary judgments

The Super Scoop was a floating dredge used in the Boston Harbor tunnel project. A clamshell bucket removed material and placed it in adjoining scows. The dredge had a captain and crew, navigation lights, ballast tanks, and a crew dining area. It was towed for long distances, including from California through the Panama Canal to Boston, and moved short distances with anchors and cables. During dredging it ordinarily moved thirty to fifty feet every few hours.

Stewart was a marine engineer maintaining the dredge's machinery. When the accident occurred, the dredge was idle because one scow had an engine fault and the other was away. Stewart was aboard Scow No. 4 feeding wires through an open hatch. The dredge's bucket moved the scow, which struck the dredge; the jolt caused Stewart to fall through the hatch. These are the published record's facts, not findings here about breach, causation, or damages.

In seeking summary judgment on the Jones Act claim, Dutra acknowledged that Stewart was a crew member, spent ninety-nine percent of his working time aboard the dredge, and contributed to its function. Its asserted ground was the dredge's supposed lack of vessel status. The district court applied the First Circuit's DiGiovanni test, using primary business and actual movement at the injury, and entered summary judgment. An interlocutory appeal affirmed that ruling, 230 F.3d 461 (1st Cir. 2000). The acknowledgments should not be converted into an independent decision of every legal element of seaman status, but neither should remand invent a factual dispute on matters conceded for the motion.

On remand, the district court entered a separate summary judgment on the alternative LHWCA §905(b) negligence claim. The First Circuit affirmed that judgment, 343 F.3d 10 (1st Cir. 2003). Dutra had conceded vessel status for that claim; the lower court considered §905(b)'s vessel concept broader than the one it used in the Jones Act inquiry. Its independent ground was that the alleged negligence occurred in Dutra's capacity as employer rather than as vessel owner. Correcting a vessel-definition premise therefore does not by itself decide whether §905(b)'s capacity ground is correct, or whether the claim remains available if Jones Act seaman status is established. The two judgments, coverage questions, and remedies need explicit treatment.

Dutra conceded that the dredge was temporarily stationary during repairs to the scow. It had not been taken out of service, permanently anchored, or otherwise rendered incapable of waterborne movement. No damages amount, compensation election, or complete liability finding follows from those facts.

Statutory setting: the then-current Jones Act provision was 46 U.S.C. App. §688(a). LHWCA §§902(3)(G), 902(21), and 905(b), together with 1 U.S.C. §3, bear on coverage and capacity. The 1873 vessel definition and the 1947 recodification predate the timeline divergence; the LHWCA's 1972 amendments also predate it. No absent post-divergence Supreme Court predicate is implicated in the existence of those texts. The statutory definition and worker-connection tests remain distinct questions.

## Convention Article V(1)(e)

```text
(e) The award has not yet become binding on the parties, or has been set aside or suspended by a competent authority of the country in which, or under the law of which, that award was made.
```

## Convention Article VI

```text
Article VI
If an application for the setting aside or suspension of the award has been made to a competent authority referred to in article V(1)(e), the authority before which the award is sought to be relied upon may, if it considers it proper, adjourn the decision on the enforcement of the award and may also, on the application of the party claiming enforcement of the award, order the other party to give suitable security.
```

## 9 U.S.C. §207

```text
Within three years after an arbitral award falling under the Convention is made, any party to the arbitration may apply to any court having jurisdiction under this chapter for an order confirming the award as against any other party to the arbitration. The court shall confirm the award unless it finds one of the grounds for refusal or deferral of recognition or enforcement of the award specified in the said Convention.
(Added Pub. L. 91–368, § 1, July 31, 1970, 84 Stat. 693.)
```
