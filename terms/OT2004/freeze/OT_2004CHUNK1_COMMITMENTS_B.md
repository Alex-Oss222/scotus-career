## 7. Theofel v. Farey-Jones — December 6, 2004

### Entering law, reach and alternatives

Konop and the Standards provisions **Contemporaneous interception and later retrieval of stored communications** and **Authorization by an actual user of a stored-communications service**, effective January 27, 2003, distinguish acquisition from storage and require the actual statutory consent predicates. Konop assumed other SCA elements; it does not decide this backup question. Hartford Underwriters preserves distinct enacted actor and remedy conditions. Sections 2510(17), 2701 and 2707 govern independently.

**Storage paths.** The preferred path gives the backup clause independent effect: delivery or opening does not end coverage when an electronic communication service actually retains a copy for backup protection. The contrary path confines the clause to backup copies of communications still in temporary intermediate transmission storage, invoking the separate remote-computing regime. Its strongest point is statutory coherence and avoiding indefinite universal storage coverage. The independent-clause reading answers it by requiring an actual backup purpose and the specified service; neither a merely potential use nor every permanent archive qualifies. The alternative text would require importing the first clause's storage limitation into a reference to the communication.

**Authorization paths.** Physical disclosure is not automatically statutory authorization when the defendants consciously procure it by exploiting a material mistake about purported legal compulsion. The contrary path treats the provider's voluntary act, including its opportunity to object, as sufficient under section 2701(c)(1). That rule offers a determinate consent boundary, but would give knowingly deceptive compulsory process the same effect as genuine permission. The preferred rule is confined to conscious material procurement, not negligence, ordinary subpoena irregularity or collateral deception. The amended lower footnote supplies that limitation. Remaining access, state of mind, civil-action, good-faith and remedial elements remain independent.

## 13. Brosseau v. Haugen — December 13, 2004

### Governing assumptions and law

### Alternative paths

**Deny immunity on the assumed facts.** Garner clearly excludes deadly force against an escaping suspect without probable cause of the requisite serious threat. The corrected assumptions supply neither present dangerous driving nor an objectively supported threatened person, weapon or violent offense. Harris, Curnow and Ting reinforce the distinction between escape or possible weapons and an actual serious-threat basis. The strongest counterargument is that a determined imminent vehicular departure near occupied cars differs from a fugitive fleeing on foot and presents prospective danger. The denial path answers that a court cannot supply the missing threat through an officer-favorable account or later driving; the clear route and absence of actual threatened persons make this an evident application, not a demand for a matching vehicle case.

**Grant personal immunity without deciding force legality.** Even on the corrected account, repeated resistance to apprehension followed by imminent vehicle escape near occupied cars creates a context in which the precise boundary of permissible anticipatory force was insufficiently settled in February 1999. Smith and Cole involved substantially greater demonstrated danger, but their legal treatment shows why Garner's general rule did not itself settle every prediction about vehicle escape. The strongest objection is that factual differences cannot manufacture uncertainty where the plaintiff's actual assumptions remove immediate danger. The grant path must therefore rest on reasonable legal uncertainty about this prospective-risk application, not a finding that the Jeep was already driving erratically, that an officer stood in its path, or that a collision was inevitable. No categorical vehicle exception or protection for mere flight is adopted.
