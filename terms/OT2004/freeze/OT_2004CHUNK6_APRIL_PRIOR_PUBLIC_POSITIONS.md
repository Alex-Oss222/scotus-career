### Daniels v. United States, No. 99-9136

Merits decision, April 25, 2001

#### Chronology and Posture

Argued January 8, 2001. On writ of certiorari to the Ninth Circuit, reviewing denial of a section 2255 motion attacking a current enhanced federal sentence through alleged constitutional defects in expired state predicates.

Daniels's 1994 felon-in-possession conviction was enhanced under the Armed Career Criminal Act using four California convictions, two burglaries and two robberies from 1977 through 1981. After a downward departure he received 176 months, above the otherwise applicable 120-month maximum. His direct appeal unsuccessfully challenged burglary classification. This motion instead alleges involuntary counseled pleas in the 1978 and 1981 robberies and ineffective representation in the latter. Those sentences had expired, no invalidation is established, and counsel identified no indication of an earlier constitutional challenge or external obstruction to one. The question concerns this section 2255 route, not a new classification or jury claim. Coss presents a distinct state-prisoner vehicle.

All nine Justices participated at argument and decision.

#### Judgment

**Affirmed, 5–4.**

**Supporting:** Stone-Zsela, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy and Thomas.

**Opposing:** Stevens, Souter, Ginsburg and Breyer.

The denial of this section 2255 motion stands and the existing federal sentence remains undisturbed against these claims. The judgment determines review access, not the constitutional validity of the expired robbery convictions. No new sentencing, predicate invalidation, evidentiary hearing or reduced sentence is ordered.

#### Opinion Topology

| Writing | Author | Joined by | Relationship to judgment | Scope joined |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Opinion of the Court | O'Connor | Stone-Zsela, Scalia, Kennedy, Thomas | Affirmance, 5–4 | Available-review holding, complete-counsel exception, application, remedy and exact limits; no affirmative faultless-review exception |
| Concurrence | Stone-Zsela | — | Joins the entire common opinion and affirmance | Additionally adopts a faultless-loss exception within his governing access rule |
| Concurrence | Scalia | — | Joins the entire common opinion and affirmance | Additional statutory objection to section 2255 adjudication of an outstanding non-Gideon state defect |
| Dissent | Souter | Stevens, Ginsburg | Would reverse and remand | Express section 2255 review of the present enhanced sentence, subject to ordinary conditions |
| Dissent | Breyer | — | Would reverse and remand | Distinct proposal to reconsider Custis's restriction at sentencing; no blanket join in Souter's statutory theory |

#### Controlling Propositions and Authority

##### Available original review does not become a renewed section 2255 forum

**Controlling proposition:** Section 2255 does not permit a prisoner to reopen an expired counseled state conviction for involuntary-plea or ineffective-assistance claims merely because that conviction enhances a current federal sentence, where meaningful original review was available and the prisoner identifies no external impediment to using it. A genuinely available earlier opportunity lost through unexcused failure is not an unavailable remedy; the Court does not declare the old conviction valid or decide the different case of faultless loss of meaningful original review.

**Authority:** The five Justices joining O'Connor's Opinion of the Court directly adopt this available-opportunity rule, its finality explanation, application and affirmance. Each Justice's separate position beyond it remains distinct.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that the current federal sentence and the expired predicate have different lawful review histories. Custis limits constitutional attacks at ACCA sentencing, but expressly leaves expired-custody and no-meaningful-review cases open. Extending its channel distinction into section 2255 requires today's statutory holding; it is not an application of an already decided universal bar.

Section 2255 expressly permits correction of an unlawful current federal sentence. That text does not erase the original judgment's review structure or make every later enhancement a renewed opportunity to litigate constitutional objections that could have been presented in the proper forum. Finality, the original jurisdiction's responsibility and the difficulty of reconstructing old proceedings support enforcing that distinction when a genuine earlier opportunity existed. Administrative inconvenience alone does not establish that every alleged unlawful sentence must escape review.

Daniels's stronger incentive to contest the predicates after committing the federal offense does not establish that the earlier forum was unavailable. His counsel identifies neither a prior attempt to pursue these constitutional challenges while in custody nor an external obstacle preventing one. The Court does not infer deliberate personal choice from every attorney failure; it decides the opportunity and allegations actually presented. Parke preserves rebuttable regularity and lawful proof rules, not an irrebuttable finding that every final plea was valid.

Maleng distinguishes expired independent custody from collateral consequences. Garlotte concerns a continuous unexpired consecutive series and does not convert these wholly expired state sentences into present custody under those judgments. Daniels's current federal custody supplies the section 2255 vehicle, but not an automatic right to relitigate each predicate. His involuntary counseled pleas and deficient-representation allegations therefore cannot proceed through this motion on the review history presented.

##### Complete unconstitutional counsel deprivation remains distinct

**Controlling proposition:** A predicate obtained through unconstitutional complete denial of constitutionally required counsel, without valid waiver, remains subject to challenge through section 2255 under applicable procedural requirements and upon a showing of its adverse effect on the current sentence. Deficient representation and an involuntary counseled plea are not that exception; ordinary procedural-default requirements remain, generally including presentation of the counsel-deprivation claim at federal sentencing or a legally sufficient excuse.

**Authority:** The same five Justices directly join the preservation of this exception, its procedural boundaries and its inapplicability to Daniels's allegations.

**Controlling explanation:** Gideon establishes the entitlement to appointed counsel when constitutionally required. Burgett and Tucker preserve the special restriction on subsequent reliance on a conviction obtained through unconstitutional complete deprivation of that protection. Custis retains this distinct exception while refusing to turn every alleged defect in counseled proceedings into an ACCA sentencing issue.

The difference concerns the kind of constitutional deprivation, not a judgment that ineffective assistance or involuntary pleas are unimportant. Daniels alleges defective counseled proceedings; he identifies no complete unconstitutional deprivation. The exception therefore supplies no route for his present motion. Nor does recognition of a qualifying claim dispense with applicable procedure, proof of invalidity or the connection between the predicate and the current enhancement. No such qualifying defect or adverse-effect determination is made here.

##### The absent faultless-review issue has no controlling resolution

**Scope and authority:** No Court holding resolves whether section 2255 permits review when, through no fault of the prisoner, meaningful original review was unavailable or frustrated. O'Connor, Kennedy and Thomas leave that absent situation open. Stone-Zsela affirmatively adopts a faultless-loss exception in his separate access rule. Scalia joins the actual available-review holding but supplies an additional statutory objection to section 2255 as the forum for an outstanding state conviction's non-Gideon defect. A statement about the Court's limited holding does not attribute personal reservation or support for an exception to either concurrence.

Souter, Stevens and Ginsburg advocate a broader express-section-2255 route, while Breyer relies on a distinct proposal to reconsider the original sentencing restriction. Those positions do not establish five direct joins to a common faultless-loss exception, and no Marks synthesis makes one controlling. Daniels alleges no external impediment placing that issue before the Court.

##### Remedy and other boundaries

The Ninth Circuit judgment denying this section 2255 motion is affirmed. The 176-month federal sentence remains operative against these claims; the Court orders no new sentencing hearing, state-predicate invalidation, evidentiary hearing or sentence reduction. The constitutional validity of the 1978 and 1981 pleas is not reached because the claims fail the governing access rule.

Statutory classification, counting and exclusions concerning a predicate are distinct from the constitutional attack decided here. No such objection is newly adjudicated or revived. A predicate independently invalidated in proper proceedings presents a different application question; any later request must identify its authorized vehicle, applicable prerequisites, effect on the remaining qualifying predicates and available relief, without guaranteed reopening. No current sentencing-fact jury claim is presented. Coss uses a different state-prisoner vehicle; no companion result supplies a premise here.

#### Precedent Treatment

- **Custis v. United States, May 23, 1994:** Its ACCA sentencing-forum distinction and complete-counsel exception are extended to the section 2255 available-review circumstances decided here. Its expired-custody reservation was not already resolved, and the absent faultless-review question receives no common answer today. Custis itself is not overruled.
- **Gideon v. Wainwright:** Its constitutional appointment entitlement and valid-waiver boundary supply the preserved complete-deprivation exception.
- **Burgett v. Texas; United States v. Tucker:** Their special treatment of convictions obtained through unconstitutional complete denial of counsel remains; they do not make these deficient-representation allegations complete deprivation.
- **Parke v. Raley, December 1, 1992:** Its rebuttable regularity and lawful collateral proof rules are preserved. It establishes neither universal constitutional validity nor an additional review forum.
- **Maleng v. Cook:** Its distinction between an expired independent sentence and present custody survives. An enhancement consequence does not itself supply custody under the expired judgment.
- **Garlotte v. Fordice, May 30, 1995:** Its continuous-consecutive-series custody holding remains, distinguished from these independently expired state sentences. Custody, procedural access and merits remain separate.
- **Nichols v. United States, June 6, 1994:** Its limited permission for a valid fine-only conviction's particular Guidelines use remains. It does not decide a forum for constitutional attacks on allegedly invalid predicates.
- **Fiore v. White, January 9, 2001:** Its established missing-element violation and distinct statutory habeas prerequisites remain. It creates no universal forum for expired-predicate review.
- **Glover v. United States, January 9, 2001:** Its rejection of minimum-duration and offense-level prejudice thresholds remains. A cognizable sentencing injury does not itself supply this missing review route.
- **Almendarez-Torres v. United States, March 24, 1998:** Its aggravated-reentry element holding and retained collateral-review distinction remain; no new indictment or jury claim is presented in this case.

#### Law After Decision

The section 2255 channel does not reopen expired counseled predicates for involuntary-plea or ineffective-assistance claims when meaningful original review was available and no external impediment is alleged. Complete unconstitutional deprivation of required counsel remains distinct, with ordinary procedural and causal requirements. The governing rule concerns access, not a conclusive presumption that all final convictions are valid.

No controlling rule adopts or rejects a section 2255 exception for faultless loss of meaningful original review. Stone's affirmative exception, Scalia's additional statutory objection and the dissenters' different routes retain their stated noncontrolling status. Independently invalidated predicates, actual statutory classification and other authorized applications retain their own prerequisites and remedies. Custis remains effective at its original sentencing scope, extended only as stated here.

#### Separate Positions Relevant to the Decision

**Stone-Zsela, concurring.** He joins the common available-review holding, counsel exception and affirmance, and additionally adopts an affirmative faultless-loss exception as part of his governing access rule. Section 2255 review should remain available when meaningful original review was unavailable or frustrated through no fault of the prisoner, subject to applicable procedure, proof of constitutional defect and adverse effect on present custody. This is his present legal position, not merely a suggestion that an exception might be recognized later.

The distinction is between meaningful first review and repetitive litigation. Custis's forum rule and reservation do not establish that every completed conviction had an effective first opportunity, while Maleng's custody requirement must still be respected. A later enhancement creates a new incentive but does not make an earlier available remedy unavailable. Conversely, administrative inconvenience should not insulate a demonstrably unlawful current sentence from every authorized challenge. He does not equate every attorney failure with the prisoner's deliberate choice. Daniels identifies no complete counsel deprivation or external obstruction to meaningful first review, so even this affirmative exception does not allow his claims. Stone's exception has no controlling majority and is not borrowed from a decision in Coss. He orders no fresh sentencing or predicate invalidation.

**Scalia, concurring.** He joins the actual available-review rule and finality reasoning, but adds a statutory objection: section 2255's remedy for an unlawful federal sentence does not itself authorize adjudicating an outstanding state predicate's non-Gideon constitutional defect. Custis supports distinguishing an operative conviction from a collateral objection, although its actual sentencing holding did not already decide every expired-custody setting. A possible constitutional duty to provide review would concern the rendering jurisdiction and would not identify section 2255 as the remedy. He does not affirmatively endorse a section 2255 faultless-review exception. The Court's statement of what it has not decided is a scope description, not his agreement that such an exception exists. He orders no alternative forum remedy and decides no absent constitutional claim.

**Souter, dissenting, joined by Stevens and Ginsburg.** Section 2255 expressly authorizes review of the current federal sentence when constitutional error allegedly makes that sentence unlawful. Custis's restriction at the sentencing proceeding supplies no sufficient statutory basis for a new categorical exclusion from this express collateral remedy. Custis's available original-custody setting and reservation make the difference particularly important. The dissent's reading permits examination of an allegedly unlawful enhancement without treating Daniels as still in custody under the old state judgments.

Finality and stale evidence matter through applicable procedure and lawful proof rules; they do not justify the additional categorical bar. Parke preserves regularity and proof, Garlotte separates custody from remaining conditions, and Fiore and Glover distinguish an actual constitutional wrong and its causal effect from access requirements. The dissent preserves applicable defaults and does not automatically import all section 2254 exhaustion requirements into section 2255. It would reverse and remand for authorized consideration, not find either plea invalid, guarantee a hearing or order a lower sentence. The remaining qualifying predicates and the actual effect on lawful federal punishment would still require adjudication.

**Breyer, dissenting.** He also would reverse the categorical denial and remand, but on a distinct statutory and practical ground. Custis should be reconsidered: the sentencing scheme is better read to permit properly raised constitutional challenges to predicates at sentencing under lawful proof rules. His earlier opinion in United States v. Paleo supplies that approach. A prohibition at the original federal sentencing then generates restrictive or increasingly complex collateral exceptions; section 2255 is an awkward substitute for deciding the predicate issue when the enhancement is imposed. His disposition agreement with Souter does not make him an automatic join in every aspect of Souter's construction of section 2255. He proposes reconsideration, not an accomplished overruling, and establishes no plea defect, excuse of default, hearing entitlement or sentence reduction.

#### Source Notes

The [official report](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/USREPORTS-532/pdf/USREPORTS-532-374.pdf) supplies argument and decision dates, the four predicates, the present federal sentence, the two robbery-plea allegations, the prior statutory-classification appeal and the acknowledged absence of an asserted external review impediment. No evidentiary finding establishes either alleged plea defect. The differing statutory vehicles and review histories remain distinct.

#### Mandate, Remedy, and Stage

This motion remains denied and the current federal sentence remains operative. The Court orders no new hearing, resentencing or state-conviction invalidation. Any independently available original-conviction challenge or later authorized application must satisfy its own jurisdiction, procedure, predicate-effect and remedial requirements; none is found available or successful here. No Supreme Court return condition is retained.

**End of entry: Daniels v. United States, Merits decision, April 25, 2001.**

### Lackawanna County District Attorney v. Coss, No. 99-1884

Merits decision, April 25, 2001

#### Chronology and Posture

Argued February 20, 2001. On writ of certiorari to the United States Court of Appeals for the Third Circuit, reviewing its en banc judgment, 204 F.3d 453, which accepted review of the current sentence and directed an alternative retry-or-resentence remedy after finding trial prejudice from ineffective assistance in the earlier case.

The question is whether a state prisoner may challenge a current sentence under section 2254 on the ground that independently completed prior convictions were unconstitutional, including where a timely state collateral challenge has never been adjudicated. The Court resolves the judgment through the confined remand without a majority rationale answering the general access question.

Coss's 1986 assault, institutional-vandalism and criminal-mischief sentences had expired before his 1990 aggravated-assault sentence. His June 1987 state petition received appointed counsel and an answer but apparently no ruling for almost fourteen years; responsibility for the lapse remains unexplained. At resentencing the judge eliminated the challenged misdemeanors' numerical score effect, considered the criminal record with other stated sentencing factors, and imposed six to twelve years. The district court found deficient performance but no trial prejudice; the Third Circuit found a reasonable probability of a different result at the 1986 assault trial. Both identified consideration of the prior convictions. The federal petition was filed in September 1994. Daniels v. United States concerns a distinct federal-prisoner vehicle and is separately decided on the same date.

All nine Justices participated at argument and decision.

#### Judgment

**Vacated and remanded, 5–4.**

**Supporting:** Stone-Zsela, Stevens, Souter, Ginsburg and Breyer.

**Opposing:** O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy and Thomas.

#### Opinion Topology

| Writing | Author | Joined by | Relationship to judgment | Scope joined |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Opinion announcing the judgment | Breyer | Stevens, Souter, Ginsburg | Explains the five-vote vacatur and remand; four joins to its reasons | Entire confined procedural explanation, exact mandate and preservation of existing determinations; no adopted indirect-review rule |
| Concurrence | Souter | Stevens, Ginsburg | Joins the lead opinion and the exact judgment | Broader current-sentence review position and objection to deciding no effect here; no exclusive faultlessness gateway |
| Concurrence | Breyer | — | Joins the exact judgment and states a further individual position | First-effective-forum inquiry before deciding a possible constitutional exception; exception remains undecided |
| Concurrence in the judgment | Stone-Zsela | — | Supplies the fifth judgment vote; does not join the lead opinion | Own bounded access rule and responsibility inquiry in every event; those reasons and that additional inquiry do not enter the mandate |
| Dissent, Part I | O'Connor | Scalia, Kennedy, Thomas | Would reverse and remand without the existing federal relief direction | General indirect-review restriction, complete-denial-of-required-counsel exception and ordinary procedural prerequisites |
| Dissent, Part II | O'Connor | Kennedy | Same proposed reversal | Leaves open an additional exception for faultless denial of meaningful earlier review |
| Dissent, Part III | O'Connor | Kennedy, Thomas | Independently would reverse and remand on no adverse sentencing effect | Appellate review is available; combined sentencing record supports the same sentence without the challenged convictions |

No opinion commands five joins. Stone-Zsela agrees with every operative term of the judgment but supplies no fifth join to Breyer's procedural reasoning or to any other Justice's access theory. Scalia does not join the dissent's additional-exception discussion or its no-effect ground; Thomas joins the latter but not the former.

#### Controlling Propositions and Authority

##### Binding judgment without a controlling access rationale

**Judgment and operative mandate:** The Third Circuit judgment is vacated, and the case is remanded for the Court of Appeals to determine whether any pursued objection that the 1986 convictions did not increase the sentence selected within the range may be heard or was waived and, if it may be heard, to apply proper appellate review; and, only if an identified requirement of former 28 U.S.C. §2254(b) is shown to remain actually unresolved, to resolve that requirement on the materials bearing on it. The existing deficient-performance, trial-prejudice and consideration determinations keep their lawful scope unless lawfully displaced. The judgment directs no denial on a newly assumed absence of sentencing effect, no automatic release and no freestanding vacation of an expired judgment.

**Authority:** Stone-Zsela, Stevens, Souter, Ginsburg and Breyer support the exact judgment. Breyer's opinion announcing it is joined by Stevens, Souter and Ginsburg; its reasons have four joins. Stone-Zsela concurs only in the judgment. The mandate binds the parties, but the fifth judgment vote does not establish a controlling procedural rationale or access rule.

**Explanation of the four-Justice opinion:** The petition challenges Coss's current imprisonment, not custody under the independently completed 1986 sentences. Maleng permits that construction while leaving the extent of indirect review distinct. Custis limits the ACCA sentencing forum and leaves the materially different absence of meaningful earlier review undecided. Neither decision makes a mention of prior convictions conclusive proof of increased imprisonment or makes an unchanged numerical range conclusive proof of no increase.

The existing record presents two causal questions: the effect on the range and the effect on the sentence chosen within it. The challenged misdemeanors were removed from the prior-record score, but the lower courts found consideration in selecting the sentence. The published account of the lower briefing distinguishes an argument about the range from an objection to the within-range effect. Proper treatment of an actually pursued objection therefore requires a determination whether it may be heard and, if so, appellate review of the actual findings and inferences. The Court does not itself decide waiver, effect or a contrary factual finding.

Former section 2254(b) requires exhaustion, absence of an available state corrective process, or circumstances rendering that process ineffective to protect the prisoner's rights. The petition's September 1994 filing places it under the former framework. That law does not justify treating an unidentified prerequisite as outstanding. Any inquiry under the mandate must first identify a requirement actually left unresolved and use the materials bearing on it. The old petition's unexplained pendency does not itself establish prisoner fault, faultlessness or satisfaction of every procedural condition. Existing performance, trial-prejudice and consideration determinations are not erased by the return.

##### Fragmentation and remaining work

**No controlling access rule under Marks:** Stone-Zsela's affirmative no-fault access rule, the broader current-sentence route supported by Stevens, Souter and Ginsburg, and Breyer's refusal to decide an additional constitutional exception are different legal positions. Stone-Zsela and the three broader-route Justices supply only four affirmative access positions even if his proposed category is treated as a subset of theirs. Breyer supplies no affirmative exception to complete that coalition. The lead's procedural explanation has four joins, and Stone-Zsela's judgment-only vote supplies no shared rationale. Marks cannot turn the five votes for this mandate into five votes for an additional review exception, an exclusive faultlessness condition or a general indirect-review bar.

The Court makes no present finding of responsibility for the failed original review, waiver, abandonment, constitutional validity beyond the existing determinations' lawful scope, or adverse sentencing effect. The preservation question and any properly available causal review are work under the mandate; a former section 2254(b) inquiry arises only upon the stated actual-unresolved-requirement condition. No unconditional responsibility inquiry or new evidentiary hearing is ordered. The broader access question lacks a majority rationale. A newly discovered actual-innocence claim is outside the claim presented. No automatic resentencing, retrial, fixed reduction or release follows.

#### Precedent Treatment

- **Maleng v. Cook, 490 U.S. 488 (1989):** Its distinction between a challenge to current custody and a freestanding attack on an independently expired sentence remains. The accepted current-sentence construction does not itself settle indirect review's reach.
- **Custis v. United States, May 23, 1994:** Its ACCA sentencing-channel restriction and qualifying complete-denial-of-counsel exception remain at their existing scope. No majority here extends that restriction into a general section 2254 bar or resolves the reserved absence-of-meaningful-review category.
- **Garlotte v. Fordice, May 30, 1995:** Its continuous, unexpired consecutive-sentence rule remains distinct from these independently completed sentences; it does not supply direct custody over them.
- **Strickland v. Washington, 466 U.S. 668 (1984):** Its distinct deficient-performance and trial-prejudice inquiries remain. The existing lower determinations retain their lawful scope rather than being discarded by the remand.
- **United States v. Tucker, 404 U.S. 443 (1972), and Glover v. United States, January 9, 2001:** Their requirements concerning unconstitutional sentencing inputs and actual causal prejudice remain. Glover excludes an added minimum-duration or offense-level threshold; it does not establish that these convictions increased this sentence.
- **Parke v. Raley, December 1, 1992:** Its collateral regularity and lawful proof rules remain; they establish neither that this pending challenge received a decision nor that every old conviction is constitutionally valid.
- **Lindh v. Murphy, June 23, 1997, and Fiore v. White, January 9, 2001:** Lindh's filing-date distinction preserves the former framework here. Fiore's separation of constitutional merits from actually outstanding habeas conditions remains useful at that scope; its later-filed petition's statutory rules are not transferred wholesale to this case.
- **Marks v. United States, 430 U.S. 188 (1977):** The judgment does not yield a controlling access rationale by combining an affirmative rule with a Justice's refusal to decide it. No precedent is overruled.

#### Law After Decision

The exact vacatur and remand govern this case. No new general rule authorizes or forbids indirect review of expired convictions through section 2254, and no additional exception or exclusive no-fault gateway acquires controlling force. Existing custody, collateral-review and former habeas requirements retain their own operation. The Court decides neither that consideration necessarily increased punishment nor that removal of prior-record points necessarily eliminated within-range influence. Existing determinations remain within their lawful scope unless lawfully displaced, and the conditional appellate work cannot be enlarged into an automatic obligation to relitigate every predicate.

#### Separate Positions Relevant to the Decision

**Souter, concurring, joined by Stevens and Ginsburg.** They join Breyer's procedural opinion and every term of the remand while retaining the broader current-sentence route they would otherwise use to sustain the lower court's conditional remedial return. Custis's ACCA sentencing restriction does not foreclose a later petition directed to actual current custody; its dissenting statutory analysis and Tucker's concern with unconstitutional sentencing predicates support their position. Garlotte preserves the actual custody boundary, and Parke preserves ordinary collateral proof requirements. Their route remains subject to the applicable former habeas framework and a sufficient connection to the current sentence; it does not authorize repeated review free of procedural limits.

The sentencing range and selection within it are different. The lower findings, the published account of the prosecutor's submissions and the disputed availability of a within-range objection make a present contrary no-effect determination improper. Other history and the sentencing judge's stated reasons make the objection substantial without establishing that this Court should resolve it now. They accept the confined appellate return, rather than declaring waiver here, and do not require an exclusive finding of faultlessness as a new review gateway. Their preferred conditional remedy addresses present custody: retrial of the 1986 assault only if independently lawful, or resentencing for the 1990 assault without the unconstitutional predicate. That preference supplies no otherwise unavailable retrial authority or immediate relief under today's mandate.

**Breyer, concurring.** He leaves a constitutionally based additional exception undecided. Before resolving that issue, he would examine whether the present petition was effectively the first and only forum for review, using the actual state process and the explanation for its long lapse. The mere pendency of the state petition proves neither a meaningful opportunity nor its absence. He does not affirm a broad constitutional entitlement to indirect review or adopt a universal bar. He also refuses a present no-effect adjudication: removal of points does not dispose of discretionary influence, and the lower findings and disputed presentation require proper appellate treatment. His individual first-forum concern does not enlarge the judgment into an unconditional inquiry; the operative former section 2254(b) direction applies only if an identified requirement is shown actually unresolved. No exception, fault finding, new-hearing requirement or immediate sentence relief follows from his concurrence.

**Stone-Zsela, concurring in the judgment.** He would recognize a limited present-custody route when meaningful original review of the expired predicate was frustrated through no fault of the prisoner, subject to ordinary applicable procedural requirements, constitutional invalidity and proof of adverse effect on current custody. Finality protects adjudications and usable opportunities; it should not treat an unprovided adjudication as one already received. Custis left this different situation open, and former section 2254(b) recognizes an absent or ineffective state process. A nominally pending petition need not require indefinite waiting, but fourteen years of unexplained inaction alone does not establish diligence, blame or satisfaction of the applicable requirements.

He maintains that responsibility for the failed original review must be determined in every event. The inquiry should examine the actual filings, notices, testimony and explanation for the delay; it should not demand a statistical demonstration that fourteen years is excessive or attribute the lapse without supported facts. Removal of points bears on one causal route, while possible influence on the sentence selected within the range remains distinct. His preferred rule would permit an appropriately conditioned opportunity for lawful resentencing only upon satisfaction of its conditions, with no automatic release or freestanding vacation of the expired judgment. He nevertheless agrees with the exact narrower mandate and preserves his additional responsibility inquiry as his separate position. He joins no lead reasoning, and his proposed access rule does not become the Court's law.

**O'Connor, dissenting, joined by Scalia, Kennedy and Thomas in Part I.** They would apply the general restriction against reopening an independently expired predicate through a later section 2254 sentence challenge after ordinary review opportunities, preserving the exception for unconstitutional complete denial of required counsel without valid waiver and the ordinary habeas prerequisites. Ineffective representation is a different claim. Finality and the practical difficulty of reviewing old convictions support the channel limitation; an excuse from exhaustion does not itself create an indirect-review channel. They accept the current-sentence construction without finding the old judgments valid, the petition abandoned or Coss responsible for the delay. Their proposed reversal removes the present federal retry-or-resentence direction while leaving independently authorized state review under its own law.

**O'Connor's Part II, joined by Kennedy.** They leave open whether faultless denial of meaningful earlier review permits an additional route. The current sentencing-effect issue can defeat relief even assuming such a route. They adopt no new innocence exception in a case presenting ineffective assistance rather than a newly discovered innocence claim. Scalia and Thomas do not join this additional-exception discussion.

**O'Connor's Part III, joined by Kennedy and Thomas.** They would independently reverse because the challenged convictions did not increase the actual six-to-twelve-year sentence. They accept consideration but regard the adverse-effect inference as clearly erroneous. They rely on the combined removal of score and range effects, the present aggravated assault, independent supported juvenile and adult history, and the sentencing judge's stated concerns with the present crime, community protection, criminal disposition, rehabilitation, testimony and continued lawbreaking. They conclude that the same sentence would have been selected without the challenged convictions; no single factor supplies a general harmlessness rule.

Their appraisal does not treat dropped-charge arrests as proved misconduct, count the challenged convictions again as independent valid history or invent an express same-sentence disclaimer. They acknowledge the contrary force of actual consideration and the absence of an explicit counterfactual sentence. Glover forbids disregarding a proved increase as too small; it does not require finding an increase whenever a conviction is mentioned. They regard the essential adverse-effect determination already addressed below as properly reviewable, without asserting that the prosecutor actually presented a within-range argument in the panel brief or announcing a general power to disregard waiver. Their no-effect ground is their appellate assessment of the existing record. Scalia does not join it.

#### Source Notes

The [official report, 532 U.S. 394](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/USREPORTS-532/pdf/USREPORTS-532-394.pdf), supplies the argument date, record chronology, lower findings and remedial direction at pages 397–400, and the published characterization of the lower appellate submissions at page 409. That characterization is not a judicial determination that a within-range objection was waived. The sentencing court's removal of points and its consideration of the record are distinct facts; neither alone establishes the effect on the sentence selected. The source does not establish responsibility for the unadjudicated state petition or prove that any identified former section 2254(b) requirement remains unresolved. The question is stated in substance, without claiming a verbatim grant formulation.

#### Mandate, Remedy, and Stage

The case returns to the Third Circuit. Vacatur removes the existing retry-or-resentence direction; further disposition must follow the confined mandate and applicable law. The current sentence is not reduced by this judgment. No unconditional inquiry into responsibility for the failed original review, new evidentiary hearing or return to this Court is directed.

### In re Independent Service Organizations Antitrust Litigation: CSU, L.L.C. v. Xerox Corporation

Merits decision, June 4, 2001

#### Chronology and Posture

Federal Circuit No. 99-1323; District of Kansas MDL-1021; judgment below, 203 F.3d 1322. The Federal Circuit affirmed summary judgment on February 17, 2000; rehearing was disposed of April 13, 2000. The intellectual-property counterclaims explain the Federal Circuit route. The Court reviews whether statutory patent or copyright exclusivity conclusively defeats the preserved monopolization claims concerning replacement parts, service manuals and diagnostic software. CSU seeks further adjudication; Xerox seeks affirmance on its statutory exclusion rights.

Chief Justice Alex-Lamar Stone-Zsela and Justices Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer participate.

#### Judgment

The Court vacates the affected antitrust judgment and remands for assessment under the governing monopolization requirements, giving the distinct patent and copyright interests substantive weight.

| Judgment component | Disposition and vote | Supporting Justices | Opposing Justices | Remedy or remand |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Patent-based defense to the preserved monopolization claims | Vacate and remand, 6–3 | Stone-Zsela, Stevens, Kennedy, Souter, Ginsburg, Breyer | O'Connor, Scalia, Thomas | Assess the preserved claim under ordinary monopolization requirements, giving the relevant statutory exclusion rights substantive weight. |
| Copyright-based defense to the preserved monopolization claims | Vacate and remand, 6–3 | Stone-Zsela, Stevens, Kennedy, Souter, Ginsburg, Breyer | O'Connor, Scalia, Thomas | Assess the preserved claim under ordinary monopolization requirements, giving the relevant statutory exclusion rights substantive weight. |

The judgment removes the conclusive legal barriers sustaining the affected summary judgments. It awards no damages, parts supply, software license or other compulsory access and determines no infringement issue. Summary judgment remains available on an adequately supported ground.

#### Opinion Topology

Justice Stevens delivered the opinion of the Court, joined in full by Chief Justice Stone-Zsela and Justices Kennedy, Souter, Ginsburg and Breyer. The opinion controls both vacaturs, both holdings, the ordinary proof requirements, the substantive exclusion interests and the bounded remand.

Justice Scalia dissented, joined by Justices O'Connor and Thomas. They would affirm both components on the patent-refusal and copyright-justification grounds, while preserving liability for an independently unlawful antitrust wrong.

#### Controlling Propositions and Authority

**Common authority:** Stevens's six-Justice opinion, with the full joins identified above, directly controls both holdings and the remand.

##### Patent exclusion and monopoly maintenance

**Holding and operative rule:** A valid patent and unilateral refusal to supply protected parts neither establish monopolization nor conclusively defeat a supported claim that control of those inputs maintains monopoly power in a distinct service market. Without an additional prerequisite of procurement fraud, sham litigation or illegal tying, the plaintiff must prove relevant-market power, unlawful acquisition or maintenance, competitive injury and causation; lawful statutory exclusion and returns on invention retain substantive justificatory force.

**Authority:** The common six-Justice Court opinion directly controls this patent holding.

**Controlling explanation:** Eastman Kodak requires actual market and exclusion analysis and permits a single-brand aftermarket only when real substitutes and constraints support it. Its separate section 2 holding is not limited to tying, but it did not decide this patent defense. Grinnell distinguishes unlawful maintenance from success through superior products, business acumen or accident. Aspen Skiing makes actual exclusion and business explanation material without imposing a general duty to help rivals.

Patent exclusion is a legal entitlement, not an ordinary preference that a rival's desire for access can overcome. Nor does a distinct antitrust market by itself prove conduct exceeds the patent grant. But neither the grant nor the patent-misuse protection against loss of relief merely for refusing to license supplies immunity from an independently proved monopolization wrong. The statutes operate together by requiring competitive misconduct beyond the ordinary consequences of enjoying exclusivity while retaining the entitlement's genuine weight.

Xerox's end-user sales, selective verification, barriers to alternate supply and alleged service consequences warrant assessment under that rule, not a finding of liability. The lower court's exhaustive gate foreclosed that inquiry without fraud, sham suit or tying. Removing it leaves CSU the burden of competent evidence; refusal, hostility to competitors, harm to CSU and a service-market label are insufficient. Quality, inventory, workable support, superiority, lawful innovation returns and supported efficiencies remain material. Documents, testimony and actual conduct may establish or defeat the elements; no universal econometric-study requirement applies. Professional Real Estate Investors protects petitioning on its own terms, not every commercial refusal.

##### Copyright justification and the required remand

**Holding and operative rule:** Copyright's exclusive rights provide a substantial justification for refusing protected manuals or diagnostic-software access, but do not conclusively defeat an objectively supported monopoly-maintenance claim merely because unlawful acquisition or extension beyond the copyright grant is absent. The same ordinary monopolization burdens apply; subjective pretext, rival injury or refusal alone cannot rebut the statutory justification, and every eventual remedy must fit a proved violation and causal injury while preserving lawful protected returns insofar as compatible with curing that violation.

**Authority:** The common six-Justice Court opinion directly controls this copyright holding and both remand components.

**Controlling explanation:** The lower court's copyright analysis differs from its patent rule: it gave exclusion a presumptively valid justification and found no definitive rebuttal through unlawful acquisition or extension beyond the grant. The Court preserves the strength of that statutory justification but rejects a conclusive limit that excludes an otherwise supported antitrust wrong from consideration. A copyright label cannot replace the competitive inquiry, just as an allegation of exclusion cannot nullify copyright.

California Dental requires attention to the actual competitive mechanism and substantial explanations rather than a fixed label or compulsory form of market study. NYNEX and Brunswick keep harm to competition and antitrust injury distinct from the loss suffered by one supplier. Those requirements remain CSU's burdens. The Court does not transfer Kodak's evidentiary result to this different record or treat ordinary copyright exclusion as unlawful intent.

On remand, the courts must identify the actual protected rights, challenged conduct, competitive mechanism and supported evidence under ordinary summary-judgment standards. A failure of proof or a claim concerning only lawful exclusion permits renewed summary judgment; this decision requires no new discovery, hearing or trial automatically. The settlement's temporary access provisions neither establish antitrust liability nor show that forced access is an appropriate remedy for this opt-out plaintiff.

**Limits and work remaining:** Actual market definition, durable monopoly power, unlawful exclusion, causation, antitrust injury and lawful relief require adjudication under the announced rule. They are remaining liability and remedial work, not reservations of the rule against categorical protection. Patent validity, infringement and copyright ownership are outside the presented question. No final misuse ruling follows; any live consequence of removing the lower court's patent-scope predicate must be decided below on its own preserved grounds. No damages or compulsory-access terms are entered.

#### Precedent Treatment

- Eastman Kodak Co. v. Image Technical Services, Inc.: applies the distinct power, conduct and justification inquiries and its fact-supported aftermarket rule; the patent and copyright defense left open there is now resolved. Its tying holding creates no tying claim here.

- United States v. Grinnell Corp.: preserves monopoly power and unlawful acquisition or maintenance as distinct requirements, with superiority, business acumen and accident outside the offense.

- Aspen Skiing Co. v. Aspen Highlands Skiing Corp.: applies scrutiny of actual exclusion and business justification without a general duty to supply competitors.

- California Dental Association v. Federal Trade Commission: applies restraint-specific consideration of effects and substantial explanations; no automatic full-market study or motive-only test follows.

- NYNEX Corp. v. Discon, Inc.: preserves the distinction between injury to one supplier and injury to the competitive process; its section 1 categories are not converted into a new section 2 exemption.

- Brunswick Corp. v. Pueblo Bowl-O-Mat, Inc.: preserves the independent antitrust-injury requirement.

- Professional Real Estate Investors, Inc. v. Columbia Pictures Industries, Inc.: preserves its objectively-baseless-litigation prerequisite for the sham petitioning theory it addressed; that rule is not transplanted as immunity for commercial refusal.

- TrafFix Devices, Inc. v. Marketing Displays, Inc.: its expired-patent functionality and trade-dress holding remains intact and supplies no disposition of valid patent or copyright exclusion here.

#### Law After Decision

A properly supported monopoly-maintenance claim involving protected parts or service information receives rights-sensitive section 2 analysis without an exhaustive fraud, sham-litigation, tying, unlawful-acquisition or beyond-grant gate. Genuine patent and copyright exclusion remain substantial legal interests. No automatic aftermarket, unlawful refusal, duty to supply or access entitlement follows. Both types of claim retain ordinary evidentiary and injury requirements; infringement and remedial design remain distinct.

#### Separate Writings

**Scalia, dissenting, joined by O'Connor and Thomas.** They would affirm both components because the presented refusal theory identifies no adequate wrong beyond protected exclusion. Patent law protects the owner's decision to withhold protected products; service-market effects do not by themselves establish conduct outside that grant. CSU presents no procurement fraud, sham enforcement or illegal tie. For copyright, the strong statutory exclusion justification remains unrebutted by a legally sufficient showing of unlawful acquisition, extension or another independent wrong. Those are distinct grounds, not identically worded immunities.

Kodak's inquiry into aftermarket power remains binding, but power does not itself establish prohibited conduct or decide the stronger statutory-right defense it left open. Its dissenting concerns about proprietary-product choices reinforce the need to identify an offense beyond refusal; they do not displace Kodak's actual holding. NYNEX keeps rival loss distinct from competitive injury, and Professional Real Estate Investors separates legally protected conduct from hostile motive without making every refusal an act of petitioning. These Justices preserve liability for an independently unlawful antitrust wrong; they reject the remand because the supported refusal theory supplies none here. Their position would end the affected antitrust claims, without deciding infringement, damages or compulsory access.

### J. E. M. Ag Supply, Inc. v. Pioneer Hi-Bred International, Inc., No. 99-1996

Merits decision, December 10, 2001

#### Chronology and Posture

Argued October 3, 2001. On writ of certiorari to the Federal Circuit. Pioneer asserted utility patents concerning corn varieties against unauthorized seed sellers. The question is whether the Plant Patent Act and Plant Variety Protection Act categorically exclude newly developed plants from utility-patent protection under §101. The accepted defense concerns statutory eligibility and exclusivity, with patent-specific and transaction-specific issues remaining distinct.

All nine Justices participated at argument or submission and decision.

#### Judgment

**Affirmed, 7–2.**

**Supporting:** Stone-Zsela, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas and Ginsburg.

**Opposing:** Stevens and Breyer.

The categorical ineligibility defense is rejected. Patent litigation may continue under the independent requirements of the protection actually invoked.

#### Opinion Topology

SOUTER, J., delivered the opinion of the Court, in which STONE-ZSELA, C. J., and O'CONNOR, SCALIA, KENNEDY, THOMAS, and GINSBURG, JJ., joined. SCALIA, J., filed a concurring opinion. STEVENS, J., filed a dissenting opinion, in which BREYER, J., joined.

#### Controlling Propositions and Authority

##### The plant statutes permit concurrent utility-patent protection

**Holding and operative rule:** Newly developed plants, including the corn varieties at issue, are not categorically excluded from 35 U.S.C. §101 because the Plant Patent Act or Plant Variety Protection Act also covers the subject. A claimant invoking utility protection must meet that statute's independent requirements; certificate-specific exceptions govern their own statutory right and do not create a general immunity from a valid utility patent.

**Authority:** The Opinion of the Court, joined by Stone-Zsela, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas and Ginsburg; seven direct joins support the complete eligibility and coexistence rationale.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that Congress's plant-specific protections can operate alongside the broadly expressed utility-patent categories. Chakrabarty rejects a categorical living-subject exclusion and gives effect to human invention within the statutory language. The Plant Patent Act's limited reproduction right and relaxed description requirement address obstacles to protection without declaring that a plant satisfying the more demanding general statute loses that protection. The Plant Variety Protection Act likewise supplies a distinct right with distinct requirements and exceptions.

The contrary argument has practical force: utility rights can restrict seed saving or research that the certificate regime permits. But different rights and eligibility requirements are not an irreconcilable statutory conflict. Neither plant statute expressly makes its protection exclusive, and enactment or recodification does not require exclusion from §101. Each statute retains work; a court may not create a hybrid by importing certificate exceptions into utility-patent law.

Asgrow construes the former certificate saved-seed provision. It does not answer utility-patent exclusivity or supply a universal agricultural exemption. Its former-law limits and the enacted transition remain effective within the certificate regime. Administrative practice and legislative silence are not treated as binding interpretations. The present category-wide answer leaves novelty, nonobviousness, disclosure and other patent requirements independently operative; it establishes neither validity of every asserted claim nor enforceability of every sales restriction.

**Precedent treatment:** Chakrabarty's broad statutory reading is applied; Asgrow remains confined to its certificate setting; Deuel and State Street's independent requirements and limits remain intact.

##### Certificate protections retain their own statutory version and exceptions

**Holding and operative rule:** Concurrent utility eligibility does not rewrite the protection supplied by a plant-variety certificate. Former-law saved-seed protection and the amendments effective April 4, 1995 retain their enacted conditions and transition; no specific certificate, seed transaction or exception entitlement is adjudicated here.

**Authority:** The same seven-Justice Opinion of the Court preserves these boundaries as part of the coexistence rule.

**Controlling explanation:** Under Asgrow, former §2543 permits reproductive sale only of seed genuinely saved for the farmer's own planting, produced from seed obtained with the owner's authority or its descendants. Seller and purchaser must primarily farm for nonreproductive crop sales, and the sale must comply with state seed-sale law. Changed planting plans may permit disposition of genuinely retained seed; intentional multiplication for marketing is not exempt. The independent protection for bona fide nonreproductive sales in customary channels remains; a buyer who diverts seed sold through those channels to planting is deemed to have section 2567 notice that the diversion infringes. Section 2544's exemption for use and reproduction for plant breeding or other bona fide research likewise belongs to that certificate regime.

The 1994 amendments delete the former farmer-sales proviso for the amended regime from April 4, 1995. Except as section 14 itself provides through the refiling rule of section 14(b) and the notice rule of section 14(c) described next, section 14(a) keeps varieties with certificates issued before April 4, 1995, or with applications pending on that date, under the Plant Variety Protection Act as in effect the day before. Section 14(b) permits refiling under amended terms while retaining the original filing date for §42. Section 14(c) requires identification of amended protection in a notice claiming it and retains sanctions for false or misleading claims or labels. These rules continue to govern the certificate right; none is converted into an exception to a separate utility patent. The Court makes no transaction-specific exception or transition finding.

**Precedent treatment:** Asgrow's saved-seed construction and former-law force are preserved; the statutory amendment's express transition remains unchanged.

#### Precedent Treatment

- Diamond v. Chakrabarty, 447 U.S. 303: its broad reading of §101 applies to qualifying human invention rather than a categorical exclusion of living subject matter.
- Asgrow Seed Co. v. Winterboer, No. 92-2038: its former certificate saved-seed construction and transition limits remain; no utility-patent exception is derived from it.
- In re Deuel, January 14, 1997: ordinary supported nonobviousness requirements remain distinct from category eligibility.
- State Street Bank & Trust Co. v. Signature Financial Group, Inc., January 14, 1999: broad category language does not remove genuine statutory limitations or independent proof requirements.

#### Law After Decision

Plants meeting the utility statute's requirements may receive utility patents alongside the plant-specific regimes. Each right carries its own requirements and exceptions. The certificate amendments and Asgrow's surviving former-law operation retain their actual reach; the decision supplies no generalized saved-seed or research exemption from utility-patent rights.

#### Separate Positions Relevant to the Decision

Scalia concurs and joins the Court in full. Chakrabarty already authoritatively construed the general subject-matter language to reject the supposed ambiguity based on living subject matter. The plant-specific statutes therefore cannot merely be used to clarify that language into the opposite categorical exclusion; a claimed change must satisfy the more demanding inquiry into displacement of the existing rule. This canon explanation supplements rather than narrows the Court's eligibility and coexistence rationale.

Stevens dissents, joined by Breyer, and would treat the plant-specific enactments as the exclusive protection for their covered plant categories. The 1930 Act expressly amended the general patent provision, conditioned protection on asexual reproduction and limited the reproduction right; the later variety statute supplied seed-reproduction protection with saved-seed and research limits. In their view, the utility route bypasses those particular congressional choices, and recodification did not undo them. Different eligibility thresholds do not explain why increased originality should eliminate protections Congress deliberately conferred on agricultural users. Chakrabarty's human-made microorganism lay outside those specialized categories, so the dissent preserves its holding and does not exclude all living inventions or independently qualifying processes. The dissent would reverse and remand on the category defense.

#### Source Notes

35 U.S.C. §§101–103, 112 and 161–164; 7 U.S.C. §§2543–2544; Pub. L. 103-349 §§10, 14–15; J. E. M. Ag Supply, No. 99-1996, 534 U.S. 124, for the patent dispute and statutory setting.

#### Mandate, Remedy, and Stage

The Federal Circuit's rejection of the categorical defense stands. Remaining patent-specific validity, exhaustion, infringement, competition and contract issues proceed only within their ordinary preserved scope; this decision resolves none of them by the eligibility answer alone.

**End of entry: J. E. M. Ag Supply, Inc. v. Pioneer Hi-Bred International, Inc., Merits decision, December 10, 2001.**

### Clay v. United States, No. 01-1500

Merits decision, March 4, 2003

#### Chronology and Posture

537 U.S. 522. Argued January 13, 2003. On writ of certiorari to the United States Court of Appeals for the Seventh Circuit, 30 Fed. Appx. 607.

The question is whether, after affirmance of a federal conviction and no petition for certiorari, §2255 finality occurs when the appellate mandate issues or when the certiorari period expires. The Seventh Circuit affirmed the dismissal of Clay's motion as untimely using the mandate date. Clay seeks consideration of his collateral motion; the United States agrees with the certiorari-expiration rule, and appointed amicus defends the judgment.

Clay's arson and drug convictions were affirmed on November 23, 1998. The mandate issued December 15, 1998. His time to seek certiorari expired February 22, 1999, and he filed his initial §2255 motion on February 22, 2000. The limitations ruling prevented adjudication of his collateral claims.

#### Judgment

**Reversed and remanded, 9–0.**

**Supporting:** Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg, Breyer.

Clay's motion is timely under the ordinary final-judgment trigger. The mandate-based limitations dismissal is reversed.

#### Opinion Topology

GINSBURG, J., delivered the opinion of the Court, in which STONE-ZSELA, C. J., and STEVENS, O'CONNOR, SCALIA, KENNEDY, SOUTER, THOMAS, and BREYER, JJ., joined.

#### Holdings

##### Finality includes the available certiorari period

**Holding and operative rule:** When a federal conviction is affirmed on direct appeal and the defendant does not petition for certiorari, the judgment becomes final for §2255's ordinary one-year limitation when the time to seek certiorari expires. The appellate mandate does not start that clock while ordinary Supreme Court review remains available. Clay's February 22, 2000 motion was timely because his certiorari period expired on February 22, 1999.

**Authority:** Ginsburg's unanimous opinion controls the finality rule, statutory explanation, application and reversal with remand.

**Controlling explanation:** Finality in the ordinary collateral-review setting includes the end of direct review and the expiration of the time available to seek it. Griffith, Barefoot, and Johnson supply that established usage. The mandate performs a different function by returning authority to the lower court. Supreme Court Rule 13 measures the ordinary certiorari period from the judgment, not the mandate, so that administrative return cannot extinguish the remaining opportunity for direct review.

The appointed amicus relies on the contrast with §2244(d)(1)(A), which expressly mentions completion of direct review or expiration of the time to seek it. Section 2255 instead uses the unqualified term final. That difference does not select the mandate as an earlier event. Unlike a provision that specifies one event while omitting another, §2255 leaves the familiar legal concept intact. The fuller state-prisoner formulation also supplies a uniform federal measure across differing state usages. Russello's recognition that textual differences may matter does not make this difference decisive without its statutory context. Lindh likewise teaches attention to the particular comparison, not an automatic rule for every omission.

The Court therefore gives §2255 the ordinary direct-review meaning of finality. The rule follows from the statute and review framework independently of the Government's agreement. The Seventh Circuit used the wrong starting event; the district court must proceed with Clay's motion under the corrected calculation and the remaining applicable requirements.

#### Precedent Treatment

- Griffith v. Kentucky, 479 U.S. 314: applies its ordinary account of finality after direct review ends or the certiorari opportunity expires.
- Barefoot v. Estelle, 463 U.S. 880, and United States v. Johnson, 457 U.S. 537: apply their established collateral-review usage including the expiration of available direct review.
- Russello v. United States, 464 U.S. 16: preserves attention to meaningful statutory differences while rejecting the inference that §2255's unqualified finality term selects the mandate.
- Lindh v. Murphy: applies contextual comparison and distinguishes its separate temporal question; its construction of AEDPA's application to identified pre-enactment cases remains intact.

#### Law After Decision

For federal convictions affirmed on direct appeal without a certiorari petition, the ordinary §2255 one-year period runs from expiration of the time to seek certiorari. Courts must use that lawful expiration date in the statutory calculation. The rule resolves the finality trigger that produced the dismissal here and governs comparable pending disputes under that trigger.

#### Source Notes

No. 01-1500; 537 U.S. 522; 30 Fed. Appx. 607. 28 U.S.C. §2255, paragraph 6(1), and §2244(d)(1)(A); Supreme Court Rule 13. The dates establish an ordinary certiorari-period calculation.

#### Mandate, Remedy, and Stage

The district court must proceed to Clay's unresolved collateral grounds under the now-settled timeliness of his motion.

**End of entry: Clay v. United States, Merits decision, March 4, 2003.**

### Verizon Communications Inc. v. Law Offices of Curtis V. Trinko, LLP, No. 02-682

Merits decision, January 13, 2004

#### Chronology and Posture

Decided January 13, 2004, on merits submission. The Court reviews the Second Circuit's reinstatement of an antitrust complaint alleging discriminatory performance of an incumbent telephone carrier's network-access obligations. The questions concern independent monopoly-maintenance conduct, the customer's entitlement to damages, and its request for prospective relief.

Trinko is an AT&T customer. AT&T obtains local-network access from Verizon. The complaint alleges that Verizon degraded rival-customer order processing to discourage rival patronage. Federal and state regulators investigated access failures and imposed remedial measures and financial consequences. The complaint seeks damages and an injunction providing rivals access on terms at least as favorable as Verizon's own. The case comes at the pleading stage.

Chief Justice Stone-Zsela and Justices Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer participated in submission and decision.

#### Judgment

| Judgment component | Disposition and vote | Supporting Justices | Opposing Justices | Remedy or remand |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Clayton Act section 4 damages | Reinstatement reversed; existing damages theory dismissed for insufficient directness, 5–4 | Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Souter and Thomas | Stone-Zsela, Kennedy, Ginsburg and Breyer | Dismiss the existing damages theory. |
| Independent section 2 conduct and section 16 prospective relief | Existing reinstatement basis vacated and claim remanded, 6–3 | Stone-Zsela, Stevens, Kennedy, Souter, Ginsburg and Breyer | O'Connor, Scalia and Thomas | Bounded adjudication under the ordinary offense, personal-threat and equitable-remedy requirements. |

The requested access-parity decree is not authorized by the regulatory duty alone. The lower courts must evaluate the existing prospective claim under the rules below and deny relief if its offense, threatened-injury or remedial requirements fail. Communications-statute and state-law claims retain their existing status outside this antitrust review.

#### Opinion Topology

STEVENS, J., delivered the opinion of the Court, in which SOUTER, J., joined; STONE-ZSELA, C. J., and KENNEDY, GINSBURG, and BREYER, JJ., joined Parts I and III; and O'CONNOR, SCALIA, and THOMAS, JJ., joined Part II. SCALIA, J., filed an opinion dissenting in part, in which O'CONNOR and THOMAS, JJ., joined. GINSBURG, J., filed an opinion dissenting in part, in which STONE-ZSELA, C. J., and KENNEDY and BREYER, JJ., joined.

| Writing | Author | Joined by | Relationship to judgment | Scope joined |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Opinion of the Court, Part I | Stevens | Stone-Zsela, Kennedy, Souter, Ginsburg and Breyer | Supports the conduct remand | Independent monopoly maintenance; six direct joins. |

#### Holdings

##### 1. Regulatory access duties and independent monopolization remain separate.

A regulated firm's breach of an access duty violates Sherman Act section 2 only when the plaintiff independently establishes relevant-market monopoly power, unlawful acquisition or maintenance through an exclusionary competitive mechanism, and causally connected antitrust injury. Deliberate discriminatory degradation of rival-customer access may qualify when it maintains monopoly power and lacks an adequate business justification; regulatory breach, rival injury or exclusionary purpose alone supplies no substitute for those elements.

**Authority:** Part I of Stevens's opinion, joined by Stone-Zsela, Kennedy, Souter, Ginsburg and Breyer.

The saving clause preserves existing antitrust law without enlarging its duties. CSU v. Xerox requires an actual input-exclusion inquiry while giving statutory entitlements and legitimate business explanations substantial force. That separation extends to this regulated setting. The complaint identifies selective impairment of rival-customer orders as the alleged means of making competing service ineffective. Courts must examine that mechanism under ordinary pleading and proof requirements, rather than infer liability from a regulatory finding or end the inquiry because access originated in a regulatory command.

Aspen Skiing illustrates unjustified exclusion; a prior voluntary course of dealing is probative rather than an exclusive element. Kodak requires attention to actual market constraints and conduct. NYNEX distinguishes a regulatory wrong from harm to competition. Technical requirements, investment incentives, efficiency and operational explanations therefore remain material to whether the alleged discrimination constitutes unlawful maintenance. Those requirements answer the contention that the Court has simply converted compulsory assistance into an antitrust supply duty.

A freestanding leveraging label cannot avoid the offense's elements. If an attempt theory is pursued, Spectrum Sports requires anticompetitive conduct, specific intent and a dangerous probability of monopolization in the relevant market. The plaintiff bears the ordinary civil burden. The lower courts must assess the existing allegations and any amendment properly permitted under ordinary rules; an insufficient theory is subject to dismissal through the ordinary procedures.

##### 2. The existing damages theory is insufficiently direct.

A customer claiming damages from interference with its carrier must satisfy Associated General Contractors by establishing a sufficiently direct personal business-or-property injury, assessed through the causal relationship, intervening injury, more direct claimants, duplicate recovery and workable allocation. Trinko's existing theory places its service loss downstream of the carriers' impaired access and service provision, so the damages claim must be dismissed.

**Authority:** Part II of Stevens's opinion, joined by O'Connor, Scalia, Souter and Thomas.

Steamfitters applies the directness inquiry to the actual chain of injury rather than a plaintiff's status. The carriers suffer the access impairment and are positioned to seek damages for it. This complaint's consequential customer loss presents the overlapping recovery and allocation concerns that the directness requirement addresses. Alleging that the strategy targeted rival patronage does not itself eliminate the intervening carrier injury.

McCready permits recovery when a plaintiff's own deprivation is the instrument of the exclusionary scheme. The present theory instead derives the customer's service loss from impairment of its carrier's access. The distinction answers the proposed targeted-customer analogy without making noncompetitor or consumer status conclusive. This statutory damages conclusion remains distinct from Article III and from the requirements governing an injunction.

##### 3. Prospective relief requires its own injury and remedy inquiry.

Section 16 permits relief upon a significant personal threat of injury of the kind the antitrust laws protect against, arising from what makes an impending, continuing or likely recurring violation unlawful. A damages-directness defect based on duplicate recovery, allocation or a more direct damages claimant does not by itself defeat that prospective claim; the plaintiff must establish the independent offense, its personal threat and the ordinary equitable grounds for a lawful, effective decree.

**Authority:** Part III of Stevens's opinion, joined by Stone-Zsela, Kennedy, Souter, Ginsburg and Breyer.

Cargill distinguishes threatened antitrust injury from completed business-or-property loss and the additional allocation concerns attending damages. The alleged systematic impairment of rival service warrants an independent examination of the threat to this existing rival-carrier customer. The actual regulatory measures bear on whether a threat persists and whether additional relief is necessary. Their existence establishes neither complete redress nor entitlement to another decree.

The requested access parity cannot follow merely from telecommunications law's sharing duty. Any injunction must target established antitrust harm, respect lawful access terms consistently with the cure, and have a necessary, workable scope. A narrower effective remedy may be considered when supported by the existing claim and ordinary equity. If the independent offense, personal prospective threat or lawful effective remedy is missing, relief must be denied. These requirements resolve the governing legal questions and define the lower courts' remaining application work.

#### Precedent Treatment

- **CSU v. Xerox:** Its separation of statutory entitlements, independent exclusion and substantial justifications is extended to regulated network-input conduct; it supplies no general right to assistance.
- **Eastman Kodak Co. v. Image Technical Services:** Applied for actual market and conduct analysis rather than categorical economic assumptions.
- **Aspen Skiing Co. v. Aspen Highlands Skiing Corp.:** Preserved as an example of unjustified exclusion; its prior-dealing evidence is not made an exclusive gateway.
- **NYNEX Corp. v. Discon:** Applied to distinguish regulatory misconduct and rival injury from the competitive harm the offense requires.
- **Spectrum Sports v. McQuillan:** Applied to preserve the independent conduct, intent and dangerous-probability elements of any attempt theory.
- **Associated General Contractors v. Carpenters and the Steamfitters decision:** Applied to dismiss this consequential damages theory on actual causal directness, claimant priority, duplication and allocation.
- **Blue Shield of Virginia v. McCready:** Distinguished because the present damages theory rests on service loss consequent on the carrier's access impairment, rather than a direct deprivation serving as the exclusionary instrument.
- **Cargill, Inc. v. Monfort of Colorado, Inc.:** Applied independently to threatened antitrust injury and the different operation of prospective relief; damages allocation does not decide section 16.

#### Law After Decision

Regulated input conduct receives the ordinary monopoly-maintenance inquiry, with substantial attention to legitimate legal and operational explanations. Regulatory noncompliance neither proves that offense nor immunizes it. The present damages theory fails the separate directness requirement, while the prospective claim returns for the independent offense, personal-threat and equitable-remedy inquiry. Access parity requires support as a lawful antitrust remedy for established harm, rather than simply as a regulatory obligation.

#### Separate Writings

**Scalia, dissenting in part, joined by O'Connor and Thomas.** The alleged customer diversion is the consequence of deficient compelled assistance, rather than an independently sufficient antitrust wrong. CSU preserves the requirement of such a wrong and the absence of a general duty to assist rivals; NYNEX separates regulatory misconduct from injury to the competitive process. Exclusionary purpose and a sharing command cannot supply the antitrust dealing obligation the complaint assumes. The dissent would dismiss the prospective claim for that conduct defect. It accepts Cargill's distinct equity standard and does not treat the damages ruling as a substitute for the missing offense analysis. All three join the Court's damages holding.

**Ginsburg, dissenting in part, joined by Stone-Zsela, Kennedy and Breyer.** The alleged impairment of end-user orders may itself be the instrument by which Verizon discourages rival patronage. McCready therefore supplies a potentially closer causal model than the remote expense chain in Steamfitters. The Court should remand for assessment of Trinko's actual personal loss, intervening steps, overlap with carrier recovery and workable allocation under Associated General Contractors. The dissent would allow any amendment properly permitted by ordinary rules and dismiss an insufficient theory, while allowing a sufficiently direct independent claim to proceed. It does not find the damages predicates established. All four join the conduct and prospective-relief holdings.

The complaint's access-parity prayer appears at App. 49–50. Alleged discriminatory order handling and continuing competitive harm remain allegations at the reviewed pleading stage.

#### Mandate, Remedy, and Stage

The lower courts must dismiss the existing damages theory and assess the prospective claim under Parts I and III. That work includes the alleged market and exclusion mechanism, substantial justifications, a significant personal prospective threat, the actual protection supplied by regulatory measures, and the necessity and scope of any effective antitrust remedy. Ordinary pleading and amendment rules govern; a failure of a required predicate permits dismissal or denial at the appropriate stage. Other statutory and state-law claims retain their separate procedural status.

**End of entry: Verizon Communications Inc. v. Law Offices of Curtis V. Trinko, LLP, merits decision, January 13, 2004.**

### Eastman Kodak Co. v. Image Technical Services, Inc., No. 90-1029

Merits decision, June 8, 1992

Argued December 10, 1991; decided June 8, 1992. On writ of certiorari under 28 U.S.C. § 1254(1) to the United States Court of Appeals for the Ninth Circuit. In its May 1, 1990 decision, [903 F.2d 612](https://static.case.law/f2d/903/cases/0612-01.json), Wiggins, joined by Chambers, reversed summary judgment on the preserved Sherman Act §§ 1 and 2 claims; Wallace dissented. The Northern District of California had entered summary judgment for Kodak on April 18, 1988, after limited discovery, rejecting a tie and a duty to deal. The [official report](https://tile.loc.gov/storage-services/service/ll/usrep/usrep504/usrep504451/usrep504451.pdf) gives the argument and decision dates. All nine Justices participated at argument and decision; Thomas took his seat before argument.

Eighteen independent service organizations allege that restrictions on Kodak-compatible replacement parts exclude them from service. The questions concern whether parts and service are distinct products and whether their sales are conditioned on one another; whether competition in equipment categorically precludes power in its parts or service markets; and whether power, exclusion, and asserted business reasons present genuine factual disputes. Kodak seeks restoration of summary judgment. Respondents seek continued proceedings, not a present damages verdict. The Ninth Circuit did not reach a rule-of-reason theory that respondents had not raised in opposition to summary judgment. Review is limited to the preserved tying and § 2 claims; an independent claim of conspiracy with original equipment manufacturers is not adjudicated.

Respondents offer evidence of demand for independent service, exceptions allowing customers to service their own machines, restrictions on alternative parts supplies, information costs in predicting lifetime service expense, and substantial costs of replacing installed equipment. Their evidence supports estimates of nearly complete control of compatible parts and eighty to ninety-five percent of Kodak-equipment service. [Prices, effects, and the asserted policy changes remain disputed](https://archive.org/details/micro_IA40385013_0064). These materials and reasonable inferences permit review of summary judgment; they do not establish actual markets, monopoly power, deception, or liability.

The Court affirms the Ninth Circuit's reversal of summary judgment, 6–3, on each of two components: the preserved § 1 tying claim, and the preserved § 2 monopolization and attempted-monopolization claims. Stone-Zsela, White, Blackmun, Stevens, Kennedy, and Souter support affirmance on both. O'Connor, Scalia, and Thomas would reverse on both and restore summary judgment. Blackmun delivers the Opinion of the Court, joined in full by Stone-Zsela, White, Stevens, Kennedy, and Souter. Scalia dissents, joined in full by O'Connor and Thomas. The six Justices adopt all four controlling holdings; there are no partial joins, judgment-only votes, or separate concurrences.

For a Sherman Act § 1 tying claim, functionally related products are distinct when sufficient separate consumer demand makes separate provision efficient. Evidence that Kodak parts and service meet that test, and that Kodak conditions parts sales on buying its service or forgoing independent service, creates genuine disputes about distinct products and conditioning; respondents may proceed but must prove all remaining tying elements. Blackmun’s Opinion of the Court, joined by Stone-Zsela, White, Stevens, Kennedy, and Souter, controls with six votes, including the author. The commerce volume is not disputed here; no liability judgment follows.

The Court holds that practical consumer demand, rather than a product’s functional relationship to another product, determines whether the two are distinct for tying purposes. Jefferson Parish Hospital District No. 2 v. Hyde supplies that demand-based inquiry and the requirements of tying power and a substantial amount of tied commerce. The record shows that independent organizations have supplied service separately, that customers have preferred independent service, and that some customers service their own equipment. Those materials permit a finding that separate provision is efficient. Kodak’s restrictions on parts sales to independent organizations and on customers’ use of independent service also permit a finding of conditioning. The fact that parts are installed during service does not conclusively combine both into one product. Respondents still must establish the necessary market power and other elements; the Court holds only that the district court could not end this claim for want of separate products or an arguable tie.

Jefferson Parish Hospital District No. 2 v. Hyde, 466 U.S. 2 (1984): applied for separate demand, conditioning, tying power, and tied commerce; functional integration alone does not merge products. Celotex Corp. v. Catrett, 477 U.S. 317 (1986): applied to preserve respondents’ burden of producing evidence on the elements challenged at summary judgment.

Competition in an equipment market does not as a matter of law preclude market power in its parts or service markets. Under Rule 56, a party cannot obtain summary judgment solely through an economic prediction contradicted by evidence permitting a rational inference of power, including material information and switching costs and actual price or exclusion effects; unsupported speculation remains insufficient. Blackmun’s Opinion of the Court, joined by Stone-Zsela, White, Stevens, Kennedy, and Souter, controls with six votes. The holding rejects a conclusive defense, not the relevance of equipment competition.

The Court holds that Kodak’s proposed equipment-market constraint must be tested against the record. Matsushita Electric Industrial Co. v. Zenith Radio Corp. requires a plausible inference from evidence; it does not authorize a court to replace disputed facts with an economic model. Here the alleged conduct can produce immediate aftermarket gains, unlike the prolonged and implausible loss-producing conspiracy examined there. Evidence of costly equipment replacement and difficult lifetime service-price comparisons permits a finding that some installed customers cannot readily escape unfavorable terms. Evidence of price and exclusion effects supports testing that account at trial. Kodak may show that informed new purchasers and equipment rivalry adequately discipline its policies, but that conclusion is not inevitable as a matter of law. Anderson v. Liberty Lobby, Inc. preserves the factfinder’s role when a genuine dispute could affect the result. The Court neither presumes power from imperfect information nor treats every durable-product seller as a monopolist.

Matsushita Electric Industrial Co. v. Zenith Radio Corp., 475 U.S. 574 (1986): applied as a rational-inference requirement and distinguished on the alleged economic mechanism; it creates no categorical equipment-market defense. Anderson v. Liberty Lobby, Inc., 477 U.S. 242 (1986): applied to identify genuine material disputes; evidence and reasonable inferences receive the ordinary Rule 56 treatment. Jefferson Parish Hospital District No. 2 v. Hyde, 466 U.S. 2 (1984): preserved; respondents must prove actual tying power despite rejection of a conclusive equipment-competition defense.

A relevant product market may consist of one brand’s parts or service when the actual commercial alternatives and competitive constraints support that definition; brand identity alone neither establishes nor defeats such a market. Evidence of Kodak-specific compatibility, a very high parts share, a high service share, and entry and switching barriers creates a genuine dispute about monopoly power sufficient to resist summary judgment on § 2. Blackmun’s Opinion of the Court, joined by Stone-Zsela, White, Stevens, Kennedy, and Souter, controls with six votes. The record permits a market and power finding; it does not conclusively establish either.

The Court holds that market boundaries turn on the alternatives buyers can actually use. United States v. Grinnell Corp. requires monopoly power in a relevant market, and United States v. E. I. du Pont de Nemours & Co. directs attention to substitution and competitive restraint. Owners needing compatible parts cannot necessarily substitute another brand’s parts, and changing equipment may cost substantially more than the aftermarket service at issue. Respondents also present evidence of Kodak’s dominant parts position, high service share, and obstacles to independent supply. Together these materials permit an inference of power to control prices or exclude competition. Kodak’s equipment competitors remain relevant because they may constrain lifetime prices; the Court does not exclude that evidence. But their existence does not settle the market question. The result establishes neither a single-brand rule for all manufacturers nor a monopoly presumption from a patent, a proprietary design, or numerical share considered apart from actual constraints.

United States v. Grinnell Corp., 384 U.S. 563 (1966): applied to require monopoly power in a relevant market, distinct from exclusionary conduct. United States v. E. I. du Pont de Nemours & Co., 351 U.S. 377 (1956): applied to practical substitution and competitive constraints; no categorical prohibition on a one-brand market follows.

Monopolization under § 2 requires monopoly power plus willful acquisition or maintenance of that power, distinguished from success through a superior product, business acumen, or accident. Evidence of parts-access restrictions that may exclude service rivals creates a genuine issue of unlawful maintenance, and Kodak’s asserted quality, inventory, and free-riding explanations do not conclusively eliminate it on this record; no general duty to supply rivals or final finding of pretext is imposed. Blackmun’s Opinion of the Court, joined by Stone-Zsela, White, Stevens, Kennedy, and Souter, controls with six votes. Attempted monopolization remains a distinct claim with its own intent and dangerous-probability requirements, not a consequence automatically established by this holding.

The Court holds that the reasons and competitive effect of Kodak’s restrictions cannot be settled on this summary-judgment record. Grinnell distinguishes unlawful maintenance of monopoly power from lawful commercial success. Aspen Skiing Co. v. Aspen Highlands Skiing Corp. makes the character of challenged exclusion and its business justification material without imposing a general obligation to help competitors. Respondents’ evidence permits a finding that restricting access to necessary parts protects Kodak’s service position. The asserted quality rationale must be considered with the continued self-service exception and evidence about independent service. Inventory explanations must be tested against evidence that equipment failures and parts needs do not change with the provider, while supplier restrictions may impede alternative sources. Calling independent service free riding because the providers did not enter the equipment and parts businesses does not conclusively justify exclusion. Kodak may prove legitimate reasons and defeat liability. The Court awards no damages, orders no compulsory supply, and leaves ultimate liability and injury for further proceedings.

United States v. Grinnell Corp., 384 U.S. 563 (1966): applied to distinguish willful monopoly maintenance from lawful success. Aspen Skiing Co. v. Aspen Highlands Skiing Corp., 472 U.S. 585 (1985): applied to the challenged conduct and offered business reasons; no universal obligation to deal is created. Brunswick Corp. v. Pueblo Bowl-O-Mat, Inc., 429 U.S. 477 (1977): preserved; injury to a competitor alone does not establish antitrust injury or entitlement to damages.

Effective June 8, 1992, equipment competition remains relevant evidence but cannot categorically bar a sufficiently supported claim concerning the parts or service markets. The Court applies existing tying and monopolization law with ordinary Rule 56 burdens. It creates no automatic monopoly from a patent or brand, universal duty to deal, or present right to recovery. Power and exclusion remain separate elements, and success through superior products remains lawful. Monopolization and attempted monopolization remain distinct. No prior decision is overruled, and the dissent does not change current law. Holmes v. Securities Investor Protection Corp., decided March 24, 1992, retains its holdings on directness under the Racketeer Influenced and Corrupt Organizations Act and participation under the Securities Investor Protection Act; those holdings neither decide these disputed Sherman Act elements nor establish injury here.

Scalia, joined by O'Connor and Thomas, would reverse and restore summary judgment on both preserved claims. They regard ordinary dependence on a brand's replacement parts after buying durable equipment as derivative power that equipment competition generally disciplines. In their view, the asserted information and switching costs do not warrant extending the category of tying treated as unlawful without a rule-of-reason inquiry, or treating every brand-specific parts or service market as an antitrust monopoly, absent the necessary primary-market power. They consider the Court's approach liable to burden ordinary integration, distribution choices, and proprietary component sales without the competitive power the Sherman Act requires. Their disagreement concerns the legal significance of the asserted dependence and the sufficiency of the evidence; they do not find respondents entitled to final relief. The separate rule-of-reason route was not preserved below. Their position does not displace the Court's inquiry into the evidence or create an immunity for manufacturers.

Supreme Court merits review is complete. The Ninth Circuit's reversal of summary judgment stands, and ordinary mandate implementation permits the properly preserved and supported claims to proceed below. Respondents must prove each claim's elements, including the distinct intent and dangerous-probability requirements of attempted monopolization and the requirements for any recovery. Ultimate markets, power, unlawful conduct, pretext, antitrust injury, causation, damages, and remedial design remain for further proceedings. The Court awards no damages, enters no final injunction, orders no compulsory parts supply, and makes no ultimate finding of liability. It retains no proceeding and imposes no nonroutine follow-on condition; later trial findings and relief require their own lawful proceedings.

**End of entry: Eastman Kodak Co. v. Image Technical Services, Inc., Merits decision, June 8, 1992.**
