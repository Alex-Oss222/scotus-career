## Components and common legal limits

**A — review law.** All eight apply Garceau to the post-effective-date merits application, notwithstanding earlier preliminary proceedings and Souter, Ginsburg and Breyer's preserved disagreement in that case. Section 2254(d)(1) tests the November 23, 1992 state merits adjudication against Supreme Court law established by that date. Williams's objective unreasonable-application distinction, Early, Visciotti and Lockyer supply the present method. Neither the number of judges on either side nor an erroneous independent constitutional answer determines objective reasonableness. No Justice treats later Penry II, Bell or current harmless-error law as newly established substantive law in 1992.

**E — protected mitigation.** Lockett and Eddings supply the opportunity to give relevant mitigation lawful effect; Skipper establishes the relevance of postcrime adjustment and future behavior; Boyde asks whether the complete charge and trial created a reasonable likelihood of forbidden application. Conversion and conduct after the crime may support a life sentence even without reducing culpability when the crime occurred. Admission alone is insufficient, and constitutionally available mitigation need receive no prescribed weight or belief. Scalia and Thomas can assume the controlling protection without affirmatively adopting the broader theory they have opposed.

**G — statutory application.** The prosecutor repeatedly made a legal exclusion argument, not merely a sincerity or weight argument. The judge did not tell the jury his privately expressed broad understanding of factor (k), and his response to the objection did not expressly correct the exclusion. The whole record also includes eight mitigation witnesses, the substantial conversion and prison-conduct presentation, defense counsel's forceful explanation, directions identifying the court as the source of law, the qualified all-evidence instruction, and the prosecutor's alternative arguments that the conversion was insincere or insufficient. These facts support an actual disagreement about the statutory range; none is erased to make the answer easier.

**H — effect and remedy.** If G bars the claim, there is no need to resolve an independent best constitutional answer or harmlessness. If E and G are established, current Brecht, O'Neal and Penry II require the State to establish beyond a reasonable doubt, on the complete penalty record, that the preserved restriction did not contribute to the actual death sentence. Error and contribution remain distinct. The inquiry must assess the mitigating presentation and the prosecutor's alternative arguments alongside the murders, sexual violence, earlier violence and incriminating statements. Strong aggravation does not by itself carry the State's burden, and a reasonable likelihood of unconstitutional exclusion does not by itself finish the separate effect inquiry. The return is adjudicative work under the proper rule, not a claim that documents are missing or an invitation to invent a record. Any eventual conditional relief concerns the penalty alone and permits a lawful new penalty proceeding within an appropriate period or a nondeath sentence. The conviction and unrelated habeas claims remain outside this ruling.

## Express remedial departure from the historical dissent

**Historical departure:** Stevens, Souter and Ginsburg retain the historical dissent's constitutional and section 2254(d)(1) conclusions, but withhold its immediate determination of harmfulness. The historical dissent reasoned from the reasonable possibility of excluded mitigation and the risk of execution to a substantial-and-injurious effect. Current Penry II expressly makes error and actual contribution separate questions for this kind of preserved mitigation restriction and returns the latter for a complete penalty-record assessment; Souter authored both pertinent parts, Ginsburg joined both, and Stevens joined effective mitigation and supported the corrective return while withholding the effect standard. These are concrete prior judicial commitments to distinct decisional work. Under that current rule, the uncorrected exclusion establishes their E/G conclusion but does not alone dispose of all the aggravation, alternative weighting arguments and actual-sentence evidence relevant to H.

The changed burden by itself would not explain a less immediate remedy: a burden more favorable to the prisoner is not a reason to discard an already demonstrated effect. The difference rests instead on the governing separation and manner of decision, and on the insufficiency of the historical dissent's stated inference to complete that particular inquiry. No new factual assurance of harmlessness is found. The remand puts the burden on the State and permits conditional sentence relief if it cannot carry it; it does not require the prisoner to prove an additional effect. Stevens's prior reservation about the governing standard remains recorded and does not become an affirmative join in Penry II Part III.
