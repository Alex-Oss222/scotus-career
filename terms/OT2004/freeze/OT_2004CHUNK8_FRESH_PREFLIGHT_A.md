## Clingman: fresh lower-record supplement

The full published Tenth Circuit opinion and every footnote were independently recovered and read from the bounded reporter transcription at `https://static.case.law/f3d/363/html/1048-01.html`: Beaver v. Clingman, 363 F.3d 1048, No. 03-6058, April 6, 2004. The saved source is `research/chunk8-a/beaver-lower-static.html`, with mechanically extracted readable text at `research/chunk8-a/beaver-lower-static.txt`. The source contains the pre-event lower opinion alone; no later citing-case wrapper was read. Its historical descriptions of prior Supreme Court doctrine are evidence of the lower court's reasoning, not authority overriding this timeline's Timmons or Jones.

1. **Actual statute and request.** Oklahoma section 1-104 limits a primary or runoff voter to the party shown on the registration, subject to the provision allowing a recognized party to invite registered independents. The LPO sought party-option participation by all registered voters. Its requested injunction concerns that restriction; it does not seek compulsory inclusion by an unwilling party or multiple-primary participation. Party recognition and other ballot provisions explain context but are not added claims.
2. **Distinct trial findings and accepted grounds.** The district court found a less-than-severe burden. It rejected the proffered confusion and administrative concerns on the evidence, and also rejected protection of the LPO from swamping or raiding. It nevertheless accepted the interest in identifiable, viable parties and primary results reflecting party membership. Thus the trial judgment did not find each asserted concern substantiated. The neutral shorthand listing the State's arguments must not be read as such a finding.
3. **Confusion and administration.** The appellate opinion reports the district court's findings that simple posted eligibility rules could address confusion and that the record supplied no basis for the asserted insuperable administrative burdens. The Tenth Circuit upheld those findings under clear-error review and reported finding no supporting record evidence of the predicted administrative problems. This is a record-specific assessment, not a rule that every future party-option system is costless or immune from proved administrative objections.
4. **Stability evidence and its contested significance.** The district court relied partly on evidence that nearly 24 percent of Oklahoma primaries in 2000 were decided by five percent or less, reasoning that crossover might change close results. The Tenth Circuit accepted that some results could change but found the connection between such changes and political instability inadequately demonstrated. The statistic does not establish actual raiding, duplicate voting or a finding that any identified result was corrupted. The model may assess the legal force of preventive interests on this record without converting possibility into an established injury.
5. **Whose association is at issue.** Tashjian's footnote 13 expressly reserves other-party invitations and identifies potential disorganization of other parties as a different consideration. Oklahoma's argument accordingly includes interests beyond protecting the inviting LPO from its own choice. The Tenth Circuit rejected an asserted associational entitlement of other parties to control their registrants' opportunity to accept another party's invitation, while distinguishing their right to exclude unwanted participants from their own primaries. The new Court must assess that argument under the actual entering law; it cannot dismiss every asserted state interest merely because the LPO consented.
6. **Comparative systems and precise lower rationale.** The opinion observes that Utah and Alaska employed party-option systems and reports no instability there. That is the appellate opinion's comparative observation, not recovered empirical proof that no instability occurred. The Tenth Circuit found the burden severe and the State's showing insufficient under strict scrutiny, reversing the denial of a permanent injunction and remanding. The new Court may reject its severity analysis, but must still apply the actual lesser-burden inquiry and address the record rather than treat strict-scrutiny error alone as proof that every state justification succeeds.

These additions preserve material facts, lower-court grounds and competing arguments without selecting the Court's judgment. They require no factual reconstruction and are effective neutral preparation before independent commitments.
