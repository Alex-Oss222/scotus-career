## Bankruptcy exemption and all three exception conditions

The following text is mechanically copied from the official 2004 Code, 11 U.S.C. §522(d)(10)(E): https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/USCODE-2004-title11/html/USCODE-2004-title11-chap5-subchapII-sec522.htm . Subsection (d)(10) introduces it as the debtor's right to receive the payment.

(E) a payment under a stock bonus, pension, profitsharing, annuity, or similar plan or contract on account of illness, disability, death, age, or length of service, to the extent reasonably necessary for the support of the debtor and any dependent of the debtor, unless—
(i) such plan or contract was established by or under the auspices of an insider that employed the debtor at the time the debtor's rights under such plan or contract arose;
(ii) such payment is on account of age or length of service; and
(iii) such plan or contract does not qualify under section 401(a), 403(a), 403(b), or 408 of the Internal Revenue Code of 1986.

The three conditions after “unless” are conjunctive: the insider must have employed the debtor when the debtor's rights arose; the payment must be age- or service-based; and the plan must fail the named tax qualification. Insider establishment alone is not enough. The reasonably-necessary-for-support ceiling remains separate from the exception.

## Account facts

The Rouseys received employer pension lump sums on termination and rolled those amounts into an IRA in each spouse's name. The accounts qualify under §408(a), are nonforfeitable, and restrict early access through the ordinary ten-percent additional tax before age 59½. Their agreements require minimum distributions to begin by April 1 following the calendar year in which they attain 70½. Failure to take the required minimum distribution carried a fifty-percent tax on the deficiency under then-effective §4974(a). The exemption sought concerns portions of the IRAs; the amount reasonably necessary for their support is a separate question from categorical eligibility.

## Early distribution qualifications

The §72(t) text from the official 2001 supplement is saved as tax_72_t_2001.txt, mechanically extracted from https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/USCODE-2001-title26/html/USCODE-2001-title26-subtitleA-chap1-subchapB-partII-sec72.htm . Event-eligible 2004 amendments in Public Law 108-311 §§207(6)-(7) update only the dependent/child cross-references pertinent here; the enacted text is saved as pl108_311.txt. Section 208 makes these amendments effective for taxable years beginning after December 31, 2004. A failed fetch of a 2004 consolidated §72 page is not treated as a source. No later distribution exception is imported.

The general tax is ten percent of the distributed amount includible in gross income. Relevant exceptions permit distributions after age 59½; after the participant's death to a beneficiary or estate; attributable to defined disability; as substantially equal payments at least annually for life or life expectancy (including joint life expectancy with a designated beneficiary); to cover qualifying medical expenses up to the §213 deduction amount, regardless of itemization; for qualified unemployment-related health premiums; for qualified higher education; and for qualifying first-home acquisition. Section 72(t)(2)(A)(vii) also covers a levy under §6331. The age-fifty-five separation and domestic-relations-order exceptions do not apply to IRAs, under §72(t)(3)(A).

Health-premium withdrawals require unemployment compensation for twelve consecutive weeks after separation, are available in the taxable year unemployment compensation is paid OR the succeeding taxable year, and cannot exceed qualifying insurance expenses for the individual, spouse and dependents. Distributions after sixty days of reemployment are excluded. A regulated self-employed exception covers individuals who would have received unemployment compensation but for self-employment. The published opinion's abbreviated same-year description must not erase the statute's express succeeding-year alternative.

Higher-education withdrawals are capped at qualified expenses for the taxpayer, spouse, child or grandchild at an eligible institution and reduced for coordinated benefits. First-home distributions have a $10,000 lifetime ceiling, a 120-day qualifying-use window and the statute's first-buyer and principal-residence definitions. Qualifying acquisition costs include acquisition, construction, reconstruction and usual or reasonable settlement, financing and closing costs. The first-buyer definition generally requires no principal-residence ownership interest during the preceding two years by the individual and spouse; the statutory preserved §1034 transition condition remains in the source. A delay or cancellation permits the specified 120-day rollover treatment.

For substantially equal payments, modification before 59½ or within five years from the first payment triggers recapture of the additional tax avoided and deferral-period interest, except modification because of death or disability. This rule is §72(t)(4), with §72(t)(2)(A)(iv) the payment exception. The ordinary separation-from-service requirement for periodic payments under the designated qualified employer plans in §72(t)(3)(B) is not a universal IRA requirement. The statute separately imposes twenty-five percent, rather than ten percent, on qualifying SIMPLE-account distributions in the first two years; the provided record describes ordinary qualifying rollover IRAs, not SIMPLE accounts.

Nothing here decides whether any particular distribution satisfies an exception. Exact definitions and coordination provisions remain in the copied statutory text.
