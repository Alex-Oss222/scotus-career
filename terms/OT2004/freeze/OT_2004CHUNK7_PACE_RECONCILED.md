## Results and operative state-law conditions

O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy and Thomas affirm the rejection of statutory tolling and reject the asserted independent equitable protection on assumed traditional authority because the necessary diligence showing is not made. Stevens, Souter, Ginsburg and Breyer reverse the statutory ruling and remand for exclusion of the state-review interval, exact federal calculation and the remaining habeas requirements; they do not reach independent equity. The Associates divide four to four on the statutory disposition. No Chief Justice vote or complete Court tally is supplied.

Section 9545(b)(1), as enacted in 1995, applies the one-year-from-finality limit to any collateral petition, including a second or subsequent petition. The petition must allege and the petitioner must prove one of three grounds for an exception: unlawful interference by government officials with presentation of the claim; predicate facts previously unknown and not ascertainable through due diligence; or a constitutional right recognized after the ordinary period by the United States Supreme Court or Pennsylvania Supreme Court and held retroactive by that recognizing court. Under subsection (b)(4), retained and appointed defense counsel are excluded from government officials. Under subsection (b)(2), an exception petition must also be filed within sixty days after the claim could have been presented. Under subsection (b)(3), finality follows completion of direct review, including the specified discretionary Supreme Court review, or expiration of review time. These requirements must not be collapsed into generic reference to “an exception.”

Act section 3(1) applies the amendments to petitions filed after the January 16, 1996 effective date but gives the one-year transition permission for previously final judgments only to a first petition. Pace's November 27, 1996 petition was his second. The actual December 3, 1998 state appellate ruling found it untimely and no exception alleged and proved. Every Associate accepts that state-law disposition; the federal meaning of properly filed remains the question. Section 9545's filing provisions and section 9543's eligibility-for-relief provisions perform distinct jobs.

## Statutory fork and adversarial test

**Affirming rule.** A collateral application rejected as untimely under a genuine application-filing deadline is not properly filed under section 2244(d)(2). Exceptions define when later filing is permitted; if the application satisfies none, receipt by a clerk, initial merits consideration and judicial work deciding the exception do not cure the filing defect. Proper filing and actual pendency remain separate requirements.

Artuz distinguished application delivery and acceptance requirements from claim-specific relief bars and expressly reserved exception-bearing deadlines. This statute states when any petition must be filed, includes second petitions, makes allegation and proof prerequisites for an exception, and imposes an additional sixty-day filing condition. The judicial nature of the inquiry does not convert that function into a merits rule. Carey prevents treating the trial court's earlier merits discussion as an override of the state appellate untimeliness ruling. The affirming four therefore decline statutory exclusion for the November 27, 1996–July 29, 1999 interval.

The strongest contrary argument is that the exceptions depend on claim-specific facts and law, resembling Artuz's relief bars, and that a later appellate ruling can consume the federal time while a prisoner pursues exhaustion. The affirming four accept the practical problem but reject its proposed statutory solution: filing conditions can require judicial factfinding, and pendency alone cannot erase Congress's additional proper-filing requirement. The rule does not recast every unsuccessful constitutional claim as an improperly filed application. It answers the real exception-bearing application deadline and the actual state rejection.

**Reversing rule.** When an application is delivered to the appropriate tribunal in the prescribed form and the timing regime requires adjudication of claim-based exceptions, a later untimeliness ruling under that regime does not retroactively destroy proper filing for section 2244(d)(2). Government interference, unknown predicate facts and retroactive constitutional rights require the judicial claim inquiry that makes this regime analogous to Artuz's relief bars. The actual state rejection remains conclusive as state law while the federal application-filing classification remains independent.

The strongest contrary argument is the explicit one-year filing command, separate statutory placement and risk of prolonging federal time with a hopelessly late paper. The reversing four answer that headings and form do not replace the federal functional inquiry; these particular claim-dependent exceptions resemble relief adjudication. Their construction still requires compliance with genuine tribunal, delivery, form and fee requirements and actual pendency of the pertinent application. Carey resolved timely continuation through an interval, not Artuz's expressly reserved exception-bearing deadline. Duncan's exclusion of federal pendency remains intact. They would exclude the actual state-review interval, calculate the resulting federal time and apply remaining exhaustion, default and merits restrictions. They do not find a state exception satisfied or award the writ.

The historical dissent's additional proposed federal good-faith/abuse screening is not silently installed as a new element in these frozen commitments; no finding of deliberate delay is made about Pace. ## Equity and changed entering-law boundaries

The affirming four assume traditional equitable authority requiring a sufficiently extraordinary impediment and reasonable diligence. They do not decide a universal habeas-equity rule. They reject this asserted entitlement on diligence, without needing to determine whether the claimed combination of uncertain state law and exhaustion doctrine is sufficiently extraordinary.

Pace's asserted impediment is a legal trap during state exhaustion: he says he needed to pursue the state remedy while uncertain timing law made it appear available. His plea and sentencing grounds concern facts available in 1986, and he raised a version of the plea claim then; the assistance ground concerns events in or before 1991. His first collateral proceeding ended in September 1992 and his second began over four years later. State review ended July 29, 1999; federal filing occurred December 24. The asserted trap addresses time spent in the state proceeding but does not establish reasonable pursuit across the material preceding interval or the additional five months after the proceeding ended.

This is a causal, record-specific conclusion. It does not make five months universally excessive, demand perfect diligence, count pre-AEDPA years against a limitation period not yet enacted, or infer the absence of papers from missing research files. The old claims and old subject of the family affidavits are supported; affidavit execution or delivery dates are not invented. Their subject matter alone is not treated as proof of when a new affidavit was received.

The strongest alternative is remand for a fuller diligence inquiry, particularly because the Third Circuit emphasized the uncertainty issue and a capital/noncapital distinction. The affirming four reject that remand on the asserted ground and supported chronology: the specified trap does not account for the necessary intervals of pursuit. They do not adopt the lower court's proposition that noncapital status categorically defeats equitable relief or that every instance of unclear state law is legally insufficient. Their affirmance rests on a different sufficient ground, as the frozen model already records.

Entering law materially limits what can be said about precedent without changing these votes:

- **Pliler:** Unlike the historical baseline's broader reservation, current Pliler affirmatively permits equitable protection for its defined court-caused loss, requiring causation, reasonable diligence, proof and a lawful effective remedy. Pace identifies no federal court-directed mixed-petition dismissal election or materially misleading federal direction. The four affirmers preserve that existing rule while assuming, rather than deciding, broader authority for the different obstacle asserted here.
- **Rhines:** Current law permits retention of a timely mixed petition on a reasonable explanation for nonexhaustion, potential merit, absence of abuse and reasonable definite deadlines; it does not use an extraordinary-circumstances good-cause test. That present protection does not automatically revive this late application or establish a procedure Pace consciously rejected in 1996–1999.
- **Johnson:** Current section 2255 paragraph 6(4) begins with diligent discovery of an actual status-changing vacatur order and adds no deadline for procuring a nonexistent order. Pace has no comparable later order or new commencement predicate. Considering diligence when equity itself is invoked does not reintroduce the procurement condition Johnson rejected for its distinct statutory trigger.

The reversing four reach no independent equity result because their statutory exclusion removes the asserted limitations barrier subject to exact recalculation. Their nonreach position does not endorse the adverse diligence conclusion, approve automatic equitable protection, or adopt the Third Circuit's capital/noncapital distinction.
