## Full appointment conditions and the additional statutory duties

The official enactment fills the ellipsis in the objective extract's conditional-plea category and supplies subsection (4), which is relevant to the asserted understanding of counsel consequences. Its components must remain separate:

| Provision | Complete operative conditions or duty | Limits material here |
|---|---|---|
| Subsection (1) | Subject to subsections (2) and (3), a defendant pleading guilty, guilty but mentally ill, or nolo contendere is denied appointed appellate counsel to review conviction or sentence. | This is the statutory default, not a determination that the Constitution permits every application or that a defendant validly waived a federal right. |
| Subsection (2)(a) | For an indigent defendant entering one of the specified pleas, the trial court must appoint appellate counsel if the prosecutor seeks leave to appeal. | This is one independently sufficient mandatory trigger. |
| Subsection (2)(b) | The same mandatory duty applies if the defendant's sentence exceeds the upper limit of the minimum sentence range of the applicable sentencing guidelines. | Preserve the upper-limit/minimum-range terms. This is not identical to subsection (3)'s comparison with the range the defendant alleges should have been scored. |
| Subsection (2)(c) | The same mandatory duty applies if the Michigan Court of Appeals or Supreme Court grants the defendant's application for leave to appeal. | The text addresses appointment after a grant; the availability of later counsel does not itself decide the federal right to assistance preparing the initial application. |
| Subsection (2)(d) | The same mandatory duty applies if the defendant seeks leave to appeal a conditional plea under Michigan Court Rule 6.301(C)(2) or its successor rule. | The actual rule-based conditional-plea category is retained; no finding that Halbert entered that kind of plea is supplied. |
| Subsection (3) | The trial court may appoint appellate counsel for an indigent defendant entering a specified plea only when all three conditions apply: the defendant seeks leave to appeal a sentence on an allegation of improper scoring of an offense variable or prior-record variable; the defendant objected to the scoring or otherwise preserved the issue; and the imposed sentence is an upward departure from the upper limit of the minimum range the defendant alleges should have been scored. | The conditions are conjunctive. Eligibility leaves appointment discretionary. A sentencing complaint alone is insufficient, but the statute permits preservation otherwise than an objection at sentencing. No matching finding for every condition is made here. |
| Subsection (4), before acceptance | While establishing an understanding and voluntary plea under Michigan Court Rule 6.302 or its successor, the court must advise that, except as otherwise provided in the section, acceptance means waiver of an attorney appointed at public expense to assist in filing a leave application or with other postconviction remedies; it must determine whether the defendant understands that waiver. | This is a statutory advice-and-understanding command. It is distinct from whether the actual record establishes an informed constitutional relinquishment. Neither statutory wording nor an omission of prescribed wording automatically resolves that federal inquiry. |
| Subsection (4), upon sentencing | The court must furnish a state-court-administrative-office form that is nontechnical and easily understood and that the defendant can complete and file as a leave application. | The duty does not establish that the form was actually furnished in this case or that a form supplies constitutionally equivalent legal assistance. Neither fact may be inferred merely from the statute. |

The precise exception conditions above are statutory commands and permissions. Their existence does not establish that Halbert satisfied an appointment exception. The original packet supplies a denial under Michigan's regime, not findings resolving every possible statutory trigger. A proposed statutory-entitlement ground therefore needs the actual matching record predicates.

## Reported colloquy and findings

The reported colloquy begins with advice that acceptance of the plea gives up an appeal as of right, which Halbert confirmed he understood. The court then described the four situations in which it must appoint counsel for an indigent defendant and the scoring-related situation in which it may appoint counsel. Halbert again gave affirmative responses to the mandatory and discretionary explanations. Those are actual reported statements relevant to understanding; they cannot be omitted simply because the judge did not use a proposed constitutional formula.

The court's description is not identical to the full statute. It summarized the mandatory sentence condition by reference to exceeding the guidelines. Its discretionary description referred to alleging improper scoring, objecting at sentencing, and receiving a sentence exceeding the guidelines as the defendant alleged they should be scored. The statute's more exact upper-minimum-range language and alternative preservation route remain controlling statutory terms. This refresh does not turn the colloquial explanation into an amended statute or decide that each difference independently invalidates the plea.

The supplied quoted exchange does not include an express statement that counsel cannot be appointed in all other circumstances, or a separate affirmative surrender of the constitutional first-tier assistance claimed here. That is a limit of the quoted account. It is not proof that no additional discussion occurred outside the supplied excerpts, and it does not eliminate the possibility that actual understanding could be established through the complete reported context.

The source separately reports that the court asked counsel about any other promises or considerations and whether counsel believed the court had complied with the rule for no-contest pleas. It reports affirmative answers by the prosecutor and defense attorney to both questions. That report must be preserved without silently changing an affirmative answer to a negative one. It supplies neither the identity or substance of any additional promise nor a basis to infer there were no promises. No undisclosed agreement or inducement is reconstructed.

The court also expressly found the plea understandingly made, voluntary and accurate. This is a reported trial-court finding, not merely the parties' characterization. Its scope must still be assessed: a general plea-validity finding may support an inference of understanding of described consequences, while not necessarily deciding the specific right to counsel for preparation of the first leave application. The counsel-compliance answer is evidence about the plea proceeding; it is not reported as a specific representation that counsel explained the federal appellate right or obtained its waiver. Neither limitation authorizes disregarding the affirmative record.

## Existing law and competing waiver implications

The applicable Standards entry, **Capacity to plead or waive counsel and independent valid waiver**, has its present plea-stage operation from Iowa v. Tovar, March 8, 2004, while preserving Godinez's June 24, 1993 separation of competence and informed voluntary waiver. Tovar rejects mandatory extra warnings where actual understanding is established; it does not substitute a legal presumption for understanding of the right at issue. Current Bradshaw v. Stumpf, June 13, 2005, likewise permits the whole record and counsel's actual explanation to prove informed assent without a prescribed judicial recital. Its accepted representation specifically concerned the offense elements and was personally confirmed. Whether the more general confirmations here prove the distinct appellate-counsel understanding is an application question, not a result supplied by Stumpf.

The independent model should assess these distinct paths:

1. **Find a sufficient waiver from the actual combined record.** The statutory scheme, the advice that appellate review changes upon the plea, the detailed must/may appointment discussion, repeated confirmations, counsel's compliance answers and the plea finding may together support an inference that Halbert understood the ordinary absence of appointed counsel and accepted that consequence. This path need not demand a prescribed recital. Its principal difficulty is proving an intentional surrender of the particular federal assistance right, rather than only awareness that an appeal is no longer as of right and that counsel remains available in stated circumstances. The statutory duty to advise cannot be assumed performed merely because it existed, and counsel's general confirmation cannot be converted into an unreported specific explanation.
2. **Find the reported record insufficient to establish that distinct surrender.** Advice describing affirmative appointment exceptions without the ordinary exclusion, followed by general plea confirmations, may leave a material gap about understanding and intentional relinquishment of appellate assistance. Halbert's later repeated requests for counsel and reported learning limitations also bear on the actual inquiry. This path must address the confirmations and trial finding rather than treating the omission of particular words as automatically decisive. The requests do not themselves prove that an earlier valid waiver was impossible, and learning limitations do not themselves establish incompetence.
3. **Return a genuinely material unresolved waiver issue for supported adjudication.** A remand may be appropriate if the legal significance depends on a factual question about the actual advice or understanding that the available record does not permit the Court to resolve. The inquiry must be identified specifically. A remand cannot be used to invent advice, promises or an unstated waiver, and absence of the original appendix from the research collection is not itself a litigant default or a judicial reason to demand new production.

These paths assume, for waiver analysis only, a right capable of being waived. The distinct antecedent issue remains whether Michigan's first-tier leave process falls within Douglas and the controlling Smith v. Robbins functional protection or within Ross's later discretionary-review rationale. No constitutional entitlement or waiver result is selected here. The appointment exceptions and post-grant counsel must be assessed in the actual process; they cannot be treated as either automatically adequate or legally irrelevant.

Kowalski's standing holding remains confined to the earlier third-party claim and leaves this personal claim's merits open. Under Robbins's existing restoration rule, if the Court finds a required advocacy function wholly absent and not validly waived, the remedial inquiry concerns adequate first-tier consideration, not proof that the plea, sentence or a particular appellate ground would ultimately fail. None of the new material adjudicates those merits.

## Complete operative statutory text


```text
Sec. 3a. (1) Except as provided in subsections (2) and (3), a defendant who pleads guilty, guilty but mentally ill, or
nolo contendere shall not have appellate counsel appointed for review of the defendant's conviction or sentence.

    (2) The trial court shall appoint appellate counsel for an indigent defendant who pleads guilty, guilty but mentally
ill, or nolo contendere if any of the following apply:

    (a) The prosecuting attorney seeks leave to appeal.
    (b) The defendant's sentence exceeds the upper limit of the minimum sentence range of the applicable sentencing
guidelines.
    (c) The court of appeals or the supreme court grants the defendant's application for leave to appeal.
    (d) The defendant seeks leave to appeal a conditional plea under Michigan Court Rule 6.301(C)(2) or its successor
rule.

    (3) The trial court may appoint appellate counsel for an indigent defendant who pleads guilty, guilty but mentally ill,
or nolo contendere if all of the following apply:

    (a) The defendant seeks leave to appeal a sentence based upon an alleged improper scoring of an offense variable or
a prior record variable.

    (b) The defendant objected to the scoring or otherwise preserved the matter for appeal.

    (c) The sentence imposed by the court constitutes an upward departure from the upper limit of the minimum
sentence range that the defendant alleges should have been scored.

    (4) While establishing that a plea of guilty, guilty but mentally ill, or nolo contendere was made understandingly and
voluntarily under Michigan Court Rule 6.302 or its successor rule, and before accepting the plea, the court shall advise
the defendant that, except as otherwise provided in this section, if the plea is accepted by the court, the defendant
waives the right to have an attorney appointed at public expense to assist in filing an application for leave to appeal or
to assist with other postconviction remedies, and shall determine whether the defendant understands the waiver. Upon
sentencing, the court shall furnish the defendant with a form developed by the state court administrative office that is
nontechnical and easily understood and that the defendant may complete and file as an application for leave to appeal.
```
