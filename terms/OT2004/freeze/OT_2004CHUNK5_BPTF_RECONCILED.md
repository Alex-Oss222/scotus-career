## Corrected legal frame and operative record

**L — live final appeal.** All eight accept the scheduled section 1253 merits appeal from the final three-judge judgment. Plan C's enactment did not erase the continuing injunction against the challenged 2001 plan or the liability predicate under review. The final judgment separately records that remaining candidate-remedy issues for the 2004 election were moot. It grants declaratory and permanent injunctive relief on Count I and reasonable fees and costs as provided by law, and dismisses Counts II and III. It acknowledges that Plan C was approved and enacted without amendment. Thus no present commitment reinstates an elapsed six-week legislative deadline, directs another map, revives the dismissed constitutional counts, or equates reversal of federal liability with repeal of Plan C.

**S — coverage.** Governing De Grandy and Valdespino, clarified by Martinez, recognize the districting-based equal-opportunity claim. Stevens, O'Connor, Kennedy, Souter, Ginsburg and Breyer apply that framework. Thomas and Scalia maintain their own recorded contrary construction limiting section 2 to voting access, casting and counting. Their threshold ground is not an affirmative finding that this map satisfied the governing dilution test and cannot be counted as a join in its application.

**P — supported causal conditions.** The six coverage Justices accept the trial findings of a feasible compact alternative, Black political cohesion and majority bloc voting ordinarily sufficient to defeat Black-preferred candidates. The alternative satisfies the material numerical showing here; Valdespino's functional route remains current law but need not supply missing numbers. Appendices A and B set out the relevant measures and explain why the result did not turn on choosing voting-age versus citizen-voting-age data, or the alone versus combined racial classification. No universal demographic method, distinct-minority coalition holding or influence-only right follows. The revised alternative rested on data already available and an absence of unfair surprise; this is not a license to disregard ordinary disclosure protections.

Candidate race is not voter preference. The trial court actually evaluated both cross-racial success and elections in which preferred candidates were white or Hispanic. Its explanations for considering exogenous elections, distinguishing majority-Black districts, and giving case-specific weight to incumbency and partisan nomination support the accepted findings. Its rhetoric suggesting that a choice between white candidates is necessarily less authentic cannot become a legal rule. Nor can every incumbent success be discarded automatically: the identified election circumstances and relevant voting evidence must do the work. The preserved findings concern the actual causal electoral conditions, not an entitlement to more officeholders of a particular race.

**T — totality errors and surviving dispute.** The lower court expressly excluded crossover opportunity districts from its proportionality numerator and spoke of a presumption confirmed by the totality after the Gingles predicates. Those propositions conflict with current De Grandy, Valdespino and Martinez and are rejected by all six coverage Justices. Actual dependable opportunity belongs in the evidentiary assessment; the same district may afford opportunity to more than one group, so the ratios are neither exclusive racial allocations nor quotas. Neither satisfaction of the preliminary conditions nor feasibility of another majority district shifts the claimant's ultimate burden. Apparent aggregate proportionality also does not excuse a proved local injury.

The lower court's additional evidence supplies the real division: six predominantly Black precincts were removed from the 11th and 12th districts, which needed population, and added to the already heavily Black 6th, which did not need that addition. The accepted findings link the transfers and Fitzgerald Amendment to protection of white incumbents and to impaired electoral opportunity, in the setting of supported polarized voting and a workable alternative. Racial purpose, population growth or a decline in majority-district count alone is insufficient. The four affirmers find the detailed local practical injury independently sufficient after rejecting the erroneous arithmetic and presumption. O'Connor and Kennedy find the errors materially entangled with the ultimate totality balance and require the trial court to reassess it. The disagreement is over whether the existing supported findings already complete a lawful totality determination; neither side treats the erroneous ratio as harmless wording.

**R — remedies.** Affirmance preserves the existing Count I relief on the lawful independent ground and leaves Plan C enacted. Reassessment vacates the affected statutory liability and dependent federal relief, including its derivative fee basis, while preserving supported subsidiary findings and permitting any necessary interim restraint only on ordinary equitable grounds. Threshold reversal removes section 2 liability and its dependent injunction and fee basis and directs dismissal of that claim. No path determines an unpresented fee amount, bars any independent lawful fee basis, adjudicates every future challenge to Plan C or automatically revives the 2001 plan as state law.

## Handoff and arithmetic

All eight accept L and the scope limits. Six apply governing coverage and accept P. Stevens, Souter, Ginsburg and Breyer affirm on independent lawful totality findings; O'Connor and Kennedy vacate for reassessment; Scalia and Thomas reverse for dismissal on noncoverage. These are three distinct liability mandates. A count of four Justices opposed to affirmance is not a common four-vote statutory rationale, and a threshold dismissal rationale is not automatically a logical subset of a reassessment rationale. Assembly must test any narrower disposition actually joined, rather than manufacture a Marks holding or alter a commitment to obtain one.
