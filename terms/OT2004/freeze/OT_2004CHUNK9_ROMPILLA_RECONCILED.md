## Common relief rule and the opposing statutory path

The five relief-supporting Associates would hold that counsel must make reasonable efforts to examine an identified, readily available source they know the prosecution will use materially in seeking death, even when interviews with the client and family and existing examinations yield little mitigation. Here the announced detailed prior-offense proof made timely investigation of its file an independent defense-preparation task. Obtaining the victim's transcript at the last moment did not reasonably replace examination of the available source for context, counterproof and further mitigation leads. The State's approval based on the other inquiries failed to account reasonably for that distinct omission. Strickland supports both the separate objective-unreasonableness conclusion and the actual performance violation.

The obligation remains one of reasonable efforts in the actual circumstances, with informed professional judgment and practical access and resource considerations retained. It is not an absolute command to read every page of every prior-case file, an assumption that every later expert disagreement proves incompetence, or a rule that a client's responses never matter. Those limits define the affirmative rule and answer the actual contrary argument.

Scalia, Kennedy and Thomas accept the general duty but regard the actual state appraisal as within the permissible range. Counsel undertook several concrete inquiries, obtained the aggravating testimony and received little reason from the client, family or experts to anticipate the later history. The identified file makes the case for additional work substantial, but these three do not find that every reasonable application of Strickland had to condemn the stopping point. The later discovery of useful evidence is insufficient by itself. Their opposition is not cured by changing authors or merely announcing that the rule is contextual; the remaining disagreement concerns this state adjudication under that rule.

## Independent prejudice and supported response to the contrary argument

Pennsylvania did not contest prejudice in this Court. That is the corrected litigation position, not an admission of every factual inference or a substitute for Rompilla's burden. Because the state merits adjudication stopped at performance, the five assess this element independently after crossing the statutory gate.

The original jury heard brief family pleas for mercy, assertions of innocence and good character, and the fourteen-year-old son's love and willingness to visit. It credited the son's appearance and possible rehabilitation but gave greater weight to the three aggravators: commission during another felony, torture, and a significant history of violent felonies. The repeated stabbing and burning and the prior violent crime remain fully in the comparison.

Reasonable review of the known file would have disclosed documentary leads concerning early deprivation, alcohol-associated incarceration and markedly low cognitive testing. The developed family, school and prison evidence supplies a substantially different account: severe parental alcoholism, drinking during pregnancy, violent domestic conflict, beatings, confinement in a filthy dog enclosure, isolation, inadequate clothing, absent plumbing, an unheated sleeping space and prolonged neglect. Experts furnished with that history reported organic brain damage, cognitive impairment, possible prenatal-alcohol effects and substantially impaired capacity at the offense. Those are expert and evidentiary accounts used in evaluating sentencing, not newly pronounced diagnoses or a categorical death-ineligibility determination.

The strongest contrary prejudice argument questions whether a reasonable review directed at rebutting aggravation would have led to the administrative transfer petition and later mitigation development at all. The historical dissent pressed that causal objection, independently of Pennsylvania's noncontest. The five answer that reasonable examination of this identified accessible file includes attention to the information it contains; unexpected indications of serious impairment and deprivation cannot be disregarded merely because the initial reason to obtain the file was the prosecution's evidence. The file's available leads and the developed record establish the connection, rather than a presumption from the State's litigation choice.

The additional material also contains unfavorable history, including assaultive conduct and recurring alcohol-associated misconduct. The five consider that information rather than subtract it from the account. Much of the violent-history aggravation already confronted the jury, while the documented explanation of deprivation and impairment was absent. The complete comparison establishes a reasonable probability sufficient to undermine confidence in the penalty selection, even though a properly informed jury could still impose death. A different result need not be more likely than not. No unverified Pennsylvania unanimity formula or one-juror numerical rule is imported from another State's case.
