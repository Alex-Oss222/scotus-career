## Alaska v. United States — June 6, 2005

### Components, supported alternatives and sufficient grounds

**A1, historic waters.** Overrule Alaska's count I exception. Historic inland status requires the demonstrated exercise of authority of the kind asserted, continuity and the requisite foreign acquiescence; control of fishing or coastal commerce alone does not establish a historic authority to exclude innocent navigation throughout the claimed pockets. The identified treaty permissions, isolated seizure met with protest, inconsistent boundary assertions and later acknowledgment of permissible Canadian fishing support the Master's rejection of the required continuous exclusion claim. The strongest contrary path treats those events cumulatively as recognized national dominion and invokes the United States' prior positions. That path fails on this record because it does not bridge the distinction between limited fishing/trading control and the broader continuous sovereign claim necessary for the requested inland classification. The national-interest submission cannot replace proof of that claim. United States v. Alaska (1997) leaves specific historic title available while rejecting a general fringe rule; United States v. Louisiana (Alabama and Mississippi Boundary Case), 470 U.S. 93 (1985), illustrates the particularized proof necessary. No new historical evidence is found by the model.

**A2, juridical bays.** Overrule the count II exception on the Master's independent Article 7(2) well-marked-indentation ground. Even with the proposed constructive peninsula assumed, the claimed North Bay and South Bay fail the rule requiring penetration relative to mouth width that creates landlocked waters and more than coastal curvature. Area sufficient to satisfy a semicircle does not dispense with that independent condition. The strongest contrary path invokes island assimilation and generous identification of mainland headlands; its strongest answer is that a properly assimilated coast could produce different mouth geography. The assumed-assimilation analysis nevertheless preserves Alaska's proposed geography and rejects its classification on the independent Convention condition. United States v. Louisiana, 394 U.S. 11 (1969), and United States v. Maine, 469 U.S. 504 (1985), permit attention to actual geography; they do not replace Article 7(2). The result requires no categorical rule barring every island assimilation and no unpresented State-wide coastal redrawing.

**A3, Glacier Bay retention.** The two lawful paths are federal retention through §6(e)'s wildlife-reservation exclusion, read with the actual reservation and §§5 and 6(m), and state title because the proviso does not extend beyond the initial transfer of property under the three named fisheries/game enactments. Inclusion of the beds is the unexcepted predicate; the disputed question is timely congressional retention. The federal path treats the proviso's own description of withdrawn or set-apart wildlife reservations as operative protection of those reservations, including this actual marine reservation, rather than a limitation confined to a distinct initial property-transfer program. The state path reads such transfer as a confined cross-reference and invokes the demanding post-1953 statutory retention requirement. Both preserve the separate clear-intent inquiry. The six supporters below find the actual prestatehood reservation and express wildlife-reservation description sufficient; Scalia and Thomas find the statutory bridge insufficient at this scope.

The retention coalition relies on United States v. Alaska's actual §6(e) legal-effect holding, not a claim that every monument retains every submerged parcel. Glacier Bay's wildlife protection is an actual reservation purpose; the proviso does not repeat the initial clause's sole-purpose or named-enactment conditions. Idaho v. United States reinforces timely enacted treatment of an identified estate and does not make purpose alone enough. The opposing path's strongest argument is the normal limiting work of a proviso and the words such transfer. The retention path answers through the Act's coordinated retention and transfer scheme and the reservation-specific description already given operative force in Alaska. No poststatehood act supplies an otherwise missing prestatehood intention.

**A4, disposition and implementation.** Count III follows the actual federal disclaimer. All eight accept resolution of the live exceptions and a direction that the parties submit a conforming proposed decree using the established report descriptions and disclaimer, with jurisdiction retained to implement the title determination. The decree must reflect the actual adopted title outcomes. No coordinates are fabricated and the earlier Arctic decree is not transferred to this geography.

## Spector v. Norwegian Cruise Line Ltd. — June 6, 2005

### Paths and proposition boundaries

**S1, domestic coverage and nonstructural obligations.** The broad exclusion path treats Title III as a comprehensive regime with structural duties affecting the internal governance of foreign vessels, requiring a clear statement before any application. Its strongest support is Benz and McCulloch's protection against pervasive ship regulation, plus uniform statutory construction. The competing path applies the enacted public-accommodation and transportation categories to domestic passenger services, while evaluating particular duties under the statutory exceptions and the maritime rule. Its strongest response is Cunard and Ariadne's domestic-contact distinction: American passenger transactions cannot be equated with governance of a foreign crew simply because they occur aboard a ship. The six reversal supporters adopt that distinction; O'Connor and Scalia regard the proposed division as an impermissible partial application of an internally regulating statutory scheme.

**S2, permanent structural modification and maritime limits.** Statutory coverage does not itself decide which alteration is required. All reversal supporters preserve the readily-achievable standard, the distinct reasonable-modification/fundamental-alteration inquiry, genuine safety restrictions and treaty compatibility. A change conflicting with the United States' actual treaty obligations is not readily achievable. The proposed design alteration must be identified before those conditions are applied. The narrower internal-affairs question produces a genuine proposition-level difference: Stevens, Kennedy, Souter and Thomas regard a substantial permanent structural alteration of the vessel as an intrusion into internal maritime affairs requiring a clear congressional instruction absent here; Ginsburg and Breyer reject permanence by itself as a sufficient legal classification and require actual interference with protected internal governance or an actual governing conflict. Those two would apply Title III's ordinary duty-specific limits to structural changes that satisfy them. Their agreement on coverage and remand must not be represented as agreement with the four's structural exclusion.

**S3, the actual remaining lower-court issue.** The district court dismissed barrier claims on the separate no-guidelines ground; the Fifth Circuit did not decide it. The reversal supporters return that ground for appellate consideration under the coverage holding, alongside the requested duties and applicable safety/treaty proof. This is an actual remaining defense, not a generic invitation to invent new thresholds. Nonstructural claims also return for ordinary merits and equitable adjudication. The granted question and lack of first-instance determinations do not support a comprehensive alteration injunction. The opposing two would leave dismissal in place on the categorical maritime construction.

Clark v. Martinez is applicable current law. A uniform meaning of the enacted category does not itself preclude a rule limiting an application because it would intrude into foreign ship governance, in the six supporters' view; the two opponents view the distinction as changing the statutory regime's coverage in precisely the manner Clark disfavors. Thomas's public Clark dissent supplies no commitment to that uniform-avoidance extension. No new severability statute or fabricated concession supplies the division.

## Common reached question and actual record

All eight Associates reach the merits of the Fourth Amendment question. A compulsory blood draw and the protected examination of the material are searches. Kincade's undisputed armed-bank-robbery conviction, actual supervised-release status, statutory collection duty, officer's demand and later forced profiling establish a concrete controversy. The actual March 2002 demand had no individualized evidentiary basis concerning another crime. The later April 2003 drug test and lifting of the custody stay cannot retroactively provide one.

The judicially recited refusal disposition was four months' imprisonment and two years' supervised release; after the stay was lifted, a sample was forcibly obtained. Each Justice's disposition therefore addresses that actual judgment, with any independently valid subsequent consequence distinguished on the record. Prior ex post facto and other district-court theories were not pursued on appeal and are outside the granted search question.

## Competing propositions, distinct grounds and remedy paths

**R — Apply Rise.** A qualifying conviction and continuing supervision, coupled with ordinary future-crime identification, limited medical extraction and use restrictions, do not independently authorize this suspicionless bodily acquisition. Rise already considered reduced offender privacy, regulated samples, identification utility, correctional conditions and the fingerprint analogy. Knights supplies authority where a lawful known condition and individualized suspicion work together; its reservation does not create authority to dispense with both a bodily-search exception and suspicion. Reject the offered collection ground, reverse the appellate affirmance and remand for removal of the refusal basis and effective relief on the actual record.

The strongest objection to R is that current supervised release creates an unusually direct governmental duty to identify and monitor a convicted offender, while a standard draw and a restricted identification profile impose a limited burden. R answers that these considerations have real weight but are the very sort of considerations Rise found insufficient to justify ordinary database acquisition; recognition of correctional authority does not eliminate a distinct bodily-search justification.

**A — Sustain the defined supervised-offender program.** A medically ordinary draw from this qualifying convicted person during supervision, for the regulated identification profile and authorized uses, is constitutionally reasonable in light of the established correctional relationship, objective selection, limited immediate intrusion and identification/deterrence interests. This is a proposed exception or limitation of Rise, not a holding already supplied by Knights or a conclusion that the biological sample contains no private information. It supports affirmance of the actual release judgment on the search issue.

The strongest objection to A is the central ordinary-investigative use, coupled with acquisition of internal biological material and retention beyond immediate supervision. A answers that the government has a distinct ongoing relationship with a person serving a criminal sentence and can impose a defined identification obligation within that relationship; medical collection and real use restrictions, rather than the bare usefulness of data, must support its proposed extension. The absence of a medical-risk objection matters to this particular application; the statute's reasonable-means constraint is not an invented absolute health exemption or one-draw rule.

**S — Special supervisory need.** The lower concurrence's Griffin theory treats deterrence and prompt identification of new misconduct during supervision as the sufficient need. The counterargument is that this uniform national evidentiary program was not tailored to a supervising officer's concrete rehabilitation judgment and is designed to solve ordinary crimes. A future-crime purpose is not automatically a non-law-enforcement purpose. No Associate below commits to S as an independent sufficient ground; those supporting A do not need it, while the R group rejects it on this record. Griffin's actual supervisory authority remains distinct from an absolute ban on all mixed purposes.

## 79. Johnson v. California, No. 04-6964

**Question and disposition:** Seven Associates would reverse the first-stage threshold ruling and remand for completion of Batson's process. Thomas would affirm California's first-stage rule and the reviewed judgment. All reach the granted merits question on the supplied final-judgment posture.

**Common reversal ground:** A defendant triggers the prosecutor's explanation duty by facts sufficient to permit an inference of purposeful racial exclusion. Requiring proof that discrimination was more likely than not before the prosecutor explains the challenged strikes improperly demands an ultimate persuasion showing at the production stage. Striking all three qualified Black prospective jurors from this venire satisfies the required inference on the supplied record. The trial court must receive and evaluate the actual reasons and determine purposeful discrimination with the ultimate burden remaining on Johnson. A retrospective hearing must permit a reliable determination; if the lapse or state of the record prevents that constitutionally adequate adjudication, the state court must provide a new trial. ## Common disposition and rule

**Disposition coalition:** Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer would vacate the Federal Circuit's rejection of the statutory defense and remand for evaluation of the preserved verdict challenge under the correct construction of §271(e)(1). No Associate would direct judgment that all challenged experiments are exempt on the supplied trial record.

Section 271(e)(1) reaches preclinical uses reasonably related, when undertaken, to developing and submitting information under the covered federal drug-regulatory law. A qualifying use need not involve a generic drug, an already selected final candidate, a clinical trial, a successful experiment, or information ultimately submitted. The connection must be objectively reasonable and concrete: when the researcher has a reasonable basis to believe that a candidate may produce a relevant physiological effect and uses it in experiments capable of generating information of a kind pertinent to FDA review, abandonment or non-submission does not retrospectively eliminate the exemption. General scientific inquiry with only a speculative prospect that something learned might someday assist drug development does not qualify on that prospect alone. These are applications of the enacted reasonable-relationship requirement, not an additional mandatory sequence of research phases.

The exemption applies to the specified acts solely for qualifying uses. A commercial research objective does not itself defeat a qualifying regulatory use; conversely, the existence of a regulatory project does not immunize a portion of a challenged use lacking the required connection. Safety, efficacy, pharmacology and mechanism work can supply the requisite regulatory information; labels such as discovery, screening or preclinical work do not decide the question. All eight would accept that scope as necessary to answer the categorical restrictions in the appellate reasoning. The biotechnology exception for the specified animal-drug and veterinary products remains operative but is not implicated by these human-drug candidates.

The decisive statutory explanation is that development of regulatory information necessarily precedes submission and can fail to yield an eventual application. Requiring success or actual submission would replace reasonable relation with hindsight. FDA's use of preclinical information defeats the categorical premise that the agency has no pertinent interest before clinical testing. Integra's strongest answer is that early commercial drug discovery consumes the patent owner's principal market and that nearly any research can be described as helping a future application. That concern requires a genuine, use-specific regulatory connection and an evidentiary burden; it does not authorize deleting development or creating categorical restrictions absent from the text.

## The five-Associate prejudice conclusion

The five do not rest on Pennsylvania's failure to contest prejudice. Strickland still requires a reasonable probability sufficient to undermine confidence in the actual penalty result. Because the state adjudication ended at performance, this element is reviewed independently after the §2254(d)(1) gate is satisfied. The state opinion's observation that some records were not wholly favorable explains its investigation assessment; it is not a separate whole-record no-prejudice ruling entitled to a second layer of §2254(d)(1) protection.

The original jury heard brief family pleas for mercy, descriptions of Rompilla as a good man, assertions of innocence and his fourteen-year-old son's love and willingness to visit. The jury found the son's appearance and possible rehabilitation mitigating but found aggravation weightier. A reasonable investigation of the known source would have supplied leads to a materially different account: records of cognitive testing near third-grade levels after years of schooling, juvenile and adult alcohol-associated problems and incarceration, and family and documentary evidence of extreme deprivation, sustained violence and parental alcoholism. The developed evidence includes maternal drinking during pregnancy, severe beatings, confinement in a filthy dog enclosure, inadequate clothing, lack of plumbing, an unheated sleeping space, neglect and social isolation. Experts supplied with the records reported organic brain damage and substantial cognitive impairment, possible prenatal-alcohol effects and substantially impaired capacity at the offense. Those opinions explain the potential significance of the supported history; they do not require the Court to diagnose Rompilla or declare him categorically ineligible for death.

The strongest independent case against prejudice is substantial. The murder involved repeated stabbing and burning; the jury found commission during another felony, torture and a significant history of violent felonies. The newly developed record also contains assaultive conduct, repeated alcohol-associated misconduct and other facts a prosecutor could use adversely. An informed jury could still impose death, and neither a difficult childhood nor cognitive impairment legally eliminates any established aggravator. The five nevertheless find the probability threshold satisfied because the added account changes the mitigation from general familial attachment and mercy to a documented explanation of prolonged damage and impaired functioning. Much of the violent-history aggravation was already before the jury, while the explanatory deprivation and impairment were absent. On the entire record, that difference is sufficiently weighty to undermine confidence in the penalty selection despite the crime's severity. It need not make a different result more likely than not.

This reasoning applies the ordinary reasonable-probability standard to the actual lawful choice between capital and noncapital punishment. It does not import Virginia's or Maryland's particular unanimity provisions from Terry Williams or Wiggins. The supplied objective sources have not independently established Pennsylvania's precise unanimity text, and no specific one-juror numerical formula is necessary to the present conclusion. Nor does the State's noncontest authorize a new assumption about the truth of every later assertion; the supplied supported records and testimony are the evidentiary basis.

## Remedy, writings and operative limits

The five would reverse the Third Circuit and remand with directions to restore conditional sentence relief. The District Court must set a definite reasonable period in which Pennsylvania conducts a constitutionally adequate new penalty proceeding or imposes a lawful noncapital sentence, require notice of the chosen course, and verify completion through the resulting sentencing order. Repeating the original penalty decision without an adequately prepared defense does not comply. If the State fails to comply, the writ must be enforced against the invalid death sentence and execution. Custody independently justified by the conviction remains subject to a lawful sentence; the guilt judgment is not set aside.

The supplied postconviction development supports deciding the prejudice element now. A new evidentiary hearing is not ordered simply because the opinion applies a cumulative standard. The lawful details of a new state sentencing proceeding and the federal court's reasonable deadline are implementation work below, not unresolved conditions that prevent deciding entitlement to conditional relief.

The three denial commitments support a joint gateway dissent. Its shared point is that the State's actual approval remained reasonable, not that counsel had an absolute option to ignore the prosecution's materials or that the omitted evidence could never matter. Kennedy's distinction from his Wiggins join should be stated substantively in that writing; it need not become a second dissent if the shared analysis preserves it. ## Graham County Soil & Water Conservation District v. United States ex rel. Wilson — June 20, 2005, conditional on chronology refresh

### Competing readings and remedy

**G1, fraud-clock limitation and state borrowing.** Reverse the Fourth Circuit's six-year ruling and remand. Section 3731(b)(1)'s reference to civil actions under §3730 must be read with its express starting event: a violation of §3729. Retaliation under §3730(h) may occur before, after or without an actual false claim; McKenzie's independent retaliation conditions make that difference controlling. Reading the six-year provision as a fraud-enforcement limitation gives both the reference and trigger work. A new retaliation-accrual date cannot simply be substituted for the cross-referenced violation. Section 3731(c)'s United-States burden provision also confirms that broad under-§3730 wording receives context rather than identical reach wherever used.

The strongest contrary argument is that Congress added retaliation and changed the limitations text in the same 1986 Act, while retaining a broad §3730 reference. The G1 response is that the legislature retained the particular §3729 trigger and elsewhere regulates employee retaliation separately; simultaneity alone cannot supply an unexpressed change in that trigger. North Star Steel then supplies ordinary appropriate-state borrowing. The same-Act fraud period has not been shown both a clearly closer analogy and significantly more suitable than a proper employment/retaliation analogue; a general preference for uniformity is insufficient. Jones v. R. R. Donnelley does not bring the 1986 claim within §1658.

**G2, federal six-year protection for retaliation.** Affirm the use of a six-year period and remand any remaining issues. The broad cross-reference includes subsection (h), and the simultaneous creation of its independent remedy supports a single federal period. The six-year duration applies to retaliation from accrual of the retaliatory injury; the reference to a §3729 violation is read in the context of the fraud remedy for which it was drafted rather than used to delete the newly included employee action. The strongest objection is that this substitutes a trigger Congress did not state. The G2 answer treats the mismatch as a confined drafting/fit problem within an expressly broad coverage clause and preserves the ordinary principle that a claimant's period begins when the claimant can sue for the asserted injury. It does not make successful fraud a retaliation element or run time from an unrelated suspected false claim. This is a genuine alternative construction, not an assumption that §3731(b)(2) independently supplies a retaliation clock.

The G1 remedy does not reinstate dismissal automatically. The Fourth Circuit must select the proper analogous state period and address actual accrual, tolling and timeliness under it. The district court's three-year choice has not been conclusively validated. Both paths leave the separate qui tam matter outside this granted limitations decision.
