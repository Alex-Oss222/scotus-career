## Merits results and common propositions

Stevens, O'Connor, Kennedy and Thomas affirm the convictions on the presented statutory property/revenue-rule question. Scalia, Souter, Ginsburg and Breyer reverse the convictions resting on the foreign-revenue scheme, with the dependent sentences falling with those convictions. Among the Associates this is four to four; no Chief Justice vote is supplied.

**Property classification.** An actual accrued entitlement to money differs from the purely regulatory licensing permission excluded by Cleveland. The affirming four treat Canada's entitlement to import taxes as money or property in its hands. The reversing four can accept that monetary character without accepting statutory coverage of this foreign fiscal object. Their reversal is not based on a categorical claim that governmental monetary entitlements are nonproperty. Cleveland's reservation concerning actual money and unpaid revenue therefore needs an answer, and Neder's materiality and scheme requirements remain independent.

**Affirming rule and strongest objection.** Section 1343 reaches domestic interstate wire use to execute an intentional, material scheme to deprive a foreign sovereign of an actual entitlement to money, including accrued tax revenue. The United States prosecutes its own domestic fraud offense; it does not sue on Canada's cause of action to collect a tax judgment. Identifying the victim's foreign-law property does not itself turn that prosecution into the collection action barred by the received revenue rule. The monetary right must exist, and the Government retains the criminal burden on the charged elements. A scheme need not succeed or inflict completed loss.

The strongest contrary argument is that guilt requires recognition of Canada's tax demand and punishment therefore indirectly enforces foreign fiscal policy. The affirmative answer distinguishes the domestic criminal interest from direct or disguised collection and tests the common-law rule at its actual established scope. General historical formulations of the revenue rule do not clearly establish immunity from a separately enacted domestic fraud offense. Territorial restraint does not eliminate the domestic execution Congress expressly regulates. This reasoning does not rely on an Executive power to waive statutory limits: statutory coverage must exist before prosecutorial choice matters. Neither foreign nationality alone nor any breach of foreign regulation supplies the property-fraud offense.

**Reversing rule and strongest objection.** Section 1343's general fraud terms, read against the received revenue rule, do not authorize this prosecution when the sole charged property is a foreign fiscal entitlement and the criminal wrong depends on implementing that demand. Congress must supply a sufficiently clear basis for that extension. Domestic wire use establishes the federal instrument but does not answer which foreign sovereign claims Congress made enforceable through punishment.

The broad property text and actual American calls are substantial contrary grounds. The reversing answer is that the statutory terms carry legal background, as Neder's borrowed-term analysis illustrates. Classification as property does not displace a distinct sovereign-revenue limit. Empagran preserves source-specific territorial construction, while Hartford Fire neither makes comity irrelevant whenever two laws can be obeyed nor grants automatic foreign-victim immunity. The United States' initiation of the case does not itself supply absent congressional authorization. The resulting rule concerns the foreign fiscal object; ordinary foreign private property is not excluded on that account.

The common four-Justice reversal ground is the revenue-rule/statutory construction. The historical Ginsburg/Breyer Part I extraterritorial argument is a distinct route, and the frozen commitments do not authorize importing a freestanding territorial holding or its complete legislative/treaty analysis. The four can join a writing confined to their recorded common ground. Any additional part requires its own supported commitment and join determination; it cannot be attributed through historical partial joins.

## Sentencing review: actual historical fork and changed premise

The validated objective facts establish a Blakely sentence-vacatur request in merits-brief note 29, with the constitutional claim absent from the court of appeals and the petition. They do not establish that Hilts's earlier loss-calculation objection preserved the constitutional claim. All eight retain their provisional decision not to add that claim to the question decided. For the reversing four, sentences fall with the convictions on their merits ground; for the affirming four, no independent sentencing correction is ordered here. This review-scope conclusion does not extinguish a claim through another independently available proper proceeding.

Historical Ginsburg's note 5, joined by Breyer, supplies a genuine competing path: reach the request despite its omission from the petition, because the presentation rule is prudential and Blakely arrived after the petition was filed and granted. That note describes merits briefing as the earliest opportunity. The proposition that scope rules are prudential, rather than jurisdictional, makes consideration legally possible; it does not compel consideration in every case.

The decisive chronology differs here. Angle, June 20, 2002, squarely held that present-offense facts increasing a binding Federal Guidelines ceiling or floor require element adjudication, and its federal charging holding already governed the covered facts. Angle preceded the July 18, 2003 en banc judgment in this case. Blakely, June 24, 2004, applied that binding-inner-range rule without changing it. Booker reaffirmed the constitutional rule and retained binding Guidelines based on constitutionally established facts. Thus no inference about unrecovered original filing dates or the earliest practical opportunity is needed: the constitutional rule invoked in the later footnote was already governing law during the earlier appellate review. Historical novelty cannot be imported into this chronology.

For Ginsburg, her complete Angle join adopted both the Federal Guidelines element rule and its charging consequences; her later Blakely and Booker constitutional joins reaffirmed the same protection. Her willingness to preserve real sentence review remains, but an intervening decision did not first make this particular legal objection available. Her Angle approval of record-sensitive Rule 52(b) remand concerned an actually presented sentencing question; it did not eliminate the separate question of adding an unpresented issue to a statutory merits review. She therefore retains the frozen refusal to enlarge this review while preserving any otherwise proper channel.

For Breyer, his Angle dissent challenged the constitutional rule and supported ordinary sentencing, but that disagreement did not erase the rule's governing force. His Booker position acknowledged the constitutional premise once it controlled; his later Shepard joinder in the present-law remand confirms that he does not authorize lower courts to ignore it. His preference for advisory resentencing also did not become the law. In this case the merits footnote neither presents a newly available constitutional rule nor permits replacing the binding framework with his preferred remedy. His historical willingness to reach a newly arising claim therefore does not require the same reach after that premise disappears. He retains the frozen review-scope conclusion without withdrawing his broader sentencing views.

**Historical departure:** Ginsburg retains refusal to add the footnote sentencing claim, unlike her historical note 5, because her own full Angle join had already established the precise Federal Guidelines constitutional and charging protections before Pasquantino's 2003 appellate judgment; Blakely did not first supply the claim in this chronology, weakening the specific novelty premise for relaxing the ordinary presentation boundary.

**Historical departure:** Breyer retains refusal to add the footnote sentencing claim, unlike his historical joinder in Ginsburg's note 5, because Angle already governed the Federal Guidelines question before appellate review and his constitutional disagreement did not postpone that law's availability; Booker neither created the claim nor adopted his advisory-system remedy, so the historical intervening-law rationale does not justify expanding this review on the frozen presentation.

These are qualitative conclusions from the individual Justices' positions and the changed legal chronology, not an automatic consequence of prior precedent. They establish neither deliberate waiver nor a universal bar to prudential consideration of later-raised claims. They make no finding about sentence error, admissions, charging sufficiency, harmlessness or plain-error prejudice. A new affirmative sentencing disposition would require the affected neutral and modeling stages and the exact necessary record.
