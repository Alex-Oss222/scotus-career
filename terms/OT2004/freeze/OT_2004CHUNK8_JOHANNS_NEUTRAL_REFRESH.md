## What was actually presented and decided below

**The commercial alternative was presented.** The appellate opinion's opening and Part III report that appellants argued both that actual government speech defeats the compelled-subsidy objection and, alternatively, that the assessment survives commercial-speech review or is justified as part of a broader regulatory scheme. Part III characterizes the Central Hudson argument as consistently advanced. It is not a newly invented argument, an unexplored defense first appearing here, or the unpresented operational defense in United Foods. The appellate source describes the government's substantial interest as protecting the welfare of the beef industry, citing its brief at page 51. That identifies the asserted objective, not an empirical finding of success.

**District treatment.** After amendment to add the First Amendment claim, cross-motions for partial summary judgment were denied, and a January 14, 2002 bench trial addressed that claim alone. The district court declined to apply Central Hudson because it understood Glickman to distinguish compelled financing from a restriction on a speaker's own expression. It instead relied on the private compelled-support and independent-function analysis and separately rejected the government-speech defense. Thus the trial court did not make the commercial test's empirical satisfaction the legal basis of its judgment.

**Appellate adaptation.** Part III disagreed with the district court's reason for refusing the commercial test. It substituted a threshold inquiry into the producers' protected interest in avoiding compelled financial support for Central Hudson's ordinary lawful-and-nonmisleading-expression inquiry. It then identified substantial governmental interest, direct advancement and a restriction no more extensive than necessary as the remaining questions. The opinion treated greater governmental responsibility and control as relevant to the asserted interest, characterized the boards as quasi-governmental and the Secretary's oversight as limited, and expressed doubt that the governmental interest was substantial.

**Actual sufficient ground.** The appellate court then assumed a substantial interest and asked whether it sufficiently justified the compelled support. Its decisive analysis returned to Abood, Keller and the independent-function/germaneness distinction: advertising cannot supply the very collective justification to which that same advertising is supposedly germane. It treated the program's advertising-centered operation as materially equivalent to the mushroom program and rejected the asserted industry-welfare justification. The opinion announces and adapts the commercial framework, but does not supply separate beef-specific findings that advertising failed to increase demand, that free riding was absent, or that a particular less burdensome financing arrangement would work equally well. Its conclusion that the interest was insufficient is a legal judgment about compelled support in this setting; it must not be restated as a recovered empirical failure finding.
