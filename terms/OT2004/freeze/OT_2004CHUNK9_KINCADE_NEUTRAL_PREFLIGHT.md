## Material entering-law completion

The supplied Kincade packet omits a directly material controlling authority: **Rise v. Oregon, June 14, 1996**. Its six-Justice bodily-search holding rejects compulsory suspicionless blood acquisition and protected examination when the offered justification consists of qualifying conviction, reduced privacy, medically modest means, statutory safeguards and ordinary future-crime identification. This is a substantive limitation on the offered authority, not merely a procedural requirement or a finding of actual misuse. The Oregon program covered persons in custody and under probation conditions, as well as regulated postconviction collection; active correctional status therefore cannot by itself distinguish the case. The program's exact health, prior-sample, use, disclosure, access and destruction provisions remain scheme-specific.

The operative Standards and Tests title is **Compulsory blood collection for future-crime identification**, present operation effective Rise, 1996-06-14. Its controlling current wording is the same Rise holding. The full provision is copied in the entering-law slice. O'Connor authored the search holding; Stevens, Scalia, Souter and Ginsburg joined, alongside the Chief. Kennedy, Thomas and Breyer publicly dissented from that component, supporting contextual reasonableness of the defined identification scheme. Their published dissent treats actual safeguards as material and recognizes its proposed extension rather than describing a preexisting DNA exception.

**Probation conditions combined with individualized search suspicion**, present operation effective United States v. Knights, 2001-12-10, requires the lawful known condition and particularized reasonable suspicion together for the residential search it upheld. Its unanimous opinion, written by Scalia, does not validate a suspicionless blood demand or treat a condition as blanket consent. **Primary ordinary-crime purpose in suspicionless checkpoints**, effective Edmond, 2000-11-28, preserves the need for independent authority; **Ferguson**, 2001-03-21, rejects the actual designed evidence/prosecution operation while preserving Griffin's distinct supervisory setting. Ferguson does not impose a categorical immediate-versus-ultimate-purpose rule or condemn every mixed purpose; the supplied packet's shorter description must be read at that exact scope.

**Exclusion in revocation after an unconstitutional supervisory search**, effective Scott, 1998-06-22, concerns use of unlawfully acquired fruits by the supervisory institution. It preserves constitutional protection during supervision but does not itself dictate expungement, destruction, damages or the disposition of an independently supported release violation. **Earls**, 2002-06-27, preserves search status and no waiver and rejects its unsupported nonathletic program; it supplies no generic reduced-privacy exception.

Current-term **Illinois v. Caballes**, January 24, 2005, permits a genuinely contraband-confined exterior canine examination only with lawful access and without added unlawful restraint or protected intrusion. It does not eliminate the acknowledged bodily intrusion here. Its published Souter and Ginsburg dissents support their continued separation of lawful presence or detention from authority to acquire protected information. Current-term Devenpeck likewise requires an actual objective predicate; it does not infer new-crime suspicion from offender status.

## Objective record completion

The lower court's published factual recitation establishes an actual later sample; the original packet's uncertainty on that point is resolved. Kincade pleaded guilty to armed bank robbery and a firearm offense and received 97 months in prison followed by three years of supervised release. His probation officer demanded a DNA blood sample on March 25, 2002 under the DNA Act. Kincade refused, including after consultation with counsel. On July 15, 2002 the district court found refusal to follow the officer's instruction a release violation and imposed four months' imprisonment and two years' supervised release. The court initially stayed the imprisonment. After an April 14, 2003 positive drug test, it lifted the stay; once in custody, Kincade was forced to submit to DNA profiling. ## Statute and program

The challenged demand arose under the DNA Analysis Backlog Elimination Act of 2000, Pub. L. 106-546, sections 3 and 7, and then-operative offense coverage. Armed bank robbery was expressly within the original coverage, independently of subsequent expansions. Section 3(a)(2) directs collection by the responsible probation office from qualifying persons on probation, parole or supervised release; section 3(b) directs FBI analysis and inclusion in CODIS. The statute defines a sample to include bodily tissue or fluid; the actual federal program used blood. The scientific and implementation details below are reported program facts, not words imposed by Congress.

If CODIS already contains an analysis or a military sample has been collected, section 3(a)(3) permits, but does not require, another sample. It is not an absolute duplicate-collection prohibition. Section 3(a)(4) authorizes reasonably necessary detention, restraint and collection means for a refusing person. Section 3(a)(5) makes failure to cooperate with authorized collection a class A misdemeanor. Section 7 adds cooperation conditions to probation and supervised release. The present judgment concerns the release consequence actually imposed, rather than an invented separate misdemeanor conviction.

The lower opinion describes a profile generated from 13 STR loci selected for identification rather than known trait coding. It also acknowledges potential probabilistic race/sex information and the distinction between profile and retained source material. The merits analysis must not call the biological sample empty of private information or assume unauthorized genomic examination. The CODIS privacy limits permit authorized law-enforcement identification, judicial proceedings, criminal-defense access, and specified anonymized statistical, identification-research and quality-control uses; the authorized uses and associated penalties are described in the published opinion and former sections 14132(b)(3) and 14135e. These constraints matter to reasonableness without making collection self-validating. Index expungement following certified final reversal of each qualifying conviction is a statutory mechanism distinct from automatic destruction on release or a judicial remedy for unconstitutional acquisition.

The Act supplies an identification program and collection authority; it does not purport to abrogate a historical Supreme Court merits decision displaced in this timeline. No special existence premise is required. Its constitutionality remains the question, and the Court must not treat the enactment itself as a constitutional search exception.

## Competing supported paths and remedy

1. Apply Rise to the offered ordinary-identification justification, reject the demand and reverse the appellate affirmance with remand to vacate the refusal-based consequence and provide effective relief on the actual record. The strongest contrary ground is the limited profile, defined current supervision and public protection served by reliable identification. Rise already weighed materially similar conviction, bodily acquisition and regulatory safeguards, while Knights deliberately retained the suspicion element for its holding.

2. Sustain the particular medically ordinary blood demand as a reasonable convicted-supervisee identification measure, acknowledging the necessary proposed limitation or replacement of Rise's bodily-search sufficiency rule. The strongest objection is that the claimed benefits are ordinary crime solving and the information survives supervision, so the offered ground cannot be inferred merely from reduced privacy, database usefulness or a reserved issue in Knights. A Justice choosing this path must explain why the supported correctional relationship and controlled identification justify the extension, and must not falsely treat the program as already authorized by Rise.

3. Rely on a special supervisory need under Griffin. This is the lower concurrence's separate rationale, not a unanimous ground below. Its strongest objection is that the actual demand arose from the uniform database statute, not a tailored rehabilitative judgment, and solving ordinary past and future crimes is central to the actual use. No future-crime-versus-past-crime distinction by itself makes the operation a noninvestigative need. It is available for testing but supplies no automatic result.

The body's search status is common to all paths. The actual profile and collection are distinct from hypothetical unrestricted genetic research. Those distinctions are legal limits relevant to the parties' arguments, not excuses to postpone the presented constitutional question.
