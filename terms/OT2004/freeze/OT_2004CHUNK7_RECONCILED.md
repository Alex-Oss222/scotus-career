## Governing alternatives and remedy

**Domestic-category position — Stevens, O'Connor, Souter, Ginsburg and Breyer.** Section 922(g)(1)'s phrase “convicted in any court” encompasses domestic convictions. “Any” has breadth within the statutory universe, and section 921(a)(20)'s domestic exclusions and restoration arrangement help identify that universe. The provision excludes the specified federal or state business-regulatory offenses and state misdemeanors punishable by two years or less; applying the general phrase worldwide while leaving those exclusions domestic would make the federal disability turn differently on otherwise comparable foreign offenses. The statute supplies no general recognition or fairness-screening procedure to select suitable foreign judgments. The ordinary domestic setting reinforces the contextual reading. This is a categorical statutory construction, not a finding that Small's Japanese trial was unfair or an application of a mechanical territorial bar to American firearm possession.

The strongest contrary argument combines the unqualified phrase with the fact that the regulated possession occurs in the United States. The five accept that domestic possession is the offense but reject the inference that every foreign judgment therefore qualifies as its predicate. The incorporation of antecedent status is its own statutory question. Beecham chooses the convicting jurisdiction's law for restoration once the relevant conviction enters the category; it does not determine that every foreign jurisdiction is included. Caron's substantial retained firearms restriction preserves a domestic predicate's federal effect; neither its rule nor a protective purpose expands the antecedent category. The five reverse and remand for proceedings consistent with exclusion of the Japanese predicate; no independent domestic predicate is established.

**Inclusive position — Scalia, Kennedy and Thomas.** “Any court” includes foreign courts entering convictions for crimes punishable by more than one year. Congress used express federal-or-state limits in selected neighboring exclusions but omitted such a modifier from the predicate. Their construction regulates subsequent American possession and neither punishes the Japanese conduct as a federal offense nor directs Japan's courts. They would affirm the presented statutory ruling.

The strongest opposing argument is the domestic structure and the possibility that foreign offenses or procedures differ profoundly. These three conclude that the differences do not authorize a categorical geographical adjective that Congress omitted. Some asymmetry between the general predicate and specified exclusions is permissible. Beecham's selector can refer to the convicting jurisdiction without changing the predicate text; Caron's disagreement about an express restoration condition does not resolve this different textual issue. A particular constitutional objection to a fundamentally unfair predicate is separate from categorical inclusion. These three do not adopt the lower court's entire civil-recognition screen or invent a new discretionary regime.

**Lower screening regime and limits.** The fresh neutral validation identifies two mandatory nonrecognition grounds—an inadequate impartiality/due-process system and absence of personal jurisdiction under the applicable standards—and six discretionary grounds: absence of subject-matter jurisdiction, inadequate timely notice, fraud, public-policy repugnance, conflict with another recognized final judgment, and proceedings contrary to an agreed alternative forum. “May refuse” does not become “must refuse.” The statutory reversal avoids an individual determination under that regime. The inclusive position affirms the statutory category while leaving the existing individual-fairness determination in its actual posture; it does not transform those factors into Supreme Court law. No Justice's frozen position finds a Japanese defect, resolves an additional hearing claim or reaches a constitutional firearms challenge outside the granted statutory question.

## Components and controlling-rule compatibility

**B1 — requirements and actual duty.** All eight accept that a common-law damages duty can be a requirement under section 136v(b), but displacement requires a duty for labeling or packaging that is different from or additional to the applicable federal obligation. **B2a application — oral representations.** All eight's frozen distinction between genuine nonlabel conduct and label duties also governs actual oral sales statements. Section 136(p)(1) defines a label as written, printed or graphic matter on or attached to the pesticide or device or its containers or wrappers. **B2b — voluntary warranty and corresponding DTPA ground.** Stevens, O'Connor, Kennedy, Souter, Ginsburg and Breyer hold an enforceable voluntary express promise outside section 136v(b)'s labeling-requirement category, even when the promise appears on the label, because the manufacturer supplies the substantive undertaking. **B4 — registration and other conflict.** All eight retain the binding Geier/Dowhal distinction between express displacement and independently established actual conflict. ## Complete statutory and record boundaries

Section 294A.320(1) makes it unlawful for a person to publish any material or information relating to an election, candidate or ballot question unless the publication contains **the name and mailing or street address of each person who paid or is responsible for paying for it**, and a statement that each such person paid or is responsible. The rule identifies payment responsibility, not automatically every member, author or distributor. The address alternative does not invariably demand a home address. Identification of a payer rather than every affiliate still creates an actual expressive and associational burden requiring its governing justification.

The separate qualifications are retained in their own terms:

- Subsection (2)(a) excludes a candidate or that candidate's political party paying or responsible for paying for a billboard, sign or other advertisement that refers only to that candidate and prominently displays the candidate's name.
- Subsection (2)(b) excludes material expressly approved and paid for by the candidate when the preparation and publication cost has been reported by the candidate as a campaign contribution under §294A.120. Candidate approval alone is insufficient.
- Subsection (2)(c) excludes a natural person acting independently and neither cooperating with nor acting at the direction of a business or social organization, nongovernmental legal entity or governmental entity. The record presents the burdens on cooperative expression; the exception does not establish organizational anonymity.
- Subsection (3) deems identification satisfying the Communications Act of 1934 and regulations under that Act sufficient. This is an alternative compliance rule, not an exemption from every identification duty.
- Subsection (4) defines material as printed or written matter or a photograph. Publication includes printing, posting, broadcasting, mailing or otherwise disseminating to the public, and causing those acts. This case decides the presented printed-communication dispute; the statutory definition is not silently reduced to printing alone.

Standing rests on actual intended anonymous publications involving local ballot matters, public-comment rules and redistricting information; the supplemented allegations, affidavits and reported prior prosecution establish the prospective self-censorship setting accepted in the packet. ## Reconciled components

**W1 — alcohol-specific authority and nondiscrimination.** The five-Associate rule is that section 2 does not authorize States to favor local wine producers over outside producers in access to a direct-shipment channel. The Wilson Act subjects imported liquor to state regulation to the same extent and in the same manner as domestic liquor. The Webb-Kenyon Act removes the barrier that had protected interstate deliveries from otherwise lawful state regulation; its prohibition of shipments intended to be received, possessed, sold or used contrary to state law does not affirmatively authorize producer-origin discrimination. Read together, the Acts and section 2 preserve substantial control over importation and distribution without allowing a State to define its own exemption from nondiscrimination merely by enacting a discriminatory law.

Bacchus directly rejects local-product favoritism in alcohol commerce. Healy's nondiscrimination ground confirms that limitation. North Dakota concerns federal procurement and intergovernmental immunity and, with its differentiated rationales, does not decide the validity of a local-producer exemption. The broad Young's Market line does not control to the extent its language purports to immunize such preferences; the present construction follows the later surviving nondiscrimination authority. The States' invitation to overrule or confine Bacchus to the point that it permits these preferences is rejected by the five. This is a merits resolution of the asserted section 2 defense, not a bare invocation of ordinary commerce doctrine.

The three dissenting Associates adopt the competing text-based rule. Webb-Kenyon's reference to any state law and section 2's transportation/importation language, reinforced by Young's Market's direct treatment of discrimination, place importation for local delivery/use within state control notwithstanding the dormant Commerce Clause. They regard direct import/distribution control as at least within the Amendment's core regulatory concern. They can distinguish Bacchus's acknowledged local tax subsidy from the channel controls; insofar as Bacchus necessarily forbids the challenged controls, they reject its limiting interpretation rather than pretend it is absent. That position is supported by the historical writings and does not become controlling law merely because expressed here.

**W2 — each preference and actual justification.** Michigan gives qualifying local wineries direct consumer access while outside wineries must sell through the wholesale/retail channel. Its $300 outside-seller license does not confer the direct access available through the $25 small local-winery route. New York makes an outside winery establish a local branch factory, office or storeroom and receive wine at the specified in-state bonded facility to gain direct-sales access. Requiring an outside producer to become locally established is not equal market access. The farm-winery license has its own production cap and New York-fruit condition, distinct from the general winery license; those provisions must not be collapsed into a claim that every New York license requires local grapes.

For Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Ginsburg and Breyer, each discriminatory difference must advance a legitimate local purpose that reasonable nondiscriminatory alternatives cannot adequately serve. Minors' protection, revenue collection and accountable distribution are legitimate purposes, but their legitimacy does not justify the geographic difference. The supported alternatives include evenhanded licenses, tax and sales-report duties, accessible records, jurisdictional accountability, age verification and sanctions. The relevant comparison is regulated equal access. The States have not established that those methods fail while exempting local producers from comparable restrictions.

Michigan's evidence is insufficient even if the contested submissions are treated as admissible and viewed favorably. This is not an evidentiary gap warranting another opportunity to justify the preference. New York's real licensing, records, bonds and accountability mechanisms show regulatory methods that can be imposed on equal terms; they do not prove that local establishment is indispensable. The five adopt no new proof quantum or demand for perfect certainty beyond the governing discrimination rule, and require no experiment or fresh economic study.
