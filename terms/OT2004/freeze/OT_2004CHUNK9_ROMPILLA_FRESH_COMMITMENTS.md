## The actual state rationale and two lawful paths

The relevant state adjudication is Commonwealth v. Rompilla, 554 Pa. 378, 721 A.2d 786 (December 10, 1998). Strickland was then established. Williams and Wiggins subsequently explain that existing duty and the separate habeas inquiry; neither supplies a new 1998 checklist. The state opinion credited counsel's accounts that family members did not disclose the later-described abuse during the original interviews. It relied on three mental-health evaluations, the experts' unhelpful conclusions, and counsel's provision of the information those experts requested. The court treated those efforts as adequate investigation. This model preserves the historical credibility findings, including the fact of nondisclosure. It does not infer that the family never suffered abuse, that counsel examined the complete prior-case file, or that experts had records they were not given.

The strongest route to denial respects counsel's substantial actual efforts and the state court's permissible evaluation of them. Family and client interviews yielded little, three experts failed to identify useful mitigation, and counsel ultimately obtained the earlier victim's testimony. A lawyer need not investigate every document merely because it exists. On that view, the later success of a new investigation cannot itself make the State's earlier approval objectively unreasonable. A court could prefer fuller preparation while still denying the statutory gateway. Kennedy, Scalia and Thomas take that route.

The strongest route to relief distinguishes a broad search for unknown mitigation from examination of the specific, available file the prosecution identified as central to its aggravation case. Preparing to meet that detailed prior-offense proof required timely scrutiny of the source, and the known file provided a concrete route to prison and cognitive-history information. Client discouragement, family silence and examinations conducted without those records could not make this independent source irrelevant. Obtaining the victim's testimony on the eve of sentencing addressed part of the prosecution's presentation but did not replace timely examination of the complete file or permit an informed choice about the substantial leads it contained. Stevens, O'Connor, Souter, Ginsburg and Breyer take that route.

For those five, the statutory conclusion is independently supported: the State's reliance on interviews and expert evaluations never supplies a professionally reasonable basis for leaving this known, accessible and central source insufficiently examined. The deficiency does not depend on a later professional guideline or on the proposition that every prior conviction file must always be obtained. Strickland's already established distinction between informed judgment and an unreasoned limit on investigation supplies the rule. On these particular facts, the state approval falls outside its permissible range, even after the trial and state-court deference are both honored. The five then independently find counsel's actual performance unreasonable for the same concrete omission. The legal application does not require disregarding any credited testimony under §2254(e)(1), and no separate §2254(d)(2) ground is adopted.

## The five-Associate prejudice conclusion

The five do not rest on Pennsylvania's failure to contest prejudice. Strickland still requires a reasonable probability sufficient to undermine confidence in the actual penalty result. Because the state adjudication ended at performance, this element is reviewed independently after the §2254(d)(1) gate is satisfied. The state opinion's observation that some records were not wholly favorable explains its investigation assessment; it is not a separate whole-record no-prejudice ruling entitled to a second layer of §2254(d)(1) protection.

The original jury heard brief family pleas for mercy, descriptions of Rompilla as a good man, assertions of innocence and his fourteen-year-old son's love and willingness to visit. The jury found the son's appearance and possible rehabilitation mitigating but found aggravation weightier. A reasonable investigation of the known source would have supplied leads to a materially different account: records of cognitive testing near third-grade levels after years of schooling, juvenile and adult alcohol-associated problems and incarceration, and family and documentary evidence of extreme deprivation, sustained violence and parental alcoholism. The developed evidence includes maternal drinking during pregnancy, severe beatings, confinement in a filthy dog enclosure, inadequate clothing, lack of plumbing, an unheated sleeping space, neglect and social isolation. Experts supplied with the records reported organic brain damage and substantial cognitive impairment, possible prenatal-alcohol effects and substantially impaired capacity at the offense. Those opinions explain the potential significance of the supported history; they do not require the Court to diagnose Rompilla or declare him categorically ineligible for death.

The strongest independent case against prejudice is substantial. The murder involved repeated stabbing and burning; the jury found commission during another felony, torture and a significant history of violent felonies. The newly developed record also contains assaultive conduct, repeated alcohol-associated misconduct and other facts a prosecutor could use adversely. An informed jury could still impose death, and neither a difficult childhood nor cognitive impairment legally eliminates any established aggravator. The five nevertheless find the probability threshold satisfied because the added account changes the mitigation from general familial attachment and mercy to a documented explanation of prolonged damage and impaired functioning. Much of the violent-history aggravation was already before the jury, while the explanatory deprivation and impairment were absent. On the entire record, that difference is sufficiently weighty to undermine confidence in the penalty selection despite the crime's severity. It need not make a different result more likely than not.

This reasoning applies the ordinary reasonable-probability standard to the actual lawful choice between capital and noncapital punishment. It does not import Virginia's or Maryland's particular unanimity provisions from Terry Williams or Wiggins. The supplied objective sources have not independently established Pennsylvania's precise unanimity text, and no specific one-juror numerical formula is necessary to the present conclusion. Nor does the State's noncontest authorize a new assumption about the truth of every later assertion; the supplied supported records and testimony are the evidentiary basis.

## Remedy, writings and operative limits

The five would reverse the Third Circuit and remand with directions to restore conditional sentence relief. The District Court must set a definite reasonable period in which Pennsylvania conducts a constitutionally adequate new penalty proceeding or imposes a lawful noncapital sentence, require notice of the chosen course, and verify completion through the resulting sentencing order. Repeating the original penalty decision without an adequately prepared defense does not comply. If the State fails to comply, the writ must be enforced against the invalid death sentence and execution. Custody independently justified by the conviction remains subject to a lawful sentence; the guilt judgment is not set aside.

The supplied postconviction development supports deciding the prejudice element now. A new evidentiary hearing is not ordered simply because the opinion applies a cumulative standard. The lawful details of a new state sentencing proceeding and the federal court's reasonable deadline are implementation work below, not unresolved conditions that prevent deciding entitlement to conditional relief.

The three denial commitments support a joint gateway dissent. Its shared point is that the State's actual approval remained reasonable, not that counsel had an absolute option to ignore the prosecution's materials or that the omitted evidence could never matter. Kennedy's distinction from his Wiggins join should be stated substantively in that writing; it need not become a second dissent if the shared analysis preserves it. The five can join one context-bound relief rationale. O'Connor's caution against a universal checklist belongs in the operative reasoning and does not by itself require an extra concurrence. Under the Engine's negative-statement rule, hypothetical objections fully answered by the common text generate no additional writing. Assembly must retest joins if it broadens the duty, changes the credited facts or collapses the three required inquiries.

## Authority treatment, chronology and material limits

- **Strickland v. Washington, 466 U.S. 668 (1984):** Applies as the established 1998 source of reasonable investigation, informed strategic judgment and actual reasonable-probability prejudice. The five apply that rule to the known file; the three disagree over the state court's permissible application.
- **Williams v. Taylor (Terry Williams), 529 U.S. 362 (2000):** Applies the independent objective-unreasonableness inquiry and the temporal limit on established law, together with the conditional sentencing remedy. It does not become a newly established constitutional rule attributed to 1998 or transfer a Virginia-specific penalty rule to Pennsylvania.
- **Wiggins v. Smith, 539 U.S. 510 (2003):** Applies its separation of the statutory gate, constitutional performance and cumulative prejudice, and its focus on actual known investigative leads. Its Maryland-specific record and penalty structure are not mechanically transplanted. The five find the central known file sufficient for relief; Kennedy distinguishes the investigative information in Wiggins; Scalia and Thomas retain their gateway objection.
- **Woodford v. Visciotti, 537 U.S. 19 (2002):** Remains effective as to actual reasonable state adjudications and whole-record scrutiny. Its state prejudice determination differs from the unadjudicated element here. All eight Associates' earlier join in its denial of relief supplies a real restraint; neither childhood abuse nor an imperfect phrase alone establishes habeas entitlement.
- **Lockett v. Ohio and Eddings v. Oklahoma:** Preserve the legal relevance of individual background and mental-condition mitigation. They do not dictate presentation of every unfavorable record or supply presumed prejudice.
