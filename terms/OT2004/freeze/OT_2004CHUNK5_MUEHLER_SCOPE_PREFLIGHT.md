## Law and lawful paths

Existing Holland and Brosseau positions distinguish objective constitutional force review from the separate conduct-date notice inquiry. Summers governs execution-related detention, not a general personal-immunity rule. The original commitments already recognize that later decisions can explain the method without supplying missing February 1998 notice. Whether immunity exists on a specified assumed account is therefore a distinct legal question, even when some authorities bear on both inquiries.

One supported path regards the specific requested defense as fairly included because it concerns the same conduct and judgment, was litigated below, and was briefed by both sides; that path could permit an independent notice ruling on a sufficiently defined account. The principal objection is that shared precedents and requested relief do not make the logically independent defense part of questions expressly directed to constitutional legality. A second path confines this decision to the constitutional questions and their necessary judgment consequences, leaving any properly preserved immunity defense for lawful lower-court proceedings. The principal objection is that this could unnecessarily return a legal defense already developed by the parties. Neither path requires inventing waiver, default, or an absent filing. Supplemental modeling must choose reach independently for each Justice, rather than infer it from the Justice's substantive view of immunity.

Preserving an existing defense on remand is not deciding it. The mandate may require the lower court to address properly preserved independent grounds under governing law; it must not guarantee relitigation contrary to ordinary finality or mandate rules, invent a new defense, or declare the defense conclusively established. Likewise, a scope limitation does not convert the original general verdict into findings on every subsidiary fact, dispose of the independent duration theory, or disturb the rejected search claims.
