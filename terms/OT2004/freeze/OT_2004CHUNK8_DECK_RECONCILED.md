## Reconciled component rules

**D1 — Constitutional protection and security exception.** Stevens, O'Connor, Kennedy, Souter, Ginsburg and Breyer support the rule that due process forbids routine physical restraints visible to the jury during capital punishment selection unless circumstances particular to the defendant and proceeding justify them. Actual danger, escape risk and courtroom order can justify appropriate restraints in the trial judge's reasonable discretion; supported history may be considered without awaiting a new incident. No fixed proof quantum, mandatory documentary format or categorical right to remain unrestrained is adopted. This record supplies no exceptional, indisputable necessity that could itself cure the absence of an articulated individualized justification.

**D2 — Application.** The same six reject conviction alone and generic juror reassurance as adequate justification. Guilt is settled, but the jury must still determine punishment fairly. The court's compelled visible chains can communicate present dangerousness at that unresolved choice and threaten meaningful participation, consultation and the dignity of adjudication. The innocence presumption is not extended to undo the conviction. The actual record supports visibility and preserved objection; no hidden-restraint or brief accidental-sighting issue is decided.

**D3 — Effect and remedy.** The same six treat unjustified visible shackling as preserved constitutional trial error. Deck need not establish a counterfactual jury verdict or prove actual incremental prejudice. Chapman places on the State the burden to establish beyond reasonable doubt, on the full penalty record, that the error did not contribute to the death sentence. Reverse the Missouri judgment and remand under that rule. If the State cannot carry its burden, the death sentence must be vacated and the State must provide a lawful new penalty proceeding or a lawful noncapital sentence. The present handoff does not itself decide the entire full-record harmlessness application. The remand cannot invent a new historical justification or retry the constitutional rule, and it does not disturb the convictions.

**D4 — Opposing sufficient ground.** Scalia and Thomas would affirm because the prior guilt-stage and medication decisions do not establish a penalty-stage rule presuming unconstitutional prejudice from visible restraints of a convicted capital murderer. Without established actual unfairness or serious impairment, the supplied showing does not justify reversal in their view. They do not invent a particular escape risk, deny the objections, find concealment or adopt a different harmlessness burden for a violation they acknowledge. Their position concerns the antecedent violation, not a holding that Chapman never governs capital error.

## Precedent treatment and independent grounds

Allen supplies legitimate courtroom control; Holbrook supplies the distinction between ordinary security and an inherently prejudicial measure; Estelle distinguishes compelled appearance and preserved objection. Riggins supports actual justification and supported effects that a transcript cannot reconstruct, but its Court opinion did not adopt the unjoined general Chapman formulation or decide this unpreserved sentencing-display theory. Sell remains confined to its treatment setting. Chapman supplies the general preserved direct-review burden; current Smith supplies closely analogous capital-error allocation and sentence-specific relief. The protection against unjustified visible restraint during capital selection is the present proposed adoption, not a rule attributed retroactively to those cases.

No material historical departure is necessary to sustain any Associate's frozen position. The changed current-law support in Smith strengthens identification of the effect inquiry without changing the historical restraint rule, disposition or remand. No historical Supreme Court factual recital was used to add an unprovided security fact or absence of impairment to the authorized record.
