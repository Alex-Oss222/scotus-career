## Historical anchor and changed legal premises

The only merits comparator is the Ninth Circuit's August 6, 2004 judgment. Judge Berzon's opinion held §294A.320 facially unconstitutional, vacated the district court's summary judgment sustaining it, and remanded for proceedings consistent with facial invalidity. The lower court first sustained standing on the supplemented allegations and affidavits, and rejected Nevada's proposed express-advocacy construction and certification request. No Supreme Court merits comparator, vote, author or lineup is identified.

The lower opinion is more qualified than a universal prohibition on on-publication identification. Part III(b)(iii) expressly permits the possibility of a carefully tailored on-publication identification rule serving campaign-finance enforcement or prevention of official corruption. Its objections nevertheless treat the report/message distinction as constitutionally determinative in the analysis of Nevada's ordinary informational justification, extend the historical McIntyre analysis to collective expression, and substantially discount the informational usefulness of identifying an organizational payer. Its footnote addressing McConnell emphasizes that decision's assumed validity of the underlying attribution duty and its limited extension question. Those are the particular premises requiring reconciliation; a claim that the Ninth Circuit forbade every sponsor-identification rule would be inaccurate.

In the governing timeline, McIntyre protects the ordinary independently produced individual leaflet and expressly leaves organizational spending and candidate-finance settings for separate analysis. Stengel, September 14, 1998, Part IV, affirmatively sustains the presented organized paid candidate-advertisement requirement, including justified payer address information. McConnell, December 10, 2003, §311, affirmatively sustains the challenged extension of sponsor identification beyond express advocacy upon a material electoral-information or source-tracing connection, retains supported associational protection, and separates attribution's trigger from §201's $10,000 reporting threshold. Thus the surviving legitimate field is established by controlling holdings; it cannot be reduced to a suggestion that some future attribution statute might be valid.

These differences do not establish Nevada's complete validity. Stengel concerns paid express candidate advocacy; McConnell concerns the actual covered paid communications and the preserved extension challenge. Nevada's broader information category, absence of a spending floor or campaign window, exposure of small cooperative speakers, and particular payer/address duty require their own application of the governing rule. A dissenting position in a prior case remains a Justice-specific basis for disagreement, not a modification of controlling law.

## Complete statutory and record boundaries

Section 294A.320(1) makes it unlawful for a person to publish any material or information relating to an election, candidate or ballot question unless the publication contains **the name and mailing or street address of each person who paid or is responsible for paying for it**, and a statement that each such person paid or is responsible. The rule identifies payment responsibility, not automatically every member, author or distributor. The address alternative does not invariably demand a home address. Identification of a payer rather than every affiliate still creates an actual expressive and associational burden requiring its governing justification.

The separate qualifications are retained in their own terms:

- Subsection (2)(a) excludes a candidate or that candidate's political party paying or responsible for paying for a billboard, sign or other advertisement that refers only to that candidate and prominently displays the candidate's name.
- Subsection (2)(b) excludes material expressly approved and paid for by the candidate when the preparation and publication cost has been reported by the candidate as a campaign contribution under §294A.120. Candidate approval alone is insufficient.
- Subsection (2)(c) excludes a natural person acting independently and neither cooperating with nor acting at the direction of a business or social organization, nongovernmental legal entity or governmental entity. The record presents the burdens on cooperative expression; the exception does not establish organizational anonymity.
- Subsection (3) deems identification satisfying the Communications Act of 1934 and regulations under that Act sufficient. This is an alternative compliance rule, not an exemption from every identification duty.
- Subsection (4) defines material as printed or written matter or a photograph. Publication includes printing, posting, broadcasting, mailing or otherwise disseminating to the public, and causing those acts. This case decides the presented printed-communication dispute; the statutory definition is not silently reduced to printing alone.

Standing rests on actual intended anonymous publications involving local ballot matters, public-comment rules and redistricting information; the supplemented allegations, affidavits and reported prior prosecution establish the prospective self-censorship setting accepted in the packet. These are not findings that every proposed communication has already been adjudged unconstitutional or that the claimants proved a reasonable probability of threats, harassment or reprisals. The latter protective claim retains its required showing.

The all-information trigger cannot be rewritten into express advocacy merely by announcing a limiting interpretation. The statute contains no general expenditure threshold or pre-election time window. These features matter to fit and relative overbreadth, but no frozen Justice adopts a universal requirement that every lawful identification rule contain each feature. Separate reporting obligations and their financial thresholds remain separate; invalidating or reconsidering §294A.320 does not adjudicate those independent laws.
