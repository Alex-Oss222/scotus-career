## 65. Lingle v. Chevron U.S.A. Inc., 544 U.S. 528 (2005)

**Verified event:** No. 04-163; argued February 22, 2005; decided May 23, 2005. The Ninth Circuit judgment, 363 F.3d 846, was **reversed and remanded**, unanimously. O’Connor wrote for all nine historical Justices: Rehnquist, Stevens, O’Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg, and Breyer. Kennedy filed the sole concurrence and joined the Court’s opinion in full.

**Writing map and full readings:** [O’Connor, Court opinion](https://www.law.cornell.edu/supct/html/04-163.ZO.html) (`lingle_ZO.txt`); [Kennedy, concurrence](https://www.law.cornell.edu/supct/html/04-163.ZC.html) (`lingle_ZC.txt`). [Official reporter](https://tile.loc.gov/storage-services/service/ll/usrep/usrep544/usrep544528/usrep544528.pdf), 544 U.S. 528–549, independently confirms both writings and the decree.

**Material distinctions:** The Court removed Agins’s substantially-advances inquiry from takings doctrine because it addresses regulatory validity rather than the burden or distribution of interference with property. The recognized physical-taking, Lucas total-deprivation (including its background-property/nuisance-law exception), Penn Central, and Nollan/Dolan exaction paths remained. The latter two exaction decisions concern unconstitutional conditions on a benefit, including nexus and rough proportionality, rather than freestanding scrutiny of regulatory effectiveness. The Court expressly relied on the historical premise that eliminating the formula disturbed no prior holding: it had never found a compensable taking on that ground. That is a historical premise to test against the law entering any later reconciliation, not an assurance that every alternate legal history has the same premise. Chevron advanced only the discarded takings theory; the Court did not decide a different takings theory or order compensation. Kennedy preserved the possibility of rare arbitrary or irrational regulation violating substantive due process, noting that Chevron had voluntarily dismissed that claim without prejudice. The Court did not adopt heightened due-process means–ends review.

## 66. Johanns v. Livestock Marketing Association, 544 U.S. 550 (2005)

**Verified event:** Nos. 03-1164 and 03-1165; argued December 8, 2004; decided May 23, 2005. The Eighth Circuit judgment, 335 F.3d 711, was **vacated and remanded**, 6–3 in judgment. Scalia wrote for Rehnquist, O’Connor, Thomas, and Breyer. Thomas and Breyer each wrote alone while joining that opinion. Ginsburg concurred only in the judgment. Souter dissented, joined by Stevens and Kennedy; Kennedy also dissented separately.

**Writing map and full readings:** [Scalia, Court opinion](https://www.law.cornell.edu/supct/html/03-1164.ZO.html); [Thomas, concurrence](https://www.law.cornell.edu/supct/html/03-1164.ZC.html); [Breyer, concurrence](https://www.law.cornell.edu/supct/html/03-1164.ZC1.html); [Ginsburg, concurrence in judgment](https://www.law.cornell.edu/supct/html/03-1164.ZC2.html); [Kennedy, dissent](https://www.law.cornell.edu/supct/html/03-1164.ZD.html); [Souter, dissent](https://www.law.cornell.edu/supct/html/03-1164.ZD1.html). Local files are the corresponding `johanns_Z*.txt`. [Official reporter](https://tile.loc.gov/storage-services/service/ll/usrep/usrep544/usrep544550/usrep544550.pdf), 544 U.S. 550–580, confirms all six writings and their joins.

**Material distinctions:** The five-Justice opinion accepted government speech because federal officials prescribed the overall message and approved every word, with participation in development and power over key personnel. It did not decide that nongovernmental control itself would suffice, and did not have to classify the Operating Committee as a state actor. Targeted assessments, as opposed to general revenue, did not change the compelled-subsidy analysis. Government identification was not a prerequisite to that holding. The Court distinguished the unaddressed government-speech argument in United Foods; it did not overrule that decision or treat Glickman’s broader market regulation as present here.

The Court kept compelled subsidy distinct from personal attribution of unwanted speech. Neither the Act nor Order required attribution; the record did not establish attribution to these respondents, so the theory could support neither facial invalidity nor affirmance on this record. The Court reserved the legal validity of a properly established as-applied attribution theory. Thomas independently endorsed such a claim and noted possible amendment on remand. Breyer preferred his economic-regulation approach but accepted government speech as a solution; Ginsburg relied on economic regulation while resisting the government-speech characterization. Souter required public governmental identification for meaningful political accountability where targeted taxes funded speech; Kennedy joined that dissent but separately reserved whether even openly acknowledged government speech may be funded by singling out a discrete group. Other statutory and constitutional claims remained for the lower court. Souter’s footnote 1 notes that the Court denied certiorari on the separate narrower-relief question; that limit should not be mistaken for a merits disposition of every alternative claim.

## 67. Clingman v. Beaver, 544 U.S. 581 (2005)

**Verified event:** No. 04-37; argued January 19, 2005; decided May 23, 2005. The Tenth Circuit judgment, 363 F.3d 1048, was **reversed and remanded**, 6–3. Thomas wrote an opinion joined in full by Rehnquist, Scalia, and Kennedy; O’Connor and Breyer joined all except Part II–A. O’Connor concurred in part and in the judgment, joined by Breyer except Part III. Stevens dissented, joined by Ginsburg in full and Souter only in Parts I, II, and III.

**Writing map and full readings:** [Thomas, Court opinion except Part II–A](https://www.law.cornell.edu/supct/html/04-37.ZO.html); [O’Connor, concurrence in part and judgment](https://www.law.cornell.edu/supct/html/04-37.ZC.html); [Stevens, dissent](https://www.law.cornell.edu/supct/html/04-37.ZD.html), corresponding `clingman_Z*.txt`. [Official reporter](https://tile.loc.gov/storage-services/service/ll/usrep/usrep544/usrep544581/usrep544581.pdf), 544 U.S. 581–621, confirms the part-specific joins.

**Material distinctions:** Six Justices found the semiclosed rule, standing alone, a modest justified burden: voters could change to Libertarian or Independent status, and Oklahoma could protect party identity, election planning, and resistance to raiding and factional splintering. Only four joined Part II–A’s diminution of association with voters unwilling to disaffiliate. O’Connor and Breyer expressly recognized constitutionally significant association through primary voting, including dual associations. The six-Justice disposition did not overrule Tashjian; it distinguished the exclusion of Independents there. The majority declined the newly advanced cumulative challenge to ballot-access and registration rules because it was neither raised nor decided below and the necessary record was undeveloped. O’Connor’s Part III concern about the combined regime and detailed timetable was hers alone; Breyer did not join it. Her Part II, which Breyer did join, nevertheless assumes reasonable avenues for reregistration. Stevens’s first three parts stressed voting and associational interests and found the offered justifications irrelevant, speculative, or protective of incumbents; his broader Part IV criticism of the two-party system and gerrymandering had Ginsburg’s, but not Souter’s, join. The decision permits, rather than requires, Oklahoma to keep the semiclosed system.

## 68. Deck v. Missouri, 544 U.S. 622 (2005)

**Verified event:** No. 04-5293; argued March 1, 2005; decided May 23, 2005. The Missouri judgment, 136 S.W.3d 481, was **reversed and remanded**, 7–2. Breyer wrote for Rehnquist, Stevens, O’Connor, Kennedy, Souter, and Ginsburg. Thomas dissented, joined by Scalia. No other writing was filed.

**Writing map and full readings:** [Breyer, Court opinion](https://www.law.cornell.edu/supct/html/04-5293.ZO.html); [Thomas, dissent](https://www.law.cornell.edu/supct/html/04-5293.ZD.html), including all eighteen dissent footnotes (`deck_ZO.txt`, `deck_ZD.txt`). [Official reporter](https://tile.loc.gov/storage-services/service/ll/usrep/usrep544/usrep544622/usrep544622.pdf), 544 U.S. 622–659, confirms the map and disposition.

**Material distinctions:** The rule concerns physical restraints visible to the jury at guilt proceedings and the penalty phase of a capital case; it permits case-specific justification such as security or escape risk in the trial judge’s discretion. The capital-sentencing extension rests on accurate life-or-death decisionmaking, communication and participation in the defense, and courtroom dignity, while acknowledging that the presumption of innocence no longer applies after conviction. On this record the jury knew of the restraints, but there were no adequate individualized findings or explanation of why invisible restraints would not suffice. The Court left room for an exceptional record that itself establishes indisputably good reasons; this was not such a record. Unjustified visible shackling is inherently prejudicial: the defendant need not prove actual prejudice, and the State bears Chapman’s beyond-a-reasonable-doubt harmlessness burden. The decree remanded for further proceedings rather than declaring every shackling error structural or ordering an acquittal. Thomas and Scalia disputed the historical grounding, the conversion of prior dicta into a constitutional rule, inherent prejudice after conviction, and a findings requirement that insufficiently accounts for actual security constraints; they did not deny that restraints seriously impairing assistance in the defense could be impermissible.

## 69. Medellín v. Dretke, 544 U.S. 660 (2005)

**Verified event:** No. 04-5928; argued March 28, 2005; decided May 23, 2005. The writ reviewing the Fifth Circuit’s denial of a certificate of appealability, 371 F.3d 270, was **dismissed as improvidently granted**, 5–4. The per curiam is supported by Rehnquist, Scalia, Kennedy, Thomas, and Ginsburg. Ginsburg concurred, with Scalia joining Part II only. O’Connor dissented, joined by Stevens, Souter, and Breyer. Souter also dissented alone. Breyer also dissented, joined by Stevens. No merits holding settled Avena’s domestic force.

**Writing map and full readings:** [Official United States Reports text](https://tile.loc.gov/storage-services/service/ll/usrep/usrep544/usrep544660/usrep544660.pdf): per curiam at 661–667; Ginsburg at 667–672; O’Connor at 672–690; Souter at 690–692; Breyer at 692–695 (`medellin_official.txt`, all 1,735 lines read). Cornell failure is a source-access limitation only; the complete official primary text supplies every writing.

**Material distinctions:** The dismissal rests on the new Texas habeas proceeding after the President’s memorandum and possible federal obstacles: Reed/Hill cognizability, AEDPA deference, Teague, the certificate statute’s constitutional-right language, and exhaustion. These are potential hurdles, not adjudicated bars. The Court did not decide that the President could bind Texas, that Avena conferred directly enforceable relief, or that a Vienna Convention violation had no domestic remedy. It contemplated review of the eventual Texas judgment in a cleaner posture. Ginsburg preferred a stay but supported dismissal over the proposed remand; Part II stresses procedural burdens and the difference between recognizing a judgment and independently retesting the correctness of its treaty interpretation.

O’Connor would vacate the certificate denial and remand because the issues were debatable; she did not resolve ultimate treaty entitlement or order release. She treated the treaty-versus-constitutional-right certificate objection as waived, distinguished the state court’s blanket view of treaties from the specific Vienna Convention question, and disputed that the cited prejudice ruling satisfied the appropriate analysis. Souter joined subject to express caveats: preserve the breadth of the remand inquiry, and require the Fifth Circuit to await completion of Texas proceedings. Breyer and Stevens likewise preferred a stay, or failing that vacatur and remand, and viewed the ICJ argument as sufficiently substantial to remove the adverse certificate ruling. The historical opinions disagree about whether Avena is a new claim or new support for the existing claim, and about the scope of the second question presented; those disagreements must not be flattened into a unanimous procedural premise.

**Source precision:** Official per curiam footnote 4 begins with a March 8 filing reference, while its own citation identifies the subsequent application as filed March 24, and the opinion text says four days before March 28 argument. This internal date inconsistency does not alter the verified disposition; do not use the footnote’s March 8 phrase to establish a filing date without the underlying docket/application.

## 71. Arthur Andersen LLP v. United States, 544 U.S. 696 (2005)

**Verified event:** No. 04-368; argued April 27, 2005; decided May 31, 2005. The Fifth Circuit judgment, 374 F.3d 281, was **reversed and remanded**, unanimously. Rehnquist wrote for all nine historical Justices. No separate opinion was filed.

**Writing map and full reading:** [Rehnquist, Court opinion](https://www.law.cornell.edu/supct/html/04-368.ZO.html), including all ten footnotes (`andersen_ZO.txt`). [Official reporter](https://tile.loc.gov/storage-services/service/ll/usrep/usrep544/usrep544696/usrep544696.pdf), 544 U.S. 696–708, confirms the unanimous authorship and remand decree.

**Material distinctions:** The opinion construes the 2000 version of §1512(b)(2)(A) and (B), not later amendments. Knowledge modifies corrupt persuasion; conviction requires consciousness of wrongdoing. Persuasion, including advice to honor a valid document-retention policy in ordinary circumstances or to invoke a lawful privilege, is not inherently corrupt. The instructions’ allowance of conviction despite sincere belief in lawfulness and their dilution of corruptness into mere impediment failed to communicate the statutory element. Independently, there must be a nexus to a particular contemplated official proceeding in which the documents might be material. A proceeding need not be pending or about to begin, but that does not mean it need not be foreseen. The Court did not explore the outer limits of consciousness of wrongdoing, determine evidentiary sufficiency, or order acquittal. Footnote 10 confirms preservation of the nexus claim while expressly declining the unpreserved claim that informal inquiries are outside the statute. The opinion’s examples of lawful retention do not establish that Andersen’s actual conduct or policy compliance was lawful; footnote 4 recites the policy’s separate threatened-litigation, litigation/subpoena, and notification provisions.

## 72. Cutter v. Wilkinson, 544 U.S. 709 (2005)

**Verified event:** No. 03-9877; argued March 21, 2005; decided May 31, 2005. The Sixth Circuit judgment, 349 F.3d 257, was **reversed and remanded**, unanimously. Ginsburg wrote for all nine historical Justices. Thomas concurred alone and joined the Court’s opinion in full.

**Writing map and full readings:** [Ginsburg, Court opinion](https://www.law.cornell.edu/supct/html/03-9877.ZO.html), including all fourteen footnotes; [Thomas, concurrence](https://www.law.cornell.edu/supct/html/03-9877.ZC.html), including all three footnotes (`cutter_ZO.txt`, `cutter_ZC.txt`). [Official reporter](https://tile.loc.gov/storage-services/service/ll/usrep/usrep544/usrep544709/usrep544709.pdf), 544 U.S. 709–733, confirms the two writings and the judgment.

**Material distinctions:** This was a facial Establishment Clause challenge to RLUIPA §3 in an interlocutory appeal from denial of a motion to dismiss, with petitioners’ sincerity and bona fide religious membership stipulated only for that stage. The Court held §3 a permissible accommodation of exceptional burdens created by institutional confinement. It required attention to burdens on nonbeneficiaries, neutral administration among faiths, and security and institutional order, with due regard for officials’ expertise and limited resources. It did not displace the statutory compelling-interest/least-restrictive-means standard with Turner review, promise success on any inmate’s accommodation claim, or require payment for devotional accessories. Sincerity may be examined; theological centrality may not. Available administrative remedies must first be exhausted. Institutions may resist excessive demands, unjustified burdens on others, or demands jeopardizing effective operation, with application-specific adjudication if necessary.

The Court did not reach §2’s land-use provisions, Spending Clause, Commerce Clause, Tenth Amendment, or additional federalism objections not addressed by the court of appeals; it resolved the case without applying Lemon. It did not decide the continuing personal claims of the two released plaintiffs because other inmates’ live claims sufficed. Thomas agreed under modern Establishment Clause precedent and added a historical federalism analysis: the Clause does not prohibit all federal legislation touching religion. His doubts about incorporation and about Congress’s spending or commerce authority remained separate views, not holdings rejecting §3. He would address the Establishment Clause federalism theory even though the Court reserved it, but agreed that the other enumerated-power challenges were outside the question and not properly decided then.

## 73. Tory v. Cochran, 544 U.S. 734 (2005)

**Verified event:** No. 03-1488; argued March 22, 2005; decided May 31, 2005. The California Court of Appeal judgment was **vacated and remanded**, 7–2; Sylvia Dale Mason Cochran was substituted for Johnnie Cochran as respondent. Breyer wrote for Rehnquist, Stevens, O’Connor, Kennedy, Souter, and Ginsburg. Thomas dissented, joined by Scalia. No other writing was filed.

**Writing map and full readings:** [Breyer, Court opinion](https://www.law.cornell.edu/supct/html/03-1488.ZO.html); [Thomas, dissent](https://www.law.cornell.edu/supct/html/03-1488.ZD.html) (`tory_ZO.txt`, `tory_ZD.txt`). [Official reporter](https://tile.loc.gov/storage-services/service/ll/usrep/usrep544/usrep544734/usrep544734.pdf), 544 U.S. 734–740, confirms substitution, all joins, vacatur, and remand.

**Material distinctions:** Cochran’s death did not moot the appeal because the injunction remained in force on its terms, counsel continued to assert enforceability, and uncertainty about California law continued to restrain speech. The Court did not definitively settle all state-law consequences of death. Its constitutional holding instead concerned the existing decree under the changed circumstances: the rationale of preventing coercion of Cochran into payment had disappeared or materially diminished, leaving an unjustifiably broad prior restraint. The Court did not resolve whether defamation injunctions are categorically impermissible, or whether this decree was invalid before death. An appropriate party remained free to seek new relief tailored to the changed circumstances, with no advance ruling on its validity. Thomas and Scalia favored dismissal as improvidently granted, not affirmance on a substantive theory that the decree was valid. They stressed loss of the vehicle for the granted question, potential state-court relief, California’s allowance of collateral challenges, and avoidance of unnecessary constitutional adjudication.

## 75. Gonzales v. Raich, 545 U.S. 1 (2005)

**Verified event:** No. 03-1454; argued November 29, 2004; decided June 6, 2005. The Ninth Circuit judgment, 352 F.3d 1222, was **vacated and remanded**, 6–3 in judgment. Stevens wrote for Kennedy, Souter, Ginsburg, and Breyer. Scalia concurred in the judgment alone. O’Connor dissented, joined by Rehnquist and Thomas in all except Part III. Thomas also dissented alone. Rehnquist did not join O’Connor’s Part III; the exception applies to both joiners.

**Writing map and full readings:** [Stevens, Court opinion](https://www.law.cornell.edu/supct/html/03-1454.ZO.html), including all forty-three footnotes; [Scalia, concurrence in judgment](https://www.law.cornell.edu/supct/html/03-1454.ZC.html), including all three footnotes; [O’Connor, dissent](https://www.law.cornell.edu/supct/html/03-1454.ZD.html); [Thomas, dissent](https://www.law.cornell.edu/supct/html/03-1454.ZD1.html), including all eight footnotes (corresponding `raich_Z*.txt`). [Official reporter](https://tile.loc.gov/storage-services/service/ll/usrep/usrep545/usrep545001/usrep545001.pdf), 545 U.S. 1–74, confirms all writings and the scope of both joins to O’Connor.

**Material distinctions:** The Court considered an as-applied Commerce Clause challenge at the preliminary-injunction stage, where the challengers conceded the CSA’s general validity. The five-Justice opinion treats production, distribution, and consumption of a commodity with an established interstate market as an economic class; Congress rationally could include local medical cultivation and possession to prevent diversion and gaps in the national regulatory scheme. It uses Wickard’s treatment of home consumption, makes no requirement of individualized interstate movement, and does not require Congress to establish actual aggregate effects through a distinct medical-use finding. It distinguishes rather than overrules Lopez and Morrison, emphasizing their noneconomic subject matter and absence of the comprehensive commodity-market setting presented here. State authorization does not itself immunize activity from a valid federal scheme.

Scalia’s judgment-only rationale is separately important: authority over intrastate activity derives from the Necessary and Proper Clause in conjunction with an enumerated power, and may reach noneconomic conduct where reasonably adapted to making an interstate regulatory scheme effective. He expressly treats simple possession as noneconomic. He retains limits requiring a legitimate end, appropriate means, and consistency with other constitutional restrictions, including state-sovereignty rules; an area’s traditional state regulation alone is insufficient to defeat otherwise proper regulation of private activity. Do not describe him as joining the Court’s economic classification, or reduce his position to an independent finding that each local instance substantially affects commerce.

O’Connor’s first two parts, joined by Rehnquist and Thomas, insist on a defined medical-use class, meaningful judicial limits on the breadth of Congress’s chosen category, and substantiation that inclusion of that class is necessary or its effect substantial. They reject reliance on packaging alone and regard general CSA declarations as inadequate on this record, while recognizing that stronger evidence could justify application. Part III’s personal statement about how O’Connor would have voted on California’s initiative is hers alone. Thomas separately advocates the narrower original meaning of commerce, and tests necessity and propriety independently; he would affirm. Neither dissent established a categorical holding that every intrastate drug regulation is invalid.

The Court expressly left substantive due process and medical necessity for the lower court because the court of appeals had not reached them; its refusal to resolve those alternative bases is not a rejection on the merits. It also identified statutory reclassification and the political process as possible avenues, without adjudicating an entitlement to reclassification. California’s substantive exceptions, later identification and quantity-control provisions, and the parties’ evidence are relevant historical premises; their treatment differs across the writings and must not be collapsed into an agreed finding that diversion was proved or that all safeguards were ineffective. The Court’s footnotes acknowledge medical-use evidence that could undermine Schedule I’s factual predicates if found credible at trial, while distinguishing that question from federal regulatory authority.
