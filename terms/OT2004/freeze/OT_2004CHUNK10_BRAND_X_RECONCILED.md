## A. Delegation and Portland's precedential force

The five reversal supporters identify the Communications Act's implementing authority, including sections151 and201(b) as construed in Iowa Utilities Board, and the Commission's actual exercise of that authority in its nationwide declaratory ruling. This is materially different from the revisable transaction letter in Mead. The court independently examines the definitions and the scope of the delegation before accepting a reasonable administrative choice within the lawful range. Smiley permits explained change within authority; neither inconsistency nor the timing of administrative action itself destroys eligibility for that review. An unexplained change remains separately reviewable under the APA.

Their new prior-circuit proposition is that an earlier court of appeals' selection of a preferred reading does not exhaust a choice Congress actually delegated to the agency if the prior holding and statute leave that range open. A binding determination that the statute requires a particular answer remains a legal boundary. The distinction requires substantive examination, not a search for an express ambiguity formula. Portland's treatment of the cable transmission component did not resolve the offer question as one having only a single lawful answer; the five therefore reject circuit precedent as an automatic bar to the FCC's later permissible choice. This is a new holding at that scope, not something current Mead or Neal has already decided.

The strongest objection is Scalia's: when a court independently determines statutory meaning, its interpretation is law, and a later executive decision cannot convert that holding into a nonbinding preference. The five answer that the court must first enforce Congress's allocation of a genuine interpretive choice; accepting the agency's exercise of that choice does not authorize disregarding a fixed statutory boundary or revising the prior judgment. Stevens's qualification concerning Supreme Court precedent remains distinct. An opinion may resolve Portland without asserting that every Supreme Court construction yields whenever it omitted an unambiguity sentence.

Souter and Ginsburg accept the FCC's actual administrative role but do not join the proposed displacement rule or its application as a reason to sustain this order. Their statutory conclusion eliminates the claimed permissible range in this case. Their stance does not adopt Scalia's universal judicial-construction rule.

## B. Offered-service classification

The governing definitions concern different things. Section153(43) defines telecommunications as transmission of user-chosen information between user-specified points without changing its form or content as sent and received. Section153(46) requires an offering for a fee directly to the public or to a class effectively available to the public, regardless of facilities. Section153(20) covers the offered information-processing capabilities via telecommunications while excluding capabilities used for managing, controlling or operating a telecommunications system or managing a telecommunications service. Section153(44)'s carrier definition and qualifications remain as supplied in the independent freeze; common-carrier treatment extends only to providing telecommunications services. Neither a facilities label nor marketing alone supplies the answer.

The five reversal supporters sustain a permissible construction of the described retail service. The Commission's paragraphs38–41 describe genuine end-user information capabilities and their integration with transmission: retrieval, storage, email, access and related functions. The statute expressly contemplates information service provided via telecommunications; it does not necessarily make every such input a distinct public telecommunications offering. The management exception remains enforceable: internal network management alone does not transform transmission into information service. The described offer includes end-user capabilities beyond that exception.

The strongest contrary argument identifies a real paid transmission function whose information arrives unchanged even though optional processing accompanies it. The five answer that functional integration of this retail service permits the Commission's finished-offering classification; mere technical identification of a transmission input is not conclusive about the public service offered. Conversely, packaging an independent telephone transmission service with voicemail or an unrelated information feature would not erase the independent service. The rule is tied to the actual described offering rather than a categorical bundling exemption. Optional nonuse of a feature does not by itself remove that feature from what the provider offers.

Scalia, Souter and Ginsburg regard the actual transmission pathway sold to this subscriber as retaining an independent identity under the statute. They distinguish delivery of the finished information to the consumer from telecommunications an information provider merely uses upstream. Thus their rule does not classify every information service as a common carrier. A standalone sale is not a statutory prerequisite; selling a defined function with another function still offers it when its independent identity remains. Management, routing or incidental processing cannot erase a telecommunications service that otherwise satisfies the definition. This is the shared statutory ground in historical Part I, including its answer to the claim that the dissent would regulate every ISP.

The three also preserve Part I's remedial and statutory caution: any permissible deregulation through section160 must satisfy that provision's own conditions, and potential Title I ancillary powers cannot automatically be treated as an alternative authority to impose every common-carrier obligation on an entity classified outside telecommunications service. The carrier limitation in section153(44) remains relevant. They identify the difficulty without adjudicating a future access order that is not before the Court.

## C. APA review and disposition

For the five reversal supporters, the classification is within the statute and the preserved differential-treatment objection therefore requires its own answer. The FCC's paragraphs42–44 identify the historical Computer II treatment of telephone-company facilities, their bottleneck role, and the decision not to extend that framework automatically to the distinct cable setting. Emerging alternative broadband platforms and the Commission's stated investment and innovation objectives support reconsideration within its lawful authority. These are the agency's reasons, not a judicially invented replacement explanation. The agency can address the different regimes incrementally; the present disposition does not decide a later DSL classification or a particular Title I access mandate.

The three classification dissenters do not need an independent final arbitrariness ruling. Regulatory history cannot cure what they identify as the threshold statutory error. They would preserve invalidation of the telecommunications exclusion and remand for implementation of the correct statutory category and any properly invoked statutory forbearance. They do not prescribe access terms, rates or an all-purpose broadband regime themselves.
