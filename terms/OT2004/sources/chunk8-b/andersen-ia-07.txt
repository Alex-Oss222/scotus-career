concerned it could potentially be misunderstood by 
investors. We pointed out that such items are, more often 
than not, included in normal operating earnings in the 
GAAP financial statements. We also insisted that the 
Company not use such a description in public filings with 
which we may have some association (i.e., in 10-Q and 10-K

Subject Enron Press Release Discussions 


On Friday evening, October 12, 2001, I received a draft of 
Enron’s anticipated press release regarding third quarter 
2001 results which indicated Enron’s intentions to record 
numerous charges against income for the quarter totaling 
approximately $1 billion on an after-tax basis. The charges 
were described as “non-recurring” in the draft. 


Enron had sometimes used this description in past press 
releases. In such cases, we had always informed 
management that, although we understood that press 
releases were the Company’s responsibility, we did not 
advise the use of “non-recurring” as a description and were 
concerned it could potentially be misunderstood by 
investors. We pointed out that such items are, more often 
than not, included in normal operating earnings in the 
GAAP financial statements. We also insisted that the 
Company not use such a description in public filings with 
which we may have some association (i.e., in 10-Q and 10-K 
MD&A information). Whether because of our views or 
otherwise, management has generally described these or 


JA-101 


similar items as “Items Impacting Comparability in such 
public filings”. 


Because of the magnitude of the anticipated third quarter 
2001 charges and because they were being described as 
“non-recurring” in the draft release, I shared excerpts of the 
draft with Mike Odom, Rich Corgel and Gary Goolsby of our 
practice risk management group. and Nancy Temple of our 
legal group for advice regarding this situation. 


After discussion with the above individuals on Sunday, 
October 14, I spoke with Rick Causey, Enron’s Chief 
Accounting Officer, about the company’s presentation 
approach. I told Rick that, while we recognized that press 
releases are solely the Company’s responsibility, we had 
strong concerns that the presentation of the charges as non- 
recurring could be misconstrued or misunderstood by 
investors. I also informed him that we were aware of 
enforcement actions undertaken against companies by the 
SEC in cases where they believe such a presentation was 
materially misleading. Our advice was that the Company 
should consider changing the presentation or should 
otherwise undertake whatever procedures they might deem 
necessary, including the involvement of counsel, to ensure 
they did not consider the presentation to be misleading. 
Rick acknowledged my advice. 


On Monday, October 15, 2001, the night before the release, I 
inquired of Rick what procedures may have been performed. 
He responded that he had raised the issue internally and 
that the press release had gone through “normal legal 
review”. 


The release was issued early Tuesday, October 16, 2001, 
with essentially the original presentation.

The conclusion that a preliminary inquiry by the 
SEC is not an “official proceeding” is a sensible one. The 
SEC launches a vast number of preliminary inquiries, only a 
smal] percentage of which turn into enforcement 
proceedings. These inquiries thus do not trigger the kind of 
attention or alarm that would justify, for example, 
suspending a firm’s document retention policies. To 
criminalize document destruction having an alleged nexus 
only with a preliminary SEC inquiry would thus encroach 
too severely on the document management practices of 
companies monitored by the SEC, while making the 
obstruction of justice statutes apply too broadly in this 
arena. As a consequence, Andersen’s proposed instruction, 
which indicates that a formal investigation “set in motion by 
a vote” of the SEC is a “proceeding” but that “an informal 
inquiry conducted by SEC staff” is not (Andersen instr. at 
6), correctly states the law; the Department’s proposed 
instruction, which declares that “all of the steps and stages 
in the performance by an agency of its governmental 
functions” is a “proceeding” (DOJ instr. at 34-35), is plainly 
wrong. 


JA-146 


b. The “official proceeding” must be 
“ongoing or scheduled” at the time 
of the corrupt persuasion. 

In United States v. Shively, 927 F.2d 804, 812-13 (5th 
Cir. 1991), the Fifth Circuit stated that Section 1512 
requires “at least a circumstantial showing of intent to 
affect testimony at some particular federal proceeding that 
is ongoing or is scheduled to be commenced in the future.” 
(emphasis added). That holding is not dictum, and it is 
binding on this Court. Accordingly, Andersen is entitled to 
an instruction reflecting the language in Shively. 

In Shively, the defendants burned down their 
property to collect insurance proceeds. They made two 
threats in connection with the subsequent state court action 
instituted to recover the insurance. Five years after the 
arson, and two and one-half years after the Shivelys’ 
threats, a grand jury indicted the Shivelys. The 
government proceeded on the theory that the threats were 
designed to prevent the potential witness from testifying 
honestly before any federal grand jury that might later be 
convened. 

The court held that there was an insufficient nexus 
between the threats and the official proceedings to support 
a conviction. It stated: 


A defendant can act without knowledge of an official 
proceeding or its federal nature and, as a result, 
influence the witness’s testimony at that proceeding. 
The coincidence of this act and result will not suffice, 
however, for criminal liability to attach under this 
statute. Without at least a circumstantial show'ng 
of intent to affect testimony at some particular 
federal proceeding that is ongoing or is scheduled to 
be commenced in the future, this statute does not 
proscribe his conduct. 


927 F. 2d at 812-13 (emphasis added). Thus, under Shively, it 


JA-147 


is insufficient for the government to show that the 
defendant intended to affect some hypothetical future 
federal proceeding. The government must demonstrate 
instead that the defendant’s acts were consciously directed 
toward a specific official proceeding that, if not yet 
commenced, is scheduled to begin in the future. The 
government's invitation to the Court to disregard this 
holding—which turns, in almost so many words, on the 
proposition that the Fifth Circuit did not mean what it 
expressly said (see DOJ Br. at 49)—is simply insubstantial. 

Contrary to the government’s argument, Shively’s

statutory requirement that “an official proceeding need not 
be pending or about to be instituted at the time of the

the proceeding, although not pending, be scheduled and 
concretely planned. See United States v. Royal Caribbean 
Cruises, Ltd., 24 F.Supp.2d 155, 163 (D.P.R. 1997) (At he 
government must notify the defendants of the specific 
proceeding which was obstructed.”). The standard set forth 
in Shively is a perfectly reasonable construction of a statute

federal official proceeding. Cf. United States v. Aguilar, 515 
U.S. 593. 599 (1995) (construing 18 U.S.C. § 1508) (“We do 
not believe that uttering false statements to an 
investigating agent ... who might or might not testify before 
a grand jury is sufficient to make out a violation of the 
catchall provision of § 1503”).

The government also asks the Court to reject

The government’s only argument—the last refuge of 
the desperate lawyer—is that Shively should, for 
unexplained reasons, be read to establish a rule only “for

phrased quite generally, as follows: 


A defendant can act without knowledge of an official 
proceeding or its federal nature and, as a result, 
influence the witness’s testimony at that proceeding. 
The coincidence of this act and result will not suffice, 
however, for criminal liability to attach under this 
statute. Without at least a circumstantial showing of 
intent to affect testimony at some particular federal 
proceeding that is ongoing or is scheduled to 
commenced in the future, this statute does not 
proscribe his conduct. 


927 F.2d at 812-813. There is no basis for construing this

perfectly reasonable construction of Section 1512, which this

than Shively, demonstrates the need for a clear and tangible 
nexus to a particular official proceeding. Without such a 
well-defined nexus, the line between innocuous file clean-up 
and criminal obstruction would be hopelessly murky.
