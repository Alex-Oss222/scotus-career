## Current Law

The opening trackers remain the baseline; the following completed Records add controlling law at their effective dates. Their Public Projections supply the complete operative rules, authority, explanations and remedies. These summaries preserve the material distinctions needed for continuation; the underlying Records control any conflict.

- **October 22 — [American Jobs Creation Act, section 703](../records/american-jobs-creation-act-section-703_source_2004-10-22.md).** Enacted noncase law adds then-section 62(a)(19) treatment for otherwise allowable attorney-fee and court-cost deductions on the covered discrimination, employment, civil-rights, retaliation, specified false-claim and Medicare secondary-payer actions defined in section 703. The deduction cannot exceed the amount included in gross income for that taxable year from the relevant judgment or settlement. Both the payment of fees or costs and the judgment or settlement must occur after October 22, 2004. The exact enacted coverage, exceptions and dual temporal trigger are preserved in the source Record. Banks and Banaitis concern earlier transactions and retain their applicable tax-year law.

- **November 9 — [Leocal v. Ashcroft](../records/leocal-v-ashcroft_merits_2004-11-09.md#public-projection).** O'Connor's unanimous opinion excludes an offense permitting accidental or negligent injury without employment of force against another from both parts of 18 U.S.C. §16. Elements and the applicable conviction record govern; injury cannot supply force use, and risk of injury cannot substitute for the specified risk of force use during the felony. A court may decide a disputed criminal predicate before applying the review restriction. The DUI aggravated-felony ground must be removed and continuing immigration consequences corrected through the reviewing court and agency; the convictions remain valid. A recklessness-specific offense is outside the presented question. Bailey and Taylor are applied; Calcano-Martinez is distinguished on its conceded predicates.

- **November 15 — [Smith v. Texas](../records/smith-v-texas_merits_2004-11-15.md#public-projection).** Souter's seven-Justice opinion applies Tennard's rejection of severe-permanent-impairment and crime-nexus relevance screens and Penry's requirement of a lawful, coherent life-verdict mechanism for independent culpability mitigation. A negative answer still certifying factual doubt the juror may not hold does not supply that mechanism. Relevance prescribes no favorable weight or disability diagnosis. The state judgment is reversed on both merits grounds. If the restriction was preserved and no remaining procedural issue precludes relief, the State must prove beyond reasonable doubt on the full penalty record that it did not contribute to death; failure requires adequate capital resentencing or a lawful noncapital sentence within a reasonable correction period. Guilt remains intact. Scalia and Thomas dissent; seven directly support every holding.

- **November 15 — [United States v. $100,348.00 in U.S. Currency](../records/united-states-v-100348-us-currency_merits_2004-11-15.md#public-projection).** Thomas's Parts I–II are unanimous: a lawful possessor may protect an established property interest or enforceable obligations throughout the property they cover, despite another person's title; this first-party protection does not revive the owner's defaulted claim. Partly punitive reporting forfeiture receives gross-disproportionality review, with the claimant's burden extended from Wagoner County, independent constitutional review and clear-error review of supported facts. Six Justices sustain $10,000 and reject $100,348; $90,348 must be returned to the legally entitled claimant. The whole-fund interest includes the established $100,000 bailment, without a personal-title finding for the remaining $348. The lower hypothetical fine level requires all four source/knowledge/recklessness/purpose conditions; the constitutional result holds under either $5,000 or $30,000 benchmark without inventing missing findings. The pre-CAFRA regime, separate civil forfeitability and independent fee/default rulings remain intact. O'Connor, Scalia and Kennedy dissent only on amount.

- **November 30 — [Anderson v. Spear](../records/anderson-v-spear_merits_2004-11-30.md#public-projection).** Kennedy's Part I has seven direct joins applying Burson's strict scrutiny to ordinary public streets and sidewalks; the unsupported 500-foot electioneering reach, including nonintimidating write-in instructions, fails. The exit-polling exclusion and confined indoor ownership exception do not restore the public-forum speech. All nine invalidate the $50,000 personal-loan ceiling and complete post-election contribution ban: initial personal funding and later donor repayment are distinct, and actual contributions remain subject to justified, closely fitted regulation. All nine support vacatur of the universal express-advocacy-only construction; eight join that ground, while Stone-Zsela joins that component's judgment alone. The decree must correct the policies' unlawful operation unless the State shows narrower relief fully cures the violation, preserve independently lawful rules and determine lawful severability. Scalia and Thomas have separate speech-method grounds.

- **November 30 — [Koons Buick Pontiac GMC, Inc. v. Nigh](../records/koons-buick-v-nigh_merits_2004-11-30.md#public-projection).** Ginsburg's eight-Justice opinion holds that the operative §1640(a)(2)(A) retains the $100-to-$1,000 individual-action range for clause (i), with the specific $200-to-$2,000 range for credit not under an open-end plan and secured by real property or a dwelling. The successive enacted structure gives effect to both ranges. The $24,192.80 statutory award is reversed for the $1,000 ceiling; independent actual damages, liability and fee entitlement retain their own bases. Thomas joins this actual text-and-structure explanation in full; Scalia dissents on amended grammar. Lamie and Hartford Underwriters are applied.

- **December 6 — [San Diego v. Roe](../records/san-diego-v-roe_merits_2004-12-06.md#public-projection).** Stevens's eight-Justice opinion restores dismissal of the reviewed employment-speech theory. The particular police-associated commercial performance fails Connick's content/form/context public-concern inquiry; public availability, compensation and lawful sexual content do not themselves establish that threshold. NTEU's protection of unrelated citizen expression and broad-prospective-ban rule remain intact; Pickering balancing is not triggered on this expression. The Court makes no operational-disruption finding as a premise. Stone-Zsela dissents on the protected-expression threshold and would require the ordinary individual employment inquiry.

- **December 6 — [Theofel v. Farey-Jones](../records/theofel-v-farey-jones_merits_2004-12-06.md#public-projection).** Stone-Zsela's unanimous opinion recognizes genuine provider backup storage under §2510(17)(B) after delivery or recipient access, requiring proof of the actual backup function by a preponderance. A sole archive without that function is insufficient. Under §2701(c)(1), physical disclosure is not conclusive authorization when consciously procured by materially deceptive invocation of patently invalid compulsory process going to the essential nature of access; negligence, routine irregularity and collateral deception do not meet that rule. Actual unauthorized access, defendant-specific mental state, remaining §2707 predicates and supported good-faith or authorization defenses remain for adjudication. Both categorical dismissal grounds are rejected; distinct statutory and state-law branches remain at their existing scope. Konop's interception and actual-user rules retain their separate operation.

- **December 7 — [Kansas v. Colorado](../records/kansas-v-colorado_exceptions_2004-12-07.md#public-projection).** Breyer's unanimous opinion resolves six exceptions and retains the original action. The Master must use the binding 1969 method for additional interest on qualifying losses incurred from 1969 onward, never before loss, preserving separately recoverable earlier principal and inflation and excluding duplicated inflation or speculative returns. The rolling ten-year H-I window is accepted as evidence under the supported error findings; it cannot authorize forbidden accumulated credits or extinguish independently proved usable-water depletion. Initial Colorado credit decisions require independent federal review of every issue essential to state-line compliance. The annual-period objection to 1997–1999 compliance is rejected on Kansas's own figures; distinct material technical objections remain returnable. The current River Master request is denied, and justified technical deferrals remain subject to a concrete, timely return through the existing reference. ### 2005-03-30 — [Rhines v. Weber](../records/rhines-v-weber_merits_2005-03-30.md#public-projection)

**Holdings.**

#### Retaining a timely mixed petition during exhaustion

**Holding and operative rule.** A district court may stay a timely mixed petition while the prisoner exhausts state remedies if the prisoner shows good cause for not exhausting first, the unexhausted claims are not plainly meritless, and the record does not show abusive litigation or intentional delay. Good cause means a reasonable explanation in the actual circumstances, not an extraordinary-circumstances or procedural-default test; the court must set reasonable, definite deadlines for seeking state relief and returning to federal court, accounting for delays outside the prisoner’s control.

**Authority.** Chief Justice Stone-Zsela and Justices Stevens, O’Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer join the complete framework. Justice Souter joins the existence of stay power and the merit, anti-abuse and deadline limits, but not the initial good-cause prerequisite.

**Controlling explanation.** Rose v. Lundy gives state courts the first opportunity to decide federal claims. Congress’s exhaustion and limitations rules can serve that purpose while a federal court retains a timely filed action. Landis v. North American Co. supplies ordinary control over pending cases, exercised here within the habeas statutes. Duncan v. Walker excludes time spent in federal court from statutory tolling under §2244(d)(2); retention preserves an existing action rather than supplying a new filing period. Pliler v. Ford left this stay question open while establishing separate protections against particular court-caused filing losses.

The warden correctly argues that routine, open-ended stays would weaken finality and reward premature filing. Cause, a potential-merit screen, exclusion of abuse and definite deadlines answer that objection. Good cause preserves an incentive to exhaust before filing and permits an individualized explanation. Its reasonable-explanation meaning also avoids turning technical uncertainty about exhaustion into a second, rigid procedural-default obstacle. Section 2254(b)(2) permits denial of an unexhausted claim on its merits; it does not compel denial. Relief remains subject to exhaustion, absence of an available state corrective process, circumstances making that process ineffective to protect the applicant’s rights, or counsel’s express waiver of exhaustion for the State, as the statute provides.

Rhines filed with more than eleven months remaining before limitations expired during federal processing. The district court identified eight unexhausted claims and imposed sixty-day initiation and return conditions. Those facts establish the practical importance of stay authority. Actual cause, potential merit, delay and the reasonableness of those conditions require findings under the announced standard.

#### Protecting timely review when applying that discretion

**Holding and operative rule.** Where good cause, potential merit and absence of intentional delay are established and dismissal would defeat timely federal review, denying a stay and dismissing the entire petition ordinarily abuses discretion; the court should retain the action, and any refusal requires a reason compatible with the statute. If a stay is inappropriate, the prisoner must be allowed to delete unexhausted claims and proceed on exhausted claims when whole-petition dismissal would unreasonably impair federal relief.

**Authority.** All nine Justices join this sufficient case for retention and the deletion protection. Justice Souter’s agreement does not make good cause a necessary condition in his separate view.

**Controlling explanation.** The exhaustion requirement directs claims first to the state courts; it does not require destroying a timely federal action whenever the two processes overlap. The filing date and continued pendency therefore matter independently of statutory tolling. A refusal must apply the governing predicates and account for its actual effect on federal review. If retention is unsuitable, deletion preserves the exhausted claims without demanding adjudication of the others. Pliler’s neutral-warning duty retains its particular triggers—an unrepresented prisoner and a qualifying court-directed dismissal election. Rhines had appointed counsel for his amended federal petition, so that duty does not automatically govern his election.

Rose v. Lundy’s total-exhaustion requirement remains operative with bounded stay-and-abeyance in the limitations setting. Duncan v. Walker retains its statutory non-tolling rule; this decision answers the distinct stay question. Pliler v. Ford retains its separate notice, causation, diligence and lawful-remedy rules. Landis v. North American Co. supplies ordinary management authority subject to the governing habeas statutes.

**Law After Decision.**

District courts have a uniform standard for retaining timely mixed petitions while state remedies are pursued. The rule preserves the existing filing and requires prompt exhaustion and return; reopening a terminated action still requires an independently valid procedural ground. The actual stay predicates and reasonable deadlines are matters of application under settled standards, including the deletion protection when whole-petition dismissal would unreasonably impair federal review.


### 2005-03-30 — [Smith v. City of Jackson](../records/smith-v-city-of-jackson_merits_2005-03-30.md#public-projection)

**Holdings.**

#### The ADEA permits an identified practice’s discriminatory effects to be challenged

**Holding and operative rule.** Section 623(a)(2) permits employees to challenge an identified neutral employment practice that causes protected-age disadvantage in employment opportunities or status, without proving that age motivated the practice. The employees must identify the particular practice and establish the causal adverse effect; an age imbalance alone does not identify its cause.

**Authority.** Chief Justice Stone-Zsela and Justices Stevens, Scalia, Souter, Ginsburg and Breyer support this operative rule. Chief Justice Stone-Zsela and Justices Stevens, Souter, Ginsburg and Breyer join the Court’s independent statutory construction and explanation below. Justice Scalia reaches recognition through the agency ground stated in his concurrence.

**Controlling explanation.** The subsection addresses limitations, segregation or classifications that adversely affect employment opportunities or employee status. Griggs v. Duke Power Co. supplies the established effects-based reading of materially parallel language. The ADEA’s additional reasonable-factors qualification gives that reading a distinct limit suitable to this statute: an adverse effect associated with age does not make a reasonable nonage differentiation unlawful.

The City and the concurrence in the judgment read the because-of-age language as requiring a motivated age decision in every case, with the reasonable-factors clause merely confirming a safe harbor. That reading does not give the effects-oriented subsection and its express qualification their separate work. The necessary causal connection is an age-linked adverse effect from the identified practice; actual age motivation remains the question for disparate treatment.

Hazen Paper Co. v. Biggins requires age motivation for intentional treatment and recognizes the difference between age and years of service, including deliberate use of service as a proxy. It expressly reserved disparate impact. General Dynamics Land Systems, Inc. v. Cline determines the direction of statutory protection, not whether an effects-based mechanism exists. Wards Cove Packing Co. v. Atonio supplies the requirement to identify the actual challenged practice and connect it to the disparity. The 1991 amendments to Title VII did not amend the ADEA; their different provisions cannot be transposed into this Act. The EEOC’s considered recognition of impact liability supports the statutory reading without supplying the Court’s independent ground.

#### Reasonable nonage factors sustain this compensation plan

**Holding and operative rule.** Under §623(f)(1), a differentiation based on reasonable factors other than age is outside disparate-impact liability. Reasonableness concerns the actual nonage factor and its relationship to the employment objective; it does not require business necessity or selection of the least disparate workable alternative. On this record, rank, tenure, existing salary and adjustment toward regional compensation levels supply reasonable nonage factors even assuming that the challenged allocation caused the reported age disparity.

**Authority.** Chief Justice Stone-Zsela and Justices Stevens, Scalia, Souter, Ginsburg and Breyer join this statutory standard and its application in the Court’s opinion.

**Controlling explanation.** Age can correlate with experience, service and compensation without making those characteristics age itself. Congress expressly preserved reasonable nonage differentiation. Griggs supplies the effects analogy, but the ADEA’s enacted qualification controls the justification required here. The EEOC regulation’s business-necessity language cannot replace that statutory standard.

The City sought to bring starting police salaries toward the regional average. A survey of comparable southeastern communities supplied wage ranges for five ranks, divided into steps and half-steps; each employee entered the lowest step that still produced at least a two-percent raise. Employees with lower existing salaries could receive larger percentage increases from the adjustment. Officers in the two highest ranks, all over forty, received larger dollar raises despite smaller percentages. Some officers over forty were also in the shorter-tenure group.

These actual mechanics connect the factors to the compensation objective. The statistics—66.2 percent of officers below forty and 45.3 percent above forty receiving raises exceeding ten percent—do not make that method unreasonable. A demand for equal percentages or another workable allocation does not defeat a reasonable nonage justification. The City therefore prevails on the impact claim on the established plan facts, assuming the claimed causal disparity.

**Precedent Treatment.**

Hazen Paper Co. v. Biggins retains its intentional-treatment and age-proxy rules; its reserved employee disparate-impact question is answered. General Dynamics Land Systems, Inc. v. Cline retains the Act’s protection of older employees against disadvantage favoring younger employees. Griggs v. Duke Power Co. supplies the effects-language analogy, limited here by the ADEA’s own reasonable-factors text. Wards Cove Packing Co. v. Atonio supplies identification and causal-linkage requirements; the ADEA’s enacted qualification governs justification. Christensen v. Harris County and United States v. Mead Corp. retain their source-specific rules for assessing agency interpretation.

**Law After Decision.**

Employees may pursue effects-based claims under §623(a)(2) by proving an identified neutral practice’s causal protected-age disadvantage. Employers’ actual nonage factors are assessed for reasonableness in relation to their employment objectives, without a universal least-disparate-alternative requirement. Ordinary job criteria are therefore challengeable, while reasonable use of nonage factors remains lawful. Deliberate use of tenure as an age proxy remains governed by intentional-treatment law.


### 2005-04-04 — [Consumers Energy Co. v. Federal Energy Regulatory Commission](../records/consumers-energy-v-ferc_merits_2005-04-04.md#public-projection)

**Holdings.**

#### Part I — Comparable access turns on actual operation

**Holding and operative rule.** FERC may authorize the covered domestic market-based sales of a foreign utility's affiliate when its reasoned assessment of the administrative record establishes functionally comparable nondiscriminatory access, satisfaction of its market-power requirements and compliance with the Federal Power Act's just-and-reasonable duties. The foreign arrangement need not duplicate every domestic tariff mechanism; section 825l(b) makes findings supported by substantial evidence conclusive, technical application remains subject to review for arbitrary action or legal error, and statutory authority remains independently reviewable.

**Authority.** Part I of the Court's opinion is joined by all nine Justices identified in Participation and controls the rule and its application.

**Controlling explanation.** The Court holds that the agency reasonably assessed the combined operation of Ontario's arrangement. FERC corrected its original affiliation conclusion on rehearing and then examined comparable access. Absent congestion, paired sales into and purchases out of Ontario offset at the common market price, leaving the export charge. Separate import and export financial transmission rights hedge the corresponding intertie price differences. These rights provide financial protection; they do not reserve physical capacity.

That difference is Consumers' strongest objection, but comparability concerns the functioning of the whole arrangement. Every participant uses the same transaction rules; at least twelve United States marketers had successfully traded through and out of Ontario; OPG lacked generation market power outside Ontario; and the alleged discriminatory manipulation of congestion lacked supporting instances or costs. Consumers identified no actual discriminatory operation. Refusal of the requested fifty-megawatt reservation therefore did not establish an inadequate access arrangement.

New York v. FERC preserves both lawful transmission authority and the mandatory response to an established section 206 violation. California ex rel. Lockyer v. FERC requires review of the agency's actual reasons. Allentown Mack Sales & Service, Inc. v. NLRB requires evidence under the announced standard, and Timken Co. v. United States distinguishes a supported permissible method from a mandatory one. Those rules sustain this explanation rather than require an identical foreign tariff. The determination concerns this authorization and record; a materially different arrangement requires its own supported assessment. Direct regulatory authority over foreign facilities lies outside the question presented.

#### Part II — The distinct independence objection was not presented on rehearing

**Holding and operative rule.** Under section 825l(b), a reviewing court may not consider an objection omitted from the application for rehearing unless reasonable ground excuses the omission. Raising independence to establish affiliation does not preserve the different claim that a lack of Order No. 2000 independence defeats comparable access.

**Authority.** Part II is joined by Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer; their eight votes directly control this rule and its application. Chief Justice Stone-Zsela agrees with the disposition of this objection without joining that rationale.

**Controlling explanation.** Consumers obtained the affiliation determination it sought. Its rehearing submissions did not separately ask FERC to decide whether the asserted lack of independence itself precluded comparable service. The Commission therefore lacked the opportunity that section 825l(b) requires to address that distinct objection, and no reasonable ground excusing its omission is established. The Court declines to reach that theory. Section 825l(b)'s separate procedure for additional evidence requires materiality and reasonable grounds for not presenting the evidence to the agency; it does not automatically excuse failure to present a legal objection on rehearing.

**Precedent Treatment.**

New York v. FERC is applied to preserve lawful domestic regulatory authority and the duty to correct an established statutory violation.

California ex rel. Lockyer v. FERC is applied to require the agency's own reasons and the administrative record.

Allentown Mack Sales & Service, Inc. v. NLRB is applied to require evidentiary support under the agency's announced standard.

Timken Co. v. United States is applied to distinguish a legally permissible supported method from a method the statute compels.

**Law After Decision.**

Foreign-affiliate access arrangements are assessed for supported functional comparability under the domestic authorization requirements. Institutional differences require reasoned assessment of their competitive effects; a demand for identical tariff form does not itself establish unlawful access. Objections to a FERC order must be presented on rehearing, subject to section 825l(b)'s reasonable-ground exception, and each distinct objection must meet that requirement.


### 2005-04-04 — [Johnson v. United States](../records/johnson-v-united-states_merits_2005-04-04.md#public-projection)

**Holdings.**

#### An order removing a particular predicate is a supporting fact

**Holding and operative rule.** An order vacating the particular state conviction used to enhance a federal sentence is a fact supporting a challenge to that sentence under §2255 paragraph 6(4). The movant must establish the order and its material connection to the asserted current-sentence claim; an actual change in that conviction’s status differs from a new judicial interpretation, repeated legal argument or unsuccessful collateral filing.

**Authority.** Part I of the Court’s opinion is joined by all nine Justices.

**Controlling explanation.** The state order changes the legal status of a particular predicate judgment. That event is distinct from the older facts underlying the state challenge. The Government’s argument that only those older facts count would start the period before the state order needed for the present claim exists.

Custis v. United States and Daniels v. United States direct covered attacks on state convictions through their proper review channel and preserve an otherwise authorized later federal application after successful invalidation. Treating the resulting order as fact gives that sequence its actual meaning. Those decisions still require the prisoner to satisfy the independent procedural and substantive requirements for relief from the current sentence.

#### Diligence concerns discovery of the existing order

**Holding and operative rule.** The one-year period under paragraph 6(4) begins when the prisoner could discover the vacatur order through due diligence. The statute requires diligent discovery and filing within one year of that discoverability date; it imposes no additional federal deadline for obtaining a state order that did not yet exist.

**Authority.** Chief Justice Stone-Zsela and Justices Stevens, Scalia, Kennedy and Ginsburg join Part II’s rule, application and remedy.

**Controlling explanation.** Congress selected discovery of facts supporting the claim. Discovering an existing event and bringing that event into existence are different acts. An order cannot be discovered before it is entered. Clay v. United States preserves attention to the particular statutory timing trigger; the distinct finality and new-right triggers do not supply an unstated procurement condition here.

The Government and dissent argue that a prisoner who initiates state proceedings should not control federal timing through unexplained delay. The concern for finality is substantial, but Congress addressed it through the one-year discovery period and the other restrictions governing collateral relief. State procedural rules govern the state challenge, and the independent federal requirements govern the later sentencing claim. Custis and Daniels establish the proper channel without adding a federal schedule for creating a state judgment. Estimating when a hypothetical earlier state proceeding would have produced relief substitutes a different condition for discovery.

Johnson obtained an order vacating convictions that included a predicate of his career-offender enhancement, then filed federally just over three months later. The categorical timing dismissal therefore cannot stand. Actual discoverability and the order’s material effect on the enhanced sentence require application of the governing rules on remand.

**Precedent Treatment.**

Custis v. United States and Daniels v. United States retain their proper-channel rule and separate prerequisites for a later authorized federal application. This decision determines the previously unresolved vacatur-fact timing question. Clay v. United States preserves the distinct statutory triggers; its ordinary-finality rule remains applicable to the trigger it governs.

**Law After Decision.**

Actual orders changing the status of particular enhancement predicates qualify under the factual-discovery trigger. Diligence is measured by when the order could be learned of, with the movant required to establish discoverability and the order’s material connection to the claim. A later state vacatur thus permits consideration through the enacted timing provision while the independent requirements governing the federal sentence challenge continue to apply.


### 2005-04-04 — [Rousey v. Jacoway](../records/rousey-v-jacoway_merits_2005-04-04.md#public-projection)

**Holdings.**

**Common authority.** The opinion of the Court, joined in full by all nine Justices identified in Participation, controls both holdings and their qualifications.

#### Qualified retirement-income accounts are similar plans

**Holding and operative rule.** A qualifying IRA that provides income replacing retirement wages and has the substantial age-related withdrawal restrictions presented here is a similar plan or contract within section 522(d)(10)(E). The exemption covers a right to future payment but remains limited to the amount reasonably necessary to support the debtor and dependents, and remains subject to the statute's conjunctive insider exception.

That exception applies only when all three conditions hold: the plan was established by or under the auspices of an insider that employed the debtor when the debtor's rights arose; the payment is on account of age or length of service; and the plan fails to qualify under Internal Revenue Code sections 401(a), 403(a), 403(b) or 408. The Rouseys' section 408 qualification defeats the third condition.

**Controlling explanation.** The Court holds that similarity concerns the common legal function of the listed plans, not identity in every term. Stock-bonus, pension, profitsharing and annuity arrangements share the provision of income replacing wages, although they differ in sponsorship, funding and payment schedules. The Rouseys rolled employer pension distributions into their IRAs. Their accounts combine tax-supported retirement saving, substantial restrictions on early full access and required later-life distributions. Minimum distributions must begin by April 1 following the year in which they reach age 70½; the then-effective tax on a distribution deficiency is fifty percent. Those features distinguish the accounts from ordinary unrestricted savings.

The trustee's emphasis on individual control overlooks the variation within the statutory list and treats the substantial early-distribution tax as inconsequential. Congress also expressly refers to section 408 in the insider exception. That reference supports inclusion of qualifying IRAs within the broader category, while leaving the support limit independent. A retirement label or subjective saving purpose alone does not establish these characteristics.

Patterson v. Shumate enforces an independently sufficient section 541(c)(2) exclusion for qualifying trust interests with enforceable transfer restrictions. That exclusion and this exemption perform different work. Patterson left the present exemption question open; its exclusion holding remains intact.

#### Substantial age-related restrictions establish the required connection

**Holding and operative rule.** These accounts provide payment on account of age because the ten-percent additional tax ordinarily restricts full withdrawal before age 59½ and ceases to impose that restriction at that age. Age need not be the sole permissible distribution trigger; limited statutory early-distribution routes do not defeat the connection between age and full access.

**Controlling explanation.** The Court holds that a substantial proportional loss of the amount withdrawn is a real restriction. The ability to obtain funds while surrendering ten percent does not create an unrestricted right to receive the entire balance. Removal of that burden at age 59½ materially changes the right to payment and supplies the causal connection the statute requires.

The early-distribution alternatives do not make full access age-independent. For example, substantially equal payments made at least annually over the holder's life or life expectancy, or the joint lives or life expectancies of the holder and designated beneficiary, may qualify. Modification before age 59½ or within five years after payments begin ordinarily recaptures the avoided additional tax and interest, except when modification results from death or disability. Medical-expense withdrawals are limited to the amount allowable under section 213's deduction rule, whether or not deductions are itemized. These bounded routes answer the trustee's contention that any early withdrawal makes an IRA equivalent to ordinary savings.

The amount reasonably necessary for support and any independent preserved account-specific objection require adjudication below. Rule 4003(c) places the burden of proving an improperly claimed exemption on the objector; the debtors must identify the accounts and the exemption asserted sufficiently for adjudication.

**Precedent Treatment.**

Patterson v. Shumate retains its section 541(c)(2) exclusion for an established qualifying trust interest with an enforceable transfer restriction. This decision resolves the distinct section 522(d)(10)(E) exemption question Patterson left open; it does not convert exemption into exclusion from the estate.

**Law After Decision.**

Courts evaluating the statutory exemption must examine the account's qualification, wage-replacement function and actual payment restrictions. Qualifying IRAs with the characteristics decided here satisfy the similar-plan and age requirements even though limited early distributions are possible. The support limit determines the exempt amount independently of categorical eligibility, and the insider exception requires all three statutory conditions.


### 2005-04-19 — [Dura Pharmaceuticals, Inc. v. Broudo](../records/dura-pharmaceuticals-v-broudo_merits_2005-04-19.md#public-projection)

**Holdings.**

**Common authority.** The opinion of the Court, joined in full by all nine Justices identified in Participation, controls both holdings and their qualifications.

#### Inflation alone does not establish a loss caused by fraud

**Holding and operative rule.** A public-market purchaser seeking private damages under section 10(b) and Rule 10b-5 must establish an actual economic loss proximately caused by the challenged fraud; alleging or proving that the purchase price was artificially inflated does not by itself establish either requirement. Section 78u-4(b)(4) places the proof burden on the plaintiff, who must establish causal loss by a preponderance of the evidence, excluding losses attributable solely to independent causes.

**Controlling explanation.** The Court holds that inducing a purchase and causing a compensable loss are different matters. A purchaser receives a security bearing the prevailing market price and may transfer the same inflation to a later purchaser before it dissipates. A subsequent price decline may instead reflect economic conditions, industry changes or new facts about the company. These possibilities do not prevent recovery for an actual loss attributable to the fraud; they defeat the assertion that inflation alone necessarily supplies that loss and its cause. A later decline alone likewise supplies no conclusive causal link.

The purchasers argue that paying more than the security's true value immediately diminishes their wealth. That argument does not explain what recoverable loss the alleged falsehood produced where the security still carries the market inflation or its value later changes for independent reasons. Basic Inc. v. Levinson addresses reliance connecting deception to a transaction. It does not presume that the deception caused every subsequent loss. Central Bank of Denver, N.A. v. First Interstate Bank of Denver, N.A. preserves actual causation and recoverable loss even within its received affirmative-assistance action. The same elements govern the present misstatement claim.

The causal account remains open to supported mechanisms rather than a single compulsory form of disclosure or economic proof. The plaintiffs need not complete a sale, identify one formal corrective announcement or present an event study merely to state a legally available theory. The required inquiry remains whether the challenged fraud caused the claimed economic loss. A comprehensive final damages methodology lies outside the accepted question.

#### The device allegations fail even ordinary fair notice

**Holding and operative rule.** Assuming for this decision that ordinary Rule 8 supplies the entire pleading demand for economic loss and loss causation, the complaint must give a short, intelligible indication of the loss claimed and its causal connection to the challenged fraud. The reviewed allegation that purchasers paid inflated prices and thereby suffered damages fails even that modest requirement; the Court does not need to decide whether Rule 9 or the PSLRA independently adds a particularity requirement for these elements.

**Controlling explanation.** Swierkiewicz v. Sorema N.A. separates fair notice from an evidentiary proof checklist. It prevents a court from demanding trial proof in a complaint, but it does not supply a missing legally adequate causal-loss account. The purchasers need to identify the loss and connection they assert; they need not prove an econometric model or eliminate every competing cause at pleading.

The complaint treats the device representation principally as a source of purchase-price inflation. Its earnings and device allegations have different histories. The February 1998 earnings announcement was followed by a steep decline; the November device announcement produced a temporary decline that almost fully recovered within a week. The earlier earnings response cannot simply be assigned to the device representation. The reviewed allegations give no adequate account beyond the inflation theory the Court rejects.

The device branch therefore cannot proceed on the present rationale. The district court must identify that defect and consider any timely proposed amendment under the applicable rules. Amendment is neither compelled nor forbidden by this decision. A sufficient causal-loss allegation permits ordinary further adjudication; if the claim remains legally insufficient after lawful amendment proceedings, dismissal follows.

**Precedent Treatment.**

Central Bank of Denver, N.A. v. First Interstate Bank of Denver, N.A. is applied for its retained causation, recoverable-loss and ordinary civil proof requirements; its coverage of qualifying affirmative substantial assistance remains in force.

Basic Inc. v. Levinson retains its transaction-reliance framework, which does not substitute for proof that the fraud caused an economic loss.

Swierkiewicz v. Sorema N.A. is applied to keep evidentiary proof requirements distinct from modest notice pleading while retaining the claim's substantive elements.

**Law After Decision.**

For private public-market securities damages claims, courts must distinguish the fraud's role in inducing a transaction from its role in causing recoverable economic loss. Inflation alone cannot supply either loss or the causal link; a complaint must at least identify the loss and the connection it asserts, and the plaintiff ultimately bears the statutory proof burden. Several supported causal mechanisms may satisfy that requirement. The assumption about ordinary Rule 8 resolves this complaint without establishing an exclusive pleading rule for all securities elements.


### 2005-04-19 — [Monsanto Co. v. McFarling](../records/monsanto-co-v-mcfarling_merits_2005-04-19.md#public-projection)

**Holdings.**

#### Part I: The saved-seed exception does not displace utility-patent coverage

**Holding and operative rule.** A plant-variety saved-seed exception does not authorize reproduction of additional seed within independently valid utility-patent claims. A no-replanting promise directed to that reproduction is not misuse merely because it prevents saving and replanting harvested seed; patent and contractual coverage must be established, and independently applicable defenses retain their force.

**Authority.** All nine Justices join Part I, including its confined contract application.

**Controlling explanation.** J. E. M. Ag Supply, Inc. v. Pioneer Hi-Bred International, Inc. permits utility-patent and plant-variety protection to coexist while keeping each statute’s exceptions within its own right. Asgrow Seed Co. v. Winterboer addresses the certificate-based saved-seed provision and supplies no general immunity from an independently applicable utility patent.

The relevant patent claims are assumed in this appeal to cover the additional generations. McFarling accepted one-season planting permission and conceded saving and replanting the harvest. Ownership of the purchased seed does not itself confer permission to make and use additional patented seed. Its reproductive character and his willingness to pay a technology fee do not compel a different reproduction license. The promise therefore survives the presented categorical defenses, and the conceded conduct establishes its breach.

This holding concerns the additional seed and the presented no-replanting promise, rather than every product made with a patented tool or every contract condition. Patent validity lies outside this appeal because the defense was not preserved in the reviewed partial judgment.

#### Part II: Patent scope does not conclusively defeat an independent tie

**Holding and operative rule.** A restriction within a patent grant does not, for that reason alone, defeat a Sherman Act §1 tying claim. The claimant must establish distinct products through actual separate consumer demand, conditioning, sufficient power in the tying market, a substantial amount of affected tied commerce, and the competitive injury and causal requirements for the relief sought; lawful patent exclusion retains substantial justificatory force.

**Authority.** All nine Justices join this general rule in Part II. Its application requiring further proceedings in this case is the separate six-Justice holding in Part III.

**Controlling explanation.** CSU, L.L.C. v. Xerox Corporation holds that patent scope does not conclusively immunize independently unlawful monopoly maintenance. The same coexistence of statutory rights and independent antitrust duties supports rejecting conclusive scope immunity for a properly established §1 tie. This extends CSU’s nonimmunity principle, while the offense remains governed by §1’s own elements.

Eastman Kodak Co. v. Image Technical Services, Inc. and Jefferson Parish Hospital District No. 2 v. Hyde supply the demand-based product inquiry and the ordinary tying requirements. Patent ownership does not prove market power, and a patent, trait or single-product label does not conclusively merge products for which separate demand exists. A preferred licensing arrangement or its royalty expense alone does not establish an independent antitrust wrong. Verizon Communications Inc. v. Law Offices of Curtis V. Trinko, LLP likewise requires the particular offense and causal basis for the requested remedy.

#### Part III: The preserved tying claim requires its own adjudication

**Holding and operative rule.** The rejection of McFarling’s tying counterclaim must be vacated because the appellate court relied on conclusive patent-scope immunity and expressly declined to resolve the separate-product inquiry. On remand, the preserved record must be tested under the independent tying elements and ordinary summary-judgment requirements; actual failure of an element may support renewed judgment.

**Authority.** Chief Justice Stone-Zsela and Justices Stevens, Kennedy, Souter, Ginsburg and Breyer join Part III and its remedy.

**Controlling explanation.** Monsanto and the partial dissent characterize the requested arrangement as a license to reproduce patented second-generation seed while paying a technology fee, rather than a coerced purchase of something independently demanded. That is a substantial merits defense. But the appellate court acknowledged the separate-demand inquiry and expressly left it unresolved, using patent scope instead. This Court cannot substitute a conclusive finding about product identity for that declined inquiry.

Kodak requires actual demand and market conditions, with the remaining elements proved in their own terms. CSU prevents the scope label from replacing that analysis; it does not transplant §2’s offense into §1. The proper course is therefore an element-based adjudication of the preserved claim. Any resulting effect on contract enforcement depends on a proved violation or defense and its own governing requirements.

#### Part IV: The actual-damages mandate remains operative

**Holding and operative rule.** The vacatur of the $780,000 liquidated sum and the existing remand for actual damages are preserved. Actual damages remain subject to surviving lawful liability, any independently established defense or offset, the proper saved-and-replanted measure and the prohibition on double recovery.

**Authority.** All nine Justices join Part IV’s preservation of the damages disposition.

**Controlling explanation.** The lower judgment applied Missouri’s requirement of a reasonable relation to compensable harm and difficulty estimating the loss. It preserved reasonableness measured against either anticipated harm or actual harm; Monsanto did not defend the grossly disproportionate award through the actual-loss alternative. The uniform 120 multiplier, based on purchased rather than saved and replanted bags, lacked the required relation to this breach’s compensable harm. The existing state-law damages disposition therefore remains in place. The question of an appropriate forecast for supplying or selling saved seed to third parties lies outside this replanting dispute and remains as the lower judgment left it.

**Precedent Treatment.**

J. E. M. Ag Supply, Inc. v. Pioneer Hi-Bred International, Inc. is applied to preserve concurrent protection and the separation of statutory exceptions. Asgrow Seed Co. v. Winterboer remains confined to the plant-variety right it construed. CSU, L.L.C. v. Xerox Corporation’s rejection of conclusive patent-scope immunity is extended from its §2 setting to the presented §1 tying defense; §1 retains its own offense requirements. Eastman Kodak Co. v. Image Technical Services, Inc. and Jefferson Parish Hospital District No. 2 v. Hyde supply actual separate-demand and tying rules. Verizon Communications Inc. v. Law Offices of Curtis V. Trinko, LLP preserves the distinct requirements for an antitrust offense and its remedy.

**Law After Decision.**

The particular patent-covered reproduction promise survives the categorical saved-seed and scope-based misuse attacks. Patent-related conditions nevertheless remain subject to independently established §1 tying liability, with statutory exclusion receiving substantial justificatory weight. Courts must assess the actual products, conditioning, markets, commerce and injury rather than treating patent scope as a conclusive defense. The general nonimmunity rule commands all nine votes; the conclusion that this record requires further tying adjudication commands six.


### 2005-04-26 — [Doe v. Porter; Sixth Circuit Nos. 02-5316 and 02-5823, 370 F.3d 558.](../records/doe-v-porter_merits_2005-04-26.md#public-projection)

**Opinion authority.**

BREYER, J., delivered the opinion of the Court. All other Justices join Parts I and II. Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas and Ginsburg also join Parts III and IV; Chief Justice Stone-Zsela concurs in the judgments in those parts.

|---|---|---|---|---|
| Opinion of the Court | Breyer | All eight other Justices in Parts I–II; Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas and Ginsburg in Parts III–IV | Affirmance | I: family standing; II: governmental devotional instruction and injunction; III: protective order; IV: fees |

**Holdings.**

#### 1. Actual classroom exposure supports the families' standing

**Rule and authority.** Families whose children are exposed to the challenged devotional teaching in their public-school classes have concrete injury traceable to the program and redressable by an injunction stopping it. Part I has all nine Justices' support.

**Controlling explanation.** The sealed sworn affidavits establish that the children attended the challenged classes. **Rule and authority.** A public school may not incorporate teaching religious doctrine as truth or prescribed religious observance into its own instructional program by assigning delivery to a private religious provider. Governmental adoption of the instructional function and its actual devotional content must be established under ordinary civil proof; summary judgment requires the absence of a genuine dispute of material fact. **Controlling explanation.** Agostini supplies the inquiry into the actual governmental act and function. Religious affiliation, location and moral vocabulary alone are insufficient. Agostini protects genuinely secular instruction by religious persons, and Good News Club protects independent private access; neither describes this school-adopted devotional curriculum. The supported operation warrants cessation of the program throughout the schools where it operates. A particular redesigned curriculum is outside the record and receives no advance approval.

**Rule and authority.** Rules 10(a) and 26(c) permit a tailored public-pseudonymity order when substantial intimate privacy outweighs the public-identification interest and meaningful confidential defense access remains. Part III, joined by all eight Associate Justices, applies that rule and sustains this order.

**Controlling explanation.** Roe II supplies the balance between public identification, substantial private injury and the defendant's ability to investigate. The very young children, religious privacy and supported threats justify protection here. Defense counsel's discovery remained unrestricted; the order limited disclosure to others. The Board's claim that anonymity prevented investigation therefore does not describe the order the court entered. The ruling sustains this exercise of discretion on those facts.

#### 4. The specific duplication objection does not establish abuse of discretion

**Rule and authority.** An attorney-fee award survives a duplication challenge when the court applies the proper rule, makes supported reductions and the remaining objection does not establish a clearly erroneous calculation or an abuse of discretion. All eight Associates join Part IV.

**Controlling explanation.** The district court deducted $9,500 for unnecessary travel and another five percent for duplication. **Holdings.**

The five Justices joining the Court's opinion control both holdings and their explanations.

#### 1. An accrued foreign tax entitlement can be the money targeted by wire fraud

The Government must prove that entitlement, material deception, fraudulent intent and the required wire use beyond a reasonable doubt; success of the scheme and completed loss are not elements.

Its authority comes from that statute, not from an Executive choice to enlarge federal jurisdiction or foreign-affairs power. The defendants contend that recognition of the Canadian entitlement indirectly enforces Canada's fiscal policy. That objection identifies why the revenue rule must be considered, but its established collection function does not confer immunity from a separate domestic fraud offense. Hartford Fire and Empagran require attention to the statute and the conduct Congress regulates; neither supplies an automatic foreign-victim exception. The actual American execution distinguishes the present application. The Court resolves the criminal question while leaving any specific monetary remedy to its governing statute and proof requirements.

- Neder v. United States: its materiality requirement and distinction between a criminal scheme and completed civil fraud remain controlling.
- Hartford Fire Insurance Co. v. California: its attention to statutory reach and foreign regulatory interests is applied to the domestic conduct actually charged.

**Law After Decision.**

A foreign sovereign's accrued right to money may be the property targeted by domestic wire fraud. ### 2005-04-27 — [Bates v. Dow Agrosciences LLC, No. 03-388; 544 U.S. 431.](../records/bates-v-dow-agrosciences-llc_merits_2005-04-27.md#public-projection)

**Opinion authority.**

STEVENS, J., delivered the opinion of the Court. Chief Justice Stone-Zsela and O'Connor, Kennedy, Souter, Ginsburg and Breyer join it in full; Scalia and Thomas join except Part III. Thomas filed an opinion concurring in part and dissenting in part, joined by Scalia.

| Writing | Author | Joined by | Relationship to judgment | Scope joined |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Opinion of the Court | Stevens | Stone-Zsela, O'Connor, Kennedy, Souter, Ginsburg, Breyer; Scalia and Thomas except Part III | Vacatur and remand with categorical protection for voluntary express warranties | I: actual duties; II: nonlabel conduct; III: voluntary warranties and corresponding DTPA; IV: parallel duties; V: registration, actual conflict and remand |
| Opinion concurring in part and dissenting in part | Thomas | Scalia | Supports vacatur and remand; opposes categorical warranty instruction | Requires actual federal comparison for label-based warranty and warranty-derived DTPA duties |

**Holdings.**

Parts I, II, IV and V of the Court's opinion are unanimous. Part III is joined by Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Kennedy, Souter, Ginsburg and Breyer and controls its warranty and corresponding DTPA holdings.

#### 1. Actual duties determine whether FIFRA displaces state law

**Operative rule.** A state statutory or common-law damages duty is preempted under 7 U.S.C. §136v(b) when its actual substantive command concerns pesticide labeling or packaging and is different from or additional to the applicable federal requirement. A manufacturer's possible response to a verdict by changing its label does not itself make the underlying duty a labeling requirement. Part I supplies the controlling authority.

**Controlling explanation.** Cipollone directs attention to the particular statutory clause and the duty on which liability rests. Medtronic establishes that common-law duties can be requirements while a state remedy enforcing equal conduct obligations is not an additional substantive command. FIFRA's text gives both propositions effect. Section 136v(a) permits state regulation of pesticide sale or use only insofar as it does not permit sale or use federal law prohibits; subsection (b) separately governs labeling and packaging. The lower court's inducement test omits those distinct statutory conditions. A manufacturer might choose to change a label after a verdict concerning defective production, but that choice does not establish what state law commanded. Conversely, describing a demand for different warnings as negligence cannot remove it from the clause. The actual duty, rather than a claim's title or an optional business response, supplies the comparison.

#### 2. Independent product duties and actual oral sales duties fall outside labeling preemption

**Operative rule.** Genuine state duties governing product design, manufacture, testing or an agent's actual oral representations are outside §136v(b)'s labeling category. Courts must identify the substantive duty for each theory, including theories combining oral statements and written labeling; a demand whose actual content requires different labeling remains subject to the statutory comparison. Part II supplies the controlling authority.

**Controlling explanation.** Design, manufacture and testing can require conduct apart from a label change. A product need not be dangerous in every possible use before its design may be defective, and a failure of proof on a particular defect theory does not transform every such theory into a warning claim. Cipollone's duty-specific method requires examination of the actual state-law obligation.

Sections 136(p)(1) and (2) define a label through written, printed or graphic matter on or attached to the pesticide, device, container or wrapper. Labeling includes labels and other such matter accompanying the product at any time, or referenced on the label or in accompanying literature. The reference branch excepts current official publications of EPA, the Departments of Agriculture, Interior and Health and Human Services, state experiment stations and agricultural colleges, and similar federal or state institutions or agencies legally authorized to conduct pesticide research. An agent's oral statement is outside these written-matter categories even when its words repeat a label. The lower court must identify whether each actual theory rests on oral conduct, label content or both. That classification preserves the statutory distinction while preventing a pleading caption from concealing a demand for different warnings.

#### 3. Voluntary express promises retain their state-law enforcement

**Operative rule.** An enforceable express warranty voluntarily undertaken by a manufacturer falls outside §136v(b)'s prohibition of state-imposed labeling requirements, even when the promise appears on the label. A DTPA claim receives the same protection to the extent it supplies a remedy for that surviving warranty. Part III's seven-Justice majority controls both propositions.

**Controlling explanation.** Cipollone distinguishes enforcement of a manufacturer's own undertaking from a state rule dictating label content. The manufacturer chooses the promised performance; state law holds it to that undertaking. The possibility that liability will lead the manufacturer to revise future promises does not change the source of the existing substantive obligation. The opposing view treats liability for a federally compliant label statement as sufficient to require an equivalence comparison. But federal compliance and a voluntary promise answer different questions: compliance establishes satisfaction of the federal command, while warranty liability enforces performance the manufacturer chose to promise. The same reasoning governs a DTPA remedy derived from the surviving warranty. Other DTPA duties receive the separate actual-duty analysis appropriate to their content.

#### 4. Equal labeling duties survive, subject to actual comparison and proper pleading

**Operative rule.** State labeling duties survive §136v(b) when they require conduct substantively equivalent to the applicable federal obligations, even if state law uses different words or adds recovery elements that narrow liability. For duties within this comparison, courts must compare the actual state and federal commands and frame jury instructions to prevent recovery for labeling conduct federal law permits. Part III separately governs voluntarily undertaken warranty promises. Part IV supplies the controlling authority.

**Controlling explanation.** Medtronic distinguishes the substantive conduct required from the additional state remedy. Section 136(q)(1)(A)'s prohibition of false or misleading labeling supplies the relevant federal comparator for the fraud and deception theories; the provisions concerning adequate directions in subsection (F) and necessary warnings in subsection (G) remain distinct. Lawfully adopted EPA regulations can specify these obligations within statutory and administrative limits. A general federal command can preempt a genuinely divergent duty; equivalence does not require identical phrasing or a product-specific rule.

The lower courts must compare the fraud and nonwarranty DTPA duties with the applicable federal requirements governing the 2000 conduct. Additional state elements such as reliance may narrow recovery while leaving the required label conduct unchanged. The first amended counterclaim expressly disclaimed a FIFRA violation, App. 192. That disclaimer receives its proper effect under ordinary pleading rules; the remand neither removes it nor grants an entitlement to amend. Its presence does not justify preserving a judgment that substituted inducement for the required substantive comparison. The surviving causes remain state-law causes governed by their own elements.

#### 5. Registration retains its statutory effect, and independent conflict requires an operative federal source

**Operative rule.** FIFRA registration is prima facie evidence of compliance with registration provisions when cancellation proceedings are absent, but §136a(f)(2) makes registration no defense to an offense under the Act. Independently of §136v(b), a state duty is displaced when the party asserting preemption identifies an operative federal command or protected regulatory method and establishes an actual incompatible state obligation. Part V supplies the controlling authority.

**Controlling explanation.** Geier preserves an independent conflict inquiry where an actual federal method requires protection; Myrick requires an operative federal source rather than an assumed policy. Dowhal applies that distinction to an established agency warning determination. Here registration alone does not establish such incompatibility. Section 136a(c)(5) permits EPA to waive efficacy data and then register a pesticide without determining that its composition warrants efficacy claims. That waiver does not eliminate the separate misbranding duty. An abstract desire for nationwide uniformity or a changed agency litigation position likewise cannot supply the missing command.

Dow must identify the actual federal source and incompatible state duty for any preserved independent-conflict contention. The record establishes no affirmative federal requirement protecting the alleged false efficacy message merely because the product was registered. Claim classification and the remaining equivalence and conflict applications proceed under these rules, while the abandoned contract challenge and the unappealed limitation-of-remedies ruling retain their effect.

**Precedent Treatment.**

- **Cipollone v. Liggett Group, Inc.:** Reaffirmed and applied for clause-specific examination of the actual duty; its distinction between voluntary promises and imposed labeling commands governs the express-warranty issue.
- **Medtronic, Inc. v. Lohr:** Reaffirmed and applied for common-law inclusion and the protection of genuinely equal substantive duties despite an additional state remedy; the same distinction governs FIFRA's comparison clause.
- **Geier v. American Honda Motor Co.:** Reaffirmed as preserving independently demonstrated conflict with an operative federal command or protected regulatory method after express-clause analysis.
- **Freightliner Corp. v. Myrick:** Applied to require the actual operative federal source for the asserted conflict.
- **Dowhal v. SmithKline Beecham Consumer Healthcare:** Its concrete agency-warning conflict is distinguished from registration, efficacy-data waiver and general uniformity advocacy here; its operative-source requirement remains controlling.

**Law After Decision.**

For pesticide liability claims, courts identify the actual state duty before asking whether it concerns labeling or packaging and differs from federal law. Genuine nonlabel safety and oral-sales duties remain outside the express clause. Enforceable voluntary express warranties, including label promises, retain state-law enforcement; a DTPA remedy shares that protection insofar as it enforces the surviving warranty. Label-based fraud and other DTPA theories remain available when their substantive duties are genuinely parallel, subject to their ordinary elements and proper pleading.

This framework applies across the covered class of pesticide claims. Registration retains its statutory evidentiary effect, and a preserved independently demonstrated conflict with operative federal law can still require displacement. The Texas duties' classification, precise equivalence and pleaded application are work for remand under these governing rules.

### 2005-04-27 — [Gates v. Cook; Fifth Circuit No. 03-60529, 376 F.3d 323.](../records/gates-v-cook_merits_2005-04-27.md#public-projection)

**Opinion authority.**

SOUTER, J., delivered the unanimous opinion of the Court.

| Writing | Author | Joined by | Scope |
|---|---|---|---|
| Opinion of the Court | Souter | All eight other Justices | Exhaustion, actual-notice constitutional judgment, retained protections and operative remedial limitation |

**Holdings.**

All nine Justices join each holding and its controlling explanation.

#### 1. The emergency process was actually completed

**Operative rule.** Section 1997e(a) requires completion of the administrative process actually available for the prisoner's grievance before suit. A substantive final-level decision on an emergency submission completes that route when the governing procedure prescribes no further review for that disposition.

**Controlling explanation.** Booth v. Churner requires exhaustion even when the process cannot grant the particular form of relief sought; Porter v. Nussle applies the requirement throughout prison life. Here the Commissioner addressed Russell's emergency grievance on its substance before suit. The policy's permission to resubmit a matter through ordinary channels if it is deemed nonemergent does not create a second mandatory sequence after the final substantive response actually given. Officials had the prescribed opportunity to correct the conditions. The statute enforces that opportunity and its real steps, rather than a later invented requirement.

#### 2. A properly exhausted common prospective claim can proceed through a certified class

**Operative rule.** A named prisoner who actually completes the available process for a common condition may represent a properly certified class seeking the same indivisible prospective correction without requiring each unnamed member to duplicate the grievance. The court must establish prefiling completion and identity among the grievance, class condition and requested correction; a different condition or individual claim retains its own exhaustion requirement.

**Controlling explanation.** Booth and Porter supply mandatory exhaustion; Rule 23 supplies representative procedure after that prerequisite is met. Russell completed the process for the systemic conditions for which the class seeks the same correction. Repetition by each absent member would add no distinct administrative claim to the already exhausted common request. This rule rests on an exhausted representative and an identical prospective claim, not administrative futility or substantial compliance. It therefore preserves the statutory requirement while allowing the representative remedy the civil rules provide.

#### 3. Officials must reasonably address known interacting dangers to an identifiable human need

**Operative rule.** Under the governing Eighth Amendment standard, prisoners must prove by the ordinary civil preponderance standard a substantial risk of serious harm and officials' actual awareness followed by unreasonable disregard. Conditions may be considered together when their interaction deprives an identifiable need such as sanitation, health or safety; reasonable protective responses remain a defense even when harm occurs.

**Controlling explanation.** Wilson v. Seiter permits analysis of mutually reinforcing conditions tied to an identified need. Helling v. McKinney permits prevention of serious future harm without awaiting disease. Farmer v. Brennan requires actual knowledge, which persistent obvious risks and repeated notice may support by inference but do not conclusively establish from official position alone. The credited evidence establishes awareness and inadequate responses to fecal contamination, sewage backflow, dangerous heat aggravated by illness and medication, insects and lost sleep, insufficient light, and serious psychiatric-care failures. Repair efforts that left the known serious dangers unreasonably unaddressed do not establish Farmer's reasonable-response defense. These particular findings sustain the constitutional judgment.

#### 4. Each prospective command must correct the proved violation within the statutory limits

**Operative rule.** Section 3626 permits only relief necessary to correct a proved federal violation, narrowly drawn and the least intrusive necessary means, with substantial weight given to public safety and criminal-justice effects. Relief requiring or permitting departure from state or local law additionally requires that federal law demand that departure, that it be necessary, and that other relief cannot correct the violation.

**Controlling explanation.** Lewis v. Casey makes the proved injury determine remedial scope. Benjamin v. Fraser supplies the current requirements for supported care and lawful continuing implementation; its pretrial liability standard does not replace Farmer for convicted prisoners. Johnson v. California likewise preserves the separate statutory remedial conditions. The particular cleaning, heat, screening, sewage, lighting and care measures answer the credited Unit 32-C dangers. Their measurements are supported means of correction on this record, not universal numerical constitutional standards. The general unpreserved demand to restart the decree for want of ritual findings does not defeat that support. Paragraph 9's unrestricted code incorporation nevertheless exceeds the required connection because it can reach practices never found to violate federal law. Actual vacatur and an operative limitation cure that defect while retaining necessary care. Officials proposing a narrower means must establish that it fully corrects the violation. The district court must preserve the findings required for continuing implementation and give security evidence its statutory weight.

**Precedent Treatment.**

- Booth v. Churner: mandatory exhaustion of the actually available process is applied to the completed emergency route.
- Porter v. Nussle: prison-life coverage is preserved; the present decision resolves the distinct representative-exhaustion question.
- Wilson v. Seiter: interacting conditions may establish deprivation of an identifiable need; generalized harshness remains insufficient.
- Helling v. McKinney: a proved serious future health risk permits preventive relief.
- Farmer v. Brennan: actual knowledge and reasonable-response protection govern the credited conditions without dilution.
- Lewis v. Casey: the proved injury fixes remedial reach, retaining the Unit 32-C boundary.
- Benjamin v. Fraser: its statutory tailoring and implementation requirements control the care remedy; its pretrial liability rule remains specific to that setting.
- Johnson v. California: its separate application of §3626's remedial requirements is preserved.

**Law After Decision.**

A certified class may pursue the same indivisible prospective prison-condition claim through a named representative who completed the actual available administrative process. Liability for the interacting dangers remains governed by actual knowledge and unreasonable disregard. Each continuing injunction command must independently satisfy §3626 and address the proved classwide violation. Outside professional standards can inform necessary care; they cannot automatically enlarge the decree.

### 2005-04-27 — [Pace v. DiGuglielmo; No. 03-9627; 544 U.S. 408.](../records/pace-v-diguglielmo_merits_2005-04-27.md#public-projection)

**Opinion authority.**

SCALIA, J., delivered the opinion of the Court, joined in full by Chief Justice Stone-Zsela and O'Connor, Kennedy and Thomas. STEVENS, J., filed a dissenting opinion, in which SOUTER, GINSBURG and BREYER, JJ., joined.

The Court's five-Justice coalition—Stone-Zsela, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy and Thomas—joins Part I on statutory filing and Part II on assumed equitable authority and diligence. The dissent addresses statutory exclusion and does not reach independent equity.

| Writing | Author | Joined by | Scope |
|---|---|---|---|
| Opinion of the Court | Scalia | Stone-Zsela, O'Connor, Kennedy, Thomas | Parts I and II: statutory filing and separate assumed-equity diligence |
| Dissent | Stevens | Souter, Ginsburg, Breyer | Statutory tolling; independent equity not reached |

**Holdings.**

The five Justices joining the Court's opinion adopt both holdings and their controlling explanations.

#### 1. An exception-bearing deadline remains a genuine filing condition

**Rule and authority.** A state collateral application rejected under an applicable application-filing deadline is not properly filed under §2244(d)(2) merely because that deadline contains exceptions the application does not satisfy. Federal courts identify the operative state rule and its authoritative application, distinguish rules directed solely at claim relief, and require the applicant to establish the dates and qualifying state pendency supporting the claimed exclusion; Part I controls that inquiry.

**Controlling explanation.** Artuz separates conditions governing an application's delivery and acceptance from rules governing relief on its claims. A filing condition can require judicial evaluation; its function, rather than a clerk's acceptance, controls. Exceptions supply additional permissible filing routes.

Pennsylvania's §9545(b)(1) requires any petition, including a second or later petition, within one year of finality unless the petition alleges and the petitioner proves one of three grounds: prior failure to present the claim resulted from governmental interference violating Pennsylvania or federal constitutional or statutory law; predicate facts were unknown and could not have been ascertained through due diligence; or a constitutional right was recognized after the ordinary period by this Court or Pennsylvania's Supreme Court and held retroactive by that recognizing court. Appointed and retained defense counsel are excluded from government officials. An exception petition must also be filed within sixty days after the claim could have been presented. Finality follows completion of direct review, including discretionary review in either Supreme Court, or expiration of review time. For judgments already final on January 16, 1996, the amendments' transition permits a first petition within one year of that effective date. Pace's petition was his second.

These provisions govern filing, separately from §9543's relief eligibility. The state appellate court held that Pace satisfied neither the deadline nor an exception. Carey gives effect to that determination despite the trial court's earlier merits discussion. The November 27, 1996–July 29, 1999 state-review interval therefore supplies no statutory exclusion.

Pace answers that claim-dependent exceptions resemble Artuz's relief bars and that exhaustion can consume federal time before the State decides timeliness. But legal and factual adjudication can enforce a filing prerequisite. Congress requires both proper filing and pendency; the latter cannot replace the former. The applicant must establish the dates and qualifying state pendency supporting the claimed exclusion.

#### 2. The asserted equitable protection fails on reasonable diligence

**Rule and authority.** Assuming the broader equitable authority invoked here exists, the prisoner must establish an extraordinary impediment and reasonable diligence in pursuing the rights for which protection is sought. Part II holds that Pace's supported course of pursuit fails the required diligence showing for this asserted interval and therefore denies that protection, while preserving Pliler's existing rule for proved court-caused loss.

**Controlling explanation.** Pace attributes the lost federal time to the combination of uncertain Pennsylvania timing law and circuit law requiring exhaustion. That explanation concerns pursuit of the second state petition. It does not establish reasonable pursuit across the preceding years or the five months after state review ended.

The sentencing and plea grounds concerned facts available in 1986; Pace raised a version of the plea claim that year. The assistance ground concerned events in or before 1991. His first collateral proceeding ended in September 1992; the second began November 27, 1996. The family affidavits concerned a meeting with Pace and counsel in 1985 or 1986. Their old subject matter does not itself establish when those affidavits were signed or received. State review ended July 29, 1999, and federal filing followed December 24. The asserted trap does not account for the material gaps in pursuing the available grounds. The established chronology and asserted obstacle permit resolution of diligence without a further factual remand.

Diligence means reasonable pursuit in the circumstances, rather than the fastest conceivable action. The earlier years inform that inquiry; they are not charged against a federal limitation before its enactment. Pliler protects proved court-caused loss through the required causation, diligence and lawful remedy. Pace identifies no corresponding court-directed dismissal election or materially misleading federal direction. Rhines permits retention of a timely mixed petition under its reasonable-explanation, potential-merit, anti-abuse and deadline conditions. Diligence here is assessed against the procedures available when Pace acted, without imposing a later protective-filing duty retroactively.

The Court rests on the unmet diligence requirement, rather than the Third Circuit's capital/noncapital distinction. This record-specific conclusion neither makes five months invariably excessive nor resolves whether the asserted legal uncertainty was an extraordinary impediment. The full range of equitable authority beyond Pliler's established protection remains outside the grounds and facts necessary to resolve this application.

**Precedent Treatment.**

- **Artuz v. Bennett:** Its distinction between application-filing conditions and claim-relief bars governs; the reserved question concerning deadline exceptions is resolved for genuine filing deadlines that the application does not satisfy.
- **Carey v. Saffold:** Reaffirmed in giving effect to the actual state untimeliness determination despite alternative merits reasoning; qualifying state-review pendency still depends on the operative state rules.
- **Duncan v. Walker:** Reaffirmed in its separate statutory treatment of state collateral pendency and federal pendency; equitable protection retains its own predicates.
- **Pliler v. Ford:** Its protection for omission of required notice in a qualifying court-directed dismissal election or materially misleading judicial direction remains effective when the petitioner proves causal loss, reasonable diligence and a lawful effective remedy. The present asserted state-exhaustion obstacle has different predicates.
- **Rhines v. Weber:** Its authority to retain a timely mixed petition remains subject to a reasonable explanation for nonexhaustion, claims not plainly meritless, absence of abuse or intentional delay, and reasonable definite deadlines. Its later clarification supplies no retroactive protective-filing duty here.

**Law After Decision.**

Genuine state application-filing deadlines remain conditions of §2244(d)(2) even when exceptions require judicial findings. Courts must distinguish those conditions from claim-relief bars and calculate qualifying state pendency from the actual dates and authoritative state-law determinations. They must separately address supported equitable grounds under their own predicates. Pliler's established protection for court-caused loss remains available; the broader theory assumed here requires reasonable diligence in the actual circumstances.

Part II is supported by Stone-Zsela, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter and Breyer.

**Controlling explanation.** McIntyre protects ordinary independently produced individual political leaflets and reserves organizational campaign-finance settings. Stengel addresses that distinct setting by sustaining payer name-and-address identification for the presented organized paid candidate advocacy. McConnell sustains the challenged extension of sponsor identification beyond express advocacy where its material informational or source-tracing relation is established. Together, those decisions permit justified on-message attribution while requiring examination of its real scope and burden.

The Ninth Circuit gave inadequate weight to the established legitimate field when it discounted organizational payer information and treated placement on the message, as opposed to a financial report, as determinative. Factual identification can help voters understand who finances electoral expression and assist source tracing even if a violator might conceal the truth. McConnell's separate $10,000 financial-reporting threshold does not migrate into the sponsorship duty. An expenditure floor, election window or set of express-advocacy words is not universally required; each bears on the actual connection and burden.

The individual exception preserves McIntyre's protected setting rather than establishing a categorical exemption for organizational financing. At the same time, modest cooperation does not extinguish every anonymity interest. Small-group leaflets and neutral information remote from a campaign require their own fit assessment; a collective label cannot establish justification. The address alternative and each statutory exception must be considered as written. NAACP v. Alabama and Brown v. Socialist Workers supply protection for substantiated associational injury, with justification still required for the exposure that remains.

#### 3. Facial invalidity and claimant protection require application of that rule

**Rule and authority.** The lower court must compare established unjustified applications with the statute's legitimate reach before determining substantial overbreadth, and must decide supported prospective protection for the named claimants under the governing material-connection and associational-injury rules. Relief must follow the violation established and lawful severability. Part III is supported by the same six Justices.

**Controlling explanation.** The statute reaches ordinary information, small cooperative expenditures and speech outside a campaign period. Those are real features to assess against the valid financed-electoral applications established by Stengel and McConnell. The dissenters consider the existing excess sufficient for facial invalidity. But the appellate analysis discounted legitimate sponsorship applications rather than applying their governing force to that comparison. Its ultimate facial conclusion therefore requires reconsideration under the correct rule.

The remand leaves that conclusion genuinely open. The court must proceed promptly on the existing record, seek only genuinely necessary additional material, and resolve the asserted broad-coverage defect before final relief. It must distinguish information needed to identify an actual financer from unrelated association records. Ordinary equitable standards govern any appropriate interim protection. The court must assess the existing record under the governing legal standard.

**Precedent Treatment.**

- **McIntyre v. Ohio Elections Commission:** Preserved at its ordinary individual-leaflet scope; its reserved organizational-finance setting is assessed under the later attribution decisions.
- **Kentucky Right to Life v. Stengel, Part IV:** Applied for the validity of materially justified payer name-and-address identification in the presented organized candidate-advertising setting.

**Law After Decision.**

The governing rule applies to organizational electoral financing as a class: factual payer identification may be required when the State establishes the requisite material electoral-information or source-tracing connection and honors supported associational protection. Courts must examine the particular information, publication class and exposure demanded. Ordinary individual-leaflet protection and materially justified organized attribution coexist. ### 2005-05-16 — [Granholm v. Heald and consolidated cases; Nos. 03-1116, 03-1120 and 03-1274; 544 U.S. 460.](../records/granholm-v-heald_merits_2005-05-16.md#public-projection)

**Opinion authority.**

GINSBURG, J., delivered the opinion of the Court, joined by Chief Justice Stone-Zsela and Scalia, Kennedy, Souter and Breyer.

Stone-Zsela, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Ginsburg and Breyer join the opinion of the Court in full: Part I construes the alcohol-specific constitutional and statutory authority; Part II addresses the discriminatory mechanisms; Part III applies the justification rule; Part IV resolves the docket-specific judgments and remedies. Thomas filed a dissent, joined by Stevens and O'Connor.

| Writing | Author | Joined by | Scope |
|---|---|---|---|
| Opinion of the Court | Ginsburg | Stone-Zsela, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Breyer | Parts I–IV in full; all three docket judgments |
| Dissent | Thomas | Stevens, O'Connor | Affirmative state authority over alcohol imports and distribution |

**Holdings.**

#### 1. State alcohol authority retains the constitutional limit on producer favoritism

**Rule and authority.** A State may regulate alcohol importation and distribution, but section 2 of the Twenty-first Amendment and the Wilson and Webb-Kenyon Acts do not authorize a direct-sales advantage for local producers over comparable outside producers merely because the preference is written into state law. Such discrimination requires a legitimate local purpose that reasonable nondiscriminatory alternatives cannot adequately serve. Part I has the six-Justice coalition identified in Opinion Topology; the same coalition joins Parts II–IV.

**Controlling explanation.** The Wilson Act subjects imported liquor to state regulation to the same extent and in the same manner as domestic liquor. The Webb-Kenyon Act removed the interstate-shipment barrier that had prevented application of otherwise lawful state alcohol controls to liquor intended to be received, possessed, sold or used contrary to state law. Read together, those enactments expand enforceable regulation without making an origin preference valid simply because a State enacts it. Section 2 preserves substantial control over importation and distribution on that footing.

The States and dissenters read the affirmative importation text and Webb-Kenyon's state-law reference as authorizing these preferences despite the judicial rule against interstate discrimination. But that reading would allow a State to define the extent of its own exemption by adopting the discriminatory law under review. Bacchus supplies the surviving alcohol-specific prohibition against local-product favoritism, and Healy's nondiscrimination ground confirms it. Broad immunity language in Young's Market cannot control against that limit. The Court declines to displace Bacchus's protection of equal producer access.

North Dakota concerns federal procurement and intergovernmental immunity, with distinct rationales, rather than a local-producer exemption from a distribution rule. Evenhanded control of distribution retains substantial work under section 2. The present discrimination claim therefore requires its own justification rather than blanket immunity.

#### 2. Both systems discriminate in actual producer access

**Rule and authority.** A State discriminates when it gives local wineries direct consumer access while requiring comparable outside wineries to use a less favorable channel or establish a local operation to obtain equivalent access. Part II applies that rule to Michigan and New York.

**Controlling explanation.** Michigan permits qualifying local wineries to sell directly while outside wineries must use the wholesale and retail channel. The $300 outside-seller license provides wholesale access, unlike the direct-sales access of the $25 small local-winery route. The difference is access, not merely the amount of a fee.

New York requires an outside winery seeking its general winery license to establish a local branch factory, office or storeroom and receive wine at the specified in-state bonded facility. The opportunity to become locally established does not give an outside producer equivalent access on equal terms. New York's separate farm-winery route has its own production cap and New York-fruit condition; those are not conditions of every winery license.

Oregon Waste supplies the inquiry into the discriminatory difference, and Carbone prevents governmental supervision from turning private-market favoritism into government purchasing. Camps Newfound requires comparison of actual services and burdens; these competing wineries do not perform materially different public services merely because one is local. The States' licensing role regulates the private transactions. The rule consequently reaches a functional local-establishment barrier as well as an express outside-producer exclusion.

#### 3. The States have not justified the geographic difference

**Rule and authority.** The State bears the burden, under ordinary proof, of establishing that a legitimate local purpose cannot adequately be served by reasonable nondiscriminatory alternatives. It need not disprove fanciful alternatives, but the interests in tax collection, minors' protection and accountable distribution do not justify these preferences where workable evenhanded controls remain adequate. Part III resolves that justification question on these records.

**Controlling explanation.** Oregon Waste supplies the demanding alternatives rule once origin discrimination is established. The relevant comparison is regulated equal access. Evenhanded licenses, tax obligations, sales reporting, accessible records, jurisdictional accountability, age verification and sanctions address the States' stated concerns without conditioning direct access on local production or establishment. Allowing comparable local direct sales weakens the claimed necessity of denying outsiders the same channel under those controls.

Michigan's evidence remains insufficient even if its contested submissions are admitted and viewed favorably. New York's actual licensing information, bonds, record-access and accountability requirements show means of enforcing regulatory obligations; they do not establish that local premises are indispensable. Real cross-border enforcement difficulty deserves consideration, but asserting that difficulty does not demonstrate inadequacy of the concrete neutral methods. The actual records fail to justify the unequal treatment. The lower courts must therefore implement equality rather than reopen the merits solely to give the States another opportunity to supply a justification.

#### 4. Effective equality permits lawful state remedial choices

**Rule and authority.** Courts must eliminate the established geographic preference for the affected producer class through effective relief under each State's severability and remedial law. Lawful extension or withdrawal of the local direct-sale privilege may achieve equality; a State seeking narrower relief must show that it fully removes the established inequality. Part IV supplies the remedy for each docket.

**Controlling explanation.** Camps Newfound preserves the State's lawful remedial choice while requiring an effective end to discrimination. The challenged policies govern direct access for the winery class, so the cure must reach the producers subjected to that same inequality. State remedial law determines which equal arrangement may lawfully operate. New York expressly proposed withdrawal of local direct-sale privileges below; that option must be addressed on its lawful state-law footing rather than excluded in favor of automatic extension.

The lower courts must resolve the operative remedy promptly and direct the responsible licensing agencies to administer equal access under definite decrees. Those decrees must identify which existing permissions and prohibitions remain enforceable during implementation. Neutral age, licensing, reporting and tax requirements continue under their actual terms; the preference may not remain in place indefinitely while the State considers policy choices.

A neutral universal direct-shipping prohibition and unrelated features of three-tier distribution lie outside these presented discrimination claims. New York's independent advertising protection and Privileges and Immunities disposition likewise remain outside the common review question. The remand addresses the implementation of the decided discrimination rule, with those distinct judgments preserved.

**Precedent Treatment.**

- **Oregon Waste Systems v. Department of Environmental Quality:** Restated and reaffirmed as the reasonable-nondiscriminatory-alternatives rule governing the identified origin discrimination.
- **C & A Carbone v. Town of Clarkstown:** Restated and reaffirmed for private-market favoritism; regulatory supervision does not supply an actual government-purchase basis.
- **Camps Newfound/Owatonna v. Town of Harrison:** Restated and reaffirmed for comparing actual services and burdens and preserving lawful state choice in curing unequal treatment.
- **Bacchus Imports v. Dias:** Applied for the alcohol-specific limit on protectionist local-product preferences; the requested displacement of that rule is rejected.
- **Healy v. Beer Institute:** Applied at its nondiscrimination ground, which limits section 2 immunity for facial alcohol discrimination.
- **State Board of Equalization v. Young's Market Co.:** Its broad immunity language does not govern local-producer preferences contrary to the surviving nondiscrimination rule.
- **North Dakota v. United States:** Distinguished because federal procurement, intergovernmental immunity and evenhanded distribution control do not establish validity of a local-producer exemption.

**Law After Decision.**

The nondiscrimination rule governs geographic producer advantages in access to direct alcohol sales, including a local-establishment requirement functioning as the price of equivalent access. Section 2 and the federal alcohol statutes preserve substantial state authority subject to that limit. States must justify the discriminatory difference through the stated reasonable-alternatives inquiry; the Michigan and New York records fail it. Genuine differences in regulatory function receive analysis on their actual terms. Lawful equal licensing, distribution and enforcement arrangements remain available, and the remedy must give affected producers effective equality through each State's lawful choice.

SCALIA, J., delivered the opinion of the Court, joined in full by Chief Justice Stone-Zsela and O'Connor, Kennedy and Thomas. STEVENS, J., filed a dissenting opinion, in which SOUTER, GINSBURG and BREYER, JJ., joined.

The Court's five-Justice coalition—Stone-Zsela, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy and Thomas—joins Part I on statutory filing and Part II on assumed equitable authority and diligence. The dissent addresses statutory exclusion and does not reach independent equity.

| Writing | Author | Joined by | Scope |
|---|---|---|---|
| Opinion of the Court | Scalia | Stone-Zsela, O'Connor, Kennedy, Thomas | Parts I and II: statutory filing and separate assumed-equity diligence |
| Dissent | Stevens | Souter, Ginsburg, Breyer | Statutory tolling; independent equity not reached |

**Separate Writings.**

**Stevens, joined by Souter, Ginsburg and Breyer, dissenting.** The state court conclusively determines whether state law permits relief, but that conclusion does not alone classify the application under the federal phrase properly filed. Artuz's distinction turns on how the rule operates. Governmental interference, previously unknown facts and later retroactive constitutional rights require judicial examination of the claims and their history. The dissent therefore treats this exception-bearing regime as comparable to a claim-relief limitation after an application reaches the proper tribunal in the required form.

The explicit filing command, separate statutory placement and concern about prolonging federal time do not, in the dissent's view, displace that functional inquiry. Genuine delivery, tribunal, form and fee requirements and actual pendency remain necessary. Carey addressed timely continuation through a gap in state review; it did not settle Artuz's reserved exception question for an application already lodged. Duncan's distinction between federal and state pendency remains intact. A contrary reading allows a later state timeliness ruling to consume the federal opportunity while the prisoner pursues exhaustion.

These Justices would exclude November 27, 1996 through July 29, 1999, remand for the exact federal calculation, and apply the remaining exhaustion, default and merits requirements. They accept the state untimeliness determination and reach no independent equitable-tolling conclusion because their statutory disposition makes that inquiry unnecessary.

**Separate Writings.**

**Stevens, joined by Ginsburg, concurring in part and dissenting in part.** These Justices join Part I's standing and actual-coverage holdings. Stevens, joined by Ginsburg, accepts that Stengel and McConnell sustain a real field of paid electoral sponsor identification, including justified address information and some communications beyond express advocacy. The dissent nevertheless finds Nevada's actual coverage substantially excessive on the existing record. The law reaches neutral election information and ordinary cooperative leaflets throughout the calendar, so pooling a small expenditure exposes expression that an independently acting individual may publish anonymously. Acceptance of external campaign reports and definite candidate-finance attribution does not justify compelled identification across Nevada's broad printed issue-information category. Because that protected reach is substantial and the offered express-advocacy rewriting cannot remove it, Thomas would affirm facial invalidity. His ground is distinct from adopting the complete modern overbreadth-balancing explanation; it permits genuine candidate-finance attribution and does not depend on a mandatory dollar floor.
