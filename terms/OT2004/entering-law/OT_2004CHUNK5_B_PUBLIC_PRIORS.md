### Johnson v. De Grandy; De Grandy v. Johnson; United States v. Florida, Nos. 92-519, 92-593 and 92-767, respectively

Merits decision, June 30, 1994

#### Chronology and Posture

Argued October 4, 1993; decided June 30, 1994. On direct appeals under 28 U.S.C. §1253 from the three-judge District Court for the Northern District of Florida, De Grandy v. Wetherell, 815 F. Supp. 1550. The trial ended and rulings were announced July 1, 1992; House and Senate judgments were entered July 2, the House judgment was supplemented July 6, and the explanatory opinion issued July 17.

The questions are whether Florida's preliminary validation precludes the private Section 2 claims or bars the United States' independent enforcement action, and whether the tried Dade-area House and Senate arrangements deny equal electoral opportunity under Section 2. State officials challenge House liability and the substituted House plan and defend the Senate result; the private Hispanic challengers and the United States seek additional Senate relief. The Black claim and proposed alternative bear on the lower court's asserted remedial conflict, without a separate Black appeal being decided here.

Florida adopted SJR 2-G on April 10, 1992, creating 120 single-member House districts and forty single-member Senate districts. The May 13 state validation, 597 So.2d 276, occurred within a thirty-day timetable and preserved later factual Section 2 challenges. Federal complaints were amended to challenge the new plan. The United States was not a party to the validation. The June 25 Hillsborough adjustment, 601 So.2d 543, responded to a Section 5 objection and did not adjudicate the Dade claims. Dade was not among Florida's five covered counties.

The District Court found continuing effects of official discrimination, substantial polarized voting, and cohesion within Hispanic and Black groups but no general cohesion between them. The House comparison is nine effective Hispanic districts among eighteen primarily-Dade districts against 50 percent Hispanic voting-age population, or nine of twenty districts touching Dade against about 47 percent. These are distinct descriptive frames. Experts identified neighborhood splitting in District 116, concentration in District 114 and fragmentation involving Districts 102 and 109. Those descriptions are not a finding that comparable minority and other neighborhoods were subjected to inconsistent districting standards.

The initial De Grandy House proposal listed eleven Hispanic districts with voting-age percentages of at least 64.5 percent. The imposed Modified De Grandy Plan 268 instead included District 109 at 63.74 percent and District 110 at 62.56 percent. The State's separate ten-district and eleven-district remedial proposals were different plans; the latter included two approximately 61-percent Miami Beach districts whose effectiveness was disputed because of recent noncitizen arrivals. The Court assumes the disputed first Gingles condition favorably and decides no citizenship method, universal percentage minimum or precise remedial-plan effectiveness.

The seven-district Senate area includes substantial Dade voters: Hispanic and Black voting-age shares are 44.8 and 15.8 percent, with three effective Hispanic-majority districts and one Black-majority district, approximately 42.9 and 14.3 percent of seven. Another district supplies Black electoral opportunity with crossover support. In the distinct five wholly-Dade districts, Hispanic and Black voting-age shares are 53.9 and 13.5 percent, with three Hispanic supermajorities and one Black majority. The broader South Florida account includes a Broward/Palm Beach Black-majority district outside the Dade seven. The July 2 Senate judgment stated no violation; the July 17 explanation described violations but conflicting proposed remedies. The Court reviews the operative judgment on the grounds below.

Congressional proceedings, the Escambia settlement, dismissed claims outside the tried area, dismissed constitutional claims and preclearance decisions remain undisturbed. No constitutional challenge to these district lines is decided.

All nine Justices participate at argument and decision: Chief Justice Alex-Lamar Stone-Zsela and Justices Blackmun, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas and Ginsburg. No recusal or nonparticipation is established.

#### Judgment

The Court reverses the House Section 2 liability and dependent remedial judgment and affirms the Senate judgment, 7–2 on each component. The State's procedural objections are rejected, and no additional districting relief is ordered.

#### Controlling Propositions and Authority

##### 1. The state validation does not preclude the reserved factual claims

**Controlling proposition:** Under 28 U.S.C. §1738, Florida's validation receives its actual state-law preclusive scope, including its express preservation of later factual Section 2 challenges; it therefore does not bar these private claims. The availability of a return to state court does not require exhaustion of that optional procedure before this preserved federal litigation.

**Authority:** Souter's Opinion of the Court, Part I-A, joined by Stone-Zsela, Blackmun, Stevens, O'Connor, Kennedy and Ginsburg; seven Justices directly adopt this limited rule. Thomas and Scalia do not join the opinion, and their different statutory disposition supplies no additional published joinder.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that respecting a state judgment requires respecting its limits as well as its conclusions. Marrese requires the federal court to begin with the preclusive effect supplied by state law. Allen preserves ordinary preclusion principles without making every federal-rights claim immune from them. Florida's constitutional validation took place on a thirty-day timetable and expressly preserved subsequent factual challenges under Section 2. The private plaintiffs now present the kind of challenge that remained available; their amended complaints concern the new plan. The State's interest in finality cannot convert that limited validation into a completed adjudication of the reserved claims. Growe requires legal effect for an operative state plan, but does not insulate it from a properly pleaded federal defect. Nor does permission to seek further state review make that route mandatory. The Court creates no general Voting Rights Act exception to Section 1738 and decides no preclusion question beyond the judgment's established reservation.

**Precedent treatment:**

- Marrese v. American Academy of Orthopaedic Surgeons, 470 U.S. 373: applies the state-law-first rule for the preclusive effect of a state judgment; no additional federal preclusion is imposed.
- Allen v. McCurry, 449 U.S. 90: preserves ordinary preclusion and its qualifications; creates no categorical federal-forum exemption for Voting Rights Act claims.
- Growe v. Emison, February 23, 1993: follows its requirement to respect the legally operative state plan and distinguishes its obsolete-plan defect; no enlarged party-preclusion rule follows.

##### 2. The United States brings an original enforcement suit

**Controlling proposition:** Rooker and Feldman do not bar the United States' independent Section 2 enforcement suit against this plan: the United States was not a party to the state validation and seeks no lower-federal appellate review of a state judgment entered against it. The holding does not decide every nonparty, privity, intervention or intertwined-claim question or permit review of a preclearance determination.

**Authority:** Souter's Opinion of the Court, Part I-B, joined by Stone-Zsela, Blackmun, Stevens, O'Connor, Kennedy and Ginsburg; seven Justices directly adopt the original-suit distinction on these facts. Thomas and Scalia publish no join in this part.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that this enforcement action falls within original federal jurisdiction. Rooker denies federal district courts appellate authority over a state judicial judgment. Feldman preserves that limit while distinguishing an independent challenge from review of a state court's determination. The United States did not participate in Florida's validation proceeding and has no adverse state judgment of its own rights to overturn. It brings its statutory claim against the enacted electoral arrangement. The fact that success could unsettle a court-approved map does not itself transform that original suit into an appeal. The Government's other federal litigation and preclearance responsibilities did not make it a party to the state proceeding. The Court need not classify every aspect of state plan validation as legislative or judicial, nor reconsider ordinary preclusion. Shaw's separate restriction on review of federal preclearance remains intact; no such review is sought here. Jurisdiction permits adjudication of the claim and does not establish its merits.

**Precedent treatment:**

- Rooker v. Fidelity Trust Co., 263 U.S. 413: applies the limit on lower-federal appellate review of state judgments; this independent enforcement action does not invoke that authority.
- District of Columbia Court of Appeals v. Feldman, 460 U.S. 462: preserves the appellate/original distinction without deciding every possible relationship between nonparties and a state judgment.
- Shaw v. Reno, June 28, 1993: preserves the designated channel and nonreviewability limits for federal preclearance; those limits do not immunize a state plan from an independent statutory claim.

##### 3. Necessary voting conditions do not require maximization

**Controlling proposition:** This Section 2 fragmentation claim requires a minority sufficiently large and geographically compact to form a district majority, political cohesion, and majority bloc voting ordinarily sufficient to defeat its preferred candidate, absent special circumstances such as an unopposed minority candidate; the claimant must also establish unequal opportunity to participate and elect chosen representatives under the totality of circumstances, without proving discriminatory purpose. Even when those necessary conditions are satisfied, Section 2 does not require every feasible additional majority-minority district; the disputed first condition is assumed here, leaving citizenship, population-denominator and coalition or influence-claim questions unresolved.

**Authority:** Souter's Opinion of the Court, Part II, joined by Stone-Zsela, Blackmun, Stevens, O'Connor, Kennedy and Ginsburg; seven Justices directly adopt the complete necessary-versus-sufficient distinction and rejection of maximization. Thomas and Scalia's statutory noncoverage position is a different rationale and supplies no join.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that a possible additional district identifies potential electoral opportunity, not an automatic statutory entitlement. Gingles supplies the conditions connecting the challenged arrangement to the asserted dilution. Growe applies those conditions to single-member fragmentation and requires local voting evidence rather than addition of distinct groups' populations. Voinovich keeps the burden on challengers to establish unequal opportunity and distinguishes state districting choices from judicial relief for a proved violation. Holder likewise separates a workable comparison from proof of injury and from remedy. Those decisions retain the statutory totality inquiry. Here the Court assumes the contested first condition in the challengers' favor and accepts the findings of cohesion within each minority group and polarized voting. Neither those premises nor the feasibility of additional districts dispenses with the remaining inquiry. Congress preserved a results claim while disclaiming proportional officeholding. The Court therefore rejects both an automatic maximization requirement and a new discriminatory-purpose requirement, without selecting a citizenship measure or deciding an influence or coalition entitlement.

**Precedent treatment:**

- Thornburg v. Gingles, 478 U.S. 30: follows its causal conditions, special-circumstances qualification and independent totality inquiry; the conditions do not establish liability by themselves.
- Growe v. Emison, February 23, 1993: preserves local cohesion and majority-bloc proof for single-member fragmentation and the unresolved coalition, influence and population questions.
- Voinovich v. Quilter, March 2, 1993: follows the challengers' burden and the distinction between a State's authority to choose districts and a court's power to remedy a proved violation.
- Holder v. Hall, June 30, 1994: applies its separation of coverage, comparison, proof and remedy; a permissible comparison creates no maximization command.
- Shaw v. Reno, June 28, 1993: preserves the independent statutory elements; its constitutional injury rule adds no further element to Section 2.

##### 4. Proportional opportunity is relevant evidence, not a safe harbor

**Controlling proposition:** The relationship between a group's share of the relevant population and the share of districts in which it has an effective opportunity to elect its preferred candidates is relevant evidence within Section 2's totality inquiry, whose weight depends on the other evidence; it is neither a conclusive safe harbor nor a burden-shifting affirmative defense, and disproportionality alone likewise does not establish liability. The inquiry concerns demonstrated electoral opportunity, not nominal district labels or the race of officeholders; it guarantees no proportional election, racial floor or ceiling, statewide offset, or universal geographic or population denominator, and cannot excuse proved local packing, fragmentation or another barrier merely because opportunities exist elsewhere.

**Authority:** Souter's Opinion of the Court, Part III, joined by Stone-Zsela, Blackmun, Stevens, O'Connor and Ginsburg; six Justices directly adopt this contextual rule and its explanation. Kennedy separately accepts the exact limited propositions that proportionality is relevant, that the necessary conditions alone do not establish liability, and that proportionality supplies no safe harbor; his concurrence does not join Part III's full rationale or Parts IV and V's affirmative applications. No combined rationale under Marks is needed.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that actual opportunity matters to the statutory question. Gingles requires an appraisal of the local electoral system under the totality, and Voinovich rejects liability inferred solely from a preferred district arrangement. A relationship between effective districts and population may bear substantially on whether an asserted lack of opportunity exists. But district opportunity, election outcomes and officeholding by racial identity are different facts. The statutory permission to consider protected-class electoral success does not guarantee proportional election or make any numerical relationship decisive. History, polarization and particular barriers retain their force when the aggregate appears favorable. Thus a State cannot defend proved local packing or fragmentation simply by pointing to compensating opportunities elsewhere. Conversely, an arithmetical shortfall does not itself prove the statutory wrong. The State's conclusive-defense proposal fails, as does treating statewide measurement as indispensable to that supposed defense. The Court uses the locally tried record without announcing a general geographic rule or resolving unnecessary preservation questions under Rule 8(c).

**Precedent treatment:**

- Gingles, 478 U.S. 30: follows its contextual inquiry and treatment of electoral success; adopts no conclusive proportional-success presumption.
- Voinovich, March 2, 1993: preserves the need for proof of actual unequal opportunity rather than automatic condemnation or approval of a district form.
- Holder, June 30, 1994: preserves independent totality proof after an adequate comparison; its local institutional comparison supplies neither a quota nor an evidentiary safe harbor.

##### 5. The House liability and dependent remedy cannot stand on this record

**Controlling proposition:** Assuming the disputed first Gingles condition in the challengers' favor and retaining the supported findings of discrimination and polarized voting, the tried House record does not establish unequal opportunity under the totality beyond the erroneous inference that every feasible additional Hispanic district must be created. The liability judgment and dependent substitution of Modified De Grandy Plan 268 are reversed; no new liability trial, Supreme Court replacement map or numerical district minimum is ordered, and implementation must follow lawful election administration.

**Authority:** Souter's Opinion of the Court, Part IV, joined by Stone-Zsela, Blackmun, Stevens, O'Connor and Ginsburg; six Justices adopt this full contextual application. Kennedy supports reversal and the mandate under Parts II and VI on the narrower rejection of maximization, without joining Part IV; the House disposition therefore has seven votes, against Thomas and Scalia's vacatur-and-dismissal alternative.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that the House judgment used the wrong measure of injury. The findings of continuing discriminatory effects and polarization remain serious and accepted. Feasibility is assumed favorably. Nine effective Hispanic districts accompany about half the voting-age population in the eighteen primarily-Dade districts; the distinct twenty-district frame yields nine opportunities against about 47 percent Hispanic voting-age population. Those relationships are evidence, not immunity. The Court also considers testimony about split neighborhoods in District 116, concentration in District 114, and fragmentation involving Districts 102 and 109. No finding establishes inconsistent standards for comparable minority and other neighborhoods; such a finding is not a new prerequisite to liability. The credited testimony, history and voting evidence do not establish the asserted unequal opportunity merely because two more districts are feasible. The liability announcement and insistence on eleven districts instead made that possibility obligatory. Gingles requires respect for local findings under Rule 52(a), but does not insulate the legal error defining the ultimate injury. Voinovich and Holder require a proved wrong before federal relief. The dependent remedy consequently falls without adjudicating its precise boundaries or reopening liability.

**Precedent treatment:**

- Gingles, 478 U.S. 30: follows the totality standard and Rule 52(a)'s protection of supported findings; corrects a legally infected liability determination without creating a ratio presumption or new intent requirement.
- Voinovich, March 2, 1993: applies the need for an established federal violation before judicial imposition of remedial districts.
- Holder, June 30, 1994: distinguishes a sufficient comparison from the separate proof of unequal opportunity; its established liability and remedy remand do not supply missing liability here.

##### 6. The Senate judgment stands because no violation was proved

**Controlling proposition:** On the tried Senate record, the asserted unequal opportunity is not established under the totality merely because an additional Hispanic district and a different additional Black district are each feasible; the judgment leaving the State's Senate plan operative is affirmed for lack of a proved violation, not because a proved violation may remain when proposed remedies conflict. The existing Black electoral opportunity with crossover support is relevant evidence, not a second Black-majority district, proof of general cohesion between Hispanic and Black voters, or a legal entitlement to a crossover district.

**Authority:** Souter's Opinion of the Court, Part V, joined by Stone-Zsela, Blackmun, Stevens, O'Connor and Ginsburg; six Justices adopt this full contextual application. Kennedy supports affirmance under Parts II and VI on the narrower no-maximization ground without joining Part V; the Senate disposition has seven votes, against Thomas and Scalia's vacatur-and-dismissal alternative.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that the Senate result rests on absence of statutory injury. The July 2 judgment found no violation, while the July 17 explanation described violations but competing remedies. The latter reasoning cannot excuse a proved wrong. Gingles requires examination of actual opportunity, and Holder requires lawful relief once injury is established. Here the seven-district area has three effective Hispanic-majority districts and one Black-majority district against respective voting-age shares of 44.8 and 15.8 percent. A further district supplies demonstrated Black electoral opportunity with crossover support. The Court considers those opportunities with the accepted discrimination and polarization findings; no ratio alone controls. The five wholly-Dade districts and broader South Florida account remain distinct. Separate cohesion within the two groups does not establish a joint coalition. The possibility of maximizing each group's districts separately does not first prove two injuries and then create a conflict of federal rights. Voinovich preserves the difference between feasible arrangements and required remedies. No additional Senate district follows, and no separate Black appeal or influence-district entitlement is decided.

**Precedent treatment:**

- Gingles, 478 U.S. 30: applies contextual unequal-opportunity proof without inferring liability from separately feasible district increases.
- Growe, February 23, 1993: preserves the need for proved common cohesion before distinct groups can be treated as one electoral coalition.
- Voinovich, March 2, 1993: preserves the unresolved influence-claim question and ties compelled relief to an established violation.
- Holder, June 30, 1994: preserves the obligation to formulate lawful relief for an established wrong; no competing-remedies exception to liability or relief is recognized.

#### Precedent Treatment and Current-Law Effect

The holding-specific treatments govern. Gingles's causal conditions, independent totality inquiry and protection of supported local findings remain; the Court rejects neither dilution coverage nor the 1982 results standard. Growe and Voinovich retain state responsibility, local proof requirements, the challengers' burden and the distinction between a permissible state district choice and a judicial remedy for a proved wrong. Holder's coverage, reasonable-comparison, proof and remedy holdings remain distinct; none creates maximization.

Allen and Marrese govern the actual preclusive scope of the state validation. Rooker and Feldman retain their lower-federal appellate limit. Shaw's constitutional boundary and its separate federal-preclearance channel remain unchanged. Presley v. Etowah County Commission, January 27, 1992, preserves the Section 5 voting/governing boundary and independent Section 2 claims; it supplies no nonretrogression defense to these Dade claims. No precedent is overruled. The dissent's contrary statutory construction is noncontrolling.

Effective June 30, 1994, the ordinary Section 2 fragmentation predicates remain necessary but do not establish liability without unequal opportunity under the totality. The Court expressly rejects maximization and a conclusive proportionality defense. Relevant proportionality concerns effective electoral opportunity and must be assessed with the complete local evidence; it does not guarantee proportional election, shift the claimant's burden or excuse proved local injury. The full contextual House and Senate applications have six direct adherents; the mandate and the necessary-versus-sufficient rule have seven. No fractured-opinion combination is required.

The disputed first Gingles condition is assumed for decision. Citizenship methods, the population denominator, a universal geographic scale, coalition cognizability, influence claims and adaptation of the majority-size condition remain unresolved. The Court neither decides a general Rule 8(c) preservation rule nor replaces the tried local claim with an untried statewide claim. Section 2's race, color and language-minority coverage and its permissive consideration of protected-class electoral success remain distinct from the disclaimer of proportional officeholding; no question under the language-minority cross-reference is separately decided.

Supported findings of history and polarization remain. Inconsistent treatment of comparable neighborhoods is not made a mandatory element, discriminatory purpose is not required, and no statutory violation may be excused because proposed remedies conflict. Constitutional claims retain their own elements, and preclearance supplies no general immunity. These appeals determine the presented statutory claims, not the legality of every racial district configuration or a future electoral record.

#### Separate Positions Relevant to the Decision

##### O'Connor's concurrence

O'Connor joins the Court throughout and emphasizes the proper evidentiary role of proportionality. The relationship between effective opportunity districts and population is relevant to the ultimate statutory inquiry and must be considered when the record supplies it. Its relevance does not depend on a presumption that a particular racial ratio defines the right. Gingles's local appraisal remains necessary, and other evidence can show unequal opportunity despite aggregate proportionality. Conversely, a shortfall does not itself establish dilution. Her concurrence creates no affirmative-defense burden shift, district quota, statewide denominator or constitutional approval. It explains why neither ignoring the evidence nor making it conclusive states the Court's rule.

##### Kennedy's concurrence in part and in the judgment

Kennedy joins the limited procedural holdings, the necessary-versus-sufficient distinction, the rejection of maximization and the mandate. Voinovich separates a possible districting arrangement from proved injury; Holder's comparison dispute likewise illustrates why a desired alternative does not itself measure the statutory right. The lower court's treatment of feasible additional districts as required supplies his ground for reversing the House judgment and affirming the Senate result.

Kennedy separately accepts that proportionality has limited relevance, that the Gingles conditions do not automatically establish liability and that proportionality cannot provide a safe harbor. He withholds the Court's full affirmative application of proportionality and any suggestion that this statutory disposition constitutionally validates race-conscious districting. His constitutional concern is a reservation consistent with his dissent in Shaw, not the governing rule of that case. Shaw's rejection of the design-only theory remains the Court's law, and no constitutional claim is presented here. His concurrence supplies no racial floor or ceiling, statewide offset, requirement to merge minority groups, or order to create an additional district. The six-Justice full application controls without expanding his joins.

##### Thomas's dissent, joined by Scalia

Thomas and Scalia would vacate the appealed judgments and remand with instructions to dismiss the consolidated Section 2 actions for failure to state a covered claim. They maintain the statutory construction stated in their Holder dissent: the Act protects registration, ballot access, casting and proper counting, rather than the allocation of electoral weight through district boundaries. In their view, subsection (b)'s results inquiry operates within the practices covered by subsection (a); it does not create a districting-dilution cause of action. They would preserve a results test for practices they regard as covered, without requiring discriminatory intent for those practices.

Their construction would reject Gingles's contrary dilution rule and the incompatible portions of Growe and Voinovich, despite their earlier joins. They recognize that current majority law takes the other view. The difficulty of selecting a theory of group representation reinforces their objection, but does not turn their broader construction into a join in the Court's no-maximization or proportionality analysis. Nor do they merely affirm the Senate judgment on a different measure of statutory injury: their proposed vacatur and dismissal is a different formal mandate. They adjudicate no constitutional claim and would not revive dismissed claims, reopen congressional proceedings or disturb the Escambia settlement. Their disagreement changes no controlling precedent.

### Valdespino v. Alamo Heights Independent School District, lower docket 98-50227

Merits decision, June 17, 1999

#### Chronology and Posture

Merits decided June 17, 1999, following a grant of review of the substituted Fifth Circuit judgment, 168 F.3d 848, issued March 11 and revised April 1. The questions concern Section 2's first district-capacity condition, the current demographic findings and exclusion of a late survey.

The seven-member school board uses at-large, by-place, majority-vote elections. Updated evidence placed the proposed district at 47.9% Hispanic citizen voting-age population with a northern addition, or 48.3% with 8.1% underpopulation. Those figures measure population rather than turnout or crossover support. The trial court relied on the April 1997 demographic report, excluded the later defense update and excluded plaintiffs' survey disclosed immediately before trial. No finding establishes dependable crossover, cohesion, ordinary majority-bloc defeat or the full totality under the alternative recognized today.

All nine Justices participated in the merits submission and decision.

#### Opinions and Joins

#### Controlling Propositions and Authority

##### The first condition includes a proved functional ability-to-elect route

For a claim by one protected group, the first Gingles condition may be met either through the retained district-majority route or through rigorous proof that a lawful, geographically compact proposed district would provide the cohesive group dependable crossover support sufficient to elect its candidates of choice. Speculation, an isolated favorable election, near-majority population arithmetic or mere influence does not suffice; the relevant majority-bloc showing, causal connection and independent totality remain necessary.

**Authority:** Stone-Zsela's Part I is joined by Stevens, Souter, Ginsburg and Breyer; five Justices adopt this express modification and its limits.

The first condition links the challenged electoral arrangement to an achievable opportunity to elect. Numerical majority provides a predictable demonstration of that capacity, but an exclusive number can reject the same actual opportunity when dependable voting evidence proves it. Section 2 protects equal participation and opportunity, without guaranteeing proportional outcomes. The Court therefore expressly modifies the numerical exclusivity reflected in Gingles and Growe; it does not declare that earlier reservations already adopted this alternative.

De Grandy rejects numerical maximization and conclusive safe harbors while preserving actual local opportunity. Brooks fits proof to a different nomination barrier. Those decisions support the inquiry's purpose but do not themselves decide this new district-capacity rule. Dependability matters because a contingent alliance or favorable candidate alone cannot establish lost opportunity attributable to the system. Plaintiffs must prove cohesion and actual voting behavior within the proposed district and the relevant majority-bloc defeat in the challenged arrangement; successful crossover in one setting does not silently negate or establish bloc defeat in another. The ordinary special-circumstances qualification, including unopposed candidacy, remains.

The majority route survives. So do lawful district population and geography, constitutional limits and the separate totality inquiry. The Court prescribes no mathematical crossover formula, racial floor, proportional-election right or preferred political tradeoff, and decides no combination of distinct minority groups or influence-only entitlement. States retain lawful choice among effective arrangements; a court-compelled remedy still requires an established violation.

##### The population figures do not resolve functional opportunity

The accepted 47.9% and 48.3% Hispanic citizen voting-age shares neither prove nor foreclose dependable ability to elect. The judgment resting exclusively on their being below fifty percent must be vacated for application of the changed rule, with all remaining statutory conditions and totality to be established before relief.

**Authority:** The same five Justices join Stone-Zsela's Part I and this application and remedy.

Population is not evidence by itself of the electorate's actual crossover behavior. The lower courts did not make the findings necessary under the functional route, and this Court cannot supply them. The proposed district must be lawful in population and geography; an 8.1% underpopulation is not approved merely because it appears in the calculation. If admissible proof does not establish dependable opportunity, the claim fails. If capacity is established, the remaining conditions and unequal opportunity under totality still require determination. Voinovich preserves the difference between state choice and judicially compelled remedy; De Grandy permits neither automatic liability nor a numerical safe harbor. The remand commands no map or voting system and guarantees no favorable result.

##### The independent demographic and disclosure rulings stand

The credited proof of concrete post-census housing, occupancy and growth changes supports the present demographic findings under clear-error review. Excluding the survey disclosed on the eve of trial for surprise under the applicable disclosure rule was within the trial court's discretion; changing the capacity rule does not itself make either independent ruling erroneous.

**Authority:** Stone-Zsela's Part II is joined by Stevens, O'Connor, Kennedy, Souter, Ginsburg and Breyer; seven Justices adopt this rule and explanation. Scalia and Thomas do not join it unconditionally.

The 1990 census's presumptive accuracy does not require treating later population as unchanged. The April 1997 report supplied documented local changes under the burden the lower court applied; the excluded August report does not become the basis for affirmance. The Court establishes neither a universal heightened demographic burden nor automatic approval of local estimates. Commerce's national-enumeration holding supplies no contrary rule about these later local facts.

The plaintiffs disclosed the survey's existence late Friday and its data late Saturday before Monday trial. The earlier deposition reference did not disclose its basis. Local Rule CV-16(e) permitted good-cause supplementation, but the trial court could enforce disclosure and avoid surprise, particularly after excluding the defense update said to occasion the survey. The Court does not decide the survey's accuracy, whether it was proper rebuttal or unestablished factors in a separate exclusion framework. Preservation of the exclusion is not a substantive rejection of the survey's results.

Evidence previously immaterial under an exclusive numerical threshold may bear on the changed inquiry. The trial court may determine whether fair supplementation is needed under ordinary rules, with adequate notice and response; reopening is neither automatic nor forbidden. No new trial, mandatory study or automatic admission of the excluded survey is ordered.

#### Precedent Treatment

- Thornburg v. Gingles: expressly modifies numerical exclusivity in the first condition for the defined single-group functional route; preserves compactness, cohesion, relevant majority-bloc proof, causation and independent totality.
- Growe v. Emison: modifies the same exclusivity while preserving its causal framework and the distinct-group reservation.
- Voinovich v. Quilter: preserves the claimant's burden, relevant bloc-voting requirement and distinction between lawful state districting choice and a court-compelled remedy; the present rule resolves only the defined functional-capacity question.
- Johnson v. De Grandy: applies the rejection of maximization and conclusive ratios; preserves independent totality and actual opportunity without attributing today's change to that earlier decision.
- Brooks v. Barnes: applies its insistence that proof address the actual electoral barrier; its nomination holding did not already decide this district-formation question.
- Department of Commerce v. United States House of Representatives / Clinton v. Glavin: preserves the national enumeration ruling; it neither freezes local demographics at the census date nor validates every projection.

#### Law After Decision

A single protected group below a numerical majority may prove the first condition through dependable actual ability to elect in a lawful compact district. A majority-population route remains available, but neither route establishes every other condition or liability. Cohesion, relevant bloc-voting causation with applicable special circumstances, totality, independent constitutional limits and ordinary evidence procedures retain their force. Mere influence and speculative near-majority calculations do not create a right to districting. The present record establishes no functional success or completed violation.

#### Separate Positions Relevant to the Decision

O'Connor, joined by Kennedy, concurs in the demographic and disclosure rulings and dissents from changing the first condition. A numerical citizen-voting-age majority, in their view, supplies a predictable causal link between the challenged electoral structure and a district opportunity that the protected group could exercise. The functional alternative risks making compelled districting turn on contingent electoral alliances. Growe and Voinovich's reservations do not require that extension, and De Grandy and Brooks concern different analytical questions. They would affirm because both accepted shares are below a majority, without prohibiting lawful voluntary crossover districts, deciding every influence or coalition claim, imposing proportional outcomes or approving the proposed population deviation.

Thomas, joined by Scalia, dissents on a different ground. Maintaining their De Grandy and Brooks position, they would limit §2 to voting access, casting and counting rather than the challenged districting dilution, reconsidering the contrary coverage rule. They would affirm the denial of relief without adopting either numerical or functional capacity. Their agreement with the demographic and exclusion assessments is conditional on applying the dilution framework and adds no unconditional join to the Court's holdings.

#### Record Limits

The earlier Fifth Circuit opinion was withdrawn; the substituted judgment controls this review. Neither the excluded survey's accuracy nor the proposed district's functional electoral capacity is adjudicated here.

#### Mandate, Remedy, and Stage

The statutory claim returns for bounded reconsideration under the expressly changed capacity rule. Supported demographics and the prior survey exclusion remain intact. Any supplementation follows ordinary procedural requirements and the trial court's determination of need, notice and fair response; no new trial, admission, map or election date is prescribed.

### Bruneau v. South Kortright Central School District, No. 98-1541

Merits decision, June 3, 1999

#### Chronology and Posture

Submitted on the merits. On review of the Second Circuit's judgment, 163 F.3d 749. The question is whether Title IX displaces section 1983 enforcement of the statutory sex-discrimination right or an independently secured Equal Protection right.

The student alleged harassment by male sixth-grade classmates during the 1993-94 school year. A jury returned a general defense verdict on the Title IX claim against the District. The Second Circuit affirmed that judgment, upheld dismissal of the District and Board section 1983 claims on statutory-displacement grounds, and separately upheld qualified immunity for teacher Parker and assistant superintendent Race. It did not decide displacement as to those individuals. The present review concerns the displacement question.

All nine Justices participated at merits submission and decision: Chief Justice Stone-Zsela and Justices Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer.

#### Judgment

The existing Title IX trial judgment and the independent individual-immunity affirmance remain undisturbed. The remand restores only the opportunity to litigate claims improperly barred by the displacement rule. It decides no defendant's liability, establishes no discriminatory purpose, awards no damages and orders no new Title IX trial. One signed opinion carries both controlling portions. The constitutional and statutory holdings rest on their stated direct majorities.

#### Controlling Propositions and Authority

##### 1. Independent constitutional claims remain available

**Operative rule:** Title IX does not displace a section 1983 claim enforcing an independently secured Equal Protection right merely because the same school harassment also supports a Title IX theory. The claimant must establish the constitutional violation, state action, defendant-specific responsibility or proper municipal attribution, causation and an available remedy; correcting displacement supplies none of those elements.

**Authority:** Thomas's opinion on this proposition and the common remedial limits is joined by all nine Justices.

**Controlling explanation:** The Fourteenth Amendment imposes duties independent of a funding recipient's statutory obligations. Blessing requires attention to the particular right and actual congressional foreclosure, not an assumption that overlapping facts create identical rights. The funding-termination process and the implied Title IX action do not provide an exclusive personal remedial system incompatible with the independent constitutional claim. The differing duties and defendants make categorical extinction especially unwarranted.

Sea Clammers and Smith v. Robinson concerned actual incompatibility with the statutory enforcement designs before the Court; neither holds that every private federal action extinguishes constitutional remedies arising from the same events. Kinman kept Title IX and constitutional supervisory and municipal liability distinct. Gebser and Davis refine responsibility under Title IX; they do not turn every peer's misconduct into unconstitutional official action or silently repeal Equal Protection enforcement.

The District and Board claims were foreclosed categorically and must receive ordinary consideration. The individual defendants prevailed on an independent immunity ground, and the general Title IX verdict establishes no new, specified factual finding in this Court. Neither judgment is overturned by correcting displacement. Purposeful official discrimination, municipal policy or final-authority attribution, causation and any actual preclusive effect remain separate questions; institutional rank, negligence and receipt of funds do not alone prove them.

##### 2. The identified statutory right is not categorically foreclosed under section 1983

**Operative rule:** Title IX's individual right against sex discrimination in a covered educational program is not categorically excluded from section 1983 enforcement by the administrative funding process or the established implied private action. A statutory section 1983 claim must prove the actual Title IX right and violation, including the statute's defendant coverage and recipient-responsibility conditions; the enforcement vehicle does not expand the underlying duty.

**Authority:** Thomas's statutory portion controls through the eight-Justice coalition identified above. Stone-Zsela does not join.

**Controlling explanation:** Blessing separates identification of an individual statutory right from the further inquiry whether Congress excluded section 1983 expressly or through an incompatible remedial scheme. This claim identifies a student's right against sex discrimination, rather than an aggregate demand for better program administration. Title IX contains no express exclusion. Its funding oversight does not give this student an exclusive adjudicatory forum, and the implied action recognized in Cannon and Franklin supplies no elaborate mandatory claim-processing route that section 1983 would evade.

The principal objection is that another enforcement label might undo the limits of the recipient's funding obligation. That objection is answered by enforcing the actual statutory right: section 1983 does not make a nonrecipient liable under Title IX, erase statutory attribution conditions or create a new harassment standard. The scheme therefore does not require categorical foreclosure of the identified right. The remand permits consideration of properly pleaded statutory claims against the District and Board subject to those limits, ordinary defenses and the existing verdict's demonstrated legal effect. No merits finding or new trial follows automatically.

#### Precedent Treatment

- **Blessing v. Freestone:** Applied to the separate inquiries into a particular individual right and actual congressional foreclosure; its rejection of aggregate program-compliance claims remains intact.
- **Cannon v. University of Chicago:** Preserved as the source of Title IX's established implied private action; that action's existence does not itself demonstrate exclusivity.
- **Franklin v. Gwinnett County Public Schools:** Preserved as recognizing compensatory relief for established intentional Title IX discrimination; this decision creates no new liability or damages finding.
- **Middlesex County Sewerage Authority v. National Sea Clammers Association:** Distinguished by the incompatible enforcement structure there; the actual foreclosure principle remains available when established.
- **Smith v. Robinson:** Distinguished by the remedial incompatibility there; factual overlap alone does not reproduce that holding.
- **Kinman v. Omaha Public School District:** Applied to the separation of statutory recipient liability, personal constitutional responsibility and municipal attribution, without treating its adjudication as an earlier decision of every preclusion question.
- **Gebser v. Lago Vista Independent School District:** Its controlling teacher-attribution route and unappealed-claim limits remain unchanged; no current rule is used to reopen the separate immunity judgment here.
- **Davis v. Monroe County Board of Education:** Its peer-harassment conditions govern actual statutory recipient liability, but do not extinguish an independent official-discrimination claim or establish liability on this record.

#### Law After Decision

From June 3, 1999, Title IX supplies no categorical bar to the independently sourced Equal Protection claims or the identified statutory-right claims enforced through section 1983 here. The constitutional holding has nine votes; the statutory enforcement holding has eight. Different sources of rights retain their different substantive conditions and defendant coverage.

The statutory enforcement vehicle does not enlarge the Title IX duty. The independent constitutional claim requires its own purposeful discrimination and responsibility; it is not a means of treating all peer harassment or negligent supervision as unconstitutional conduct. Existing Title IX attribution law, conduct-date immunity rules, municipal-liability requirements and valid preclusion defenses remain in force.

#### Separate Writings

**Stone-Zsela, concurring in part and dissenting in part:** The Chief joins the independent Equal Protection holding and its limited remand. A funding statute does not silently remove the Fourteenth Amendment's independently sourced duties. Each defendant must have the requisite discriminatory purpose or legally sufficient deliberate conduct, causal responsibility and actionable injury; supervisory title alone does not suffice. Municipal liability requires its own policy or final-decision link, and personal immunity is measured under conduct-date law.

He would retain dismissal of the duplicative Title IX statutory-right route. Section 1983 cannot be used to circumvent Congress's deliberate funding-recipient liability limits by relabeling the same statutory right. He therefore would restore only the independent constitutional claims improperly foreclosed by displacement. He would leave the favorable Title IX verdict intact absent a separately preserved trial error with the required prejudice showing; correcting constitutional pleading itself supplies no such error and defeats no properly established preclusion defense. He does not find a constitutional violation, revive claims defeated on independent immunity grounds or award relief on the merits.

#### Mandate, Remedy, and Stage

The case returns through the Second Circuit for ordinary proceedings on the District and Board section 1983 claims within the two controlling holdings. Proper pleading, defendant coverage, constitutional intent, statutory attribution, municipal responsibility, causation, injury and supported defenses remain to be adjudicated. #### Holdings

##### These FERPA nondisclosure provisions do not support the asserted Section 1983 claim

**Holding and operative rule:** FERPA's education-record nondisclosure provisions do not establish the individual judicially enforceable entitlement asserted in this Section 1983 action. The FERPA-based federal judgment must therefore be reversed; the state-action determination, excluded from review, and independent state-law judgments are unaffected.

**Authority:** Six Justices agree on this statute-specific conclusion. Thomas's lead opinion is joined by O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy; Breyer, joined by Souter, concurs in the judgment on the separate FERPA-specific explanation below. **Controlling explanation:** Section 1983 requires an individual federal right, not merely an asserted statutory violation. Six Justices conclude that these FERPA provisions do not establish the individual judicially enforceable entitlement asserted here, so this federal component cannot stand. Their agreement does not extend to one general formula for finding statutory rights or to a single full explanation of this statute. The four-Justice lead and the two-Justice concurrence supply their own reasons, stated separately. The holding concerns these nondisclosure provisions; it neither categorically excludes funding legislation nor makes the absence of an implied statutory action dispositive. Because this threshold fails, a separate damages ruling is unnecessary.

- Suter v. Artist M., March 25, 1992: its provision-specific distinction between enforceable entitlement and institutional obligation remains effective.
- Bruneau v. South Kortright Central School District, June 3, 1999: an established individual Title IX right remains enforceable through Section 1983 absent incompatible displacement; that established right does not create a missing FERPA right.

- Powell v. Ridge, April 3, 2000, and Alexander v. Sandoval, April 24, 2001: their distinct regulation and statutory-channel holdings are not substituted for the right-creation inquiry here.
- Barnes v. Gorman, June 17, 2002: its incorporated remedial channels do not supply a Section 1983 damages ruling in this threshold disposition.

The asserted FERPA nondisclosure claim cannot proceed through Section 1983 because these provisions do not establish the required judicially enforceable entitlement. Existing law continues to distinguish right creation, the enacted Section 1983 channel, implied statutory actions and incompatible remedial displacement. **Breyer, concurring in the judgment, joined by Souter.** This statute's broad education-record coverage, uncertain reach in practical administration and institutional policy-or-practice structure do not establish the asserted judicially enforceable privacy entitlement. ### Holland ex rel. Overdorff v. Harrington, Tenth Circuit No. 99-1373

Merits decision, June 30, 2003

#### Chronology and Posture

On review of the Tenth Circuit's October 19, 2001 interlocutory judgment, 268 F.3d 1179, reversing the district court's denial of deployment immunity, directing dismissal of Schirard and Harrington, and otherwise affirming further proceedings against Davis. The questions concern the force deliberately organized for the April 16, 1996 warrant operation and continued gunpoint restraint of compliant children after control. Review is confined to those Fourth Amendment claims and their individual immunity defenses.

#### Judgment

**Affirmed in part, vacated in part, and remanded.**

The appellate mandate must give effect to both components. A dispute solely about the sufficiency of evidence of participation or historical events remains outside immediate appellate review. Further summary-judgment disposition remains available on properly framed legal questions or genuinely undisputed dispositive facts.

#### Opinion Topology

GINSBURG, J., delivered the opinion of the Court, unanimous as to Parts I and III. STONE-ZSELA, C. J., filed a concurring opinion. KENNEDY, J., filed an opinion concurring in part and dissenting in part.

| Writing | Author | Joined by | Relationship to judgment | Scope joined |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Opinion of the Court | Ginsburg | Stone-Zsela, Stevens, Souter, Breyer in full; O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Thomas as to Parts I and III | Both judgment components for the complete-opinion majority; partial joiners agree with the continued-restraint disposition and oppose deployment vacatur | Parts I and III: all nine; Part II: Stone-Zsela, Stevens, Souter, Ginsburg, Breyer |
| Concurrence | Chief Justice Stone-Zsela | — | Joins the Court's opinion and judgment in full | Proposes a different personal defense after a proved violation |
| Opinion concurring in part and dissenting in part | Kennedy | O'Connor, Scalia, Thomas | Would affirm deployment immunity and the dependent dismissals; agrees with the continued-restraint disposition | Joins Parts I and III; dissents from Part II |

#### Controlling Propositions and Authority

##### I. The planned and continuing force is subject to objective review

**Controlling proposition:** The Fourth Amendment governs a deliberate choice of force that immediately and materially shapes the ensuing seizure, as well as the force used during execution. Courts assess the actual degree of force under the circumstances reasonably known to the officials and each defendant's own causal conduct; a legal immunity appeal uses supported plaintiff-favorable facts and cannot resolve a dispute solely about evidentiary sufficiency.

**Authority:** Part I of the Opinion of the Court, joined by all nine Justices.

**Controlling explanation:** Graham requires objective reasonableness in the actual encounter, and Garner makes the degree of force part of that inquiry. Offense severity, known danger, resistance, the presence of children, available information and feasible precautions bear on that assessment. They require reasonable judgment in context, not a least-intrusive-tactic rule. Summers permits reasonable control during warrant execution; Wilson distinguishes the warrant's authority from the way officers execute it.

The choice alleged here organized the immediate armed seizure. Calling it deployment does not detach that force from the Fourth Amendment. Each planner nevertheless answers for the force that his or her conduct caused, rather than automatically for every later excess by another officer. A hostile remark or violation of departmental policy does not replace the constitutional inquiry.

Johnson limits interlocutory review to the legal question on the proper assumed facts; Behrens preserves review of a genuinely separable legal defense. Disputed violent-history and resistance intelligence cannot be selected in the officials' favor. Weapons discovered later and the operation's success do not establish what was reasonably known beforehand. The same discipline applies to disputed participation. Plaintiffs must prove each defendant's conduct, unreasonable force and causal constitutional deprivation by a preponderance of the evidence at trial; compensatory loss requires its own causal proof. Carey preserves otherwise available nominal relief for a proved deprivation without compensable loss.

##### II. The deployment judgment requires renewed analysis of the actual plan

**Controlling proposition:** The deployment-immunity disposition and the dismissals resting on it must be vacated because the appellate analysis did not resolve the actual planned force on the supported resident-favorable account. On remand, the court must identify the force and responsibility at issue without deciding disputed facts, apply objective reasonableness, and separately determine whether April 16, 1996 law gave sufficiently particular warning concerning that conduct.

**Authority:** Part II of the Opinion of the Court, joined by Stone-Zsela, Stevens, Souter, Ginsburg and Breyer, a majority of the nine participating Justices.

**Controlling explanation:** The residents challenge advance authorization of the degree of force used for misdemeanor arrest and evidentiary warrants at a property with known children and disputed grounds for anticipating armed resistance. Their account includes prior cooperation and removal of the identified source of known violence. The issue is the force deliberately organized under those circumstances, rather than the bare decision to select officers assigned to a tactical unit.

Harlow and Anderson preserve an objective conduct-date defense distinct from constitutional reasonableness. Denius preserves that defense without an actual-reliance requirement, and Elder requires consideration of all pertinent preconduct authority. Saucier requires the distinct inquiries without imposing a universal order of decision. Those rules permit a dispositive legal immunity ruling on a properly defined factual premise.

The partial dissent would affirm on legal uncertainty even while accepting the residents' facts. Its distinction between initial armed control and later direct threats is sound, but it does not cure the insufficient specification of the challenged initial plan. Treating the claim merely as an objection to SWAT deployment understates the asserted wrong. The actual authorization and each official's responsibility must first be identified under Johnson's assumptions; the separate notice inquiry must address that conduct. The remand reinstates consideration under those standards and permits any supported independent defense to be adjudicated in its proper forum.

##### III. Continued direct threats after control violate a sufficiently clear rule

**Controlling proposition:** Intentionally continuing to aim firearms directly at compliant, nonthreatening children after submission and complete control, without a supported safety need, is unreasonable force, and April 16, 1996 law gave sufficient warning of that violation. Davis is therefore denied legal immunity on the supported account of his own supervision and continuation of that restraint; a challenge only to the evidence of his participation cannot be resolved in this interlocutory appeal.

**Authority:** Part III of the Opinion of the Court, joined by all nine Justices.

**Controlling explanation:** Graham ties force to its actual justification. An initial need to secure premises does not supply a continuing reason to aim directly at children who are controlled and present no threat. Holding a firearm ready for a genuine safety need is different from using it as the sustained direct threat alleged here. Intentional gunpoint restraint is a seizure whose reasonableness does not depend on a shot or physical wound.

McDonald addressed a gun threat against a nonthreatening child, and Baker addressed continuing gunpoint restraint of nonresisting occupants, including minors. Considered with Graham's rule, those preconduct decisions gave particular warning about the supported after-control conduct. They are persuasive notice authorities, not substitutes for the constitutional rule. The later Hope and McClendon decisions explain the governing fair-warning method; the notice here comes from law already available in April 1996. A factual replica or proof that an officer personally read the cases is unnecessary.

The officials' continuing-search argument can justify readiness when actual danger remains. It does not answer an account that assumes submission, complete control and no safety reason for direct aiming. Davis's alleged presence, supervision of the ongoing restraint and direction ending it supply the assumed affirmative personal link. Liability ultimately depends on his actual causal conduct, not his rank. Johnson requires participation disputes to be adjudicated in the proper factual forum. On the same supported ongoing-threat assumptions, the legal notice question is resolved.

#### Precedent Treatment

- Graham v. Connor is applied to changing force during execution and expressly extended to the deliberate plan immediately shaping the seizure; its contextual reasonableness inquiry remains operative.
- Tennessee v. Garner supplies the requirement that the degree and manner of force have adequate justification in the actual circumstances.
- Michigan v. Summers permits reasonable execution-related control, bounded by the force actually justified as control is obtained.
- Brower v. County of Inyo supplies the intentional-control requirement for seizure; the alleged directed gunpoint restraint satisfies that character.
- Johnson v. Jones preserves the bar on immediate appellate resolution of evidentiary sufficiency and requires legal review on proper assumed facts.
- Behrens v. Pelletier preserves immediate review of a separable legal immunity question without authorizing a second factual adjudication.
- Elder v. Holloway requires all pertinent conduct-date authority in the immunity inquiry.
- Harlow v. Fitzgerald retains its objective legal-uncertainty defense for individual damages liability.
- Anderson v. Creighton retains the requirement of sufficiently particular conduct-date notice while allowing an evident application of governing principles.
- Denius v. Dunlap retains its objective defense and its rejection of an actual-reliance requirement; that existing rule governs the deployment remand.
- Wilson v. Layne preserves the distinction between warrant authorization and lawful execution, together with the independent notice inquiry.
- Saucier v. Katz preserves separate force and immunity analysis without a mandatory merits-first sequence; its existing-law mandate informs the deployment remand.
- Hope v. Pelzer supplies the current fair-warning method, including obvious applications and individual responsibility; the April 1996 notice determination rests on earlier law.
- McClendon v. City of Columbia likewise preserves particularized conduct-date analysis and supplies no later-created notice for this operation.
- Carey v. Piphus preserves separate proof of compensatory injury and otherwise available nominal relief for a proved constitutional deprivation.

#### Law After Decision

Fourth Amendment review reaches the actual degree of force deliberately organized to execute a seizure when the plan immediately and materially determines it. Justification is reassessed as officers obtain control. Continued direct firearm threats to controlled, nonthreatening children without a safety need violate the force rule, and the described conduct was sufficiently warned against by April 1996. Individual responsibility, supported factual assumptions and a distinct conduct-date immunity inquiry govern adjudication. The deployment branch returns for that properly specified inquiry; the continued-threat legal immunity question is resolved on its stated assumptions.

#### Separate Positions Relevant to the Decision

**Stone-Zsela, concurring.** The Chief Justice joins the opinion and judgment applying existing law but proposes a different personal defense after a proved constitutional violation. He would require an official claiming legal reliance to prove, by a preponderance of the evidence, identified binding precedent or enacted authority directly authorizing the conduct, genuine reliance, and an objectively reasonable basis for treating that authority as valid and applicable. Courts would independently decide its legal content. Silence, customary tactics, a warrant's general authorization and a supervisor's assurance would not suffice.

He reasons that Graham already accounts for urgency, available information and reasonable factual mistakes in determining whether force is unlawful. After unreasonable force is proved, uncertainty about a general prohibition should not itself defeat accountability. He would replace Harlow's and Anderson's contrary defense, overrule Denius's holding that actual reliance is unnecessary, and displace Wilson and Hope to that extent while preserving their substantive protections and individual-responsibility holdings. Saucier did not previously adopt his replacement. Defendants would receive a fair opportunity to establish any qualifying reliance. Under Harper the new defense would apply in this and other nonfinal civil cases; Reynoldsville would preserve independently valid defenses, remedial requirements and finality rather than selective nonapplication based on generalized reliance. His proposal concerns personal damages defenses after an established violation and supplies no new cause of action or institutional liability.

**Kennedy, joined by O'Connor, Scalia and Thomas, concurring in part and dissenting in part.** They join Parts I and III but would affirm deployment immunity and the dependent dismissals. On the residents' assumed facts, April 1996 law did not make the initial choice of armed control at this multi-occupant property sufficiently clearly unlawful. Graham, Garner and Summers supplied contextual limits; McDonald and Baker addressed direct threats and continued restraint after the need for control, not the distinct advance tactical decision. The known children, low offense severity and time to plan make the constitutional objection substantial, but do not remove that legal uncertainty. Further remand is therefore unnecessary on their independent notice ground. They accept the planning-review rule and reject reliance on disputed intelligence; they also agree that continued direct threats after control cannot receive the same immunity and that Davis's separate claim proceeds.
