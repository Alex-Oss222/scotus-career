### Lamie v. United States Trustee, No. 02-693

Merits decision, January 26, 2004

#### Chronology and Posture

Argued November 10, 2003; decided January 26, 2004. On writ of certiorari to the Fourth Circuit. The question is whether amended 11 U.S.C. §330(a)(1) authorizes estate compensation for a Chapter 7 debtor's attorney who was not employed by the trustee with court approval under section 327.

Lamie represented the debtor during Chapter 11 and continued legal services after conversion to Chapter 7, without the required section 327 employment. The Fourth Circuit affirmed denial of compensation for those postconversion services.

Chief Justice Stone-Zsela and Justices Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer participated in argument and decision.

#### Judgment

**Affirmed, 9–0.**

**Supporting:** Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer.

The denial of the requested postconversion compensation from the Chapter 7 estate under section 330(a)(1) remains in force.

#### Opinion Topology

THOMAS, J., delivered the opinion of the Court, in which STONE-ZSELA, C. J., and O'CONNOR, SCALIA, KENNEDY, SOUTER, GINSBURG, and BREYER, JJ., joined. STEVENS, J., filed an opinion concurring in the judgment, in which SOUTER and BREYER, JJ., joined.

| Writing | Author | Joined by | Relationship to judgment | Scope joined |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Opinion of the Court | Thomas | Stone-Zsela, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Ginsburg and Breyer | Affirms denial of compensation | Full opinion: recipient rule and sufficient textual-and-structural explanation. |
| Concurrence in the judgment | Stevens | Souter and Breyer | Expressly adopts the recipient rule and affirmance | Additional drafting-error inquiry; Stevens does not formally join the Court's opinion. Souter and Breyer join both writings. |

#### Holdings

**A Chapter 7 debtor's lawyer must qualify under the enacted compensation authorization.** Section 330(a)(1) does not authorize compensation from a Chapter 7 estate for the debtor's attorney unless the attorney qualifies as a professional employed under section 327 with the required approval. Eligibility precedes valuation of services, and Lamie's acknowledged lack of section 327 employment defeats this application; the distinct provision permitting reasonable compensation to an individual Chapter 12 or Chapter 13 debtor's attorney under section 330(a)(4)(B) retains its own operation.

**Authority:** The eight Justices identified above join the Court's opinion in full. Stevens's concurrence in the judgment expressly adopts the recipient-list operative rule, giving that rule nine direct supporters without his formal join to the Court's opinion. Souter and Breyer join both writings.

The recipient list and the description of compensable services perform different tasks. The amended list identifies the persons entitled to an award; the retained reference to an attorney in the services clause describes work performed by an eligible professional. Treating that reference as an independent entitlement would reinsert the category removed from the list. The missing conjunction and imperfect parallelism leave a workable authorization because an employed attorney may be a professional person.

Hartford Underwriters requires the statutory claimant entitlement before recovery, and Patterson v. Shumate enforces the Code's operative language. Dewsnup's confined contextual reading of lien language does not require adding a recipient to this provision. Lamie's drafting-error contention identifies real textual awkwardness, but the proposed restoration would change the functioning recipient rule rather than resolve language that cannot operate as enacted. Trustee employment provides a Chapter 7 compensation route, and section 330(a)(4)(B) separately addresses individual Chapter 12 and Chapter 13 debtors. The usefulness of services cannot supply an omitted estate-payment entitlement. Lamie's continued service after conversion did not itself supply the section 327 employment that this route requires.

#### Precedent Treatment

- **Hartford Underwriters Insurance Co. v. Union Planters Bank:** Applied to require an enacted claimant entitlement before a bankruptcy recovery; service value cannot substitute for eligibility.
- **Patterson v. Shumate:** Applied for enforcement of the operative Bankruptcy Code provision.
- **Dewsnup v. Timm:** Distinguished as a confined contextual construction of lien language; it does not require restoring this omitted recipient category.

#### Law After Decision

Under the amended section 330(a)(1), Chapter 7 debtor's counsel seeking payment from the estate must establish the required section 327 employment before a court evaluates compensation. Continued representation after conversion supplies no independent eligibility. The distinct individual Chapter 12 and Chapter 13 authorization remains effective under its own terms.

#### Separate Writings

**Stevens, concurring in the judgment, joined by Souter and Breyer.** A plausible claim that a significant deletion resulted from a drafting error calls for examination of legislative history rather than treating the existing words alone as conclusive. The recipient-list reading excluding this unretained Chapter 7 attorney is the more natural construction, and the competing drafting-error arguments presented do not justify restoring the deleted category. The concurrence expressly adopts that operative rule and agrees with affirmance, while declining the view that text and structure alone complete the inquiry in this circumstance. Souter and Breyer accept this additional caution while joining the Court's opinion in full.

#### Source Notes

11 U.S.C. §§327, 330(a)(1), 330(a)(4)(B); the 1994 amendment governing the claimed services; No. 02-693. The absence of employment under section 327 is acknowledged. The question concerns compensation from the Chapter 7 estate through section 330(a)(1).

#### Mandate, Remedy, and Stage

The bankruptcy court must leave the requested estate compensation denied under this application. The ordinary mandate implements the affirmed eligibility determination.

**End of entry: Lamie v. United States Trustee, merits decision, January 26, 2004.**

### Tennessee Student Assistance Corporation v. Hood, No. 02-1606

Merits decision, 2004-05-17

#### Chronology and Posture

**Tennessee Student Assistance Corporation v. Hood, No. 02-1606.** Argued March 1, 2004. Decided May 17, 2004, on review of the Sixth Circuit's affirmance of the denial of TSAC's immunity objection. The question is whether state immunity bars a bankruptcy court from determining undue hardship and dischargeability of a state-held student loan.

Hood reopened her Chapter 7 case and sought a hardship discharge. TSAC had filed no proof of claim and objected to the adversary complaint and summons. The relief determines the debt's enforceability through Hood's discharge, rather than seeking affirmative monetary recovery from the State.

#### Judgment

**Affirmed and remanded, 7–2.**

**Supporting:** Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Kennedy, Souter, Ginsburg and Breyer.

**Opposing:** Scalia and Thomas.

The denial of TSAC's immunity motion stands. The bankruptcy court may adjudicate dischargeability under §523(a)(8), with Hood bearing the applicable undue-hardship burden and TSAC afforded notice and an opportunity to contest it.

#### Opinion Topology

SOUTER, J., delivered the opinion of the Court. O’CONNOR, J., filed an opinion concurring in part and in the judgment. THOMAS, J., filed a dissenting opinion. The part-specific joins are set out below.

| Writing | Author | Joined by | Relationship to judgment | Scope joined |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Court opinion, Parts I and III | Souter | Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Kennedy, Ginsburg and Breyer | Affirmance and hardship adjudication | Discharge jurisdiction; actual statutory conditions and remand |
| Court opinion, Part II | Souter | Stone-Zsela, Stevens, Ginsburg and Breyer | Independently sufficient ground for affirmance | Express authorization under the Article I enforcement rule |
| Concurrence in part and in the judgment | O'Connor | Kennedy | Affirmance | Joins Parts I and III; withholds Part II's Article I ground |
| Dissent | Thomas | Scalia | Reverse and dismiss the adversary action against TSAC | Compulsory private process; express reconsideration of contrary Article I precedent |

#### Holdings

##### I. Determining the debtor's discharge does not require the State's consent

**Holding and operative rule:** A bankruptcy court may determine whether a state-held student-loan debt falls within the debtor's discharge when the requested relief fixes the debtor's obligations rather than awarding affirmative personal recovery against the State. The prescribed hardship complaint and summons do not change the jurisdictional character of that discharge determination, and the State's choice not to file a claim supplies no veto over it.

**Authority:** Part I of the Court’s opinion supplies seven direct joins for this independently sufficient ground, as identified above.

**Controlling explanation:** Bankruptcy jurisdiction determines the debtor's estate, obligations and discharge. Local Loan v. Hunt supplies the discharge-status principle and ancillary authority to give a discharge effect. A hardship inquiry determines whether an education debt remains outside that statutory discharge; it does not impose an independent duty on the State to pay. The creditor receives notice and an opportunity to oppose the debtor's showing. Calling the prescribed notice a summons does not convert this limited inquiry into personal recovery.

The strongest contrary argument identifies the real burdens of defending a debtor-specific adversary proceeding. Those burdens matter, but they do not alter the object and legal effect of this proceeding. Nordic Village distinguishes genuine bankruptcy jurisdiction from an affirmative money demand dressed in property language. Here the operative relief actually defines the debtor's discharge. The holding is confined to that function, rather than every proceeding bearing a bankruptcy label. Irving Trust supplies bankruptcy-administration context; its State-participation setting supplies no waiver by TSAC.

##### II. Express Article I authorization independently permits the proceeding

**Holding and operative rule:** Congress may subject a State to private enforcement when the obligation and implementing adjudication lie within a valid Article I power and Congress unmistakably authorizes that exposure. Bankruptcy adjudication of the education-debt discharge exception meets those conditions, and §106(a)'s express inclusion of §§523 and 524 independently defeats TSAC's immunity objection.

**Authority:** Part II supplies five direct joins for this alternative holding, as identified above. O’Connor and Kennedy do not join this part.

**Controlling explanation:** Florida Prepaid establishes the valid-obligation, appropriate-enforcement and unmistakable-authorization rule; Garrett and Hibbs retain it. Determining which debts survive the debtor's discharge is part of the Bankruptcy Clause's debt-adjustment function, and ordinary adjudication of the statutory exception implements that function. Section 106(a)(1) expressly names the relevant provisions, subsection (a)(2) authorizes determination of their application, and subsection (a)(3) permits necessary process. Clarity and constitutional power are both present. The objection distinguishing substantive power from compulsory private adjudication is the objection those precedents rejected; the Court adheres to their rule.

Authorization is distinct from consent. TSAC's nonfiling supplies no claim-linked waiver under §106(b); the estate-claim offset in §106(c) is a separate mechanism. Section 106(a) prohibits punitive damages, subjects costs and fees to 28 U.S.C. §2412(d)(2)(A)'s provisions and limitations, requires enforcement consistent with appropriate nonbankruptcy law, and creates no otherwise absent substantive cause of action.

##### III. The education-debt exception still requires an undue-hardship determination

**Holding and operative rule:** Section 523(a)(8) excepts the covered education obligations from discharge unless that exclusion would impose undue hardship on the debtor and the debtor's dependents. Hood must establish that statutory hardship through the applicable adjudicative process; immunity and the State's nonparticipation do not replace the showing.

**Authority:** Part III is joined by the same seven Justices as Part I.

**Controlling explanation:** The provision covers an educational benefit overpayment or loan made, insured or guaranteed by a governmental unit; a loan made under a program funded in whole or part by a governmental unit or nonprofit institution; and an obligation to repay funds received as an educational benefit, scholarship or stipend. Determining whether the statutory hardship condition is met is ordinary merits work on remand. The Court's two jurisdictional grounds permit that determination; the loan remains subject to the statutory discharge exception until Hood carries her burden.

#### Precedent Treatment

- **Local Loan Co. v. Hunt, 292 U.S. 234:** Applied for discharge as a determination of the debtor's status and for authority ancillary to that determination; the present State-creditor application rests on the actual relief here.
- **New York v. Irving Trust Co., 288 U.S. 329:** Supplies bankruptcy-administration context; its participation and distribution holding does not establish waiver by this nonfiling creditor.
- **United States v. Nordic Village:** Distinguished from an affirmative personal recovery under original §106(c); its remedy-specific consent rule remains in force.
- **Florida Prepaid; Garrett; Hibbs:** Applied in Part II to express enforcement of a valid Article I obligation through an appropriate measure.
- **Federal Maritime Commission v. South Carolina State Ports Authority:** Its function-and-remedy analysis remains distinct; this decision rests on bankruptcy discharge and express statutory authorization.

#### Law After Decision

The bankruptcy court may adjudicate the education-debt exception against a State creditor on both the seven-Justice discharge ground and the five-Justice Article I ground. The debtor must prove undue hardship to obtain the discharge determination sought. An affirmative recovery from the State, recovery of property it holds or a separate coercive enforcement remedy lies outside the requested discharge relief and requires its own source and remedy analysis.

#### Separate Positions Relevant to the Decision

**O'Connor, concurring in part and in the judgment, joined by Kennedy.** The two join the discharge-specific analysis and statutory remand. They retain their structural objection to general Article I authorization of private suits against a State, and therefore withhold Part II. Their agreement rests on the bankruptcy court's actual determination of Hood's discharge and the absence of an affirmative State payment obligation. Nordic Village's remedy distinction and the function inquiry emphasized in their Federal Maritime Commission positions support that limited basis. Their concurrence creates no exemption for every bankruptcy adversary proceeding.

**Thomas, dissenting, joined by Scalia.** The two would reverse the immunity denial and remand for dismissal of this adversary action against TSAC. In their view, compelling a nonparticipating State creditor to defend a separate, individualized hardship claim imposes the private-suit burden immunity protects. A claim presumptively outside the discharge cannot escape that objection merely by being characterized as an adjustment of status. Local Loan involved a private creditor, and Irving Trust involved participation in distribution; neither resolves the coercive proceeding here.

They accept §106(a)'s clear authorization but would reconsider Florida Prepaid, Garrett and the related Article I enforcement rule to the extent necessary to preserve immunity against this private judicial compulsion. Their objection expressly challenges existing precedent. They would dismiss the adversary action against TSAC while leaving Hood's underlying bankruptcy case intact; they prescribe no substitute motion procedure.

#### Source Notes

11 U.S.C. §§106, 523(a)(8), 524, as in force for this proceeding; Fed. R. Bankr. P. 7001; U.S. Const., Art. I, §8, cl. 4; Local Loan Co. v. Hunt, 292 U.S. 234; New York v. Irving Trust Co., 288 U.S. 329. The hardship allegations await adjudication; TSAC filed no proof of claim.

#### Mandate, Remedy, and Stage

The case returns to the bankruptcy court for a scheduled hardship adjudication. TSAC must receive notice and an opportunity to present its position; Hood must prove the statutory basis for bringing the debt within her discharge. The court must then enter the dischargeability determination with the binding effect bankruptcy law authorizes.

**End of entry: Tennessee Student Assistance Corporation v. Hood, Merits decision, 2004-05-17.**

### United States v. Mead Corp., No. 99-1434

Merits decision, June 18, 2001

#### Chronology and Posture

Argued November 8, 2000. On writ of certiorari to the Court of Appeals for the Federal Circuit. The questions concern the judicial weight of Customs tariff-classification ruling letters and whether the bound-diary provision includes these ring-bound day planners. The Federal Circuit reversed judgment for the United States, rejected both controlling and persuasive weight and held the planners outside the challenged dutiable classification. The January 1993 headquarters letter changed earlier duty-free treatment and offered dictionary and commercial reasons.

All nine Justices participate.

#### Judgment

**Vacated and remanded, 7–2.**

**Supporting:** Stevens, O'Connor, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer.

**Opposing:** Stone-Zsela and Scalia.

The remand requires independent tariff construction with persuasive consideration of Customs' explanation. It directs no particular classification or monetary amount. The two dissenters would instead reverse and sustain the classification, on distinct grounds.

#### Opinion Topology

| Writing | Author | Joined by | Relationship to judgment | Scope joined |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Opinion of the Court, Parts I and II | Kennedy | Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg, Breyer | Supplies controlling interpretive rules | No controlling deference for this mechanism; independent judicial construction with Skidmore consideration |
| Opinion of the Court, Part III | Kennedy | Stevens, O'Connor, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg, Breyer | Vacates and remands | Corrected classification inquiry without a predetermined tariff answer |
| Concurrence in Parts I and II and dissent from the judgment | Stone-Zsela | No other Justice | Would reverse and sustain the classification | Independent reading of bound diary; remand only for implementation |
| Dissent | Scalia | No other Justice | Would reverse and sustain the classification | Controlling reasonable-choice treatment for the official agency interpretation |

#### Controlling Propositions and Authority

##### I. This ruling mechanism does not exercise authority to bind the reviewing court

**Holding and operative rule:** Controlling Chevron treatment requires congressional conferral, expressly or through the statutory scheme, of authority to make interpretations carrying the force of law and an interpretation issued in exercise of that authority. This transaction-specific, revisable Customs classification letter does not satisfy that requirement; general administrative responsibility, expertise and official institutional endorsement do not themselves confer controlling interpretive force on the mechanism.

**Authority for Holdings I and II:** Parts I and II of Kennedy's opinion have the eight direct supporters identified in the opinion table, including its author. Scalia does not join either proposition.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that the power to administer and classify does not make every administrative interpretation controlling law for a reviewing court. Haggar requires actual delegated authority and statutory fit before acceptance of a reasonable choice within genuine ambiguity. Its formally promulgated Customs regulation does not establish the same authority for the different mechanism here. Christensen distinguishes controlling authority from an advisory interpretation's useful reasoning without deciding that all informal actions have the same status.

Customs letters guide particular transactions, may be modified, issue from numerous offices and do not bind unrelated importers as a generally applicable regulation would. Their place in the protest and judicial-review scheme confirms that administrative operation is distinct from delegated interpretive lawmaking. Formal rulemaking or adjudication can be important evidence of delegation, but neither notice and comment nor a document label is an exclusive prerequisite. Independent judicial factfinding likewise does not itself defeat lawful deference; Haggar remains intact.

The Solicitor General's brief, cosigned by Treasury's General Counsel, represents this as Customs' official position. The Court accepts that representation. It identifies whose interpretation is defended, but does not establish that Congress gave this ruling mechanism controlling legal effect. Headquarters' developed reasons and the responsible institution's endorsement strengthen the claim to consideration, not the statutory delegation. The 1993 action remains governed by its own legal regime; later procedures for changing rulings do not supply retrospective authority. Other statutory mechanisms and interpretation of an agency's own regulations are outside this grant.

##### II. Lack of controlling force does not eliminate persuasive consideration

**Holding and operative rule:** A court independently construing the tariff must consider this ruling letter's reasons according to their actual thoroughness, expertise, logic, consistency, commercial understanding and other persuasive force under Skidmore. No fixed weight or result follows; the Federal Circuit erred by excluding that consideration merely because the letter lacked controlling force.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that an agency can offer useful reasons without possessing authority to bind the court's answer. Skidmore provides that separate inquiry, and Christensen preserves it when controlling statutory deference is unavailable. Customs addressed dictionary meaning, commercial usage and whether a ring mechanism binds pages. Those reasons bear on the actual tariff language and cannot be discarded by attaching a nonregulatory label to the letter.

Mead correctly points to the change from earlier duty-free treatment. That change may weaken a consistency claim; it does not eliminate every reason the agency offers. Expertise, national responsibility and official endorsement likewise do not automatically make the position persuasive. The court evaluates the explanation on its merits within the enacted tariff boundaries. Neither the fact that Customs changed course nor the fact that it administers a complex scheme decides the tariff question in advance. The distinction preserves meaningful independent judicial interpretation rather than converting persuasion into a lesser form of mandatory deference.

##### III. The judgment returns classification under the corrected rules

**Holding and operative rule:** The Federal Circuit judgment is vacated and the case remanded for independent classification of the imported planners with the persuasive consideration required by Holding II. The remand establishes neither the bound-diary classification nor the residual duty-free classification, and it directs no particular assessment, refund amount, new agency proceeding or rulemaking.

**Authority:** Part III has the seven direct supporters identified in the opinion table, including Kennedy as author. Stone-Zsela and Scalia oppose the open classification remand and would sustain the challenged classification on different grounds.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that the appellate judgment cannot stand on an inquiry that wholly excluded the permissible persuasive contribution of Customs' interpretation. The goods are described as ring-bound planners containing calendar and memorandum pages; no missing product fact supplies the reason for return. Nor is there a general entitlement to have a lower court decide every interpretive question first. The complete tariff question is presented, but neither proposed definitive construction commands a majority: independent construction sustaining the category and controlling acceptance of Customs' reasonable construction rest on distinct noncontrolling grounds. The seven-Justice disposition instead requires the corrected judicial inquiry without predetermining its answer.

The arguments remain concrete. Mead contends that removable pages and prospective scheduling are outside bound diaries. Customs answers that rings bind pages and that diaries include dated prospective entries. The reviewing court must decide the statutory meaning, consider the actual agency reasons and implement the resulting classification through ordinary tariff procedures. The disputed tariff meaning is genuinely undecided here for want of a majority construction; assessment calculations following the eventual lawful classification are ordinary implementation, not a second unresolved deference rule.

#### Precedent Treatment

- Chevron U.S.A. Inc. v. Natural Resources Defense Council, Inc.: retained for reasonable choices within actual congressionally delegated interpretive authority; its threshold is applied to this Customs mechanism.
- United States v. Haggar Apparel Co.: its actual-delegation and statutory-boundary requirements are applied; its reserved informal-letter issue is resolved for this mechanism, while its regulation and independent-factfinding holdings remain intact.
- Christensen v. Harris County: its distinction between controlling authority and persuasive reasoning is extended to this classification letter without excluding every informal agency interpretation.
- Skidmore v. Swift & Co.: applied as an independent persuasive inquiry, with no predetermined weight or mandatory classification outcome.
- Appalachian Power Co. v. EPA: its operational-legal-consequence reviewability holding and prohibition on interpretive amendment remain distinct; reviewability does not itself establish authority to bind a court's statutory interpretation.

#### Law After Decision

Materially equivalent Customs ruling letters do not command controlling Chevron treatment through this mechanism. Courts must independently interpret the tariff while giving the agency's actual reasons the persuasive consideration they earn. Other genuine delegations, including valid regulations governed by Haggar, retain their own authority. Official endorsement does not collapse the delegation and persuasion questions. Neither ultimate classification of these planners commands a majority; the governing judicial inquiry is settled, and the tariff answer returns for decision under it.

#### Separate Writings

Stone-Zsela joins Parts I and II but dissents from the judgment. He would independently classify the goods under the tariff's ordinary words. A diary may organize prospective appointments and daily affairs, rather than only retrospective narrative. Rings fasten pages into a bound volume despite removability; neither sewing, glue nor permanence is required. Calendar, note and appointment pages satisfy that description, and auxiliary telephone pages do not defeat it. Loose unattached sheets remain different from the assembled imported articles. Customs' dictionary and commercial reasons persuade but do not control. The narrower appellate reading adds restrictions absent from the text. With both the question and undisputed product features before the Court, he would sustain the bound-diary classification and remand only for entry-specific judgment, assessment or refund implementation, without fixing an amount.

Scalia dissents on a different ground. Congress's classification assignment, genuine tariff ambiguity and Customs' publicly confirmed official interpretation warrant controlling reasonable-choice treatment in his view. The Solicitor General's brief cosigned by Treasury's General Counsel establishes the official-position condition; the statutory scheme supplies delegation, not those signatures alone. Ring binding and dated calendar and note pages make the classification reasonable despite the residual category. He objects to the Court's less determinate delegation threshold and its substitution of open persuasive review, while declining to treat every employee opinion as authoritative. He would sustain Customs' classification and leave only ordinary assessment implementation. His controlling-deference ground does not join Stone-Zsela's independent textual construction or either Court deference proposition.

#### Source Notes

The petition presents both questions in substance: [Government petition for certiorari](https://www.justice.gov/osg/brief/united-states-v-mead-petition-writ-certiorari). The agency mechanism, merchandise and public representation of Customs' official position are reported in [United States v. Mead Corp., 533 U.S. 218](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/USREPORTS-533/pdf/USREPORTS-533-218.pdf), including the representation at page 258. That public endorsement is distinct from an original agency-head signature or a regulation. Later procedures for modifying rulings do not govern the legal character of the 1993 action.

#### Mandate, Remedy, and Stage

The Federal Circuit must reconsider the tariff classification through independent statutory construction that evaluates the actual persuasive force of Customs' explanation. Ordinary further proceedings follow within the applicable review structure; no new agency hearing, missing-signature search or future rulemaking is a condition of return. Any resulting assessment or refund depends on the lawful classification and the relevant entries and calculations. No monetary amount is awarded here.

**End of entry: United States v. Mead Corp., Merits decision, June 18, 2001.**

### Barnhart v. Thomas, No. 02-763

Merits decision, November 12, 2003

#### Chronology and Posture

On merits submission. On review of the Third Circuit's judgment, the Court considers whether the Social Security Administration may deny an adult disability claim because the claimant can perform past relevant work without determining that the occupation currently exists in significant numbers in the national economy. Thomas's elevator-operator position ended, and the agency found retained capacity to perform that work. The Commissioner seeks reversal of the additional national-existence requirement; Thomas seeks reconsideration under that requirement.

All nine Justices participated at submission and decision.

#### Judgment

**Reversed and remanded, 9–0.**

**Supporting:** Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer.

Thomas is not entitled to a separate national-existence inquiry as a prerequisite to step-four denial. The lower court must implement the valid rule on the established capacity finding and resolve any remaining preserved challenge to that finding under ordinary substantial-evidence review. If none succeeds, the step-four denial stands.

#### Opinion Topology

SOUTER, J., delivered the opinion of the Court, in which STONE-ZSELA, C. J., and STEVENS, O'CONNOR, SCALIA, KENNEDY, THOMAS, GINSBURG, and BREYER, JJ., joined.

#### Controlling Propositions and Authority

##### Retained capacity for previous work may end the inquiry

**Controlling proposition:** Under the adult disability definitions in sections 423(d)(2)(A) and 1382c(a)(3)(B), the agency may deny a claim at step four when the claimant retains capacity to perform qualifying past relevant work, without separately proving that the occupation currently exists in significant numbers in the national economy. The claimant retains the applicable step-four burden; the agency must support the actual capacity finding and apply its valid regulations. National availability governs the subsequent other-work step.

**Authority:** Souter's opinion, joined by all eight other Justices; the Court sustains a permissible construction, not an exclusively mandated statutory interpretation.

**Controlling explanation:** The statute distinguishes inability to perform previous work from inability, considering age, education and work experience, to perform other substantial gainful work existing in the national economy. The word connecting those categories does not unambiguously carry the later qualification backward. The overall impairment-caused-inability definition leaves room for this separate capacity screen. Congress's exclusions of a particular vacancy, hiring likelihood and the claimant's immediate locality fit that structure, although those exclusions alone do not resolve nationwide obsolescence.

Section 405(a), incorporated for SSI through section 1383(d)(1), supplies the actual statutory assignment, and the binding five-step regulations exercise it. Mead requires both delegation and its actual exercise; Walton identifies both in this disability scheme, while Heimmermann preserves the enacted limits. Expertise, complexity or administrative longevity alone would not supply that authority.

Past relevant work demonstrates demands the claimant actually performed and offers a reasonable measure of retained functional ability. Thomas's strongest objection is that ability to do a vanished occupation may not establish ability to earn income in any existing job. That exposes the proxy's imperfect fit, but does not make the permissible distinction between impairment-caused incapacity and job disappearance irrational. Administrative practicality supports a choice the statute permits; it cannot override the statute. The required actual capacity and qualifying-past-work findings remain essential. The agency need not conduct the additional occupational-existence inquiry that the Third Circuit required.

#### Precedent Treatment

- United States v. Mead Corp.: applied through identified congressional delegation and exercise in binding regulations, preserving its distinction between administrative responsibility and controlling interpretive authority.
- Barnhart v. Walton: applied to permissible implementation within section 405(a) and its SSI incorporation; the same actual-delegation ground governs this different eligibility condition.
- Heimmermann v. First Union Mortgage Corp.: applied to an authorized interpretive mechanism consistent with statutory limits; no general power arises from ambiguity or expertise alone.
- Chevron U.S.A. Inc. v. Natural Resources Defense Council, Inc.: applied within the actual statutory assignment and permissible construction identified here.

#### Law After Decision

The valid adult-disability sequence may stop at retained capacity for qualifying past relevant work. The later national-economy inquiry does not become an additional step-four condition. The decision applies existing delegated-choice doctrine to this statutory screen while preserving supported capacity findings, valid regulatory qualifications and ordinary review.

#### Source Notes

Barnhart v. Thomas, No. 02-763; 540 U.S. 20; Thomas v. Commissioner, 294 F.3d 568; 42 U.S.C. §§405(a), 423(d)(2)(A), 1382c(a)(3)(B) and 1383(d)(1); 20 C.F.R. §§404.1520 and 416.920. Thomas's individual job ended on August 25, 1995; nationwide extinction of the occupation was asserted rather than found.

#### Mandate, Remedy, and Stage

The case returns for implementation of the step-four rule and ordinary disposition of any remaining preserved capacity challenge. A labor-market survey is not required at that step; the denial stands unless a remaining challenge to the actual capacity determination succeeds.

**End of entry: Barnhart v. Thomas, Merits decision, November 12, 2003.**

### United States v. Shabani, No. 93-981 (513 U.S. 10)

Merits decision, November 1, 1994

Argued October 3, 1994, on writ of certiorari to the United States Court of Appeals for the Ninth Circuit.

The question is whether 21 U.S.C. §846 requires proof of an overt act in furtherance of a narcotics conspiracy. Shabani was convicted after the district court refused his requested overt-act instruction. The Ninth Circuit, 993 F.2d 1419, reversed under its circuit precedent and expressly left sufficiency and other appellate claims undecided. The indictment omitted an overt-act allegation; its sufficiency on that ground follows from the same statutory construction.

The Court reverses the Ninth Circuit’s judgment and remands for the reserved appellate issues, 9–0. Chief Justice Alex-Lamar Stone-Zsela and Justices Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer support that disposition; no Justice opposes it. All participated at argument and decision. Justice O'Connor delivered the Opinion of the Court, joined in full by the other eight Justices. All nine adopt the following statutory construction and its application. No separate opinion was filed.

The appellate court must apply the rule that §846 has no additional overt-act element and address the remaining preserved issues. No new factfinding is required on the overt-act question. The Court does not order acquittal, a new trial or unconditional reinstatement of every aspect of the conviction.

Section 846 of Title 21 requires neither an allegation nor proof of an overt act beyond the unlawful narcotics agreement. The Government must still prove the unlawful agreement and the defendant’s knowing participation beyond a reasonable doubt; neither mere association nor awareness of another person’s plan is enough.

The Court holds that Congress made the unlawful agreement the completed conspiracy offense. Section 846 contains no additional overt-act language, while 18 U.S.C. §371 expressly requires an act to effect the conspiracy’s object. Nash supplies the common-law understanding of conspiracy, and Singer shows why Congress’s omission of an extra act requirement matters when it uses that settled term. This reading does not punish a thought: the agreement itself is conduct, and the Government must prove knowing participation in it. The Ninth Circuit’s contrary element cannot rest on circuit precedent when the governing statute supplies none. Felix’s treatment of successive prosecutions did not decide this statutory question and cannot add the omitted element. Nor does lenity supply it; ordinary construction resolves the question without the residual ambiguity addressed in Ratzlaf. The refused overt-act instruction therefore supplies no ground for reversal of the conviction. The Court leaves the appeal’s reserved sufficiency and other claims to the court of appeals.

*Nash v. United States*, 229 U.S. 373 (1913), applies the common-law meaning under which the agreement completes conspiracy without a further overt act.

*Singer v. United States*, 323 U.S. 338 (1945), applies the significance of Congress’s omission of an additional overt-act requirement from a statutory conspiracy offense.

*United States v. Felix*, 503 U.S. 378 (1992), remains a double-jeopardy decision distinguishing agreement from substantive conduct; it did not decide §846’s overt-act element. Its narrower separate position likewise supplies no contrary statutory element.

*Ratzlaf v. United States*, 510 U.S. 135 (1994), preserves lenity for genuine ambiguity remaining after ordinary construction; that condition is absent here. Its distinct willfulness rule is unchanged.

No universal rule for every conspiracy statute is announced. The Court does not decide credibility, evidentiary sufficiency, the remaining appellate objections or the ultimate validity of the conviction. Current double-jeopardy law is unchanged.

Effective November 1, 1994, §846 conspiracy requires the unlawful agreement and knowing participation, but no further overt act in allegation or proof. This adds an offense-specific statutory construction. It does not alter the knowledge requirements of other crimes, create a universal rule about omitted statutory language, or change Felix, Dixon, Grady or Ratzlaf beyond the distinctions stated above.
