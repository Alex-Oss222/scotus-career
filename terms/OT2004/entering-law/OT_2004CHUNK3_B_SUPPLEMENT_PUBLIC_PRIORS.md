### Chandris, Inc. v. Latsis, No. 94-325

Merits decision, June 14, 1995

#### Chronology and Posture

Argued February 21, 1995. On writ of certiorari to the Second Circuit, Latsis v. Chandris, Inc., 20 F.3d 45 (Mar. 24, 1994), which ordered retrial after a status-only verdict and June 17, 1993 final judgment. The questions concern the employment connection needed to assess the drydock instruction and whether all Galileo drydock time could categorically be excluded. The preserved specific objection suffices; broader Rule 51 objections are not automatically reopened.

Chief Justice Stone-Zsela and Justices Stevens, O’Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer participated at argument and decision.

#### Judgment

The Second Circuit judgment requiring a new trial is affirmed, 9–0.

| Judgment component | Disposition and vote | Supporting Justices | Opposing Justices | Remedy or remand |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Judgment requiring new trial on seaman status | Affirm, 9–0 | Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg, Breyer | No opposing Justice | New trial under lawful status instructions; liability and damages remain conditional |

#### Opinion Topology

| Writing | Author | Joined by | Relationship to judgment | Scope joined |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Opinion of the Court | O'Connor | Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Ginsburg; Stone-Zsela in the general connection and preserved drydock discussions | Affirm retrial | Five support full framework including nonabsolute temporal guide; Stone joins general actual-assignment connection and preserved drydock holding only |
| Concurrence in the judgment | Stevens | Thomas, Breyer | Affirm retrial | Actual-crew/high-seas-voyage approach; refuses full duration framework |

#### Controlling Propositions and Authority

##### Contribution and substantial employment-related vessel connection

**Controlling proposition:** Jones Act seaman status requires work contributing to a vessel's function or mission and an employment-related connection to a vessel in navigation or identifiable fleet that is substantial in both duration and nature. The actual assignment governs; a job label or isolated moment aboard is not conclusive, and a genuine change of essential assignment is assessed on the new position rather than mechanically diluted by earlier shoreside service.

**Authority:** O'Connor's Opinion of the Court, joined at this level by Stone-Zsela, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter and Ginsburg; six support the general connection rule. Stevens, Thomas and Breyer concur only in the judgment on their different actual-crew approach.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that contribution and substantial employment connection are distinct requirements. Wilander removes any requirement that a seaman perform navigational duties, while retaining the line between a vessel's workforce and land-based employees incidentally aboard. Gizoni rejects occupational labels and voluntary compensation as conclusive substitutes for that employment inquiry. Nature and duration together identify whether the relationship ordinarily exposes the worker to the vessel's work as part of its company. The lower court's duration-or-nature formula does not preserve both dimensions. The parties stipulated that Latsis contributed to the mission, but disputed the nature and extent of his shipboard assignment. Neither that stipulation nor his presence on the injury voyage settles the remaining connection. A genuinely new essential assignment is evaluated on its own activities; prior shoreside years cannot mechanically defeat a newly established shipboard role. Conversely, later employment cannot retroactively create a relationship absent when injury occurred. The Court leaves actual status to proper factual evaluation and decides neither negligence nor damages.

**Precedent treatment:**

- McDermott International, Inc. v. Wilander, 498 U.S. 337 — applied and clarified: mission contribution is necessary but does not eliminate substantial employment connection.
- Southwest Marine, Inc. v. Gizoni — applied to reject categorical occupational exclusions and preserve genuine connection disputes; conclusive-record summary judgment remains possible.

##### A nonabsolute temporal guide for ordinary assignments

**Controlling proposition:** For an ordinary employment arrangement, about 30 percent of service in connection with a vessel in navigation supplies a rule of thumb for substantial duration, not an inflexible cutoff or jurisdictional condition. Justified departures and genuinely changed essential assignments remain possible; courts may resolve status as a matter of law only when the properly assessed facts permit one reasonable conclusion.

**Authority:** O'Connor's Opinion of the Court, this temporal-guide discussion, joined by Scalia, Kennedy, Souter and Ginsburg; five directly support it. Stone-Zsela does not join this discussion. No judgment-concurrence vote is aggregated into its authority.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that a temporal guide may make the duration inquiry usable without replacing the actual employment relationship. Wilander supplies the statutory distinction between seamen and land-based workers; it does not provide a numerical answer to every arrangement. Gizoni preserves factfinding when work and connection remain genuinely disputed, and leaves room for judgment on a conclusive record. The approximate proportion reflects the practical difference between substantial vessel service and incidental presence in an ordinary assignment. Its purpose fails if the number becomes an automatic bar regardless of a worker's newly established essential duties or other justified circumstances. Nature remains an independent dimension, so passing the guide does not prove seaman status and falling below it is not invariably dispositive. The competing estimates of Latsis's vessel time are not reconciled by selecting one as fact. The Court accordingly preserves ordinary judicial definition and genuine jury application, rather than converting a useful guide into an immutable threshold or deciding this worker's ultimate status.

**Precedent treatment:**

- Wilander — clarified through a practical nonabsolute guide to duration; mission contribution remains separately necessary.
- Gizoni — preserved: disputed status ordinarily requires factual resolution, while conclusive-record judgment remains open.

##### The preserved categorical drydock exclusion requires a new trial

**Controlling proposition:** Temporary repair does not invariably withdraw a vessel from navigation, although major reconstruction can do so on a sufficient record; withdrawn-vessel time is not automatically qualifying navigation service but may illuminate the actual employment relationship. Here the record did not justify a single categorical navigation conclusion and the instruction excluded the drydock period for every purpose, prejudicing the preserved status issue and requiring a new trial without deciding ultimate status or negligence.

**Authority:** O'Connor's Opinion of the Court, joined on this preserved instructional holding by Stone-Zsela, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter and Ginsburg; six support this rationale. The three judgment concurrences supply no full-framework join.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that the preserved drydock objection independently supports retrial. Senko and Butler reject treating ordinary immobility or repair as a mechanical withdrawal from navigation; they do not establish that a major conversion is temporary repair. Galileo underwent substantial reconstruction, including removed bottom plates and propellers, engine work and passenger-space rebuilding. A properly developed conclusive record could therefore support withdrawal as a matter of law. This record supplied no adequate findings or stipulations making the categorical instruction unavoidable. The instruction also barred consideration of the period for every purpose, confusing qualifying navigation time with evidence of the nature and continuity of an assignment. Later work may support or undermine an inference about the earlier relationship without creating retroactive status. Because the verdict addressed status alone, that broad exclusion materially affected the contested issue. Rules 51 and 61 require a preserved, prejudicial error; the specific drydock objection supplies it. The Court need not excuse every unpreserved objection to the broader charge or ratify that charge.

**Precedent treatment:**

- Senko v. LaCrosse Dredging Corp., 352 U.S. 370 — applied against mechanical repair/immobility exclusions; no finding that every reconstructed vessel remains in navigation follows.
- Butler v. Whiteman, 356 U.S. 271 — applied to genuine navigation/status fact disputes, not to eliminate conclusive-record rulings.
- Wilander and Gizoni — applied to distinguish contribution, connection and factual status from the occurrence of an injury aboard.

#### Precedent Treatment and Current-Law Effect

Wilander's mission-and-connection distinction and Gizoni's employment/factfinding rule remain. The Court clarifies both duration and nature; the five-Justice temporal guide is nonabsolute. Senko and Butler do not prevent withdrawal rulings on a properly conclusive major-reconstruction record.

Lower courts apply contribution plus substantial connection in both nature and duration, using the qualified temporal guide at its five-Justice scope. Neither contribution alone, a job label, every voyage, every drydock period nor a numerical threshold conclusively supplies status. A genuine essential reassignment is assessed on its own work. Later drydock work may be evidence of the employment relation, but cannot retroactively create status. Actual navigation, status, negligence, causation and damages are unresolved. The broader unpreserved charge is not ratified.

#### Separate Positions Relevant to the Decision

Stevens, joined by Thomas and Breyer, would identify a worker serving as part of the ship's company on the actual high-seas voyage as a seaman during that voyage. They rely on statutory crew membership and maritime work rather than the Court's broader temporal framework, and therefore concur in retrial while refusing its duration test. They decide no negligence or damages. Stone-Zsela's partial join supports the general substantial nature-and-duration connection and the preserved instructional correction; it does not adopt the numerical guide or ratify unpreserved parts of the broader charge. No separate opinion is filed by him.

#### Source Notes

The Second Circuit majority and Kearse dissent, petition appendix A1–A33, and status-only verdict and final judgment A34–A36 appear in the [Archive docket collection](https://archive.org/details/micro_IA40385013_0554); petitioner’s brief pp. 20–31 and respondent’s brief pp. 2–13 supply the competing positions. The substantial six-month conversion and competing work estimates remain distinct from findings about navigation or status. Mission contribution was stipulated; the connection was not. The review questions are stated without a quotation from the grant order, and the decision does not resolve every unpreserved objection.

#### Mandate, Remedy, and Stage

The district court conducts a new trial under lawful employment-connection instructions, without excluding all drydock work for every purpose on this record. The factfinder determines genuinely disputed navigation and assignment facts; negligence and damages are reached only if status and the other statutory elements are established. No seaman finding, injury liability or damages award is entered. The proceedings on the merits in this Court are complete; trial proceedings resume.

**End of entry: Chandris, Inc. v. Latsis, merits decision, June 14, 1995.**

### Harbor Tug & Barge Co. v. Papai, No. 95-1621

Merits decision, May 12, 1997

#### Chronology and Posture

Argued January 13, 1997. On writ of certiorari to the United States Court of Appeals for the Ninth Circuit, September 25, 1995, 67 F.3d 203. The questions concern Jones Act seaman status and whether a final LHWCA award forecloses the seaman claims. Papai injured his knee during a one-day dockside painting assignment aboard Pt. Barrow; the parties dispute the legal significance of his staffing role and prior work. The appeal below also challenged the later vessel-negligence trial judgment, which the Ninth Circuit did not reach.

#### Judgment

| Judgment component | Disposition and vote | Supporting Justices | Opposing Justices | Remedy or remand |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Jones Act and seaman-dependent claims | Status reopening reversed, 6–3 | Stone-Zsela, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter and Thomas | Stevens, Ginsburg and Breyer | Reinstate status summary judgment only for these claims. |
| Separately appealed vessel-negligence judgment | Returned for appellate consideration, 9–0 | Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer | — | Ninth Circuit must consider the challenge it did not reach; no automatic new negligence trial or blanket reinstatement of the entire final judgment. |

Stone-Zsela agrees in the second component's disposition without an additional ground. The compensation award is neither vacated nor modified; no damages, refund or replacement compensation order is entered.

#### Opinion Topology

| Writing | Author | Joined by | Relationship to judgment | Scope joined |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Opinion of the Court | Souter | O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy and Thomas throughout; Stone-Zsela in Parts I–II; Stevens, Ginsburg and Breyer in Part III | Parts I–II support reversal of status reopening; Part III returns the unresolved vessel appeal | Parts I–II: fleet and status; Part III: independent appeal. Six joins to Parts I–II, eight to Part III. |
| Dissent in part | Stevens | Ginsburg and Breyer | Opposes reversal of status reopening; joins Part III | Triable crew relationship, broader shared-hall alternative and rejection of both award-foreclosure theories for this pending claim. |

Stone-Zsela agrees only in the disposition returning the separately appealed vessel-negligence judgment, without an additional ground on that component.

#### Controlling Propositions and Authority

##### I. A shared hiring hall does not itself identify a fleet

**Holding and operative rule:** A worker may aggregate service on an identifiable fleet for Jones Act seaman status only when the vessels share ownership or operational control. Obtaining assignments through one union hiring hall does not itself supply that connection, although a hiring-hall worker may establish the required substantial connection to an actual vessel or commonly owned or controlled fleet.

**Authority:** Part I of Justice Souter's opinion, joined by Stone-Zsela, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy and Thomas; six direct joins.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that a common source of labor does not turn independently directed vessels into one fleet. Chandris requires both contribution to the vessel's work and a connection substantial in nature and duration to a vessel or identifiable fleet. Its actual-assignment inquiry protects an employment relationship, rather than every maritime task a worker performs. Wilander rejects a requirement of traditional navigation duties, but does not eliminate the required vessel connection.

The contrary view treats the operators' use of the same crew market as enough to combine otherwise unrelated engagements. That would make the relevant fleet depend on which vessels happened to employ workers from that hall, without a shared operational relationship. Common ownership or control supplies the necessary objective boundary and connects the inquiry to the employment undertaken. This decision clarifies the fleet alternative; it does not attribute that previously disputed answer to Chandris. No wider common operational arrangement is established here. Daily hiring is not itself disqualifying, and neither permanent employment nor a voyage on the injury day is an absolute condition. Rejecting hall-wide aggregation leaves Papai's distinct same-employer theory for its own examination.

##### II. The supported employment relationship cannot establish substantial connection in nature

**Holding and operative rule:** Considering the entire accepted engagement and the supported prior Harbor Tug work, the record permits no reasonable finding that Papai had a vessel connection substantial in nature. Summary judgment therefore must be reinstated against his Jones Act and other seaman-dependent claims; neither the deckhand designation nor a staffing declaration supplies sea-service obligations absent from the actual undertaking.

**Authority:** Part II of Justice Souter's opinion, joined by the same six Justices as Part I. This is the sufficient status ground for their judgment, applying the fleet boundary and independently assessing the same-employer alternative.

**Controlling explanation:** Chandris directs attention to the actual assignment and preserves factfinding when the properly considered evidence permits competing reasonable conclusions. Here Papai accepted one day of dockside chipping and painting, with no planned sailing or commitment to return. His three or four specifically recalled earlier Pt. Barrow jobs and the immediately preceding Sea Fox job were maintenance. The twelve earlier Harbor Tug engagements do not establish a different continuing sea-service obligation; the Court does not find that every unidentified job was identical.

Secchitano's declaration supplies real contrary staffing evidence: Papai replaced a more regularly assigned deckhand, filled the two-deckhand complement and was dispatched into the operational crew rather than a shoreside maintenance gang. Taking those assertions into account does not change the accepted limits of this engagement. Ordinary maintenance can be part of established crew service; that proposition does not make this discrete job such a continuing relationship. The contract describes qualifications and possible duties, not performance of each duty. General testimony that most earlier hiring-hall work was deckhand work on moving boats does not establish the nature of the Harbor Tug relationship.

The Court decides no withdrawal from navigation merely because the tug was docked with idle engines, and calculates no unsupported percentage. Chandris's existing guide for ordinary arrangements is about thirty percent: a rule of thumb, not an inflexible cutoff or jurisdictional condition, permitting justified departures and genuinely changed essential assignments. Meeting it cannot replace nature; falling below it is not invariably fatal. A brief assignment involving actual sea service, a genuine continuing crew obligation or different supported prior work remains outside this holding. Gizoni's protection of genuine disputes remains intact; it does not require trial when the correctly assessed relationship permits only one legal conclusion.

##### III. Reversing the status ruling does not decide the separate vessel-negligence appeal

**Holding and operative rule:** Reversal of the Ninth Circuit's reopening of the seaman claims reinstates status summary judgment only. The case returns for ordinary appellate consideration of the separately challenged post-trial vessel-negligence judgment, which the Ninth Circuit did not reach; the Court neither affirms that judgment on its merits nor automatically orders a new negligence trial.

**Authority:** Part III of Justice Souter's opinion is joined by all eight Associates. Stone-Zsela agrees in this component's disposition without an additional ground; the eight direct rationale joins control.

**Controlling explanation:** The January 20, 1993 appeal challenged both status and the judgment after the vessel-negligence trial. The court of appeals reopened status and did not resolve the latter challenge. Its expectation that a Jones Act case would use a more favorable standard did not adjudicate the independent vessel claim. Removing that expectation cannot substitute for appellate consideration of the actual judgment.

Section 905(b) permits its specified vessel-negligence action subject to its own limits. It excludes seaworthiness as a basis of liability, bars employer indemnity to the vessel and makes its remedy exclusive against the vessel except for LHWCA remedies. Its stevedoring restriction applies where the vessel employed the worker for that service and the injury arose from negligence of those providing it. Its separate shipbuilding, repairing or breaking restriction applies when the employer is the vessel owner, owner pro hac vice, agent, operator or charterer, and bars the specified action against that employer in any capacity or its employees. The Court decides neither whether this painting is repairing service within that provision nor whether such a contention is properly available here. Section 905(c)'s Outer Continental Shelf reciprocal-indemnity qualification has no established predicate.

The trial court's findings about the ladder, Papai's movement and Low's credibility belong to the unresolved negligence appeal; they are not adverse concessions on the earlier status motion. No finding of negligence, causation, consortium entitlement, jury waiver or damages follows. This remand preserves the lawful appellate task without guaranteeing any independent remedy.

**Questions not reached:** The final compensation order's effect under §905(a) and ordinary administrative issue preclusion are separate questions, both unnecessary to the status holding. The Court neither adopts the lower court's nonforeclosure reasoning nor orders a new award-effects inquiry. The award remains operative in its own system. No refund, credit amount, fee adjustment, modification deadline or factual correction of that award is decided.

#### Precedent Treatment

- **Chandris, Inc. v. Latsis:** Applies its substantial nature-and-duration requirement, actual-assignment rule and conclusive-record boundary; clarifies the disputed fleet alternative through common ownership or operational control. Its approximate thirty-percent guide remains qualified and supplies no numerical disposition here.
- **McDermott International, Inc. v. Wilander:** Applies contribution without a traditional-navigation requirement while retaining a substantial employment-related vessel connection and conclusive-record summary judgment.
- **Southwest Marine, Inc. v. Gizoni:** Preserves occupational neutrality, the voluntary-unadjudicated-benefits rule, statutory credit and genuine status disputes. Its express reservation of final awards remains open; today's majority does not extend its payment holding to this adjudicated award.
- **Astoria Federal Savings & Loan Association v. Solimino:** Its contextual administrative-preclusion framework remains undisturbed; the Court does not apply it to decide this award's collateral effect.
- **Scindia Steam Navigation Co. v. De Los Santos:** The vessel/employer framework used below remains for the unresolved vessel-negligence appeal; status reversal decides no Scindia negligence issue.

#### Law After Decision

Effective May 12, 1997, the fleet alternative requires common ownership or operational control; a shared labor source alone is insufficient. The independent nature requirement defeats status on this actual limited engagement even after the replacement declaration and supported same-employer history are considered. Brief actual seagoing service, ordinary maintenance within an established crew relationship and genuinely disputed different records remain governed by Chandris and Wilander. The qualified temporal guide survives without a new cutoff. Final-award exclusivity and preclusion remain open; the three-Justice nonforeclosure theory does not establish law.

#### Separate Positions Relevant to the Decision

**Justice Stevens, dissenting in part, joined by Ginsburg and Breyer.** They would affirm reopening of status and the rejection of foreclosure by this award, while joining Part III's preservation of the independent vessel appeal. The actual crew relationship, rather than ownership alone, should determine which recurring hiring-hall work is relevant. Operators who obtain their deckhands through that established market know the character of the work; daily dispatch should not systematically exclude people doing seaman work. This does not make every vessel a worker visits one fleet or dispense with a substantial relationship.

Even on the narrower same-employer theory, Secchitano's replacement and crew-complement evidence, repeated Harbor Tug engagements and ordinary deckhand maintenance permit a crew-service inference. The known one-day dockside job and identified prior maintenance support the employer, but do not compel its conclusion. No declaration label determines federal status, no twelve prior voyages are assumed and the general seventy-percent account is not a Harbor Tug percentage. Ginsburg applies the full Chandris framework; Stevens and Breyer retain their actual-crew approach without acquiring a join to its full temporal discussion. All three find the narrower record sufficient to reopen status without deciding that Papai is a seaman.

They also reject the two distinct award-foreclosure theories. The ALJ held a real hearing, expressly decided crew status and entered a final order; the dissent does not equate it with a voluntary check. Rather, Gizoni's accommodation of pursuing compensation while status is disputed, together with the statutory credit arrangement, supports extending nonforeclosure to this pending judicial claim. In their view §905(a) does not independently make this order an immutable classification, and Astoria's contextual inquiry permits displacement of ordinary preclusion here. Section 903(e) expressly credits specified other compensation or Jones Act payments against LHWCA liability; it does not state a universal reciprocal preclusion exemption. The dissent treats that structure as evidence of the accommodation, while acknowledging that preventing duplicate recovery and honoring final adjudication ordinarily can coexist. It finds no fictitious hearing, automatic estoppel or employer manipulation, and decides no general rule for consensual settlements or judicially reviewed status judgments. The award's own operation remains intact.

#### Source Notes

The reported lower decision is [67 F.3d 203](https://openjurist.org/67/f3d/203). The published Joint Appendix includes the replacement-crew declaration and actual-work testimony; contractual duties and general occupational history do not prove duties on every prior Harbor Tug engagement. The ALJ expressly adjudicated status after a hearing and entered an unappealed order, signed August 27 and filed/served August 31, 1992. Its merits are not reviewed here. [33 U.S.C. §905](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/USCODE-1994-title33/html/USCODE-1994-title33-chap18-sec905.htm) supplies the distinct exclusivity and vessel-action provisions. Unexcerpted trial testimony and complete payroll are not treated as established facts; no precise prior-voyage tally is found.

#### Mandate, Remedy, and Stage

The case returns to the Ninth Circuit. It must implement the status ruling and consider the still-unresolved appeal from the vessel-negligence judgment under the ordinarily available grounds and statutory limits. The Court sets no new filing deadline, awards no damages, decides no negligence finding or jury waiver, and directs no compensation appeal or modification. Independent remedies remain available only on their own lawful predicates.

**End of entry: Harbor Tug & Barge Co. v. Papai, merits decision, May 12, 1997.**

### Sattazahn v. Pennsylvania, No. 01-7574

Merits decision, January 14, 2003

#### Chronology and Posture

Argued November 4, 2002. On writ of certiorari to the Pennsylvania Supreme Court, reviewing its judgment affirming the death sentence after the second murder trial. The March 18, 2002 grant encompasses double jeopardy and due process. At the first trial Pennsylvania sought death, the penalty jury reported a hopeless nine-to-three division for life, and state law required a life sentence upon discharge. Sattazahn obtained reversal of the conviction for trial error. The State again sought death at retrial, including an additional aggravator, and obtained it. The questions concern renewed death exposure, not the legality of retrying guilt.

#### Judgment

**Reversed as to death, 5–4.**

**Supporting:** Stone-Zsela, Stevens, Souter, Ginsburg and Breyer.
**Opposing:** O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy and Thomas.

The death sentence is vacated. The case is remanded for a lawful life sentence; the second murder conviction remains intact and no new capital penalty hearing is authorized.

#### Opinion Topology

STEVENS, J., delivered the opinion of the Court, in which STONE-ZSELA, C. J., and SOUTER, GINSBURG, and BREYER, JJ., joined. STONE-ZSELA, C. J., filed a concurring opinion. O'CONNOR, J., filed a dissenting opinion, in which SCALIA, KENNEDY, and THOMAS, JJ., joined. SCALIA, J., filed an additional dissenting opinion, in which THOMAS, J., joined.

#### Controlling Propositions and Authority

##### 1. A final mandatory life judgment ends the completed capital opportunity

**Holding and operative rule:** When a State completes a trial-like capital penalty proceeding and its law conclusively ends the jury's unresolved deliberations with a mandatory final life judgment, that disposition terminates the State's opportunity to obtain death for the offense. A successful appeal of guilt permits a new guilt trial but does not restore the extinguished capital exposure; this rule does not govern a penalty mistrial or an expressly nonfinal order lawfully leaving the capital question open.

**Authority:** Stevens's five-Justice opinion of the Court controls this rule and the sentence-only remedy.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court extends Bullington's capital-finality protection to this mandatory final disposition. Bullington and Rumsey protected capital acquittals; the present jury made no unanimous finding rejecting an aggravator. The decisive event is instead the life judgment Pennsylvania required after the State completed its capital opportunity. The nine-to-three division is not a verdict.

The contrary position would make an acquittal on the merits indispensable and treat life as a default attached only to the first conviction. That account overlooks the different legal choices between an unresolved penalty mistrial and this statute's completed life judgment. Richardson permits renewed proceedings after an ordinary deadlock, but Pennsylvania concluded this penalty proceeding with a final punishment rather than leaving it pending. Poland involved no intervening life judgment ending the capital opportunity. Stroud's permission for a higher sentence after reversal does not govern this separate completed capital determination.

Jones gives a completed federal penalty deadlock a nondeath consequence and distinguishes a new hearing for penalty error; its statute does not itself decide this constitutional question. Monge's bounded noncapital recidivist-status rule remains unchanged. Ring and Harris regulate punishment-authorizing factfinding; they neither manufacture an acquittal nor answer finality. The State may retry guilt after trial error, but correcting that error does not entitle it to repeat the capital opportunity its completed process ended. The Court's sufficient double-jeopardy ground requires vacation of death and lawful life sentencing while preserving the second murder conviction.

#### Precedent Treatment

- Bullington v. Missouri: extends capital-finality protection beyond an actual acquittal to the defined completed statutory-life judgment; the extension is made here.
- Arizona v. Rumsey: preserves protection of a genuine capital acquittal even when its legal premise is mistaken; no acquittal finding is attributed to this jury.
- Richardson v. United States: distinguishes ordinary unresolved deadlock; its retrial permission remains for a proceeding that has not ended in the final disposition held controlling here.
- Poland v. Arizona: distinguishes a capital proceeding with no intervening final life judgment; rejection of a particular aggravator remains distinct from acquittal of death.
- Stroud v. United States: confines its higher-sentence-after-reversal permission to circumstances lacking this separate completed capital determination.
- Monge v. California: preserves the noncapital serious-prior classification and renewed-proof rule without extending it to the capital finality question.
- Jones v. United States (federal capital sentencing): preserves the statutory nondeath consequence and separate penalty-error hearing rule; it supports the distinction but does not itself establish today's constitutional extension.
- Apprendi v. New Jersey; United States v. Angle; Harris v. United States; Ring v. Arizona: retain their present-offense element and factfinder rules and actual reservations; they supply no finding of acquittal or universal prior-conviction exception.
- Hicks v. Oklahoma: its protection of an actual state-created sentencing entitlement remains; the independent due-process ground receives no controlling resolution here.

#### Law After Decision

Capital finality now protects the defined mandatory final life judgment after a completed capital opportunity, although the penalty jury made no unanimous acquittal finding. Ordinary guilt retrial remains available. The Court does not adopt a general finality rule for every sentence or change Monge's noncapital holding. The independent due-process theory lacks a majority: four majority Justices find it unnecessary, Stone-Zsela adopts it separately, and the dissenters reject it. No combination of opposing rationales supplies a controlling due-process or element-identity rule.

#### Separate Positions Relevant to the Decision

**Stone-Zsela, concurring.** He joins the double-jeopardy holding and would independently reach the same sentence-only result under due process. Pennsylvania made life the conclusive consequence of its completed unsuccessful capital opportunity. Requiring surrender of that final limit to correct guilt-phase error burdens meaningful appellate review and upsets the adjudicated liberty interest without a new lawful basis. Guilt retrial and life punishment remain available; the appeal alone cannot recreate capital exposure the completed process ended. Hicks supplies protection for an actual sentencing entitlement, while this application develops its operation in the distinct final-life setting. The position does not presume prosecutorial vindictiveness from a harsher sentence, preserve every favorable sentencing term after reversal, or convert the jury's division into an acquittal. The other four majority Justices rest on the sufficient double-jeopardy ground and do not join this independent theory.

**O'Connor, joined by Scalia, Kennedy and Thomas, dissenting.** They would affirm rejection of both federal challenges. Bullington and Rumsey protect an adjudication that death is unavailable on the merits. Here neither the jury nor the judge found the State's aggravation proof insufficient or selected life on the merits. The statutory consequence of deadlock therefore supplies no federal acquittal finality once the defendant obtains reversal of the underlying conviction for trial error. Poland preserves that distinction, and Richardson permits retrial of unresolved allegations. The State must still obtain the lawful findings and comply with the procedures governing the new capital proceeding. Hicks protects the entitlement state law actually confers, but state law establishes no life ceiling independently surviving reversal of the conviction. Their due-process rejection concerns that asserted entitlement, not every possible state-created sentencing interest. O'Connor and Kennedy do not adopt the separate element-based explanation below.

**Scalia, joined by Thomas, additionally dissenting.** Even if a necessary present-offense aggravator performs the constitutional work of an element, a hung jury has not rejected it. Their Monge position concerns a completed failure of proof in an element adjudication; Apprendi, Angle, Harris and Ring supply present-offense factfinding protections without treating deadlock as acquittal. They therefore agree with the common dissent and explain why its result survives this limited element analysis. They adopt no universal prior-conviction exception, proposition that every aggravator is a separate offense, or rule that eligibility requires selection of death. O'Connor and Kennedy join no part of this additional writing.

#### Source Notes

No. 01-7574; 42 Pa. Cons. Stat. §9711; reported proceedings, 537 U.S. 101. The first jury's numerical division supplies no unanimous finding rejecting an aggravator. The statutory life judgment, rather than an inferred factual acquittal, is the basis of the Court's holding.

#### Mandate, Remedy, and Stage

The case returns through the Pennsylvania Supreme Court for lawful life sentencing consistent with the finality of the first capital disposition. The retrial conviction remains effective. This Supreme Court merits review is complete.

**End of entry: Sattazahn v. Pennsylvania, No. 01-7574, Merits decision, January 14, 2003.**

### United States v. Dixon, No. 91-1231

Merits disposition, 1993-06-28

#### Chronology and Posture

Argued 1992-12-02; decided 1993-06-28. On writ of certiorari to District of Columbia Court of Appeals, 598 A.2d 724 (1991).

**Question:** Whether the Double Jeopardy Clause bars substantive criminal prosecutions based on conduct already tried in nonsummary criminal-contempt proceedings, including the different counts against Dixon and Foster and the continued force of Grady v. Corbin.

**Material record:** Dixon’s release order prohibited criminal offenses. After an evidentiary hearing, he was convicted of contempt for possession of cocaine with intent to distribute and received 180 days; the later prosecution concerns that same drug offense. Foster’s protection order prohibited assault and threats against his estranged wife. Her counsel prosecuted a three-day contempt trial. Foster was convicted for two assaults and acquitted of the three threats relevant here. The later indictment charges simple assault for the first assault (I), those three threats (II, III, IV), and assault with intent to kill for the second assault (V). The Government did not prosecute the first Foster proceeding, but it was a court’s criminal-contempt prosecution with punitive sanctions.

**Relief sought:** The United States seeks reversal permitting the substantive prosecutions. Both respondents seek affirmance of the bars to renewed prosecution.

All nine Justices—Stone-Zsela, White, Blackmun, Stevens, O’Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter and Thomas—participated at argument and decision.

#### Judgment

Affirmed as to Dixon and Foster I, 6–3; reversed and remanded as to Foster II–V, 5–4. Grady is retained, 5–4. The contempt judgments remain undisturbed.

#### Controlling Propositions and Authority

##### Nonsummary punitive contempt triggers double-jeopardy protection

**Controlling proposition:** A completed nonsummary criminal-contempt prosecution places the accused in criminal jeopardy; private initiation and an order’s protective purpose do not categorically remove that protection. Whether a later prosecution is for the same offense remains a separate inquiry; coercive civil sanctions, summary courtroom contempt and separate sovereigns are not decided.

**Authority:** White’s Opinion of the Court, this proposition; Stone-Zsela, White, Stevens, O’Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas (8 Justices). Direct majority support at the stated level of generality; no Marks aggregation.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that punishment for violating a court order can implicate the protection against renewed criminal prosecution. Bloom recognizes criminal contempt as a crime for procedural protection, while Gompers distinguishes a fixed punitive sanction from a civil sanction avoidable through compliance. Foster underwent criminal adjudication, including acquittals and imprisonment, under the court’s authority. The fact that his wife’s counsel initiated enforcement does not make that completed punitive trial a private civil remedy. The Government’s institutional distinction identifies why an order matters, but cannot eliminate the constitutional character of the punishment already imposed. Young recognizes the judiciary’s authority to enforce its orders; that authority does not itself answer the separate identity-of-offenses question. The Court does not impair prospective enforcement of protective orders or decide every civil or summary contempt setting. The later counts must therefore be examined under the applicable double-jeopardy rules, with their different outcomes and rationales stated separately.

**Precedent treatment:**

- Bloom v. Illinois, 391 U.S.194: criminal character of punitive contempt applied.
- Gompers v. Bucks Stove & Range Co., 221 U.S.418: punitive/coercive distinction applied.
- Young v. United States ex rel. Vuitton, 481 U.S.787: independent judicial enforcement authority retained, not a categorical exception from criminal protection.

##### Previously prosecuted necessary offenses cannot be tried again

**Controlling proposition:** When a criminal-contempt adjudication necessarily prosecutes the substantive offense forbidden by the order, the Government cannot prosecute that same substantive offense again. Dixon’s drug offense and Foster I’s November assault were necessarily prosecuted in the respective contempts and are barred; this shared proposition does not supply a common analysis of Foster II–V.

**Authority:** White’s Opinion of the Court, this proposition; Stone-Zsela, White, Stevens, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter (6 Justices). Direct majority support at the stated level of generality; no Marks aggregation.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that the Government cannot retry the substantive offenses already necessarily established in these contempts. Brown protects against successive prosecution for greater and included offenses, and Harris prevents an abstract description of a greater offense from concealing the substantive crime necessarily prosecuted within it. Dixon’s order required compliance with the criminal law, and his contempt adjudication established the same possession with intent to distribute later charged. Foster’s November assault similarly supplied the offense prosecuted and punished under his protective order. The existence and knowledge of the order do not erase the previously prosecuted substantive crime. The opposing abstract-elements view gives insufficient effect to the necessary content of these particular charges. The six Justices supporting this rule differ on the broader comparison for additional elements and on Grady’s application, but agree directly on this limited protection. No other contempt conviction is vacated, and the Court does not resolve the remaining counts by treating every act in a single episode as one offense.

**Precedent treatment:**

- Brown v. Ohio, 432 U.S.161: successive greater/included-offense protection applied.
- Harris v. Oklahoma, 433 U.S.682: necessary substantive offense cannot be erased by an abstract description of the first charge.
- Blockburger v. United States, 284 U.S.299: elements inquiry retained; disagreement over its broader application separately recorded.

##### Grady remains governing successive-prosecution law

**Controlling proposition:** Grady is not overruled: beyond Blockburger’s elements protection, a later prosecution is barred when establishing an essential element requires proof of conduct constituting an offense for which the defendant has already been prosecuted. Mere reuse of evidence or a common episode does not suffice, and Felix’s independently punishable conspiracy holding remains; no five-Justice rationale determines Grady’s application to Foster II–V in this fractured decision.

**Authority:** Souter’s Opinion of the Court, this proposition; Stone-Zsela, White, Blackmun, Stevens, Souter (5 Justices). Direct majority support at the stated level of generality; no Marks aggregation.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court retains the distinction between another prosecution of already prosecuted criminal conduct and the reuse of evidence about that conduct. Grady states that distinction, while Felix applies it to a separate conspiracy agreement and rejects a same-evidence rule. The Government’s concern about administering the conduct inquiry does not establish that this recent protection has become unworkable. Brown’s concern with finality after trial and the successive-prosecution line examined in Vitale support maintaining protection beyond a purely formal comparison of statutory language. The contrary proposal would discard Grady rather than merely clarify Felix; five Justices decline that change. Retention does not make all charges arising from one episode inseparable. Blackmun’s distinct view of contempt as vindication of judicial authority prevents a majority from applying this retained rule to bar Foster’s remaining counts, while four other Justices would overrule it. That fractured application must not be transformed into either an overruling or a new universally controlling contempt exception. Ashe remains separately available where an acquittal necessarily resolved an issue.

**Precedent treatment:**

- Grady v. Corbin, 495 U.S.508: expressly retained; no overruling by this decision.
- United States v. Felix, March 25, 1992: conspiracy and evidentiary-overlap distinctions preserved, not recast as an overruling.
- Illinois v. Vitale, 447 U.S.410: successive-prosecution concern beyond formal elements supports retention.
- Brown v. Ohio, 432 U.S.161: finality and greater/included-offense protection retained.
- Ashe v. Swenson, 397 U.S.436: independent issue-preclusion rule preserved for the remanded threat charges.

#### Current-Law Effect

Effective June 28, nonsummary criminal contempt receives criminal-jeopardy protection, and Dixon/Foster I are barred 6–3. Grady survives by five votes. Foster II–V may proceed under a 5–4 judgment, subject to Ashe where applicable, but no majority adopts either the four-Justice Blockburger-only/overruling view or Blackmun’s special judicial-order distinction. These are incompatible rationales, not logical subsets; no Marks synthesis creates a general contempt exception or overrules Grady. Later courts retain the existing rule and the case-specific judgment without inventing a five-vote test.

#### Separate Positions Relevant to the Decision

Scalia, joined by Kennedy, compares the offenses incorporated by the orders for Dixon and Foster I, but considers Foster II–IV’s specified threats and V’s intent to kill additional elements absent from the contempt charges. Each contempt also requires an order and its known violation. They would overrule Grady, preserve Ashe’s independent protection and allow those additional charges. O’Connor, joined by Thomas, instead compares the abstract contempt elements to every substantive offense and would allow all later prosecutions; they agree with Scalia/Kennedy on overruling Grady but not on the incorporated-offense bars. Blackmun would permit all counts for a different reason: Young’s judicial-order enforcement serves interests distinct from the community’s ordinary criminal law. He retains Grady and objects to its abandonment for ordinary successive substantive prosecutions. His special contempt ground is not a logical subset of a Blockburger-only rule.

White, joined by Stevens and Souter in his additional discussion, would compare the substantive offenses actually prosecuted and bar every renewed charge. He regards adding intent to kill as no answer to the finality of an already tried assault and emphasizes acquittal finality on the threats. Souter, joined by Stevens in his further discussion, reads Nielsen, Harris and Vitale as a continuing protection against serial prosecution of the same criminal conduct, distinguishes multiple punishment from repeated trials, and opposes the II–V judgment. Only his limited Grady-retention section has the five named Court joins. Stone separately applies retained Grady count by count: Dixon’s drug conduct, Foster’s November assault, all three acquitted threats and the May assault are precisely the conduct the later charges would require proving. He treats Felix as a separate-agreement case and does not impose compulsory joinder of every charge from an episode. He would affirm all bars and therefore would not need an additional Ashe determination. That last procedural preference does not eliminate the Ashe inquiry preserved by the actual remand.
