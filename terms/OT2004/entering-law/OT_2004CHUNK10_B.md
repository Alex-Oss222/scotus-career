## Standards and Tests selection: Agency FTCA exclusivity and sue-and-be-sued immunity

### Agency FTCA exclusivity and sue-and-be-sued immunity

**Governing question:** How FTCA agency exclusivity interacts with an express sue-and-be-sued clause.

**Current rule:** Section 2679(a) makes FTCA relief exclusive against agency sue-and-be-sued authority only for claims cognizable under the whole of §1346(b), including its private-person and state-law requirements. A federal constitutional tort lies outside that category. Former §1725(c)(4)’s broad FSLIC clause waives immunity for such a claim; an implied restriction requires a clear showing of inconsistency with the statutory or constitutional scheme, necessity to avoid grave interference with governmental functions, or plain congressional intent to use the clause narrowly. Waiver does not supply a substantive cause of action or establish recovery.

**Authority by component:**

- Complete §1346(b) cognizability and agency exclusivity: FDIC v. Meyer — FTCA exclusivity does not encompass this federal constitutional claim, 1994-02-23. The statutory-exclusivity portion of Thomas's opinion of the Court, joined by Stone-Zsela, Blackmun, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter and Ginsburg. All nine adopt this separate threshold holding.
- Former FSLIC waiver and three implied-restriction grounds: FDIC v. Meyer — Former FSLIC's sue-and-be-sued clause waives immunity, 1994-02-23. The waiver portion of Thomas's opinion of the Court, joined by Stone-Zsela, Blackmun, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter and Ginsburg. Nine Justices adopt the specific waiver and its stated limits.

**Present operation effective:** FDIC v. Meyer, 1994-02-23.

**Exceptions and limits:** The separate employee-exclusivity rule in §2679(b)(1) is unchanged. The former clause governs the covered agency claims; no universal federal waiver or successor-clause construction is supplied.

**Holdings navigation:** FTCA exclusivity does not encompass this federal constitutional claim; Former FSLIC's sue-and-be-sued clause waives immunity.

## Standards and Tests selection: APA waiver and substitute-payment equitable liens

### APA waiver and substitute-payment equitable liens

**Governing question:** Whether §702 waives federal immunity for a lien seeking payment of a private debtor’s obligation from Government property.

**Current rule:** Section 702 does not waive immunity for an equitable lien functioning as substitute payment from Government property for a private debtor’s default when no identified retained property belonging to the claimant or specific statutory payment entitlement supports recovery. Equitable form and identification of an account do not establish consent. Specific enforcement of an actual entitlement remains a separate question; the substantive duty, remedy and waiver must each be established.

**Authority by component:**

- Substitute compensation, actual entitlement and remedy-specific consent: Canonical Decision Record `department_of_the_army_v_blue_fox_merits_1999-01-20.md`, Public Projection / Holdings / The requested lien supplies substitute compensation without an applicable waiver, 1999-01-20; unanimous opinion.

**Present operation effective:** 1999-01-20.

**Exceptions and limits:** Not every equitable lien is money damages. The Miller Act’s bond duty does not make the Government a guarantor when it fails to obtain a bond. Independent reviewability and adequate-remedy conditions are not eliminated.

**Holdings navigation:** [Department of the Army v. Blue Fox, Inc. — The requested lien supplies substitute compensation without an applicable waiver](../../../state/HOLDINGS.md#the-requested-lien-supplies-substitute-compensation-without-an-applicable-waiver-1).

## Standards and Tests selection: Adjudicatory waiver and monetary assessments under the McCarran Amendment

### Adjudicatory waiver and monetary assessments under the McCarran Amendment

**Governing question:** What instructions govern adjudicatory waiver and monetary assessments under the McCarran Amendment?

**Current rule:** The McCarran Amendment subjects the United States to state water-adjudication procedures but does not specifically waive immunity from the filing assessments reviewed in Idaho. Express exclusion of judgments for costs and the blurred line between those charges and former costs reinforce the absence of monetary consent. Participation does not itself authorize assessments. ## Standards and Tests selection: Representation on a first appeal as of right

### Representation on a first appeal as of right

**Governing question:** Whether an appellant has a constitutional right to discharge appointed counsel and conduct a first appeal personally.

**Current rule:** Faretta's Sixth Amendment trial right does not confer a right to conduct a first appeal as of right without counsel. The Fourteenth Amendment's fair-access and effective-assistance protections likewise permit a State to require represented appellate advocacy. A State may allow personal advocacy or supplemental submissions, but neither option is constitutionally required by these protections.

**Authority by component:**

- Proceeding-specific limit on self-representation: Martinez v. Court of Appeal, Public Projection / Holdings / Trial self-representation does not establish an appellate Sixth Amendment right; Fair first-appeal access permits represented advocacy, January 12, 2000; Thomas with all Justices except Scalia (eight).

**Present operation effective:** Martinez v. Court of Appeal, January 12, 2000.

**Exceptions and limits:** Faretta remains fully effective at trial. The decision resolves no independent competence, conflict, ineffective-assistance or record-access claim and creates no mandatory personal-submission or waiver-colloquy procedure.

## Standards and Tests selection: Advocate-assisted screening of an allegedly frivolous first appeal

### Advocate-assisted screening of an allegedly frivolous first appeal

**Governing question:** What assistance is required before a first appeal as of right may be disposed of as wholly frivolous.

**Current rule:** A State may substitute genuinely equivalent safeguards for Anders's particular format, but must preserve meaningful assistance by an appellate advocate in determining frivolousness. Counsel must provide legal assistance about issues that might arguably support the appeal, or participate in an equivalent mechanism enabling the court to assess both the diligence of counsel's search and the soundness of the frivolousness conclusion. A cited factual narrative, continued nominal appointment, judicial record examination and an opportunity for the indigent appellant to submit points do not replace the missing advocate-assisted legal screening. Omission of that function warrants restoration of adequate appellate consideration without first requiring proof that an omitted issue was nonfrivolous or probably would have changed the appellate result.

**Authority by component:**

- Equivalent formats: Smith v. Robbins, Public Projection / Holdings / I. Equivalent safeguards may replace the precise Anders format, January 19, 2000; Stevens Part I, all nine.
- Required counsel function, old-law treatment and restoration: same Record, II. Wende omits necessary counsel-assisted legal screening; III. The defined protection and restoration of its process apply as old law; IV. The conditional appellate-process relief and remaining-claims remand stand; Stevens Parts II-IV with Stone-Zsela, Souter, Ginsburg and Breyer (five).

**Present operation effective:** Smith v. Robbins, January 19, 2000, states the controlling screening and restoration rule; the defined protection and process remedy were dictated by Anders, McCoy and Penson before Robbins's finality.

**Exceptions and limits:** No required filing title, precise form or advocacy of a frivolous claim follows. Adequate screening may find every available issue frivolous. Ordinary preservation remains; technical departures and deficient performance within an otherwise supplied function do not automatically receive this restoration rule. Individual Strickland claims retain their own performance-and-prejudice inquiry. No new Teague exception is created.

## Standards and Tests selection: Capacity to plead or waive counsel and independent valid waiver

### Capacity to plead or waive counsel and independent valid waiver

**Governing question:** What instructions govern capacity to plead or waive counsel and independent valid waiver?

**Current rule:** Dusky's standard governs capacity to stand trial, plead guilty and choose to waive counsel: sufficient present ability to consult with counsel with rational understanding, and rational as well as factual understanding of proceedings. A competent person's actual waiver must separately be knowing, intelligent and voluntary. Further competence determination is necessary when circumstances give reason to doubt present competence, not automatically for every plea or waiver. Existing competence findings do not settle informed, uncoerced relinquishment; then-effective treatment of state findings remains independent.

For an uncounseled guilty plea resulting in imprisonment, informed relinquishment includes understanding the charge, counsel’s availability and assistance before and at the plea, and the applicable punishment. The Sixth Amendment does not invariably require warnings that counsel might discover an overlooked defense or offer an independent view about pleading. Omission of those two warnings alone therefore proves no invalid waiver. Trial-only counsel advice is not automatically equivalent to knowledge of plea-stage availability; actual understanding controls rather than prescribed words, and States may require fuller colloquies.

**Authority by component:**

- A common capacity standard and a separate actual waiver: Godinez v. Moran — A common capacity standard and a separate actual waiver, 1993-06-24. Thomas’s Opinion of the Court, this proposition; Stone-Zsela, White, O’Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas (7 Justices). Direct majority support at the stated level of generality; no Marks aggregation.
- Existing findings do not eliminate the distinct inquiry on remand: Godinez v. Moran — Existing findings do not eliminate the distinct inquiry on remand, 1993-06-24. Thomas’s Opinion of the Court, this proposition; Stone-Zsela, White, O’Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas (7 Justices). Direct majority support at the stated level of generality; no Marks aggregation.

- [Iowa v. Tovar — Controlling proposition](../../OT2003/records/Iowa_v_Tovar_merits_2004-03-08.md#holdings), March 8, 2004; O’Connor for all nine Justices, for plea-stage information and the limits of mandatory warning content.

**Present operation effective:** Iowa v. Tovar, March 8, 2004, for the plea-stage waiver instruction; Godinez’s June 24, 1993 capacity and separate-waiver rules remain.

## Standards and Tests selection: One-year state-custody habeas filing period

### One-year state-custody habeas filing period

**Governing question:** The limitation period under amended §2244(d).

**Current rule:** A person in custody pursuant to a state-court judgment has one year to apply for habeas relief, running from the latest of: finality by completion of direct review or expiration of time to seek it; removal of a State-created filing impediment violating federal law that actually prevented filing; initial Supreme Court recognition of the asserted constitutional right, if newly recognized and made retroactively applicable on collateral review; or when due diligence could have discovered the claim’s factual predicate. Time during which a properly filed application for state postconviction or other collateral review of the pertinent judgment or claim is pending is excluded.

Proper filing concerns compliance with requirements for delivery and acceptance of the application, rather than the success or relief eligibility of its individual claims. Claim-relief bars therefore do not invalidate an otherwise proper filing. Clerk acceptance cannot cure a genuine filing defect. Form, deadlines, the proper receiving court or office, fees and filing preconditions must be distinguished from rules requiring denial of relief on a claim.

The state-proceeding limitation applies to both postconviction review and other collateral review. Pendency of a federal habeas application is not excluded under section 2244(d)(2), even if that application was timely and dismissed without prejudice for unestablished exhaustion. Accompanying civil-rights allegations do not convert it into state review. A later application's non-successive status and an earlier application's timely filing do not themselves make the later application timely; proper filing, actual pendency and relation to the pertinent judgment or claim remain independent conditions.

A properly filed state collateral application remains pending through timely continuation of the ordinary state-review process, including qualifying intervals before filing at the next available level. Successive-level original petitions qualify when they perform that review function. Completion, abandonment, an unreasonable interval or an unrelated later attack does not receive exclusion merely because another petition is possible. A state merits discussion does not override an independent valid untimeliness determination. Where the order is ambiguous, the federal court must determine its actual meaning, the applicable state timing rule, the supported explanation for the interval and elapsed time; lawful clarification may be used but is not invariably required. No national fixed deadline or automatic reasonableness presumption applies.

**Authority by component:**

- Four alternative latest-event triggers and pending-state-review exclusion: Source, authority and operative text, Public Law 104-132, Title I, §101, 28 U.S.C. §2244(d), enacted 1996-04-24; the Record’s retained complete enacted text supplies these components.

- Proper application filing distinguished from claim-relief bars: Canonical Decision Record `Artuz_v_Bennett_merits_2000-11-07.md`, Public Projection / Holdings / Filing an application is distinct from entitlement to relief on its claims, 2000-11-07; Ginsburg for all nine Justices.
- State-only collateral pendency, independent timing and preservation of actual alternative grounds: Canonical Decision Record `Duncan_v_Walker_merits_2001-06-18.md`, Public Projection / Holdings / 1. Federal habeas pendency is outside section 2244(d)(2); 2. The statutory error requires reversal, with actual independent alternatives preserved, 2001-06-18; seven direct joins to O’Connor’s opinion.

- Canonical Decision Record `carey-v-saffold_merits_2002-06-17.md`, Public Projection / Holdings / Timely ordinary state review includes its intervals; State timeliness and the actual federal calculation remain necessary; June 17, 2002; Ginsburg with Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Souter and Breyer (six direct joins).

**Present operation effective:** Carey v. Saffold, June 17, 2002, for ordinary-review interval pendency and state-timeliness limits. Duncan, Artuz and the enacted commencement triggers retain their earlier effective dates and distinct functions.

**Effective transition:** Enacted April 24, 1996. “Temporal reach of ordinary AEDPA habeas amendments” states Lindh's noncapital chapter 153 application-date construction, effective 1997-06-23. Separately directed provisions, chapter 154's express pending-case clause and Felker's successive-petition treatment remain distinct; Lindh does not settle every other Title I provision's application to pending proceedings. Authority: Lindh v. Murphy — The chapter 153 amendments at issue do not govern this pre-enactment noncapital application, Ginsburg's six-Justice opinion, 1997-06-23.

**Holdings navigation:** Lindh v. Murphy (1997-06-23) — The chapter 153 amendments at issue do not govern this pre-enactment noncapital application. [Artuz v. Bennett, 531 U.S. 4 — Habeas limitations and state collateral review](../../../state/HOLDINGS.md#artuz-v-bennett-531-us-4); [Duncan v. Section 2254(b)(2) permits, but does not require, merits denial of an unexhausted application.

## Standards and Tests selection: Court-directed mixed-petition elections and loss of timely review

### Court-directed mixed-petition elections and loss of timely review

**Governing question:** What protections apply when a federal court directs an unrepresented prisoner to choose how to dispose of a timely mixed habeas petition?

**Current rule:** When the court directs a choice between proceeding on exhausted claims and dismissal to exhaust the others, and its materials disclose a substantial risk that dismissal will prevent timely return, it must neutrally explain that dismissal without prejudice does not preserve the original filing date and that a later petition may be time barred. It must give a meaningful opportunity to delete unexhausted claims and proceed on exhausted claims under their ordinary requirements. The trigger is the court-directed election and apparent filing risk, not pro se status or complexity alone. The duty requires neither investigation of missing materials, exact deadline computation, claim evaluation, strategic advice nor a promised stay, and states a minimum rather than an exhaustive judicial duty.

Under §2244(d), failure to give required notice or another materially misleading judicial direction may support equitable tolling when it causes loss of an otherwise timely proceeding or prevents timely return and the petitioner proves reasonable diligence. The petitioner bears preponderance on the factual predicates, including causation and diligence during the relevant period and after the obstacle ends. Diligence is reasonable in the actual circumstances, not perfect diligence or anticipation and correction of the very misleading direction. Notice error alone establishes neither causation nor diligence.

The court must identify the interval needing protection and a lawful remedy that cures the entire timing defect, including any pre-election expiration, while excluding independently avoidable delay. Loss of a timely original and prevention of timely later filing are distinct remedial problems. A later omission need not physically cause earlier elapsed days, but the supported causal chain and lawful remedy must reach the actual expiration. Federal pendency cannot automatically be subtracted. Separate later and closed original actions do not automatically merge; any reopening requires an independently lawful predicate.

**Authority by component:** [Pliler v. Ford — Parts I–IV](../../OT2003/records/pliler_v_ford_merits_2004-06-21.md#holdings), June 21, 2004. Stevens, Stone-Zsela, Souter, Ginsburg and Breyer directly join Parts I–III; O’Connor also joins Parts II–III for actual materially misleading judicial directions, not omitted notice. All nine join Part IV’s no-automatic-relation-back rule.

**Present operation effective:** June 21, 2004; governs the pending dismissal consequences before the Court and later qualifying elections, without a new full limitation period.

**Exceptions and limits:** A lawful remedy must be legally available for the actual defect; factual causal loss alone does not establish availability of tolling. General mixed-petition stay authority and relation back after an independently established improper dismissal and lawful reopening remain unresolved. First-application classification does not establish timeliness, and the statutory state-pendency rules retain their separate conditions.

## Standards and Tests selection: Completing a timely fee application by relation back

### Completing a timely fee application by relation back

**Governing question:** May a timely application under §2412(d)(1)(B) receive its omitted substantial-justification allegation after the filing period?

**Current rule:** A timely fee application identifying prevailing status, financial eligibility, the requested amount and itemized attorney work may be amended after the thirty-day filing period to add the required allegation that the Government’s position lacked substantial justification, relating back to the same identified request. Demonstrated prejudice from the amendment defeats relation back. The allegation remains mandatory; completing it does not confer eligibility or fees automatically. Except as another statute specifically provides, §2412(d)(1)(A) requires reasonable fees and expenses for an eligible prevailing party other than the United States in a covered nontort civil action brought by or against the United States in a court with jurisdiction, including agency judicial review, unless the Government proves substantial justification or special circumstances make an award unjust. Substantial justification means justification to a degree that could satisfy a reasonable person.

**Authority by component:**

- Bounded relation back and distinct fee-entitlement conditions: Scarborough v. Principi, May 3, 2004, both holdings; Ginsburg with Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O’Connor, Kennedy, Souter and Breyer [Canonical Decision Record](../../OT2003/records/scarborough_v_principi_merits_2004-05-03.md#holdings).

**Present operation effective:** May 3, 2004.

**Exceptions and limits:** The rule permits completion of the omitted allegation, not expansion of substantive monetary coverage. Whether the second sentence’s allegation has the same deadline and whether an omitted first-sentence showing can be cured remain unresolved. Amendment prejudice, substantial justification and unjust special circumstances are different inquiries. The rule is not confined to unrepresented applicants or Civil Rule 15 pleadings.

## Standards and Tests selection: Authorized party expansion and independently qualified slate components

### Authorized party expansion and independently qualified slate components

**Governing question:** Whether a State may deny ballot access through a geographic party-name barrier or failure of candidates in a different electorate to qualify.

**Current rule:** A State may require meaningful support in each separate electorate. It may not categorically bar authorized candidates from expanding an existing party under its name, or disqualify otherwise qualified countywide and city candidates solely because candidates in a different district lack sufficient signatures. Protection against unauthorized appropriation of a party's name does not justify a geographic barrier that also prohibits authorized expansion; support requirements for one electorate do not justify invalidating candidates who qualified in their own constituencies.

**Authority by component:**

- Authorized name expansion, independent qualification, and separate-electorate support: Norman v. Reed — Ballot-access holding and operative rule, January 14, 1992; Souter's opinion joined by Stone, White, Blackmun, Stevens, O'Connor, and Kennedy.

**Present operation effective:** *Norman*, January 14, 1992.

**Open question:** Other district structures, cumulative burdens, and actual unauthorized name use remain outside this holding.

**Holdings navigation:** Norman v. Reed, 502 U.S. 279 (1992) — Ballot access and political parties.

## Standards and Tests selection: Write-in voting within the complete ballot-access system

### Write-in voting within the complete ballot-access system

**Governing question:** Whether a ban on write-in voting violates electoral-choice or independent expressive rights.

**Current rule:** Under the existing Anderson inquiry, the court assesses the character and magnitude of the actual injury against the State's precise interests and the extent to which those interests require the burden. Severe burdens require narrow tailoring to a compelling interest; reasonable, nondiscriminatory burdens ordinarily may be justified by important regulatory interests. A write-in prohibition is assessed within the complete access system by what the prohibition itself contributes to the asserted interests; the independent validity of surrounding filing, support and primary rules does not make it presumptively valid. When ordinary nomination routes close before voters can respond, the prohibition must leave an effective route for a genuine, legally eligible alternative candidate. The ballot need not provide an official count and publication of votes for fictional figures or arbitrary protest messages as an independent expressive entitlement.

**Authority by component:**
- Governing inherited framework: Burdick v. Takushi, “1. Review of election regulations follows the actual burden”, June 8, 1992; Stone, joined by Blackmun, Stevens, Kennedy and Souter (five), applying Anderson and the earlier simulated Norman rule.
- Complete-system assessment and effective-route requirement: Burdick, “2. Hawaii’s complete write-in prohibition leaves no effective route for a genuine alternative candidate,” June 8, 1992; same five Justices.
- Arbitrary-message claim: Burdick, “3. The ballot need not serve as an official tally of arbitrary political messages,” June 8, 1992; same controlling five-Justice opinion, with all nine agreeing in rejection of that component.

**Present operation effective:** Burdick v. Takushi, June 8, 1992, for the complete-system assessment, effective-route requirement and expressive boundary; the underlying Anderson framework is applied unchanged.

**Exceptions and limits:** No absolute write-in right follows, and no particular route, counting mechanism or ballot form is required. Valid qualifications, narrow anti-raiding and sore-loser rules and materially different systems remain open. The rule does not replace strict scrutiny of content-based campaign restrictions on public sidewalks.

**Holdings navigation:** Burdick v. Takushi, 504 U.S. 428 (1992) — Ballot access and political parties.

## Standards and Tests selection: Meaningful official recognition of a qualified party's consenting nomination

### Meaningful official recognition of a qualified party's consenting nomination

**Governing question:** Whether a State may categorically prevent a qualified political party from nominating, and having recognized on the official ballot, a consenting eligible candidate who also seeks or holds another party's nomination.

**Current rule:** Under the existing Anderson inquiry, denying a qualified party's legally operative nomination of its chosen, consenting candidate solely because another party also nominated him imposes a substantial, though not severe, burden on the party's candidate selection and on its members' ability to vote for that nominee under the party's name. Endorsement and campaigning outside the ballot do not replace the nomination the State has made legally operative. No new scrutiny tier follows; the State's precise interests must be weighed and genuinely connected to the burden. Ballot integrity, defined nomination choices and political stability are legitimate interests, but stability cannot mean preserving established parties' competitive position, and conjecture does not justify a categorical ban where consent, recognized-party status, clear labeling and single counting address the asserted concerns and the record shows no confusion, fraud, duplicate counting or splintered vote. When a qualified party genuinely nominates an eligible, consenting candidate, the State must provide a meaningful official means of recognizing that nomination. It may choose separate party lines or a single consolidated listing with the applicable party names, require the candidate's consent and prevent any ballot from being counted more than once.

**Authority by component:**

- Burden, fit and meaningful official recognition: Timmons v. Twin Cities Area New Party — II. The categorical ban is not justified as applied to this consenting nomination, 1997-04-28; Stone-Zsela, joined by Stevens, Kennedy, Souter and Ginsburg (five). Thomas, joined by O'Connor, Scalia and Breyer, dissents.
- Live controversy and the statutory consequence of affirmance: Timmons — I. The conditional enactment leaves a live controversy; III. Affirmance does not engage the enacted expiration condition, and no election is reopened, 1997-04-28; all nine Justices.

**Present operation effective:** Timmons, 1997-04-28.

**Exceptions and limits:** No right to a separate ballot line, multiple vote credit, a particular ballot format or separate party vote totals is recognized, and no party-specific counting rule is decided. No independent challenge to consent, counting, format, support or deadline rules is adjudicated. The holding concerns a timely nomination of a candidate who was not defeated in another party's primary; sore-loser rules, a required other-party consent, the distinct two-major-party prohibition and the independent validity of Minnesota's chapter 419 conditions are not decided. Whether a demonstrated threat to effective governing, connected to fusion, could justify a narrower limit is reserved. Completed elections are not reopened.

**Related current rules:** “Authorized party expansion and independently qualified slate components” protects authorized party identity; “Write-in voting within the complete ballot-access system” supplies the calibrated framework, and “Effective alternative-candidate route after early closing of nomination” protects a genuine choice of an actual candidate.

Twin Cities Area New Party (1997-04-28) — The conditional enactment leaves a live controversy; Timmons v. Twin Cities Area New Party (1997-04-28) — Affirmance does not engage the enacted expiration condition, and no election is reopened.

## Standards and Tests selection: Three levels of calibrated review of ballot-access burdens

### Three levels of calibrated review of ballot-access burdens

**Governing question:** How a court places a burden on candidate, party or voter access to the ballot within Anderson's calibrated review, and the justification each level requires.

**Current rule:** Under Anderson–Burdick, the court weighs the character and magnitude of the actual burden against the State’s precise interests and the extent to which those interests require it, and explains where the burden falls on that single calibrated scale. A severe burden requires narrow tailoring to a compelling interest. A substantial burden requires an important objective and a demonstrated substantial relationship between that objective and the restriction’s actual reach; a burden may be substantial without proof that success is impossible, and a valid interest does not by itself establish a sufficient relationship. An ordinary burden imposed by reasonable, nondiscriminatory administration may be justified by important regulatory interests without proof of past disorder. The complete system remains the unit of analysis. A petition percentage sustained within one open system does not answer as a matter of law a pleaded claim that a later system’s combined operation imposes a substantial or severe burden; accepted allegations of that kind require factual development rather than dismissal.

For an otherwise qualified candidate actually unable to pay a filing fee without financial hardship, making the fee the exclusive route imposes a severe burden. The State must provide a realistically usable nonmonetary route or hardship mechanism satisfying the severe-burden standard. Reasonable hardship proof, fees for candidates able to pay, and independent lawful qualification or support requirements remain permissible; inconvenience or preference to avoid payment is not inability, and the alternative’s actual deadlines and conditions must preserve access.

**Authority by component:**

- Three levels, placement and the pleading-stage application: Canonical Decision Record `Cartwright-v-Barnes_merits_2003-02-25.md`, Public Projection / Holdings / Anderson review has three levels, and these cumulative allegations require factual development, 2003-02-25; Souter’s Part II, joined by Stone-Zsela, Stevens, Ginsburg and Breyer; five direct joins. Thomas, joined by O’Connor, Scalia and Kennedy, would affirm the access dismissal without reaching the statement of levels.
- Underlying calibrated framework: Burdick v. Takushi, “1. Review of election regulations follows the actual burden,” June 8, 1992; the calibrated framework accepted by all nine Justices.

- Actual financial exclusion and a usable alternative: Belitskus v. Pizzingrilli, June 10, 2004, Part II; Kennedy for all nine Justices [Canonical Decision Record](../../OT2003/records/belitskus_v_pizzingrilli_merits_2004-06-10.md#holdings).

**Present operation effective:** Belitskus, June 10, 2004, clarifies the severe-burden category for actual financial exclusion; Cartwright’s calibrated scale remains effective February 25, 2003, and Burdick’s underlying framework June 8, 1992.

**Exceptions and limits:** The levels describe the structure of Anderson’s calibrated scale, not a new tier, and they leave Part I’s distinct Article I classification unchanged: a genuine support petition is not an added congressional qualification. No universal five-percent safe harbor follows; Jenness remains authority for the complete system it sustained, and the statewide one-percent rule was not independently decided. Party-specific failure to qualify is evidence, not proof of severity.

**Related current rules:** Write-in voting within the complete ballot-access system; Effective alternative-candidate route after early closing of nomination; Meaningful official recognition of a qualified party's consenting nomination; Exclusive congressional qualifications and ballot restrictions; Compulsory outsider selection of official party nominees.

## Standards and Tests selection: Lemon test

### Lemon test

**Governing question:** How the Lemon test governs Establishment Clause claims.

**Current rule:** Lemon remains the governing Establishment Clause test, modified so that purpose, effects and entanglement are evidence within one inquiry: a specific prohibition or distinct doctrine controls when it resolves the conduct; an established permissible relationship defeats the claim on that ground; otherwise a legally sufficient unresolved allegation is decided through attribution and function under ordinary procedure. These are three routes within the updated Lemon test, not freestanding prongs, exemptions from review or an alternative test for uncertain cases. Clear cases apply that test; its former independent prongs do not remain a separate or fallback method. Government may not pay for worship, religious indoctrination or instructional work in which religious formation is inseparable from the publicly financed service, and parental choice does not alter the constitutional character of what government purchases; neutral eligibility does not cure an unconstitutional governmental expenditure. Courts examine the actual expenditure and the function it finances, treating labels such as voucher, parental choice or secular grant as evidence of a program's structure rather than answers. A religious institution may provide an independently identifiable secular service where the funded work is genuinely secular and enforceable conditions prevent public payment for devotional activity. Religious affiliation alone neither proves a violation nor establishes an entitlement to funding, and genuinely private benefits, private forum access and reliably separated secular services remain distinct.

For publicly administered equipment and library-book assistance, the actual funded instructional function governs. Actual financing of religious instruction or demonstrated religious use under recipient control as the funded instructional function requires correction; neutral allocation, public title or a secular label cannot cure it. Religious affiliation or location, control, fungibility, possible diversion and incidental savings, singly or accumulated, do not themselves prove forbidden financing. Reliably lawful secular use or an independently protected benefit cannot be condemned categorically because the object is equipment or a library book. Meek and Wolman lose force only to the extent their equipment-category reasoning conclusively condemns that relationship by recipient character or presumed diversion; unrelated holdings and applicable safeguards remain. ## Event

Kowalski v. Tesmer, No. 03-407. On writ of certiorari to the United States Court of Appeals for the Sixth Circuit; argued October 4, 2004; decided December 13, 2004. The en banc judgment, 333 F.3d 683, permitted attorneys to assert future indigent clients' constitutional rights and invalidated Michigan's appointment restriction. The question is whether these attorneys may assert the rights of unidentified future indigent clients seeking leave to appeal after guilty or no-contest pleas.
## Opinion Topology

STONE-ZSELA, C. J., delivered the opinion of the Court, in which O'CONNOR, SCALIA, KENNEDY, THOMAS and BREYER, JJ., joined. GINSBURG, J., filed a dissenting opinion, in which STEVENS and SOUTER, JJ., joined.
## Holdings

**Controlling rule.** Attorneys' anticipated representation of unidentified future clients does not establish the close relationship required to litigate those clients' constitutional rights when no concrete existing or specifically prevented representation is shown. Practical barriers faced by indigent litigants require serious consideration, but cannot by themselves supply that missing relationship.

**Authority.** Stone-Zsela's opinion of the Court, joined in full by the other members of the six-Justice coalition of Stone-Zsela, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Thomas and Breyer, controls this relationship holding and its remedy.

**Controlling explanation.** The Court holds that personal injury and entitlement to assert another person's rights are distinct. *Powers v. Ohio*, which requires personal injury, a close relationship and a hindrance to the right-holder's own suit, gives each inquiry independent work. These attorneys allege that Michigan's restriction reduced paid appointments in the regular strict-rotation appellate practice they intend to continue. The Court assumes their personal Article III stake for this decision; their claim fails on the separate relationship requirement.

*Caplin & Drysdale v. United States*, which involved an actual client, and *Department of Labor v. Triplett*, which involved known claimants and enforcement against the attorney, do not equate professional readiness with a concrete client connection. *Campbell v. Louisiana*, which involved a defendant and the excluded jurors connected to his indictment, and *Miller v. Albright*, which permitted a daughter's particular claim concerning her father's citizenship rights, likewise preserve the relationship inquiry.

The contrary position has force: the challenged restriction prevents appointments and makes the right to counsel difficult to assert without counsel. But the obstruction alleged here does not identify a particular representation prevented or burdened by the rule. The prospective-relationship permissions recognized in *Craig v. Boren* and *Singleton v. Wulff*, which turn on the particular relationship and impediment, do not abolish that inquiry for lawyers asserting unascertained future clients' rights. Because this relationship defect suffices, the Court need not decide the full hindrance question. The constitutional entitlement to appointed counsel is not reached because the attorneys fail the antecedent third-party requirement. Existing clients, concrete prevented representations and indigent litigants asserting their own rights remain governed by their applicable standards.
## Separate Writings

GINSBURG, J., dissenting, joined by STEVENS and SOUTER, JJ. The dissent would permit these attorneys' third-party route and return only for genuinely unresolved preserved matters within the limited review. A present-versus-future-client distinction does not adequately examine a law that prevents the very representation on which a claim depends. The attorneys regularly accept appellate appointments through strict-rotation rosters, intend to continue that practice, and allege reduced paid assignments caused by the challenged restriction. Taken under the governing pleading standard, those allegations support a concrete personal economic stake as well as the setting in which the prospective representation arises.

The dissent reads *Craig v. Boren* and *Singleton v. Wulff*, which permit sufficiently direct prospective relationships, together with *Campbell v. Louisiana* and *Miller v. Albright*, which require attention to the actual claimant connection and enforcement obstacle. Here the intended assistance and the constitutional right are directly linked: indigent applicants must assert a right to appellate counsel while proceeding without that counsel. ## Event

Rhines v. Weber, No. 03-9046. Argued January 12, 2005; decided March 30, 2005. On writ of certiorari to the Eighth Circuit, 346 F.3d 799. The question is whether a district court may stay a timely habeas petition containing exhausted and unexhausted claims while the prisoner seeks state-court exhaustion, and what limits govern that discretion. The court of appeals vacated the existing stay under its restrictive view of that authority.
## Opinion Topology

## Holdings

### Retaining a timely mixed petition during exhaustion

**Holding and operative rule.** A district court may stay a timely mixed petition while the prisoner exhausts state remedies if the prisoner shows good cause for not exhausting first, the unexhausted claims are not plainly meritless, and the record does not show abusive litigation or intentional delay. Good cause means a reasonable explanation in the actual circumstances, not an extraordinary-circumstances or procedural-default test; the court must set reasonable, definite deadlines for seeking state relief and returning to federal court, accounting for delays outside the prisoner’s control.

**Authority.** Chief Justice Stone-Zsela and Justices Stevens, O’Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer join the complete framework. Justice Souter joins the existence of stay power and the merit, anti-abuse and deadline limits, but not the initial good-cause prerequisite.

**Controlling explanation.** Rose v. Lundy gives state courts the first opportunity to decide federal claims. Congress’s exhaustion and limitations rules can serve that purpose while a federal court retains a timely filed action. Landis v. North American Co. supplies ordinary control over pending cases, exercised here within the habeas statutes. Duncan v. Walker excludes time spent in federal court from statutory tolling under §2244(d)(2); retention preserves an existing action rather than supplying a new filing period. Pliler v. Ford left this stay question open while establishing separate protections against particular court-caused filing losses.

The warden correctly argues that routine, open-ended stays would weaken finality and reward premature filing. Cause, a potential-merit screen, exclusion of abuse and definite deadlines answer that objection. Good cause preserves an incentive to exhaust before filing and permits an individualized explanation. Its reasonable-explanation meaning also avoids turning technical uncertainty about exhaustion into a second, rigid procedural-default obstacle. Section 2254(b)(2) permits denial of an unexhausted claim on its merits; it does not compel denial. Relief remains subject to exhaustion, absence of an available state corrective process, circumstances making that process ineffective to protect the applicant’s rights, or counsel’s express waiver of exhaustion for the State, as the statute provides.

Rhines filed with more than eleven months remaining before limitations expired during federal processing. The district court identified eight unexhausted claims and imposed sixty-day initiation and return conditions. Those facts establish the practical importance of stay authority. Actual cause, potential merit, delay and the reasonableness of those conditions require findings under the announced standard.

### Protecting timely review when applying that discretion

**Holding and operative rule.** Where good cause, potential merit and absence of intentional delay are established and dismissal would defeat timely federal review, denying a stay and dismissing the entire petition ordinarily abuses discretion; the court should retain the action, and any refusal requires a reason compatible with the statute. If a stay is inappropriate, the prisoner must be allowed to delete unexhausted claims and proceed on exhausted claims when whole-petition dismissal would unreasonably impair federal relief.

**Authority.** All nine Justices join this sufficient case for retention and the deletion protection. Justice Souter’s agreement does not make good cause a necessary condition in his separate view.

**Controlling explanation.** The exhaustion requirement directs claims first to the state courts; it does not require destroying a timely federal action whenever the two processes overlap. The filing date and continued pendency therefore matter independently of statutory tolling. A refusal must apply the governing predicates and account for its actual effect on federal review. If retention is unsuitable, deletion preserves the exhausted claims without demanding adjudication of the others. Pliler’s neutral-warning duty retains its particular triggers—an unrepresented prisoner and a qualifying court-directed dismissal election. Rhines had appointed counsel for his amended federal petition, so that duty does not automatically govern his election.
## Separate Writings

Justice Souter, joined by Justices Ginsburg and Breyer, would omit an initial good-cause requirement and focus on demonstrated intentional delay. Exhaustion can be difficult even for courts, and prisoners often lack legal training; a further threshold dispute may consume time without improving protection against abuse. He accepts retention power, the plainly-meritless screen, anti-abuse limits, reasonable deadlines and the judgment. Justices Ginsburg and Breyer share that practical preference while also accepting and joining the Court’s governing reasonable-explanation requirement.

Chief Justice Stone-Zsela joins the Court and would expressly require the prisoner to establish the predicate factual showing by the ordinary civil standard. That showing concerns justification and the procedural conditions for retaining the case, with the merits inquiry remaining whether the claims are plainly meritless.

## Event

Pace v. DiGuglielmo, No. 03-9627. Argued February 28, 2005; decided April 27, 2005 (2005-04-27). Pace filed his second state collateral petition on November 27, 1996. The trial court denied relief on other grounds; the Superior Court held on December 3, 1998 that the petition was untimely and no exception was alleged and proved. State review ended July 29, 1999. The federal application followed December 24, 1999.
## Opinion Topology

SCALIA, J., delivered the opinion of the Court, joined in full by Chief Justice Stone-Zsela and O'Connor, Kennedy and Thomas. STEVENS, J., filed a dissenting opinion, in which SOUTER, GINSBURG and BREYER, JJ., joined.

## Holdings

The five Justices joining the Court's opinion adopt both holdings and their controlling explanations.

### 1. An exception-bearing deadline remains a genuine filing condition

**Rule and authority.** A state collateral application rejected under an applicable application-filing deadline is not properly filed under §2244(d)(2) merely because that deadline contains exceptions the application does not satisfy. Federal courts identify the operative state rule and its authoritative application, distinguish rules directed solely at claim relief, and require the applicant to establish the dates and qualifying state pendency supporting the claimed exclusion; Part I controls that inquiry.

**Controlling explanation.** Artuz separates conditions governing an application's delivery and acceptance from rules governing relief on its claims. A filing condition can require judicial evaluation; its function, rather than a clerk's acceptance, controls. Exceptions supply additional permissible filing routes.

Pennsylvania's §9545(b)(1) requires any petition, including a second or later petition, within one year of finality unless the petition alleges and the petitioner proves one of three grounds: prior failure to present the claim resulted from governmental interference violating Pennsylvania or federal constitutional or statutory law; predicate facts were unknown and could not have been ascertained through due diligence; or a constitutional right was recognized after the ordinary period by this Court or Pennsylvania's Supreme Court and held retroactive by that recognizing court. Appointed and retained defense counsel are excluded from government officials. An exception petition must also be filed within sixty days after the claim could have been presented. Finality follows completion of direct review, including discretionary review in either Supreme Court, or expiration of review time. For judgments already final on January 16, 1996, the amendments' transition permits a first petition within one year of that effective date. Pace's petition was his second.

These provisions govern filing, separately from §9543's relief eligibility. The state appellate court held that Pace satisfied neither the deadline nor an exception. Carey gives effect to that determination despite the trial court's earlier merits discussion. The November 27, 1996–July 29, 1999 state-review interval therefore supplies no statutory exclusion.

Pace answers that claim-dependent exceptions resemble Artuz's relief bars and that exhaustion can consume federal time before the State decides timeliness. But legal and factual adjudication can enforce a filing prerequisite. Congress requires both proper filing and pendency; the latter cannot replace the former. The applicant must establish the dates and qualifying state pendency supporting the claimed exclusion.

### 2. The asserted equitable protection fails on reasonable diligence

**Rule and authority.** Assuming the broader equitable authority invoked here exists, the prisoner must establish an extraordinary impediment and reasonable diligence in pursuing the rights for which protection is sought. Part II holds that Pace's supported course of pursuit fails the required diligence showing for this asserted interval and therefore denies that protection, while preserving Pliler's existing rule for proved court-caused loss.

**Controlling explanation.** Pace attributes the lost federal time to the combination of uncertain Pennsylvania timing law and circuit law requiring exhaustion. That explanation concerns pursuit of the second state petition. It does not establish reasonable pursuit across the preceding years or the five months after state review ended.

The sentencing and plea grounds concerned facts available in 1986; Pace raised a version of the plea claim that year. The assistance ground concerned events in or before 1991. His first collateral proceeding ended in September 1992; the second began November 27, 1996. The family affidavits concerned a meeting with Pace and counsel in 1985 or 1986. Their old subject matter does not itself establish when those affidavits were signed or received. State review ended July 29, 1999, and federal filing followed December 24. The asserted trap does not account for the material gaps in pursuing the available grounds. The established chronology and asserted obstacle permit resolution of diligence without a further factual remand.

Diligence means reasonable pursuit in the circumstances, rather than the fastest conceivable action. The earlier years inform that inquiry; they are not charged against a federal limitation before its enactment. Pliler protects proved court-caused loss through the required causation, diligence and lawful remedy. Pace identifies no corresponding court-directed dismissal election or materially misleading federal direction. Rhines permits retention of a timely mixed petition under its reasonable-explanation, potential-merit, anti-abuse and deadline conditions. Diligence here is assessed against the procedures available when Pace acted, without imposing a later protective-filing duty retroactively.

The Court rests on the unmet diligence requirement, rather than the Third Circuit's capital/noncapital distinction. This record-specific conclusion neither makes five months invariably excessive nor resolves whether the asserted legal uncertainty was an extraordinary impediment. The full range of equitable authority beyond Pliler's established protection remains outside the grounds and facts necessary to resolve this application.
## Separate Writings

**Stevens, joined by Souter, Ginsburg and Breyer, dissenting.** The state court conclusively determines whether state law permits relief, but that conclusion does not alone classify the application under the federal phrase properly filed. Artuz's distinction turns on how the rule operates. Governmental interference, previously unknown facts and later retroactive constitutional rights require judicial examination of the claims and their history. The dissent therefore treats this exception-bearing regime as comparable to a claim-relief limitation after an application reaches the proper tribunal in the required form.

The explicit filing command, separate statutory placement and concern about prolonging federal time do not, in the dissent's view, displace that functional inquiry. Genuine delivery, tribunal, form and fee requirements and actual pendency remain necessary. Carey addressed timely continuation through a gap in state review; it did not settle Artuz's reserved exception question for an application already lodged. Duncan's distinction between federal and state pendency remains intact. A contrary reading allows a later state timeliness ruling to consume the federal opportunity while the prisoner pursues exhaustion.

These Justices would exclude November 27, 1996 through July 29, 1999, remand for the exact federal calculation, and apply the remaining exhaustion, default and merits requirements. They accept the state untimeliness determination and reach no independent equitable-tolling conclusion because their statutory disposition makes that inquiry unnecessary.

Dodd v. United States, No. 04-5286. Merits decision June 20, 2005; argued March 22, 2005. On writ of certiorari to the United States Court of Appeals for the Eleventh Circuit. The question is whether the year under 28 U.S.C. §2255 paragraph 6(3) begins with this Court’s initial recognition of the right or a later decision making the right retroactively applicable.

Dodd moved on April 4, 2001, to set aside his continuing-criminal-enterprise conviction under Richardson v. United States, decided June 1, 1999, which requires jury unanimity on the specific predicate violations. The District Court dismissed the motion as untimely and rejected equitable tolling. The Eleventh Circuit affirmed the initial-recognition clock. Dodd invokes that court’s April 19, 2002 retroactivity decision in Ross.
## Opinion Topology

THOMAS, J., delivered the opinion of the Court, in which Stone-Zsela, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy joined. STEVENS, J., filed a dissenting opinion, in which SOUTER, GINSBURG, and BREYER, JJ., joined.

## Holdings

### Initial recognition starts the special filing year

**Operative rule.** Under §2255 paragraph 6(3), the one-year limitation runs from the Supreme Court’s initial recognition of the asserted right, provided the right is newly recognized and made retroactively applicable on collateral review. A later retroactivity determination does not restart that year; Richardson initially recognized the right Dodd asserts on June 1, 1999, making his April 4, 2001 motion untimely under this trigger even assuming its retroactive qualification.

**Authority.** The complete Court opinion is joined by Stone-Zsela, O’Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, and Thomas.

**Controlling explanation.** The statute names a date and then limits the rights eligible to use it. Treating the retroactivity condition as a second date replaces the event Congress selected. Both conditions must be satisfied for paragraph 6(3) to apply, but satisfying them does not change the initial-recognition date. Richardson supplies that date and the predicate-unanimity right at issue.

Paragraph 6 uses the latest independently qualifying event. Clay v. United States supplies the separate finality rule, and the factual-discovery provision has its own due-diligence condition. Those provisions do not turn a later legal retroactivity decision into a new recognition event. The Court assumes the collateral qualification in Dodd’s favor because the timing defect is independently decisive.

The contrary reading responds to a serious practical concern: the year may expire before retroactivity is established. That concern is especially acute for a successive motion, whose separate authorization provision requires a previously unavailable new constitutional rule made retroactive by this Court. Tyler v. Cain permits express retroactivity or holdings whose necessary propositions jointly compel it; eligibility can therefore be established through existing holdings. But that possibility does not guarantee that every right will qualify within a year. The statute’s strict interaction does not authorize a judicially substituted clock.

The general availability of equitable tolling is outside the granted timing question. The identity of the court competent to establish paragraph 6(3)’s retroactivity qualification, and the predicate-instruction merits, likewise need not be decided to resolve that question on the assumed qualification.

**Precedent treatment.** Richardson is applied to recognition; Clay retains its distinct finality function; Tyler is distinguished as a retroactivity-eligibility rule rather than a timing reset.
## Separate Writings

**Stevens, joined by Souter, Ginsburg, and Breyer, dissenting.** The dissent would read the new-right and retroactivity conditions together so that the special year begins when both are satisfied. A period that predictably can be consumed before the collateral qualification exists frustrates the opportunity Congress supplied. The completed-condition reading is textually permissible because the subordinate clause describes the qualifying right and can identify the point at which the provision becomes usable. The ordinary concern for a complete claim and the statutory structure support that construction.

Tyler’s multiple-holdings route can establish retroactivity and has been applied to the confined Cage rule, but it does not ensure prompt qualification of every newly recognized right. The dissent’s concern remains the short year expiring before a qualifying collateral route is available, particularly in combination with successive-motion authorization. It would reverse the timing dismissal and remand for any remaining qualification and merits questions. The shared ground rests on the assumed qualification in this case and does not resolve which court may make the retroactivity determination.

## Holdings

### Protected association and the governing inquiry

**Holding and operative rule:** A qualified party's invitation and an otherwise eligible voter's acceptance are protected political association even when the voter retains another party registration. A court must assess the actual burden, the State's precise interests and their fit under Anderson and Burdick: severe burdens require compelling justification and narrow tailoring; lesser burdens still require a sufficient regulatory connection.

**Authority:** Part I, joined by all nine Justices, directly controls.

**Controlling explanation:** Participation in an official nomination is legally effective association. Permission to campaign or endorse outside that process does not replace it. Timmons protects a consenting party's meaningful nomination choice; Tashjian protects invitations to independents while leaving other-party registrants for decision. Jones protects an objecting party against compelled outsiders. These decisions require attention to the parties' actual choices and the restriction's operation. Registration remains an administrable way to define an electorate, but its label does not eliminate the association that it burdens. The State may preserve a single nominating vote and genuine election administration through rules fitted to those purposes.

### The continued-registration exclusion lacks sufficient justification

**Holding and operative rule:** Oklahoma may not exclude an otherwise qualified voter from a consenting party's primary solely because the voter retains another party registration when the State has not shown a sufficient regulatory need for that exclusion. On this record the burden is substantial but not severe, and the categorical disaffiliation condition fails contextual review without automatic strict scrutiny.

**Authority:** Part II, joined by Stone-Zsela, Stevens, Kennedy, Souter and Ginsburg, supplies five direct joins.

**Controlling explanation:** Timmons requires a real connection between an electoral burden and the State's asserted stability interests. The trial findings rejected confusion, insuperable administrative difficulty and protection of the inviting Libertarian Party from its own choice. The legitimate interests in identifiable parties, orderly nominations and other parties' continuity remain, but the evidence does not connect this additional exclusion adequately to their preservation. The close-primary statistic shows that invited votes could change results; it does not demonstrate disorganization or corrupted nominations. Other parties may exclude unwanted voters from their own primaries, but that protection does not entitle them to prevent their registrants' willing association elsewhere. The dissent properly recognizes the legal significance of nomination and the ease of a registration choice. Those considerations do not explain why an exclusive primary vote and ordinary verification are insufficient here, or why continuing affiliation itself establishes a threat. The Court therefore affirms the constitutional judgment on the existing record while rejecting the appellate court's severe-burden classification.

### Prospective relief follows the common exclusion

**Holding and operative rule:** The prospective decree must protect qualified inviting parties and their otherwise eligible invited voters against the adjudicated categorical exclusion; Oklahoma may obtain narrower party-specific relief only by showing that it fully cures that same policy defect. The decree must preserve an unwilling party's choice, a voter's single primary vote, lawful deadlines, ordinary verification and independently valid eligibility conditions.

**Authority:** Part III, joined by the five Justices supporting Part II, directly controls the remedy.

**Controlling explanation:** Anderson v. Spear requires relief that corrects the unlawful operation for those governed by the policy unless the State establishes a fully effective narrower cure. Here the common defect is the demand to surrender an existing registration before accepting a qualified party's invitation. Naming only the plaintiffs does not itself establish that a smaller decree will cure it. Election officials must accept timely invitations and administer a single-primary choice without recreating the forbidden exclusion. ### 2. A public school cannot outsource its own devotional instruction

McCollum and Schempp establish the public-school instructional prohibition, while Lee and Santa Fe show why private delivery or an opportunity to abstain does not erase official sponsorship.
