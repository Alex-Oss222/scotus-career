### Riggins v. Nevada, No. 90-8466

Merits decision, May 18, 1992

#### Chronology and Posture

Argued January 15, 1992; decided May 18, 1992. On writ of certiorari under 28 U.S.C. §1257 to the Nevada Supreme Court, 107 Nev.178, 808 P.2d535 (March 28, 1991), which affirmed murder and robbery convictions and a death sentence. The granted question concerns forced medication through trial and the Sixth and Fourteenth Amendment right to a full and fair trial.

Chief Justice Alex-Lamar Stone-Zsela and Justices White, Blackmun, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter and Thomas participated at argument and decision.

#### Judgment

The Court reverses, vacates the convictions and dependent sentence, and remands for a new trial.

| Judgment component | Disposition and vote | Supporting Justices | Opposing Justices | Remedy or remand |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Murder and robbery convictions and dependent death sentence | Reversed and vacated, 7–2 | Stone-Zsela, White, Blackmun, Stevens, O'Connor, Kennedy, Souter | Scalia, Thomas | New trial if the State proceeds. Any renewed involuntary medication requires constitutionally adequate contemporaneous justification. |
| Fifth Amendment demeanor/self-incrimination theory | Not reached; all nine agree | Stone-Zsela, White, Blackmun, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas | No Justice opposes nonreach | No independent Fifth Amendment holding; the preserved medication and fair-trial ground resolves the case. |
| Competency-waiver question | Not reached; all nine agree | Stone-Zsela, White, Blackmun, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas | No Justice opposes nonreach | No ruling authorizing trial while incompetent or resolving waiver of competence. |

The distinct unpreserved Eighth Amendment claim is not decided. No finding that Riggins was incompetent, no fixed treatment instruction and no categorical medication prohibition accompanies the new-trial order.

#### Opinion Topology

| Writing | Author | Joined by | Relationship to judgment | Scope joined |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Opinion of the Court | O'Connor | Stone-Zsela, White, Blackmun, Stevens, Souter | Reversal and new-trial mandate | Parts I–IV: record and scope, justification rule, trial-risk holding and mandate; six Justices support these parts |
| Concurrence | Stone-Zsela | No other Justice | Joins the Court | Additionally applies Chapman's harmless-beyond-a-reasonable-doubt burden |
| Concurrence in the judgment | Kennedy | No other Justice | Joins reversal and new-trial judgment | Distinct extraordinary-showing protection for trial participation |
| Dissent | Thomas | Scalia | Would affirm | Parts I, II-B and II-C: distinction between personal-liberty remedies and proven trial unfairness |
| Dissent | Thomas | No other Justice | Would affirm | Part II-A: additional objections about proof of involuntariness and the preservation and scope of an independent liberty claim |

#### Controlling Propositions and Authority

##### 1. The State must justify antipsychotic medication continued over objection

**Controlling proposition:** Once a pretrial detainee objects to continued antipsychotic medication, the State must establish medical appropriateness and a need sufficient to override the protected refusal interest, considering reasonable less intrusive means. The unexplained order here supplies neither an essential justification nor findings about alternatives and cannot authorize the compelled treatment through trial.

**Authority:** O'Connor's Part II, joined by Stone-Zsela, White, Blackmun, Stevens and Souter: six of nine participating Justices adopt this rule and application.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that clinical acceptability does not itself establish legal authority to override refusal. Washington v. Harper recognizes the substantial liberty interest affected by unwanted antipsychotic medication and permits justified treatment of dangerous, mentally ill prisoners. An accused awaiting trial retains the protected refusal interest recognized in Harper. Riggins initially sought treatment, but continued administration became involuntary after his objection. Medical appropriateness is accepted for this review; necessity is not. The hearing contained testimony that he could remain competent without Mellaril and questions about the high dosage. The trial court's unexplained denial establishes neither an essential safety need nor that less intrusive means could not permit adjudication. The State's legitimate interest in a competent trial cannot replace those missing determinations. The Court does not impose a new documentary ritual, universal proof quantum or strict-scrutiny formula. Medically appropriate treatment essential to safety after considering alternatives remains possible. The complete conditions for competency-restoration medication, and whether a defendant may insist on becoming incompetent, remain undecided.

**Precedent treatment:**

- Washington v. Harper, 494 U.S. 210 (1990): applied for the protected refusal interest and requirement of overriding justification and medical appropriateness; its convicted-prisoner procedures do not automatically govern trial medication.
- Illinois v. Allen, 397 U.S. 337 (1970): relied on for the State's legitimate trial interest and the possibility of necessary courtroom measures; no comprehensive competency-medication exception is adopted.

##### 2. This unsupported medication order requires a new trial

**Controlling proposition:** When unjustified compelled antipsychotic medication through trial creates a supported, substantial risk of impairing the defense that the record cannot reliably reconstruct, the defendant need not demonstrate exactly how an unmedicated trial would have differed. The risk here extends beyond visible demeanor to testimony, comprehension and communication with counsel; explanatory expert testimony does not cure it, and the convictions and dependent sentence must be vacated for a new trial.

**Authority:** O'Connor's Part III, joined by Stone-Zsela, White, Blackmun, Stevens and Souter: six of nine participating Justices adopt this record-specific rule and remedy. Kennedy concurs in the reversal and new-trial judgment on a distinct trial-integrity ground.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that this trial record cannot establish that the unsupported treatment left the defense intact. Illinois v. Allen and Estelle v. Williams recognize that compelled practices can affect an accused's trial in ways a transcript cannot measure. The doctors' testimony identifies a strong possibility that this dosage affected attention, communication and the substance of testimony, not merely the appearance the jury observed. Expert explanation may help jurors interpret demeanor, but cannot restore a conversation with counsel or a response that medication changed. The contrary demand for a precise account of the trial that would have occurred without treatment would therefore require speculation. Allen permits necessary measures to protect the trial, but this record contains no finding establishing an essential justification for this treatment. Reversal and a new trial follow from this combination of unjustified treatment and supported, unreconstructable trial risk. The Court neither finds Riggins incompetent nor decides that every medication error is structural. Renewed compelled treatment requires a constitutionally adequate contemporaneous justification.

**Precedent treatment:**

- Illinois v. Allen, 397 U.S. 337 (1970): applied by analogy to potentially prejudicial compelled trial measures and the need for an essential justification.
- Estelle v. Williams, 425 U.S. 501 (1976): applied by analogy to effects of compelled trial appearance that cannot be fully reconstructed from a transcript; its particular waiver disposition is not extended.

#### Current-Law Effect and Limits

Effective May 18, 1992, an objection to continued trial-related antipsychotic medication obligates the State to establish its medical appropriateness and overriding need with attention to reasonable alternatives. Unjustified treatment with the supported, unreconstructable trial risk present here requires reversal and a new trial. Harper remains governing in its convicted-prisoner setting; this decision adds the stated application for an objecting detainee at trial. No universal proof quantum, strict-scrutiny formula, structural-error category or comprehensive competency-medication test is adopted. Kennedy's extraordinary-showing standard and Stone-Zsela's express Chapman analysis remain noncontrolling supplemental grounds. The unpreserved Eighth Amendment sentencing-display issue and competent-defendant refusal that would produce incompetence remain open. Six direct joins resolve each holding; no Marks construction is necessary.

#### Separate Positions Relevant to the Decision

Kennedy concurs in the judgment. He would require an extraordinary showing that involuntary medication for competency presents no significant risk of materially altering the accused's reactions, participation or assistance to counsel. He doubts that the contemporary medical evidence often permits that showing; if competence cannot be restored without such impairment, lawful civil commitment rather than trial may be the available route. This proposed standard is not the Court's rule.

Stone-Zsela joins the Court and separately applies Chapman v. California, 386 U.S. 18 (1967): the State bears the burden of showing constitutional trial error harmless beyond a reasonable doubt, and the medication record fails that burden. His concurrence supplies no general structural-error classification, categorical ban on competency treatment or order to stop medically necessary care. The Court's holding remains the record-specific unjustified-treatment and trial-risk rule.

Thomas, joined by Scalia except in Part II-A, would affirm. They regard expert testimony and the competence finding as adequate protection of the trial, distinguish a refusal-interest violation remediable in a civil action from an established basis to reverse a conviction, and reject reversal based on possible impairment alone. Thomas alone also disputes proof of involuntariness and whether a separate Harper liberty theory was preserved and included in the grant; Scalia does not join those additional objections.

#### Mandate, Remedy, and Stage

Supreme Court merits review is complete. The case returns to Nevada with the murder and robbery convictions and dependent death sentence vacated, for a new trial if the State proceeds. Renewed involuntary medication must have a constitutionally adequate contemporaneous justification under the Court's holding; a new trial is not an order to withhold medically necessary treatment. The Court does not authorize trial of an incompetent defendant, fix a treatment plan, decide an unpresented sentencing claim, set a new date or retain supervision.

**End of entry: Riggins v. Nevada, merits decision, May 18, 1992.**

### Del Monte Dunes at Monterey, Ltd. v. City of Monterey, Ninth Circuit Nos. 94-16248 and 94-16313; district court No. CV-86-05042-CAL

Merits decision, June 16, 1997

#### Chronology and Posture

Del Monte Dunes at Monterey, Ltd. v. City of Monterey; Ninth Circuit Nos. 94-16248 and 94-16313, 95 F.3d 1422; district court No. CV-86-05042-CAL. On writ of certiorari to the United States Court of Appeals for the Ninth Circuit under 28 U.S.C. §1254(1). Certiorari granted January 13, 1997. Submitted and decided June 16, 1997.

Following repeated proposals to develop 37.6 oceanfront acres and the City's final denial, the owners obtained separate takings and equal-protection liability verdicts supporting one $1,450,000 award. The Ninth Circuit's September 13, 1996 judgment sustained the taking claim and the rejection of post-trial relief; it did not reach the City's preserved equal-protection objections. Further appellate and rehearing proceedings were held in abeyance pending this review by a separate appellate order. The grant itself did not remove appellate jurisdiction. The adverse substantive-due-process ruling was not appealed. The owners waived their additional-damages cross-appeal only if the judgment was affirmed.

The questions are whether this §1983 damages action carries a Seventh Amendment jury right on the disputed taking facts; whether the repeated denials can constitute a taking through complete loss of economically viable use or the then-governing substantial-advancement formulation; and whether damages are available through §1983 rather than only prospective or administrative relief. No separate equal-protection merits question is presented.

All nine Justices participated.

#### Judgment

**Affirmed, 5–4.**

**Supporting:** Stone-Zsela, Stevens, Scalia, Kennedy and Thomas.

**Opposing:** O'Connor, Souter, Ginsburg and Breyer.

The $1,450,000 award and denial of the City's new-trial motion stand within the questions reviewed. Affirmance activates the owners' conditional waiver of additional damages. No new award, permit, equal-protection merits judgment or reinstatement of the unappealed substantive-due-process claim is ordered. The opposing Justices would vacate and remand for independent judicial taking findings, with lawful use of the existing record and further consideration of preserved equal-protection objections if necessary to sustain the common award.

#### Opinion Topology

| Writing | Author | Joined by | Relationship to judgment | Scope joined |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Opinion of the Court, I–III | Stevens | Stone-Zsela, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer | Common grounds | Review posture, taking/exaction limits and damages availability |
| Same opinion, IV | Stevens | O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer | Statutory preliminary ruling | Section 1983's language alone creates no jury entitlement; Stone-Zsela does not join this ruling |
| Same opinion, V–VII | Stevens | Stone-Zsela, Scalia, Kennedy and Thomas | Supports affirmance | Constitutional jury right, specific factual allocation and supported verdict |
| Concurrence in part and dissent in part | Souter | O'Connor, Ginsburg and Breyer | Joins I–IV; would vacate and remand | Specific condemnation analogue, judicial taking determination and bounded further proceedings |

Every Court holding has a direct majority at its stated scope. No additional rule is derived by combining distinct writings under a narrowest-grounds rule.

#### Holdings

##### I. The final decision and the scope of review

**Holding and operative rule:** A definitive municipal decision fixing the permitted use of the parcel satisfies the final-decision requirement; later valuation does not make that decision provisional. This claim may proceed on the established absence of an adequate state compensation route for the challenged period, without eliminating any compensation prerequisite in other cases.

**Authority:** Stevens's Court opinion, I–III, is joined by all nine Justices; that common coalition controls Holdings I–III.

**Controlling explanation:** Williamson County requires a final application of land-use rules and an adequate-compensation inquiry. Suitum separates a definitive use decision from valuation and ultimate liability. The City's fixed position and the earlier finality proceedings establish the former here; the case proceeded on the stated compensation-route predicate. Neither finality nor the futility of another proposal proves a taking. The grant presents the taking, jury and damages questions, while the City's preserved equal-protection objections and the unappealed substantive-due-process ruling retain their distinct status.

##### II. A permit denial is not subject to Dolan's dedication burden

**Holding and operative rule:** The government must establish nexus and individualized rough proportionality when it demands conveyance of identified property interests as a permit condition. That special burden does not govern this ordinary permit-denial claim; the Ninth Circuit's contrary proportionality discussion supplies no independent ground to disturb the judgment because it was not part of the jury instructions and was unnecessary to verdict review.

**Controlling explanation:** Dolan protects the owner's interest against compelled dedication; it does not impose a government justification burden upon every unsuccessful development proposal. Prior dedication discussions do not change the action actually challenged. The jury instead received the Agins-based reasonable-relationship instruction and the complete-economic-deprivation alternative. Lucas requires proof of complete loss of economically beneficial use, after which the government must substantiate a background property or nuisance limitation that independently excluded the use; a newly declared environmental harm is insufficient by itself. Penn Central remains applicable to partial deprivation. Legitimate environmental aims, repeated rejection and a later sale price are relevant without conclusively proving or defeating either submitted taking theory. The Court does not replace Agins's received formulation with a new general rule.

##### III. Compensation remains available through the established municipal action

**Holding and operative rule:** Section 1983 permits compensatory damages for a proved taking attributable to a municipality's own qualifying policy or final decision, subject to the action's constitutional and procedural predicates. Recovery is limited to the legally attributable extent and duration of the deprivation, accounting for sale proceeds or other recovery to prevent duplication; prospective relief alone does not extinguish a completed compensable injury.

**Controlling explanation:** Monell requires the municipality's own policy or final decision and causation, rather than liability solely for employing an official. McMillian preserves the function-specific inquiry into the institution represented by a final policymaker; it supplies no new finding about an unidentified Monterey official. First English permits compensation for the actual period of a temporary taking. Franklin distinguishes available relief in an established action from creation of a new action or proof of loss. Those principles defeat a categorical limit to prospective or administrative relief without guaranteeing anticipated profits, treating ordinary permit processing as compensable, or dispensing with proof. The questions reviewed establish no basis for a second damages amount or a construction order.

##### IV. The statutory wording alone does not resolve jury entitlement

**Holding and operative rule:** Section 1983's provision for legal, equitable and other proper redress does not by itself confer a statutory jury right. Any jury entitlement in the present action must therefore be established under the Seventh Amendment.

**Authority:** Part IV of Stevens's Court opinion, joined by O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer: eight direct joins. Stone-Zsela does not join this statutory ruling.

**Controlling explanation:** Lorillard's inference from another statute depended on incorporated enforcement procedures as well as the availability of legal relief. Section 1983's remedial wording alone does not provide that answer. This conclusion leaves the damages action available and requires the separate constitutional inquiry; it neither decides that inquiry against the owner nor converts legal compensation into equitable relief.

##### V. This legal §1983 action carries the constitutional jury right

**Holding and operative rule:** A §1983 action against a municipality seeking compensatory legal damages for violation of federally protected rights sounds in tort and falls within the legal actions protected by the Seventh Amendment. Its legal character does not disappear because the particular federal right concerns property or because an inverse-condemnation remedy could otherwise exist; equitable-only claims, sovereign immunity and allocation of subsidiary tasks remain separate questions.

**Authority:** Parts V–VII of Stevens's Court opinion, joined by Stone-Zsela, Scalia, Kennedy and Thomas, supply five direct joins for Holdings V–VII. No vote from a separate rationale is needed.

**Controlling explanation:** Imbler, Carey and Memphis Community School District identify the tort character and compensatory purpose of §1983 relief. Curtis protects the jury in statutory tort actions seeking legal relief; Tull and Terry direct attention to the actual action and remedy. The present action seeks compensation for the municipality's alleged completed federal violation and proceeds on the absence of an adequate state compensation route. Calling the underlying injury a taking does not remove the statutory action's legal character. The contrary comparison to ordinary condemnation does not overcome that character here. The holding does not settle every inverse-condemnation proceeding, authorize damages against an immune sovereign, dispense with an underlying violation, or assign every subsidiary legal issue to a jury.

##### VI. The disputed tasks submitted here belong to the jury

**Holding and operative rule:** Under legal standards stated by the judge, the jury may decide whether this parcel retained economically viable use and whether this particular final denial, in its development history, bore the required relationship to the City's specified legitimate purposes. The judge determines governing law, the relevant property interest, legal legitimacy of the purposes and evidentiary sufficiency; the jury may not choose a constitutional standard or revise general land-use policy.

**Controlling explanation:** Markman separately examines the action's jury status and allocation of its tasks. Its assignment of patent meaning, including embedded technical and credibility issues, to the judge remains intact. This permit denial's practical effects and highly particularized application of the stated purposes present different work. The actual instructions use the received Agins reasonable-relationship formulation, not Dolan's proportionality burden. The absence of objection to that wording does not waive the City's allocation objection, which the Court rejects on its merits. The decision does not comprehensively decide the full validity or elements of every substantial-advancement formulation, general zoning challenges, all as-applied regulations or all condemnation proceedings.

##### VII. The supported taking verdict sustains the single award

**Holding and operative rule:** The City is not entitled to judgment as a matter of law or a new trial on the reviewed grounds because sufficient evidence supports both undifferentiated taking alternatives under the instructions actually given. The taking verdict therefore sustains the existing single $1,450,000 compensatory award without decision of the separate equal-protection theory.

**Controlling explanation:** The owners offered evidence that the combined beach/access, viewshed and habitat requirements left no feasible development area and that further proposals could not satisfy the City's fixed position. Their evidence also addressed compliance with the fifteen conditions, prior grading/restoration approval, favorable habitat evidence and the City's role in the access difficulty. Crediting that evidence and reasonable favorable inferences supports the complete-deprivation and reasonable-relationship findings without reweighing credibility. No substantiated antecedent title limitation independently requires the entire deprivation found. The sale to California for $800,000 above the purchase price is relevant to value and compensation, but does not conclusively establish a productive use during the earlier period or payment for its loss. Neither denial of the preferred housing project alone nor disagreement among environmental experts would suffice. Support for both taking alternatives avoids any need to decide the preserved equal-protection objections; the award is not sustained by assuming those objections were abandoned. Affirmance gives effect to the owners' conditional additional-damages waiver and leaves the unappealed substantive-due-process ruling untouched.

#### Precedent Treatment

- Williamson County is applied to finality and the record's compensation-route predicate; no general prerequisite is abolished.
- Suitum is applied to distinguish definitive permitted use from valuation and taking liability.
- Dolan is confined to demanded conveyances of identified property interests; its government burden is not extended to this ordinary denial.
- Lucas is applied with its complete-loss prerequisite and substantiated background-property/nuisance exception; environmental purpose alone neither proves the exception nor defeats compensation.
- Penn Central retains its contextual role for partial deprivation.
- Agins supplies the received substantial-advancement formulation relevant to the actual instruction; this decision does not comprehensively approve every formulation or general zoning application.
- Monell and McMillian preserve institutional attribution and causation as requirements separate from a proved federal violation.
- First English permits compensation for an actual temporary taking period without guaranteeing profits or a permit.
- Franklin preserves the distinction between an existing action, available relief and proof of compensable injury.
- Lorillard is distinguished because its statutory-jury inference depended on incorporated procedures as well as legal remedies.
- Imbler, Carey and Memphis Community School District supply the legal-tort and compensatory character of §1983; no immunity question is decided here.
- Curtis applies Seventh Amendment protection to legal statutory tort relief.
- Tull and Terry supply the action-and-remedy inquiry without making a money request conclusive of every allocation.
- Markman preserves separate action and task inquiries and its actual judicial patent-construction rule; it does not place every constitutional mixed question with the judge.

#### Law After Decision

Municipal §1983 compensation claims of the kind presented carry a constitutional jury right, with law and legally legitimate purposes supplied by the judge and these concrete factual applications entrusted to the jury. The existing verdict and award stand under the actual instructions. The statutory language alone establishes no jury right. Ordinary exactions, complete deprivation, partial deprivation, finality, municipal attribution and compensation remain distinct. No precedent is overruled.

#### Separate Writings

**Souter, concurring in part and dissenting in part, joined by O'Connor, Ginsburg and Breyer.** The closer analogue is the constitutional compensation proceeding in direct condemnation, which has no constitutional jury entitlement merely because money is sought. Section 1983 supplies a remedy for that right without changing the specific analogue. Markman requires a task-specific inquiry even within a legal action; these Justices would require the judge to determine independently whether a compensable taking occurred. They do not make disputed historical facts intrinsically legal, foreclose an advisory jury or require a new evidentiary trial where the existing record permits lawful findings and conclusions. They join the Court's ordinary-denial limit on Dolan, its other common-law propositions and the statutory-language ruling. Dolan's unnecessary appellate discussion is not an independent ground for their remand. They would preserve the separate equal-protection verdict but require resolution of the City's outstanding objections if necessary to sustain the common award. Their disposition would not activate the additional-damages waiver, order a permit or reinstate substantive due process; nor would it automatically extinguish the award.

#### Source Notes

The record and preservation account are supplied by the [Ninth Circuit opinion, 95 F.3d 1422](https://openjurist.org/95/f3d/1422), including its distinct liability verdicts, single award, actual instructions, preserved objections and conditional cross-appeal. The published opinion does not fully describe the equal-protection evidence or every objection to that claim; the Court does not decide those merits. Submission and decision occurred June 16, 1997; no separate oral-argument date is established. The January 13 grant and separate appellate abeyance order define the review posture; no later rehearing event enters the decision.

The earlier authorities include [Tull, 481 U.S. 412](https://tile.loc.gov/storage-services/service/ll/usrep/usrep481/usrep481412/usrep481412.pdf), [Terry, 494 U.S. 558](https://tile.loc.gov/storage-services/service/ll/usrep/usrep494/usrep494558/usrep494558.pdf) and [Granfinanciera, 492 U.S. 33](https://tile.loc.gov/storage-services/service/ll/usrep/usrep492/usrep492033/usrep492033.pdf). Their different historical analogues and subsidiary-task rules are applied only at the scopes stated above. Allegations, trial evidence and reasonable verdict inferences are not converted into new appellate credibility findings.

#### Mandate, Remedy, and Stage

The appellate judgment stands. The ordinary mandate implements affirmance and the owners' conditional waiver of additional damages; the separately held appellate proceedings may conclude consistently with that judgment. No new trial, judicial recomputation, second award or permit is directed. The Court does not decide the separate equal-protection merits or reopen the unappealed substantive-due-process ruling.

**End of entry: Del Monte Dunes at Monterey, Ltd. v. City of Monterey, merits decision, June 16, 1997.**

### Glickman v. Wileman Brothers & Elliott, Inc., No. 95-1184

Merits decision, June 25, 1997

#### Chronology and Posture

Argued December 2, 1996. On writ of certiorari to the Ninth Circuit, whose June 27, 1995 opinion, amended September 18 and October 3, held the generic advertising assessments unconstitutional under Central Hudson's advancement and fit requirements.

The Agricultural Marketing Agreement Act marketing orders replace important aspects of ordinary competition with collective decisions about price, quality, quantity, inspection, research, packaging and promotion. Federally supervised producer committees assess regulated California nectarine, peach and plum growers and handlers for program expenses, including generic product advertising. Respondents remain free to advertise or criticize independently and are not identified as the generic message's authors. The question is whether these compulsory assessments violate the First Amendment and whether the Ninth Circuit applied the correct framework. The Secretary seeks reversal; respondents seek relief from the advertising assessments.

All nine Justices participated in the argument and decision.

#### Judgment

**Reversed and remanded, 6–3.**

**Supporting:** Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Kennedy, Ginsburg and Breyer.
**Opposing:** Scalia, Souter and Thomas.

The challenged assessments survive the First Amendment objection on this record. The mandate concerns the reviewed speech judgment; it does not reopen resolved or unreviewed statutory and administrative issues or itself order a collection award. Claims involving materially different false or ideological content, personal attribution, discrimination, interference with independent speech or stand-alone advertising remain outside the holding.

#### Opinion Topology

| Writing | Author | Joined by | Relationship to judgment | Scope joined |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Opinion of the Court | O'Connor | Stone-Zsela, Stevens, Kennedy, Ginsburg, Breyer | Reversal and remand | Entire opinion; integrated-program ground and express limits |
| Dissent | Souter | Scalia | Would affirm the First Amendment judgment | Entire dissent, Parts I and II |
| Same dissent | Souter | Thomas | Would affirm | Part I's compulsory-subsidy ground; not Part II's Central Hudson framework |
| Separate dissent | Thomas | Scalia | Additional speech-protection ground for affirmance | Part I: compelled financing does not lose speech protection |
| Same separate dissent | Thomas | No other Justice | Additional framework disagreement | Part II: rejects discounted Central Hudson review; retains the conditional lesser-test observation |

#### Controlling Propositions and Authority

##### Germane generic promotion may be funded as part of this collective marketing program

**Holding and operative rule:** A comprehensive agricultural marketing program may require regulated producers to finance generic, nonideological product promotion germane to its substantially collective economic functions when the message is not attributed to dissenting producers and they remain free to advertise and criticize independently. On this combination of facts, Central Hudson's framework for restrictions on a speaker's commercial message does not require invalidation of the assessment; common benefit or majority approval alone would not suffice.

**Authority:** O'Connor's opinion of the Court is joined in full by Stone-Zsela, Stevens, Kennedy, Ginsburg and Breyer. Six Justices directly adopt this program-specific ground, its qualifications and reversal with remand.

**Controlling explanation:** The orders already displace important individual decisions concerning price, quality, supply and marketing. Generic commodity promotion is a germane expense of that genuinely collective system. Abood and Keller distinguish permissible common-function charges from compelled support for unrelated ideological activity; Lehnert's germane-function inquiry supports the distinction, while Gibson preserves chargeable common activity and safeguards disputed nongermane dues. Respondents contend that advertising remains expression even when government requires payment rather than personal speech. The Court accepts that compelled funding can implicate expression without personal attribution or ideology. It nevertheless finds this integrated, germane expense permissible, rather than treating every expressive assessment as a restriction on respondents' own message. Respondents remain free to promote their products, criticize the campaign and speak independently; the advertisements do not identify them as authors. Hurley's protection of private expressive composition is therefore not infringed on these facts. Rosenberger expressly reserved compelled-fee questions and supplies no independent authorization for this assessment. Central Hudson, 44 Liquormart and Turner preserve scrutiny of supported restrictions on speech; they do not require an effectiveness trial for every germane administrative expense in this collective program. The Court makes no finding that generic advertising is superior to individual advertising. Its holding does not approve advertising-only arrangements in an otherwise independent market, ideological advocacy, false statements, viewpoint penalties, personal attribution or interference with independent expression. A materially different showing concerning those features would remove the factual basis for this disposition. The Court neither denies commercial and corporate speakers First Amendment interests nor holds that compelled funding is always ordinary nonexpressive regulation. It reverses only the reviewed speech judgment and supplies no ruling on unchallenged regulatory provisions.

#### Precedent Treatment

- Abood retains the distinction between chargeable common functions and compelled ideological support; the Court does not create a universal exception for commercially useful speech.
- Keller's germaneness principle informs this common expense without making the integrated-bar setting identical to agricultural regulation.
- Lehnert's common-function analysis supports the limited integrated-program rationale; shared benefit alone does not justify every public-relations charge.
- Gibson retains protection of disputed nongermane dues and the permissibility of germane professional-regulatory activity; its safeguards are not displaced.
- Hurley is distinguished because these assessments do not compel inclusion in respondents' own expressive product or make them apparent authors; its private-composition holding remains intact.
- Rosenberger leaves compulsory-fee challenges unresolved and therefore does not decide the funding objection here.
- Central Hudson remains operative for covered restrictions on commercial expression; its restriction-of-message framework does not control this combination of collective-program facts.
- 44 Liquormart retains its advancement, fit and commercial-speech holdings at their actual proposition-level scope; it creates no categorical rule against this germane common expense.
- Turner retains its cable-carriage justification and fit analysis without creating a general economic-regulation exemption or governing this distinct compelled-financing arrangement.

#### Law After Decision

The challenged generic advertising assessments are valid as germane expenses of this substantially collective marketing scheme on the stated nonideological, nonattribution and independent-speech facts. The decision does not validate stand-alone compulsory advertising or all expressive spending within a regulated industry. Compelled funding may still implicate speech, and materially different content, attribution, discrimination or speech restrictions require their own analysis. No precedent is overruled, and no new efficacy finding is made.

#### Separate Positions Relevant to the Decision

Souter dissents, joined by Scalia throughout and by Thomas on the sufficient compulsory-subsidy ground. Paying for expression remains a First Amendment burden even if the message is commercial, not personally attributed and not ideological. Abood, Keller and Lehnert justify charges through a legally required representative function, not every useful public-relations campaign. The program's regulatory breadth and shared commercial benefit therefore do not adequately justify this separate forced expressive expenditure. The dissent would affirm the judgment against the advertising assessments while leaving independently valid nonexpressive regulation untouched. In Part II, Souter and Scalia additionally retain Central Hudson's inquiry into substantial interest, direct advancement and reasonable fit, with the burden on government and without a least-restrictive-means or mandatory-study requirement. That framework discussion supplies no separate factual holding about this program's efficacy, geographic coverage or credits.

Thomas separately dissents. In Part I, joined by Scalia, he emphasizes that compelled financing does not cease to implicate speech merely because it uses money or promotes commerce. In Part II, joined by no other Justice, he rejects reduced constitutional protection and Central Hudson's discounted balancing approach. His observation that failure under a lesser standard would also establish failure under his stronger rule does not adopt that lesser framework or add a factual application absent from the record. He would affirm on the shared compulsory-subsidy ground, without prohibiting every compulsory regulatory fee or adjudicating unchallenged parts of the orders.

#### Source Notes

The lower ruling and amendments are reported in Wileman Bros. & Elliott, Inc. v. Espy, 58 F.3d 1367. The Agricultural Marketing Agreement Act, 7 U.S.C. §§601–624, and the marketing orders supply the regulatory setting. [The public record recited at 521 U.S. 457](https://www.law.cornell.edu/supct/html/95-1184.ZS.html) supports the background and chronology. The disposition rests on the collective structure, generic nonideological promotion, lack of attribution and retained independent expression. It makes no new finding about comparative sales effectiveness or detailed geographic and credit arrangements.

#### Mandate, Remedy, and Stage

### Timmons v. Twin Cities Area New Party, No. 95-1608

Merits decision, April 28, 1997

#### Chronology and Posture

Argued December 4, 1996. On writ of certiorari to the Eighth Circuit, Twin Cities Area New Party v. McKenna, 73 F.3d 196 (January 5, 1996). The court below reversed summary judgment for state officials and held Minnesota's old prohibition unconstitutional as applied to the New Party's nomination of Andy Dawkins, a consenting, supported candidate already seeking the Democratic-Farmer-Labor nomination. The DFL did not object. The grant concerns that minor/major-party combination under the First and Fourteenth Amendments, not the separate two-major-party prohibition. Minnesota Laws 1996, chapter 419, conditionally changed the statute and expressly makes reversal consequential.

#### Judgment

**Affirmed, 5–4.**

**Supporting:** Stone-Zsela, Stevens, Kennedy, Souter and Ginsburg.

**Opposing:** O'Connor, Scalia, Thomas and Breyer.

All nine reach the live controversy. The old prohibition may not be applied to the New Party's consenting nomination, and relief is prospective; Minnesota must provide a meaningful official means of recognizing such a nomination and may choose its form. Affirmance does not engage chapter 419's expiration-and-revival condition, so its amendments remain effective according to their terms. The completed 1994 election is not reopened, and no particular intervening filing or election is adjudicated.

#### Opinion Topology

| Writing | Author | Joined by | Relationship to judgment | Scope joined |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Opinion of the Court | Stone-Zsela | Stevens, Kennedy, Souter, Ginsburg; O'Connor, Scalia, Thomas and Breyer in I and III | Affirmance | Five join merits Part II; all nine join live-controversy Part I and statutory-consequence Part III |
| Concurrence | Stevens | Ginsburg | Joins the opinion in full | Adds that the stability interest Minnesota pressed describes the established parties' competitive position; treats the Court's reserved question as undecided |
| Dissent | Thomas | O'Connor, Scalia, Breyer | Would reverse; joins Parts I and III | Less-than-severe limitation on an additional nomination designation, justified by ballot integrity, defined nomination choices and a stable electoral system |

#### Controlling Propositions and Authority

##### I. The conditional enactment leaves a live controversy

**Holding and operative rule:** A challenge to the old prohibition remains live where the officials seek reversal of its invalidation and an enacted provision expressly revives that prohibition upon reversal. Chapter 419 supplies that concrete consequence; neither its replacement scheme nor completion of the 1994 election makes this dispute advisory.

**Authority:** Stone-Zsela, joined in Parts I and III by Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer: nine direct joins.

**Controlling explanation:** City of Mesquite distinguishes a genuine end to a challenged restriction from repeal that leaves its recurrence consequential, while keeping mootness separate from entitlement to an injunction. The Minnesota officials continue to contest the same party's asserted right, and chapter 419 conditions revival of the old law on the judgment they seek. That express condition makes adjudication affect legal rights even without finding a present stay or mandate recall. Norman preserves a continuing election challenge despite completion of the immediate election. The new scheme's existence is therefore relevant but not conclusive. Dawkins's earlier consent and the DFL's lack of objection do not establish compliance with every later written-chair-consent requirement. The Court does not decide the independent constitutionality of that replacement scheme or determine which law governed an unexamined intermediate filing.

##### II. The categorical ban is not justified as applied to this consenting nomination

**Holding and operative rule:** Minnesota may not apply its categorical prohibition to prevent this qualified minor party from timely nominating a consenting, eligible candidate, and having that nomination officially recognized on the ballot, merely because he also seeks or holds another party's nomination. In this application the burden on the Party's candidate selection and on its members' ability to vote for their nominee under its name is substantial but not severe, and the interests in ballot integrity, defined nomination choices and a stable electoral system, though legitimate, are not shown to require the ban under Anderson and Burdick. When a qualified party genuinely nominates an eligible, consenting candidate, the State must provide a meaningful official means of recognizing that nomination; it may regulate ballot presentation through separate party lines or a single consolidated listing, require consent and prevent any ballot from being counted more than once.

**Authority:** Stone-Zsela, joined by Stevens, Kennedy, Souter and Ginsburg: five direct joins in Part II.

**Controlling explanation:** Anderson and Burdick require attention to the character and magnitude of the burden, the precise interests asserted and the extent to which those interests make the burden necessary. Severe restrictions require narrow tailoring to a compelling interest; lesser burdens still require correspondingly weighty and genuinely connected interests, and no new scrutiny tier is created. A party acts through its nominations, and Minnesota has made the ballot nomination legally operative. It admits the New Party to the ballot but denies it the candidate it chose solely because another party also chose him, and voters who would support Dawkins as the Party's nominee cannot do so. Endorsement and campaigning remain, so the burden is not severe; they do not replace the nomination the State created, so the burden is substantial. Burdick protects a genuine choice of an actual candidate while rejecting compelled tallies of arbitrary messages. Norman bars categorical rules that erase authorized party identity beyond the State's concern, and Colorado Republican confirms that shared candidate support does not erase a party's independence. Ballot integrity, defined nomination choices and stability are legitimate interests, but none is shown to require this ban as applied. Dawkins consented, the DFL did not object, the Party met its support requirement and Dawkins was unopposed in the DFL primary, so no defeated candidate seeks reentry. The nomination adds no candidate and splinters no vote, and the record shows no confusion, fraud, duplicate counting or ballot congestion. Chapter 419 itself lists a multiply nominated candidate once with the applicable party names, which shows that ballot intelligibility does not require a categorical ban; the Court relies on it for that point only. Political stability cannot mean preserving the competitive position of established parties. Whether a demonstrated threat to effective governing, connected to fusion, could justify a narrower limit is not decided; Minnesota offered conjecture, not that showing. The holding recognizes no right to a separate line, multiple vote credit or a particular format, decides no party-specific counting rule, and leaves sore-loser rules, a required other-party consent and the two-major-party prohibition for cases presenting them.

##### III. Affirmance does not engage the enacted expiration condition, and no election is reopened

**Holding and operative rule:** Chapter 419 §10 makes its amendments effective for the 1996 state primary and thereafter, suspends them while the McKenna judgment is stayed or its mandate recalled, and provides that reversal causes the amendments to expire and prior law to revive. Affirmance does not engage that expiration-and-revival condition: the amendments remain effective according to their terms, and the old prohibition may not be applied to this nomination. Further proceedings under the affirmed judgment assume no unproved stay, recall or continuous intervening statutory regime.

**Controlling explanation:** The enacted condition links the state-law consequence to reversal, not to a finding about an earlier filing. Because the McKenna judgment is affirmed, that condition does not occur and prior law does not revive. Recognizing that consequence does not adjudicate the replacement provisions' independent validity, including their written-chair-consent and party-status counting requirements, or establish that their requirements were satisfied in 1994. The existing case asks whether the old ban may be applied to this nomination; holding that it may not supports affirmance and prospective relief. It supplies no basis to reopen the completed election or allocate rights in an unexamined intervening candidacy. Any such distinct question would require its actual filing facts and relevant stay or mandate history.

##### Statutory scope and limits

The challenged 1994 §204B.06 subdivision 1(b) barred an affidavit when another affidavit was on file for any office at the same primary or next general election. Section 204B.04 subdivision 2 barred petition nomination for the same office by a primary candidate, subject to two distinct exceptions. Under §204D.10 subdivision 2, if any major-party primary candidate received ten percent of the average vote for the party's state officers at the last state general election in the relevant district, its highest-vote candidates became nominees; if none met that threshold, none were nominated and all could seek petition nomination under §§204B.07–.09. The relevant state officers were governor, lieutenant governor, secretary of state, state auditor, state treasurer and attorney general. The §204B.13 subdivision 4 nonpartisan-vacancy exception allowed a petition within one week of the vacancy and no later than four calendar days before election; an eligible voter could sign despite voting in the primary or signing another petition. Neither exception applied to Dawkins. The distinct ban on nomination by two major parties is not decided.

The conditional replacement permitted a major-party and one-or-more-minor-party nomination for the same partisan office with written consent of the parties' state chairs; petition nomination also required the candidate's written consent. Losing the major-party primary forfeited the simultaneous minor-party nominations, and a party candidate could not also file as an independent. It retained the nonpartisan-vacancy exception, barred a defeated major-party primary candidate except to fill a §204B.13 vacancy, and repealed the old primary-turnout provision. Its affidavit rule referred to any other office, required petition acceptance and the consent forms, and contained a distinct dual-office exception for a soil-and-water district supervisor outside Anoka, Hennepin, Ramsey and Washington counties and a mayor/council or town-supervisor office in a city or town of no more than 2,500 people wholly or partly within the district. That different-office exception is not fusion. A multiply nominated candidate's name appeared once with party names; major/minor nomination used the major-party position and multiple-minor nomination the first filing party's position. Votes for multiply nominated candidates were excluded from the specified party-status qualification or retention counts. These features neither establish compliance in this case nor receive independent constitutional approval.

The Court recognizes no right to duplicate votes, separate party vote totals or a particular ballot design, decides no party-specific counting rule, and adjudicates no independent challenge to consent, counting, format, support, deadline or sore-loser rules.

#### Precedent Treatment

- Anderson v. Celebrezze supplies the actual-burden, precise-interest and fit inquiry.
- Burdick v. Takushi supplies calibrated scrutiny within the complete access system and protects a genuine choice of an actual candidate; its rejection of forced counting of arbitrary messages confirms that this genuine nomination is not a fictional vote.
- Norman v. Reed's protection of authorized party identity against categorical bars broader than the State's concern applies to this nomination; its continuing-election principle also supports reaching the controversy.
- Colorado Republican confirms that shared candidate support does not erase a party's independent identity; it does not impose campaign-expenditure scrutiny on ballot designations or itself decide fusion.
- Tashjian v. Republican Party of Connecticut protects a party's choices about its own association, including its choice of standard bearer.
- Eu v. San Francisco County Democratic Central Committee preserves party choice and expression; the State may regulate the official ballot's presentation of that choice without erasing it.
- Storer v. Brown supports orderly candidate-selection rules; a nominee unopposed in the other party's primary presents no defeated candidate's reentry, and sore-loser rules are not decided.
- Jenness v. Fortson preserves meaningful support requirements; a qualified party with a consenting nominee has met them.
- Munro v. Socialist Workers Party permits preventive regulation without proof of actual past disruption, but a substantial burden still requires a connection between the interest and the restriction.
- City of Mesquite v. Aladdin's Castle prevents automatic mootness where repeal leaves a concrete prospect of recurrence, while keeping injunctive entitlement separate.

#### Law After Decision

A State may not categorically bar a qualified minor party's timely nomination of an eligible, consenting candidate because the candidate also seeks or holds another party's nomination, absent a demonstrated justification. When a qualified party genuinely nominates such a candidate, the State must provide a meaningful official means of recognizing that nomination; it may regulate ballot presentation through separate lines or a single consolidated listing, require consent and prevent double counting. The governing constitutional method remains contextual Anderson-Burdick review, with no automatic strict scrutiny or new scrutiny tier, no actual-confusion premise and no permission to insulate established parties from competition. Affirmance leaves chapter 419's amendments effective according to their terms; their consent and party-status counting conditions, the two-major-party prohibition, sore-loser rules and a demonstrated stability limit remain undecided.

#### Separate Positions Relevant to the Decision

Stevens concurs, joined by Ginsburg. They join the Court's opinion in full. A willing, sufficiently supported standard bearer is a central associational choice, and endorsing him or choosing someone else does not replace the Party's legally operative nomination. They add that the stability Minnesota invoked, as pressed here, describes preserving the established parties' competitive position, an end that cannot justify excluding this consensual alliance, and that Norman, Tashjian and Eu require attention to the actual association rather than to general predictions. They do not treat the question the Court reserves as decided, and they recognize no right to duplicate votes, separate party totals or a court-designed ballot.

Thomas dissents, joined by O'Connor, Scalia and Breyer, who join Parts I and III. The Party's nomination choice is real association, not an arbitrary protest message, and endorsement is not literally the same legal act. But the rule excludes neither this actual candidate nor the Party from meaningful electoral participation; it limits an additional nomination designation within the State's selection machinery, a less-than-severe burden. Proportionate interests in ballot integrity, defined nomination choices and a stable electoral system justify it without proof of a past disruption in this alliance; Storer, Jenness and Munro support orderly, preventive selection rules. Norman barred unrelated obstacles to authorized expansion and independently qualified candidacies, and Colorado Republican protects independent campaign expression, a different mechanism. Consent and qualifying signatures do not compel the State to redesign its nomination structure whenever an alliance proposes a workable alternative, and the single listing the Legislature chose in chapter 419 is a policy choice, not a constitutional necessity. The dissent does not rest on voter confusion or proof of past fraud and approves neither suppression of competition to protect incumbents nor rational-basis immunity for ballot laws. They would reverse.

No new election or retrospective ruling on an intervening filing is ordered; no unproved stay or mandate recall is declared.

#### Chronology and Posture

The case concerns withdrawal in July 1991 of an accepted civilian staff-attorney offer because of a planned same-sex religious commitment ceremony. #### Controlling Propositions and Authority

##### I. Private association requires its own protection and employment inquiry

**Holding and operative rule:** The actual selective, enduring intimate relationship and its religious expression cannot be denied constitutional protection merely because they lack civil-marriage recognition or include an invitation-only ceremony. A public employer’s genuine operational interests remain relevant, but generalized forecasts, presumed lawbreaking and presumed government endorsement do not conclusively justify this defense summary judgment.

**Authority:** Part I of the Court’s opinion is joined by Stevens, Kennedy, Souter, Ginsburg and Breyer, five direct votes.

**Controlling explanation:** Roberts and Rotary distinguish close personal association from association for protected expression and require examination of the actual relationship and activity. On the plaintiff-favorable record, the selective, enduring relationship and its religious expression satisfy those protected predicates; their protection is not left open merely because the relationship lacks civil-marriage recognition. Shahar seeks protection against an employment penalty, not marital status or benefits. Umbehr separates entitlement to a benefit from an unconstitutional reason for withholding it. Jeffries requires attention to actual functions and institutional needs: prevention need not await disruption, but a reasonable forecast does not alone settle the legality of penalizing protected activity. Confidentiality, credible representation, assignment flexibility and professional trust remain substantial concerns. Neither a general office label nor speculation about endorsement establishes inability to perform lawful duties. No lawbreaking or refusal of duties is established, and later insurance or name-change evidence cannot supply contemporaneous knowledge. Waters protects reasonable factual reliance only when the substantive rule permits disciplining the conduct described and establishes no universal interview requirement. The remaining employment justification, causal application and relief return for adjudication; the Court orders no retention of a lawyer unable to perform necessary work.

##### II. Free exercise cannot be absorbed into a general employee-speech balance

**Holding and operative rule:** A public-employment decision alleged to burden sincere private religious exercise must be examined for neutrality and general applicability under its own governing rule. If the actual restriction is nonneutral or not generally applicable, the employer must establish a compelling justification and narrow tailoring; religious impact alone does not establish that trigger.

**Authority:** Part II has Stevens, O’Connor, Kennedy, Souter, Ginsburg and Breyer, six direct joins.

**Controlling explanation:** Smith preserves neutral, generally applicable rules. Lukumi requires examination of objective targeting and comparable secular conduct; Brown applies the required justification to religion-specific employment restrictions while preserving control of work, resources, coercion and official representation. The lower court assumed religious protection and treated the claim as resolved by the same general employment balance. That does not answer whether this withdrawal imposed the prohibited religious disability. The contrary argument is substantial: Bowers may have responded to the represented relationship and perceived job consequences regardless of religious form. That is an argument requiring proper assessment, not an established hypothetical fact that disposes of the independent claim. Sincerity does not prove targeting, and the absence of theological compulsion does not defeat sincere exercise. All six joiners reserve the trigger’s ultimate application and any properly supported justification. No finding of discrimination, general religious exemption or new statutory claim follows.

##### III-A. Conduct and hiring labels do not remove a sexual-orientation classification from equality review

**Holding and operative rule:** A conduct or hiring label does not remove an adverse civilian employment classification from equal-protection review. Government that disadvantages an individual because of sexual orientation, including through a nominal conduct rule operating as a deliberate status proxy, classifies by sexual orientation. Government that regulates actual conduct because of independently relevant professional obligations is reviewed under the applicable constitutional protections.

**Authority:** Part III-A has Stone-Zsela, Stevens, Kennedy, Souter, Ginsburg and Breyer, six direct joins.

**Controlling explanation:** Romer examines the actual disability, not its description, and Valot separates equality review from justification for penalizing protected activity and from a proved same-decision defense. A label cannot decide what a classification is. Conduct operates as a status proxy when it is penalized because it reveals or expresses orientation, as when comparable conduct is treated differently or the asserted concern attaches to a relationship’s same-sex character rather than to a professional obligation the conduct implicates. Conduct that genuinely conflicts with confidentiality, representation or assigned duties may be regulated on that ground, subject to whatever separate protections that conduct independently carries under other constitutional provisions. Walmer, Thomasson and Richenberg address military and conduct-related settings, not an irrebuttable civilian inference from a religious ceremony. Bowers v. Hardwick concerns its specified conduct; orientation or the ceremony alone proves no violation. The lower court’s conduct/employment characterization ended its equality analysis without asking which kind of action this withdrawal was. On the plaintiff-favorable record, the asserted concerns could be found to attach to what a same-sex religious commitment ceremony disclosed; no refusal of duties or lawbreaking is established. That question returns on remand.

##### III-B. Sexual-orientation classifications receive intermediate scrutiny

**Holding and operative rule:** Sexual-orientation classifications receive intermediate scrutiny. Once government is shown to have disadvantaged an individual because of sexual orientation, including through a deliberate status proxy, it must demonstrate a genuine, important objective and a substantial relationship between that objective and the actual classification; it bears that burden, and moral disapproval, private bias, stereotype and post hoc or merely conceivable reasons do not carry it. Supported professional judgment and reasonable contemporaneous predictions receive substantial but nonconclusive weight, and the standard requires neither least restrictive means, an individual exception in every case nor an entitlement to a particular position. The standard adopted here does not itself establish fair notice in July 1991; prospective constitutional validity and personal damages liability remain separate on remand.

**Authority:** Part III-B has Stone-Zsela, Stevens, Souter, Ginsburg and Breyer, five direct joins. Kennedy joins Part III-A and the equality vacatur but not Part III-B. O’Connor, Scalia and Thomas reject Part III-B and would sustain the equality judgment under ordinary rationality. Stone-Zsela joins Part III but would formally reverse the equality judgment.

**Controlling explanation:** Romer’s ordinary review excludes bare harm but accepts conceivable reasons, reasonable generalizations and imperfect fit, leaving the challenger to negate them. Walmer, Thomasson, Richenberg and this withdrawal show that allocation at work when government acts through homosexual conduct, statements, relationships or ceremonies: the label can let an orientation classification stand on grounds the government need not establish. Adarand’s common standard supplies the method: an important objective and a substantial, evidence-supported relationship to the classification actually used. Neither Romer nor Virginia already supplies a tier for this classification; the Court adopts this one. It is demanding, not an absolute prohibition. Confidentiality, professional competence, credible representation and workplace administration remain available interests, and prevention need not await disruption. But generalized predictions about gay employees do not show that a particular exclusion substantially serves them, and an assertion of deference is not a showing; experience may suffice without scientific certainty or formal findings. Bowers v. Hardwick’s specified-conduct holding is not reopened. The military decisions sustained under ordinary review are not reopened; their particular rules are assessed under this standard when it materially affects them. The summary judgment required no such showing.

##### IV. Causation, conduct-date immunity and relief remain independent

**Holding and operative rule:** Remand requires claim-specific constitutional predicates, personal responsibility, causation and lawful relief. Where retaliation analysis applies, protected activity must be a substantial or motivating factor and a same-decision defense requires proof by a preponderance; ordinary rationality’s conceivable basis is not that proof.

**Authority:** Part IV is joined by the five common Court-opinion Justices; O’Connor joins its application to the free-exercise remand.

**Controlling explanation:** Umbehr and Jeffries preserve actual causal responsibility and the counterfactual defense. Bowers pleaded qualified immunity, but neither lower court decided it and he did not request appellate affirmance on that ground. The Court does not award the defense, decide waiver or automatically revive it. Any procedurally available personal-damages defense concerns notice in July 1991; later Romer, Brown, Umbehr, Jeffries, Valot and Danahy cannot supply retrospective notice. Prospective relief, any proper successor, personal damages and official-capacity relief retain separate predicates. Later evidence may have a lawful remedial relevance without becoming contemporaneous motive. Elapsed time establishes neither mootness nor a continuing job entitlement. The mandate compels no interview, discovery plan, trial, reinstatement, backpay, fees or damages award.

#### Controlling Propositions and Authority

##### I. A-738 does not establish the necessary prospect of prospective relief for a current consular violation

**Holding and operative rule:** Even assuming the invoked notice duties were binding and violated, and applying the applicants' favorable formulation requiring a reasonable probability that four Justices would grant review, a significant possibility of reversal, likely irreparable harm and appropriate consideration of competing equities, A-738 does not establish the necessary prospect that the older consular duties require the requested execution restraint as relief for a current violation. A continuing consequence of past nonnotification is not by itself a continuing denial of these applicants' presently available consular rights; the distinct most-favored-consul notification theory does not itself establish the requested remedy.

**Authority:** Kennedy's Part I, joined by all seven other Associate Justices, supplies eight direct joins to this complete rule and application. Stone-Zsela independently agrees that the current domestic duty and remedial showing is insufficient, without adopting the complete explanation.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court considers Paraguay's own rights, not a supposed default of Breard's personal claim. Article 36 protects communication and access; consular notification must occur without delay if the national requests it, while advice to the national and forwarding covered communications must also occur without delay. It protects visits, correspondence and arranging representation, including after judgment, but requires consular officers to abstain from action on the national's behalf if the national expressly opposes that action. Its receiving-State-law clause is qualified by the requirement that those laws give full effect to the rights' purposes. Neither half may be omitted.

The most-favored-consul provision of the 1859 Treaty supports a distinct notification theory without the same request condition. Even accepting that theory for this inquiry, consular access has been available since 1996. The current presentation does not sufficiently connect the old omission to a present duty requiring this execution restraint.

Young supplies prospective officer relief for an ongoing federal violation. Seminole and Coeur d'Alene preserve that route within actual duties and remedies; they remove categorical immunity shortcuts without supplying this treaty remedy. Papasan and Milliken permit relief for a legally continuing violation produced by past events, but not every continuing harm establishes a current violation of each claimant's right. The full-effect argument therefore deserves consideration but does not establish the required interim prospect. Irreversibility and the international interests are material, not substitutes for that showing. The Court decides neither the ultimate treaty remedy nor a categorical immunity rule, and does not import A-771's newly pleaded ICJ duty into this older claim.

**Controlling explanation.** Article 36 requires advice without delay of the detained person's consular rights, notification to the consulate on request, and forwarding of communications without delay. Consular assistance rights coexist with the duty to refrain if the national expressly opposes action. Paragraph (2) permits receiving-State procedures subject to their enabling full effect to the rights' purposes. The Court gives that proviso weight: failure to give notice can bear on timely exercise of the right. But the proviso does not itself establish that this applicant could not have presented the available claim with reasonable diligence, that novelty supplies external cause, or that correction of the alleged violation permits the requested domestic remedy. Coleman (1991), Carrier and Reed require those distinct inquiries; treaty supremacy does not collapse them. The stipulated notice failure does not concede prejudice. Counsel's advice to accept a noncapital offer, Breard's refusal and his choice to testify leave the asserted different effect of consular advice disputed. The Fourth Circuit rejected cause without deciding prejudice. The Court makes no final contrary factual finding and does not say consular advice could never change a defense. The present submission does not show the required prospect of legally consequential relief past those obstacles. A notification allegation alone also supplies no reliable new innocence evidence satisfying Schlup.

##### III. The separate challenge to Virginia's proportionality-review method does not independently justify a stay

**Holding and operative rule.** The present claim that Virginia administered its statutory proportionality review through an inadequate comparison pool does not show sufficient reversal prospects to support a stay. State-law error alone is not a federal habeas ground; a genuine arbitrary deprivation of a state-created sentencing entitlement remains a distinct federal question, but this submission does not adequately demonstrate such a deprivation.

**Authority.** Chief Justice Stone-Zsela's Part III, joined by O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer, supplies eight direct joins. Ginsburg and Breyer nevertheless support a stay on the treaty ground. Stevens alone regards the process claim as independently sufficient for protection.

**Controlling explanation.** Pulley rejects a universal federal requirement of comparative proportionality review, and Lewis v. Jeffers does not authorize federal correction of state-law error alone. Hicks preserves protection against arbitrary deprivation of an existing sentencing entitlement. This claim therefore cannot be dismissed merely by confusing it with a different claim that the sentence itself was disproportionate. The district court recognized the federal method claim and expressly rejected default before denying relief; the parties dispute how the Fourth Circuit treated it. Virginia's §17-110.1(C) requires determinations concerning arbitrary influence and excessiveness or disproportionality compared with similar crimes and defendants. Subsection (E) permits collection within a period the court chooses, requires consideration of available records as a guide, and requires accumulated records to be available to circuit courts. Permission to accumulate records is not a command to collect every prosecution. The state court reported examining capital cases it reviewed, including life sentences. That account is not proof of an exhaustive comparison, but the offered aggregate sentencing figures likewise do not establish which crimes and defendants are legally similar or that the required determination was rendered arbitrary. The objection thus disputes how review was performed without adequately showing deprivation of the review or substantive protection the statute supplies. The Court decides no state-law construction or final due-process merits question. The possible appellate claim-treatment error does not by itself establish sufficient prospects of relief despite the independent weaknesses of the asserted federal deprivation.

It does not attribute the substantive sentence claim's default to District Claim IV. No blanket AEDPA hearing bar, hearing entitlement, chapter 154 qualification, final §2254(d) determination or Teague disposition is adopted. Section 2254(d) requires an actual state merits adjudication of the relevant claim; a federal district court's merits discussion alone does not supply one. If the applicant failed to develop the claim's factual basis in state proceedings, §2254(e)(2) bars a federal evidentiary hearing unless the claim relies on either a previously unavailable new constitutional rule made retroactive on collateral review by this Court or a factual predicate not previously discoverable through due diligence, and the underlying facts would establish clearly and convincingly that, but for constitutional error, no reasonable factfinder would have found the applicant guilty of the underlying offense. No finding of that failure or satisfaction of those exceptions is made here. Teague's actual presentation and finality inquiries, its exception for substantive limits on the State's power to punish conduct or classes of persons, and its exception for watershed procedures essential to fundamental accuracy and fairness retain their separate predicates. ### Equality Foundation of Greater Cincinnati, Inc. v. City of Cincinnati, Sixth Circuit Nos. 94-3855 and 94-3973 (merits), No. 94-4280 (fees)

Merits decision, December 3, 1998

#### Chronology and Posture

On review of the Sixth Circuit's October 23, 1997 judgment, 128 F.3d 289, reversing the District Court, vacating its permanent injunction and fee award, and directing judgment for defendants.

The question is whether Cincinnati Charter Article XII's withdrawal and prospective prohibition of municipal sexual-orientation protection satisfies equal protection under Romer, and what follows for the injunction and fees. The charter provision followed ordinances covering City employment and, separately, private employment, housing and public accommodations. Article XII provides:

> NO SPECIAL CLASS STATUS MAY BE GRANTED BASED UPON SEXUAL ORIENTATION, CONDUCT OR RELATIONSHIPS.
>
> The City of Cincinnati and its various Boards and Commissions may not enact, adopt, enforce or administer any ordinance, regulation, rule or policy which provides that homosexual, lesbian, or bisexual orientation, status, conduct, or relationship constitutes, entitles, or otherwise provides a person with the basis to have any claim of minority or protected status, quota preference or other preferential treatment. This provision of the City Charter shall in all respects be self-executing.
>
> Any ordinance, regulation, rule or policy enacted before this amendment is adopted that violates the foregoing prohibition shall be null and void and of no force or effect.

Generally applicable laws and access to higher levels of Ohio government remain available. The Court considers the provision's removal of ordinary municipal protection, not merely quotas or preferential treatment.

| Dependent fee vacatur, No. 94-4280 | Set aside and remanded, 7–2 | Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Kennedy, Souter, Ginsburg, Breyer | Scalia, Thomas | Restore the plaintiffs' prevailing-party basis on the constitutional claim; leave lawful §1988 issues and calculation review for further proceedings. |

#### Controlling Propositions and Authority

##### Article XII's sexual-orientation classification fails Shahar's intermediate scrutiny

**Operative rule:** Article XII classifies by sexual orientation: it names homosexual, lesbian and bisexual orientation and status, and its accompanying references to conduct and relationships regulate no independently relevant conduct but operate as proxies for the same status. Under Shahar's controlling intermediate scrutiny the City must establish a genuine important objective and a substantial relationship between that objective and the actual classification. It has not shown that categorical withdrawal and prospective prohibition of ordinary municipal protection for the named class substantially serves its fiscal, associational, regulatory or local-policy objectives. The rule does not make every repeal unlawful, require the City to enact a particular protection or treat sexual orientation as a suspect classification.

**Authority:** Part I of the Opinion of the Court described above has Stone-Zsela, Stevens, Souter, Ginsburg and Breyer, five direct joins, and directly adopts this rule, its application and limits. O'Connor and Kennedy join Parts II, III and IV but not Part I; Scalia and Thomas dissent.

**Controlling explanation:** Shahar holds that a conduct or relationship label does not remove a sexual-orientation classification from equality review and that such classifications receive intermediate scrutiny. Article XII withholds protection from persons because of homosexual, lesbian or bisexual orientation or status and attaches conduct and relationships to the same class; it regulates no actual conduct for an independently relevant reason. The City therefore bears the burden of establishing a genuine important objective and a substantial relationship between that objective and the classification it actually drew. Moral disapproval, private bias, stereotype and post hoc or merely conceivable reasons cannot carry that burden. Supported professional and institutional judgment receives substantial but not conclusive weight, and the standard requires neither least restrictive means nor exact fit.

The City invokes complaint and defense costs, private association, regulatory restraint and durable local policy. Fiscal management and genuine associational freedom may be important objectives. But the City relies on generalized predictions about the cost of protecting this class, not a showing that categorical, prospective ineligibility for every form of ordinary municipal protection substantially serves those interests. The cost rationale would equally justify removing any class's remedy and does not explain why this class alone is excluded. Associational concerns may support tailored exemptions; they do not explain the bar on the City's own employment policy or on protection in impersonal commercial settings. Charter durability concerns who decides and for how long, not whether the classification serves an important objective. Access to Ohio's higher government and generally applicable law neither changes the classification nor supplies its justification.

The Court does not hold every repeal of an orientation protection unconstitutional, requires no particular ordinance and adopts neither strict scrutiny nor a suspect classification. Bowers's specified-conduct holding is not reopened. Part II independently holds that Article XII fails even the ordinary rational-basis review applied in Romer.

##### The municipal class-specific foreclosure fails ordinary rational-basis review

**Operative rule:** Article XII's combined withdrawal of existing sexual-orientation nondiscrimination protection and prospective prohibition of ordinary municipal protection for the named class violates equal protection because its actual breadth bears no rational relationship to the asserted fiscal, associational, regulatory and local-policy interests. Municipal scope, continued general-law remedies and access to higher government do not cure that unexplained selective disability; the rule does not make every repeal unlawful or require the City to enact a particular protection.

**Authority:** Part II of the Opinion of the Court described above has Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Kennedy, Souter, Ginsburg and Breyer, seven direct joins, and directly adopts the ordinary rationality rule, application and limits as a ground independent of Part I.

**Controlling explanation:** Romer requires a rational connection between legitimate objectives and the actual class-specific disadvantage. Heller's separate holdings preserve that connection while allowing reasonable generalizations, conceivable grounds and imperfect categories without formal findings, empirical certainty or least restrictive means. Moreno and Cleburne forbid bare harm and irrational prejudice; no finding about every voter's private motive is required.

Local fiscal management, regulatory restraint, private association and durable policy are legitimate subjects. Yet Article XII reaches the City's own employment and ordinary protection across housing, public accommodations and private employment, regardless of a particular protection's expense or operation. Avoiding some complaint and defense costs does not explain why this class alone becomes prospectively ineligible for all that ordinary protection. Charter authority explains who sets policy and its durability, not the missing rational connection to the full selective burden.

Hurley protects actual private expression; it does not make impersonal services or the City's own conduct categorically expressive association. Those distinct concerns cannot justify this entire public and private reach. Bowers's specified-conduct holding likewise does not explain the broader disability based on orientation or status. No particular expression or religious exemption is adjudicated and Bowers is not overruled.

The City emphasizes that Ohio institutions and general laws remain accessible. Romer already assumed generally applicable protection remained. The relevant breadth is the civic activity excluded from ordinary protection, not simply the number of governmental levels affected. Ordinary repeal remains permissible; the reported concession about repeal is not assent to prospective charter foreclosure. Coalition's distinct racial political-process inquiry supplies neither the sexual-orientation standard nor a universal entitlement to favorable legislation. This Part applies ordinary rationality to Article XII's own operation, independently of Part I.

##### Equitable protection must address the discriminatory barrier without writing municipal law

**Operative rule:** The appellate direction to enter judgment for defendants cannot stand, and declaratory and injunctive protection must be restored against Article XII's unconstitutional ordinary-protection foreclosure. The lower courts must conform the injunction to that common operation and any genuinely independent lawful remainder under applicable severability law, without rewriting the charter federally, imposing a nondiscrimination code or reopening the resolved rational-basis merits.

**Authority:** Part III of the Opinion of the Court, with the same seven direct joins, directly adopts this remedial holding.

**Controlling explanation:** Removing the barrier returns ordinary lawmaking to its lawful operation; it does not require the City to adopt every requested remedy or prevent a lawful policy revision. Romer's relief followed the violation and an authoritative Colorado inseparability determination. This record supplies no corresponding final Ohio severability determination or established autonomous preference-only remainder. The Court therefore neither performs textual severance nor declares every conceivable separate quota restriction invalid.

The remand concerns fitting the injunction to the decided constitutional defect and respecting any genuinely independent lawful operation under the applicable state rule. It is not permission to continue the invalid common foreclosure or to require a new rationality showing from the plaintiffs. No damages, new ordinance, mandatory preference or adjudicated religious exemption is supplied.

##### The dependent fee vacatur loses its merits foundation

**Operative rule:** Because the constitutional merits judgment is reversed and protection against the charter barrier is restored, the dependent vacatur of the plaintiffs' fee award cannot stand on loss of merits success. Their prevailing-party basis on this constitutional claim is restored, while remaining lawful §1988 issues and review of the calculation return to the lower courts; no particular amount is established here.

**Authority:** Part IV of the Opinion of the Court, with the same seven direct joins, directly adopts the fee disposition and its limits.

**Controlling explanation:** The Sixth Circuit removed the fee award as a consequence of its direction to enter judgment for defendants. The rejection of that merits direction removes the stated basis for erasing the award. It does not decide every statutory fee question or make an earlier contested calculation unreviewable. The lower courts must address those remaining matters in light of the restored constitutional success, separately from conforming the injunction.

#### Precedent Treatment

- Shahar v. Bowers (March 2, 1998): Part I applies its controlling holdings that a conduct or relationship label does not remove a sexual-orientation classification from equality review and that such classifications receive intermediate scrutiny, with the government's burden. - Heller v. Doe: preserves both the actual connection required by its proof-burden holding and the reasonable generalizations accepted in its distinct family-participation holding. A narrower alternative alone does not defeat rationality.
- Department of Agriculture v. Moreno and Cleburne v. Cleburne Living Center: apply the limits on bare harm and irrational prejudice without a voter-by-voter motive inquiry.
- Hurley: preserves actual private expressive choice without establishing a universal exemption for ordinary impersonal transactions.
- Bowers v. Hardwick: remains in force on its specified-conduct question; it does not supply a sufficient explanation for this broader status-based foreclosure.
- Coalition for Economic Equity v. Wilson (November 10, 1998): preserves its distinct racial political-process inquiry, separate sex analysis and fitted relief; it does not automatically decide orientation classifications or constitutionalize every policy withdrawal.
- Hunter v. Erickson and Washington v. Seattle School District: retain their racial political-restructuring settings; no new orientation-based strict scrutiny is adopted.

#### Law After Decision

Article XII cannot enforce its unconstitutional common withdrawal and foreclosure of ordinary municipal protection for the named class. The City remains free to make rational policy choices and lawful revisions; it is not commanded to adopt a particular ordinance or preference. Generally applicable law and actual expressive or religious claims retain their own operation. No suspect classification, general fundamental right of political access, Bowers overruling or damages remedy is created. Injunctive terms and remaining fee questions receive the separate treatment stated in the mandate.

#### Separate Positions Relevant to the Decision

Scalia dissents, joined by Thomas. They would affirm the merits judgment, dissolution of the injunction and dependent fee vacatur. They accept that Article XII reaches ordinary nondiscrimination protection and that municipalities remain subject to equal protection. In their view, regulatory restraint, limited resources, private association and the electorate's authority to make a local policy durable rationally support the measure despite imperfect coverage. Higher-level and generally applicable remedies remain available, and a local body's control over its own delegated policies differs from Colorado's restriction of state and local channels.

The dissent maintains its disagreement with Romer's application of ordinary rationality, adheres to the Shahar dissent's view that ordinary rationality rather than intermediate scrutiny governs sexual-orientation classifications and so rejects Part I, and proposes the municipal distinction as an additional limit. It does not claim Romer's majority adopted that reasoning or that every affected protection is a quota. O'Connor's Coalition writing supplies support for withdrawal of optional policies, while Hurley preserves actual expression; neither is treated as making every orientation protection unlawful. #### Chronology and Posture

#### Holdings and Controlling Reasons

##### I. The judicial certificate denial is reviewable

**Holding and operative rule:** A court of appeals' judicial denial of appellate certification is a case reviewable by certiorari under section 1254(1), whether the certificate was correctly or incorrectly required. That review does not itself issue a certificate or authorize the underlying merits appeal.

**Authority:** Part I of Justice Ginsburg's opinion, joined by Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas and Breyer, controls unanimously.

**Controlling explanation:** Hohn establishes the reviewability of the actual judicial certificate determination while preserving the separate conditions for certification and collateral relief. The Ninth Circuit decided that threshold judicial request; the absence of authorization for the merits appeal does not eliminate the case before it.

Felker bars ordinary review of an actual circuit decision granting or denying authorization for a second or successive district application. This proceeding reviews a certificate denial, not that different statutory determination. Stewart likewise requires attention to the actual judicial action rather than treating every habeas threshold as authorization review. The Court therefore may decide the certificate and classification issues without exercising an unrecognized review exception or awarding the underlying writ.

##### II. The post-enactment appeal requires the amended certificate

**Holding and operative rule:** Amended section 2253(c) governs an applicant's appeal initiated after April 24, 1996, even when the identified pre-AEDPA law governs district adjudication of the earlier habeas application. Slack's April 29, 1998 notice therefore invokes the certificate-of-appealability requirement; the prior CPC label and the State's earlier contrary legal position do not dispense with that required authorization.

**Authority:** Part II is joined by O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy and Thomas with its author, Ginsburg: five direct rationale votes. Stone-Zsela agrees with applying that regime but joins no Part II rationale. Stevens, Souter and Breyer would retain former CPC treatment.

**Controlling explanation:** Section 2253 regulates taking an appeal. The district proceeding and the later effort to enter appellate review are distinct events for the provision governing each. Slack began the renewed district proceeding in 1995 under either supported date but sought appellate review in 1998, after the amendment's enactment.

Lindh's chapter comparison preserves the specified pre-enactment district applications and does not decide every later appellate provision. Carriger's capital extension concerns its identified district-law provisions and does not settle this noncapital certificate question. Felker applies a review restriction to the post-enactment proceeding that restriction regulates; it does not transfer every new merits limitation backward. Hohn expressly reserved certificate transition, so its review holding supplies no already settled answer here.

The contrary whole-pending-case construction gives one commencement date to all later steps. The text's focus on appellate entry warrants the separate treatment adopted here. Applying the new certificate requirement does not apply amended section 2254(d) to Slack's pre-enactment application or adopt a universal claim-by-claim temporal rule for amendments. The State's earlier legal concession cannot authorize an appeal without the certificate the governing statute requires. That conclusion neither makes every subsection jurisdictional nor excuses every possible forfeiture.

The request may be considered under its proper legal character despite the CPC label. Rule 22 permits district or circuit certification and treats a notice without an express certificate request as a request to the circuit judges. A request is not an issued certificate.

##### III. A procedural-dismissal certificate requires both constitutional and procedural showings

**Holding and operative rule:** When a district court dismisses on procedure without reaching the constitutional claim, a certificate of appealability requires reasonable debate both about whether the petition states a valid claim of denial of a constitutional right and about whether the procedural ruling was correct. Both are threshold showings; the court may address the more apparent component first, and any certificate must identify the qualifying issue or issues.

**Authority:** Part III has nine direct joins: Ginsburg, Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas and Breyer. Stevens, Souter and Breyer adopt this interpretation conditionally while maintaining that former CPC governs this appeal; their Part III agreement does not enlarge the Part II coalition.

**Controlling explanation:** Section 2253(c)(2) demands a substantial showing of the denial of a constitutional right. Barefoot supplies the reasonable-debate threshold, which requires more than a nonfrivolous assertion but not ultimate success. A procedural barrier does not eliminate the underlying constitutional claim from that inquiry. The State's categorical exclusion of procedural-dismissal appeals would insulate an erroneous barrier even when it blocks a substantial constitutional claim.

The opposite procedure-only approach also fails. A debatable procedural question or bare constitutional label alone does not make the required constitutional showing. The two inquiries must each be satisfied without converting certification into final adjudication of the constitutional claim. A plain and correctly applied procedural bar can defeat certification; a court need not decide the constitutional issue unnecessarily when the procedural answer is clear. It may instead begin with the constitutional component when that answer is more apparent. No universal sequence is prescribed.

Where a district court actually rejects the constitutional merits, the corresponding substantial showing asks whether reasonable jurists could debate that assessment or find it wrong. Slack's procedural dismissals are not silently reclassified as merits adjudications. Section 2253(c)(3) separately requires issue identification. Peguero addresses the effect of an actually issued certificate; none exists here. Neither this Court's review nor an unaccompanied notice supplies one. Rule 22's State-appeal exception remains distinct and is not implicated by this applicant's appeal.

##### IV. This exhaustion return remains a first application, including its additional grounds

**Holding and operative rule:** When an initial federal application is dismissed without prejudice solely to permit completion of state exhaustion before any federal merits adjudication, the renewed application is not second or successive merely because it follows that filing or adds grounds. Rule 9(b)'s new-ground abuse branch does not supply the missing second-or-successive predicate in this exhaustion-return setting; the contrary classification here is rejected, while actual independent default, exhaustion, timing and lawful case-management requirements remain.

**Authority:** Part IV of Ginsburg's opinion is joined by Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Kennedy, Souter and Breyer, making seven direct votes for this rule, its application and reversal with remand. Scalia and Thomas dissent from this classification and disposition.

**Controlling explanation:** Rose's complete-exhaustion procedure contemplates return for a first federal merits opportunity. The February 1992 dismissal did not adjudicate Slack's claims and expressly permitted renewal after exhaustion. Rule 9(b) begins with the second-or-successive category. Its repeated-ground branch requires a prior merits determination; its new-ground branch addresses omission constituting abuse within that category. The latter does not erase the opening classification in this setting. Former section 2244(b)'s prior-merits setting reinforces that reading.

The State emphasizes the new-ground branch's separate wording, Sanders's treatment of a nonmerits denial and McCleskey's pleading and excuse framework. Those authorities preserve structured abuse inquiries, but a deficient pleading or completed merits application is not this without-prejudice exhaustion return. The State's adequate abuse pleading and Slack's response cannot supply a statutory or Rule category that does not apply. The additional claims therefore do not alone convert this application into a successive one.

Stewart supplies a confined analogy: its Ford claim had actually been presented, dismissed as premature and preserved. It did not already decide omitted claims or every nonmerits dismissal. Today's holding supplies the exhaustion-return rule. Lonchar requires governing dismissal predicates rather than general disapproval of repeated litigation. Legitimate concerns about serial filings may support lawful pleading rules, exhaustion requirements or properly communicated return conditions on their actual predicates; they do not establish intentional disobedience here.

The holding creates no immunity for actual deliberate withholding, a applicable abuse rule, state default, prejudicial delay or lawful anti-duplication measures. It confers no unlimited right to amend. The classification error settles the procedural objection under review; the Ninth Circuit must still determine the substantial constitutional showing before issuing an issue-specific certificate. Reversal of its denial does not decide the unreviewed exhaustion grounds, the constitutional claims or entitlement to collateral relief.

**Operative procedural qualifications:** Rule 9(a)'s distinct delay provision permits dismissal where filing delay prejudiced the State's ability to respond, unless reasonable diligence could not have disclosed the grounds before that prejudice arose. No such prejudice or diligence finding is made here. Rule 9(b)'s dismissal permission is not an automatic command, and former section 2244(b)'s exception to its prior-merits permission requires both an unadjudicated ground and absence of deliberate withholding or other abuse; that prior-merits predicate is absent here. No free-floating equitable sanction replaces an applicable source's conditions.

**Limits and questions not reached:** No universal rule covers every nonmerits dismissal or every later claim. Amended section 2244's restrictions retain their own applicability and classification predicates; no authorization determination or exception is decided here. The State's exhaustion alternatives and any actual independent, adequate and properly applied state default remain separate. No present claim is found substantial, exhausted, excused or meritorious, and the lower court's former reopening period is not renewed.

### Troxel v. Granville, No. 99-138

Merits decision, June 5, 2000

#### Chronology and Posture

Argued January 12, 2000. On writ of certiorari to the Washington Supreme Court in In re Custody of Smith, 137 Wash. 2d 1, 969 P.2d 21. Only the Troxel/Granville visitation dispute is before the Court. The question is whether former RCW 26.10.160(3), as applied over a fit parent's objection, violated parental liberty in directing the children's care and upbringing. The state court rejected a pending-custody-action limitation but sustained dismissal on broader federal constitutional grounds.

The former subsection permits any person to petition for visitation at any time, including but not limited to custody proceedings, and permits an order when visitation may serve the child's best interest whether or not circumstances have changed. After the children's father died, Granville continued contact with the paternal grandparents and proposed one daytime visit monthly and family holidays. The grandparents sought two overnight weekends monthly and two summer weeks; the trial order required one monthly weekend, one summer week and four hours on each grandparent's birthday. The dispute concerns the amount and conditions of continuing contact, not complete exclusion. The published account identifies no allegation or finding of parental unfitness and does not establish concrete harm from Granville's proposal.

All nine Justices participated.

#### Judgment

**Affirmed, 6–3.**

**Supporting:** Stone-Zsela, O'Connor, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer.
**Opposing:** Stevens, Scalia and Kennedy.

Stevens and Kennedy would vacate the constitutional dismissal and remand for further state consideration with proper parental protection. Scalia would reverse that dismissal without adopting the asserted new federal visitation rule. None of the three directs automatic reinstatement of the trial order.

#### Opinion Topology

| Writing | Author | Joined by | Relationship to judgment | Scope joined |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Opinion announcing the judgment; Opinion of the Court in Part I | Breyer | O'Connor and Ginsburg throughout; Stone-Zsela, Souter and Thomas in Part I only | Announces the six-Justice affirmance | I: protected parental-care liberty and prohibition of mere judicial-preference substitution; II: special weight and particular record application, three Justices only |
| Concurrence in part and in the judgment | Stone-Zsela | No other Justice | Affirms invalidation as applied; joins Part I | Serious-harm or comparable concrete legal-need trigger, strong presumption and narrowly fitted relief |
| Concurrence in part and in the judgment | Souter | No other Justice | Affirms dismissal; joins Part I | Unrestricted statutory authorization as authoritatively construed |
| Concurrence in part and in the judgment | Thomas | No other Justice | Affirms dismissal; joins Part I | Fundamental parental right and strict scrutiny |
| Dissent | Stevens | No other Justice | Would vacate and remand | Individualized assessment including the child's relational interests |
| Dissent | Scalia | No other Justice | Would reverse and remand | Refusal to extend established parental domains to this new substantive visitation rule |
| Dissent | Kennedy | No other Justice | Would vacate and remand | Contextual parental protection, actual relationships and extent of intrusion |

#### Controlling Propositions and Authority

##### Parental liberty forbids displacement based only on a judge's preferred arrangement

**Holding and operative rule:** The protected liberty of a fit parent includes directing the child's care and upbringing. A court may not override that parent's visitation decision solely because the judge considers a different arrangement better for the child; ordinary judicial preference is not a sufficient constitutional justification.

**Authority:** Part I of Breyer's opinion has six direct joins: Stone-Zsela, O'Connor, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer. Their agreement is at precisely this level; their distinct additional scrutiny, trigger and statutory-scope rules are not combined.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that the parent's constitutional interest cannot disappear into an unrestricted comparison of preferred arrangements. Meyer and Pierce establish protected parental direction, while Brown and Curtis preserve parental instruction and lawful educational choices even as they reject particular public-school vetoes. The State's ordinary family-law authority does not make the parent's decision equivalent to a stranger's proposed schedule.

The grandparents' continuing relationship and the benefits of contact are significant context, but a preference for more contact is not itself a sufficient basis for judicial displacement. Recognizing this limit does not establish an absolute parental veto or deny legitimate child protection. Baby Richard recognizes a bounded interest in avoiding serious harm from compulsory custody implementation while preserving the fit parent's entitlement against a preferred-home comparison. A. St. P. C. addresses a distinct complete-contact cutoff. Neither automatically supplies the threshold, proof quantum or remedy for this visitation dispute.

The six Justices sharing this proposition affirm dismissal, but differ about the further constitutional rule and how broadly the statutory authorization must be rejected. Their agreement establishes the prohibition of mere judicial-preference substitution, not a complete governing test for every nonparent-visitation order.

##### The judgment does not adopt a comprehensive visitation standard

**Authority and limits:** The judgment binds the parties and leaves this order unenforceable. Part II's full special-weight and record-application analysis has three votes, O'Connor, Ginsburg and Breyer. Stone-Zsela's serious-harm or comparable-need rule, Souter's statutory-breadth rationale and Thomas's strict-scrutiny ground remain separate. No common scrutiny level, universal harm requirement, parental-unfitness prerequisite, proof burden or general facial invalidation receives a controlling adoption. No Marks synthesis supplies one. Other statutes and materially different nonparent relationships remain open; the Smith and Wolcott disputes and collateral fees are not adjudicated here.

#### Precedent Treatment

- **Meyer v. Nebraska** and **Pierce v. Society of Sisters:** Apply the established protection of parental care and direction; neither supplies the entire ordinary-visitation test.
- **Brown v. Hot, Sexy & Safer Productions, Inc.** and **Curtis v. School Committee of Falmouth:** Preserve protected family choice and instruction while distinguishing particular public-school veto claims from this intrusion.
- **Baby Richard v. Kirchner:** Preserves its bounded child-transition interest and protection of lawful parental custody against preferred-home substitution; it creates no permanent grandparent-contact entitlement.
- **A. St. P. C. v. B. C.:** Its distinct clear-and-convincing rule remains intact. That decision concerned a complete cutoff of contact and supplies no universal visitation burden.
- **Washington v. Glucksberg:** Remains a decision concerning the asserted physician-assistance entitlement, without an exclusive most-specific-history method for all liberties.
- **Parham v. J.R., Stanley v. Illinois, Prince v. Massachusetts** and **Santosky v. Kramer:** Retain their distinct parental, child-protection, fitness-process and termination principles; their use in the separate analyses creates no new common visitation threshold.

#### Law After Decision

Effective June 5, 2000, six Justices directly establish parental-care protection against displacement solely by judicial preference for another arrangement, and six affirm dismissal of this petition. No complete special-weight framework or general serious-harm, strict-scrutiny, facial-invalidation or proof-burden rule is adopted. Existing child-protection, educational-choice, custody-transition and severe-contact-restriction holdings retain their distinct force.

#### Separate Writings

**Part II of the lead opinion, Breyer with O'Connor and Ginsburg.** These three require special weight for a fit parent's judgment, drawing on Parham's presumption that parents ordinarily act in their children's interests. The trial court instead presumed grandparent contact beneficial unless contrary harm appeared, failed to credit Granville's continuing-contact offer, and relied in part on its own favorable grandparent experiences. The written findings establish benefits, not unfitness or concrete injury from her proposal. These circumstances make the order unconstitutional without deciding a universal harm or unfitness requirement. Limited visitation differs from custody termination, but that difference does not permit ordinary better-schedule substitution. Other statutes, relationships and justified protective interventions remain open.

**Stone-Zsela, concurring in part and in the judgment.** Meyer, Pierce, Prince and Santosky support his proposed extension: a fit parent's reasonable visitation choice receives a strong presumption, and the nonparent must establish particularized serious harm to the child or a comparably concrete legally recognized need before the State may override it. Any order must be narrowly fitted to that need. The order fails because an unbounded best-interests preference supplies neither predicate nor fit. He treats that specification as an additional rule to be adopted, not as a threshold already established by the earlier cases. His rule preserves emergency protection, adjudication of unfitness and tailored relief on the required showing, and creates no absolute veto. No other Justice joins this proposed complete standard.

**Souter, concurring in part and in the judgment.** The authoritatively construed authorization permits any person to seek visitation at any time and leaves protected parental choice exposed to an unrestricted judicial preference for a better arrangement. That breadth supplies his sufficient ground without reaching Part II's particular record assessment. Possible justified applications cannot add restrictions the state court declined to find in the statute. Brown, Curtis and his Glucksberg approach support protection of the actual interest and assessment of the restraint's fit; he does not hold that harm is invariably the sole sufficient justification.

**Thomas, concurring in part and in the judgment.** Established parental care is a fundamental right, so its infringement requires strict scrutiny and compelling justification. Benefit-only second-guessing of this fit parent's decision lacks that justification. He joins Part I's exact liberty and anti-substitution rules, but not an unspecified special-weight standard that leaves the required scrutiny unstated. His ground does not transfer the termination proof burden to every visitation case or establish an absolute veto.

**Stevens, dissenting.** Parental protection must coexist with the child's supported interests in important relationships. His Parham position and the bounded interests recognized in Baby Richard and A. St. P. C. support individualized assessment, not a permanent grandparent right. Because a universal harm condition or broad facial invalidation exceeds the established rules, he would vacate and return the matter for state consideration with a presumptive parental role and attention to the child's actual interests. He neither finds the order valid nor commands new evidence or visitation.

**Scalia, dissenting.** He preserves the judicially protected parental domains recognized in Meyer, Pierce, Brown and Curtis but declines to infer this new substantive visitation boundary from those distinct decisions. His Baby Richard and A. St. P. C. positions require an established interest before transferring rules across materially different family-law settings. He would reverse the constitutional dismissal and allow ordinary state disposition without a new federal weighting or harm formula. That position does not deny every enforceable parental liberty, overrule existing family protections or command visitation.

**Kennedy, dissenting.** A categorical harm condition is too broad where an established caregiving relationship and limited intrusion may matter. Brown, Curtis and his Glucksberg opinion require attention to the actual liberty and restraint; the family-law cases do not erase those distinctions. He would vacate and remand for assessment of the order with meaningful parental protection and attention to the actual relationships. His proposed remand does not presume harm, validate the existing order, demand supplementation or reinstate visitation.

Benefit findings are not findings of necessity, and the absence of a reported fitness allegation is not an adjudication of every conceivable parental concern.

The Troxel petition remains dismissed and the visitation order unenforceable. The affirmance permits no renewed contest based merely on a preferred visitation schedule. No new best-interests proceeding, custody transfer, evidentiary hearing, fee award or disposition of the separate Smith and Wolcott controversies is ordered. Materially different visitation laws and relationships remain for cases presenting them.

### California Democratic Party v. Jones, No. 99-401

Plenary merits decision, June 26, 2000

#### Chronology and Posture

Argued April 24, 2000; decided June 26, 2000. On writ of certiorari to the United States Court of Appeals for the Ninth Circuit, reviewing 169 F.3d 646 (1999), which adopted the District Court's post-trial ruling, 984 F. Supp. 1288 (1997).

The question is whether California may require objecting political parties to select their official nominees for state and congressional office through a blanket primary that permits every voter to cross party lines office by office. Proposition 198 gives each party's nomination to its highest vote recipient even though voters unaffiliated with that party participate on equal terms. Elections for party committees remain confined to party registrants. All four plaintiff parties oppose the compelled participation through their governing rules.

The trial record supports likely crossover voting and effects in some contests, while distinguishing sincere crossover from comparatively rare organized raiding. Its estimates vary by race and denominator; the Court does not adopt a universal numerical threshold. The parties retain advocacy and endorsement, but those activities do not themselves select the legal nominee. No separate Elections Clause claim, presidential-delegate judgment, damages claim or retrospective election challenge is decided.

All nine Justices participated at argument and decision.

#### Judgment

**Reversed and remanded, 7-2.**

**Supporting:** Stone-Zsela, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas and Breyer.

**Opposing:** Stevens and Ginsburg.

The blanket-primary requirement cannot constitutionally compel these objecting parties to submit their official nominations to equal participation by unaffiliated voters. The lower courts must provide declaratory and prospective injunctive relief fitted to that defect. The Court does not reopen an election, award damages, prescribe a replacement ballot, decide every severability question or invalidate an unpresented voluntary or nonpartisan arrangement.

#### Opinion Topology

Justice O'Connor delivered the opinion of the Court, joined in full by Chief Justice Stone-Zsela and Justices Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas and Breyer. All seven join the severe-burden rule, strict scrutiny, application, limits and remedy, and adopt all three holdings at the same level of generality.

Justice Kennedy also filed a concurrence concerning political autonomy and the distinction between a party's choice to broaden its appeal and state-compelled alteration of its nominating constituency. Justice Stevens filed a dissent, joined by Justice Ginsburg, addressing the public-primary distinction and an independently sufficient contextual justification. The dissent would affirm and supplies no independent Elections Clause disposition.

#### Controlling Propositions and Authority

##### 1. Compulsory outsider selection of an official party nominee imposes a severe burden

**Holding and operative rule:** A State severely burdens political association when it requires an objecting party to give voters unaffiliated with that party equal participation in choosing its official nominee through this blanket-primary mechanism. The requirement therefore must be narrowly tailored to serve a compelling state interest; ordinary authority to administer elections does not alone supply that justification.

**Authority:** O'Connor's opinion, joined in full by the seven Justices identified above, controls all three holdings at the same level of generality.

**Controlling explanation:** Tashjian protects meaningful associational choice while preserving the State's role in a workable election system. Eu protects party governance against state direction, and Democratic Party v. Wisconsin ex rel. La Follette rejects state-imposed participation that overrides a party's protected selection rules. Hurley protects the composition of private expression; it does not exempt every association from ordinary regulation. Selecting the official standard bearer is a central party act even when the State supplies the primary machinery. California's system changes who makes that choice, rather than merely regulating its time, ballot or administration. Sincere crossover can alter nominations and candidates' positions without organized raiding. The parties need not prove organizational collapse or await an election that conclusively changes every nominee. Retained endorsement and advocacy do not restore the lost selection choice. Burdick and Timmons require attention to the actual burden: Timmons protects a qualified party's voluntary choice of a consenting nominee and its official recognition, while this law makes outsiders participants in the party's own nomination. The public character of elections permits regulation and requires protection of voters' constitutional rights; it does not remove this distinct associational injury. The Court decides neither all conventional open primaries nor every membership or registration rule.

##### 2. The asserted interests do not justify this compulsory nomination mechanism

**Holding and operative rule:** The interests advanced for Proposition 198 do not justify its severe interference with the objecting parties' nomination choices. Participation, choice, competition, privacy and stable elections may support appropriate election rules, but the State has not established a compelling justification and necessary fit for imposing equal outsider participation in the party's own official selection.

**Controlling explanation:** The State may seek representative government and robust participation, but it cannot justify this intrusion merely by preferring more moderate party nominees or nominees responsive to voters who have chosen another association. That purpose changes the party's political choice rather than protecting the electoral process from an independent injury. Giving independents or minority-party voters influence in another party's safe-district primary likewise does not establish a right to select that party's standard bearer. Increased choice, turnout and competition are legitimate electoral aims, yet the necessary connection to compelled party nomination is missing. A nonpartisan winnowing election can select candidates without purporting to select each party's official nominee, and a party may itself invite additional voters. Those possibilities show why broader participation does not require this mechanism; their mention decides no unpresented system. A privacy interest in political affiliation also does not establish an entitlement to participate secretly in every other party's nomination. The stability and fairness arguments do not identify a necessity sufficient to overcome this severe burden. The Court does not substitute a numerical prediction for constitutional analysis or treat every participation objective as impermissible. The defect is the asserted purposes and insufficient fit of this particular compulsory means.

##### 3. Relief follows the nomination defect and does not decide other election systems

**Holding and operative rule:** The defense judgment must be reversed and the case remanded for declaratory and prospective injunctive relief against compulsory blanket selection of these objecting parties' official nominees. The ruling leaves ordinary election administration and constitutional voting protections intact and reserves the validity of unpresented primary designs and any remaining severability or implementation questions.

**Controlling explanation:** The plaintiffs established a constitutional defect in the compulsory choice mechanism, not a right to have this Court administer elections. The remedy therefore prevents that compelled selection without prescribing a ballot, reopening completed elections or awarding damages. Timmons protects a party's voluntary nomination while leaving the State neutral regulation of ballot presentation, consent and double counting; the white-primary decisions preserve voters' constitutional protection against exclusion. Describing less intrusive alternatives does not hold that every top-two or nonpartisan system, party invitation or registration condition is valid. The same defect governs the state and congressional nominations presented. No independently presented Elections Clause or national-delegate claim supplies an additional judgment.

#### Precedent Treatment

- Tashjian v. Republican Party of Connecticut supplies protection for meaningful party associational choices within a regulated electoral system; this decision does not adopt an unlimited power of party leaders to override members.
- Eu v. San Francisco County Democratic Central Committee supplies the protection against state control of party governance and political choice; ordinary election administration remains available.
- Democratic Party v. Wisconsin ex rel. La Follette supplies protection against forced participation inconsistent with protected party selection rules; the present decision applies that principle to the challenged official nominations without deciding national-delegate questions.
- Burdick v. Takushi supplies burden-sensitive review of election regulations. The severe burden here triggers compelling-interest and narrow-tailoring review rather than the lesser justification sufficient for ordinary mechanics.
- Timmons v. Twin Cities Area New Party remains controlling in requiring meaningful official recognition of a qualified party's voluntary nomination of a consenting candidate. That protection of the party's own choice distinguishes it from compelled outsider participation in the actual choice.
- Reform Party of Allegheny County v. Allegheny County Department of Elections retains its equal-access holding concerning a timely consensual cross-nomination and its limits on the independent association issue. It does not itself decide the present public-primary question.
- Hurley v. Irish-American Gay, Lesbian and Bisexual Group of Boston supplies protection for the composition of private expression, with its actual limits; this decision neither exempts ordinary services from neutral regulation nor classifies every regulated association as private expression.
- The white-primary decisions, including Smith v. Allwright and Terry v. Adams, retain their prohibitions on unconstitutional exclusion from electoral processes. Associational protection supplies no exemption from those constitutional commands.

#### Law After Decision

California may not enforce the compulsory blanket selection of the objecting parties' official nominees. Election regulation remains subject to the burden-sensitive framework, with strict scrutiny for the severe selection injury established here. Timmons and Reform Party retain their distinct holdings about official recognition of a qualified party's consenting nomination and unequal cross-nomination opportunities. The State may pursue broad electoral participation through lawful, less intrusive designs; this decision does not approve every nonpartisan, top-two, voluntary open-primary or registration system. A party's nomination autonomy remains subject to applicable constitutional voting protections.

#### Separate Positions Relevant to the Decision

**Kennedy, concurring.** He joins the Court's complete rule and remedy. Political parties may choose to broaden their own appeal, change their principles or invite participation. The State's effort to achieve moderation by changing the electorate that selects a party's official representative instead directs the association's political development. The distinction is between the party's own adaptation and a compulsory substitution of decision makers, not hostility to broader democratic participation. His concurrence adds no campaign-finance holding or adjudication of another primary design.

**Stevens, joined by Ginsburg, dissenting.** They would affirm on the presented associational claim for both state and congressional nominations. They distinguish a publicly required and administered nominating election from the party's private leadership, endorsement and advocacy decisions. Protecting a party's voluntary candidate choices against exclusion does not necessarily give it a constitutional veto over the qualified electorate in that public primary. Timmons's consensual candidate-access dispute and Hurley's private expressive composition do not erase this boundary; Reform Party's equality holding does not resolve it. Independently, the retained party functions, limited and variable injury supported by the trial record, and participation, choice and representative-government interests adequately justify the law under contextual review. They acknowledge that official nomination differs from endorsement and that sincere crossover can matter; neither observation establishes the severe private-association injury the Court finds. Their position does not depend on implied party consent from a popular vote or on the absence of every crossover effect. The unpresented Elections Clause question remains undecided and supplies no different judgment.

### United States v. United Foods, Inc., No. 00-276

Merits decision, June 25, 2001

#### Chronology and Posture

Argued April 17, 2001; decided June 25, 2001. On writ of certiorari to the Sixth Circuit, 197 F.3d 221, which reversed summary judgment sustaining the challenged compulsory mushroom-promotion assessments. The question is whether independent producers may be required to finance this generic advertising under the First Amendment. United Foods sought administrative relief; following its denial, district review and the government's enforcement action were consolidated.

The Mushroom Promotion, Research, and Consumer Information Act funds an industry Council through domestic fresh-market producer and importer assessments. First handlers collect and remit; direct-marketing producers remit themselves. Producers and importers at or below the 500,000-pound annual threshold fall outside the assessed categories, and mushrooms certified by a first handler for export as mushrooms cannot be assessed. Most funds support generic promotion, which United Foods opposes while seeking to advertise its own mushrooms as superior. Producers otherwise retain independent market decisions; this is not a comprehensive cooperative marketing order.

Section 6104 requires approval of plans, projects and budgets before implementation and provides contractual accounting controls. Its ban on spending to influence legislation or governmental action or policy separately permits Council recommendations to the Secretary for order amendments and voluntary grade and quality standards. Section 6105 requires both a majority of voters and a majority accounting, on average, for more than half the voting producers' and importers' annual volume. The mandatory five-year referendum differs from the optional referendum available after three years upon a request by at least 30 percent of producers and importers. These statutory terms do not establish constitutional validity or actual control of the advertising message.

All nine Justices participated at argument and decision.

#### Judgment

**Affirmed, 6-3.**

**Supporting:** Stone-Zsela, Stevens, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter and Thomas.

**Opposing:** O'Connor, Ginsburg and Breyer.

The challenged advertising obligation cannot be enforced against United Foods on the adjudicated private-subsidy ground. The lower courts must implement the judgment through the statutory review and consolidated enforcement proceedings. Unresolved accounting, severability and monetary relief receive their own lawful treatment; no general refund entitlement or invalidation of independently lawful research assessments is ordered.

#### Opinion Topology

Justice Thomas delivered the opinion of the Court, joined in full by Chief Justice Stone-Zsela and Justices Stevens, Scalia, Kennedy and Souter. Justice Stevens concurred separately. Justice Thomas also filed a separate concurrence. Justice Breyer dissented, joined by Justices O'Connor and Ginsburg; they would reverse the constitutional bar and return the case for proceedings under their commercial-speech ground.

#### Controlling Propositions and Authority

##### I. The independent-market advertising obligation violates the freedom against compelled promotional support

**Holding and operative rule:** Government may not compel otherwise independent producers to finance the selected private generic promotion here merely because the campaign benefits the industry, overcomes free riding, receives majority approval or leaves producers free to advertise separately. Where no independently sufficient collective function justifies the required association, the advertising cannot supply its own justification; the challenged mushroom advertising obligation therefore violates the First Amendment.

**Authority:** The six Justices joining Justice Thomas's Opinion of the Court directly adopt all three holdings and affirmance. The dissent rests on an independent commercial-speech ground.

**Controlling explanation:** Abood v. Detroit Board of Education and Keller v. State Bar require identifying the function that justifies compelled association before asking whether financed expression is germane to it. An advertising campaign cannot meet that requirement merely by declaring advertising to be the association's purpose. Compelled financing can burden expression even when the payer need not utter the message, the objection is commercial and no personal endorsement is attributed.

Glickman v. Wileman Brothers & Elliott permits germane nonideological promotion within its substantially collective agricultural market, with no attribution and continued independent expression. This program lacks comparable output and sales restrictions, an antitrust exemption or comprehensive cooperative marketing. The producers remain independent competitors; promotion is the principal activity for which they must associate. Common benefit alone was insufficient under Glickman, which expressly left this arrangement open. Southworth supplies another sufficient function: a broad, neutral educational opportunity for independent private expression. The present rule therefore does not require collective price or output regulation for every valid speech subsidy.

The government has offered an alternative commercial-speech argument, including substantial market interests, advancement and reasonable fit; the Court does not find it waived. Even assuming promotion increases demand and the national scope, small-producer line and denial of brand-advertising credits are reasonable economic choices, those propositions do not cure the separate compelled-private-support defect. They explain how the campaign can serve an economic objective, not the missing independent justification for forcing dissenters to finance its selected message. No empirical Central Hudson ruling or finding that advertising failed is necessary.

United Foods' freedom to promote its own mushrooms as superior does not eliminate that objection. The holding preserves Glickman's comprehensive-program result, actual government speech financed by taxes, and factual commercial disclosures governed by their own requirements. Separate health or safety purposes and independently lawful research or information assessments are outside this adjudicated advertising obligation.

##### II. The operational government-speech alternative is not decided in this review

**Holding and operative rule:** The Court declines, as an exercise of review discretion, to decide the government's operational government-speech defense, which was neither raised nor addressed below. Its asserted inclusion within the preserved First Amendment controversy does not require the Court to decide a materially different attribution theory or order its development; the private-subsidy dispute is resolved on the ground properly adjudicated here.

**Authority:** The same six Justices join Part II. The dissenting three find government speech unnecessary under their own sufficient commercial-speech ground; their nonreach does not join the Court's review rationale.

**Controlling explanation:** Yee v. Escondido and Lebron v. National Railroad Passenger Corp. distinguish an included legal argument from a different question and preserve the Court's discretion over review. Lebron permits consideration of an included argument; it does not compel consideration of every late alternative or an operational remand whenever statutory federal control is identified.

The Secretary appoints Council members through the statutory producer/importer nomination process. Plans, projects and budgets require approval before implementation; contracts require the Secretary's prior plan approval and accounting. These are real statutory controls. They do not, by themselves, establish who actually adopted or controlled the messages at issue. The Court neither finds those powers absent nor decides that the Council can never convey governmental speech.

Lebron's determination that an institution bears constitutional duties is different from deciding that a particular financed message belongs to government. Keller preserves that distinction despite the integrated Bar's official status, while Rosenberger separates governmental provision of a benefit from the independently edited private publication it supports. Those distinctions counsel against resolving the new operational theory by label alone. The Court's refusal is prudential, not a categorical forfeiture rule or a general prohibition on financing genuine governmental speech. The disputed Central Hudson presentation is a different matter; its legal answer appears in Part I and does not rest on borrowing this theory's conceded nonpresentation.

##### III. Affirmance bars the challenged advertising enforcement and requires lawful implementation

**Holding and operative rule:** The Sixth Circuit's judgment rejecting the challenged advertising assessment is affirmed; the consolidated enforcement proceeding must conform to that constitutional holding. Under §6106(b)(3), a contrary-to-law administrative ruling must be remanded to the Secretary either for a lawful ruling directed by the reviewing court or for the further action the law requires; unresolved accounting, severability and monetary relief remain governed by their own legal and factual predicates.

**Authority:** The same six Justices join Part III. O'Connor, Ginsburg and Breyer would reverse the constitutional bar and return for lawful implementation under their commercial-speech ground, without a fixed collection award or a new proof-gathering inquiry.

**Controlling explanation:** The government may not enforce the unconstitutional advertising obligation merely because administrative review and enforcement were consolidated. Section 6106 provides a petition and hearing, timely district review and a mandatory lawful administrative return when the ruling is contrary to law. The legal defect is now settled; the return implements it rather than postpones it for another effectiveness study or an unadjudicated government-speech inquiry.

Section 6106(b)(4) separately provides that mere pendency of the administrative petition does not impede enforcement under §6107. That rule does not authorize collection of an obligation adjudged unconstitutional or settle the distinct requirements for any properly sought interim relief. The two provisions perform different work.

The Act also authorizes research and information activities. The record does not decide the actual operation and separability of every such expenditure, the accounting attributable to the challenged advertising or the legal predicates for monetary recovery. Their treatment remains for the proper proceedings under governing remedial law. No nationwide refund entitlement, damages amount, collection award, invalidation of every Council function or automatic relief for other producers is ordered. Any independently lawful assessment must be considered on its own operation; no new statute, funding protocol or remedy is invented.

#### Precedent Treatment

- Glickman v. Wileman Brothers & Elliott, Inc. — distinguished at its express boundary. Its permission for germane nonideological promotion within the substantially collective program, without attribution and with independent expression preserved, remains binding; neither common benefit nor majority approval alone supplies an independent exception. The reserved advertising-centered independent market receives the new rule stated here.
- Abood v. Detroit Board of Education and Keller v. State Bar of California — their independent common-function and germaneness principles are applied to the compelled advertising. Keller also preserves the distinction between official institutional status and every funded message; neither case supplies an automatic ban on every compulsory payment.
- Board of Regents v. Southworth — its separately justified broad, neutral educational private-expression program remains valid. It demonstrates that comprehensive agricultural regulation is not the only possible independent function; its actual conditions are not replaced by a common-benefit formula.
- Central Hudson Gas & Electric Corp. v. Public Service Commission — its commercial-speech framework remains in its proper field. The Court decides no empirical application of that framework here; asserted commercial advancement and fit do not answer the independently sufficient subsidy objection.
- Yee v. City of Escondido and Lebron v. National Railroad Passenger Corp. — their distinction between an included argument and a separate question, and their review discretion, are preserved. Lebron's governmental-actor holding does not determine every message's attribution or require a new operational remand.
- Rosenberger v. Rector and Visitors of the University of Virginia — its distinction between government funding and independently edited private expression remains; the Court adopts no governmental-message finding from the funding label.

#### Current Law

The First Amendment now bars the challenged advertising-centered compelled association in an otherwise independent market because the selected promotion supplies no independent justification for forcing dissenters to fund it. Glickman's integrated-market permission and Southworth's distinct educational program remain intact. Commercial benefit, reasonable economic design, majority approval and freedom to counteradvertise do not cure this separate defect. The Court does not decide that the program failed an empirical commercial-speech test or that the Council can never convey government speech. The operative advertising ruling is settled; administrative implementation and supported residual remedies remain below.

#### Separate Positions Relevant to the Decision

Justice Stevens concurs and joins the Court's opinion in full. Compelling payment differs from forcing a person to speak, and not every governmental use of money impairs the same liberty. But requiring an independent producer to finance competitors' preferred advertising still burdens protected choice. The assertion that a useful campaign benefits everyone does not supply the sufficient independent function that justified Glickman's collective regulation or Southworth's broad educational program. He finds the compulsory-subsidy ground sufficient and declines the new operational government-speech inquiry. He decides nothing about a materially different health, safety or artistic purpose and does not announce that every compulsory expression payment is forbidden.

Justice Thomas concurs separately as well as delivering the Court's opinion. He would not reduce the protection of compelled financing because the message is commercial. His Glickman position rejects Central Hudson's discounted framework as the governing answer to that burden. The Court's sufficient compulsory-subsidy rule resolves this case without adopting his broader standard. His observation that failure under a lesser test would also establish failure under a stronger one does not adopt that lesser test, find its elements empirically unsatisfied or supply an additional Court holding. He preserves Southworth's separately justified neutral educational program and does not prohibit every regulatory fee or tax supporting actual government speech. No other Justice joins this stronger-framework concurrence.

Justice Breyer, joined by Justices O'Connor and Ginsburg, dissents. They accept that compelled financial support can burden expression and that actual Glickman supplies no general economic-regulation immunity. They would nevertheless sustain this assessment under independent commercial-speech scrutiny: government must show a substantial interest, direct advancement and reasonable fit; no issue of unlawful or misleading commercial content is presented.

They would reach that alternative because it concerns the same assessment, constitutional controversy and relief, the government denies waiver, and United Foods addresses its merits. This exercise of discretion does not establish automatic consideration of every reply argument or new evidentiary admission. Government speech's conceded nonpresentation is separate and need not be decided on their sufficient ground.

National agricultural-market maintenance and development, together with the supported collective-action problem, supply substantial interests. The actual promotion serves those interests, and the legislative and program record and reported experience support its practical capacity to advance them. The reported failures of voluntary campaigns support compulsory contributions. Brand advertising seeks a producer's share and need not perform the same market-wide function, so an automatic credit is unnecessary. The exemption at or below 500,000 pounds can avoid disproportionate collection and administration; the reply's numerical illustration is arithmetic, not a verified cost study.

National promotion need not return benefits to each region in proportion to a producer's distribution. Exempt beneficiaries, elastic supply, new entry and possible intermediary capture do not establish that market expansion fails to serve the public objective. Reasonable fit does not require the least restrictive method, identical treatment of every commodity, guaranteed profit for United Foods or a prescribed empirical study. The reported effectiveness submission supports their assessment of the existing record; they adopt no numerical coefficient, new methodology finding, evidentiary-admission order or concession by the producer.

These three reject a conclusive subsidy bar where the government has independently justified the commercial burden. They would reverse and remand for lawful conforming proceedings, without a fixed assessment award or a new evidentiary inquiry. Accounting and other lawful implementation remain; they make no finding that an official authored, adopted or controlled the message.

The [predecision record and briefs](https://archive.org/details/micro_IA40386019_1795) support the statutory program, consolidated posture, competing commercial-speech arguments and separate admission that government speech was not raised or addressed below. The government denied waiver of Central Hudson in reply; that dispute is not the government-speech concession. Its reported effectiveness review is a proffer, not a numerical finding adopted here. #### Mandate, Remedy, and Stage

The Sixth Circuit judgment is affirmed. The consolidated review and enforcement proceedings return for implementation of the constitutional holding. Accounting, actual separability and any monetary relief remain subject to their own legal predicates. No fresh government-speech or effectiveness inquiry, new filing period, nationwide refund, fixed collection amount or invalidation of unrelated Council activities is directed. Ordinary mandate procedure applies.

### Lawrence v. Texas, No. 02-102

Merits decision, June 26, 2003

#### Chronology and Posture

539 U.S. 558. Argued March 26, 2003. On writ of certiorari to the Texas Court of Appeals, sitting en banc, which upheld Lawrence's and Garner's convictions under Texas Penal Code §21.06. The questions are whether Texas may punish private consensual adult same-sex intimacy, whether its selective prohibition violates equal protection, and whether Bowers v. Hardwick should be overruled.

The prosecution and review proceed on private, consensual conduct between adults. Lawrence and Garner pleaded no contest while preserving their constitutional objections and received fines. They seek reversal and relief from the prohibition as applied to that conduct; Texas seeks affirmance.

All nine Justices participated at argument or submission and at decision.

#### Judgment

**Reversed, 7–2.**

**Supporting:** Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Kennedy, Souter, Ginsburg and Breyer.
**Opposing:** Scalia and Thomas.

#### Opinion Topology

GINSBURG, J., delivered the opinion of the Court, in which STONE-ZSELA, C. J., and STEVENS, KENNEDY, SOUTER, and BREYER, JJ., joined throughout, and in which O'CONNOR, J., joined as to Part II. STONE-ZSELA, C. J., filed a concurring opinion. O'CONNOR, J., filed an opinion concurring in part and in the judgment. SCALIA, J., filed a dissenting opinion, in which THOMAS, J., joined. THOMAS, J., filed a separate dissenting opinion.

| Writing | Author | Joined by | Relationship to judgment | Scope joined |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Court opinion, Part I | Ginsburg | Stone-Zsela, Stevens, Kennedy, Souter, Breyer | Requires reversal, conviction vacatur and termination of the prosecutions | Private adult intimacy; express overruling of Bowers; six Justices |
| Court opinion, Part II | Ginsburg | Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Kennedy, Souter, Breyer | Independently requires the same conviction remedy | Ordinary equal protection; seven Justices |
| Concurrence | Stone-Zsela | Unjoined | Joins both parts and the complete remedy | Proposes express fundamental status, a compelling-objective and necessity rule, and substantive collateral effect |
| Concurrence in part and in the judgment | O'Connor | Unjoined | Joins Part II and reversal | Equality suffices; would retain Bowers |
| Dissent | Scalia | Thomas | Would affirm | Bowers and ordinary equal-protection review |
| Separate dissent | Thomas | Unjoined | Would affirm; also joins Scalia's dissent | Policy objection supplies no constitutional ground for reversal |

#### Controlling Propositions and Authority

##### I. Private consensual adult intimacy is protected against this criminal prohibition

**Holding and operative rule:** The liberty protected by the Fourteenth Amendment includes private consensual sexual intimacy between adults. A State may not criminally punish that conduct solely to enforce moral disapproval of the participants' intimacy, and Bowers v. Hardwick is overruled insofar as it permits that punishment; the convictions and penalties resting on the prohibition must be set aside.

**Authority:** Part I of Ginsburg's opinion of the Court, directly joined by Stone-Zsela, Stevens, Kennedy, Souter, Ginsburg and Breyer, including the author. All six adopt the complete rule, explanation, overruling and remedy.

**Controlling explanation:** Griswold and Eisenstadt protect individual intimate choice rather than merely a particular physical act. Casey retains the constitutional protection of the person's reproductive choice and supplies the related requirement that a departure from precedent have a justification beyond renewed disagreement. Those decisions do not already decide this prohibition; they expose the defect in Bowers's account of the personal interest at stake. Its isolated description of the act permits inherited disapproval to replace constitutional examination of a criminal intrusion into private adult intimacy.

Glucksberg sustained a different prohibition because protection of life, error, pressure on vulnerable people and medical responsibility rationally supported it. Its holding adopted no exclusive method requiring every liberty to have been historically described in the precise terms of the forbidden act. The accepted private, consensual adult setting here presents no comparable protective feature. Texas identifies no separate injury sufficient to justify punishment. Romer's demand for a connection to the actual disadvantage reinforces attention to real justification, while its preservation of Bowers makes express overruling necessary.

The dissent's reliance on longstanding prohibition and the continuing criminal precedent is substantial. But the continuing intrusion, the inadequacy of Bowers's description of liberty and its conflict with the individual-choice principles retained in the later cases warrant overruling. Continued criminal punishment supplies no comparable settled private-transaction reliance. An equality-only remedy would leave the same protected choice exposed to an evenhanded prohibition; the preserved liberty question therefore receives its own answer. The rule concerns private consensual adult intimacy. Its category does not include coercion, minor-involved conduct, public conduct or commercial sex, and it determines no civil relationship status.

##### II. The selective criminal disadvantage independently violates equal protection

**Holding and operative rule:** Under ordinary rational-basis review, Texas may not impose this criminal penalty on same-sex participants while leaving otherwise comparable private consensual adult conduct unpunished when the distinction has no rational connection to a legitimate reason for the additional disadvantage. The challenger must negate a reasonably conceivable legitimate explanation; that burden is met here because disapproval of the same-sex class does not explain a separate injury from the matched conduct.

**Authority:** Part II of the Court opinion, directly supported by Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Kennedy, Souter, Ginsburg and Breyer. This seven-Justice ground independently requires reversal of both convictions.

**Controlling explanation:** Romer requires a rational relationship to the actual disadvantage, while Heller preserves legislative generalizations, conceivable grounds and imperfect fit without formal findings, empirical certainty or a least-restrictive-means requirement. Moreno and Cleburne establish the limits of bare harm and irrational prejudice within that ordinary review. The relevant comparison is the same private, voluntary adult intimacy with different-sex participants. Texas identifies no difference in injury explaining why only these participants receive criminal punishment.

The authority to regulate an act generally does not answer the distinct question why the State adds a penalty for one class. For that reason, the conduct-based morality argument accepted by the dissent does not rationally explain this selective disability. The rule rests on the particular disadvantage. Because the selective penalty fails even ordinary review, the Court need not decide whether this same-sex distinction is a sexual-orientation classification subject to Shahar's intermediate scrutiny, and it adopts no universal prohibition on moral interests in legislation.

#### Precedent Treatment

- Bowers v. Hardwick, 478 U.S. 186: overruled insofar as it authorizes punishment of covered private consensual adult intimacy; it no longer sustains either these convictions or a comparable criminal prohibition within the protected category.
- Griswold v. Connecticut, 381 U.S. 479: its protection of intimate choice informs the personal liberty extended here.
- Eisenstadt v. Baird, 405 U.S. 438: its individual-choice principle informs the adult intimacy protected here.
- Planned Parenthood of Southeastern Pennsylvania v. Casey, 505 U.S. 833: its protected-choice foundation and stare-decisis requirements are applied; its six-step abortion-regulation framework retains that distinct setting.
- Washington v. Glucksberg: distinguished by its concrete protective grounds for the assisted-death prohibition; its holding establishes no exclusive historical-description method.
- Romer v. Evans, 517 U.S. 620: applied to the actual unequal disadvantage under ordinary review; its former preservation of Bowers gives way to Part I's express overruling.
- Heller v. Doe, 509 U.S. 312: applied to the challenger burden, conceivable grounds and required connection to the actual disadvantage.
- United States Department of Agriculture v. Moreno, 413 U.S. 528, and City of Cleburne v. Cleburne Living Center, 473 U.S. 432: applied for the ordinary-review limits on bare disadvantage and irrational prejudice.
- Shahar v. Bowers (March 2, 1998): Part II does not decide whether this same-sex criminal distinction is a sexual-orientation classification subject to its intermediate scrutiny, because the selective penalty fails even ordinary review.

#### Law After Decision

Private consensual adult sexual intimacy is protected from criminal punishment resting solely on moral disapproval. The protection covers the defined category of conduct, including a formally evenhanded prohibition, and applies in other covered nonfinal prosecutions and direct appeals. Texas's additional same-sex penalty also fails ordinary equal protection. Bowers supplies no authority for continued enforcement within that category. Courts must give the rule effect through the lawful procedures governing the case before them; this mandate requires vacatur and termination for Lawrence and Garner.

#### Separate Writings

**Stone-Zsela, concurring.** He joins both controlling grounds and the complete conviction remedy. He would expressly call the protected liberty fundamental and require government to establish a compelling harm-preventing objective and a closely fitted necessity for invading it. The claimant must first establish private, consensual adult conduct; the State then must supply actual reasons and evidence rather than presumed harm. Texas fails that requirement and independently fails the ordinary equality comparison. Casey, Griswold and Eisenstadt support this development without making Casey's abortion framework universal. Glucksberg's life-protection, coercion, error and professional concerns distinguish its assistance-in-death claim. Bowers must be overruled because inherited disapproval cannot itself justify this intrusion.

He understands the rule to apply under Griffith to covered nonfinal cases and under Teague's substantive exception in otherwise proper collateral proceedings because it removes the covered conduct from permissible criminal punishment. Jurisdiction, procedural default, a lawful reopening vehicle and independent statutory gates remain separately applicable. The criminal-review mandate does not itself reopen absent persons' judgments. Civil relationship status and conduct outside the adult, private, consensual category require their own governing law and facts.

**O'Connor, concurring in part and in the judgment.** She joins the ordinary-equality holding and reversal, but would retain Bowers and decide no additional liberty ground. The authority to prohibit conduct generally does not justify singling out same-sex participants for the additional criminal disadvantage. Romer and Heller permit ordinary legislative latitude while requiring a rational connection to that actual distinction. This conclusion neither adopts an orientation-based tier nor declares all moral interests illegitimate. Equality supplies complete relief from these convictions.

**Scalia, dissenting, joined by Thomas.** Bowers directly permits the specified prohibition, and the intimate-choice precedents do not justify overruling it in their view. Romer concerned a broad withdrawal of ordinary civil protection and expressly preserved the conduct question. They regard this statute as regulating conduct that the State may condemn on moral grounds, rather than imposing a distinct disability without a rational connection. The selective category therefore survives ordinary review. They reject the Court's extension of substantive liberty and would affirm both convictions. Their position does not depend on treating Glucksberg as an exclusive historical methodology or on equating every moral distinction with constitutionally permissible hostility.

**Thomas, dissenting.** He joins Scalia's dissent and separately states that disagreement with the wisdom of the criminal prohibition does not establish a constitutional right to invalidate it. Bowers and the limited reach of the intimate-choice cases govern his constitutional vote despite that policy objection.

No. 02-102; Texas Penal Code §21.06; Fourteenth Amendment. The constitutional adjudication rests on private, consensual conduct between adults as the basis of the convictions. The entry and search issue is outside the granted questions. Argued March 26, 2003.

#### Mandate, Remedy, and Stage

The case returns to the Texas courts to vacate both convictions and their dependent penalties and terminate the prosecutions. The liberty holding prevents Texas from curing the violation by imposing the same criminal prohibition on different-sex participants. Prosecution of the covered conduct must cease. Other covered nonfinal cases receive the substantive rule through their ordinary proceedings.

### Miller-El v. Cockrell, No. 01-7662

Decision, February 25, 2003

#### Chronology and Posture

Argued October 16, 2002. The Court reviews the Fifth Circuit's denial of a certificate of appealability after denial of federal habeas relief. The question is whether the proper certificate standard permits appellate debate about Miller-El's Batson jury-selection claim under the applicable habeas restrictions.

Chief Justice Stone-Zsela and Justices Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg, and Breyer participated at argument and decision.

#### Judgment

**Reversed and remanded, 8–1.**

**Supporting:** Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Ginsburg, Breyer.

**Opposing:** Thomas.

The Fifth Circuit must issue the issue-specific certificate and permit the authorized appeal. The conviction and sentence remain intact pending ordinary proceedings.

#### Opinion Topology

GINSBURG, J., delivered the opinion of the Court, in which STONE-ZSELA, C. J., and STEVENS, O'CONNOR, SCALIA, KENNEDY, SOUTER, and BREYER, JJ., joined in full. SCALIA, J., filed a concurring opinion while joining the complete opinion of the Court. THOMAS, J., filed a dissenting opinion.

#### Controlling Propositions and Authority

##### Certification tests reasonable debate under the actual habeas framework

**Holding and operative rule:** After a district court rejects constitutional merits, §2253(c) requires a substantial showing that reasonable jurists could debate its assessment under the applicable habeas restrictions, exceeding mere nonfrivolousness but not requiring proof of entitlement to a writ. Sections 2254(d)(2) and (e)(1) remain distinct: the certificate inquiry asks whether the district court's application is debatable, rather than requiring legal or factual unreasonableness to be conclusively proved by clear and convincing evidence before an appeal.

**Authority:** Ginsburg's opinion of the Court, joined in full by the eight-Justice coalition identified above, controls both holdings.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that certification precedes final adjudication. Hohn permits review of the judicial certificate denial; Slack requires an issue-specific substantial constitutional showing rather than ultimate success. Williams keeps objective unreasonableness distinct from ordinary disagreement and preserves each factual-review provision's function. At this stage the court considers those statutory restrictions when asking whether the district court's treatment permits reasonable debate. A claim indisputably barred by a governing habeas provision does not qualify merely because its abstract constitutional premise is arguable.

Thomas would require completed clear-and-convincing rebuttal of the state finding before certification. That construction makes final resolution of the disputed factual question a prerequisite to the appeal that would review it. The Court instead asks whether the district court's assessment under the applicable factual provisions is reasonably debatable, preserving those provisions for the merits appeal. Early and Visciotti require attention to the actual state reasoning and whole supported record, not automatic relief for imperfect wording. They do not collapse Slack's threshold into a final merits judgment. Abdur'Rahman's bar on review of a different authorization act does not govern this judicial certificate denial.

##### The cumulative jury-selection evidence warrants the Batson certificate

**Holding and operative rule:** Miller-El's combined strike pattern, comparative-juror evidence, disparate questioning, jury shuffles and historical office-practice evidence make the denial of his Batson claim reasonably debatable under the applicable habeas framework. He is entitled to a certificate identifying that claim and to appellate adjudication under the distinct governing statutory standards.

**Authority:** The same eight-Justice opinion controls.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that the evidence must be considered together. Prosecutors struck ten of eleven eligible African-American venire members. The comparisons, questioning, shuffles and office history add context to that pattern. Batson and Franklyn/Gonzalez distinguish the production of neutral explanations from the ultimate inquiry into purposeful discrimination; the parties' acceptance of the first two stages leaves the latter disputed.

The State's death-penalty and criminal-history explanations, substantive juror differences and credibility findings receive their proper statutory consideration. They are substantial responses, but do not establish that no reasonable jurist could debate the district court's assessment of the combined evidence. Numbers create neither a quota nor a conclusive presumption; office history does not itself assign present intent, and a delayed hearing does not automatically eliminate deference. The relevant non-demeanor evidence nevertheless permits meaningful examination of the credited explanations. The claim therefore warrants the appeal rather than a remand merely to recite the standard. Clearly established substantive law remains measured at the state merits adjudication; the authorized appeal must apply that law and the independent habeas restrictions to determine ultimate entitlement.

#### Precedent Treatment

- Hohn v. United States, 524 U.S. 236, and Slack v. McDaniel, 529 U.S. 473, are applied for reviewability, issue-specific certification and the reasonable-debate threshold.
- Williams v. Taylor, 529 U.S. 362, retains the separation of objective statutory unreasonableness and factual presumptions; this decision clarifies their role at certification.
- Batson v. Kentucky, 476 U.S. 79, and United States v. Franklyn / United States v. Gonzalez preserve the actual purposeful-discrimination inquiry after neutral explanations, with the ultimate persuasion burden unchanged.
- Early v. Packer and Woodford v. Visciotti (November 4, 2002) supply contextual statutory review within the certificate inquiry rather than a requirement to win the merits first.
- Abdur'Rahman v. Bell (December 10, 2002) retains its actual authorization-review bar; a judicial certificate denial is a distinct reviewable act.

#### Law After Decision

Certificate review requires meaningful preliminary assessment of the whole claim under applicable habeas restrictions. The factual provisions retain their separate functions without becoming a merged clear-and-convincing requirement of final success before appeal. This cumulative record establishes the right to a Batson appeal; ultimate discrimination and habeas relief remain for that appeal.

#### Separate Positions Relevant to the Decision

Scalia joins the opinion and emphasizes that statutory eligibility must be debatable as well as the underlying constitutional claim. He regards the State's individualized reasons and substantial evidence as strong merits arguments even though the complete record warrants appellate consideration.

Thomas would affirm. He reads §2254(e)(1) to require actual clear-and-convincing rebuttal at the certificate stage, distinguishing Slack's context. He finds the historical and shuffle evidence insufficient to show pretext in these particular strikes and requires comparisons to account for the prosecutors' combined reasons. He rejects reducing the factual presumption merely because the Batson hearing followed voir dire by two years. His additional doubt under the majority's test does not replace that distinct statutory ground.

No. 01-7662; 28 U.S.C. §§2253(c), 2254(d)(2), 2254(e)(1). The parties accept the prima facie showing and production of neutral reasons; credibility and purposeful discrimination remain disputed. The state findings and the full comparative and historical evidence inform the certificate inquiry.

#### Mandate, Remedy, and Stage

The Fifth Circuit must issue the Batson certificate and permit briefing and decision of that appeal, applying the governing substantive-law cutoff and distinct habeas standards. Ultimate purposeful discrimination and entitlement to a writ remain for those proceedings.

### Sosa v. Alvarez-Machain and United States v. Alvarez-Machain, Nos. 03-339 and 03-485

Merits decision, June 29, 2004

#### Chronology and Posture

The two dockets are consolidated. Argued March 30, 2004; decided June 29, 2004. On writs of certiorari to the Ninth Circuit, which sustained the Alien Tort Statute damages judgment against Sosa and revived the Federal Tort Claims Act claim against the United States. DEA officials arranged a seizure by Mexican nationals, including Sosa, followed by detention in Mexico for less than a day before delivery into lawful United States custody. After acquittal, Alvarez sought civil recovery. The questions concern a domestic action for that asserted arbitrary detention and whether domestic planning removes the same foreign-detention claim from the FTCA's foreign-country exception.

#### Controlling Propositions and Authority

##### I. A federal forum, a domestic action and sovereign consent remain separate

**Controlling proposition:** Section 1350 gives district courts jurisdiction over an alien's civil tort claim alleging a violation of the law of nations or a United States treaty, but does not itself convert every alleged violation into a domestic damages action or waive sovereign immunity. The international norm, its actor coverage, the domestic action, personal responsibility, causation and authority for relief must each be established under the governing law; the existing specifically adopted actions retain their own predicates.

**Authority:** Part I of Stevens's opinion, joined by all nine Justices, applies these common distinctions and preserves the existing holdings without making their original recognition rationales unanimous.

**Controlling explanation:** Abebe-Jira v. Negewo separates §1350's forum from the received domestic action for personal official torture. Kadic v. Karadzic and Doe v. Karadzic apply the same separation to defined genocide and core war crimes. The jurisdictional character of the statute cannot silently erase those holdings. Conversely, their recognition of particular actions does not establish liability for a different asserted norm or supply the United States' consent to suit.

The official-torture action reaches deliberate participation in or personal supervision of severe physical or mental suffering intentionally inflicted under official authority for interrogation, punishment or intimidation. The existing genocide action runs against an individual for personally perpetrating, planning, or directing genocide, whether or not that individual acted under state authority. The plaintiff must establish intent to destroy, in whole or in part, a national, ethnical, racial, or religious group as such, through killing members, causing serious bodily or mental harm, deliberately imposing conditions calculated to bring about the group's physical destruction in whole or in part, imposing measures intended to prevent births within the group, or forcibly transferring the group's children to another group, together with the defendant's qualifying responsibility, causation, and a lawful basis for relief. The core-war-crime action reaches personally perpetrated, planned or directed deliberate killing, torture and grave sexual violence, with the relevant armed conflict, protected victim, prohibited act and connection to the conflict. Each retains its responsibility, causation and remedy requirements; official position alone is insufficient.

Those defined actions furnish the existing law against which this asserted addition must be examined. United States v. Alvarez-Machain decided the treaty-based objection to criminal trial. Its trial-authority holding neither authorized the capture nor supplied a civil action, and it does not resolve the independent predicates now presented.

##### II. The asserted brief-detention norm does not support this additional action

**Controlling proposition:** Recognition of an additional received action requires a specifically defined, widely accepted and obligatory international prohibition whose content and actor coverage support judicial application, together with a sound domestic remedial basis. The asserted rule that any detention unauthorized by domestic law is an internationally actionable tort does not establish those predicates for the detention of less than a day in Mexico followed by lawful custody here.

**Authority:** Part II of Stevens's opinion is joined by Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Kennedy, Souter, Ginsburg and Breyer. Seven direct joins supply the controlling ground for rejecting this new action.

**Controlling explanation:** Abebe-Jira and Kadic/Doe recognize determinate personal torts through a confined domestic reception of international law. They support a disciplined inquiry into an additional action, rather than either automatic incorporation of every international objection or categorical withdrawal of that received route. Their existing conduct, actor and remedial requirements explain why the source objection in the separate opinion does not justify closing the inquiry altogether.

Alvarez relies on article 9 of the Universal Declaration of Human Rights, article 9 of the International Covenant on Civil and Political Rights, other international statements and the absence of an express minimum detention period. Those materials do not establish the particular customary rule and domestic enforcement predicate his broad theory requires. The Declaration does not itself impose binding treaty obligations. The Covenant's non-self-executing status prevents it from itself supplying this damages action, although a treaty with that status may still inform evidence of customary law. General language against arbitrary detention is not enough to make every violation of local arrest law an internationally actionable tort.

The Restatement's treatment of prolonged detention as state policy illustrates the specificity missing from this claim; it does not create a duration safe harbor. The defect concerns the proposed norm and its domestic enforcement basis. The existing official-torture, genocide and core-war-crime actions remain available on their established terms. This award rests on the asserted brief arbitrary-detention action and must be set aside.

##### III. Domestic planning does not relocate the same foreign-detention claim

**Controlling proposition:** A claim seeking compensation for the detention injury occurring in Mexico arises in a foreign country within 28 U.S.C. §2680(k), even when domestic planning or direction is alleged as a cause of that same detention. The claimant must establish a waived claim on its actual injury and statutory predicates; a domestic causal link alone does not avoid the exception, and §2680(h)'s law-enforcement proviso does not displace it. A genuinely independent domestic injury falls outside this holding.

**Authority:** Part III of Stevens's opinion is joined by all nine Justices at this bounded foreign-detention scope.

**Controlling explanation:** The asserted false detention took place and endured in Mexico; its liability-creating detention and injury were there. Planning in the United States may have causal importance, but it does not change the claim for which Alvarez seeks compensation. If an allegation of domestic direction alone removed that claim from the exception, nearly every overseas operation could be recast through a headquarters instruction and §2680(k) would lose its enacted function.

Smith v. United States enforced the foreign-country exception together with the FTCA's liability and place-of-law provisions for acts wholly in Antarctica. The present decision applies that statutory structure to the additional domestic-planning argument; Smith did not itself decide this mixed-location question. Richards v. United States identifies the governing law under §1346(b), but that choice-of-law inquiry operates within the waiver and its exceptions. Even a domestic causal wrong subject to domestic law does not alone establish consent for the same excluded Mexican detention.

Section 2680(h)'s law-enforcement proviso removes specified intentional torts from that subsection's exclusion when its own conditions are satisfied. It does not repeal the separate foreign-country exception. The asserted detention claim therefore must be dismissed. The Court resolves a claim whose detention and injury occurred together abroad; a separately supported domestic injury requires its own waiver and exception analysis.

#### Precedent Treatment

- Abebe-Jira v. Negewo is applied and preserved: its jurisdiction/action distinction, confined official-torture action and distinct remedy rules remain effective.
- Kadic v. Karadzic and Doe v. Karadzic are applied and preserved at their defined genocide, core-war-crime, actor, responsibility and remedy predicates; they do not make every international norm privately enforceable.
- United States v. Alvarez-Machain is distinguished as a treaty-based criminal trial-authority decision. Its holding supplies neither capture authorization nor this civil remedy.
- Smith v. United States is extended from its wholly Antarctic acts to the domestic planning of the same Mexican detention; its territorial and statutory-waiver analysis remains confined to its actual work.
- Richards v. United States is applied within the FTCA's consent structure. Selection of domestic law does not remove a claim from an independently applicable exception.

#### Law After Decision

The specifically adopted international-tort actions remain enforceable on their existing terms. A proposed additional action must satisfy the norm, actor and domestic remedial requirements stated here; §1350 jurisdiction alone is insufficient. The broad brief-detention theory presented fails that inquiry. Under the FTCA, domestic planning or direction of the same foreign detention does not avoid §2680(k), which operates independently of §2680(h)'s law-enforcement proviso.

#### Separate Positions Relevant to the Decision

Justice Scalia, joined by Justice Thomas, joins Parts I and III and concurs in the ATS reversal on an additional-action source ground. They distinguish an international prohibition, federal jurisdiction and domestic authority to impose damages. Section 1350 supplies a forum, while the offered Declaration and Covenant do not themselves enact this private action. The received common-law setting is the strongest argument for the Court's method, but in their view it does not supply sufficient congressional authorization for the additional arbitrary-detention liability.

Their earlier writings in Abebe-Jira and Kadic/Doe preserve that source objection. Suter requires an actual source of private liability; O'Melveny and Atherton reject federal rules derived merely from a general federal interest; Meyer keeps forum, consent and substantive liability separate. Central Bank's established securities action and Musick, Peeler's treatment of incidents of recognized liability do not independently authorize this different extension. They respect the already adopted official-torture, genocide and core-war-crime holdings as precedents at their exact scopes, but decline to extend them to the present detention action without a sufficient enacted source. That independently sufficient ground explains their ATS judgment agreement and their refusal to join Part II's recognition methodology.

Justice Ginsburg, joined by Justice Breyer, joins the Court's opinion and explains the conduct-based reason for the bounded FTCA result. Section 2680(k) must be read with §1346(b)(1), which locates the governing law by the relevant act or omission. For this claim, Mexico was the place of the last significant act necessary to create the alleged tort: the seizure and detention took place there, and the detention injury accrued there. Domestic planning was causally relevant but did not by itself complete the false-detention claim or convert its Mexican core into a domestic injury.

Richards supplies the place-of-conduct inquiry within the FTCA's statutory structure. Smith enforced the exception for acts wholly in Antarctica; its actual scope does not require an injury-only test for every combination of domestic and foreign conduct. These Justices therefore support the Court's common disposition of the same Mexican detention notwithstanding headquarters planning, while retaining a construction attentive to liability-creating conduct. Their direct join in Part III does not adopt a categorical rule that any injury abroad is conclusive regardless of where the tortious conduct occurred.

Justice Breyer joins the Court's opinion and adds a comity concern. International acceptance of a substantive prohibition does not necessarily establish agreement that the courts of every nation may enforce it against foreign persons for foreign conduct. Territorial or nationality connections ordinarily reduce that concern. For certain grave international crimes, acceptance of universal criminal jurisdiction supports related civil recovery because many legal systems combine criminal and civil adjudication; torture, genocide and core war crimes fit that established inquiry. He identifies no comparable procedural consensus for this brief arbitrary arrest. This further reason supports rejection here while preserving the existing actions and his join in Part II; it supplies no general controlling universal-jurisdiction or exhaustion rule.
