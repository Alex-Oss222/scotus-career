### Cavanaugh v. Roller, No. 92-1510

Merits decision, November 30, 1993

#### Chronology and Posture

Argued November 8, 1993. On writ of certiorari to the United States Court of Appeals for the Fourth Circuit, reviewing Roller v. Cavanaugh, 984 F.2d 120 (1993), which reversed summary judgment for state parole officials on the prospective-relief claim and remanded for a declaration and, if necessary, an injunction. The questions are whether the retrospective replacement of annual parole reconsideration with biennial reconsideration violates the Ex Post Facto Clause, and whether this request for prospective relief may proceed under 42 U.S.C. §1983 rather than only through habeas corpus. The official- and individual-capacity damages dismissals were not appealed.

Roller committed voluntary manslaughter and grand larceny on December 13, 1982, and received consecutive thirty- and five-year sentences on March 25, 1983. The offense-date rule in S.C. Code §24-21-620 required reconsideration every twelve months after denial. The 1986 provisions in §§24-21-645 and -650 required reconsideration every two years for violent offenders, including persons convicted of voluntary manslaughter, without changing the initial eligibility date. The Board denied parole October 31, 1990 and scheduled reconsideration for October 31, 1992; the lower-court account records a second denial in 1992. Parole itself remained discretionary.

At argument, the officials represented that Roller had not received a 1993 hearing, acknowledged an obligation to provide one under the outstanding decisions, and described scheduling difficulties. They also represented that, after rejection, the Board was fixed to the annual or biennial interval and its manual supplied no expedited-hearing provision. The record establishes no later completed hearing. That account of ordinary reconsideration is distinct from §24-21-610: on medical information showing a physical condition so serious that the prisoner would not reasonably be expected to live more than one year, the Board may parole him no sooner than one year before his prescribed eligibility date. No such medical predicate is shown for Roller. The same section requires a report from a duly qualified psychiatrist or psychologist on mental condition and ability to adjust to life outside prison before parole for a prisoner who has served ten consecutive years or more. Neither provision supplies an ordinary intervening annual-review right.

South Carolina's Act No. 184, approved June 21, 1993, adds §16-1-60(B), requiring that an offense be defined as violent when committed. Section 266 makes that subsection retroactive to all persons convicted under state law, while making the Act's other provisions prospective to crimes committed after its effective date. It saves pending proceedings and existing, acquired, or incurred rights and liabilities; sentences pronounced on or after effectiveness must comply with the classification system except when that would require a greater penalty than the crime-date penalty. Section 268 provides that §267 takes effect July 1, 1993. Section 269 makes the Act, other than §§267 and 268, effective January 1, 1994; that is the effective date of the relevant classification provisions. Those provisions are not already operative on November 30. Counsel discussed anticipated January 1 mootness and disputed its consequences; neither side represented that the controversy was already moot at argument.

Griffin v. State, 315 S.C. 285, 433 S.E.2d 862, decided July 19, 1993, with rehearing denied August 17, adopted the Fourth Circuit's reasoning in Roller and overruled Gunter v. State's contrary holding. Griffin is confirming, available state-court authority. Its reasoning reinforces the distinction between a procedural label and a substantive loss of parole consideration; it does not establish completed compliance in this case.

All nine Justices participate at argument and decision: Stone-Zsela, Blackmun, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, and Ginsburg. No case-specific nonparticipation is established.

#### Judgment

The Court holds the controversy live on the present record and affirms the Fourth Circuit's prospective-relief judgment, 6–3. Annual reconsideration under the pre-amendment rule is restored without directing release; the unappealed damages dismissals remain undisturbed.

#### Controlling Propositions and Authority

##### A live claim for the outstanding hearing

**Controlling proposition:** A prospective parole-hearing claim remains live on this record because a court can still require outstanding consideration and no completed compliance or effective legal change has eliminated that relief. Enactment of a future change, coupled with a prediction of later mootness, does not establish present mootness before the relevant effective date.

**Authority:** Part I of O'Connor's opinion, joined by Stone-Zsela, Blackmun, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, and Ginsburg; all nine adopt this threshold holding.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court can still grant useful relief. Church of Scientology requires attention to meaningful relief that remains possible, even when a court cannot undo every consequence of the challenged conduct. Here a direction securing the outstanding hearing can provide the opportunity Roller seeks. The officials acknowledged an obligation to schedule it, but an acknowledgment is not completed consideration, and the record establishes no later hearing. Griffin confirms the annual-review obligation and rejects Gunter's contrary analysis; it does not establish that Roller received its practical benefit. Act No. 184 likewise does not end this controversy on November 30. Its relevant provisions take effect January 1, and its savings language must be respected rather than assumed away. The parties' forecasts of later mootness cannot substitute for an operative event eliminating present relief. The Court decides neither the precise consequences of January 1 nor the remedy if a later event actually moots further proceedings. No basis for present mootness vacatur exists.

**Precedent treatment:**

- Church of Scientology of California v. United States, decision of November 16, 1992: applies its requirement to consider meaningful remaining relief; the rule is unchanged.
- United States v. Munsingwear, Inc., 340 U.S. 36 (1950): its mootness-vacatur setting is absent because no present mooting event is established; no future vacatur rule is decided.
- Griffin v. State, 315 S.C. 285 (1993): recognizes its existing annual-review holding as confirming state authority, without equating an outstanding legal obligation with completed relief or deciding a separately sufficient state constitutional ground.

##### Section 1983 permits this opportunity-only claim

**Controlling proposition:** A state prisoner may seek prospective declaratory and injunctive relief under §1983 against responsible officials to restore the legally required frequency of discretionary parole consideration when the judgment neither orders release nor establishes an entitlement to release or a shorter period of custody. Habeas is not the exclusive channel for this claim, and no general state-remedy exhaustion requirement attaches to the properly brought §1983 action.

**Authority:** Part II of O'Connor's opinion, joined by Stone-Zsela, Blackmun, Stevens, O'Connor, Kennedy, and Souter; six Justices adopt the same rule and affirm this remedial channel.

**Controlling explanation:** The relief sought requires a decision, not a favorable decision. Preiser reserves habeas for the claim there presented, which sought restoration of credits determining immediate or speedier release; it does not make habeas exclusive whenever a prisoner's ultimate hope is freedom. Wolff and Gerstein support the distinction between enforcing lawful procedures and directing release. An annual consideration order leaves the Board free to deny parole under the same lawful standards and determines neither the validity of Roller's conviction nor the duration of his confinement. The officials' strongest objection is that restoring an earlier opportunity may ultimately shorten custody. That possible consequence explains why the opportunity matters, but it does not change what the judgment itself awards. Chatman-Bey's broader treatment of a federal prisoner's eligibility challenge does not control this state prisoner's limited process remedy. Once this claim is properly within §1983, Patsy forecloses a general exhaustion prerequisite. The Court does not declare habeas unavailable or extend this holding to a judgment awarding release or credits that themselves shorten custody.

**Precedent treatment:**

- Preiser v. Rodriguez, 411 U.S. 475 (1973): distinguishes its exclusive-habeas holding for relief determining immediate or speedier release; that rule remains controlling within its scope.
- Wolff v. McDonnell, 418 U.S. 539 (1974), and Gerstein v. Pugh, 420 U.S. 103 (1975): apply their distinction between process relief and a release order to this mandatory consideration opportunity.
- Patsy v. Board of Regents, 457 U.S. 496 (1982): applies its rejection of a general exhaustion prerequisite after determining that §1983 is an available channel; it does not itself decide that antecedent question.
- Chatman-Bey v. Thornburgh, 864 F.2d 804 (D.C. Cir. 1988) (en banc): declines to extend its federal eligibility/habeas analysis to require exclusive habeas for this limited state-prisoner remedy.

##### The binding loss of annual reconsideration increases punishment on this record

**Controlling proposition:** The Ex Post Facto Clause forbids applying to a pre-enactment offender a later statute that removes every alternate mandatory parole-reconsideration opportunity by doubling the post-denial interval from twelve to twenty-four months, where ordinary parole depends on consideration and no retained ordinary means of obtaining the intervening review is established. This binding loss of a meaningful release opportunity makes punishment more onerous despite unchanged initial eligibility, maximum sentence, and ultimate parole discretion; the prisoner need not prove that the omitted hearing would have produced release.

**Authority:** Part III of O'Connor's opinion, joined by Stone-Zsela, Blackmun, Stevens, O'Connor, Kennedy, and Souter; six Justices adopt the rule, its application to Roller, and the prospective remedy. Ginsburg's conditional agreement in her separate opinion adds no vote to this controlling coalition.

**Controlling explanation:** The offense-date statute required an occasion for the Board to exercise its discretion every year. The later statute removes that occasion for an entire intervening year on the basis of offense class. Weaver rejects a vested-right objection to protection of a legally available avenue to reduced imprisonment. Miller shows why unchanged outer sentence limits and the possibility of the same ultimate result do not answer a binding adverse change. Collins confines the Clause to crime and punishment while preserving scrutiny of punitive effects concealed by procedural labels; Dobbert therefore does not settle this case merely by calling a calendar procedural. The State retains release discretion, but that discretion cannot benefit Roller during an ordinary hearing the law prevents. Its narrow medical authority, requiring a condition associated with life expectancy of no more than one year, is no substitute for general intervening reconsideration and has no established application here. Griffin's repudiation of Gunter reinforces this conclusion. The Court reserves individualized scheduling and systems retaining meaningful interim reconsideration, and does not constitutionalize every administrative inconvenience.

**Precedent treatment:**

- Weaver v. Graham, 450 U.S. 24 (1981): extends its protection of a legally available avenue to reduced imprisonment to the binding loss of this mandatory consideration opportunity; no vested entitlement to release is required.
- Miller v. Florida, 482 U.S. 423 (1987): applies its distinction between binding adverse legal changes and flexible guidance; unchanged outer limits or a possibly identical outcome are not sufficient answers.
- Collins v. Youngblood, 497 U.S. 37 (1990): applies its punishment-related limitation and rejects a free-standing rule against every procedural disadvantage; no broader substantial-rights theory is restored.
- Dobbert v. Florida, 432 U.S. 282 (1977): distinguishes permissible procedural alteration from the binding withdrawal of a meaningful opportunity for earlier release on this record.
- Griffin v. State, 315 S.C. 285 (1993): treats its adoption of Roller's analysis and overruling of Gunter v. State, 298 S.C. 113 (1989), as confirming state authority, not an independent substitute for this Court's federal analysis.

#### Limits and Questions Not Reached

The decision governs this mandatory annual-to-biennial change and the actual availability of ordinary intervening consideration. It does not decide individualized postponement based on a determination that no material change could justify release during the omitted year, a system preserving meaningful interim review, changed voting rules or suitability criteria, abolition of parole, or the reach of every extraordinary clemency power. No general probability formula or requirement to prove the parole outcome is adopted. Counsel's representation about the manual is not a finding about an independently recovered complete manual. The medical authority and the report required before release of a prisoner with ten consecutive years of service remain distinct from ordinary hearing frequency. Independent due-process and state constitutional claims are not additional questions decided here. Future statutory implementation, actual January 1 mootness, and any later vacatur dispute are reserved. The damages dismissals remain outside this review.

#### Precedent Treatment and Current-Law Effect

The Court applies Church of Scientology's remaining-relief rule and leaves mootness-vacatur doctrine unchanged. Preiser's exclusive-habeas holding remains operative for its release-determining relief; Wolff, Gerstein, and Patsy support the distinct §1983 process remedy recognized here. Weaver and Miller extend to the binding loss of the annual opportunity under the conditions stated, while Collins and Dobbert preserve the distinction between increased punishment and genuinely procedural alteration. Griffin confirms the state court's adoption of the same underlying analysis and its overruling of Gunter; this Court neither reviews Griffin's judgment nor treats a federal disposition as automatically erasing it. No Supreme Court precedent is overruled.

Effective November 30, 1993, this limited opportunity-only claim may proceed under §1983 without a general state-remedy exhaustion requirement, and South Carolina may not impose the challenged retrospective biennial interval on Roller under the record established here. The constitutional rule protects the mandatory occasion for discretionary consideration; it neither entitles him to parole nor freezes every feature of parole administration at the crime date. The Board retains its release discretion and lawful suitability standards. Prospective federal relief rests on the federal constitutional holding, not merely on a direction to obey state law. Act No. 184's relevant classification provisions become operative only on their specified January 1 date, with their savings and transition terms preserved. This decision does not itself adjudicate their later effect.

#### Separate Positions Relevant to the Decision

Scalia concurs in the conclusion that the case is presently live and dissents from affirmance. Thomas joins his entire separate opinion; Ginsburg joins its channel discussion, Part A, only. That discussion treats a statutory postponement of eligibility for renewed release consideration as a challenge to the terms of custody, even though a favorable hearing is not certain to produce release. Preiser's protection of the specific habeas remedy, reinforced by Chatman-Bey's analysis of a federal prisoner's eligibility claim, would require dismissal of this §1983 claim without prejudice to habeas. They distinguish a challenge to the fairness of an otherwise available hearing and do not require habeas for every prison-procedure claim. They find no exhaustion exception merely because Gunter previously stood in the way; Griffin now supplies favorable state authority.

In Part B, Scalia and Thomas would reject the constitutional claim if the Court reaches it. Weaver's mandatory credits and Miller's binding sentencing range differ from the unchanged discretion to grant or deny parole. They regard the reduced frequency, without further demonstrated punitive operation, as insufficient to establish increased punishment under Collins. They do not accept the State's broader position that every parole change is outside the Clause, invoke a vested-right requirement, infer an undisclosed expedited-review policy, or direct the State to disregard Griffin. This alternative constitutional position has two votes and does not control.

Ginsburg separately explains her conditional agreement with the Court's constitutional conclusion while maintaining her habeas objection and dissent from the judgment. Chatman-Bey's treatment of a federal eligibility claim informs her view that this timing restriction concerns custody; she recognizes its different statutory and procedural setting but finds the distinction between timing eligibility and the fairness of an available hearing material here. On the constitutional issue, her Warren dissent had insisted on an opportunity to establish whether later parole guidelines actually operated rigidly; it did not itself find a violation. Here the statute, appendix, and account of actual operation establish a fixed lost occasion for consideration. Weaver and Miller therefore support the Court's narrow result if the claim is properly reached. She neither assumes that a hearing would produce release nor supplies a seventh join in Part III. Her constitutional agreement remains part of her separate, noncontrolling explanation.

### Heck v. Humphrey, No. 93-6188

Merits decision, June 24, 1994

#### Chronology and Posture

512 U.S. 477. Argued April 18, 1994; decided June 24, 1994. On writ of certiorari to the Seventh Circuit, 997 F.2d 355, affirming dismissal without prejudice. The question is whether a state prisoner may pursue §1983 damages claims necessarily negating an outstanding conviction and how that action relates to habeas. Heck seeks reinstatement; the officials seek affirmance.

Heck was serving a fifteen-year voluntary-manslaughter sentence when he alleged unlawful investigation, destruction of exculpatory evidence and an unlawful voice-identification procedure by prosecutors James Humphrey and Robert Ewbank and investigator Michael Krinoph. He sought compensatory and punitive damages, not release or an injunction. The allegations are not findings. His conviction was upheld on direct review; one federal habeas petition failed for lack of exhaustion and another was denied. The lower courts characterized the action as necessarily challenging the conviction; Heck did not dispute that characterization in his petition or principal merits brief, and the late reply does not establish a separately preserved independent-injury count requiring remand.

Chief Justice Stone-Zsela and Justices Blackmun, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas and Ginsburg participated at argument and decision. No Justice is recused.

#### Judgment

**Dismissal without prejudice affirmed, 9–0.**

**Supporting:** Stone-Zsela, Blackmun, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg.

Dismissal rests on lack of present cognizability, rejecting conversion into habeas and a general exhaustion rationale.

No present damages action, stay or remand; a later claim may proceed after qualifying invalidation, subject to remaining elements and defenses.

No release, misconduct finding, damages award, immunity determination or state-law preclusion ruling is ordered. The claim barred by this prerequisite has not accrued; its limitations period is not consumed while the prerequisite remains unmet. Independently cognizable injuries are not barred merely because a conviction exists.

#### Controlling Propositions and Authority

##### I. Necessary invalidity on the present in-custody record

**Controlling proposition:** A state prisoner in Heck's position, in custody with a lawful opportunity to challenge the conviction, has no presently cognizable §1983 damages claim when success necessarily establishes the invalidity of that outstanding conviction or sentence, unless the judgment has undergone qualifying invalidation. The rule decides that setting; it does not make present habeas availability a universal element or decide plaintiffs who never had a realistic custody-review opportunity or whose custody has ended.

**Authority:** Part I of Justice O'Connor's Opinion of the Court, joined by Chief Justice Stone-Zsela and Justices Blackmun, Stevens, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas and Ginsburg. All nine directly adopt this bounded rule; the distinct sufficient rationales are not added together to obtain its support.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that the necessary effect of the civil claim, not its connection with a criminal prosecution, determines the present bar. Preiser identifies the special statutory channel for obtaining release from unlawful custody. Wolff preserves civil claims whose success does not determine an entitlement to release, and Patsy rejects a general exhaustion condition for civil-rights actions. None answers this damages claim simply by identifying the requested form of relief. Heck seeks money, but the accepted characterization of his action makes invalidity of the operative conviction indispensable to success. The absence of a release demand therefore does not establish present cognizability. Conversely, a mere possibility that civil liability might cast doubt on a conviction is insufficient. The Court imposes no jurisdictional immunity for officials and decides no constitutional misconduct claim. The question here concerns a prisoner who had a lawful avenue to contest custody; the broader consequences of lost or unavailable custody review do not receive a controlling answer.

**Precedent treatment:**

- Preiser v. Rodriguez, 411 U.S. 475 (1973): its release-channel distinction informs the new, bounded damages prerequisite; its holding is not misdescribed as already deciding damages-only claims.
- Wolff v. McDonnell, 418 U.S. 539 (1974): preserves civil relief compatible with the operative conviction and custody judgment.
- Patsy v. Board of Regents, 457 U.S. 496 (1982): remains the rule against general §1983 exhaustion; this decision addresses present cognizability instead.

##### II. Statutory coordination supports this limited prerequisite

**Controlling proposition:** For the in-custody, conviction-negating damages claim described in Part I, coordination of §1983 with the available habeas channel supports favorable invalidation as a prerequisite, with the common-law malicious-prosecution analogy implementing that coordination. This is a new, limited restriction on §1983, not conversion of the damages complaint into habeas, a general exhaustion rule, or adoption of every malicious-prosecution element.

**Authority:** Part II of O'Connor's Opinion of the Court, joined by Stone-Zsela, Blackmun, Stevens and Souter; five Justices adopt this sufficient statutory explanation at this limited scope. Scalia, Kennedy, Thomas and Ginsburg do not join Part II.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court recognizes the cost of withholding an otherwise available form of compensation. Section 1983 does not expressly require favorable termination, and habeas cannot award the damages Heck requests. Those objections prevent treating the prerequisite as an obvious consequence of the damages label or as a cost-free procedural arrangement. Preiser nevertheless requires respect for the specific route by which this prisoner can contest custody. Wolff and Patsy preserve the civil remedy without answering whether it may establish the opposite of an outstanding criminal judgment. Carey permits common-law principles to inform the remedy; the favorable-termination analogy supplies a way to coordinate these statutes without importing malice or want of probable cause into every constitutional claim. Deferred accrual preserves a later compensation action if lawful invalidation occurs. This reasoning suffices for Heck because his civil theory necessarily negates the judgment and he has had a lawful custody-review channel. It does not resolve the materially different position of a person lacking that opportunity or no longer in custody.

**Precedent treatment:**

- Preiser v. Rodriguez: extends statutory coordination to the particular damages claim while preserving the distinct relief and conditions of habeas.
- Wolff v. McDonnell: preserves its permissible civil-remedy category; factual overlap with prosecution does not remove a claim from that category.
- Patsy v. Board of Regents: distinguishes general exhaustion from the newly adopted prerequisite; exhaustion alone cannot satisfy it.
- Carey v. Piphus, 435 U.S. 247 (1978): applies common-law remedial guidance as an aid to construing the federal remedy, not a wholesale importation of a state tort.

##### III. Qualifying invalidation and deferred accrual

**Controlling proposition:** The claim barred under Part I becomes cognizable only when the conviction or sentence has been reversed on direct review, expunged by executive order, declared invalid by a state tribunal authorized to do so, or called into question by a federal court's issuance of a writ of habeas corpus. That claim accrues only upon such invalidation; exhaustion, unsuccessful review, or loss of a review opportunity is not invalidation, and neither immediate accrual with equitable tolling nor a preservation stay is required for this unaccrued claim.

**Authority:** Part III of O'Connor's Opinion of the Court, joined by Stone-Zsela, Blackmun, Stevens, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas and Ginsburg; all nine directly adopt the four invalidation routes and the accrual consequence for the barred claim.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court makes nonaccrual integral to the prerequisite. A rule withholding the cause of action while allowing its limitations period to expire would frustrate the later remedy the rule preserves. Carey's use of appropriate common-law remedial principles supports considering the completion of the claim rather than pretending that a presently barred action must already be filed. The Seventh Circuit's suggested equitable protection for a diligent litigant addresses a different premise: accrual before the invalidity prerequisite is met. The Court does not adopt that premise. Heck's conviction was sustained on direct review; one federal petition failed for lack of exhaustion and another was denied. Neither result meets any qualifying invalidation route. Patsy's protection against general exhaustion remains intact, but completing review cannot itself establish the opposite of the judgment reviewed. Dismissal without prejudice therefore preserves the possibility of a later action following invalidation, subject to its remaining elements and defenses. Accrual of an independently cognizable injury is not decided by this rule.

**Precedent treatment:**

- Carey v. Piphus: applies its common-law-informed approach to the federal remedy without imposing additional constitutional-liability elements.
- Patsy v. Board of Regents: preserves the distinction between exhaustion and existence of a cognizable damages claim.

###### Limits and questions not reached

Recognition of a conviction's invalidation by an authorized state tribunal identifies one circumstance in which the present §1983 prerequisite is satisfied. It does not itself create jurisdiction, confer authority to reopen the underlying conviction, or prescribe grounds upon which a state tribunal must grant collateral relief. Those questions remain governed by the independently applicable law authorizing that tribunal's action.

##### IV. Independent injuries, claim-specific analysis and this dismissal

**Controlling proposition:** A claim whose success does not necessarily establish invalidity falls outside this prerequisite even if it concerns the same investigation or trial; courts must compare the civil claim's elements with the basis of the conviction, and compensatory recovery still requires an actual compensable injury. On the accepted characterization of Heck's presented action, dismissal without prejudice stands on lack of present cognizability, without deciding misconduct, immunity, personal responsibility, preclusion, damages, or entitlement to release.

**Authority:** Part IV of O'Connor's Opinion of the Court, joined by Stone-Zsela, Blackmun, Stevens, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas and Ginsburg; all nine directly adopt this boundary, application and remedy.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court does not equate every investigative wrong with an invalid conviction. Wolff preserves compatible civil claims, while Cavanaugh permits an opportunity-only parole claim whose success establishes neither invalidity nor shorter custody. An unlawful-search claim may likewise coexist with a valid conviction because independent source, inevitable discovery or harmless error can preserve the criminal judgment. That possibility neither saves every Fourth Amendment claim nor defeats every evidence-related claim. Carey still requires a compensable injury; conviction or imprisonment injury cannot be recovered while its necessary invalidity remains unestablished. Ordinary causation, immunity and preclusion questions remain separate. Here the lower courts characterized the action as necessarily attacking the conviction, and Heck did not dispute that characterization in his petition or principal merits brief. His late reply does not establish a separately preserved independent-injury count requiring remand. The Court accepts that review posture without making findings that officials committed the alleged wrongs or that every conceivable severable theory would fail.

**Precedent treatment:**

- Wolff v. McDonnell: preserves the distinction between compatible civil relief and relief dependent on invalidating custody.
- Cavanaugh v. Roller, November 30, 1993: distinguishes its opportunity-only claim; the existing §1983 holding remains intact.
- Carey v. Piphus: applies the requirement of actual compensable injury without adjudicating the amount or merits here.

#### Authority limits and questions not reached

The nine-vote propositions in Parts I, III and IV and the five-vote statutory explanation in Part II have direct support. No Marks aggregation is necessary or used. The common-law rationale extending beyond continued custody has four votes, as does the affirmative statutory protection for people genuinely outside the habeas intersection. Neither becomes a Court holding. No universal constitutional malicious-prosecution action, automatic state-law preclusion, automatic conversion into habeas, or abstention rule for pending criminal proceedings is adopted. The application of this prerequisite to former prisoners and persons who never had a realistic custody-review opportunity remains unresolved by the Court. Custis's separate no-meaningful-review reservation for the ACCA sentencing channel remains intact.

#### Precedent Treatment and Current-Law Effect

The holding-specific treatments above govern. Cavanaugh's opportunity-only §1983 rule remains intact. Albright supplies no general constitutional malicious-prosecution holding, and this decision creates none. Custis's ACCA sentencing rule and its no-meaningful-review reservation remain confined to that channel. Reed's distinction between federal habeas jurisdiction and collateral entitlement remains intact; denial of review is not qualifying invalidation. No precedent is overruled.

Effective June 24, 1994, the particular in-custody necessary-invalidity damages claim is subject to favorable invalidation and deferred accrual. Courts must examine the civil claim and the basis of the conviction, preserving claims whose success can coexist with the criminal judgment. Parts I, III and IV have nine votes; Part II's limited statutory explanation has five. The Court adopts neither a universal custody-independent bar nor an affirmative outside-habeas exception.

Section 2254's custody requirement, exhaustion command and merits inquiry remain distinct. The exhaustion exceptions for absence of an available state corrective process or circumstances rendering that process ineffective to protect the prisoner's rights remain in force. Neither exception invalidates a conviction by itself. Ordinary immunity, personal responsibility, causation, damages and applicable preclusion defenses remain for a cognizable action. Pending-criminal-case abstention is not decided.

#### Separate Positions Relevant to the Decision

Justice Scalia, joined by Justices Kennedy, Thomas and Ginsburg, agrees with the bounded rules and dismissal but would rest the prerequisite on the common-law-informed character of a claim necessarily contradicting an outstanding criminal judgment. Carey permits that remedial guidance, and the favorable-termination analogy addresses the contradiction without creating a freestanding constitutional malicious-prosecution tort. These Justices do not make continued custody or present habeas jurisdiction an element: release alone does not permit the contradictory damages judgment. They reject generic exhaustion, automatic habeas conversion and blanket preclusion. Independent injuries remain available under their own elements and defenses, and invalidity-dependent damages accrue only upon qualifying invalidation. Their broader sufficient ground has four votes; it does not become the Court's rule for persons outside custody or displace Custis's distinct sentencing reservation.

Justice Souter, joined by Justices Blackmun, Stevens and O'Connor, joins the Court's opinion but explains an affirmative boundary beyond the case decided. Preiser justifies reconciliation where the habeas and civil-rights statutes intersect; Patsy and Wolff resist turning that reconciliation into an indiscriminate civil-remedy prohibition. Common-law analogy may implement the statutory accommodation but cannot itself eliminate redress for people genuinely outside habeas's reach. These Justices therefore would not impose the same prerequisite on such plaintiffs merely because favorable termination would be demanded by a common-law analogy. For Heck, who could contest custody, they accept all four invalidation routes, delayed accrual and dismissal without prejudice. They reject conversion and premature merits or immunity rulings. Their affirmative outside-habeas conclusion has four votes; the Court leaves that question unresolved.

### Nguyen v. United States; Phan v. United States, Nos. 01-10873 and 02-5034

Merits decision, June 9, 2003

#### Chronology and Posture

Argued March 24, 2003. On certiorari to the Ninth Circuit, which affirmed federal narcotics convictions entered in the District Court of Guam. The consolidated cases ask whether a panel containing two Article III circuit judges and the fixed-term chief judge of the District Court for the Northern Mariana Islands was authorized under the assignment statutes, and whether lateness, de facto service or a quorum preserves its judgments. Neither petitioner objected before decision or sought rehearing on composition; both seek a fresh lawful appeal.

#### Judgment

**Vacate both appellate judgments and remand, 6–3.**

**Supporting:** Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Kennedy, Souter and Thomas.

**Opposing:** Scalia, Ginsburg and Breyer, who would affirm against the late composition challenge.

Each consolidated docket has the same judgment and coalition.

#### Opinion Topology

STONE-ZSELA, C. J., delivered the opinion of the Court, in which STEVENS, O'CONNOR, KENNEDY, SOUTER, and THOMAS, JJ., joined. SCALIA, J., filed a dissenting opinion, in which GINSBURG and BREYER, JJ., joined.

#### Controlling Propositions and Authority

**Authority.** All three holdings are adopted by the six Justices joining the opinion of the Court: Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Kennedy, Souter and Thomas. Scalia, Ginsburg and Breyer agree that the designation lacked statutory authority but dissent from correction and the judgment.

##### I. The territorial judge was outside the statutory class eligible for this designation

**Holding and operative rule.** Section 292(a), read with Title 28's definitions and district-court structure, authorizes designation of the specified Article III district judges to a court of appeals; it does not include the fixed-term judge of the District Court for the Northern Mariana Islands. Participation by that judge therefore violated the statutory qualification for membership of this appellate panel.

**Controlling explanation.** The Court holds that the relevant judicial office must satisfy the statute's definition. Sections 451 and 132–134 place the district-judge designation provision within the organization of the Article III district courts. Mookini v. United States distinguishes territorial courts from that statutory category even when they exercise federal jurisdiction. The territorial judge's valid appointment to his own office and competence to decide federal questions cannot supply the different qualification Congress required for this assignment. The Government correctly concedes the statutory defect. That concession leaves the disputed consequence for the judgments to be decided.

##### II. This substantial membership defect requires correction despite the late direct challenge

**Holding and operative rule.** On nonfinal direct review, participation by a judge whom Congress excluded from the eligible class for this appellate tribunal requires vacatur for fresh lawful appellate adjudication even though the litigants first objected in this Court. The litigants must establish the substantial membership defect, rather than prove that a properly constituted panel would have reached another result; the rule distinguishes a missing statutory qualification from a merely technical designation irregularity.

**Controlling explanation.** American Construction Co. v. Jacksonville, Tampa & Key West Railway Co. and United States v. American-Foreign Steamship Corp. give effect to substantial congressional choices about the composition of federal courts. Glidden Co. v. Zdanok recognizes direct-review correction of substantial adjudicator-qualification defects despite a late objection. Here the unauthorized member actually participated in the appellate decision. Treating that participation as de facto service would leave the very adjudication Congress withheld authority to perform as the defendants' only appeal.

Ryder v. United States and Landry v. Federal Deposit Insurance Corporation establish that a litigant entitled to lawful adjudication must receive the missing adjudicative function itself. Those decisions involved timely challenges; this case extends that remedial principle to the present late challenge because the substantive membership restriction protects the authority of the appellate tribunal. Olano and Johnson ordinarily require a separate justification for correcting forfeited error. The substantial composition defect and the court-composition precedents supply that justification here. Speculation about how the authorized judges might have decided cannot replace the prescribed tribunal. The broader constitutional claim concerning Article III is unnecessary to the statutory disposition of the complete challenge.

##### III. A quorum does not validate the unauthorized member's participation

**Holding and operative rule.** Section 46's quorum rule permits qualified members of a lawfully constituted panel to act under the statute's conditions; it does not retrospectively convert this three-member judgment into a separate judgment by its two eligible members. Both appellate judgments must be vacated and the appeals heard afresh by a lawfully constituted panel.

**Controlling explanation.** A quorum concerns the number entitled to act for a legally constituted tribunal. It cannot supply the missing membership qualification or erase the unauthorized member's participation in the judgment actually rendered. The record establishes no independently rendered two-judge judgment. American-Foreign Steamship's enforcement of Congress's composition requirements and Ryder's insistence on the actual missing lawful adjudication foreclose the proposed substitute. Fresh appellate consideration supplies the remedy for both petitioners.

#### Precedent Treatment

- Mookini v. United States, 303 U.S. 201: applied to distinguish territorial courts from the Article III district-court category in the designation statutes.
- American Construction Co. v. Jacksonville, Tampa & Key West Railway Co., 148 U.S. 372: applied to enforce a substantial statutory rule organizing an appellate tribunal.
- United States v. American-Foreign Steamship Corp., 363 U.S. 685: applied to require compliance with statutory appellate membership limits and reject retrospective validation through the eligible participants.
- Glidden Co. v. Zdanok, 370 U.S. 530: applied to support exceptional direct-review consideration of a substantial adjudicator-qualification defect.
- Ryder v. United States, June 12, 1995, and Landry v. Federal Deposit Insurance Corporation, June 15, 2001: their requirement of the missing lawful adjudication is extended to this substantial statutory membership defect despite a late direct challenge; their distinct military and agency applications remain intact.
- United States v. Olano, April 26, 1993, and Johnson v. United States, May 12, 1997: their ordinary forfeiture and corrective-discretion rules remain in force; this substantial court-composition defect supplies the exceptional ground for correction.

#### Current Law

Effective June 9, 2003, the right to the missing lawful adjudication extends to this substantial statutory appellate-membership defect on nonfinal direct review despite a late objection. The court must apply the distinction between a missing eligibility qualification and a technical designation irregularity. The quorum statute operates only within its lawful-panel premise.

#### Separate Positions Relevant to the Decision

**Scalia, joined by Ginsburg and Breyer, dissenting.** The dissent agrees that §292 did not authorize the designation but would affirm both appellate judgments because the defendants forfeited the objection. Under Olano and Johnson, which distinguish the existence of error from the discretion to correct it, even an assumed effect on substantial rights does not itself establish that correction is required to protect the fairness, integrity or public reputation of proceedings. The record shows neither outcome prejudice nor bias or incompetence. The two qualified circuit judges and the territorial judge's lawful original office bear on that inquiry, although they do not authorize the assignment or establish a separate two-judge judgment. Ryder and Landry protect timely challengers and expressly leave the default question open. The dissent therefore rejects the majority's treatment of this violation as a sufficient reason for exceptional correction. Its position rests on the consequence of forfeiture, rather than an endorsement of future unauthorized designations or a rule that actual innocence is required.

#### Source Notes

Nos. 01-10873 and 02-5034; 539 U.S. 69; 28 U.S.C. §§46, 132–134, 292 and 451. The record establishes participation by the territorial judge and two Article III circuit judges, together with the absence of an earlier composition objection.

#### Mandate, Remedy, and Stage

The court of appeals must designate a panel under valid statutory authority and provide fresh, independent consideration of both appeals. The designation record and replacement judgment must identify lawful composition and the new adjudication. The trial convictions remain subject to those appeals.

**End of entry: Nguyen v. United States; Phan v. United States, Merits decision, June 9, 2003.**
