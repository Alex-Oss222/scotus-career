### Franklin v. Massachusetts, No. 91-1502

Merits decision, June 26, 1992

#### Chronology and Posture

Argued April 21, 1992. On direct appeal from the three-judge District Court for Massachusetts, [785 F. Supp. 230 (1992)](https://static.case.law/f-supp/785/html/0230-01.html), which set aside the inclusion of overseas federal employees in state apportionment counts and ordered corrective relief. Massachusetts and its voters challenge that inclusion, the Secretary's residence methodology and the explanation for using home-of-record data after cancellation of a proposed survey. The questions concern standing, review of the Secretary's completed decision under the Administrative Procedure Act (APA), constitutional and statutory counting authority, and review for arbitrary or capricious administrative action.

Chief Justice Stone-Zsela and Justices White, Blackmun, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter and Thomas participated at argument and decision.

#### Judgment

The Court reverses the District Court's judgment and dissolves its corrective relief.

| Judgment component | Disposition and vote | Supporting Justices | Opposing Justices | Remedy or remand |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Operative judgment and constitutional claim requiring exclusion of overseas personnel | Reversed; constitutional exclusion claim rejected, 9–0 | Stone-Zsela, White, Blackmun, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas | No Justice opposes | Corrective relief dissolved; no replacement allocation or injunction against the President |
| Review of the Secretary's completed action and statutory and APA merits | Review available; statutory objections and arbitrary-or-capricious challenge rejected by five Justices | Stone-Zsela, Blackmun, Stevens, Kennedy, Souter | White, O'Connor, Scalia and Thomas oppose APA review and would reach reversal on that threshold ground; they do not vote that the methodology violates the APA | No further factual proceedings or new allocation required |

#### Controlling Propositions and Authority

##### Standing for the continuing apportionment injury

**Controlling proposition:** A State and its voters have standing to contest inclusion of an identified population in the operative apportionment when that inclusion costs the State a seat and authoritative relief concerning the responsible census determination would likely correct the allocation through the prescribed statutory sequence. Likely redress here does not require an injunction against the President or a general assumption that persons outside the litigation follow judicial advice.

**Authority:** Parts I–III of Stone-Zsela's Opinion of the Court, joined by all eight Associate Justices, control. This holding concerns the proved inclusion injury; it does not find that any particular alternative data source would independently give Massachusetts a seat.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that this continuing representational injury can be judicially redressed. Massachusetts would receive an additional seat if the challenged overseas population were excluded. The injury therefore rests on the operative allocation, not an abstract objection to governmental procedure. This Court's Montana decision establishes that authoritative declaratory adjudication can probably correct an ongoing apportionment through the prescribed governmental sequence. Lujan requires a concrete injury, causal connection and likely relief at the pertinent evidentiary stage; its absent-agency application did not command a majority and does not supply a categorical bar here. The statutory sequence and the Government's litigation position support the likelihood of correction following an authoritative determination concerning the census. The Court need not presume a power to command presidential discretion. Nor does it assume that a different residence dataset would necessarily produce another seat: that separate causal proposition has not been established. The inclusion injury and its connected legal challenges suffice for the review undertaken here.

**Precedent treatment:**

- United States Department of Commerce v. Montana, March 31, 1992, this Court: applied for continuing representational injury and likely declaratory redress through the allocation process; the express unanimous holding remains controlling and its reservation of coercive presidential relief remains intact.
- Lujan v. Defenders of Wildlife, June 12, 1992, this Court: applied for injury, causation, likely redress and evidentiary demands; the noncontrolling application concerning absent funding agencies is not promoted into law. Its rule for procedure connected to concrete injury does not require certainty that a new method changes the result.

##### The Secretary's completed census determination is reviewable

**Controlling proposition:** The Secretary's completed census determination supplies final agency action under APA §704 even though the President later transmits the resulting apportionment statement. The census statutes neither preclude review nor commit the counting decision entirely to unreviewable discretion: the duty to count the proper population supplies law to apply, while leaving substantial methodological choice to the Secretary.

**Authority:** Part IV of Stone-Zsela's Opinion of the Court, joined by Blackmun, Stevens, Kennedy and Souter, controls with five votes. White, O'Connor, Scalia and Thomas disagree with this rule while agreeing with reversal on their own reviewability ground.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that the official responsible for the count cannot escape review merely because another official transmits its apportionment consequence. Section 141 assigns the census and its completed population figures to the Secretary. Section 2a directs transmission of numbers ascertained under that census and application of the prescribed formula; it does not confer a new presidential choice of the population to count. Abbott Laboratories supports review of a completed administrative determination producing concrete consequences. Waterman involved a statutory presidential judgment over foreign air routes; that independent decision differs from the prescribed transmission here. Possible correction and general executive supervision do not make every completed agency decision tentative. Japan Whaling confirms that broad administrative discretion remains judicially reviewable when governing statutory commands supply a legal standard. The Secretary retains latitude over census form and method, but must count the proper population under the governing law. Review of that completed administrative choice is sufficient. The Court therefore leaves the President's own APA status and susceptibility to coercive relief undecided.

**Precedent treatment:**

- Abbott Laboratories v. Gardner, 387 U.S. 136 (1967): applied for reviewability and practical completion of an agency determination with concrete consequences; the statutory transmittal here does not postpone finality.
- Chicago & Southern Air Lines, Inc. v. Waterman Steamship Corp., 333 U.S. 103 (1948): distinguished because Congress there required an independent presidential foreign-affairs judgment; its rule for that decision structure is preserved.
- Japan Whaling Assn. v. American Cetacean Society, 478 U.S. 221 (1986): applied for review against statutory limits despite broad administrative judgment; methodological latitude is not absence of law to apply.

##### Temporarily absent residents may be included in the constitutional count

**Controlling proposition:** The constitutional command to count persons in each State permits inclusion of federal personnel temporarily overseas whose enduring ties support residence in a particular State. It does not require a literal census-day physical presence rule, and it does not authorize attribution to a State without a supportable residence connection.

**Authority:** Part III of Stone-Zsela's Opinion of the Court, joined by all eight Associate Justices, controls with nine votes. The constitutional rule does not itself resolve the separate statutory finality or APA explanation questions.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that temporary government service abroad does not necessarily sever residence in a State. The constitutional text requires an enumeration of the population belonging to each State, but does not define residence exclusively by bodily presence on census day. The first census legislation treated persons temporarily absent from their usual abode as belonging there. That early implementation gives concrete content to the counting command. The challenged personnel retain identified state connections, and the Secretary may reasonably treat their absence on federal assignment as temporary for this purpose. Wesberry and Karcher enforce equality in drawing districts within a State; they do not dictate this answer to who belongs in an interstate population count. Montana likewise recognizes that interstate apportionment must respect the constitutional structure rather than import every intrastate rule unchanged. Home-of-record information is imperfect, but that does not make every supported attribution constitutionally forbidden. The Court decides this record and rejects mandatory exclusion; unsupported connections and different methodological records remain open.

**Precedent treatment:**

- United States Department of Commerce v. Montana, March 31, 1992, this Court: applied for the constitutional structure of interstate apportionment and the distinction from intrastate districting; no new tolerance for unequal districts is adopted.
- Wesberry v. Sanders, 376 U.S. 1 (1964): distinguished as an intrastate congressional district-equality rule; its equal-population command remains effective in its own setting.
- Karcher v. Daggett, 462 U.S. 725 (1983): distinguished on the same interstate/intrastate ground; the present residence decision does not excuse avoidable inequality among a State's districts.

##### The statutory and administrative objections fail on this record

**Controlling proposition:** Section 141 permits the supported inclusion of temporarily overseas personnel with enduring state ties, and the Secretary's comparative choice of supplemented home-of-record information is not arbitrary or capricious on this record. The agency must supply a contemporaneous, reasonably discernible explanation connecting its available information and residence objective to its choice; imperfection alone does not require rejection of every available dataset.

**Authority:** Part V of Stone-Zsela's Opinion of the Court, joined by Blackmun, Stevens, Kennedy and Souter, controls with five votes. The other four Justices do not join the statutory or APA merits determination.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that the Secretary made a permissible comparative choice and adequately explained it. State Farm requires examination of relevant considerations and a rational connection between the record and the decision. Bowman Transportation permits an explanation whose path is reasonably discernible even if its expression is imperfect. Cancellation of the proposed survey is a serious objection because improved residence information helped motivate the change. But the contemporaneous record also contains assurances of supplementary personnel information and continuing reasons to recognize temporarily absent residents. Home of record can identify an enduring connection; legal residence may instead reflect taxation, and last duty station may identify employment rather than home. The Secretary could weigh those comparative defects under the statutory counting responsibility. The Court relies on the recorded reasons, not later declarations or a rationale invented by counsel. It does not demand a perfect dataset unavailable to the agency. Nor does it excuse persistence when an agency's only factual premise collapses. Different methodological challenges require their own records.

**Precedent treatment:**

- Motor Vehicle Manufacturers Assn. v. State Farm Mutual Automobile Insurance Co., 463 U.S. 29 (1983): applied for consideration of relevant factors, reasoned explanation and review on the agency's actual rationale; no exemption from review accompanies census discretion.
- Bowman Transportation, Inc. v. Arkansas-Best Freight System, Inc., 419 U.S. 281 (1974): applied for a reasonably discernible decisional path despite imperfect exposition; a court may not substitute a new rationale.

**Limits and questions not reached.** No replacement allocation, judicially selected dataset, injunction against the President, or general APA status for the President is decided. No claim that another residence method necessarily restores a seat is found proved. The statutory and administrative holdings are record-specific applications; no earlier Supreme Court precedent is overruled.

#### Precedent Treatment and Current-Law Effect

Effective June 26, 1992, the Secretary's completed census decision is reviewable under APA §704 despite subsequent presidential transmission; the counting obligations supply law for a court to apply. Supported temporarily absent residents may be included in the constitutional count. The statutory and APA merits uphold this inclusion and the home-of-record choice on the contemporaneous explanation. Montana supplies the standing premise, and Lujan applies at the level of its controlling propositions. Waterman remains effective for its distinct structure requiring an independent presidential decision; State Farm and Bowman govern the actual administrative record. The Court creates no presidential-immunity or presidential-APA holding, new evidentiary burden, replacement formula or tolerance for unequal districts.

#### Separate Positions Relevant to the Decision

O'Connor, joined by White, Scalia and Thomas, views the Secretary's report as an intermediate step because the President transmits the operative apportionment and may seek corrections; they would also exclude the President from the APA's agency definition. They concur in the constitutional rule and reversal but oppose review of the Secretary under §704. Scalia additionally explains that this Court's express Montana standing decision, which he joined, governs likely declaratory redress for the proved inclusion injury; it does not decide whether a court may coerce the President. These positions do not displace the five-Justice APA holdings.

#### Mandate, Remedy, and Stage

The District Court judgment is reversed and its corrective relief dissolved. This judgment orders no replacement allocation, further factual proceedings or injunction against the President. Supreme Court review of these claims ends without a merits remand.

**End of entry: Franklin v. Massachusetts, merits decision, June 26, 1992.**

### County of Yakima v. Confederated Tribes and Bands of the Yakima Indian Nation, Nos. 90-408 and 90-577

Judgment affirmed and cause remanded with modified instructions, January 14, 1992

*County of Yakima v. Confederated Tribes and Bands of the Yakima Indian Nation*, 502 U.S. 251 (1992), Nos. 90-408 and 90-577. Argued November 5, 1991 and decided January 14, 1992, on certiorari to the Ninth Circuit, 903 F.2d 1207. The questions concern ad valorem and sales-excise taxation of reservation fee land. An ad valorem tax is a property tax based on value; the sales excise is a tax on the transaction.

The Court affirms the judgment and remands the cause with modified instructions. All nine Justices participated. The ad valorem component is resolved for the County, 8–1: Stone, White, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, and Thomas support that disposition; Blackmun dissents. The sales excise remains prohibited, 9–0, with all nine Justices supporting that result.

Scalia delivered the opinion of the Court, joined by Stone, White, Stevens, O'Connor, Kennedy, Souter, and Thomas. Blackmun concurred as to the excise and dissented as to ad valorem authority.

The Court holds that the General Allotment Act's established fee-land rule and the Burke Act proviso authorize state ad valorem taxation of reservation land validly patented in fee under that Act, including qualifying land later held by the Nation. The tax follows the land, but patent source and any independently applicable federal protection must be determined parcel by parcel.

Scalia's opinion supplies this holding for the eight Justices supporting it.

*Blackfeet* requires unmistakably clear congressional authorization, and here the County relies on more than alienability or policy inference. *Goudy* construed the General Allotment Act to permit taxation following an ordinary fee patent, while the Burke Act proviso expressly addresses removal of restrictions on sale, encumbrance, and taxation. The Indian Reorganization Act halted future allotments and preserved trust periods but also created a distinct mechanism for restoring land to trust; it does not automatically return every existing fee parcel to tax-protected status. *Mancari* requires compatible enactments to operate together. *Moe* rejects general personal taxing jurisdiction over reservation Indians, but a specific tax on qualifying land does not revive that discarded jurisdiction. *Brendale*'s fractured analysis of tribal regulation of nonmember land supplies no governmental-impact test for this statutory tax question. Remand is necessary because the stipulated record does not establish every parcel's patent source.

The Court holds that congressional permission to tax qualifying fee land ad valorem does not unmistakably authorize a tax imposed on an Indian owner's sale of that land; securing the transactional tax with a lien does not change its legal incidence.

Scalia's opinion states this proposition for all nine Justices.

The annual levy is triggered by ownership and burdens the realty. The sales excise is triggered by a transaction. Under *Blackfeet*, authorization must be clear at the level of the tax actually imposed. A collection lien cannot transform an otherwise unauthorized transactional charge into a land tax, because that device would allow the State to enlarge congressional permission through its own collection law. *Capoeman*'s discussion of taxation after fee patent arose in the allotment context and does not provide blanket authorization for every tax related to land. The Court therefore gives the allotment provisions their demonstrated property-tax scope without creating general state dominion over the owner or transaction.

The Court reaffirms *Goudy* for qualifying General Allotment Act fee land. It applies and preserves *Blackfeet*, *Mancari*, and *Moe*, and distinguishes *Brendale*, whose analysis of tribal regulation supplies no governmental-impact test here. Qualifying General Allotment Act fee land is subject to ad valorem taxation; sales of that land by Indian owners remain outside the identified authorization.

Blackmun agrees that the sales excise is unauthorized but would also reject the ad valorem tax because the asserted authority is not sufficiently clear after later federal Indian legislation.

The *Brendale* impact remand is vacated. The case returns for parcel-specific analysis of the sources of fee patents and relief consistent with both tax holdings. No foreclosure is ordered.

**End of entry: County of Yakima v. Confederated Tribes and Bands of the Yakima Indian Nation, Judgment affirmed and cause remanded with modified instructions, January 14, 1992.**

### Franklin v. Gwinnett County Public Schools, No. 90-918

Merits decision, February 26, 1992

#### Chronology and Posture

Argued December 11, 1991. On writ of certiorari to the Eleventh Circuit, 911 F.2d 617. The district court dismissed the complaint on the categorical ground that Title IX did not permit damages; the Eleventh Circuit affirmed.

All nine Justices participated at argument and decision.

The questions are whether compensatory damages are available in the recognized Title IX private action for intentional discrimination, and whether the Spending Clause notice principle bars that relief.

Franklin seeks reversal and ordinary litigation of her damages claim. She alleges repeated sexual harassment and abuse by teacher/coach Andrew Hill while she attended a federally funded high school, official awareness and failure to act, discouragement of her complaint and closure of the investigation following Hill’s conditional resignation. These are allegations, not judicial findings. She no longer attends the school. The lower court relied on its Title VI precedent Drayden and a Spending Clause limitation; Judge Johnson regarded Drayden as controlling without reaching the additional constitutional characterization. Respondents and the United States defend the damages bar. Backpay and prospective student relief would not redress the pleaded completed injury.

#### Judgment

The Eleventh Circuit is reversed, 9–0, and the case is remanded for further proceedings on the damages claim.

| Judgment component | Disposition and vote | Supporting Justices | Opposing Justices | Remedy or remand |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Categorical bar to Title IX damages | Reversed and remanded; 9-0 | Stone, White, Blackmun, Stevens, O’Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas | No opposing vote | Compensatory relief may be sought for proved intentional discrimination; liability, attribution, causation and amount remain to be adjudicated. |

#### Opinion Topology

| Writing | Author | Joined by | Relationship to judgment | Scope joined |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Opinion of the Court | White | Stone, Blackmun, Stevens, O’Connor, Kennedy, Souter | Supports reversal and remand | Appropriate-remedies rule and its application to intentional Title IX discrimination, including the Spending Clause objection. |
| Concurrence in the judgment | Scalia | Thomas | Supports reversal and remand on a narrower statutory ground | The 1986 remedies enactment acknowledges damages; no automatic full-remedies presumption for all judicially implied causes. |

#### Controlling Propositions and Authority

##### Compensatory relief in the established Title IX action

**Controlling proposition:** When a cognizable federal statutory cause of action exists, courts may award appropriate relief absent clear congressional direction limiting it; recognizing that remedy does not create a new cause of action. In Title IX’s established private action, compensatory damages are available for proved intentional discrimination, subject to proof of recipient responsibility, causation and compensable loss.

**Authority:** White’s opinion, joined by Stone, Blackmun, Stevens, O’Connor, Kennedy and Souter; seven Justices control the remedial rule and application.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that Title IX does not confine an established private action to remedies incapable of redressing a completed personal injury. Cannon supplies the cause of action; Bell v. Hood and Davis v. Passman distinguish that authority to sue from the appropriate relief a court may grant. Borak confirms that statutory silence about remedy does not itself confine a recognized action to a declaration. Guardians and Darrone support compensatory relief for intentional violations of closely related discrimination statutes. Congress’s 1986 reference to remedies at law and in equity, followed by legislation preserving private enforcement, supplies no direction to bar damages. Recognizing compensation therefore implements the existing action rather than expanding judicial power to hear a new one. Franklin’s departure from school shows why backpay and prospective relief cannot compensate the pleaded past injury. The allegations still require adjudication; the Court awards nothing and decides neither a final recipient-attribution rule nor punitive or unintended-effects liability.

**Precedent treatment:**

- *Cannon v. University of Chicago*, 441 U.S. 677 — applied as the established Title IX cause of action; not reopened.
- *Bell v. Hood*, 327 U.S. 678 — applied for appropriate remedies in an existing cognizable action, absent a congressional limit.
- *Davis v. Passman*, 442 U.S. 228 — applied to separate existence of an action from available relief.
- *J. I. Case Co. v. Borak*, 377 U.S. 426 — applied against limiting relief merely because the statute does not enumerate the remedy.
- *Guardians Association v. Civil Service Commission*, 463 U.S. 582 — its intentional-discrimination remedial support is applied without treating that decision as having a single majority rationale.
- *Consolidated Rail Corp. v. Darrone*, 465 U.S. 624 — analogous monetary relief supports the availability of compensation.

##### Notice does not bar damages for the pleaded intentional violation

**Controlling proposition:** The Spending Clause notice principle does not categorically bar compensatory relief for an intentional violation of Title IX’s express prohibition of sex discrimination within its established private action. Ambiguous funding conditions, unintentional effects and the separate requirements for attributing misconduct to a recipient are not resolved.

**Authority:** White’s opinion, joined by the same seven Justices, rejects the distinct funding-notice ground offered to sustain dismissal.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that the asserted lack of notice cannot sustain this categorical damages bar. Pennhurst requires fair notice of conditions imposed on recipients of federal funds; it does not authorize intentional breach of a clear nondiscrimination condition while limiting the injured person to ineffective prospective relief. Title IX expressly forbids sex discrimination, and Cannon supplies the established enforcement action. The pleaded sexual harassment and abuse describe intentional discrimination of the kind addressed by Meritor Savings Bank’s explanation of sex-based harassment, without deciding here when a school is responsible for a teacher’s conduct. Darrone also defeats the premise that a spending-based statute necessarily excludes a monetary award for intentional violations. The objection cannot succeed merely by describing compensation as payment from a recipient’s treasury. This conclusion rests on the enacted condition and existing enforcement law, not a general power to redress every injury. Genuinely ambiguous conditions, proof of institutional responsibility and unintended discriminatory effects remain distinct questions.

**Precedent treatment:**

- *Pennhurst State School and Hospital v. Halderman*, 451 U.S. 1 — its funding-condition notice rule is preserved; it does not bar relief for this alleged intentional violation.
- *Meritor Savings Bank, FSB v. Vinson*, 477 U.S. 57 — its identification of sex-based harassment informs the pleaded discrimination; no Title VII attribution formula is imported.
- *Consolidated Rail Corp. v. Darrone*, 465 U.S. 624 — applied against a categorical objection to monetary remedies under spending legislation.
- *Cannon v. University of Chicago*, 441 U.S. 677 — the established action supplies the enforcement premise for the notice analysis.

#### Precedent Treatment and Current-Law Effect

Effective February 26, appropriate relief in a cognizable statutory action is not barred by remedial silence alone, and Title IX permits compensation for intentional discrimination. Before this decision, the cause existed but the presented damages bar was unresolved; afterward, that bar and the categorical Spending Clause objection are rejected. No new implied action, punitive remedy, liability for unintended effects, automatic institutional responsibility, or finding against the school is established.

#### Separate Positions Relevant to the Decision

Scalia, joined by Thomas, agrees that the damages claim may proceed because the 1986 enactment acknowledges legal as well as equitable remedies. They decline to presume every remedy merely from a cause of action that judges previously implied, and do not join the Court’s broader remedial reasoning.

Dates and procedural background: [503 U.S. 60 (1992), pp. 60–78](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/USREPORTS-503/pdf/USREPORTS-503-60.pdf).

#### Mandate, Remedy, and Stage

Supreme Court review ends with reversal and remand. The damages complaint returns for ordinary proceedings on liability, attribution, causation and compensable loss. No amount is awarded and no prospective school-supervision order is entered.

**End of entry: Franklin v. Gwinnett County Public Schools, merits decision, February 26, 1992.**

### Hagen v. Utah, No. 92-6281

Merits decision, February 23, 1994

#### Chronology and Posture

510 U.S. 399. Argued November 2, 1993. On certiorari to the Supreme Court of Utah, 858 P.2d 925, which reinstated Hagen's conviction for distribution of a controlled substance in Myton. The intermediate appellate court had vacated the conviction because it regarded Myton as Indian country. Hagen's status as an Indian is settled for this review. The question is whether Congress diminished the Uintah Reservation through the enactments opening its unallotted lands, thereby permitting the territorial criminal jurisdiction asserted in this prosecution. Myton is within the original reservation boundaries and in the opened area.

The May 27, 1902 Act, 32 Stat. 263–264, conditioned allotment on consent of a majority of the adult male Uintah and White River Ute Indians. It provided eighty acres of irrigable agricultural land to each head of family and forty acres to each other member, with allotments by October 1, 1903, and directed restoration of unallotted lands to the public domain at that time. Homestead entrants were to pay $1.25 per acre. Sale proceeds first reimbursed federal advances implementing the program; the remainder benefited the Indians under the Secretary's direction. The separately appropriated $70,064.48 settled other claims and was originally payable upon majority consent to allotment and restoration; it was not a fixed purchase price for the surplus lands. The June 19, 1902 resolution required adequate common grazing land to be set aside before opening and released that payment without awaiting consent to allotment or restoration.

The March 3, 1903 Act authorized allotment without consent if consent was not obtained by June 1, 1903, confined the common grazing lands to no more than 250,000 acres south of the Strawberry River, and postponed opening under the earlier program until October 1, 1904. The April 21, 1904 Act funded the necessary surveys and postponed opening again, to March 10, 1905. The March 3, 1905 Act expressly repealed the south-of-Strawberry location restriction and postponed opening until September 1, 1905, unless the President chose an earlier date. It prescribed homestead and townsite disposition by presidential proclamation, barred settlement, occupation or entry except as the proclamation prescribed during the first sixty days, and directed cash disposal of land remaining after five years, in parcels of no more than 640 acres to one person. It retained the earlier sale-proceeds arrangement but did not repeat the public-domain phrase or expressly repeal it generally. The July 14 proclamation recited the earlier restoration instruction and opened the lands under the prescribed entry arrangements on August 28, 1905. Congress never obtained the required majority consent; the later statutory authority to proceed without it therefore matters.

All nine Justices participated at argument and decision: Chief Justice Stone-Zsela and Justices Blackmun, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas and Ginsburg.

#### Judgment

The judgment of the Supreme Court of Utah is affirmed, 6–3.

| Judgment component | Disposition and vote | Supporting Justices | Opposing Justices | Remedy |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Reservation boundary and resulting territorial jurisdiction in this prosecution | Affirm, 6–3 | Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Thomas, Ginsburg | Stone-Zsela, Blackmun, Souter | Utah's reinstatement of the conviction stands; dissent would reverse and remand to set aside the judgment resting on the rejected territorial premise |

All nine leave the separate preclusion issue unadjudicated. No additional relief or general jurisdictional allocation is ordered.

#### Opinion Topology

| Writing | Author | Joined by | Relationship to judgment | Scope joined |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Opinion of the Court | O'Connor | Stevens, Scalia, Kennedy, Thomas, Ginsburg | Affirmance | Entire bounded statutory construction, application and limits |
| Dissent | Blackmun | Stone-Zsela, Souter | Reversal and remand | Entire statutory-sequence disagreement and confined remedy |

#### Controlling Propositions and Authority

##### Congressional diminishment and the jurisdiction asserted at Myton

**Controlling proposition:** Congress diminished the Uintah Reservation as to the unallotted lands covered by the opening program: the 1902 operative direction restoring those lands to the public domain remained effective through the 1903–1905 amendments and implementation. Myton's location in that opened area is therefore outside the reservation on this record, and Utah may exercise the territorial criminal jurisdiction asserted over Hagen's offense; opening or fee ownership alone does not establish that result elsewhere.

**Authority:** O'Connor's opinion of the Court, joined by Stevens, Scalia, Kennedy, Thomas and Ginsburg. These six Justices adopt the statutory construction, its clear-intent basis and its application to this prosecution. There is a direct majority rationale; no fractured-opinion inference is necessary.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that the enactments must be read together. Solem requires congressional intent to diminish, gives statutory language primary weight, and resolves remaining ambiguity for the Indians. Its framework does not require a particular cession formula or fixed purchase payment. Rosebud shows that later implementation can preserve an earlier boundary decision. Here the 1902 restoration command has operative force beyond permission to sell. The later Acts postponed that program, supplied surveys and altered its implementation. The 1905 Act retained the proceeds arrangement and depended on the earlier allotment provisions. Its homestead and townsite restrictions can operate alongside restoration; its omission of the earlier phrase therefore does not establish repeal. Congress expressly repealed the geographic grazing restriction, further supporting this reading of the remaining provisions. Seymour and Mattz distinguish opening alone from an actual boundary change; this statutory sequence establishes the latter. Contemporary administrative understandings and subsequent settlement can corroborate that conclusion, but neither supplies congressional authority independently. The holding establishes the territorial premise for this Myton prosecution without deciding every parcel, retained tribal power or other category of Indian country.

**Precedent treatment:**

- Solem v. Bartlett, 465 U.S. 463: applies its clear-intent inquiry and unequal weighting of text, contemporaneous context and later treatment; distinguishes its nonoperative public-domain reference from the surviving operative command here.
- Rosebud Sioux Tribe v. Kneip, 430 U.S. 584: applies statutory continuity across a sequence of enactments and preserves the rule that no particular formula or fixed payment is indispensable.
- Seymour v. Superintendent, 368 U.S. 351: preserves the distinction between opening and boundary change and the rule that fee ownership within retained boundaries does not itself remove Indian-country status.
- Mattz v. Arnett, 412 U.S. 481: preserves the need for congressional diminishment beyond mere sale and settlement; this sequence supplies the additional direction.
- DeCoteau v. District County Court, 420 U.S. 425: preserves clear congressional cession as sufficient evidence without making its precise words or payment structure mandatory.
- South Dakota v. Bourland, June 14, 1993: preserves its particular displacement of exclusion-derived regulation and its separate residual-authority inquiry; it does not itself establish reservation boundaries.
- Negonsott v. Samuels, February 24, 1993: preserves its express Kansas-specific criminal authorization; it supplies no independent Utah authorization within Indian country.
- Oklahoma Tax Commission v. Sac and Fox Nation, May 17, 1993: preserves the distinct §1151 categories and the need to determine actual Indian-country status.

#### Limits and Questions Not Reached

The collateral-estoppel issue arising from Ute Indian Tribe v. Utah was omitted from the petition and expressly disavowed by Hagen; the Court does not decide its merits. This case does not adjudicate private titles, all remaining trust or allotted lands, every tract of the larger Uintah and Ouray territory, unrelated tribal powers, or every sovereign's jurisdiction over every offense. The $70,064.48 payment is not relied upon as a purchase of the surplus land. Later population and state administration do not independently change boundaries.

#### Precedent Treatment and Current-Law Effect

No cited precedent is overruled. The Court leaves the merits of the disavowed preclusion theory unresolved.

Effective February 23, 1994, the Court's construction of the Uintah opening enactments establishes diminishment for the opened area implicated by Myton and sustains the jurisdiction asserted in this prosecution. Solem's existing clear-intent inquiry remains the governing method. The decision rejects a mandatory cession-wording or sum-certain-payment requirement without weakening the rule that opening, alienation or demographic change alone is insufficient. It supplies no universal rule assigning all fee parcels to state jurisdiction and does not displace the separate retained powers and Indian-country categories recognized in Bourland and Sac and Fox.

#### Separate Positions Relevant to the Decision

Blackmun dissents, joined by Chief Justice Stone-Zsela and Souter. The dissent would reverse the Utah Supreme Court and remand for disposition of the prosecution consistently with the absence of the asserted territorial jurisdiction. Its disagreement concerns the application of Solem's requirement of clear congressional intent, not Congress's power to act or an indispensable wording or payment formula.

The 1902 restoration language is substantial evidence for the State and cannot be dismissed as an ordinary opening provision. The dissent nevertheless finds that the final 1905 enactment leaves the territorial consequence uncertain. Congress changed the manner of disposition, omitted the public-domain language from the operative opening directions, and expressly carried forward the use of proceeds for the Indians. The cross-references in the intervening Acts and the absence of an express general repeal support continuity, but do not remove the difference between continuing allotment and sale arrangements and clearly preserving a change in jurisdictional boundaries. The express repeal of the grazing-location restriction proves that Congress made that particular change; it does not conclusively settle the force of every omission elsewhere. The dissent neither treats the entire 1902 Act as repealed nor makes tribal consent after the 1903 authorization a continuing statutory prerequisite.

Seymour and Mattz establish that opening land to settlers is compatible with retained reservation boundaries. Rosebud supports the State's continuity argument, but the dissent distinguishes its established cession purpose from the ambiguity remaining in this sequence. Neither absence of a fixed purchase payment nor retained sale proceeds alone controls; the payment here settled separate claims. Under Solem, uncertainty after reading the enactments in context must be resolved for retained Indian rights.

The administrative history does not supply the missing clarity. Inspector McLaughlin described removal of an outside boundary but also promised continuing agency protection and privileges; the proclamation cannot itself exercise Congress's boundary power. The later jurisdictional history includes competing tribal and federal assertions, and non-Indian settlement cannot amend an Act. The dissent gives such evidence its subordinate role rather than categorically excluding it. Section 1151 does not make a fee patent within retained boundaries dispositive. Reversal on this territorial ground would leave private titles and any other sovereign's lawful prosecution undisturbed. The dissent does not rely on the disavowed preclusion theory or claim that all surplus-land enactments preserve reservations.

#### Source Notes

The enactments and procedural record appear in the [United States Reports, 510 U.S. 399](https://tile.loc.gov/storage-services/service/ll/usrep/usrep510/usrep510399/usrep510399.pdf); the judgment below is 858 P.2d 925. The legal account paraphrases the statutes and verified record. The separate preclusion issue was expressly disavowed and supplies no alternative ground.

#### Mandate, Remedy, and Stage

The conviction reinstated by the Utah Supreme Court remains in place. No merits remand, title adjudication, damages award or order reallocating all jurisdiction in the region issues. Proceedings implementing the affirmed judgment remain for the Utah courts.

**End of entry: Hagen v. Utah, merits decision, February 23, 1994.**

### Blessing v. Freestone, No. 95-1441

Merits decision, April 21, 1997

#### Chronology and Posture

Argued January 6, 1997. On writ of certiorari to the United States Court of Appeals for the Ninth Circuit, which reversed summary judgment for Arizona on October 10, 1995 and recognized a broad individual right to substantial compliance with Title IV-D. Five mothers alleged failures of paternity establishment, parent location, support orders and collections caused by staffing shortages, backlogs and structural defects; Madrid separately alleged failure to distribute collected support. Review concerns identification of individual rights enforceable through § 1983 and whether Congress displaced that channel through federal oversight. The request for programwide relief is not a finding of any violation.

#### Judgment

**Vacated and remanded, 9–0.**

**Supporting:** Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg, Breyer.

The asserted individual right to programwide substantial compliance, including general administrative adequacy in staffing and computer operations, is rejected. No particular Title IV-D right, violation or payment is decided. The District Court must identify the specific claims and operative law before separately addressing § 1983 foreclosure, defendant, causation and relief; neither blanket dismissal nor an undifferentiated statewide injunction is ordered.

#### Opinion Topology

Justice Stevens delivered the Opinion of the Court, joined in full by Stone-Zsela, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer. The unanimous opinion controls every holding and qualification below. Justice Scalia filed a concurrence, joined by Justice Kennedy; both join the Court in full and reserve only the unpresented original-meaning funding-contract question.

#### Controlling Propositions and Authority

**1. A § 1983 statutory claim must identify an individual right.** A claimant must identify the particular statutory provision intended to benefit that claimant, imposing a binding rather than merely precatory obligation, and defining the asserted individual entitlement sufficiently for judicial enforcement rather than leaving it vague or amorphous. The text, statutory setting and actual entitlement asserted govern; violation of a beneficial statute or mandatory administrative duty alone does not establish the right.

**Controlling explanation:** Wright and Wilder recognize defined individual entitlements within funding statutes; they do not make an entire program an indivisible promise. Livadas separately identifies the federal right, the § 1983 channel, congressional foreclosure, defendant and remedy. Suter likewise preserves provision-specific adjudication rather than one answer for all state-plan provisions. The families must therefore identify what each particular command entitles them to demand. Program importance, mandatory wording and remedial usefulness cannot take the place of that inquiry; qualitative language is not universally unenforceable merely because applying it requires judgment.

**2. Title IV-D supplies no individual right to general administrative adequacy.** The federal substantial-compliance measure does not give each beneficiary a personal right to compel systemwide compliance. The same general theory fails when restated as an entitlement to sufficient agency staffing or satisfactory computerized administration as such; rejection of those theories does not decide whether a distinct, actually asserted provision creates a particular individual right.

**Controlling explanation:** The substantial-compliance measure evaluates aggregate performance for federal oversight and funding consequences. A State may meet that benchmark while failing one family; aggregate success cannot extinguish an independently existing personal right. Section 654(3) directs a single, separate organizational unit meeting prescribed staffing requirements, but identifies no beneficiary's right to a particular staff complement, caseload or assigned worker. Former and amended § 654(16), their associated requirements and § 654a's management and performance functions similarly organize program administration. Their technical detail does not make the system itself an individual entitlement. Mandatory duties important to effective collections therefore do not sustain the general claim recognized below. Alleged shortages, backlogs and accounting defects may still bear on a proved violation or fitted remedy, but create no right by themselves. No separately pleaded claim to each standardized computer requirement, confidentiality protection or individual transmission deadline is established here. The Court adjudicates no individual clause of 45 C.F.R. §§ 303.20 or 307.10 and announces no categorical rule against regulatory elaboration of an established statutory right. A genuinely distinct claim must receive its own provision-specific inquiry, rather than automatic approval or rejection because it appears in an administrative section.

**3. General federal oversight does not categorically displace § 1983.** Once a provision creates an individual federal right, that right is presumptively enforceable through § 1983 unless Congress expressly excludes the channel or supplies a sufficiently comprehensive remedial scheme incompatible with it. Title IV-D's federal audits, corrective plans and funding sanctions do not alone foreclose every otherwise existing individual claim; any specific incompatible remedy must be assessed for the actual provision and claim.

**Controlling explanation:** Livadas separates enforceability from whether a right exists. Funding sanctions regulate the federal-state relationship and offer no general administrative forum for a family to obtain case-specific relief. Their detail and practical importance do not create the incompatibility found in the different remedial arrangements of Sea Clammers and Smith v. Robinson. Wright and Wilder likewise reject automatic foreclosure from federal administrative enforcement. The District Court's chapterwide preclusion ground cannot support dismissal of every possible concrete right. This holding does not establish a particular right, excuse ordinary defendant or relief requirements, or resolve a hypothetical provision-specific remedy not identified here.

**4. State-plan placement neither defeats nor creates a right.** Sections 1320a-2 and 1320a-10 prohibit rejecting enforceability merely because a Social Security Act provision appears among requirements for a state plan or its contents. They do not dispense with the individual-right inquiry or alter the preserved result that § 671(a)(15) is not enforceable in a private action.

**Controlling explanation:** These provisions apply to actions pending on their respective October 20 and October 31, 1994 enactment dates and actions brought thereafter, including this action. Their text leaves other established private-action grounds intact except for overturning any state-plan ground applied in Suter but not in earlier enforcement decisions, and expressly saves the § 671(a)(15) result. The Court applies Suter's actual provision-specific rights and separate implied-action holdings, including its preservation of the independent § 671(a)(16) case-plan claim. No broader state-plan immunity is imported through the statutory reference, and no every-provision entitlement follows from removing that categorical obstacle.

**5. Madrid's concrete distribution allegation and the operative law require separate examination.** A broad prayer for programwide relief does not erase Madrid's allegation that collected support was not distributed under former § 657(b)(1). The District Court must identify each asserted provision, conduct, remedy, governing version and effective date before deciding right, violation and relief; neither a superseded duty nor a duty not yet effective may silently support an injunction.

**Controlling explanation:** Former § 657(b)(1) is materially more specific than an aggregate administrative benchmark, but specificity does not decide Madrid's unadjudicated eligibility, payment facts, legal right, defendant or amount. It concerns the first $50 of periodic monthly support for a month received in that month, plus the first $50 for each prior month received then if the absent parent made that prior payment in the month due, without reducing assistance or affecting eligibility. It does not promise a fresh $50 for each installment regardless of timing. Subsection (b) covers collections under an approved plan in fiscal years beginning after September 30, 1976, subject to subsection (d)'s distinct foster-care rules. Those rules first reimburse current foster-care maintenance, direct qualifying excess up to ordered support to the placement agency for the child's interests, then reimburse unrepaid past foster-care or family-assistance payments from the remainder, with the federal financing share reimbursed appropriately and any balance going to that agency for the child's interests. The agency may reserve its payments for future needs or make them available to the person meeting the child's daily needs. No relevant exception or payment fact is established for Madrid. The 1996 changes likewise have separate operative dates and transitions; compilation together does not make all duties presently applicable. The court below must apply them to identified claims and dates, without inferring Arizona implementation or default.

**Statutory timing limits on the remand:**

- Pub. L. 104-193 § 395(a) generally makes title III changes requiring state-law enactment or amendment under § 666 or state-plan revision under § 654 effective for periods beginning October 1, 1996; other provisions generally take effect on August 22, 1996. Specifically provided dates and the following separate grace rules remain controlling.
- Under § 395(b), the applicable date is the later of the otherwise specified date or the effective date of implementing state legislation, but no later than the first day of the first calendar quarter after the close of the first regular legislative session beginning after August 22, 1996. Each year of a two-year session counts separately. Under § 395(c), a State unable to comply without amending its constitution is protected from a noncompliance finding until the earlier of one year after the necessary amendment takes effect or five years after August 22, 1996. Neither rule establishes an Arizona event or compliance fact here.
- The former § 654(24) operational-computer deadline was changed from October 1, 1995 to October 1, 1997 by the October 12, 1995 amendment; the old date establishes no present default. The 1996 text separately requires compliance by October 1, 1997 with requirements enacted on or before October 13, 1988, and by October 1, 2000 with requirements enacted on or before August 22, 1996. The latter deadline extends one day for every day the Secretary misses the final-regulations deadline of two years after August 22, 1996. Implementation deadlines remain distinct from enactment and general effective dates.
- Section 654a(g), added by § 312(c), takes effect October 1, 1998. Section 312(d)(2) separately permits a State processing receipts through local courts on August 22, 1996 to continue that receipt processing through September 30, 1999, at its option, through the courts already processing those payments by enactment. That narrow exception concerns § 654b(b)(1)'s unit receipt-handling requirement; it is not a wholesale extension of every computer-system obligation. No Arizona qualification for it is found, and these future duties are not April 1997 mandates merely because they appear in the compilation.

**6. Any remedy must follow the particular proved right and violation.** On remand, the District Court must identify and assess the actual individual claims, then any § 1983 foreclosure, proper defendant, breach, causation and lawful relief. Neither general administrative failure nor rejection of the programwide theory authorizes an undifferentiated substantial-compliance injunction, blanket dismissal, specified payment, damages award or prescribed staffing or computer redesign.

**Controlling explanation:** General administrative shortcomings can be evidence relevant to an established right and a remedy fitted to its violation. They are not a general commission to supervise Arizona's program. The Court decides no directly implied Title IV-D action through the § 1983 inquiry. The Eleventh Amendment argument and request to overrule Maine v. Thiboutot were neither presented below nor included in the certiorari questions and are not reached; that preservation limit does not resolve every future defendant-specific question. The Ninth Circuit's undifferentiated right cannot stand, but the State's requested dismissal of every possible particular claim does not follow.

#### Precedent Treatment

- Suter v. Artist M.: Preserves its actual § 671(a)(15) and (9) rights and separate implied-action holdings and its unanimous preservation of the independent § 671(a)(16) claim for specific adjudication. State-plan placement is not a chapterwide answer.
- Livadas v. Bradshaw: Applies the separate inquiries into individual federal right, § 1983, congressional foreclosure, defendant and relief; statutory duty or preemption alone is insufficient.
- Wright v. Roanoke Redevelopment and Housing Authority: Preserves its defined rent-and-utility entitlement and independent foreclosure analysis; no programwide Title IV-D right follows.
- Wilder v. Virginia Hospital Association: Preserves its identified reimbursement obligation and rejection of automatic foreclosure through funding withdrawal; it does not resolve the unparsed claims here.
- Pennhurst: Applies the need for clear notice of an asserted funding obligation without categorical immunity for state-plan provisions.
- Middlesex County Sewerage Authority v. National Sea Clammers Association and Smith v. Robinson: Preserve foreclosure through comprehensive incompatible remedies; Title IV-D's general audits and funding measures alone do not supply that incompatibility.
- Maine v. Thiboutot: Remains undisturbed; the unpreserved request to overrule it is not reached.

#### Law After Decision

Title IV-D's aggregate compliance measure and general administrative machinery create no individual entitlement to systemwide adequacy. Specific actual claims remain subject to the individual-right test and a separate congressional-foreclosure inquiry. General federal oversight does not alone displace § 1983, and state-plan location does not alone defeat enforcement. Remedy must fit an established right and violation under the applicable statutory version; no individual distribution right or statewide structural remedy is newly established.

#### Separate Writings

**Scalia, joined by Kennedy, concurring.** Both join the Court's application of existing precedent and its full mandate. They reserve whether a beneficiary of a federal-state funding agreement holds a right secured by law within § 1983's original meaning, rather than standing only as a third-party beneficiary of a contract between governments. Their concern is that such a beneficiary's claim historically belonged to the contracting governments, not necessarily to the beneficiary through this statutory action. They acknowledge that Wright and Wilder permitted beneficiary suits and that this foundational question was not raised there. They leave it for further consideration; the concurrence creates no categorical bar to spending-statute claims, identifies no additional reason to dismiss these plaintiffs' concrete allegations, and changes no holding of the Court.

#### Source Notes

The complaint and lower proceedings are reported in Blessing v. Freestone, 520 U.S. 329; the allegations are not findings. Official United States Code editions supply former § 657(b)(1) and (d), §§ 654 and 654a, and §§ 1320a-2 and 1320a-10, with their amendment and effective-date notes. [Former distribution provision](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/USCODE-1994-title42/html/USCODE-1994-title42-chap6-subchapIV_2-partD-sec657.htm) supplies monthly timing and the foster-care qualification. [Section 312(d) transition](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/USCODE-1996-title42/html/USCODE-1996-title42-chap6-subchapIV_2-partD-sec654b.htm) supplies the narrow optional local-court receipt-processing exception through September 30, 1999. No Arizona implementation date or individual payment fact is established, and no individual regulatory clause is adjudicated.

#### Mandate, Remedy, and Stage

The case returns through the Ninth Circuit for further District Court proceedings identifying and assessing the actual provision-specific claims. Madrid's distribution allegation remains for its own inquiry. The court must apply the operative statutory version, temporal qualifications, defendant and remedial requirements. No payment, damages, staffing formula, computer design, programwide injunction or wholesale dismissal is commanded.

### Cass County v. Leech Lake Band of Chippewa Indians, No. 97-174

Plenary merits decision, June 8, 1998

#### Chronology and Posture

Argued February 24, 1998. On writ of certiorari to the United States Court of Appeals for the Eighth Circuit.

The county taxed twenty-one reservation parcels reacquired by the Band. This review concerns only the eight parcels sold to non-Indians under the Nelson Act; the thirteen allotted parcels are outside review. The District Court sustained the land taxes, and the Eighth Circuit exempted the eight sale parcels. The question is whether tribal repurchase in ordinary fee restores tax immunity after the federal disposal.

All nine Justices participated at argument and decision.

#### Judgment

**Reversed and remanded, 9–0.**

**Supporting:** Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer.

The remand concerns the eight sale parcels. The county must establish each parcel's qualifying statutory chain, and the courts must preserve any independently applicable federal protection. No refund, interest or penalty amount is determined.

Justice Scalia delivered the opinion of the Court, joined in full by Chief Justice Stone-Zsela and Justices Stevens, O'Connor, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer.

#### Holdings

##### Completed Nelson Act disposal and subsequent tribal repurchase

**Holding and operative rule.** A parcel actually conveyed into the ordinary non-Indian fee estate through the Nelson Act's completed public-sale or homestead-disposal program is subject to county ad valorem land taxation; the Band's later ordinary fee purchase does not itself restore federal tax protection. The county must identify the qualifying statutory chain parcel by parcel, and an independently applicable federal exemption, retained restriction or legally effective trust protection remains controlling.

**Authority.** Scalia's Opinion of the Court, joined in full by all eight other Justices, supplies nine direct votes for this rule and explanation.

**Controlling explanation.** Congress directed a specific disposal of these lands. Sections 4–5 provide for appraisal and public cash sale of pine lands, followed by private sale of remaining parcels at the appraised price. Section 6 provides homestead disposal of agricultural land not allotted or reserved for future Indian use, subject to payment, occupancy, final-entry and patent requirements. Completed conveyance under those programs places the qualifying parcel in the ordinary non-Indian fee estate, with its land-tax consequence. Ordinary repurchase does not reverse that congressional disposition.

County of Yakima supplies the distinction between a tax authorized on qualifying land and personal taxing authority over its Indian owner. Its General Allotment Act and Burke Act holding does not make alienability alone sufficient; the Nelson Act's particular completed disposal supplies the additional source here. Montana v. Blackfeet Tribe requires unmistakably clear congressional authorization. That requirement is met by this completed statutory disposal, although Congress used no separate tax formula. The absence of such words does not establish a protection that reappears solely upon tribal repurchase.

The rule does not reach unsold, reserved, restricted or incompletely patented land by analogy. A retained reservation boundary is consistent with a particular congressional land-tax authorization. The Court decides neither boundary diminishment nor general personal or regulatory jurisdiction.

**Limits and questions not reached.** The thirteen allotted parcels, transaction excises, income taxes, collection or execution immunity, and particular refund or penalty amounts are not decided. Applicability of the Nonintercourse Act to reacquired land is outside the presented question and remains unresolved.

#### Precedent Treatment

- County of Yakima is applied for its statutory-source, land-tax-incidence and parcel-specific protection rules; its GAA/Burke Act holding is not converted into a general alienability rule or permission for transactional taxes.
- Montana v. Blackfeet Tribe supplies the clear-authorization requirement, satisfied here by the particular completed congressional disposal.
- Oklahoma Tax Commission v. Sac and Fox Nation preserves distinct predicates for member income and vehicle taxation; it supplies no automatic exemption or authorization for this land tax.
- Hagen v. Utah preserves the distinction between fee ownership and congressional reservation diminishment. No boundary changes follow from today's tax holding.

#### Law After Decision

The Nelson Act sale provisions supply an additional, specific source of ad valorem authority for qualifying completed disposals. Tribal repurchase alone does not undo that source. Federal protections applying independently to a particular parcel remain effective, and authority to tax land does not itself establish authority for another tax or immunity from a collection proceeding.

#### Source Notes

The [Nelson Act, 25 Stat. 642–646](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/STATUTE-25/pdf/STATUTE-25-Pg642.pdf) supplies the disposal provisions. The reported proceedings appear at [524 U.S. 103](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/USREPORTS-524/pdf/USREPORTS-524-103.pdf); the [opinion's note 5](https://www.law.cornell.edu/supct/html/97-174.ZO.html) identifies the unpresented Nonintercourse Act question. The record's completed-sale provenance does not establish that every other parcel or uncompleted homestead entry qualifies.

#### Mandate, Remedy, and Stage

The Eighth Circuit's exemption ruling for the eight sale parcels is reversed. Further proceedings must apply the statutory chain and independent-protection inquiry to each parcel. The thirteen allotted parcels remain outside this judgment.

**End of entry: Cass County v. Leech Lake Band of Chippewa Indians, Plenary merits decision, June 8, 1998.**

### Gebser v. Lago Vista Independent School District, No. 96-1866

Decision, June 22, 1998

#### Chronology and Posture

Argued March 25, 1998. On writ of certiorari to the Fifth Circuit. The question is what showing permits private damages against a school district under Title IX for a teacher's sexual harassment of a student. Review concerns only the appealed Title IX judgment against the district.

In October 1992, parents of two other students complained to the principal about Waldrop's classroom comments. The principal met with him, received an apology and assurance against repetition, cautioned him and informed the guidance counselor, but did not transmit the complaint to the superintendent, the Title IX coordinator. Gebser did not report the relationship herself. Police discovered it in January 1993, and the district terminated Waldrop.

The evidence about grievance procedures is disputed. The superintendent's affidavit described a policy, while deposition testimony described no formal policy; she did not remember whether a handbook notifying students of procedures had been distributed. Gebser testified that she probably would have reported had she known what to do, while describing bewilderment and fear. These are evidentiary accounts, not findings of institutional fault or causation. The District Court granted summary judgment for the district, and the Fifth Circuit affirmed under an actual-supervisory-knowledge requirement.

All nine Justices participated at argument and decision.

#### Judgment

**Reversed and remanded, 5–4.**

**Supporting:** Stone-Zsela, Stevens, Souter, Ginsburg and Breyer.
**Opposing:** O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy and Thomas.

The reversal affects only the Fifth Circuit's affirmance of the district's Title IX summary judgment. Further proceedings must apply the controlling attribution propositions and determine the unresolved elements, causal facts, damages and defenses. No compensation or compulsory trial is ordered, and no unappealed claim or defendant is restored.

#### Opinions and Joins

| Writing | Author | Joined by | Relationship to judgment | Scope joined |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Opinion of the Court, Part I | Stone-Zsela | Stevens, Souter, Ginsburg, Breyer | Supports reversal and remand | Five directly reject actual notice to a corrective official as the exclusive attribution route, with the stated limits. |
| Opinion announcing the judgment, Parts II–III | Stone-Zsela | No other Justice | Supports reversal and remand on the author's grounds | Part II states the narrower attribution ground with the component-specific Marks force explained below. Part III gives his own record application. The four other reversal supporters do not join either part. |
| Concurrence in part and in the judgment | Breyer | Stevens, Souter, Ginsburg | Joins Part I and supports reversal and remand | Independent actual-authority/material-aid and negligent-correction grounds; does not join Parts II–III. |
| Separate concurrence | Ginsburg | Souter, Breyer | Supports reversal and remand within their broader framework | Proposed, noncontrolling avoidable-harm defense. Stevens reserves it; Stone-Zsela leaves defenses unresolved. |
| Dissent | O'Connor | Scalia, Kennedy, Thomas | Would affirm | Kinman's exclusive actual-notice rule and its record-specific insufficiency application. |

#### Controlling Propositions and Authority

##### I. Actual notice to a corrective official is not the exclusive attribution route

**Controlling proposition:** A funding recipient's responsibility for a teacher's intentional discrimination cannot invariably be defeated solely because no official empowered to correct the harassment had actual notice. Kinman is limited insofar as it made that particular notice route exclusive; employment, ordinary teacher access or a technical regulatory breach, standing alone, still does not establish recipient liability.

**Authority:** The five Justices joining Part I directly adopt this limited negative proposition. They do not adopt a common complete damages framework.

**Controlling explanation:** Recipient responsibility remains distinct from the teacher's intentional wrong. That distinction does not make actual notice to a particular corrective official the only possible means of attribution. The categorical rule would exclude other supported grounds before their predicates and causal connection were examined. Franklin preserves compensation for proved intentional Title IX discrimination while requiring attribution and loss to be established separately. Kinman's actual-notice and deliberate-disregard route remains available, but its former exclusivity does not survive. The Court neither equates teacher employment with recipient responsibility nor treats a violation of grievance regulations as automatic discrimination or damages liability.

##### II. Authority materially enabling discrimination, combined with the recipient's causal fault, supplies a narrower attribution route

**Controlling proposition:** A recipient may be held responsible when school-conferred authority materially enables a teacher's intentional discrimination and the recipient's own actual knowledge or willfully blind, deliberately indifferent reporting failure causes that discrimination to continue. This is a sufficient route for attributing the discrimination beyond the actual-corrective-official-notice route; it establishes neither the underlying discrimination nor causal loss, damages or the defeat of a supported defense.

**Authority:** Part II of Stone-Zsela's opinion has no other formal joins. Its stated attribution component supplies the narrower controlling ground under Marks v. United States and Cipollone's component-specific application. Stevens, Souter, Ginsburg and Breyer independently support attribution whenever actual delegated educational power materially aids intentional harassment, without Stone-Zsela's additional recipient-fault requirement. School-conferred authority must actually and materially enable the wrong; title or opportunity alone is insufficient under either ground. Every case meeting the narrower conjunction therefore meets that independently sufficient broader attribution route. Those judgment-supporting positions total five, without converting the four Justices into joins in Stone-Zsela's opinion.

**Controlling explanation:** Franklin's damages remedy does not make every employee wrong the recipient's own wrong. The actual use of school-conferred authority must materially connect the discrimination to the educational relationship, and this narrower route additionally requires the recipient's own causal fault in its continuation. It does not permit the recipient to defeat attribution merely by deliberately disabling transmission of substantial warning information. Ordinary negligence or a technical policy omission alone does not satisfy this route. The underlying discrimination, institutional fault and causal continuation must still be proved.

Marks permits a narrower judgment-supporting ground without whole-opinion joins; Cipollone applies that inquiry to a particular legal component while leaving distinct liability and remedial questions unresolved. Here the four Justices' independent actual-authority ground contains the narrower attribution category. Their additional negligent-correction route is unnecessary to that inclusion. The proposed avoidable-harm limit supported by three of them concerns the extent of recovery; it does not negate their attribution ground. This limited nesting establishes neither their defense nor a complete damages formula. No general circumstantial-awareness presumption, automatic policy-failure inference or pooled-knowledge rule follows.

**Limits and questions not reached:** The authority, recipient-fault and causal-continuation conditions are conjunctive. The alternative involving reporting failure requires willful blindness and deliberate indifference, not constructive notice or negligence alone. The Court does not adopt ordinary agency liability as a complete general rule, the four-Justice negligence route or every Title VII attribution principle. Discrimination, institutional fault, causation, compensable injury, damages and defenses remain unresolved. Official-policy cases, prospective enforcement, individual liability and state-law remedies are outside this holding. The separate regulatory duties to adopt and publish prompt, equitable grievance procedures under 34 C.F.R. §106.8(b) and give nondiscrimination notice under §106.9(a) are not converted into strict private damages liability.

##### III. The appealed Title IX summary judgment is reversed for ordinary further proceedings

**Judgment and operative remedy:** The Fifth Circuit's judgment is reversed only insofar as it affirms summary judgment for the district on the appealed Title IX claim, and the case is remanded. The lower courts must determine whether school-conferred authority materially enabled the discrimination and whether the recipient's own actual knowledge or willfully blind, deliberately indifferent reporting failure caused it to continue, while preserving the actual-notice route and the remaining elements, ordinary procedures and defenses.

**Authority and application limit:** Stone-Zsela, Stevens, Souter, Ginsburg and Breyer support this formal reversal and ordinary remand. Stone-Zsela alone adopts Part III's application of his recipient-fault reasoning to the disputed warning and reporting evidence; the other four support further proceedings on their separately stated grounds. Their judgment agreement is not a five-Justice finding of actual knowledge, deliberate disregard, willful blindness, negligence, material aid, causation or liability. The Court orders neither compensation nor a compulsory trial. The unappealed constitutional, personal and state-law claims are not reopened.

#### Precedent Treatment

- **Kinman v. Omaha Public School District:** Limited insofar as actual notice to a corrective official was the exclusive recipient-attribution threshold. Its actual-notice, deliberate and unreasonable disregard and causal-injury route remains available, together with the other Title IX elements. Its rejection of automatic teacher-status attribution and a technical policy breach as sufficient by themselves remains. No earlier sole-Justice awareness inference becomes Court law merely through this decision.
- **Franklin v. Gwinnett County Public Schools:** Preserved as allowing appropriate compensation for proved intentional Title IX discrimination, subject to recipient responsibility, causation and loss. The present attribution holding does not establish a damages award or unintended-effects liability.
- **Cannon v. University of Chicago:** The established private action remains intact; no new regulatory cause of action is recognized.
- **Marks v. United States:** Applied to the particular sufficient attribution ground, without treating every reason or remedy proposed by a judgment supporter as controlling.
- **Cipollone v. Liggett Group:** Its component-specific logical-subset inquiry supports the limited attribution holding despite the absence of whole-opinion joins; separate proof, defenses and damages questions remain distinct.
- **Gade v. National Solid Wastes Management Association:** Its distinction between a directly shared negative proposition and unadopted complete affirmative frameworks supports keeping the exclusivity holding separate from the limited Marks analysis.
- **Meritor Savings Bank v. Vinson, Andrade and Faragher:** Their employment-attribution principles are not transferred wholesale to Title IX. Faragher's unresolved effective-protection/nonuse defense is not resolved here.
- **Pennhurst State School and Hospital v. Halderman:** Its funding-notice principle remains; neither it nor Title IX's administrative enforcement sequence becomes a literal exhaustion requirement for this private action.

#### Law After Decision

Actual notice to a corrective official is an available but no longer exclusive route to recipient responsibility. The narrower authority-plus-recipient-fault attribution route stated in Holding II controls at that component-specific scope under Marks. It supplies no comprehensive damages formula, no general awareness presumption and no rule of automatic liability from employment, nonreporting or a policy defect. The broader agency and negligent-correction theories and the three-Justice avoidable-harm defense remain noncontrolling. Kinman's distinct substantive harassment elements and the ordinary requirements of causation, injury, remedy and defense remain material. This record returns for further proceedings without any liability or compensation finding.

#### Separate Positions Relevant to the Decision

**Chief Justice Stone-Zsela's opinion announcing the judgment, Part III.** The complaint to the principal, the internal warning and the evidence about the absence of a functioning grievance route create, in his view, a factual dispute about the recipient's own fault. He would require the lower courts to examine whether school-conferred authority materially enabled the discrimination and whether actual recipient knowledge or willfully blind, deliberately indifferent reporting failure caused its continuation. A district cannot defeat that inquiry through its own failure to transmit substantial warning information. He does not treat the complaint about classroom remarks as an established report of a sexual relationship, resolve the conflicting policy evidence, or find liability. Employment, ordinary negligence and a technical regulatory violation alone remain insufficient. This record-specific application has no other joins; the attribution component of Part II has only the limited Marks force stated above.

**Justice Breyer, concurring in part and in the judgment, joined by Justices Stevens, Souter and Ginsburg.** They join Part I's rejection of exclusivity and support reversal and ordinary remand, but do not join Parts II–III. They renew the two independent attribution routes previously stated in their Kinman position. First, actual delegated educational power materially aiding intentional harassment can attribute the wrong to the recipient without a separate higher-management knowledge requirement. Second, negligent failure to correct after responsible officials knew or reasonably should have known, causing injury or continuation, supplies an independent ground. Teacher title or opportunity alone does not establish actual aid, and a policy breach alone establishes neither negligence nor causation.

Franklin permits compensation for intentional discrimination. These Justices distinguish the teacher's intentional wrong from the agency principles attributing that wrong; their negligence alternative is not compensation for every unintended discriminatory effect. Cannon supplies the established private action, while Title IX's administrative notice and correction process does not require a second intentional wrong by recipient management in every damages case. They acknowledge that Kinman had rejected their broader grounds and would reconsider that exclusivity. Title VII's agency decisions supply a limited analogy, not an automatic transfer of every employment rule.

The actual delegation, material aid, information received, correction, reporting arrangements and causal injury require ordinary further consideration. The affidavit/deposition tension over policy and uncertainty about dissemination are evidence, not conclusive findings. These Justices do not establish liability or compel a trial. Their judgment agreement does not endorse Stone-Zsela's record-specific awareness reasoning. Their broader attribution theories do not become controlling merely because they support the same judgment; only the narrower component identified in Holding II has the stated Marks force.

**Justice Ginsburg, separately concurring, joined by Justices Souter and Breyer.** Within their broader attribution framework, these three would permit a limited avoidable-consequences defense. The district would bear the burden of proving that internal remedies were adequately publicized and likely to provide redress without undue risk, effort or expense. Recovery would be barred only to the extent the plaintiff unreasonably failed to use those measures and consequently suffered avoidable harm. A paper policy, nonreporting alone, an unsafe or ineffective route or eventual correction would not establish the defense. The uncertain policy and dissemination record does not conclusively establish it here. This is a causal limit on avoidable recovery, not automatic complete immunity or an additional Court-adopted attribution condition. Stevens reserves the defense and does not join its terms; Stone-Zsela leaves defenses unresolved.

**Justice O'Connor, dissenting, joined by Justices Scalia, Kennedy and Thomas.** They would affirm under Kinman's actual-notice and deliberate-disregard rule. Franklin's availability of compensation does not answer recipient responsibility. Title IX's recipient structure, its administrative correction mechanism and Pennhurst's notice principle support attention to the recipient's own culpable nonresponse rather than ordinary agency attribution or knowledge imputed from the teacher's conduct. They would preserve Kinman's exclusivity and reject the additional attribution route.

Even assuming that the principal had corrective authority, the reported information and response do not, in their view, establish actual knowledge of the relationship or a substantial risk followed by deliberate and unreasonable disregard. The parents complained about classroom comments. The principal met with Waldrop, received an apology and assurance, cautioned him and informed the guidance counselor; termination followed police discovery. These responses are not declared perfect, and a warning does not invariably suffice. But this record does not establish the required culpable refusal to protect.

The possible absence of a functioning complaint procedure is not itself institutional actual notice. The principal's information cannot be pooled with Waldrop's knowledge, and the policy conflict cannot be resolved by assuming that every procedure was absent. Their rule requires neither a written complaint, a victim's personal report, a confession nor proof that a completed sexual relationship was already known. Actual notice of a substantial danger can suffice. Administrative and injunctive enforcement remain distinct, and the district receives no personal qualified immunity. These four oppose both reversal and the limitation of Kinman.

#### Source Notes

The procedural and evidentiary record is described at [524 U.S. 274](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/USREPORTS-524/pdf/USREPORTS-524-274.pdf) and in [the original record for No. 96-1866](https://archive.org/details/micro_IA40385014_0076). App. 44a–45a, 64a–65a and 72a–73a and the identified summary-judgment deposition materials preserve the policy conflict and reporting testimony. A complaint about classroom comments is not a reported sexual relationship. Title IX, 20 U.S.C. §§1681–1682, and 34 C.F.R. §§106.8(b) and 106.9(a) address distinct substantive, administrative and regulatory duties. [Marks v. United States, 430 U.S. 188](https://www.law.cornell.edu/supremecourt/text/430/188) supplies the narrowest-ground inquiry.

#### Mandate, Remedy, and Stage

The appealed Title IX judgment is reversed and the claim returns for ordinary further proceedings. The lower courts must examine the school-conferred authority, material enablement, recipient fault and causal continuation under the route identified above, as well as any actual-corrective-official-notice route and the remaining requirements and supported defenses. The district may prevail if the applicable elements fail or a proper defense defeats relief. Individual, constitutional and state-law claims, administrative enforcement and prospective relief receive no new disposition.

### South Dakota v. Yankton Sioux Tribe, No. 96-1581

Plenary merits decision, January 26, 1998

#### Chronology and Posture

Argued December 8, 1997. On writ of certiorari to the Eighth Circuit, 99 F.3d 1439, which affirmed a declaration that the 1894 Act left the reservation's exterior boundary unchanged and that the waste-site tract lies within it. Review concerns the ceded, unallotted lands and the declaration's continuing consequences after EPA permitted a compacted-clay liner.

The Tribe did not appeal the separate denial of its injunction and rejection of its asserted regulation of this non-Indian activity. The Waste District supported the State without appealing after construction could proceed. The existing state permit and operative reservation-status declaration remain the subject of the controversy.

#### Judgment

**Reverse and remand, 9–0.**

**Supporting:** Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer.

#### Opinion Topology

Stevens delivers the opinion of the Court, joined in full by Stone-Zsela, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer. All nine adopt the three holdings and their qualifications. No separate writing issues.

#### Controlling Propositions and Authority

##### The existing declaration presents a continuing concrete controversy

**Controlling proposition:** The EPA's waiver of a particular liner requirement does not moot the State's challenge where the operative declaration continues to determine the territorial legal regime governing its issued permit for this identified site. That continuing legal relationship permits review; anticipated similar projects or a wish to erase unfavorable precedent alone would not.

**Authority:** The unanimous Opinion of the Court supplies direct authority.

**Controlling explanation:** Lee requires concrete personal application throughout review and distinguishes limited jurisdictional correction from power to decide the merits. This case concerns an actual permitted facility and an extant declaration classifying its land, rather than a chain of future requests or possible enforcement. Reversing that declaration changes the parties' current legal relationship. The EPA waiver permits clay-lined construction but does not vacate the territorial determination. The Court adopts no rule that an adverse declaration always preserves jurisdiction after every underlying consequence disappears. Nor does jurisdiction to review this declaration reopen the Tribe's separate, unappealed regulatory and injunction ruling.

##### The cession and purchase diminished the reservation as to the ceded lands

**Controlling proposition:** The Act of August 15, 1894 diminished the Yankton Sioux Reservation by removing the ordinary ceded, unallotted lands disposed of under its settler-opening program. The comprehensive surrender and unconditional federal purchase, read with the entire agreement, establish clear congressional intent. Article XVIII's treaty-saving language protects its annuity and associated entitlement assurances without preserving the 1858 boundary over those ceded lands.

**Authority:** All nine Justices join this entire statutory construction, its evidentiary limits and its application to the identified site.

**Controlling explanation:** Solem makes statutory text most probative and gives exceptional, but not conclusive, weight to comprehensive cession coupled with guaranteed compensation. Article I surrenders all tribal claim, right, title and interest in the specified unallotted lands; Article II obligates the United States to pay $600,000. The appropriation and distribution provisions confirm a purchase, not an undertaking merely to sell for the Tribe and remit proceeds. Hagen preserves that clear-cession route even though this Act lacks the operative public-domain restoration command decisive there. No particular formula is indispensable.

Article XVIII is substantial contrary text: it disclaims treaty abrogation, preserves all 1858 provisions as though the agreement had not been made, and separately guarantees continued annuities. Ownership and reservation status can differ, so a sale alone would not answer the Tribe's boundary-preservation argument. But the whole agreement's comprehensive surrender and federal purchase carry territorial significance beyond opening for settlement. Literal preservation of the treaty's possession and exclusion provisions would also contradict the settlement commanded by the transaction. The documented concern over federal annuities, past claims and future entitlements explains the clause's reassurance; its broad words do not undo the specific land surrender. The Court neither deletes those words nor decides every other treaty entitlement.

The state school-section disposition and Article XVII's distinction between ceded and other lands reinforce this reading. A specific school exception and a permanent liquor restriction could also serve purposes within retained boundaries, so neither independently controls. Article VIII's retained agency and school uses caution against total termination. Contemporary common-property and state-law statements corroborate the enacted choice without establishing every signatory's understanding of future boundaries. Conflicting later labels carry little force. State administration, settlement and demographics remain subordinate clues, not events changing boundaries.

Dion's clear-and-plain requirement and the tribal ambiguity canon remain intact. The complete enactment supplies the congressional choice here; genuine unresolved ambiguity would favor retention. No quantified proof burden or demographic threshold is created.

##### The supported parcel category determines the mandate's limits

**Controlling proposition:** The identified waste-site tract falls outside the reservation because it is established as ceded, unallotted land disposed of under this program, not merely because a non-Indian now owns it in fee. The declaration must be corrected on that ground. Total disestablishment and the status or authority associated with other land categories remain undecided.

**Authority:** The unanimous Opinion of the Court adopts this application and bounded remedy.

**Controlling explanation:** The reported 1904 homestead deed matches the complaint's parcel and supplies affirmative evidence of the land category. Seymour's distinction between title and reservation boundaries remains controlling; fee ownership alone cannot remove land from a retained reservation. The Court therefore decides the ceded-land question without classifying every allotment, trust parcel, Article VIII agency or school tract, or later acquisition. Separate dependent-community and unextinguished-allotment categories under §1151 retain their own predicates. Hagen likewise requires the territorial holding to remain distinct from every sovereign's powers. Neither the present holding nor remand restores the unappealed tribal regulatory claim, cancels the EPA waiver, orders a composite liner or adjudicates private title. Properly remaining parcel-specific and statutory jurisdiction questions may be addressed below consistently with this boundary ruling.

#### Precedent Treatment and Current-Law Effect

- Hagen is applied for an operative congressional-act inquiry, unequal evidentiary weights and geographic limits. Its surviving public-domain restoration command is distinguished from this Act's cession-and-purchase ground; neither mere opening nor fee ownership suffices.
- Solem is applied to the complete Act and its strongly weighted cession/guaranteed-compensation inference. Its sales-agency setting is distinguished; its tribal ambiguity canon and subordinate treatment of later evidence remain intact.
- DeCoteau's controlling clear-cession principle supports the reading without making its precise words, purchase structure or negotiations mandatory for every case.
- Dion retains the clear-and-plain requirement concerning the particular treaty right affected; no general cancellation of treaty obligations is adopted.
- Rosebud preserves the absence of a mandatory verbal formula. Seymour and Mattz continue to distinguish sale, settlement and fee ownership from congressional diminishment.
- Bourland's separate inquiry into exclusion-derived regulation and residual authority is not converted into a boundary rule. Sac and Fox's distinct Indian-country categories remain intact; Negonsott's Kansas-specific authorization supplies no general territorial rule here.
- Lee is applied to the actual continuing permit/declaration controversy, without treating corrective jurisdiction alone as power to decide merits.

The original 1858 line does not preserve reservation status over the ordinary ceded, unallotted settler lands governed by the 1894 transaction. The State has primary territorial jurisdiction at the established waste site. This whole-act construction adds that specific diminishment holding while retaining the existing congressional-intent framework, tribal ambiguity canon and distinction between ownership and territory.

The decision supplies no complete regional boundary map, total-disestablishment holding, universal state jurisdiction or cancellation of every treaty promise. Allotments, trust property, retained agency and school parcels, later acquisitions, other §1151 categories and surviving federal or tribal powers require their own legal and factual predicates. Demographic change and long state administration cannot themselves create or reverse diminishment.

#### Record and Sources

The enacted agreement and ratification appear in the [Act of August 15, 1894, §12, 28 Stat. 314–319](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/STATUTE-28/pdf/STATUTE-28-Pg286-2.pdf). The lower proceedings, statutory dispute and majority/dissent appear in 99 F.3d 1439. The specific parcel recitation, EPA waiver and unappealed regulatory ruling are reported at 522 U.S. 329–358 in [the official report](https://tile.loc.gov/storage-services/service/ll/usrep/usrep522/usrep522329/usrep522329.pdf). Regional acreage and population estimates vary; none determines the particular parcel classification or supplies a numerical boundary test. The ruling does not presume an exact understanding of future boundaries by every tribal signatory or classify unexamined remaining parcels.

#### Mandate, Remedy, and Stage

The Eighth Circuit's judgment is reversed. The case returns for judgment recognizing diminishment as to the ceded lands and further proceedings on properly remaining parcel-specific or statutory jurisdiction questions consistently with that boundary. South Dakota has primary territorial jurisdiction at the established waste site; no universal allocation of every sovereign's authority follows. The unappealed denial of tribal regulatory and injunctive relief remains undisturbed. The EPA waiver remains operative; no different liner, new enforcement action, hearing, regional survey, deadline, damages award or automatic further Supreme Court review is directed. This Supreme Court review is complete.

### Minnesota v. Mille Lacs Band of Chippewa Indians, No. 97-1337

Merits decision, March 24, 1999

#### Chronology and Posture

Argued December 2, 1998. On certiorari to the Eighth Circuit, whose judgment, 124 F.3d 904, recognized continuing treaty hunting, fishing and gathering rights subject to lawful conservation regulation.

Minnesota asks whether the 1850 Executive Order, 1855 Treaty, statehood or later events terminated the rights reserved on the ceded territory in the 1837 Treaty during the President's pleasure. The Court considers each asserted terminating act separately.

#### Judgment

**Affirmed, 6–3, preserving recognition of the surviving treaty rights and the declaratory and injunctive framework within the questions reviewed.**

**Supporting:** Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Souter, Ginsburg and Breyer.

**Opposing:** Scalia, Kennedy and Thomas, each relying on the three independent extinction grounds described in the dissents.

The treaty rights remain subject to lawful, necessary nondiscriminatory conservation measures. No particular regulation, harvest allocation, private-land-access claim or right outside the ceded territory is decided.

#### Opinion Topology

Justice Stevens delivers the Opinion of the Court, Parts I through IV, joined in full by Chief Justice Stone-Zsela and Justices O'Connor, Souter, Ginsburg and Breyer. All six join the combined-order, 1855-treaty, statehood and bounded-remedy holdings. Justice Scalia dissents, joined in full by Justices Kennedy and Thomas, on three independent extinction grounds confined to their party- and treaty-specific scope. Justice Thomas also dissents separately on the treaty-specific scope of state regulation; no other Justice joins that additional objection.

#### Controlling Propositions and Authority

##### I. The combined removal order did not terminate the reserved use rights

**Holding and operative rule.** An unauthorized compelled-removal order does not terminate reserved treaty use rights when its revocation and removal commands are inseparable parts of the same purpose and operation. The 1850 order therefore did not extinguish these rights; the Court does not decide the full extent of an independently exercised presidential power under the treaty's pleasure clause.

**Authority.** Part I of Justice Stevens's Opinion of the Court, adopted by the six Justices identified above. All six join the bounded combined-order rationale and its reservation of independent presidential revocation authority.

**Controlling explanation.** The Removal Act's consent structure did not authorize the compelled removal described by this record. That unlawful removal command could not cancel the separately reserved use rights. The order connected its revocation command to that same removal purpose and operation; treating revocation as a standalone measure would create a materially different executive action rather than preserve an independently chosen lawful termination. Minnesota's strongest argument is that the treaty separately permitted revocation during the President's pleasure. Even assuming an independently lawful revocation could occur, that possibility does not make this combined order severable or validate its unauthorized removal command. The Court decides this order's supported relationship and operation. It adopts neither a prohibition of every independent presidential revocation nor a rule that subsequent nonenforcement restores rights already lawfully extinguished.

##### II. The 1855 land cession did not surrender these independent use rights

**Holding and operative rule.** A later land cession does not extinguish separately retained treaty use rights unless the agreement, read in its setting and as the tribal parties reasonably understood it, clearly relinquishes those rights. The 1855 agreement did not surrender the Band's retained hunting, fishing and gathering privileges.

**Authority.** Part II of Justice Stevens's Opinion of the Court, adopted by the same six Justices.

**Controlling explanation.** Dion requires clear evidence that the disputed right was considered and relinquished, without imposing an invariable formula of express words. Menominee and Fishing Vessel protect retained rights against casual implication. Here the supported negotiations concerned a land purchase and did not address surrender of the independent use privileges. The broad cession language must be read in that setting. Klamath's reservation-dependent exclusive rights do not make every land transfer a surrender of a separately retained use right. Bourland likewise distinguishes regulation based on a power to exclude from personal treaty uses that remain after the relevant land change. The Court therefore rejects Minnesota's claim that comprehensive conveyancing words alone settle this distinct right. No cession by a nonsignatory Band or surrender under another agreement is inferred.

##### III. Statehood did not itself extinguish the treaty rights

**Holding and operative rule.** Minnesota's admission to the Union did not itself terminate otherwise surviving federal treaty use rights. Those rights can coexist with lawful, necessary nondiscriminatory conservation regulation; the treaty's presidential-pleasure condition does not make state admission the terminating event.

**Authority.** Stevens's Part III has the same six-Justice coalition.

**Controlling explanation.** The Supremacy Clause preserves enforceable federal treaty rights after state admission. Winans, Tulee and Fishing Vessel demonstrate the compatibility of reserved uses with lawful conservation authority. Race Horse addressed a differently conditioned right on unoccupied lands. Its treaty-specific construction does not establish that this pleasure-qualified reservation ended at statehood. To the extent Race Horse treats equal footing alone as incompatible with surviving treaty use rights, the Court rejects that proposition. Minnesota's resource-management interest remains substantial, but it can operate through the governing conservation standards rather than an inference of wholesale extinction. The Court does not decide every concrete regulation or displace Race Horse's separate construction of its own treaty condition.

##### IV. The affirmance preserves the rights at their actual scope

**Holding and operative rule.** Recognition of the surviving rights within the ceded territory remains in force against the presented extinction claims, subject to the applicable treaty and conservation standards. Survival does not itself decide private access, unrelated criminal conduct, numerical allocations or rights beyond the treaty's territory.

**Authority.** Stevens's Part IV has the same six-Justice coalition.

**Controlling explanation.** None of the presented terminating acts extinguished the rights, and no supported later event supplies another termination premise. That resolves the reviewed judgment. It does not require reopening every resource-management ruling in the larger proceedings, granting an unrestricted harvest or conferring a veto over every state rule. The declaration and related injunctive framework therefore remain in force within their lawful scope, without a new allocation, title transfer or retrospective award.

#### Precedent Treatment

- Dion is applied: clear abrogation is required, but no mandatory form of express words is imposed.
- Menominee and Fishing Vessel are applied: independently reserved rights are not extinguished by casual implication.
- Klamath is distinguished: reservation-dependent exclusive rights do not determine the fate of these retained use privileges.
- Bourland is distinguished and preserved: exclusion-derived nonmember regulation and retained personal uses remain different rights.
- Hagen and Yankton retain their instrument-specific inquiry into actual federal displacement; land or ownership change alone does not decide every retained right.
- Race Horse is limited: its broader equal-footing incompatibility premise cannot terminate otherwise surviving rights merely at statehood; its distinct treaty-condition construction is not decided here.
- Winans and Tulee are applied with Fishing Vessel: treaty uses and lawful conservation regulation can coexist.

#### Law After Decision

The Band's reserved uses survive the three asserted extinction grounds. The 1850-order, 1855, statehood and bounded-remedy holdings each control through the same six direct joins. No combination of separate opinions is needed to identify the governing rules. Race Horse no longer supports extinction solely from equal-footing incompatibility at statehood.

#### Separate Positions Relevant to the Decision

The common dissent of Scalia, Kennedy and Thomas would find the rights extinguished on three independent grounds. The treaty's revocation authority gives the revocation command independent operation even if removal was unauthorized. The 1855 comprehensive cession, in their view, reaches the retained use interest; negotiation silence does not overcome that text. Race Horse's temporary-right analysis also applies to a privilege conditioned on presidential pleasure. They reject the Court's removal-purpose, land-purchase-context and conservation-coexistence answers, while confining their 1855 ground to the agreement's parties and rights and declining to nullify every tribal right at statehood.

Thomas separately questions whether survival alone establishes that Minnesota's regulatory authority is limited to conservation necessity. He considers the treaty's pleasure-qualified privilege distinct from the perpetual usual-and-accustomed-place language in other fishing cases and would require a treaty-specific examination of regulatory scope. His objection decides no particular state regulation and receives no additional join.

#### Source Notes

The record concerns the 1837 and 1855 Treaties, the 1850 Executive Order and the Eighth Circuit judgment reported at 124 F.3d 904. The public materials are reproduced in the [official United States Reports](https://www.supremecourt.gov/opinions/boundvolumes/526bv.pdf), 526 U.S. 172–226. The holding is confined to the supported terminating acts and does not establish unreported subsequent events or new findings about particular regulations.

#### Mandate, Remedy, and Stage

Supreme Court review of the presented extinction claims is complete. The judgment recognizing the rights and its related framework remain in force, subject to applicable treaty and conservation standards. The Court directs no new allocation, hearing, deadline, compensation or adjudication of unpresented management claims.

**End of entry: Minnesota v. Mille Lacs Band of Chippewa Indians, No. 97-1337, merits decision, March 24, 1999.**

### Davis v. Monroe County Board of Education, No. 97-843

Merits decision, May 24, 1999

#### Chronology and Posture

Argued January 12, 1999. On certiorari to the en banc Eleventh Circuit, 120 F.3d 1390, which affirmed dismissal of the district's Title IX damages claim. The question is whether the funding recipient may be responsible for its own deliberate response to serious student-on-student sexual harassment within its substantial control.

A fifth-grade student allegedly endured repeated touching and sexual propositions by a classmate over several months. The child and her mother reportedly informed teachers and the principal; officials allegedly failed to protect her while the conduct continued and interfered with education. The harasser's later guilty plea is a procedural recital, not proof of every civil element or earlier institutional notice. The case concerns pleading sufficiency, not findings of recipient fault or damages.

All nine Justices participated at argument and decision.

#### Judgment

**Reversed and remanded, 6–3.**

**Supporting:** Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Souter, Ginsburg and Breyer.
**Opposing:** Scalia, Kennedy and Thomas.

Only the dismissal of the appealed Title IX damages claim against the district is reversed. The recipient claim may proceed under the conditions explained below. Knowledge, culpability, control, sex-based educational deprivation, causation, compensable injury and any supported defense remain to be proved or decided. No compulsory trial, damages award, particular punishment, individual-capacity recovery or reopening of unrelated claims is ordered.

#### Opinion Topology

| Writing | Author | Joined by | Relationship to judgment | Scope joined |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Opinion of the Court, Part I | Ginsburg | Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Souter, Breyer | Supports reversal | Sufficient actual-notice recipient-fault route for controlled peer harassment, including control, culpability, causation and limits. |
| Opinion of the Court, Part II | Ginsburg | Stevens, O'Connor, Souter, Breyer | Supplies the governing educational-harm standard | Severe-or-pervasive, objectively abusive and actually experienced environment impairing educational access or benefit. |
| Opinion of the Court, Part III | Ginsburg | Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Souter, Breyer | Supports ordinary reversal/remand | Sufficiency of these allegations and the bounded remedy; no final element or liability finding. |
| Concurrence in part and in the judgment | Stone-Zsela | No other Justice | Supports reversal; joins Parts I and III | Requires severe, pervasive and objectively offensive harassment; separately proposes willfully blind reporting-failure responsibility. |
| Dissent | Kennedy | Scalia, Thomas | Would affirm the appealed dismissal | Statutory attribution and funding-notice objection to nonagent peer responsibility. |

#### Controlling Propositions and Authority

##### A school's own culpable response can support damages for controlled peer harassment

**Holding and operative rule:** A Title IX funding recipient may be liable for its own response to peer harassment because of sex when an official empowered to correct the harassment or its substantial danger has actual notice, the recipient substantially controls both the harasser and the relevant educational context, and deliberate and unreasonable disregard of that danger causally contributes to further harassment or vulnerability producing educational deprivation and compensable injury. A peer's attendance does not impute the peer's acts to the recipient; knowledge, culpability, control, sex-based discrimination and causal loss are independent requirements.

**Authority:** The six-Justice opinion in Ginsburg's Part I establishes this sufficient route.

**Controlling explanation:** Franklin permits compensation for proved intentional Title IX discrimination but leaves recipient responsibility to be established. Kinman's actual-notice route identifies a recipient's own deliberate disregard of a known educational danger. The Court extends that sufficient route to peer conduct where the school has substantial remedial control; it does not claim Kinman already decided peers. The school's knowing, culpable continuation of educational deprivation supplies the institutional wrong, answering the objection that a student cannot personally bind the funding recipient. Mere discipline authority or an ineffective result does not suffice. Actual notice must reach an official empowered to correct the danger; generic constructive notice, the harasser's own knowledge and technical policy noncompliance do not establish this route. A reasonable response is lawful even if harassment continues, and courts prescribe neither a selected punishment nor a victim's preferred remedy. Constitutional and statutory limits on discipline inform control and response. Gebser's school-delegated teacher authority does not transfer to classmates; its conjunctive teacher route and the other teacher holdings remain intact. The Court neither adopts ordinary negligence or general agency law nor rejects every distinct theory of direct official discrimination. Receipt of assistance remains separately necessary under NCAA v. Smith. Causal injury cannot be inferred solely from harassment predating legally sufficient recipient notice.

##### Educational deprivation follows the contextual severe-or-pervasive standard

**Holding and operative rule:** The harassment must be sufficiently severe or pervasive to create an objectively abusive educational environment actually experienced as abusive and impair educational access or benefit in its full context. Sexually charged conduct or offense alone does not establish discrimination because of sex, the required educational harm or the independent institutional elements.

**Authority:** The five-Justice opinion in Ginsburg's Part II controls. Stone-Zsela does not join this disjunctive threshold; no combination of separate rationales is needed for its authority.

**Controlling explanation:** Kinman adopted severe-or-pervasive, objectively abusive and actually experienced educational harassment without mandatory psychiatric injury, measurable grade loss or a rigid list of factors. Its reliance on Meritor and Harris preserves attention to welcome, objective and experienced abuse, and the actual setting. The peer relationship affects the contextual application and recipient-control inquiry; it does not silently replace the substantive standard with a requirement that conduct be both severe and pervasive. Age, relationship, educational setting and actual effect may matter without becoming a mandatory checklist. Neither physical exclusion nor total loss of schooling is invariably required, and no one proxy is sufficient by itself. Ordinary teasing or offense fails without the substantive and institutional requirements. No universal rule determines whether one incident always suffices or never can. These allegations involve repeated touching and propositions over months with educational interference, a materially different claimed environment. The Court decides no ultimate fact and does not treat every unwelcome interaction as actionable discrimination.

##### The pleaded recipient claim warrants ordinary further proceedings

**Holding and operative rule:** Allegations of repeated school-setting harassment, reports including to the principal, continued conduct, deliberately inadequate protection and resulting educational interference state a claim for examination under the sufficient recipient-fault route. The categorical dismissal must be reversed, but an absent element, reasonable response, inadequate control or notice, missing causation or a supported defense may defeat recovery on the developed record.

**Authority:** The six-Justice opinion in Part III controls this allegation-stage application and remedy.

**Controlling explanation:** The alleged response concerns the district's own use of authority after reports of a known danger, rather than automatic responsibility for the classmate. The asserted repeated abuse and educational disruption permit the necessary elements to be tested. Allegations that reports reached the principal do not finally establish every official's corrective power, knowledge or opportunity; the criminal plea supplies no earlier notice or complete causal showing. The plaintiff must prove the institutional and substantive conditions, including postnotice causal injury. Existing compensation principles therefore require ordinary further adjudication, not immediate damages or judicial control of discipline. No compulsory trial follows if ordinary procedure establishes a missing element or valid defense. The decision reaches only the appealed district Title IX claim and leaves unrelated and individual claims outside the mandate.

#### Precedent Treatment

- Franklin v. Gwinnett County Public Schools is applied to available compensation for proved intentional discrimination, with recipient responsibility, causation and loss still distinct.
- Kinman v. Omaha Public School District is extended at its sufficient actual-notice/culpable-disregard route to substantially controlled peer conduct. Its severe-or-pervasive contextual educational-harm standard remains; the peer extension is stated expressly.
- Gebser v. Lago Vista Independent School District retains the limited teacher-authority-plus-recipient-fault conjunction and its rejection of exclusivity in teacher cases. Neither delegated teacher power nor that complete route is imputed to peers.
- NCAA v. Smith preserves the distinct federal-assistance recipient requirement; economic connection or control alone does not establish receipt.
- Meritor Savings Bank v. Vinson informs the distinction between participation and welcome through Kinman without importing employment attribution wholesale.
- Harris v. Forklift Systems informs the contextual objective-and-experienced abuse inquiry through Kinman, without mandatory psychiatric injury or any indispensable factor.
- Pennhurst State School and Hospital v. Halderman retains its funding-notice principle; the controlling recipient-own-wrong route concerns intentional educational discrimination, not automatic liability for a third party.

#### Law After Decision

A funding recipient's own actually informed, deliberately unreasonable response to peer harassment within substantial control can support damages when all independent discrimination, educational-harm and causal-injury elements are established. The controlling environment threshold remains severe or pervasive, objectively abusive and actually experienced as abusive, with impaired educational access or benefit. Teacher-attribution rules remain separately bounded. Ordinary negligence, policy breach, unsuccessful reasonable correction and peer attendance alone do not establish liability. The Chief's separate reporting-failure extension does not become a controlling peer rule, and no complete alternative-attribution or defense code is decided.

#### Separate Positions Relevant to the Decision

**Stone-Zsela, concurring in part and in the judgment:** The Chief joins the actual-notice recipient-fault route and the reversal on these allegations. The school is answerable for its own deliberately indifferent failure to use available authority after reliable notice, with meaningful control, clearly inadequate response, sex-based educational deprivation and causal injury independently proved. Kinman's sufficient notice route remains available, while Gebser's teacher authority cannot simply be attributed to a classmate. He would require severe, pervasive and objectively offensive peer harassment depriving the victim of equal educational access, and therefore withholds assent from the Court's severe-or-pervasive threshold. He additionally would recognize the recipient's willfully blind, deliberately indifferent reporting failure causing continuation as a sufficient institutional ground. That proposed reporting-failure extension is his separate position, not the Court's peer rule. Neither misconduct nor negligent supervision alone suffices; officials retain disciplinary discretion so long as the response is not deliberately indifferent. He requires no particular punishment and makes no liability finding.

**Kennedy, joined by Scalia and Thomas, dissenting:** They would affirm the appealed peer-harassment dismissal because Title IX's funding condition does not sufficiently establish this institutional attribution. Franklin makes compensation available after recipient discrimination is shown; it does not identify the recipient's wrong. Kinman concerned a teacher, and Gebser's additional route still requires materially enabling delegated teacher authority. A nonagent pupil does not have that link. These Justices address the Court's own-response theory directly: disciplinary power and knowing nonresponse to a student's discrimination do not, on their view, supply the necessary statutory duty or clear funding notice. Legal limits on school control and the breadth of the proposed obligation reinforce the objection. They neither find the allegations false nor approve the conduct. A supported claim that school officials themselves enforce rules differently because of sex remains distinct; they decide no such new claim. Existing teacher law and remedies for established recipient discrimination remain intact.

#### Source Notes

The reported pleading posture, argument and decision dates appear in the [official United States Reports, 526 U.S. 629–686](https://www.supremecourt.gov/opinions/boundvolumes/526bv.pdf), and the judgment below is 120 F.3d 1390. Exact warnings, each official's power, grievance efficacy and postnotice causal events remain allegation-stage matters; the allegations are not findings on a complete evidentiary record. Title IX's 20 U.S.C. §1681(a) prohibition, the existing recipient requirement and the stated limits govern the claim.

#### Mandate, Remedy, and Stage

The appealed Title IX dismissal is reversed and the case returns for ordinary proceedings under the controlling recipient and educational-harm rules. The lower courts determine the actual facts, elements, supported defenses and any compensable injury. No damages, compulsory trial, disciplinary measure, reopening of unrelated claims or additional Supreme Court date is directed.

**End of entry: Davis v. Monroe County Board of Education, merits decision, May 24, 1999.**

### Gonzaga University v. Doe, No. 01-679

Merits decision, June 20, 2002

#### Chronology and Posture

Argued April 24, 2002. On certiorari to the Washington Supreme Court, which reinstated the FERPA-based Section 1983 damages claim. The granted question concerns whether FERPA's nondisclosure provisions create an enforceable individual right. The certification specialist disclosed identifying information after investigating an allegation of misconduct; the allegation is not a finding that the misconduct occurred. State action is outside the grant.

All nine Justices participated.

#### Judgment

**FERPA-based federal judgment reversed and remanded, 6–3; independent state-law judgments remain intact.**

**Supporting:** O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Breyer.


**Opposing:** Stone-Zsela, Stevens, Ginsburg.

#### Opinions

THOMAS, J., announced the judgment of the Court and delivered an opinion, in which O'CONNOR, SCALIA, and KENNEDY, JJ., joined. BREYER, J., filed an opinion concurring in the judgment, in which SOUTER, J., joined. STONE-ZSELA, C. J., filed a dissenting opinion, in which STEVENS and GINSBURG, JJ., joined.

| Writing | Author | Joined by | Relationship to judgment | Scope joined |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Opinion announcing the judgment | Thomas | O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy | Reverses the FERPA-based judgment | Institution-directed no-right analysis; four Justices |
| Concurrence in the judgment | Breyer | Souter | Agrees in reversal | FERPA-specific absence of the asserted enforceable entitlement; no new general formula |
| Dissent | Stone-Zsela | Stevens, Ginsburg | Would affirm federal enforceability | Defined nondisclosure entitlement enforceable through Section 1983, subject to statutory and independent predicates |

No opinion commands a majority.

**The four-Justice lead opinion.** Thomas, joined by O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, reads FERPA's policy-or-practice funding condition as an institutional obligation administered through federal oversight rather than the asserted individual nondisclosure entitlement. Mandatory wording, identified records and consent instructions do not alone change that statutory object. Blessing requires a particular individual right; Bruneau preserves Section 1983 once that right is established but does not create it here. This analysis does not make funding statutes categorically unenforceable, treat absence of an implied action as dispositive, or establish automatic administrative foreclosure. Breyer and Souter agree with the judgment under their narrower explanation and do not join the lead opinion.

#### Holdings

##### These FERPA nondisclosure provisions do not support the asserted Section 1983 claim

**Holding and operative rule:** FERPA's education-record nondisclosure provisions do not establish the individual judicially enforceable entitlement asserted in this Section 1983 action. The FERPA-based federal judgment must therefore be reversed; the state-action determination, excluded from review, and independent state-law judgments are unaffected.

**Authority:** Six Justices agree on this statute-specific conclusion. Thomas's lead opinion is joined by O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy; Breyer, joined by Souter, concurs in the judgment on the separate FERPA-specific explanation below. No majority adopts a new general formula equating a statutory right with intent to create a private remedy.

**Controlling explanation:** Section 1983 requires an individual federal right, not merely an asserted statutory violation. Six Justices conclude that these FERPA provisions do not establish the individual judicially enforceable entitlement asserted here, so this federal component cannot stand. Their agreement does not extend to one general formula for finding statutory rights or to a single full explanation of this statute. The four-Justice lead and the two-Justice concurrence supply their own reasons, stated separately. The holding concerns these nondisclosure provisions; it neither categorically excludes funding legislation nor makes the absence of an implied statutory action dispositive. Because this threshold fails, a separate damages ruling is unnecessary.

#### Precedent Treatment and Current Law

- Blessing v. Freestone, April 21, 1997: applied through the particular asserted statutory entitlement, not a generalized interest in sound administration.
- Suter v. Artist M., March 25, 1992: its provision-specific distinction between enforceable entitlement and institutional obligation remains effective.
- Bruneau v. South Kortright Central School District, June 3, 1999: an established individual Title IX right remains enforceable through Section 1983 absent incompatible displacement; that established right does not create a missing FERPA right.
- Owasso Independent School District No. I-011 v. Falvo, February 19, 2002: its definition of education records remains; it did not resolve private enforcement.
- Powell v. Ridge, April 3, 2000, and Alexander v. Sandoval, April 24, 2001: their distinct regulation and statutory-channel holdings are not substituted for the right-creation inquiry here.
- Barnes v. Gorman, June 17, 2002: its incorporated remedial channels do not supply a Section 1983 damages ruling in this threshold disposition.

The asserted FERPA nondisclosure claim cannot proceed through Section 1983 because these provisions do not establish the required judicially enforceable entitlement. Existing law continues to distinguish right creation, the enacted Section 1983 channel, implied statutory actions and incompatible remedial displacement. No broader rights formula commands five votes in this decision.

#### Separate Writings

**Breyer, concurring in the judgment, joined by Souter.** This statute's broad education-record coverage, uncertain reach in practical administration and institutional policy-or-practice structure do not establish the asserted judicially enforceable privacy entitlement. That FERPA-specific conclusion is sufficient. A new general demand for language proving intent to authorize a private remedy would blur the separate question whether Congress already supplied enforcement through Section 1983. Administrative consistency informs the interpretation of this particular obligation; it does not independently extinguish a right already found.

**Stone-Zsela, dissenting, joined by Stevens and Ginsburg.** FERPA identifies covered records, persons whose consent controls and authorized disclosures. Under Blessing, those terms define a manageable personal privacy entitlement. The policy-or-practice condition limits the violation; it does not make the student merely an incidental beneficiary. The protected education records contain information directly related to a student and are maintained by the institution or a person acting for it. Consent rights pass from the parent to the student upon reaching eighteen or attending a postsecondary institution. A claimant must still establish the required institutional policy or practice and an unauthorized disclosure under the enacted exceptions, together with Section 1983's independent predicates; the dissent does not decide any disputed statutory violation beyond the granted enforceability issue. Central correction and funding sanctions neither expressly exclude that channel nor supply an incompatible individual remedial substitute. Unlike Powell's regulation-created claim, the asserted entitlement arises from the statute. Sandoval addresses a different direct enforcement route; the absence of an implied FERPA action does not defeat Congress's existing Section 1983 remedy. State action is assumed solely within the grant limit. The dissent would affirm the federal enforceability ruling without adding damages or reopening state-law awards.

#### Source Notes

Gonzaga University v. Doe, 143 Wash. 2d 687, 24 P.3d 390 (2001); 20 U.S.C. § 1232g, including subsections (b), (d), (f) and (g); 42 U.S.C. § 1983. Review excludes state action. The certification allegation is not an adjudicated finding of misconduct.

#### Mandate, Remedy, and Stage

The case returns for proceedings consistent with rejection of the FERPA-based Section 1983 claim. Independent state-law judgments remain intact. The Court does not reopen the excluded state-action issue or adjudicate an ungranted damages question.

**End of entry: Gonzaga University v. Doe, Merits decision, June 20, 2002.**
