# Eulitt v. Maine Department of Education; 386 F.3d 344 (First Circuit).
Source: ../records/eulitt-v-maine-department-of-education_merits_2005-06-20.md

## Event

Eulitt v. Maine Department of Education; review of 386 F.3d 344 (First Circuit).

Merits decision, June 20, 2005. The First Circuit affirmed summary judgment for Maine on October 22, 2004. The questions are whether equal protection requires payment of the ordinary St. Dominic's tuition requested by these parents and whether they may assert the school's separate constitutional rights without an obstacle to its own suit.

Minot contracts for at least ninety percent of its high-school students to attend Poland Regional High School. Up to ten percent may attend another approved nonsectarian school upon showing educational needs the public school cannot meet. The parents seek ordinary tuition at St. Dominic's specifically for Catholic instruction and viewpoint. They did not submit a formal application because the statutory sectarian exclusion would foreclose it. Their own challenge is adjudicated separately from the attempted assertion of the school's rights.

## Participation

Chief Justice Stone-Zsela and Justices Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer participated.

## Public Action

The judgment of the Court of Appeals for the First Circuit is affirmed.

## Judgment & Remedy

**Affirmed, 9–0.**

**Supporting both components:** Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer.

The parents' equal-protection claim concerning this integrated-tuition purchase fails, and their requested tuition and injunction are denied. Their attempted assertion of St. Dominic's independent rights is rejected for lack of a demonstrated obstacle to the school's own suit; that disposition does not adjudicate the merits of the school's claim.

## Opinion Topology

BREYER, J., delivered the opinion of the Court, in which Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg joined.

| Writing | Author | Joined by | Relationship to judgment | Scope joined |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Opinion of the Court | Breyer | Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg | Supports both components | Complete opinion, including the actual-purchase equality rule, independent hindrance requirement and disposition |

## Holdings

The opinion of the Court commands all nine votes on both holdings. Its author and complete joins are stated in Opinion Topology; each holding is directly supported at the stated level of generality.

### 1. Equal treatment depends on the educational work publicly purchased

**Holding and operative rule:** Equal protection does not require a State to purchase integrated devotional instruction when its educational program purchases eligible secular work. Religious identity alone cannot disqualify an independently identifiable, reliably separated secular service that meets lawful neutral program requirements and effective use controls; the parents' request for ordinary St. Dominic's tuition to obtain integrated Catholic instruction falls on the devotional-purchase side of that distinction.

**Controlling explanation:** Agostini requires the Court to identify the government's actual function. Strout applies that inquiry to ordinary integrated tuition at St. Dominic's, while Zelman and McCallum establish that parental choice and payment routing do not change the function a restricted payment buys. The parents identify Catholic instruction and viewpoint as the need the public school does not meet. Their requested tuition purchases that integrated instructional package.

The equality objection is substantial when religious affiliation excludes otherwise eligible secular work. Columbia Union and K.D.M. protect participation on equal terms in such work, and the present rule preserves that protection. Here the financed services differ in the constitutionally material respect established by the governing cases. The program's support for private secular alternatives therefore does not require the requested devotional purchase. The educational-needs condition, school-approval requirements and sectarian exclusion remain distinct; the Court decides the actual funding claim rather than presuming an additional denial under an unapplied neutral criterion.

Strout resolved its granted Religion Clause questions. Its rule supplies substantive authority here; it did not previously adjudicate these different parents' equality claim. Locke's permission concerning an individual ministry-degree scholarship likewise cannot become a general license to exclude religious institutions. These distinctions answer the parents' equal-access argument while retaining equal eligibility for genuinely controlled secular services.

### 2. A close relationship does not replace a genuine obstacle to the school's own suit

**Holding and operative rule:** Parents seeking to assert a school's separate constitutional rights must establish a genuine hindrance to the school's own assertion, in addition to their own injury and a sufficiently close relationship. These parents have an actual relationship with St. Dominic's, but have not established the independent obstacle required to litigate its institutional claim.

**Controlling explanation:** Kowalski requires examination of the relationship and obstacle as separate prerequisites. Its hypothetical future clients differ from the identified school here. That difference satisfies neither the missing obstacle nor a general entitlement to litigate another legal person's claims. The parents' purchase of the school's services and the financial consequences of the exclusion explain their personal controversy, which the Court has decided. They do not show why the school cannot present its own rights. The institutional claim is therefore rejected at the representative-standing threshold.

## Precedent Treatment

- Agostini v. Felton supplies the unified governmental-act-and-function inquiry; the decision applies that inquiry to the public purchase actually requested.
- Strout v. Albanese supplies the integrated-tuition rule, applied to a distinct equal-protection claim rather than treated as an earlier adjudication of that claim.
- Zelman v. Simmons-Harris and Freedom From Religion Foundation v. McCallum establish that neutral selection and private routing do not alter the devotional function bought by a restricted public payment; both retain their stated boundaries.
- Columbia Union College v. Oliver and K.D.M. v. Reorganized School District No. 7 preserve equal eligibility for genuine secular services under their separation, accountability and service-specific requirements.
- Locke v. Davey retains its bounded ministry-training holding in the individual-scholarship setting.
- Kowalski v. Tesmer is applied to the independent requirements for third-party assertion, with its absent-client circumstance distinguished from the existing relationship here.

## Law After Decision

The existing financed-function framework now supplies the direct answer to the equality theory presented by an ordinary integrated devotional-tuition request. Eligible controlled secular work remains open to religious providers on equal terms. The third-party-standing rule continues to require a supported institutional hindrance even when the claimant has a close existing relationship with the rights holder.

## Procedure After Action

The affirmed judgment ends this action against the parents' presented funding claim and their attempted assertion of the school's independent rights. Maine may continue refusing this requested integrated-tuition payment under the governing function distinction.

# Mid-Con Freight Systems, Inc. v. Michigan Public Service Commission, No. 03-1234.
Source: ../records/mid-con-freight-systems-v-michigan-public-service-commission_merits_2005-06-20.md

## Event

Mid-Con Freight Systems, Inc. v. Michigan Public Service Commission, No. 03-1234.

Argued April 26, 2005; decided June 20, 2005. On review of the Michigan Court of Appeals, which sustained the annual $100 charge under Michigan Compiled Laws §478.2(2) for Michigan-plated trucks operating entirely interstate. The question is whether the federal Single State Registration System preempts that separate charge under 49 U.S.C. §14504.

The State requires vehicle-identifying information and a decal for the separate program and appears to waive its otherwise available $10 federal-system fee for covered carriers choosing Michigan as their registration State. The carriers seek invalidation and relief from collection. Michigan contends that its interstate and intrastate $100 provisions together form a general neutral program; the carriers dispute that characterization. The distinct local-haul fee and constitutional challenge are presented in American Trucking Associations.

## Participation

Chief Justice Stone-Zsela and Justices Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer participated.

## Public Action

The judgment of the Michigan Court of Appeals is affirmed.

## Judgment & Remedy

**Affirmed, 7–2.**

**Supporting:** Stone-Zsela, Stevens, Scalia, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer.

**Opposing:** O'Connor and Kennedy, who would vacate and remand for consideration of the combined state scheme under their interstate-status construction.

The carriers' requested invalidation and refund relief on the section 14504 ground are denied. Michigan's separate section 478.2(2) charge remains enforceable against this statutory challenge.

## Opinion Topology

SCALIA, J., delivered the opinion of the Court, in which Stone-Zsela, Stevens, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg, Breyer joined. KENNEDY, J., filed a dissenting opinion, in which O'CONNOR, J., joined.

| Writing | Author | Joined by | Relationship to judgment | Scope joined |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Opinion of the Court | Scalia | Stone-Zsela, Stevens, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg, Breyer | Supports affirmance | Complete defined-subject construction, application and remedy |
| Dissent | Kennedy | O'Connor | Would vacate and remand | Interstate-status construction, general-neutral qualification, independent federal-system limits and combined-scheme characterization remand |

## Holdings

**Holding and operative rule:** Section 14504 preempts additional state registration obligations concerning the federal system's specified subjects—evidence of federal operating authority, insurance, a service agent and associated registration fees—when those obligations exceed the federal standards. It does not displace a genuinely separate state regulatory obligation on another subject merely because it applies to interstate carriers; Michigan's section 478.2(2) charge falls outside the covered subjects on this record.

**Authority:** The signed opinion of the Court, with the seven complete joins stated in Opinion Topology, directly controls the rule, application and remedy.

**Controlling explanation:** Both sentences of section 14504(b) measure registration against the Secretary's standards. Subsection (a) defines those standards by their forms-and-procedures function, and subsection (c) identifies the federal-registration system. The repeated registration language concerns that defined subject. The excess-obligation prohibition therefore protects the federal system against additional covered demands without absorbing every state carrier-registration program.

The competing interstate-status reading would treat every registration requirement triggered by interstate operation as covered, while preserving general neutral registration. That distinction is narrower than a universal fee cap, but the statute identifies the transportation governed by the federal system; it does not say that an obligation on a different subject becomes covered whenever interstate operation triggers it. Dividing the work between subsections (b) and (c) cannot overcome their common reference to the defined system. The replacement system's centralization of relevant filings and fees confirms the enacted structure. Yellow Transportation enforces the benchmark within that system and preserves unrelated taxes and licensing policies.

Michigan's separate program seeks vehicle information and a fee rather than the listed federal permit, insurance or service-agent filings. The apparent $10 waiver creates an administrative connection but does not establish that payment of the $100 is necessary to complete federal registration. A fee's label, age or regulatory purpose alone would not establish independence; the actual obligation supplies the distinction here. Because general neutrality is not the adopted test, the dispute over the combined coverage of Michigan's two provisions does not require a characterization remand under this rule.

**Operative qualifications:** Covered federal-registration demands remain subject to the federal standards despite a different state label. The insurance-proof fee system retains its November 15, 1991 benchmark and $10 ceiling. The prohibition on charging for filing and maintaining evidence of federal registration is distinct. The vehicle-identification restriction governs what the Secretary's standards may require, and the separate inconsistent-fee provision concerns fees under the federal system. These commands do not create a general $10 ceiling for state carrier regulation.

## Precedent Treatment

Yellow Transportation, Inc. v. Michigan is applied for its enforcement of the delegated registration benchmark, qualifying reciprocity and express system-specific boundary. The present decision resolves which subject section 14504 governs; Yellow's separate fee-calculation holding remains in force.

## Law After Decision

The federal registration ceiling governs demands within the Single State Registration System's specified subjects. Courts must identify the actual information, payment and conditions a state program imposes. A separate program on a different subject remains outside that ceiling, while relabeling an additional covered demand cannot evade it. Michigan's challenged fee satisfies that distinction on the present record.

## Separate Writings

**Kennedy, joined by O'Connor, dissenting.** The dissent reads subsection (b) to prohibit additional registration obligations imposed because a carrier conducts interstate transportation, while preserving general neutral registration that only incidentally covers interstate carriers. Subsection (a) defines the permitted federal standards, rather than every additional demand subsection (b) forbids. On that reading, a State cannot avoid the ceiling by asking for different information or spending the fee on safety administration. The information, decal and payment constitute registration even though they serve a regulatory purpose.

The Court's defined-subject construction, in the dissent's view, adds an exemption the excess-obligation sentence does not contain. Applicable subsection (c) commands still independently protect the specified federal demands; the general-neutral qualification does not permit excess covered fees. Yellow's enforcement of the federal benchmark leaves this scope question open.

The dissent would vacate and remand rather than invalidate the fee outright. Michigan argues that sections 478.2(1) and (2) together impose one neutral $100 requirement on Michigan-plated for-hire vehicles; the carriers dispute that account. The lower court's regulatory-purpose rationale did not resolve that characterization under the dissent's rule. The required inquiry concerns the combined scheme's operation, rather than an assumed condition linking the $100 payment to federal registration.

## Procedure After Action

The affirmed judgment concludes this section 14504 challenge. Michigan may continue collecting the reviewed separate fee, subject to the federal requirements governing any actual covered registration demands.

## Source Notes

Mid-Con Freight Systems, Inc. v. Michigan Public Service Commission, 545 U.S. 440; 49 U.S.C. §14504(a)–(c); Mich. Comp. Laws §§478.2(1), (2). The combined-scheme characterization is disputed. The record does not establish that payment of the $100 is required to complete federal registration.

# Exxon Mobil Corp. v. Allapattah Services, Inc., No. 04-70; del Rosario Ortega v. Star-Kist Foods, Inc., No. 04-79.
Source: ../records/exxon-mobil-v-allapattah-services_merits_2005-06-23.md

## Event

Exxon Mobil Corp. v. Allapattah Services, Inc., No. 04-70, and del Rosario Ortega v. Star-Kist Foods, Inc., No. 04-79.

Argued March 1, 2005; decided June 23, 2005. The Eleventh Circuit sustained supplemental jurisdiction over smaller dealer claims in a Rule 23 class action after a jury verdict. The First Circuit restored an injured child's qualifying diversity claim but excluded the related claims of her relatives, joined under Rule 20, because each fell below the amount requirement. The question in both cases is whether 28 U.S.C. §1367 authorizes those smaller related claims when a named plaintiff presents an independently sufficient claim and the other original-jurisdiction requirements are met.

## Participation

Chief Justice Stone-Zsela and Justices Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer participated.

## Public Action

The Eleventh Circuit judgment in No. 04-70 is affirmed. The First Circuit judgment in No. 04-79 is reversed insofar as it excludes the related family claims solely for their individual amount deficiencies, and that case is remanded.

## Judgment & Remedy

| Component | Judgment and relief | Vote |
|---|---|---|
| No. 04-70: smaller dealer class claims | Affirm the supplemental-jurisdiction ruling; the claims may proceed subject to section 1367 and Rule 23. | 5–4 |
| No. 04-79: joined relatives' claims | Reverse their exclusion solely for deficient individual amounts and remand under section 1367 and Rule 20; the child's qualifying claim remains in place. | 5–4 |

**Supporting both dispositions:** Stone-Zsela, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter and Thomas.

**Opposing both dispositions:** Stevens, O'Connor, Ginsburg and Breyer, who would reverse the dealer jurisdictional ruling and affirm the relatives' exclusion.

## Opinion Topology

THOMAS, J., delivered the opinion of the Court, in which Stone-Zsela, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter joined. GINSBURG, J., filed a dissenting opinion, in which STEVENS, O'CONNOR and BREYER, JJ., joined. STEVENS, J., filed a dissenting opinion, in which BREYER, J., joined.

| Writing | Author | Joined by | Relationship to judgment | Scope joined |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Opinion of the Court | Thomas | Stone-Zsela, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter | Supports both judgments | Complete statutory rule, exceptions, application and remedies |
| Dissent | Ginsburg | Stevens, O'Connor, Breyer | Opposes both judgments | Original-action prerequisite and preservation of the separate-amount rules |
| Additional dissent | Stevens | Breyer | Opposes both judgments | Legislative evidence corroborating the contextual construction |

## Holdings

**Holding and operative rule:** When at least one named plaintiff satisfies the diversity amount requirement and the action otherwise meets original-jurisdiction requirements, section 1367(a) authorizes additional claims forming the same Article III case, including claims by additional parties, unless an enacted exception excludes them. Rule 23 class membership and the Rule 20 plaintiff joinder presented here create no such exclusion; the related smaller claims may proceed subject to the statute and applicable procedural rules.

**Authority:** The five Justices joining the complete Opinion of the Court control both docket components and every proposition below.

**Controlling explanation:** The qualifying named claim supplies an action within original jurisdiction. The amount of another plaintiff's claim does not eliminate that independently sufficient claim. Subsection (a) then expressly reaches related claims involving additional parties. Requiring independent jurisdiction over every such claim would defeat the grant's work. City of Chicago applies this distinction between original power, related-claim authority and discretion.

Subsection (b) identifies particular diversity exclusions. It distinguishes claims against persons brought into the case under designated rules from claims by particular added plaintiffs. Congress omitted Rule 23 members and Rule 20 plaintiffs from the latter categories. The Court cannot insert those exclusions to preserve every feature of the earlier amount regime. Finley required legislative authorization for additional-party jurisdiction; Congress supplied it. Zahn and Clark therefore yield for the claims this grant covers.

The competing original-action reading treats every amount deficiency as destructive of the entire action. That approach fails to give the additional-claims language its enacted effect. Complete diversity remains independently necessary: Grupo Dataflux preserves the citizenship requirement, while Caterpillar addresses an actual cure through dismissal of a party. Neither makes a smaller related claim destroy an otherwise sufficient diversity action. Asserted legislative expectations cannot add exclusions absent from the enacted structure.

The dealer and family claims present the same statutory problem. Each must have the required relationship to the qualifying claim and satisfy the applicable procedural rules. Section 1367(c) permits lawful declination after authority is established; it does not make retention automatic.

**Operative qualifications:** The rule preserves complete diversity and requires an independently qualifying original claim; separate claims cannot be aggregated to manufacture that claim. In diversity-only actions, subsection (b) excludes claims by plaintiffs against persons made parties under Rules 14, 19, 20 or 24, and claims by persons proposed as plaintiffs under Rule 19 or seeking to intervene as plaintiffs under Rule 24, when exercising jurisdiction would be inconsistent with section 1332. Subsection (c) permits declination for a novel or complex state-law issue, substantial predominance over the original-jurisdiction claims, dismissal of all claims supplying original jurisdiction, or exceptional circumstances presenting other compelling reasons. Discretion to decline cannot supply absent statutory power.

## Precedent Treatment

- Finley v. United States required statutory authorization for the additional-party jurisdiction it rejected; section 1367 supplies that authorization within its terms and supersedes the contrary result at that scope.
- Zahn v. International Paper Co. no longer requires every class member to satisfy the amount requirement when section 1367 covers the related claim.
- Clark v. Paul Gray, Inc. no longer requires a separate sufficient amount for each joined plaintiff whose claim falls within the supplemental grant.
- City of Chicago v. International College of Surgeons is applied for the separate inquiries into original jurisdiction, related-claim authority and discretionary retention.
- Grupo Dataflux v. Atlas Global Group retains its citizenship rule; an insufficient amount on a related claim is a distinct statutory issue.
- Caterpillar Inc. v. Lewis retains its rule concerning an actual party dismissal that cures the citizenship defect before trial and judgment.

## Law After Decision

Federal courts must identify an independently sufficient original claim, verify citizenship and same-case relatedness, apply subsection (b)'s actual exclusions and then consider any proper subsection (c) ground for declining jurisdiction. An otherwise covered class member or Rule 20 plaintiff may litigate a smaller related claim under that sequence. The pre-enactment separate-amount rules yield only to the extent the later grant reaches the claim.

## Separate Writings

**Ginsburg, joined by Stevens, O'Connor and Breyer, dissenting.** Section 1367 begins with an action already within original jurisdiction. The dissent reads that phrase to incorporate section 1332's settled amount requirements, including Zahn for class members and Clark for joined plaintiffs. Citizenship and amount are both statutory prerequisites; neither may be dispensed with merely because another claim independently qualifies.

The additional-party sentence still performs substantial work under this reading by changing Finley's restriction once an adequate original action exists. Subsection (b) prevents specified attempts to circumvent diversity limits through supplementary additions. Its omission of Rule 23 members and Rule 20 plaintiffs does not itself change the antecedent meaning of original jurisdiction. The dissent acknowledges the force of the broad grant and the omitted categories, but reads the statute as a whole against the established jurisdictional background. It would exclude the smaller dealer claims and sustain exclusion of the relatives' claims.

**Stevens, joined by Breyer, dissenting.** Reliable legislative evidence may corroborate the contextual reading even when the opposing interpretation claims textual clarity. The relevant House report described restoration of pre-Finley supplemental practice and preservation of diversity-only class-action requirements. An earlier study's discussion of changing Zahn does not establish that Congress enacted that different policy. This explanation supports the common dissent's interpretation; it treats legislative material as evidence of meaning rather than an independently enacted jurisdictional rule.

## Procedure After Action

The dealer litigation proceeds under the affirmed jurisdictional ruling. The family case returns for consideration of the restored related claims alongside the child's qualifying claim. The lower courts apply the governing joinder rules, resolve any preserved original-jurisdiction and relatedness issues, consider any proper subsection (c) request and conduct the remaining merits proceedings.

Exxon Mobil Corp. v. Allapattah Services, Inc., 545 U.S. 546; 28 U.S.C. §§1332, 1367; Federal Rules of Civil Procedure 20 and 23. The jurisdictional issue assumes the qualifying named claim, complete diversity and same-case relationship identified in the consolidated disputes. Legislative-history references describe the report passages recited in the judicial opinions.

# Green Party of New York State v. New York State Board of Elections; 389 F.3d 411 (Second Circuit).
Source: ../records/green-party-v-new-york-state-board-of-elections_merits_2005-06-23.md

## Event

Green Party of New York State v. New York State Board of Elections; review of 389 F.3d 411 (Second Circuit).

Merits decision, June 23, 2005. The Second Circuit's November 17, 2004 judgment affirmed preliminary injunctions issued in May and September 2003. The question is whether New York may erase affiliations and exclude future enrollment for organizations that lack statutory party status but demonstrated sufficient support to place statewide candidates on the 2002 ballot.

New York conditions full party status on 50,000 gubernatorial votes. The Green, Liberal and Right to Life organizations lost that status; the Libertarian and Marijuana Reform organizations had not held it. All five nevertheless qualified statewide candidates. The official lists serve organization, contact, mobilization and fundraising as well as primary administration. The plaintiffs challenge the resulting enrollment exclusion and seek continued interim protection. The State seeks dissolution of that protection.

## Participation

Chief Justice Stone-Zsela and Justices Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer participated.

## Public Action

The preliminary protection is affirmed, and the case is remanded for continued proceedings and determination of effective remedial scope.

## Judgment & Remedy

**Affirmed and remanded, 7–2.**

**Supporting:** Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Kennedy, Souter, Ginsburg and Breyer.
**Opposing:** Scalia and Thomas.

The existing injunction remains operative. The district court must apply the common-policy remedy rule to qualifying organizations and voters, determine any supported narrower cure, and continue the merits proceedings. Recording affiliation does not confer primary eligibility or full statutory party status.

## Opinion Topology

SOUTER, J., delivered the opinion of the Court, in which STEVENS, O'CONNOR, KENNEDY, GINSBURG and BREYER, JJ., joined. STONE-ZSELA, C. J., joined Parts II and III and filed an opinion concurring in part and concurring in the judgment. SCALIA, J., filed a dissenting opinion, in which THOMAS, J., joined.

## Holdings

### I. The substantial enrollment burden lacks the required regulatory fit

**Holding and operative rule:** When a State maintains official affiliation information for political organization as well as primary administration, erasing and refusing the affiliations of organizations with demonstrated statewide ballot support imposes a substantial associational burden. Under the calibrated Anderson–Burdick inquiry, an important state objective must have a demonstrated substantial relationship to that exclusion; the preliminary findings establish a substantial likelihood that New York's categorical exclusion fails that requirement.

**Authority:** Part I receives six direct joins: Stevens, O'Connor, Kennedy, Souter, Ginsburg and Breyer. Its substantial-burden classification and application control without combining them with the Chief Justice's severe-burden rationale.

**Controlling explanation:** Burdick requires examination of the actual burden within the electoral system. Cartwright gives independent work to a substantial burden: serious injury need not make political success impossible, but it requires a substantial connection between the restriction and an important objective. The organizations retain nomination, campaigning and other political activity. Their exclusion from a useful continuing official channel nevertheless causes concrete injury to identification, communication and organization.

Norman requires support rules to fit the electoral function at issue, and Timmons requires a real relationship between an associational restriction and the asserted stability interest. A threshold governing primary and statutory party status does not by itself justify destroying or refusing affiliation information. Accurate instructions can distinguish recorded association from primary eligibility.

The State and dissent regard enrollment as a limited, publicly financed primary service whose incidental organizing value need not be extended. That description overlooks how the existing system operates. The State supplies continuing nonprimary advantages to established parties while removing them from organizations with demonstrated statewide support. The challenged erasure contributes insufficiently to administering primaries or avoiding confusion. The same actual-burden-and-fit inquiry resolves the overlapping First and Fourteenth Amendment claims; unequal treatment does not automatically establish severe burden.

### II. Ongoing associational loss warrants the existing interim protection

**Holding and operative rule:** The continuing loss of identification and communication with willing affiliates establishes irreparable associational injury, and the preliminary findings satisfy the substantial-likelihood requirement applied to this affirmative injunction. Effective interim protection must preserve existing affiliations and permit new affiliations for the qualifying organizations while the merits proceed.

**Authority:** Part II has seven direct joins: the six Justices joining Part I and Stone-Zsela. Agreement concerns the need and scope of interim protection, with the Chief Justice relying on his separately stated merits ground.

**Controlling explanation:** A static copy of former lists cannot identify new voters or preserve a continuing channel of political organization. The preliminary record establishes both losses. Preserving only old data would therefore leave part of the continuing injury intact. The findings meet the substantial-likelihood standard the lower court used for affirmative relief. This interlocutory determination preserves effective association during litigation; final merits proceedings remain necessary under the same governing constitutional standard.

### III. Implementation must cure the common exclusion for the qualified category

**Holding and operative rule:** Interim relief retains the Other write-in mechanism with accurate primary-eligibility instructions, continued maintenance and updating for organizations satisfying the established support criterion, best efforts to notify already disenrolled Liberal and Right to Life voters of reenrollment, and equal-form access to Green Party enrollment data. The decree must cure the policy for organizations and voters subject to it who satisfy the support condition, unless the State shows that narrower relief fully cures the same defect; an equally effective administrative arrangement remains permissible under supervision.

**Authority:** The same seven Justices directly join Part III.

**Controlling explanation:** Clingman requires a remedy for the unlawful common policy rather than a limitation justified solely by naming particular plaintiffs. The district court must apply the objective qualification requirement and determine an administrable scope. Continuing data maintenance applies to organizations with sufficient support to place a statewide candidate on the ballot at the most recent gubernatorial election. The form may permit a voter to name any organization without requiring continuing maintenance for every name supplied.

The separate notice and data commands perform distinct work. Best-efforts notice gives already disenrolled Liberal and Right to Life voters an opportunity to reenroll using the new form. The Green Party data order bars publication of its voters as unenrolled and requires the same enrollment data in the same form supplied to statutory parties. Equivalent implementation may replace particular mechanics if it preserves those functions. Existing protection continues while implementation and scope are settled. Separate primary eligibility and full-party requirements remain operative.

## Precedent Treatment

- Burdick v. Takushi supplies the actual-burden inquiry within the complete electoral system.
- Cartwright v. Barnes supplies the substantial-burden requirement of an important objective and a demonstrated substantial relationship to the restriction.
- Norman v. Reed is applied to the connection between a support requirement and the particular electoral function.
- Timmons v. Twin Cities Area New Party is applied to require an actual connection between a restriction and claimed electoral stability.
- Clingman v. Beaver supplies the common-policy remedy rule and the State's opportunity to show a fully effective narrower cure.
- Belitskus v. Pizzingrilli reinforces examination of actual access burdens; its inability-to-pay application remains distinct.

## Law After Decision

Qualifying political organizations receive protection against the selective loss of ongoing official affiliation information under the substantial-burden standard. States retain objective support criteria and primary-eligibility rules, but must justify the different enrollment restriction by its actual function. The injunction continues while the district court resolves final merits and applies the governing qualification and common-policy remedy requirements.

## Separate Writings

**Chief Justice Stone-Zsela, concurring in part and concurring in the judgment.** The Chief Justice regards the demonstrated loss of affiliation records, organizing opportunities and equal access as severe. Burdick's severe-burden rule therefore requires a narrowly drawn restriction serving a compelling interest. The organizations' continuing ability to campaign does not adequately measure the injury to this established channel of political identity and organization. Erasure does not administer the independent primary threshold, and clear instructions address confusion without destroying the association. He would sustain the injunction under that stricter standard.

He joins the Court's interim-protection, functional-implementation and common-policy remedy holdings. To make that relief effective, he would require concrete form, record and publication compliance dates tied to the next registration cycle, immediate preservation of existing data, and reports showing correction. Any alternative administration must provide equivalent protection, and any narrower relief must fully cure the common injury for the objectively qualified category.

**Justice Scalia, dissenting, joined by Justice Thomas.** The State may define official enrollment as part of an administrable primary system. The dissent accepts that political affiliation is protected and that the lists have real organizing value. It nonetheless regards the added benefits of a publicly financed system as an ordinary burden rather than the substantial restriction the Court finds. The organizations remain able to nominate, campaign and associate, and the State may link its enrollment service to the parties whose primaries it administers.

Burdick permits reasonable regulatory administration and does not require the State to await actual confusion or disruption. The dissent acknowledges that these organizations have demonstrated statewide support and that the challenged erasure removes useful information. Those facts do not, in its view, require continuing state recognition and data services beyond the defined primary structure. It would reverse the interlocutory judgment and dissolve the injunction.

## Procedure After Action

The case returns for continued merits proceedings, actual qualification determinations where needed, and a decree implementing the common-policy standard. Current record preservation, enrollment, notice and data-access protection remains in effect during that work.

## Source Notes

Green Party of New York State v. New York State Board of Elections, 389 F.3d 411 (2d Cir. 2004), No. 03-7679; U.S. Const. amends. I, XIV. The May 30 and September 18, 2003 injunctions govern the distinct enrollment, notice and data functions described above.

# Halbert v. Michigan, No. 03-10198.

## Event

Halbert v. Michigan, No. 03-10198. Argued April 25, 2005; decided June 23, 2005. On writ of certiorari to the Michigan Court of Appeals, which denied Halbert's uncounseled application for leave to appeal for lack of merit after appointment was refused. The Michigan Supreme Court declined review.

The questions are whether an indigent defendant convicted on a guilty or no-contest plea is entitled to appointed counsel for this first appellate merits examination, and whether Halbert knowingly waived that assistance. He pleaded no contest to two criminal-sexual-conduct counts, twice requested appellate counsel, and identified sentencing and representation issues. He seeks restoration of the first-tier application with assistance; Michigan defends both its selective-review structure and an asserted waiver.

## Participation

Chief Justice Stone-Zsela and Justices Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer participated.

## Public Action

The judgment denying the uncounseled application is vacated, and the case is remanded for adequate first-tier consideration with appointed assistance.

## Judgment & Remedy

**Vacated and remanded, 7–2.**

**Supporting:** Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Kennedy, Souter, Ginsburg and Breyer.
**Opposing:** Scalia and Thomas.

Michigan must appoint qualified counsel, provide access to the relevant record and restore a meaningful opportunity to prepare and submit the first-tier application under a renewed filing period. Counsel must be supplied before that period runs. The earlier pro se merits denial cannot serve as the completed review. Halbert's conviction remains in force during restored review.

## Opinion Topology

O'CONNOR, J., delivered the opinion of the Court, in which STONE-ZSELA, C. J., and STEVENS, KENNEDY, SOUTER, GINSBURG and BREYER, JJ., joined. THOMAS, J., filed a dissenting opinion, in which SCALIA, J., joined.

## Holdings

The seven Justices joining the Court's opinion directly support all three holdings at the stated level of generality.

### I. Counsel must assist the initial appellate merits examination

**Holding and operative rule:** The Fourteenth Amendment requires appointed advocacy for an indigent plea-convicted defendant seeking initial appellate examination of alleged error in the Michigan Court of Appeals. A State may use a genuinely equivalent advocate-assisted screening mechanism and valid frivolousness procedures, but must supply the legal-assistance function before deciding the initial application.

**Controlling explanation:** Douglas protects fair access to initial merits review. Ross permits later discretionary selection after the defendant has received counseled appellate consideration and the materials that consideration produces. Halbert has received no such first presentation. Michigan's intermediate court examines the merits of the claimed error; the leave label cannot replace attention to that function.

Smith v. Robbins requires an appellate advocate's actual legal-screening contribution while allowing equivalent formats. A plea narrows the available claims but leaves potentially difficult questions concerning sentence, plea and representation. Post-grant counsel comes after the initial screening, and an application form cannot identify and present legal issues as an advocate can. Resource conservation and the narrower range of surviving issues therefore do not justify the missing first-review function.

Michigan's statute provides meaningful but bounded exceptions. Appointment is mandatory for an indigent plea defendant when the prosecutor seeks leave, the sentence exceeds the upper limit of the minimum applicable guideline range, an appellate court grants leave, or the defendant challenges a conditional plea under Rule 6.301(C)(2) or its successor. Appointment is discretionary when all three scoring conditions hold: an alleged offense-variable or prior-record-variable scoring error, objection or other preservation, and an upward departure above the upper limit of the minimum range the defendant contends should have applied. Those routes do not supply the assistance denied here. The constitutional protection follows the initial merits function rather than those exceptions alone.

### II. The whole reported plea record does not establish surrender of this distinct assistance

**Holding and operative rule:** A guilty or no-contest plea does not itself waive counsel for the first appellate review that remains available. The State must establish a knowing, intelligent and voluntary surrender of that distinct assistance from the actual record; Halbert's reported advice, confirmations and general plea finding do not establish that surrender.

**Controlling explanation:** Godinez distinguishes capacity from actual waiver. Tovar requires real understanding without a prescribed judicial formula. Stumpf permits the whole record, including counsel's actual explanation and the defendant's confirmation, to establish informed assent. These rules require weighing the affirmative evidence rather than invalidating a waiver because particular words are absent.

Halbert confirmed that he understood loss of an appeal as of right and the described mandatory and discretionary appointment categories. The court found his plea understandingly made, voluntary and accurate. Counsel gave the reported affirmative responses concerning other promises or considerations and compliance with the no-contest rule. Those facts support Michigan's contextual argument, but they do not identify an explanation and intentional surrender of the distinct assistance needed to prepare the remaining leave application. Stumpf's counsel representation specifically concerned the essential elements whose understanding was disputed; the general confirmations here concern different subjects. The content of any additional promise is unspecified.

The statute commands advice about the ordinary appointment consequence, a determination of understanding and provision of a nontechnical application form. That command is not evidence that every unreported act occurred or a substitute for constitutional waiver. Halbert's later requests and reported learning limitations bear on the inquiry but neither make a valid waiver revocable nor alone prove incapacity. On the combined record, the State has not established the particular informed surrender it invokes.

### III. Restoration supplies the missing first-review function

**Holding and operative rule:** When required first-tier appellate advocacy was wholly absent and was not validly waived, the defendant must receive renewed adequate consideration with appointed assistance without first proving that an omitted argument probably would prevail. Halbert must receive counsel, relevant-record access and a meaningful renewed application period, subject to ordinary preservation and constitutionally adequate frivolousness screening.

**Controlling explanation:** Robbins restores a missing process whose value lies in finding and presenting potentially arguable issues. Requiring an uncounseled defendant to demonstrate a winning omitted claim would demand the very professional work withheld. The state court must therefore permit an assisted application before treating the first review as complete. Valid screening may conclude that no arguable issue exists; it must include the required advocate function.

## Precedent Treatment

- Douglas v. California is applied to initial appellate error correction and extended expressly to Michigan's first-tier leave process.
- Ross v. Moffitt is distinguished by the earlier counseled presentation and later selective function on which its ruling rests.
- Smith v. Robbins supplies the actual advocate-assisted screening requirement, equivalent-format permission and restoration remedy for a wholly absent function.
- Godinez v. Moran preserves the independent inquiry into actual knowing, intelligent and voluntary waiver.
- Iowa v. Tovar permits flexible proof of understanding without mandatory ritual warnings.
- Bradshaw v. Stumpf is applied as a whole-record rule; its specific counsel explanation is distinguished from general plea-compliance confirmations.
- Kowalski v. Tesmer retains its third-party relationship holding; Halbert presents his own denial.

## Law After Decision

Indigent plea defendants seeking initial merits review in the Michigan Court of Appeals are entitled to appointed advocacy before the leave determination, subject to valid waiver and adequate frivolousness procedures. The State must establish an actual informed surrender rather than infer it automatically from the plea. A wholly missing, unwaived function requires restored assisted review under Robbins.

## Separate Writings

**Justice Thomas, dissenting, joined by Justice Scalia.** The dissent would sustain the judgment on two independently sufficient grounds. First, Douglas governs an appeal as of right, while Ross permits a State to limit counsel at a genuinely selective stage. Michigan affords a leave application after a plea has narrowed the possible issues and supplies appointment in defined circumstances, including after leave is granted. The absence of an earlier counseled appellate presentation is significant, but the dissent does not regard it as sufficient to extend Douglas to this different procedure. Robbins governs the required assistance once that entitlement exists; it does not itself settle this classification.

Second, assuming an entitlement, the dissent finds an actual waiver from the reported record. Halbert repeatedly confirmed the changed appellate rights and finite appointment categories. Counsel's compliance response and the trial court's understanding-and-voluntariness finding reinforce the inference that he understood and accepted the ordinary absence of appointed assistance. The statutory duty supplies the legal context, rather than proof of unreported advice.

The dissent recognizes that surrender of an appeal as of right differs from surrender of help preparing an available application. It nevertheless reads the cumulative advice and confirmations as sufficiently conveying that residual consequence. Under Tovar and Stumpf, demonstrated understanding may be inferred without an express residual exclusion or a prescribed waiver formula. The subsequent requests do not negate the knowing choice the dissent finds. It would affirm on either ground.

## Procedure After Action

The case returns to Michigan for appointment, relevant-record access and a meaningful renewed filing opportunity before first-tier merits consideration. The renewed process must contain the required legal screening function, with ordinary preservation and valid frivolousness rules.

U.S. Const. amend. XIV; Mich. Comp. Laws §770.3a, enacted by 1999 Mich. Pub. Act 200; Michigan Court Rules 6.301(C)(2) and 6.302. The reported plea colloquy and finding concern the subjects described in the waiver holding; the content of additional promises or advice is unspecified.

# Kelo v. City of New London, No. 04-108.

## Event

Kelo v. City of New London, No. 04-108. Argued February 22, 2005; decided June 23, 2005. On writ of certiorari to the Supreme Court of Connecticut, which sustained the challenged condemnations under federal public-use and state redevelopment law, reversing the trial court's partial relief for some parcels.

The question is whether the Fifth Amendment permits compensated condemnation for an actual comprehensive economic redevelopment plan implemented partly by private parties. New London's Fort Trumbull plan combines commercial, residential and public components in response to prolonged economic distress. The petitioners' homes were not individually blighted. They seek an injunction preventing the acquisitions; the City seeks to proceed with the plan through lawful condemnation and compensation.

## Participation

Chief Justice Stone-Zsela and Justices Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer participated.

## Public Action

The judgment of the Supreme Court of Connecticut is affirmed.

## Judgment & Remedy

**Affirmed, 6–3.**

**Supporting:** Stone-Zsela, Stevens, Kennedy, Souter, Ginsburg and Breyer.
**Opposing:** O'Connor, Scalia and Thomas.

The requested federal public-use injunction is denied. The challenged acquisitions may proceed through the lawful condemnation and compensation process for the adjudicated plan, subject to applicable state law and preserved compensation claims.

## Holdings

### Actual public redevelopment, private implementation and favoritism

**Holding and operative rule:** A compensated condemnation implementing an adopted comprehensive redevelopment plan satisfies public use when it serves a rationally supported actual public purpose rather than a pretext for private favoritism, even though private parties will implement portions and the particular homes are not blighted. Courts must meaningfully examine supported claims that the stated public purpose masks a private favor, using the actual plan, process and beneficiary relationships under the ordinary presumption of lawful public action and ordinary proof rules.

**Authority:** The Court's six-Justice opinion, whose author and complete joins appear in Opinion Topology, directly controls the entire rule and its application.

**Controlling explanation:** Berman permits an integrated redevelopment undertaking and private implementation rather than requiring every parcel to be independently harmful. Midkiff makes the public objective, rather than the mechanics of the transfer, decisive. Boston & Maine applies that rule to private reconveyance while retaining an enforceable prohibition on naked private favoritism.

The adopted plan responds to prolonged economic distress through coordinated commercial, residential and public uses. That established undertaking supplies an actual public objective beyond replacing one owner with another. Individual homes need not be blighted for their inclusion in an otherwise public plan. Forecasts of jobs and revenues support the purpose without becoming guarantees; a court need not establish that every expected benefit will materialize.

The owners and common dissent object that every ordinary successor use can promise greater productivity, making all property vulnerable. The answer lies in the public character of the actual undertaking and the independently enforceable private-favor boundary. Reciting economic benefits cannot validate a sham whose actual object is favoring a private recipient and whose public gains are merely incidental. Supported allegations require genuine record examination. On this record, the established planning purpose supports the condemnations and supplies no unresolved pretext issue requiring remand.

The Constitution does not confine privately implemented public acquisitions to removal of already harmful conditions. Public ownership and enforceable public access are sufficient examples of public use under these precedents, but do not exhaust the public-purpose rule. Just compensation and stricter state restrictions retain their independent force. An isolated transfer devised for a favored recipient is outside the rule sustained here.

## Precedent Treatment

- Berman v. Parker is applied to integrated public redevelopment and private implementation; individual parcel blight is not an indispensable condition.
- Hawaii Housing Authority v. Midkiff is applied to the actual public objective rather than the ultimate titleholder.
- National Railroad Passenger Corp. v. Boston & Maine Corp. is applied to public purpose despite private reconveyance and its surviving prohibition on naked favoritism. Its railroad statutory need presumption does not govern this municipal plan.
- Lingle v. Chevron U.S.A. Inc. retains the distinction between regulatory-taking liability and independent constitutional restraints, including the public-use condition.

## Law After Decision

The public-purpose rule permits the challenged comprehensive redevelopment condemnations with compensation and lawful authority, while requiring meaningful examination of supported private-favor allegations. The operative inquiry concerns the actual undertaking and its public object; economic forecasts need rational support rather than guaranteed success. State law may impose stricter acquisition limits.

## Separate Writings

**Justice Kennedy, concurring.** Kennedy joins the Court's opinion in full. Ordinary deference must still examine a supported allegation that private favoritism supplies the actual object of a taking. The rational-basis inquiries in Cleburne and Moreno illustrate that judicial respect for governmental choices can coexist with a genuine examination of an impermissible object. The plan, decision process and beneficiary relationships matter to that inquiry; merely identifying incidental private gain does not establish invalidity.

The record supports this comprehensive plan under that standard. Kennedy leaves open whether a narrowly drawn, exceptionally suspicious class of transfers could warrant stronger review. He identifies no governing heightened standard or set of triggers for that different category in this case.

**Justice O'Connor, dissenting, joined by Justices Scalia and Thomas.** The dissent would prohibit taking ordinary private property for new private users solely because their normal activities are expected to produce greater economic benefits. Public ownership and actual public service remain valid. Berman addressed a harmful area, and Midkiff a harmful concentration of ownership; the acquisition itself removed the identified condition. The petitioners' ordinary homes present a materially different case.

The precedents' broad public-purpose language favors the City's position, but the dissent would confine that language at this economic-successor boundary. A sincere comprehensive plan does not cure the substantive problem because virtually every private activity yields incidental public gains. Meaningful pretext review addresses bad purpose but leaves the lawful-purpose rule too broad. Compensation and possible state-law protection cannot replace an independent federal public-use limit. The dissent would reverse and remand for relief preventing these condemnations.

**Justice Thomas, dissenting.** Thomas also joins the common dissent as an alternative under existing precedents, while proposing a further change to their constitutional rationale. The public-use condition should require governmental ownership or use, or a legally enforceable entitlement of the public to use the property. Common-carrier property may satisfy that rule despite private title; projected jobs and taxes from ordinary private premises do not.

The constitutional text and structure distinguish public use from the independent duty to compensate. Cole and Kohl illustrate public functions and the private-use prohibition without proving an exhaustive historical catalogue. Thomas would reconsider Berman's and Midkiff's broader public-purpose propositions and incompatible private-transfer permission. He would replace Boston & Maine's broader rationale while recognizing that secured passenger service could satisfy the narrower use rule. His alternative reliance on the common dissent does not amount to approval of those precedents' permanent constitutional correctness.

## Procedure After Action

The Connecticut judgment stands against the federal public-use challenge. The City may undertake the adjudicated acquisitions through applicable condemnation and compensation proceedings. Preserved compensation and independent state-law claims follow their proper procedures.

U.S. Const. amend. V; Kelo v. City of New London, 268 Conn. 1, 843 A.2d 500 (2004). The challenged parcels are included in the adopted Fort Trumbull plan; projected economic benefits are forecasts. The public-use inquiry concerns that plan and its established purpose.

## Event

Mayle v. Felix, No. 04-563. Argued April 19, 2005; decided June 23, 2005. On writ of certiorari to the Ninth Circuit. The granted question concerns relation back under Rule 15(c)(2) of an untimely new habeas ground based on different underlying events but directed at the same trial and conviction.

Felix's timely May 8, 1998 petition presented confrontation, instructional-error and insufficient-evidence grounds. Counsel was appointed May 29. His January 28, 1999 amendment, filed after expiration of the limitation, retained confrontation, omitted the other two grounds, and added coercion and related appellate-assistance theories. The new coercion ground concerned his own pretrial interrogation; the confrontation ground concerned Williams's videotaped testimony. The original petition did not attempt the interrogation factual core. The Ninth Circuit sustained relation back on a trial-and-conviction theory and separately affirmed rejection of the confrontation claim.

## Holdings

### Relation back requires shared operative facts, not merely the same conviction

**Holding and operative rule:** Under Rule 15(c)(2), an amended habeas ground asserted after limitations expire relates back only when it shares a common core of operative facts set forth or attempted in a timely pleading. A new legal theory, additional detail or fair elaboration of an imperfect pro se account may qualify; the same trial, conviction or requested release alone does not supply the factual connection.

**Authority:** All seven Justices joining the Court's opinion directly adopt the rule, its stated qualifications and its application.

**Controlling explanation:** Section 2242 permits civil-rule amendment, but Rule 15(a)'s permission to amend differs from Rule 15(c)(2)'s effect on an expired limitation. The latter provision links the amendment to conduct or events attempted in the original pleading. Habeas Rule 2(c)'s requirement of supporting facts for each ground gives that link practical content because a prosecution can contain many independent alleged wrongs.

Scarborough permits completion of an already identified timely request. Tiller permits a different legal theory concerning the same identified event. Neither makes an entire criminal judgment the pleaded transaction for every later allegation. Courts must read actual factual allegations fairly, including pro se attempts, rather than demand unchanged legal labels or professional drafting.

The contrary trial-occurrence reading emphasizes that both evidentiary challenges concern one trial and that Rule 15 deliberately accommodates limitations. But the State's possession of the prosecution file is not notice that every act in it is challenged. Treating the whole trial as the occurrence would allow almost any new ground while the first action remains pending, making the effective limitation depend on how long that action lasts. Ordinary amendment controls against prejudice and delay do not perform the separate factual-connection work enacted in Rule 15(c)(2).

Felix's allegedly coerced interrogation and the circumstances of Williams's videotaped testimony are distinct episodes. None of the original grounds attempted the interrogation facts. The amended coercion ground therefore does not relate back. The related assistance theory must be handled under the same rule within the proceedings actually before the lower court. The separately resolved confrontation merits lie outside the grant.

**Operative application:** Timely amendments remain available under the applicable rules. Later amendments sharing the pleaded factual core may relate back even when they change the legal theory. Independent commencement, pendency or equitable grounds require their own established predicates; the remand must enforce the common-core rule and adjudicate any actually preserved independent ground on its own basis.

## Precedent Treatment

- Scarborough v. Principi is applied to distinguish completing an identified timely matter from adding a different factual wrong; its fee-specific prerequisites do not become habeas rules.
- Tiller v. Atlantic Coast Line Railroad Co. supports changing a legal theory while retaining the same identified factual occurrence.
- Artuz v. Bennett, Duncan v. Walker and Carey v. Saffold retain their distinct rules governing filing and qualifying state pendency.
- Rhines v. Weber retains bounded authority to preserve an existing timely mixed petition; it does not supply relation back for a new factual ground.
- Pliler v. Ford retains its specific judicial-act, causal-loss, diligence and lawful-remedy requirements.
- Pace v. DiGuglielmo and Dodd v. United States retain their separate state-filing and new-right timing holdings.

## Law After Decision

Post-limitations habeas amendments must be compared with the actual operative events pleaded or attempted on time. Genuine factual elaboration and new legal theories concerning those events may relate back; distinct episodes do not become timely merely because they contributed to the same trial or conviction. The lower court must apply that rule to the added theories within their actual procedural scope and preserve the separate confrontation disposition.

## Separate Writings

**Justice Stevens, dissenting, joined by Justice Souter.** The dissent treats the trial's use of the challenged evidence as the relevant connected occurrence. Rule 15(c) deliberately permits some amendments despite expiration of limitations; its text does not itself require every habeas ground to be a separate transaction. The State received a timely challenge to the trial's validity, and both the videotape and interrogation theories concern evidence introduced there.

The dissent acknowledges the distinct interrogation facts and does not claim that Felix originally pleaded coercion. It instead reads the transaction broadly in this pending attack on one conviction. Section 2242 provides ordinary civil amendment, and Rule 15(a) allows courts to reject abuse, delay or prejudice. The timely original filing and the different regime governing later actions after final judgment provide additional limits. Those safeguards, in its view, answer the Court's finality concern without narrowing occurrence to each constitutional episode.

That reading also preserves the usefulness of counsel appointed after a prisoner's initial filing: later identification of a trial defect need not be excluded simply because an unrepresented petition used a different legal or factual route. The dissent would affirm the relation-back ruling and permit the remaining proceedings under the ordinary amendment rules.

## Procedure After Action

The Ninth Circuit must remove the trial-wide relation-back ground and return the case for proceedings consistent with the common-core requirement. The added coercion claim is time barred absent an independently preserved sufficient basis; the related assistance theory must be handled within its actual procedural scope. The separate confrontation merits judgment remains in force.

The amended assistance theory received no separately identified appellate timeliness ruling.

## Event

Orff v. United States, No. 03-1566. Argued February 23, 2005; decided June 23, 2005. On writ of certiorari to the Ninth Circuit, which rejected the farmers' reclamation-contract action under the asserted waiver and beneficiary theory.

Westlands Water District receives Bureau of Reclamation water under a 1963 contract. Farmers and farming entities buy water from Westlands and claim intended-beneficiary rights. The farmers' surviving action runs directly against the United States and its agents. The question is whether 43 U.S.C. §390uu authorizes that action, independently of the farmers' asserted contract entitlement.

## Holdings

### The statute permits qualifying joinder rather than this stand-alone action

**Holding and operative rule:** Section 390uu permits the United States to be added as a necessary defendant in a qualifying suit adjudicating a contracting entity's and the United States' rights under a federal reclamation contract. It does not itself authorize a stand-alone contract action against the United States and its agents; an asserted benefit under the contract cannot supply the missing procedural condition.

**Authority:** The unanimous Opinion of the Court supplies nine direct joins to the rule and disposition.

**Controlling explanation:** Congress authorized joinder as a necessary party defendant. That language describes adding the sovereign to a qualifying controversy between other parties, rather than creating general consent whenever relief is sought from it. The farmers now pursue a direct federal claim; the districts' earlier participation does not supply a continuing independent nonfederal controversy requiring federal joinder.

The farmers rely on the breadth of the statute's reference to suits adjudicating contractual rights, the second sentence's amenability to judgments and review, and the venue provision. Those words have substantial effect within the consent Congress initially grants. After qualifying joinder, the United States is subject to the specified adjudication and review. The venue sentence identifies a forum for that qualifying suit. Treating each sentence as an independent direct-action waiver would erase the opening procedural condition.

Meyer requires attention to the actual breadth of enacted consent; its broad sue-and-be-sued clause differs from this joinder provision. Blue Fox separates an asserted substantive obligation from consent to the action and requested relief. Idaho likewise distinguishes a defined adjudicatory consent from an additional permission asserted against the Government. These authorities require enforcing Congress's words rather than presuming that every waiver receives the narrowest conceivable construction.

Because the direct action falls outside the asserted waiver, intended-beneficiary entitlement cannot supply jurisdiction. The Court affirms on sovereign consent and leaves that beneficiary question without a merits determination. The sufficient direct-action ground also leaves unresolved whether every qualifying joinder must satisfy all of Rule 19(a), whether section 390uu permits money damages in a suit otherwise within its consent, and whether a noncontracting entity may invoke it in a qualifying joined controversy. Those questions are unnecessary to this threshold disposition; alternative statutory waivers lie outside the granted issue.

## Precedent Treatment

- FDIC v. Meyer is applied to honor the text and actual breadth of consent while keeping waiver distinct from substantive entitlement; its broader agency clause does not supply the different consent asserted here.
- Department of the Army v. Blue Fox, Inc. is applied to require consent for the actual action and relief, independently of the alleged contractual obligation.
- United States v. Idaho is applied to distinguish a specified adjudicatory waiver from an additional asserted governmental obligation.

## Law After Decision

Section 390uu remains available for qualifying necessary-party joinder in reclamation-contract controversies. Its amenability and venue provisions operate within that consent. Farmers asserting contract benefits cannot use it alone to maintain the surviving stand-alone suit against the United States and its agents; substantive beneficiary entitlement remains distinct from the threshold waiver.
