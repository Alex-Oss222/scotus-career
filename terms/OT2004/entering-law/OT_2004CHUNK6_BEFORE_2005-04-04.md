## Source: california-public-employees-retirement-system-v-worldcom-inc_merits_2005-03-30.md

Authority: `../records/california-public-employees-retirement-system-v-worldcom-inc_merits_2005-03-30.md`; effective 2005-03-30.

### Event

California Public Employees’ Retirement System v. WorldCom, Inc. — plenary merits decision, March 30, 2005. On review of the Second Circuit, 368 F.3d 86, No. 04-0219, following full merits submission. The Second Circuit affirmed orders denying remand in an accepted interlocutory appeal under 28 U.S.C. §1292(b). The question is whether bankruptcy-related removal under §1452(a) overrides Securities Act §22(a)’s express protection against removal of the individual Securities Act actions.

### Opinion Topology

### Holdings

### The express nonremoval command governs these individual actions

**Holding and operative rule.** Section 1452(a) does not authorize removal of an individual Securities Act action filed in a state court of competent jurisdiction when §22(a), 15 U.S.C. §77v(a), prohibits its removal. Section 22(a)’s exception concerns §77p(c) removal of covered class actions based on state law that allege a material misstatement or omission, or manipulative or deceptive conduct, in connection with the purchase or sale of a covered security; the present individual actions fall outside that exception.

**Authority.** The Court’s opinion, joined in full by Chief Justice Stone-Zsela and Justices Stevens, O’Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer, supplies the controlling rule and the review-channel holding below.

**Controlling explanation.** Congress’s broad bankruptcy-removal permission and its express prohibition for a defined class of securities cases can operate together. Things Remembered, Inc. v. Petrarca supplies that coexistence principle: bankruptcy procedure does not automatically displace independently applicable statutory limits. Section 1452(a) continues to authorize removal within its field; §22(a) prevents removal of these particular cases. A potential effect on a bankruptcy estate establishes, at most, a jurisdictional predicate rather than permission to disregard the forum command.

The absence from §1452(a) of §1441(a)’s introductory reservation for other Acts does not itself repeal §22(a). The removal provisions already differ in who may remove, what may be removed, and the jurisdictional conditions and exclusions. Section 22(a) addresses removal to any federal court. Its retained prohibition is not confined to §1441. The advantages of centralizing bankruptcy-related litigation cannot supply an exception Congress did not enact.

The statutory protection for actions brought by a State, political subdivision or state pension plan on its own behalf remains operative, as does its protection for an authorized class consisting solely of such named public entities. The entity-counting rule treats a pension plan or other qualifying entity as one person unless formed to participate in the action; its beneficiaries do not by themselves convert its individual case into a covered class action. On the individual-action status presented here, state-court return follows even assuming the asserted contribution, indemnification or reimbursement interests satisfy §1334. The actual sufficiency of the estate connection lies outside the certified statutory question, which assumes that connection.

### The appeal concerns legal removal authority

**Holding and operative rule.** The accepted §1292(b) appeal presents the legal authority to remove these actions. Section 1452(b)’s bar on appellate review of a remand or refusal to remand on equitable grounds does not govern that distinct statutory question.

**Controlling explanation.** Quackenbush v. Allstate Insurance Co. requires attention to the ground of the remand decision and its lawful review channel. Things Remembered preserves the restrictions governing the kinds of remand it addresses. Here the order rejected the asserted statutory prohibition, and the certified appeal concerns that legal ruling. Section 1452(b) expressly protects both equitable remand and equitable refusal; the distinction rests on the ground of decision, not merely whether remand was granted or denied.

### Precedent Treatment

Things Remembered, Inc. v. Petrarca is applied for the coexistence of overlapping statutes and preserves its covered-remand review rule. Connecticut National Bank v. Germain supplies the coexistence principle adopted in Things Remembered; that principle now resolves this distinct statutory overlap. Quackenbush v. Allstate Insurance Co. is applied to distinguish the legal ground and authorized review channel.

### Law After Decision

The nonremoval protection applies throughout the statutory category of covered individual Securities Act cases brought in competent state courts. Establishing bankruptcy-related jurisdiction cannot alone remove that protection. Section 1452(a) retains its separate operation, including its exclusions for Tax Court proceedings and governmental police-or-regulatory enforcement actions and its requirement of §1334 jurisdiction. Original federal Securities Act actions, bankruptcy claims administration and independently applicable bankruptcy stays remain governed by their own statutory provisions.

### Procedure After Action

The mandate returns the matter for entry and implementation of the corresponding state-court remand orders. The district court shall transmit the covered actions to their originating courts and cease merits adjudication of those actions after remand. Any independently applicable bankruptcy stay continues according to its own terms.

## Source: exxon-mobil-corp-v-saudi-basic-industries-corp_merits_2005-03-30.md

### Event

Exxon Mobil Corp. v. Saudi Basic Industries Corp., No. 03-1696. Argued February 23, 2005; decided March 30, 2005. On writ of certiorari to the Third Circuit, 364 F.3d 102. The question is whether entry of judgment in a parallel state action deprives a federal district court of jurisdiction over an independently filed action concerning the same dispute. The Third Circuit dismissed the federal action after the Delaware judgment, while hearing SABIC’s immunity appeal.

### Opinion Topology

KENNEDY, J., delivered the opinion of the Court, in which STONE-ZSELA, C. J., and STEVENS, O'CONNOR, SCALIA, SOUTER, THOMAS, GINSBURG, and BREYER, JJ., joined.

| Writing | Author | Joined by | Relationship to judgment | Scope joined |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Opinion of the Court | Kennedy | Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg, Breyer | Reversal and remand | All propositions |

### Holdings

### A parallel state judgment does not extinguish an independent federal action

**Holding and operative rule.** A federal district court does not lose original jurisdiction merely because a parallel state action reaches judgment while the federal action is pending. The Rooker–Feldman prohibition on district-court appellate review applies when a state-court loser brings a federal action after the state judgment, complains of injury caused by that judgment and seeks its review and rejection; independent claims remain subject to the applicable jurisdictional statutes, §1738 preclusion and properly applicable abstention rules.

**Authority.** The opinion of the Court, joined in full by Chief Justice Stone-Zsela and Justices Stevens, O’Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer, controls the rule and its application.

**Controlling explanation.** Jordahl v. Democratic Party of Virginia distinguishes an injury caused by the asserted legal error of a state judicial order from an independently actionable wrong. It therefore bars a damages claim that would require appellate rejection of an earlier state judgment, while preserving an original claim based on underlying conduct. The present case applies that distinction to the sequence of parallel proceedings.

Exxon filed its federal royalty claims before the Delaware judgment, sought relief for SABIC’s conduct and prevailed in the state trial. The later judgment did not turn that original suit into an appeal. Rooker v. Fidelity Trust Co. and District of Columbia Court of Appeals v. Feldman preserve the appellate boundary reflected in §1257; that allocation does not make original jurisdiction disappear whenever state litigation reaches judgment first. Congress’s express grants of federal review, including habeas review under §2254, retain their own terms.

SABIC’s finality and duplication concerns call for the doctrines that actually govern those concerns. Section 1738 requires the federal court to give the state judgment the preclusive effect it receives under the applicable state law. Colorado River Water Conservation District v. United States supplies the distinct exceptional-circumstances inquiry for parallel-litigation abstention, and Quackenbush v. Allstate Insurance Co. requires an applicable source before surrendering federal adjudication. Overlapping issues, or an independent claim inconsistent with a state court’s legal conclusion, do not alone create an appellate action.

The federal complaint invoked §1330. SABIC’s remaining immunity contentions, the actual effect of the Delaware judgment and any residual mootness issue require adjudication on their own predicates because the court of appeals disposed of the action on the different jurisdictional theory rejected here.

### Precedent Treatment

Jordahl v. Democratic Party of Virginia retains its claim-specific distinction between judgment-caused and independent injury; this decision resolves the pending-action timing question it left open. Rooker v. Fidelity Trust Co. and District of Columbia Court of Appeals v. Feldman retain their prohibition on district-court appellate reassessment of state judgments. Quackenbush v. Allstate Insurance Co. and Colorado River Water Conservation District v. United States preserve the separate sources and conditions of abstention. Marrese v. American Academy of Orthopaedic Surgeons supplies §1738’s direction to examine the State’s preclusion law.

### Law After Decision

The jurisdictional rule governs civil original actions involving parallel state litigation. A state judgment may determine the fate of particular federal claims through preclusion, while the federal action retains its original character. Courts must examine the source of the asserted injury, the relief requested and the commencement sequence instead of treating overlap as jurisdictional extinction. A complaint styled as damages remains subject to the appellate bar when its actual claim seeks rejection of a preexisting state judgment.

### Procedure After Action

The case returns for the remaining immunity appeal and consistent further proceedings. The lower courts must identify each live claim and requested relief, apply the Delaware judgment’s preclusive effect under §1738 and address any genuine jurisdictional or mootness defense. The Delaware judgment remains effective, and any remaining recovery must respect its binding effect and the prohibition on duplicate recovery.

## Source: rhines-v-weber_merits_2005-03-30.md

Authority: `../records/rhines-v-weber_merits_2005-03-30.md`; effective 2005-03-30.

### Event

Rhines v. Weber, No. 03-9046. Argued January 12, 2005; decided March 30, 2005. On writ of certiorari to the Eighth Circuit, 346 F.3d 799. The question is whether a district court may stay a timely habeas petition containing exhausted and unexhausted claims while the prisoner seeks state-court exhaustion, and what limits govern that discretion. The court of appeals vacated the existing stay under its restrictive view of that authority.

### Opinion Topology

STONE-ZSELA, C. J., filed a concurring opinion. GINSBURG and BREYER, JJ., also joined the Court’s opinion in full.

### Holdings

### Retaining a timely mixed petition during exhaustion

**Holding and operative rule.** A district court may stay a timely mixed petition while the prisoner exhausts state remedies if the prisoner shows good cause for not exhausting first, the unexhausted claims are not plainly meritless, and the record does not show abusive litigation or intentional delay. Good cause means a reasonable explanation in the actual circumstances, not an extraordinary-circumstances or procedural-default test; the court must set reasonable, definite deadlines for seeking state relief and returning to federal court, accounting for delays outside the prisoner’s control.

**Authority.** Chief Justice Stone-Zsela and Justices Stevens, O’Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer join the complete framework. Justice Souter joins the existence of stay power and the merit, anti-abuse and deadline limits, but not the initial good-cause prerequisite.

**Controlling explanation.** Rose v. Lundy gives state courts the first opportunity to decide federal claims. Congress’s exhaustion and limitations rules can serve that purpose while a federal court retains a timely filed action. Landis v. North American Co. supplies ordinary control over pending cases, exercised here within the habeas statutes. Duncan v. Walker excludes time spent in federal court from statutory tolling under §2244(d)(2); retention preserves an existing action rather than supplying a new filing period. Pliler v. Ford left this stay question open while establishing separate protections against particular court-caused filing losses.

The warden correctly argues that routine, open-ended stays would weaken finality and reward premature filing. Cause, a potential-merit screen, exclusion of abuse and definite deadlines answer that objection. Good cause preserves an incentive to exhaust before filing and permits an individualized explanation. Its reasonable-explanation meaning also avoids turning technical uncertainty about exhaustion into a second, rigid procedural-default obstacle. Section 2254(b)(2) permits denial of an unexhausted claim on its merits; it does not compel denial. Relief remains subject to exhaustion, absence of an available state corrective process, circumstances making that process ineffective to protect the applicant’s rights, or counsel’s express waiver of exhaustion for the State, as the statute provides.

Rhines filed with more than eleven months remaining before limitations expired during federal processing. The district court identified eight unexhausted claims and imposed sixty-day initiation and return conditions. Those facts establish the practical importance of stay authority. Actual cause, potential merit, delay and the reasonableness of those conditions require findings under the announced standard.

### Protecting timely review when applying that discretion

**Holding and operative rule.** Where good cause, potential merit and absence of intentional delay are established and dismissal would defeat timely federal review, denying a stay and dismissing the entire petition ordinarily abuses discretion; the court should retain the action, and any refusal requires a reason compatible with the statute. If a stay is inappropriate, the prisoner must be allowed to delete unexhausted claims and proceed on exhausted claims when whole-petition dismissal would unreasonably impair federal relief.

**Authority.** All nine Justices join this sufficient case for retention and the deletion protection. Justice Souter’s agreement does not make good cause a necessary condition in his separate view.

**Controlling explanation.** The exhaustion requirement directs claims first to the state courts; it does not require destroying a timely federal action whenever the two processes overlap. The filing date and continued pendency therefore matter independently of statutory tolling. A refusal must apply the governing predicates and account for its actual effect on federal review. If retention is unsuitable, deletion preserves the exhausted claims without demanding adjudication of the others. Pliler’s neutral-warning duty retains its particular triggers—an unrepresented prisoner and a qualifying court-directed dismissal election. Rhines had appointed counsel for his amended federal petition, so that duty does not automatically govern his election.

### Precedent Treatment

Rose v. Lundy’s total-exhaustion requirement remains operative with bounded stay-and-abeyance in the limitations setting. Duncan v. Walker retains its statutory non-tolling rule; this decision answers the distinct stay question. Pliler v. Ford retains its separate notice, causation, diligence and lawful-remedy rules. Landis v. North American Co. supplies ordinary management authority subject to the governing habeas statutes.

### Law After Decision

District courts have a uniform standard for retaining timely mixed petitions while state remedies are pursued. The rule preserves the existing filing and requires prompt exhaustion and return; reopening a terminated action still requires an independently valid procedural ground. The actual stay predicates and reasonable deadlines are matters of application under settled standards, including the deletion protection when whole-petition dismissal would unreasonably impair federal review.

### Procedure After Action

On remand, the lower courts shall assess Rhines’s actual reason for nonexhaustion, the potential merit of the eight claims, any abusive or intentional delay and his state-court progress. They shall evaluate the existing sixty-day conditions and impose reasonable exhaustion-and-return deadlines if a stay remains warranted. If a stay is refused, Rhines must receive a meaningful opportunity to delete the unexhausted claims where wholesale dismissal would unreasonably impair federal relief. Any dismissal must identify its actual effect on timely federal review.

### Event

Smith v. City of Jackson, No. 03-1160. Argued November 3, 2004; decided March 30, 2005. On writ of certiorari to the Fifth Circuit, 351 F.3d 183. The questions are whether the Age Discrimination in Employment Act permits a disparate-impact challenge to a neutral employment practice and whether the City’s compensation plan satisfies the governing statutory rule. The Fifth Circuit affirmed summary judgment on the impact claim because it regarded that theory as categorically unavailable.

### Opinion Topology

O'Connor filed an opinion concurring in the judgment, joined by Kennedy and Thomas.

|---|---|---|---|---|
| Opinion of the Court | Stevens | Stone-Zsela, Souter, Ginsburg, Breyer in full; Scalia except as stated | Affirms on reasonable nonage factors | Six support operative recognition, particular-practice causation, reasonable-factors rule and application; five support independent judicial construction |
| Concurrence | Scalia | — | Joins the stated portions and the judgment | Recognition through the EEOC’s delegated interpretation |
| Concurrence | Stone-Zsela | — | Joins the Court in full | Employer bears the reasonable-factors defense |

### Holdings

### The ADEA permits an identified practice’s discriminatory effects to be challenged

**Holding and operative rule.** Section 623(a)(2) permits employees to challenge an identified neutral employment practice that causes protected-age disadvantage in employment opportunities or status, without proving that age motivated the practice. The employees must identify the particular practice and establish the causal adverse effect; an age imbalance alone does not identify its cause.

**Authority.** Chief Justice Stone-Zsela and Justices Stevens, Scalia, Souter, Ginsburg and Breyer support this operative rule. Chief Justice Stone-Zsela and Justices Stevens, Souter, Ginsburg and Breyer join the Court’s independent statutory construction and explanation below. Justice Scalia reaches recognition through the agency ground stated in his concurrence.

**Controlling explanation.** The subsection addresses limitations, segregation or classifications that adversely affect employment opportunities or employee status. Griggs v. Duke Power Co. supplies the established effects-based reading of materially parallel language. The ADEA’s additional reasonable-factors qualification gives that reading a distinct limit suitable to this statute: an adverse effect associated with age does not make a reasonable nonage differentiation unlawful.

The City and the concurrence in the judgment read the because-of-age language as requiring a motivated age decision in every case, with the reasonable-factors clause merely confirming a safe harbor. That reading does not give the effects-oriented subsection and its express qualification their separate work. The necessary causal connection is an age-linked adverse effect from the identified practice; actual age motivation remains the question for disparate treatment.

Hazen Paper Co. v. Biggins requires age motivation for intentional treatment and recognizes the difference between age and years of service, including deliberate use of service as a proxy. It expressly reserved disparate impact. General Dynamics Land Systems, Inc. v. Cline determines the direction of statutory protection, not whether an effects-based mechanism exists. Wards Cove Packing Co. v. Atonio supplies the requirement to identify the actual challenged practice and connect it to the disparity. The 1991 amendments to Title VII did not amend the ADEA; their different provisions cannot be transposed into this Act. The EEOC’s considered recognition of impact liability supports the statutory reading without supplying the Court’s independent ground.

### Reasonable nonage factors sustain this compensation plan

**Holding and operative rule.** Under §623(f)(1), a differentiation based on reasonable factors other than age is outside disparate-impact liability. Reasonableness concerns the actual nonage factor and its relationship to the employment objective; it does not require business necessity or selection of the least disparate workable alternative. On this record, rank, tenure, existing salary and adjustment toward regional compensation levels supply reasonable nonage factors even assuming that the challenged allocation caused the reported age disparity.

**Authority.** Chief Justice Stone-Zsela and Justices Stevens, Scalia, Souter, Ginsburg and Breyer join this statutory standard and its application in the Court’s opinion.

**Controlling explanation.** Age can correlate with experience, service and compensation without making those characteristics age itself. Congress expressly preserved reasonable nonage differentiation. Griggs supplies the effects analogy, but the ADEA’s enacted qualification controls the justification required here. The EEOC regulation’s business-necessity language cannot replace that statutory standard.

The City sought to bring starting police salaries toward the regional average. A survey of comparable southeastern communities supplied wage ranges for five ranks, divided into steps and half-steps; each employee entered the lowest step that still produced at least a two-percent raise. Employees with lower existing salaries could receive larger percentage increases from the adjustment. Officers in the two highest ranks, all over forty, received larger dollar raises despite smaller percentages. Some officers over forty were also in the shorter-tenure group.

These actual mechanics connect the factors to the compensation objective. The statistics—66.2 percent of officers below forty and 45.3 percent above forty receiving raises exceeding ten percent—do not make that method unreasonable. A demand for equal percentages or another workable allocation does not defeat a reasonable nonage justification. The City therefore prevails on the impact claim on the established plan facts, assuming the claimed causal disparity.

### Precedent Treatment

Hazen Paper Co. v. Biggins retains its intentional-treatment and age-proxy rules; its reserved employee disparate-impact question is answered. General Dynamics Land Systems, Inc. v. Cline retains the Act’s protection of older employees against disadvantage favoring younger employees. Griggs v. Duke Power Co. supplies the effects-language analogy, limited here by the ADEA’s own reasonable-factors text. Wards Cove Packing Co. v. Atonio supplies identification and causal-linkage requirements; the ADEA’s enacted qualification governs justification. Christensen v. Harris County and United States v. Mead Corp. retain their source-specific rules for assessing agency interpretation.

### Law After Decision

Employees may pursue effects-based claims under §623(a)(2) by proving an identified neutral practice’s causal protected-age disadvantage. Employers’ actual nonage factors are assessed for reasonableness in relation to their employment objectives, without a universal least-disparate-alternative requirement. Ordinary job criteria are therefore challengeable, while reasonable use of nonage factors remains lawful. Deliberate use of tenure as an age proxy remains governed by intentional-treatment law.

### Procedure After Action

The mandate sustains summary judgment on the disparate-impact branch. The separate intentional-treatment claim continues on its existing discovery remand under its own governing law.
