## Event

Pasquantino v. United States, No. 03-725. Argued November 9, 2004; decided April 26, 2005 (2005-04-26). On review of the Fourth Circuit's en banc judgment, 336 F.3d 321. The question is whether the federal wire-fraud statute reaches a scheme using domestic interstate wires to deprive Canada of import-tax revenue, despite the common-law rule against enforcing foreign revenue claims.

## Participation

Chief Justice Stone-Zsela and Justices Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer participate.

## Public Action

The convictions are affirmed on the statutory question presented.

## Judgment & Remedy

**Judgment: Affirmed, 5–4.** Supporting: Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Kennedy and Thomas. Opposing: Scalia, Souter, Ginsburg and Breyer.

The judgment sustains the Pasquantino brothers' six wire-fraud convictions and Hilts's Count I conviction against the statutory and revenue-rule challenge. The constitutional sentencing request first made in merits briefing is outside this review. Any independently available sentencing or restitution issue must proceed under its own authority and presentation requirements.

## Opinion Topology

Chief Justice STONE-ZSELA delivered the opinion of the Court, joined by Stevens, O'Connor, Kennedy and Thomas. Ginsburg filed a dissent joined by Scalia, Souter and Breyer.

## Holdings

The five Justices joining the Court's opinion control both holdings and their explanations.

### 1. An accrued foreign tax entitlement can be the money targeted by wire fraud

**Operative rule.** Section 1343 reaches an intentional, material scheme executed through the requisite domestic interstate wires to deprive a foreign sovereign of an existing entitlement to money. The Government must prove that entitlement, material deception, fraudulent intent and the required wire use beyond a reasonable doubt; success of the scheme and completed loss are not elements.

**Controlling explanation.** Cleveland v. United States distinguishes a government's regulatory licensing authority from money or property already belonging to it. The object here is the import revenue due upon the transactions, not Canada's power to decide who may engage in regulated activity. The domestic calls furthered concealment and procurement for the charged scheme. The victim's sovereign nationality does not alter the monetary character of the object. Neder v. United States supplies materiality and the enacted fraud requirements while distinguishing a criminal scheme from every condition of a completed civil fraud. Thus foreign-law noncompliance alone is insufficient, but the charged material scheme against actual money falls within the statute.

### 2. The revenue rule does not bar this domestic criminal prosecution

**Operative rule.** The common-law rule against judicial collection of foreign taxes does not categorically bar the United States from enforcing its own wire-fraud prohibition against a scheme executed through domestic interstate wires. Identifying the foreign victim's property entitlement is distinct from enforcing that sovereign's civil tax claim; a separate collection or restitution remedy requires its own authority.

**Controlling explanation.** This prosecution vindicates the domestic criminal prohibition Congress enacted. Its authority comes from that statute, not from an Executive choice to enlarge federal jurisdiction or foreign-affairs power. The defendants contend that recognition of the Canadian entitlement indirectly enforces Canada's fiscal policy. That objection identifies why the revenue rule must be considered, but its established collection function does not confer immunity from a separate domestic fraud offense. Hartford Fire and Empagran require attention to the statute and the conduct Congress regulates; neither supplies an automatic foreign-victim exception. The actual American execution distinguishes the present application. The Court resolves the criminal question while leaving any specific monetary remedy to its governing statute and proof requirements.

## Precedent Treatment

- Cleveland v. United States: its distinction between governmental regulation and existing property governs; its unpaid-revenue reservation is resolved for this domestic criminal application.
- Neder v. United States: its materiality requirement and distinction between a criminal scheme and completed civil fraud remain controlling.
- Hartford Fire Insurance Co. v. California: its attention to statutory reach and foreign regulatory interests is applied to the domestic conduct actually charged.
- F. Hoffmann-La Roche Ltd. v. Empagran S.A.: its source-specific territorial inquiry is preserved; the domestic execution here supplies a different statutory connection from the independent foreign injury addressed there.

## Law After Decision

A foreign sovereign's accrued right to money may be the property targeted by domestic wire fraud. Courts must establish the actual entitlement and each criminal element, then distinguish punishment for the domestic offense from any separately authorized collection remedy. The revenue rule remains applicable to the collection setting it addresses.

## Separate Writings

**Ginsburg, joined by Scalia, Souter and Breyer, dissenting.** The general fraud language carries the established background limit on implementing foreign revenue claims. Although an accrued tax entitlement is monetary, that classification does not answer whether Congress authorized criminal punishment whose sole property object is the foreign fiscal demand. Domestic telephone calls supply the federal instrument but do not eliminate the need for clearer congressional authorization. Neder's treatment of inherited legal meaning and the statutory attention to foreign sovereign interests reflected in Empagran support that distinction. The four would reverse the convictions based on this theory, with their dependent sentences. Their shared ground concerns foreign fiscal claims, not a general exclusion of foreign private property.

## Procedure After Action

The lower courts implement the affirmance of the convictions on the statutory issue. Independently proper sentencing or restitution matters retain their own governing law, procedural prerequisites and evidentiary burdens.

## Event

Small v. United States, No. 03-750. Argued November 3, 2004; decided April 26, 2005 (2005-04-26). On review of the Third Circuit's judgment, 333 F.3d 425. The question is whether a foreign conviction qualifies as a conviction in any court under 18 U.S.C. §922(g)(1), read with §921(a)(20).

## Participation

Chief Justice Stone-Zsela and Justices Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer participate.

## Public Action

The judgment is reversed and the case remanded.

## Judgment & Remedy

**Judgment: Reversed and remanded, 6–3.** Supporting: Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Souter, Ginsburg and Breyer. Opposing: Scalia, Kennedy and Thomas.

The Japanese conviction cannot serve as this prosecution's §922(g)(1) predicate. The district court must examine the actual judgment and conditional plea, vacate the count and dependent punishment resting solely on the Japanese predicate, and conduct any remaining proceedings on their independent lawful bases.

## Opinion Topology

O'CONNOR, J., delivered the opinion of the Court, joined by Chief Justice Stone-Zsela and Stevens, Souter, Ginsburg and Breyer. Thomas dissented, joined by Scalia and Kennedy.

| Writing | Author | Joined by | Scope |
|---|---|---|---|
| Opinion of the Court | O'Connor | Stone-Zsela, Stevens, Souter, Ginsburg, Breyer | Domestic conviction category and reversal |
| Dissent | Thomas | Scalia, Kennedy | Inclusive statutory category |

## Holdings

### Foreign convictions fall outside this firearm-disability predicate

**Rule and authority.** A conviction qualifies under §922(g)(1) only if it is domestic and satisfies the definition and qualifications in §921(a)(20). The Government must establish a qualifying predicate beyond a reasonable doubt; the seriousness or perceived fairness of an excluded foreign judgment cannot supply statutory coverage. The six Justices joining the Court's opinion adopt this rule and its application to Small.

**Controlling explanation.** The word any is broad within the statutory category it describes. Section 921(a)(20) identifies that category through a domestic structure: it excludes the specified federal or state antitrust, unfair-trade and similar business-regulatory offenses, and state misdemeanors punishable by two years or less, and it provides for the effect of pardons, expungement, setting aside and restoration of civil rights. A worldwide general category combined with those domestic exclusions would treat comparable foreign offenses differently without supplying a recognition system to administer that result.

The Government correctly observes that the charged firearm possession occurred in this country. That answers where the federal offense occurs, but not which antecedent convictions Congress included. Beecham v. United States and Jones v. United States selects the convicting jurisdiction's law for the restoration inquiry once a conviction falls within the statute; it does not define every foreign judgment as a qualifying conviction. Caron v. United States governs the federal effect of a substantial firearm restriction retained after domestic restoration; it likewise concerns the consequence of a qualifying conviction. Reading those separate statutory steps together supports the domestic category.

Small's Japanese conviction is therefore excluded categorically. The Court need not decide whether the individual Japanese proceeding satisfies the lower court's recognition standards. The holding construes this statutory context rather than imposing a general geographical meaning on any wherever Congress uses it.

## Precedent Treatment

- Beecham v. United States and Jones v. United States: its convicting-jurisdiction selector remains controlling for restoration of a qualifying conviction; it does not resolve the antecedent foreign-predicate question.
- Caron v. United States: its rule for a substantial firearm restriction retained after domestic restoration remains effective. The present holding determines the earlier category question rather than altering Caron's restoration consequence.

## Law After Decision

Courts first determine whether the asserted conviction is domestic and within §921(a)(20)'s definition. They then apply the existing rules for exclusions, pardon, expungement, setting aside and restoration. Foreign judgments are outside the §922(g)(1) predicate category, so that provision requires no individual foreign-fairness screening.

## Separate Writings

**Thomas, joined by Scalia and Kennedy, dissenting.** The unqualified phrase any court includes foreign courts, while Congress expressly confined neighboring exceptions to federal or state offenses. The dissent considers that contrast a reason to retain the broader predicate, even if the exceptions produce some asymmetry. The statute regulates later American firearm possession, not foreign conduct or foreign courts. Beecham's selector can operate by reference to the convicting jurisdiction, and Caron's restoration dispute supplies no missing geographical limitation. General concerns about foreign procedures cannot replace the enacted language. These Justices would affirm the statutory ruling; their position does not adopt the lower court's complete recognition test or decide a distinct constitutional objection to an individual predicate.

## Procedure After Action

The case returns for implementation of the reversal, removal of the conviction and sentence dependent on the Japanese predicate, and any further proceedings consistent with the domestic-category holding.
