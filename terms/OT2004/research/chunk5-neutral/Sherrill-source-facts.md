## Record and judgment below

The City describes three consolidated Sherrill actions: the Nation's declaratory action; the City's tax-sale/eviction action removed to federal court; and the City's action against members of the Tribal Council. The petitioner's merits brief, printed pages 9–11, states that the district court granted the Nation summary judgment in the lead and eviction actions, dismissed the City's counterclaims and member action, denied the City's request to restrain further acquisitions, and denied leave to amend. It identifies three separate final judgments. The Second Circuit affirmed the Sherrill judgments. These are lower-court events; the separate Madison County matter is not folded into the Sherrill mandate.

Both briefs identify ordinary purchases in 1997 and 1998 of parcels used for businesses, including a gas station/convenience store and a textile manufacturing/distribution facility. The City assessed taxes; the Nation declined to pay; the City pursued tax-sale and eviction remedies. Both trace the challenged parcels through a 100-acre tract conveyed in 1805 to Cornelius Dockstader, an Oneida, and in 1807 to Peter Smith, a non-Indian. The Nation characterizes New York as arranging the first transfer and authorizing the second without the federal approval required by the 1802 Act. The City maintains its legal theories that federal restraints did not apply. Those legal characterizations must remain distinct from the common chain-of-title facts.

## Actual competing grounds

The City's first principal merits argument is that the reservation originated in the 1788 state Treaty of Fort Schuyler and does not satisfy the federal set-aside and superintendence requirements it derives from Venetie. It urges that those requirements govern all three categories in 18 U.S.C. §1151. It reads the 1794 Treaty of Canandaigua as acknowledging a state reservation rather than establishing federal protection. It stresses state and local services and the ordinary fee title of the reacquired properties. This is an actual argued path, not merely a general appeal to delay.

The Nation responds that the 1788 reservation of land qualified the cession, and independently that the 1784, 1789 and 1794 federal treaties secured possession. It points to Canandaigua's Articles 2 and 4, the federal superintendent and annuity provisions, and federal dispute-resolution provisions. It distinguishes an express congressional termination in Venetie and the statutory disposal in Cass County. It argues that a new trust acquisition under former §465 cannot be required to obtain protection that never lawfully ceased. The disputed reach of Venetie should be checked against the simulated holding supplied separately.

The City's second principal argument concerns the 1838 Treaty of Buffalo Creek. It treats the Kansas reservation, the agreement to remove after satisfactory arrangements for sale of New York lands, the period's removal policy, and the eventual damages recovery for the lost Kansas land as demonstrating New York disestablishment. It cites the 1898 and 1899 New York Indians cases and Santa Fe Pacific. It disputes the legal force of Commissioner Gillet's assurances and objects that neither formal cession language nor a fixed payment is indispensable.

The Nation responds that Articles 1 and 2 expressly exchange Wisconsin interests for Kansas land; Article 13 conditions Oneida removal on satisfactory future arrangements; and Articles 3 and 15 contemplate nonremoval and discretionary funding. It contrasts the provisions addressing Seneca and Tuscarora lands. It relies on Gillet's written assurance and reported explanation to the Oneidas that removal would not be compelled. It describes the federal government's discouragement of removal and eventual sale of the Kansas land. It treats the damages recovery as compensation for the Wisconsin interest, rather than consideration for New York relinquishment. Its printed pages 29–42 set out these arguments and their source references.

The City's third principal argument is a lapse in the Nation's tribal existence. Its printed pages 40–45 cite United States v. Elm, nineteenth- and early-twentieth-century reports, a census map, and a later legislative report. The Nation's printed pages 43–50 responds with current recognition, continuous federal annuities/census treatment, the Boylan proceedings, the 1936 reorganization vote and federal recognition records. It states that current tribal recognition and succession to the treaty party were not disputed, while the City asserted a legally consequential historical lapse. Statements in the competing briefs about the evidentiary sufficiency of these materials are arguments, not newly made Supreme Court findings.

## Equitable and remedial presentation

The Nation's printed page 19 expressly distinguishes an equitable limit on ejectment of land held by others from protection of land it now possesses. It also distinguishes tribal authority over nonmember land from the taxation of the Nation's own parcels.
