# Bell v. Cone — neutral state rule and application supplement

This handoff contains the state construction and the state court's actual application, as quoted in an authenticated published recitation. It contains no assessment that those actions establish a sufficient federal adjudication or cure. Each block below is copied mechanically from the complete primary source `bell_full.txt` and cross-checked against `bell_official.txt`; no original state brief or transcript is claimed recovered. The legal status of each quoted passage is a state rule or a state judicial finding, not an independently established historical fact beyond that description.

## 1. Dicks construction

State v. Dicks, 615 S. W. 2d 126, 132 (Tenn. 1981), supplies this construction of the heinousness aggravator. Source recitation: 543 U.S. 458.

"directed at `the conscienceless or pitiless crime which is unnecessarily torturous to the victim,'" Dicks, supra, at 132.

## 2. Pritchett's stated narrowing rule

State v. Pritchett, 621 S. W. 2d 127, 139 (Tenn. 1981), states the operative construction below. The decision also treated instantaneous death as insufficient for its torture branch and a second shotgun blast after the victim's death as insufficient to establish the requisite depravity. Source recitation: 543 U.S. 458–459. These qualifications describe the state rule; this handoff makes no federal holding about it.

"Although the Tennessee aggravating circumstances [sic] [that the murder was heinous, atrocious, or cruel], does not contain the phrase, `an aggravated battery to the victim' it is clear that a constitutional construction of this aggravating circumstance requires evidence that the defendant inflicted torture on the victim before death or that [the] defendant committed acts evincing a depraved state of mind; that the depraved state of mind or the torture inflicted must meet the test of heinous, atrocious, or cruel." Pritchett, 621 S. W. 2d, at 139 (citation omitted).

## 3. Melson's applications

State v. Melson, 638 S. W. 2d 342, 367 (Tenn. 1982), gives these concrete applications. The first concerns conscious suffering and opportunity to resist; the second describes an application based on repeated blows and the resulting injuries. They are the state court's factual characterizations and legal applications in Melson. Source recitation: 543 U.S. 458–459.

"the uncontradicted proof shows that [the victim] had defensive injuries to her arms and hands, proving that there was time for her to realize what was happening, to feel fear, and to try to protect herself," Melson, supra, at 367.

"a killing wherein the victim is struck up to thirty times, causing an entire room to be covered with a spray of flying blood, and causing the victim's brains to extrude through the gaping hole in her skull," sufficed, Melson, supra, at 367.

## 4. The actual direct-review passage in Cone

The following is the Tennessee Supreme Court's own explanation of the heinousness aggravator in State v. Cone, 665 S. W. 2d 87, 94–95 (Tenn. 1984), quoted at 543 U.S. 449. It preserves what the state court said about resistance, multiple blows, noninstantaneous deaths, fear and the jury's finding. Whether that explanation actually applied the authoritative rule, decided the particular federal claim, or corrected a distinct constitutional defect remains for independent analysis under the governing law.

"The jury also found that the murders in question were especially heinous, atrocious, or cruel in that they involved torture or depravity of mind as provided in [Tenn. Code Ann.] § 39-2-203(i)(5). The evidence abundantly established that both of the elderly victims had been brutally beaten to death by multiple crushing blows to the skulls. Blood was spattered throughout the house, and both victims apparently had attempted to resist, because numerous defensive wounds were found on their persons. The only excuse offered in the entire record for this unspeakably brutal conduct by the accused was that these elderly victims had at some point ceased to `cooperate' with him in his ransacking of their home and in his effort to flee from arrest. As previously stated, it was stipulated by counsel for [respondent] that there was no issue of self-defense even remotely suggested. The deaths of the victims were not instantaneous, and obviously one had to be killed before the other. The terror, fright and horror that these elderly helpless citizens must have endured was certainly something that the jury could have taken into account in finding this aggravating circumstance." Id., at 94-95.

## 6. The jury definitions reported below

The following instruction is reported in the Sixth Circuit opinion at 359 F.3d 794 and reproduced at 543 U.S. 452 n.4. It is included to permit comparison of the actual jury language and the authoritative state construction without assuming that their contents were identical. Its constitutional effect remains an independent legal question.

The jury was instructed with respect to this aggravated circumstance as follows:

"`Heinous' means extremely wicked or shockingly evil.

"`Atrocious' means outrageously wicked and vile.

"`Cruel' means designed to inflict a high degree of pain, utter indifference to, or enjoyment of, the suffering of others, pitiless." 359 F. 3d, at 794.
