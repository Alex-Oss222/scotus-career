## Women's Medical Professional Corp. v. Taft, 353 F.3d 436

Official source: https://www.opn.ca6.uscourts.gov/opinions.pdf/03a0446p-06.pdf . Decided December 17, 2003, No. 01-4124. Ryan wrote the court's opinion, joined by Batchelder; District Judge Tarnow dissented. Court opinion pp. 2–29; dissent pp. 30–54. The downloaded PDF places two printed pages on each of its 27 pages.

The majority reversed the district court's judgment and vacated the permanent injunction. It upheld both the pre- and post-viability health exception and concluded that the procedure definition, read with the express D&E exclusion, did not impose an undue burden by prohibiting ordinary D&E.

On health, the majority read historical Casey and Stenberg to require an exception for significant health risks, including comparative safety, rather than one triggered by a physician's preference or negligible risks. It emphasized the state's interests throughout pregnancy and read Stenberg's rejection of absolute medical necessity together with its rejection of unfettered physician discretion. Invoking constitutional avoidance, it construed the statute to permit the intact procedure when other procedures would expose this woman to significant risks. The prospective words concerning danger and risk meant that a preexisting complication was not necessary: reasonable medical judgment that failure to use the method would produce a qualifying complication could trigger the exception. It tied the standard to developing medical knowledge and responsible differences of medical opinion. It applied its previability analysis to the identically worded postviability provision (pp. 14–25).

The majority's mental-health response was narrower than a general denial of mental-health protection. It distinguished Voinovich's ban on all postviability abortions from a restriction of one method; it asserted that the record lacked evidence of severe irreversible mental harm caused by limiting the patient to D&E instead of D&X (p. 21 n.1). That framing is disputed in the dissent and must not become an uncontested factual finding about mental illness or comparative physical risk.

On definition, the majority relied on section (F)'s express method exclusions, (A)(1)'s distinction between extraction and evacuation, and the sequential intentional acts in (A)(3). It treated dismemberment before removal in several passes as outside the prohibition, even when extraction to the navel occurs. For a breech presentation, the prohibited death-causing act must follow intact extraction to the navel. It did not read the act to prohibit every intact abortion. The statute's distinction between legal methods supplied what the historical Nebraska statute lacked (pp. 25–29).

Tarnow agreed that the act did not encompass ordinary D&E (p. 30 n.1), so there was no panel disagreement on that coverage issue. He would affirm the injunction because the health exception was inadequate. He agreed that truly negligible risks need not trigger an exception, but understood the relevant standard to protect against any nonnegligible comparative risk. He rejected the majority's characterization of plaintiffs as demanding unrestricted medical preference (pp. 31–38, nn.3, 5–7).

The dissent relied on district-court medical evidence concerning infection, cervical/uterine trauma, clotting disorders, blood loss, anesthesia, retained tissue, induction, major surgery, and added risks from inducing fetal death. It treated the statutory requirement of a medically diagnosed condition complicating pregnancy as incompatible with protection of a healthy woman against future procedural risks. Because an abortion ends pregnancy, a consequence of the abortion could not naturally be a condition complicating that pregnancy. Avoidance could not erase an explicit definition (pp. 38–44).

Tarnow separately reasoned that serious irreversible mental-health harm from continuing pregnancy could make abortion necessary while the intact procedure offered the safest physical means of performing it. The majority addressed mental harm from choice of method instead of this combined mental/physical situation. The dissent relied on Doe, Voinovich, and record testimony, while acknowledging that the district court had not made detailed final findings on mental-health risk in this case (pp. 45–51, n.9). He accepted a possible saving reading for postviability patients who already had a physical complication but found both pre- and postviability provisions inadequate for the mental-health situation (p. 44 n.8). Finally, he distinguished Casey's temporary delay regulations from a permanent method prohibition: time eventually ends the delay but never removes the prohibited-method restriction (pp. 51–54).

Preservation limits: the imported recklessness requirement for viability and health-exception applicability was not appealed. The third-party civil-action challenge was dismissed for standing and not appealed. The ordinary-D&E issue was not decided at the permanent-injunction stage, but both parties asked for appellate determination on a developed record. Those limits survive the historical opinion's broad introductory language. The dissent's citation at p. 49 to a 911 F. Supp. page is facially irregular; do not use that citation alone to create a concession. The statutory words and the competing analyses are independently available.

Statutory existence: the opinion expressly says HB351 was written and adopted before Stenberg, following the Sixth Circuit Voinovich decision (pp. 3, 27–28). The official historical code supplies an August 18, 2000 effective date. The enrolled act's section 3(C) acknowledges Roe and claims that it does not protect infanticide; that general constitutional framing does not establish a specific legislative response abrogating, codifying, or modifying a later Supreme Court judgment. The supported historical replacement predicate is lower-court litigation. This review makes no prediction about what lawmakers would have done under different timeline doctrine.

## GDF Realty Investments, Ltd. v. Norton, 326 F.3d 622; rehearing at 362 F.3d 286

Panel official source: https://www.ca5.uscourts.gov/opinions/pub/01/01-51099.cv0.wpd.pdf . March 26, 2003, No. 01-51099. Barksdale wrote for Davis, Barksdale, and Dennis; Dennis joined and wrote a separate concurrence. The court affirmed summary judgment rejecting the as-applied Commerce Clause challenge.

The panel rejected the district court's primary reliance on the developers' commercial project. It treated the expressly regulated activity as species taking and reasoned that an actor's separate commercial activity cannot supply unlimited power to regulate the actor's otherwise local conduct. It also rejected the scientific-travel/publication evidence and possible future medicinal uses as independent grounds for a substantial effect: the former had negligible demonstrated impact, the latter was conjectural. The panel acknowledged that these cave-species takes were not themselves commercial in the ordinary sense (pp. 21–35).

Its affirmative ground was that the ESA constituted an economic regulatory scheme and regulation of the local takes was essential to that scheme's efficacy. It used legislative concern for genetic resources, the economic origin of many takes, and ecosystem interdependence to explain why excluding a species could undercut the scheme through piecemeal extinction. It did not regard substantial aggregate effects alone as sufficient to justify aggregation; the essential relation to the economic scheme was the bridge. The plaintiffs conceded that aggregation with all ESA takes would yield substantial effects. The court did not decide the government's treaty and foreign-commerce arguments (pp. 35–40 and concluding footnote).

Dennis's concurrence (pp. 41–48) expressly grounded the comprehensive-scheme approach in the interaction of the Commerce Clause and Necessary and Proper Clause. Darby, McCulloch, Wrightwood Dairy, Wirtz, and Hodel supported regulation of local conduct integral to effective interstate regulation even if that conduct could not be reached standing alone. He distinguished the freestanding provisions in Lopez and Morrison. Ecosystem relationships could rationally justify protection of local noncommercial species needed by commercial species; the government need not add numerous small independently economic effects to reach a threshold. The concurrence joined the panel fully while supplying this additional explanation.

Rehearing official source: https://www.ca5.uscourts.gov/opinions/pub/01/01-51099-CV1.wpd.pdf . Filed February 27, 2004; source marked revised March 25, 2004. The per curiam order denied panel and en banc rehearing after a poll failed to produce a majority for rehearing. The source does not identify a complete voting roster or authorize inference of the votes of every other active judge. Edith Jones dissented from denial, joined by Jolly, Smith, DeMoss, Clement, and Pickering. No other separate writing appears; PDF page 19 has no extracted text, while the dissent concludes at printed page 18.

Jones accepted the panel's focus on takes and its rejection of independent scientific/future-use theories. Her disagreement concerned essentiality and the economic character of the larger scheme. In her view a speculative web of interdependence did not establish that destruction of these species would impair interstate commerce; treating all species protection as essential was circular and had no limiting principle. She contended that the panel inconsistently returned to commercial development after rejecting it as the relevant regulated activity. Many species-specific or market-linked applications could remain valid, but biodiversity itself was a condition of nature, not commerce. She invoked Lopez, Morrison, SWANCC's federalism concerns, and limits on Hobbs Act applications. Her criticism sought rehearing and did not itself alter the panel judgment.

## Karaha Bodas Co. v. Pertamina, 364 F.3d 274

Official source: https://www.ca5.uscourts.gov/opinions/pub/02/02-20042-CV0.wpd.pdf . March 23, 2004, Nos. 02-20042 and 03-20602. District Judge Rosenthal wrote the single opinion for a panel with Chief Judge King and Judge Davis; no separate writing. All 64 pages and 131 footnotes were read.

The court affirmed recognition/enforcement and rejected the Rule 60(b) grounds. A secondary-jurisdiction court may refuse recognition on the Convention's specified grounds, narrowly construed, and does not retry the arbitral merits. Ordinary factual or legal error is insufficient. The resisting party bears the burden (pp. 15–19).

The governing-law analysis distinguishes substantive contract law from arbitral procedural law. Selecting Geneva presumptively selected Swiss arbitral procedure; references to and waivers of specified Indonesian procedural rules did not amount to an express contrary choice. The hearing's physical occurrence in Paris did not change the legal seat. The tribunal's contract interpretation received deference, and Pertamina's earlier repeated Swiss-law assertions supported contractual intent. Judicial estoppel independently supported the result because Pertamina's contrary position had been accepted by both the tribunal and the district court, including the latter's delay to permit Swiss review (pp. 19–31).

The court upheld consolidation because the contracts were integrated, signed together, contained substantially identical arbitration terms, and contemplated a single project; UNCITRAL procedure and lack of prejudice reinforced that conclusion. It rejected the arbitrator-selection challenge because the ESC's joint-nomination provision contemplated aligned interests, not a dispute between KBC and Pertamina, and because Pertamina did not timely nominate or object despite opportunities. The decision did not create a general power to disregard a contrary express arbitration agreement (pp. 31–39).

Due process under Article V(1)(b) required notice, meaningful opportunity to present evidence, and an impartial decision, not all Federal Rules procedures. The preliminary award had not foreclosed liability in the way Pertamina claimed, and the liability theory was briefed. Pertamina presented extensive financing evidence and cross-examined witnesses; the denial of further prehearing discovery and continuance did not deprive it of a fair hearing. Its counsel later stated that the record was fully made and did not maintain the discovery demands. The damages award accounted for financing risk and reduced the requested profits. The district court's refusal of Rule 56(f) discovery and further Rule 60(b) evidentiary proceedings was not an abuse of discretion (pp. 39–54).

The public-policy defense was confined to the forum's most basic notions of morality and justice. The abuse-of-rights theory was not established American law and was unsupported on the facts; enforcing the agreed allocation of governmental risk did not punish mere obedience to a decree. Insurance nondisclosure did not establish deliberate deception, and Pertamina had opportunities to investigate. The court described the fraud test as clear-and-convincing proof, nondiscoverability with due diligence, and material relation to an arbitral issue; proof of a different result was unnecessary. The $75 million insurance payment did not justify an offset because subrogation prevented double recovery and collateral payment ordinarily did not reduce the judgment debt (pp. 49–59, especially n.89).

On Article V(1)(e), both possible connecting criteria pointed to Switzerland: the award was made there under Swiss procedural law. Indonesia's substantive-law connection did not supply annulment authority. The opinion discussed whether the Convention might permit more than one primary country and the predominant single-country view, but the concrete holding did not require deciding every genuinely split-seat/procedure case. Likewise, it did not decide whether a valid primary-country annulment always requires refusal or can sometimes be disregarded. The Indonesian judgment in this record did not provide the asserted defense (pp. 59–64).
