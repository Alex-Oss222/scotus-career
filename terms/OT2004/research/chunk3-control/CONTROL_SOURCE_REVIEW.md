## Smith v. Massachusetts, 543 U.S. 462

Read Cornell's syllabus first, followed by the complete Scalia opinion and Ginsburg dissent, including all seven Court-opinion footnotes and all four dissent footnotes. Official United States Reports cross-check: 19 pages, 543 U.S. 462–480, read in full. The historical comparator is accurate: reversal and remand, five to four; Scalia for Stevens, O'Connor, Souter, and Thomas; Ginsburg dissent joined by Rehnquist, Kennedy, and Breyer. There is no separate concurrence.

The historical Court treated the granted insufficiency motion as a substantive acquittal, regardless of whether its assessment of the evidence was legally mistaken. Its finality rule addressed an unqualified midtrial allowance followed by defense proceedings when no preexisting rule or case expressly made such rulings reconsiderable. It rejected a same-trial exception and a requirement of demonstrated actual prejudice. It distinguished correction of a mistaken announcement from reconsideration of a legal conclusion, and distinguished the reinstatement of an already returned guilty verdict from renewed guilt factfinding. It allowed that States can establish a genuinely provisional midtrial procedure; it did not declare every possible reconsideration scheme unconstitutional.

Ginsburg's dissent agreed that States can permit reconsideration but treated Massachusetts's general common-law reconsideration rule and its appellate court's construction as sufficient. She distinguished a continuous trial before the original tribunal from an appeal following a final acquittal. Her safeguard was a fair opportunity to defend under due process, rather than a double-jeopardy finality rule keyed to the apparent allowance. She emphasized the absence of an asserted strategy change or attempt to reopen, the continued jury, the legal sufficiency of the original evidence under Sperrazza, and the prompt correction before argument and instructions. This is a substantive state-procedure and finality disagreement, not a disagreement about whether acquittals generally receive protection.
