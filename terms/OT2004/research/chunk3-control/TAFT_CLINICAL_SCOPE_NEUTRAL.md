# Taft — clinical evidence and relief scope

Sanitized supplement for fresh neutral validation. This supplies lower-court procedure, reported clinical findings, and the limits of those findings. It supplies no reviewing result, lineup, or proposed Supreme Court disposition. Source: official Sixth Circuit opinion, https://www.opn.ca6.uscourts.gov/opinions.pdf/03a0446p-06.pdf, saved as `taft-official.txt`; page numbers below are printed pages, two per PDF page. The whole opinion was read by the lower-source reader. The control researcher additionally checked the specific passages below. No original medical transcript or district-court judgment is claimed as independently read.

The district court entered a permanent injunction against enforcement of the challenged Act. Its health ruling addressed the previability prohibition and the postviability prohibition as applied to women medically needing a postviability abortion. It reasoned that the exception failed to allow the method when the medical evidence showed it was the safest. Ohio appealed those health components. The preliminary decision rejected the ordinary-D&E coverage claim; the permanent decision did not decide that claim, but both parties requested appellate resolution. The district court's incorporation of recklessness for viability and the exception's applicability was unappealed, as was dismissal for lack of standing of the separate third-party civil-action challenge (pp. 10–11). Thus the source supports a broad enforcement injunction against the Act on the stated health ground, while identifying the reach of the postviability constitutional conclusion; it does not supply the injunction order's exact binding-party or territorial language. A remand order should identify any interim protection expressly rather than invent those missing terms.

The published source expressly reports that the district court found the intact method may be safer than ordinary D&E, based on comparative risks of infection, cervical and uterine trauma from repeated instrument passes, and disseminated intravascular coagulation, a potentially fatal clotting disorder. It reports possible advantages concerning blood loss, time under general anesthesia, and retained fetal tissue. Those findings are conditional comparative evidence, not a stipulation that one method is always safer for every patient (pp. 38–40; district citations 162 F. Supp. 2d at 947–950, 954).

The district court also considered induction/instillation's longer induction time, effects of the inducing agent, hemorrhage and infection risk, and possible additional surgery for retained placenta. It compared hysterectomy and hysterotomy as substantially more traumatic surgery. The source reports these findings without supplying a quantified risk difference applicable to every patient. No numerical constitutional threshold follows from the clinical recitations (pp. 39–40; district citations at 942, 948–951).

Because the statute contains conditions concerning a living fetus and the timing of the death-causing act, a claim that every patient can instead undergo prior fetal demise requires its own clinical evaluation. The district court found additional risk from digoxin injection and from severing the umbilical cord. The published account identifies expert testimony about embolism, bleeding and injury to other organs, uterine injury, and arrhythmia in women with cardiac conditions. It reports the district finding that locating and severing an unpresented cord with sharp instruments increases uterine-perforation risk. These are supported risk categories; they are not an adjudication that prior demise is impossible or inappropriate in every case (pp. 40–41; district citations at 956–958).

On mental health, the published source reports expert testimony that continued pregnancy or childbirth may create severe risks for some patients, including some victims of rape, incest, or abuse and patients with severe psychiatric disorders. The permanent decision did not reach the distinct mental-health exception issue and did not make detailed final findings on it; the preliminary decision had found a substantial likelihood of invalidity on that ground. The published account therefore supports the presence of an unresolved evidentiary issue, not a final finding of necessity for every listed patient category (pp. 46–47 and n.9).

Two different factual questions must remain distinct: whether the choice of one abortion method instead of another itself causes grave mental injury, and whether pregnancy creates a grave mental-health danger requiring abortion while comparative physical risks make one method necessary for safe care. The clinical record cannot be replaced by the label of an available alternative. Equally, the evidence of possible safety advantages does not establish universal necessity. The precise physical-condition language, the prospective operation of the exception, the necessary degree of risk, and the available patient-specific alternatives remain issues under the governing law. A source-specific qualification: the lower majority's explanatory discussion at printed p. 23 uses a disjunctive formulation where the enacted definition uses a conjunction. The enacted words control the text; the explanation is a claimed construction to evaluate, not an amendment. The competing legal positions also differed over whether future comparative procedural risks can count as a condition complicating pregnancy. Neither position supplies a newly found clinical fact.
