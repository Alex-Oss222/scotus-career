## GDF Realty

Source: official Fifth Circuit text, https://www.ca5.uscourts.gov/opinions/pub/01/01-51099.cv0.wpd.pdf, pp. 2–8. Local source: `gdf-panel-official.txt`.

- The property comprises 216 acres within the approximately 1,200-acre Parke, near Austin, Texas. It contains caves and other karst features. Development plans included a shopping center with a Wal-Mart, a residential subdivision, and office buildings.
- The six listed invertebrate species occur only in underground portions of Travis and Williamson Counties, Texas. Five were listed in 1988 and the sixth in 1993. The listing notices identified habitat loss from development and the vulnerability of the small habitats.
- There was no commercial market for these species. This does not mean that no specimen ever crossed a state line: the opinion reports scientific study by fifteen scientists, at least fourteen articles, travel by some scientists to Texas, and specimens transported to and from museums in five other states. The existence and extent of those activities must be distinguished from any conclusion about their constitutional significance.
- In 1990 the developers conveyed approximately six acres containing caves and sinkholes to a nonprofit environmental organization and installed gates at sensitive caves. A proposed 1991 sale failed after the agency declined to state that development would not cause a take. After brush clearing in 1993, Dr. Purcell was informed of a criminal investigation; the source does not thereby establish an adjudication that a prohibited take occurred.
- A 1994 agency letter said proposed development likely would take cave species and two bird species. It also said the developers' property could be developed without a take if, among other measures, development were scaled back from canyons and drainage and nutrient exchange were provided for. This is the agency's statement, not a presently issued permit or an unconditional finding of developability.
- Incidental-take permit efforts began in 1997. The regional plan declined the application because the relevant land lay wholly within a protected area. The federal agency ultimately issued formal permit denials after litigation over its delay. The constitutional suit began in 1999. A distinct Fifth Amendment compensation action was stayed pending the constitutional action. Two plaintiffs filed Chapter 11 petitions in 2000.
- The parties agreed that there were no factual disputes and filed cross-motions for summary judgment. The litigation concerned the constitutional application of the taking prohibition, not an award of a development permit or compensation.
- Statutory distinctions: section 9's taking prohibition, section 3's definition of take, the agency's habitat-harm definition, and section 10's permit mechanism are distinct provisions. A constitutional power ruling does not establish the injury and causation predicates of a statutory violation.

## Women's Medical Professional Corp. v. Taft

Sources: https://www.opn.ca6.uscourts.gov/opinions.pdf/03a0446p-06.pdf, factual/posture portions at printed pp. 3–11; official historical statute at https://codes.ohio.gov/ohio-revised-code/section-2919.151/8-18-2000. Local sources: `taft-official.txt`, `ohio-2919.151-2000.txt`. The full statute and mechanically copied opinion quotations are in `OHIO_STATUTE_EXTRACT.md`.

- WMPC is an Ohio abortion-services corporation owned and operated by Dr. Martin Haskell. They perform procedures covered by the challenged law and asserted threatened criminal and civil liability. The defendants are Ohio officials sued in their official capacities.
- House Bill 351 enacted the revised section 2919.151. The official historical code identifies its effective date as August 18, 2000. The official opinion expressly places its drafting and adoption before the historical Supreme Court decision in Stenberg, after the earlier Sixth Circuit Voinovich litigation. That chronology supplies no specific historical predicate of legislation responding to Stenberg. General awareness of existing Supreme Court abortion doctrine is not itself evidence of a statute specifically enacted to abrogate, codify, or modify a particular Supreme Court disposition. The operator must separately compare any genuinely identified predicate with timeline law; this source extraction does not decide that comparison.
- The enacted definition requires all four acts in sequence, not one listed act alone: intentional cervical dilation; the specified intentional extraction of an intact fetus; the specified intentional death-causing act known to cause death; completion of vaginal delivery. The extraction threshold differs for breech and cephalic presentation and is measured beyond the vaginal introitus.
- Divisions (B) and (C) separately address viable and nonviable fetuses and both use the complete life-or-health language copied in the statute extract. Division (A)(5)'s medically diagnosed condition definition must remain visible. Describing the exception solely as a significant-risk exception substitutes a construction for the enacted wording. Whether the wording includes comparative procedure risks or a future complication is a contested legal question, not an independently established clinical fact.
- Division (F) excludes suction curettage, suction aspiration, and dilation and evacuation. Division (A)(1) distinguishes dilation and extraction from dilation and evacuation. Division (G) separately excludes a legal abortion when the death-causing act precedes partial birth even if death follows it. Those are distinct exclusions with distinct conditions. Division (E) excludes criminal responsibility of the pregnant woman under the specified prohibitions. Division (D) classifies the offense as a second-degree felony.
- The July 27, 2000 complaint challenged breadth affecting ordinary D&E, health protection, scienter, and third-party civil actions. At the preliminary-injunction stage the district court addressed ordinary D&E coverage; at the permanent-injunction stage it did not decide that issue, but both parties requested appellate resolution. The permanent judgment addressed the pre- and post-viability health exception.
- The district court imported recklessness under Ohio law for viability and applicability of the health exception; neither party appealed that part of the judgment. The district court rejected the challenge to the third-party civil-action provision for lack of standing; plaintiffs did not appeal that ruling. Do not invent a preserved appellate attack on either ruling.
- The medical record contains comparative-risk evidence. The source does not support a stipulation that the intact method is always safer, nor an unqualified factual finding that it is never medically preferable. Medical conclusions, disputed characterizations, and any chosen statutory construction must remain distinct. No new clinical finding is supplied here.

## Karaha Bodas

Source: official Fifth Circuit opinion, https://www.ca5.uscourts.gov/opinions/pub/02/02-20042-CV0.wpd.pdf. Local source: `karaha-official.txt`.

- In November 1994 KBC entered a Joint Operation Contract with Pertamina and an Energy Sales Contract involving Pertamina and PLN. Pertamina and PLN are Indonesian state-owned enterprises; the Republic was found by the arbitral tribunal not to be a contracting or arbitral party. The two contracts contained arbitration clauses selecting Geneva and UNCITRAL rules, general Indonesian governing-law provisions, and references waiving certain Indonesian procedural provisions. The complete clauses are quoted in source pp. 5–6 and 22–23.
- The place selected by the contracts was Geneva. The hearing physically occurred in Paris; the award stated that it was made in Geneva (p. 25). A note saying that every arbitral event physically occurred in Switzerland would be inaccurate.
- The contracts did not expressly name the procedural law governing arbitration (p. 22). Whether their seat clause and Indonesian references selected that law was disputed. Pertamina's contention was not simply that Indonesian substantive law automatically governed annulment: it argued that the actual contract references selected Indonesian procedural law too (pp. 21–23). Any account of its strongest preserved position should retain that claim while distinguishing it from an accepted factual premise.
- The government temporarily suspended the project on September 20, 1997, and indefinitely suspended it on January 10, 1998. KBC commenced arbitration April 30, 1998. The final award in December 2000 included $111.1 million in expenditures and $150 million in lost profits; KBC had requested $512.5 million in lost profits (pp. 6–10, 52).
- Pertamina sought Swiss relief in February 2001. The Swiss proceeding was dismissed in April 2001 for untimely payment of costs; reconsideration was denied in August 2001 (p. 11). This was not a Swiss merits adjudication sustaining the award.
- The United States enforcement judgment preceded the Indonesian annulment. Pertamina filed the Jakarta action in March 2002; the Indonesian annulment occurred August 27, 2002 (p. 60). The recognition appeal consolidated review of summary judgment with denial of Rule 60(b) relief. A separate earlier appeal concerned an anti-suit injunction and did not decide the effect of the actual Indonesian annulment on recognition (pp. 2–3).
- The lower recognition appeal included separate objections concerning consolidation, arbitrator selection, opportunity to present a case, denial of discovery and continuance, public policy, political-risk insurance, and the Indonesian annulment (pp. 10–15). The supplied question may delimit review, but the other objections should not be described as absent from the historical appeal.
- The later-discovered insurance paid $75 million to investors or associated insured entities. The source contains a qualification concerning FPL versus an owned entity (p. 14 n.8). It should not be rewritten as an undisputed direct payment of $75 million to KBC. The policy contained a repayment/subrogation provision, relevant to any double-recovery assertion (p. 50 n.89).
- Recognition, arbitral merits, execution against particular assets, sovereign immunity, and an anti-suit injunction remain different issues. None of these facts determines an asset-specific execution right.
