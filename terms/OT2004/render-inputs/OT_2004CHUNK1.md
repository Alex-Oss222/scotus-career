## Event

Leocal v. Ashcroft, No. 03-583. Merits decision, November 9, 2004. Argued October 12, 2004. The Court reviews the Eleventh Circuit's June 30, 2003 dismissal of a petition challenging the Board's aggravated-felony removal decision. The question is whether Florida driving under the influence causing serious bodily injury, an offense permitting accidental or negligent injury, qualifies as a crime of violence under either part of 18 U.S.C. §16. Leocal seeks correction of the classification and removal order; the Government defends both statutory routes. Continuing immigration consequences preserve the dispute after removal.

## Participation

Chief Justice Stone-Zsela and Justices Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer participated at argument and decision.

## Public Action

The judgment of the Eleventh Circuit is reversed, and the case is remanded.

## Judgment & Remedy

**Judgment:** Reversed and remanded, 9–0.

**Supporting:** Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer.

The reviewing court must return the matter with the binding instruction that Leocal's DUI convictions cannot support the charged section 16 aggravated-felony ground. The agency must enter a conforming disposition and address correction of the continuing immigration consequences. Any independently preserved removal basis must be adjudicated under its own law and proof requirements. The Florida criminal convictions remain valid.

## Opinion Topology

O'CONNOR, J., delivered the opinion of the Court, in which STONE-ZSELA, C. J., and STEVENS, SCALIA, KENNEDY, SOUTER, THOMAS, GINSBURG and BREYER, JJ., joined.

## Holdings

**Authority common to the holdings:** The unanimous opinion of the Court controls each proposition below; all nine Justices join at the stated level of generality.

**Review of the disputed predicate.** A court may decide whether a conviction satisfies the criminal predicate on which 8 U.S.C. §1252(a)(2)(C)'s review restriction depends. Calcano-Martinez v. INS concerned conceded qualifying convictions; it does not allow the agency's disputed classification to establish its own conclusiveness. Leocal disputes that classification, and its continuing consequences permit the Court to resolve it.

**Section 16(a): the offense must require the specified force use.** An offense permitting conviction for accidental or negligent injury without employment of physical force against another's person or property is not a crime of violence under section 16(a). The Government must establish the qualifying conviction through the legal elements and applicable conviction record; injury alone cannot supply the missing requirement.

The Court reads the complete statutory phrase, which concerns force used against another. Driving employs a vehicle, and a collision may cause serious injury, but those facts do not make force employment against another an element of the offense. Bailey v. United States gives statutory use independent significance through its active-employment analysis; its firearm holding supplies an analogy suited to this text. Taylor v. United States requires attention to the offense definition, preventing a reconstruction of the collision from adding an element. Florida's offense permits conviction without the required employment of force, so it fails subsection (a).

**Section 16(b): the specified risk concerns force use during the crime.** A felony qualifies under section 16(b) only if, by its nature, it presents a substantial risk that physical force against another's person or property may be used in committing it. A substantial risk of injury, without the corresponding force-use risk, does not satisfy that definition; Florida's accidental-or-negligent-injury offense therefore fails this route as well.

The Government's alternative would change the risk Congress identified. Both subsections retain the force-use concept, although they ask different questions: one about the offense's elements and the other about the substantial risk inherent in committing it. The danger created by impaired driving establishes a risk of injury; it does not turn unintended injury into employment of force during the crime. Reading the residual provision otherwise would erase language Congress repeated. The sentence condition under 8 U.S.C. §1101(a)(43)(F) is separately satisfied here, but cannot supply the missing crime-of-violence predicate.

An offense requiring recklessness lies outside the minimum-conduct question presented, and its treatment remains open for that reason.

## Precedent Treatment

Bailey v. United States is applied for its contextual active-employment reasoning; its firearm-specific holding retains its existing scope.

Taylor v. United States is applied to require an offense-based inquiry instead of adding elements through the particular accident facts.

Calcano-Martinez v. INS is distinguished because its qualifying criminal predicates were conceded; its treatment of those review restrictions remains intact.

## Law After Decision

Offenses whose minimum elements permit accidental or negligent injury without employment of force against another are excluded from section 16 under both statutory routes. That classification governs the corresponding aggravated-felony inquiry for covered convictions. Courts must distinguish injury causation from the specified force use, and injury risk from the specified risk of force use during the offense.

## Procedure After Action

The case returns to the Eleventh Circuit for proceedings implementing the Court's classification and the agency's conforming disposition. The reviewing court and agency must give effect to the corrective immigration relief stated in the judgment.

## Event

Smith v. Texas, No. 04-5323. Merits decision, November 15, 2004, following merits submission. The Court reviews the Texas Court of Criminal Appeals' denial of state postconviction relief, 132 S.W.3d 407. Smith challenges a heightened relevance screen for his intellectual-functioning and special-education mitigation and the supplemental instruction used with the deliberateness and future-danger special issues. He seeks correction of his penalty judgment; Texas seeks to sustain the denial of relief.

## Participation

Chief Justice Stone-Zsela and Justices Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer participate.

## Public Action

The state judgment is reversed on its mitigation relevance and instructional grounds, and the case is remanded.

## Judgment & Remedy

**Judgment:** Reversed and remanded, 7–2.

**Supporting:** Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Kennedy, Souter, Ginsburg and Breyer.

**Opposing:** Scalia and Thomas.

The state court must resolve genuinely remaining procedural questions and the complete-record effect inquiry. If the mitigation restriction was preserved and no genuinely remaining procedural issue precludes relief, the State bears the burden of showing beyond reasonable doubt that the defect did not contribute to death. If that showing fails, the court must provide constitutionally adequate capital resentencing or a lawful noncapital sentence, fixing a reasonable period for correction and specifying the lawful noncapital consequence if correction does not occur. The guilt judgment remains in place.

## Opinion Topology

SOUTER, J., delivered the opinion of the Court, in which STONE-ZSELA, C. J., and STEVENS, O'CONNOR, KENNEDY, GINSBURG and BREYER, JJ., joined. SCALIA, J., filed a dissenting opinion, in which THOMAS, J., joined.

## Holdings

**Common authority:** The seven Justices joining Souter's opinion directly support every holding below.

**Mitigation relevance.** Supported evidence of reduced capital culpability must receive constitutional consideration even when the defendant has not shown a severe permanent impairment or a causal connection to the crime. Tennard v. Dretke rejects both screening requirements; Graham v. Collins and Johnson v. Texas establish that culpability mitigation may matter independently of deliberateness or future danger. Smith's intellectual-functioning and educational evidence falls within that principle. Relevance gives the juror authority to consider the evidence, without prescribing favorable weight or requiring a categorical disability diagnosis.

**A coherent verdict must give that consideration effect.** When supported mitigation may reduce culpability independently of the factual special issues, the jury must have an authorized, coherent means of giving that judgment effect in a life verdict. A negative special-issue answer cannot serve that purpose if the final instructions simultaneously require it to certify factual reasonable doubt that the juror may not hold.

Penry v. Johnson supplies that distinction between considering evidence and lawfully acting on it. Under Boyde v. California, the Court examines the complete charge and trial context for a reasonable likelihood of restricted mitigating use. Smith's instruction retains the conflict: a juror may accept the facts supporting deliberateness and future danger yet find a death sentence unwarranted on mitigation. A stronger direction to consider mitigation does not reconcile the incompatible certification. The dissent's specific-exception reading cannot eliminate what the final answer still requires the juror to certify. The constitutional requirement protects the independent culpability judgment recognized in Graham and Johnson; the structured factual questions do not exhaust it.

**Error and actual effect remain distinct.** For a preserved original-charge restriction of this kind, the State must establish beyond reasonable doubt on the complete penalty record that the defect did not contribute to the death sentence. If it cannot, constitutionally adequate capital resentencing or a lawful noncapital sentence must follow under the conditional remedy stated in the judgment.

Penry supplies this effect rule; the present case reaches this Court directly from the state judgment. Factual special-issue answers cannot themselves establish the result of an unconflicted mitigation decision. The complete penalty record must be considered. That remaining factual adjudication applies settled law.

## Precedent Treatment

Tennard v. Dretke is reaffirmed and applied to reject the heightened relevance screen.

Penry v. Johnson is reaffirmed and applied to the contradictory verdict mechanism and the separate preserved-error effect inquiry.

Graham v. Collins and Johnson v. Texas are applied for mitigation's independent culpability significance.

Boyde v. California supplies the complete-charge reasonable-likelihood standard used in assessing the instruction.

## Law After Decision

Capital courts must allow supported culpability mitigation both to be considered and to be given lawful effect beyond the factual special issues. A direction to consider it is inadequate when the verdict mechanism retains the identified contradiction. Sentence correction follows the separate whole-record effect inquiry under the State's beyond-reasonable-doubt burden for the preserved restriction.

## Separate Writings

Scalia, joined by Thomas, would affirm. The dissent maintains that the constitutional requirement of mitigation beyond structured capital special issues should be reconsidered, and does not give that broader requirement controlling stare-decisis weight. It also offers an independent reading under the continuing rule: the specific supplemental permission to answer negatively for mitigation qualifies the general factual-answer instructions, allowing the charge to operate coherently as a whole. On that reading there is no reasonable likelihood that jurors lacked authority to give mitigation effect. The dissent therefore opposes the merits correction and effect remand.

## Procedure After Action

The case returns to the Texas Court of Criminal Appeals for the remaining procedural and effect adjudication and, if required, implementation of the conditional sentencing correction. The two constitutional errors are settled for those proceedings.

## Source Notes

No. 04-5323; 132 S.W.3d 407; U.S. Const. amends. VIII, XIV; 28 U.S.C. §1257(a); Boyde v. California, 494 U.S. 370 (1990); Graham v. Collins, January 25, 1993; Johnson v. Texas, June 24, 1993; Penry v. Johnson, June 4, 2001; Tennard v. Dretke, June 24, 2004.

## Event

United States v. $100,348.00 in U.S. Currency, reviewing the Ninth Circuit's January 16, 2004 judgment, 354 F.3d 1110. Merits decision, November 15, 2004, following merits submission. The questions concern Mayzel's right to challenge forfeiture of the entire fund through his own legally protected possession and whether the Government may take the full sum for the reporting violation. The Government seeks $100,348; Mayzel defends the judgment limiting forfeiture to $10,000. The proceeding is governed by the pre-CAFRA regime.

## Participation

Chief Justice Stone-Zsela and Justices Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer participate.

## Public Action

The pertinent judgment of the Ninth Circuit is affirmed.

## Judgment & Remedy

| Constitutional amount | Affirmed, 6–3 | Stone-Zsela, Stevens, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg, Breyer | O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy | Government retains $10,000 and must return $90,348 under the final judgment to the legally entitled claimant |

The district court may enforce return and resolve ministerial implementation. The owner's untimely independent claim remains closed, and the independent forfeiture and fee rulings outside the questions presented remain in place.

## Opinion Topology

THOMAS, J., delivered the opinion of the Court, in which STONE-ZSELA, C. ## Holdings

**A protected possessor may vindicate his own interest.** A lawful possessor whose property rights or enforceable obligations are impaired by punitive forfeiture may challenge the exaction against all property to which that established interest extends. The claimant must establish the interest under applicable property law and the record; another person's title does not by itself defeat the claim. Part I is unanimous.

Mayzel's actual possession and California bailment duties give this claim legal substance beyond physical custody. The entrusted amount is $100,000 within a total fund of $100,348; the full-fund challenge rests on his protected possessory interest, including that bailment. Delivery and care duties survive the unproved purported disclaimer. Austin v. United States directs attention to punitive operation rather than the civil form: deprivation of this claimant's own interest answers the Government's objection that only the titled owner is punished. The interest exists without a completed damages judgment against Mayzel. The owner's procedural default governs the owner's claim while leaving Mayzel's independently established interest subject to adjudication.

**Punitive reporting forfeiture is governed by gross disproportionality.** A partly punitive reporting forfeiture violates the Excessive Fines Clause when the claimant proves that it is grossly disproportionate to the actual offense. Courts compare supported culpability, harm, the property's relation to the wrongdoing and authorized sanctions where relevant, with substantial respect for legislative punishment judgments. Constitutional proportionality receives independent appellate review; supported subsidiary facts receive clear-error review. Part II is unanimous.

Austin supplies coverage; United States v. Bajakajian supplies the constitutional comparison and distinct review standards. United States v. Wagoner County Real Estate supplies the supported-finding discipline and claimant burden, which the Court extends from its real-property setting to this reporting exaction. These considerations retain their contextual operation rather than becoming an exhaustive checklist. A statutory maximum deserves weight but cannot make the separate constitutional inquiry conclusive in the Government's favor.

**The full demand is excessive; the actual $10,000 forfeiture is permissible.** On the supported findings, forfeiture of $100,348 is grossly disproportionate to the reporting offense, while the $10,000 exaction is not. Part III is joined by Stone-Zsela, Stevens, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer.

The district court credited lawful source and found no governmental financial loss. The reporting system suffered informational harm, and Mayzel's false statements support meaningful culpability; the civil forfeiture nevertheless rests on the reporting violation. His criminal reporting acquittal turned on failure to prove knowledge of the reporting duty beyond reasonable doubt and leaves the civil forfeitability judgment intact. The supported wrongdoing and harm do not justify confiscating the full fund. The dissent's reliance on the amount withheld and Congress's strong preventive interest does not authorize measuring that wrong against unproved laundering or other criminal activity.

The hypothetical fine maximum is $5,000 at level 6 or $30,000 at level 12. The lower level requires no knowledge or belief of unlawful proceeds, no reckless disregard of source, lawful proceeds, and intended lawful use; only lawful source was expressly found. The $10,000 exaction survives the constitutional comparison even under the lower benchmark, while full forfeiture fails under either. Thus the missing bracket findings do not require another proportionality hearing. The Court judges the actual exaction, without adopting the lower court's arithmetic compromise as a constitutional formula or a fixed ratio for other cases.

## Precedent Treatment

Austin v. United States is applied for partly punitive coverage and attention to the claimant's actual protected interest.

United States v. Bajakajian is reaffirmed and applied for gross disproportionality, legislative respect and separate legal and factual review; its prior dollar result remains an application to its own findings.

United States v. Wagoner County Real Estate is applied for supported findings and its claimant-burden rule is extended to this punitive reporting forfeiture. It requires additional findings when material, rather than a further hearing over an alternative that cannot change the constitutional result.

## Law After Decision

A legally substantive possessory interest may support a first-party challenge throughout the property it covers despite another person's title. In punitive reporting forfeiture, the claimant bears the gross-disproportionality burden under an actual-offense comparison and independent constitutional review. Here that comparison sustains $10,000 and requires return of the $90,348 balance.

## Separate Writings

Kennedy, joined by O'Connor and Scalia, agrees that Mayzel may protect his own interest and that the common proportionality and review rules govern. He would reverse the reduction and permit full forfeiture. The unreported amount measures the scale of a serious reporting wrong; the information system prevents offenses before the Government can identify their proceeds, and Congress may make forfeiture operate alongside ordinary fines. The smaller amount than in Bajakajian, Mayzel's supported deception and the unsettled entitlement to the lowest hypothetical fine distinguish the earlier application. In the dissent's view those matters leave gross disproportionality unproved, without assuming another crime or treating the $30,000 bracket as a finding.

## Procedure After Action

The affirmed judgment governs disposition of the fund. The Government must return the nonforfeitable balance through the existing proceeding, subject to the district court's authority to enforce the judgment and complete ministerial implementation.

## Event

Anderson v. Spear, Sixth Circuit No. 02-5529, 356 F.3d 651. Merits decision, November 30, 2004, following merits submission. The Court reviews the reversal of summary judgment for Kentucky officials in a challenge to the 500-foot electioneering prohibition, the $50,000 personal campaign-loan ceiling and the complete post-election contribution ban. The candidate seeks declaratory and injunctive relief; officials defend the restrictions as safeguards for voting and against corruption.

## Participation

Chief Justice Stone-Zsela and Justices Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer participate.

## Public Action

The judgment is affirmed in part, vacated in part and remanded.

## Judgment & Remedy

## Holdings

**Ordinary public forums retain protection near polling places.** A campaign-specific restriction on ordinary public streets and sidewalks must be necessary to serve a compelling voting-protection interest and narrowly drawn to achieve it. Kentucky has not justified the actual 500-foot reach and suppression of nonintimidating write-in instructions and advocacy. Part I controls through the seven Justices identified above.

The statute operates on election day at polling places and within 500 feet of the county clerk's office or an entrance to a building containing a voting machine. It covers signs, literature, cards, handbills, petition signatures and vote solicitation. Exit polling is excluded. The private-property exception concerns activity inside a private residence or establishment other than the polling premises by a person with an ownership interest; it does not restore ordinary public-sidewalk speech. Burson v. Freeman supplies the controlling scrutiny proposition, while its divided 100-foot validity judgment cannot justify a different distance and record automatically. Mills v. Alabama protects election-day advocacy, and United States v. Grace preserves ordinary sidewalk status near government premises. Preventing intimidation is compelling, but the State has not shown why nonintimidating communication throughout this broad area must be suppressed. Administrability alone does not establish that fit.

**Initial personal funding and later donor repayment are different transactions.** The State may regulate actual third-party contributions used to repay campaign debt under the governing contribution standard, but the possibility of later repayment does not justify this $50,000 ceiling on a candidate's initial use of personal campaign resources. Part II is unanimous.

Buckley v. Valeo distinguishes the candidate's own spending from a donor's transfer. A later contribution retiring personal debt can confer a personal benefit and carry corruption risk. That concern concerns the later donor transaction; it does not transform all initial personal funding into a contribution or justify suppressing the candidate's own campaign expression at this ceiling.

**The complete post-election contribution ban lacks the required fit.** A State restricting debt-retirement contributions must support its anticorruption justification and show close fit while preserving meaningful political association. Kentucky's categorical prohibition on candidates or agents soliciting or accepting contributions after the general election fails that standard on this record. Part III is unanimous.

Russell v. Burris and Nixon v. Shrink Missouri Government PAC require actual fit rather than an assumed immunity or categorical ban. Federal Election Commission v. Beaumont preserves source-specific circumvention analysis. Winner identification and personal debt retirement can heighten the risk of donor influence, but those interests do not justify closing every lawful post-election contribution channel. Ordinary donor amount, source, true-source and disclosure restrictions retain their independent operation under the governing law.

**Relief must correct the unlawful policies without rewriting state law.** The district court must enter precise declaratory and injunctive relief against the restrictions' adjudicated unlawful operation and determine lawful state-law severability. Agreement that a speech restriction fails does not authorize a universal federal insertion of an express-advocacy-only definition. Part IV's construction ground has the eight direct joins shown above; all nine support its vacatur and the general implementing remedy.

The lower court's universal narrowing construction is therefore vacated. The remedial inquiry must apply permissible construction and severability principles to the enacted provisions while carrying out the invalidations and scope of relief stated in the judgment.

## Precedent Treatment

Burson v. Freeman is applied for its independently majority-supported scrutiny rule; its fractured judgment retains its bounded force without becoming a numerical safe harbor.

Mills v. Alabama and United States v. Grace are applied for election-day advocacy and the continuing public-forum character of ordinary sidewalks.

Buckley v. Valeo is applied to distinguish initial personal campaign financing from later donor contributions.

Russell v. Burris and Nixon v. Shrink Missouri Government PAC are reaffirmed and applied for justified, closely fitted contribution restrictions preserving meaningful advocacy.

Federal Election Commission v. Beaumont is applied for actual source and circumvention analysis in assessing contribution regulation.

## Law After Decision

The challenged public-forum perimeter, personal-loan ceiling and complete post-election contribution prohibition are unenforceable at their adjudicated reach. Protection of voting remains a compelling objective requiring fit to the actual speech restriction. Candidate financing and later debt-repayment contributions retain their distinct constitutional treatment, with lawful donor safeguards operating independently. Precise relief and permissible state-law severability implement these rules.

## Separate Writings

Scalia agrees with the judgment and joins the financing and remedial parts. His polling-environs position distinguishes the historically administered immediate environs during voting from a 500-foot reach across ordinary neighborhood streets. The record supplies insufficient historical or functional justification for extending his Burson approach that far, so he reaches invalidity without joining the Court's full forum-and-strict-scrutiny analysis.

Thomas likewise joins the financial and remedial parts and agrees with the speech judgment. His own historical and strict-scrutiny approach protects direct political communication against this broad exclusion, drawing on his positions in McIntyre and Watchtower. That sufficient ground does not adopt Part I's complete forum methodology.

## Procedure After Action

The case returns for entry of a conforming judgment, precise declaratory and injunctive terms, and lawful severability. The district court must preserve separately lawful voting and contribution safeguards and enforce the decree's prohibitions against renewed unlawful application.

## Event

Koons Buick Pontiac GMC, Inc. v. Nigh, No. 03-377. Merits decision, November 30, 2004. Argued October 5, 2004. The Court reviews the Fourth Circuit's affirmance, 319 F.3d 119, of $24,192.80 in Truth in Lending Act statutory damages for Nigh's used-truck credit transaction. The question is whether the 1995 amendment to 15 U.S.C. §1640(a)(2)(A) removed the $1,000 ceiling for the individual personal-property credit category in clause (i). The dealer seeks application of that ceiling; Nigh defends twice-finance-charge recovery without it.

## Participation

Chief Justice Stone-Zsela and Justices Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer participated at argument and decision.

## Public Action

The judgment is reversed on the statutory-damages component, and the case is remanded.

## Judgment & Remedy

**Judgment:** Reversed and remanded, 8–1, for application of the $1,000 statutory ceiling.

**Supporting:** Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer.

**Opposing:** Scalia.

The district court must enter a conforming statutory-damages judgment, identify independently sustained components that remain in place, and recalculate only amounts actually affected. Any consequential fee calculation proceeds under its own law; independent actual damages and fee entitlement retain their separate statutory bases.

## Opinion Topology

GINSBURG, J., delivered the opinion of the Court, in which STONE-ZSELA, C. J., and STEVENS, O'CONNOR, KENNEDY, SOUTER, THOMAS and BREYER, JJ., joined. SCALIA, J., filed a dissenting opinion.

## Holdings

**The retained general ceiling governs clause (i).** Under the applicable version of 15 U.S.C. §1640(a)(2)(A), individual statutory damages calculated under clause (i) remain within the $100-to-$1,000 range, except where the statute expressly provides another range. Clause (iii) supplies $200 to $2,000 for an individual action involving credit not under an open-end plan and secured by real property or a dwelling. Nigh's personal-property credit transaction falls under the retained $1,000 ceiling.

**Authority:** Ginsburg's opinion, joined in full by Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas and Breyer, controls this construction and its remedy.

The statute retains a range for the subparagraph, while its new clause sets a distinct range for a defined credit category. A general rule and a specific qualification can operate together. The successive enacted versions confirm that Congress added the specified category's range while retaining the earlier language. The contrary interpretation relies on the general range's location after the lease clause and the presence of three separate alternatives. But location does not require reducing the retained subparagraph reference to the adjacent clause when both monetary ranges can be given effect according to their scopes.

Lamie v. United States Trustee requires enforcement of enacted eligibility and structure; the Court's reading inserts no omitted ceiling and deletes no specific range. Hartford Underwriters Insurance Co. v. Union Planters Bank likewise requires an enacted entitlement before a remedy. Those principles resolve the amount through statutory language rather than a preferred level of deterrence. The $24,192.80 statutory award therefore exceeds the authorized ceiling. Actual damages under subsection (a)(1), and costs and reasonable fees under subsection (a)(3), remain governed by their distinct provisions.

## Precedent Treatment

Lamie v. United States Trustee is applied for enforcement of enacted text and structure; the general/specific construction gives effect to both ranges rather than repairing the statute based on remedial preference.

Hartford Underwriters Insurance Co. v. Union Planters Bank is applied for the requirement that the remedy remain within its enacted entitlement.

## Law After Decision

The operative individual-action provision retains the $100-to-$1,000 range for clause (i) personal-property credit, with the expressly different clause (iii) range governing its defined closed-end real-property-or-dwelling category. The statutory cap controls that damages component while the separate bases for other recovery continue to govern their own awards.

## Separate Writings

Scalia would affirm the statutory award. In his reading, the amended three-clause arrangement leaves the old exception attached to the lease provision, while clause (i) supplies twice the finance charge without that ceiling. He agrees that subparagraph identifies (A), but disputes that the word determines how far the exception reaches. An uncapped twice-finance-charge formula is a plausible enacted choice; the prior arrangement and a preferred remedial policy cannot move a clause-bound qualification across the amended alternatives. His disagreement concerns the statutory amount reviewed here.

## Event

San Diego v. Roe, No. 03-1669. Merits decision, December 6, 2004, after merits submission. On review of the Ninth Circuit's judgment, 356 F.3d 1108, reversing dismissal of Roe's constitutional employment-speech claim.

The question is whether the City violated the First Amendment by terminating a police officer for selling sexually explicit recordings and police-related merchandise, and which public-employee speech framework governs this outside expression. The City seeks restoration of dismissal; Roe seeks adjudication of his retaliation claim. ## Participation

Chief Justice Stone-Zsela and Justices Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer participated in submission and decision.

## Public Action

The Court reverses the Ninth Circuit's judgment on the reviewed speech theory.

## Judgment & Remedy

**Reversed, 8–1.**

**Supporting:** Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer.
**Opposing:** Stone-Zsela.

## Opinion Topology

STEVENS, J., delivered the opinion of the Court, in which O'CONNOR, SCALIA, KENNEDY, SOUTER, THOMAS, GINSBURG and BREYER, JJ., joined. STONE-ZSELA, C. J., filed a dissenting opinion.

## Holdings

#### The police-associated commercial performance fails the public-concern threshold

**Holding and operative rule.** Public availability, compensation and lawful sexual content do not themselves establish that a public employee's expression addresses a matter of public concern. On the content, form and context here, Roe's deliberately police-associated commercial performance fails that threshold, so the First Amendment employment-speech claim does not require Pickering balancing and was properly dismissed.

**Authority.** Stevens's opinion of the Court, adopted in full by Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer, supplies eight direct votes for this rule, its application and the remedy.

**Controlling explanation.** The Court holds that the relevant inquiry concerns the expression actually presented. Connick v. Myers requires attention to content, form and context. Roe used police-associated presentation and his claimed law-enforcement status to market an explicit performance; making that performance available to paying members of the public did not turn it into commentary on a matter of public concern. Pickering v. Board of Education supplies the balance between protected expression and employment interests after the threshold is met.

United States v. National Treasury Employees Union protects citizen expression beyond political subjects and workplace criticism, and requires adequate justification for a broad prospective restriction on unrelated expression. Its protection of that expression does not place this deliberate commercial use of police status within the same category. The asserted analogy therefore fails at the contextual threshold. The determination rests on this presentation's content and official association, not sexual subject matter or payment alone. Waters v. Churchill addresses genuine employer grounds and reasonable accounts of speech; deciding the antecedent protection question here requires no finding of actual operational disruption. The dismissal follows from the legal character of the expression, rather than acceptance of an unproved employer account of harm.

## Precedent Treatment

- Connick v. Myers, 461 U.S. 138: applies its content, form and context inquiry to the particular police-associated performance; its public-concern threshold remains operative.
- Pickering v. Board of Education, 391 U.S. 563: preserves employment balancing for protected public-concern expression; that stage is not triggered here.
- United States v. National Treasury Employees Union, February 22, 1995: distinguishes its protection of unrelated citizen expression and its justification requirement for a broad prospective ban; neither rule is displaced.
- Waters v. Churchill, May 31, 1994: preserves its genuine-and-reasonable-account, actual protected-speech and causation rules.

## Law After Decision

The public-concern inquiry applies to the actual content, form and context of outside employee expression. Lawful commercial distribution does not itself require employment balancing when a deliberately police-associated performance lacks public-concern character. NTEU's protection of unrelated citizen expression and its rule for broad prospective restrictions continue to govern their own settings.

## Separate Writings

Chief Justice Stone-Zsela dissents. He would affirm the removal of categorical dismissal and return the claim for the ordinary employment-speech inquiry. In his view, lawful expression addressed to an outside audience does not lose protection merely because it is sold or sexually explicit. The police association makes the City's employment justification relevant; it does not establish the sufficiency of that justification at the pleading stage.

He would extend NTEU's citizen-expression reasoning to this particular-publication setting while applying ordinary Pickering balancing, not automatically the heavier burden applicable to a broad prospective ban. ## Event

Theofel v. Farey-Jones, Ninth Circuit Nos. 02-15742 and 03-15301, 359 F.3d 1066. Merits decision, December 6, 2004, after merits submission, reviewing the amended appellate judgment of February 17, 2004.

The granted questions concern whether provider-retained email serving a backup function remains in electronic storage after delivery or recipient access, and whether disclosure consciously procured through a materially deceptive invocation of patently invalid compulsory process establishes provider authorization. The users seek restoration of their statutory claims; the accessing parties seek dismissal on the storage and authorization grounds.

## Participation

Chief Justice Stone-Zsela and Justices Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer participated in submission and decision.

## Public Action

The Court affirms rejection of both categorical grounds for dismissal of the Stored Communications Act claims and remands.

## Judgment & Remedy

**Affirmed and remanded, 9–0.**

**Supporting:** Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer.

The appellate reversal of the SCA dismissal stands on both questions granted. The district court must adjudicate the actual backup function, access and conscious procurement, the remaining statutory conditions and any supported defenses and relief. The separate Wiretap Act, computer-fraud, state-law and petitioning-immunity dispositions remain outside this limited review.

## Opinion Topology

STONE-ZSELA, C. J., delivered the opinion of the Court, in which STEVENS, O'CONNOR, SCALIA, KENNEDY, SOUTER, THOMAS, GINSBURG and BREYER, JJ., joined.

## Holdings

**Common authority.** Stone-Zsela's opinion of the Court, adopted in full by Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer, supplies nine direct votes for both holdings and their complete explanations.

#### Delivery does not end genuine backup protection

**Holding and operative rule.** Section 2510(17)(B) covers storage by an electronic communication service for backup protection of a communication even after delivery or recipient access, when the retained copy actually serves that function. The claimant must prove the backup function by a preponderance; continued possession, potential usefulness or a sole permanent archive without that function does not suffice.

**Controlling explanation.** The Court holds that the two electronic-storage clauses perform separate work. Clause (A) concerns temporary intermediate storage incidental to transmission. Clause (B) refers to backup protection of the communication; it does not import clause (A)'s temporal storage condition. Reading or delivering the message therefore does not itself remove a functioning backup copy from clause (B).

The competing construction invokes the Act's remote-computing provisions. Those provisions retain work because a remote computing service is not invariably an electronic communication service and retained messages do not invariably serve a backup purpose. Requiring the actual statutory function preserves those distinctions without adding an unopened-message limitation. Konop v. Hawaiian Airlines separated later retrieval from contemporaneous interception and expressly assumed electronic storage for its different consent question. It thus preserves the need to decide this definition on its own terms. The pleaded backup copies may qualify; ordinary proceedings must establish their actual function.

#### Deliberately induced disclosure is not conclusive provider authorization

**Holding and operative rule.** A requesting party cannot establish the provider-authorization exception in §2701(c)(1) merely by showing physical disclosure when that party consciously procured it through a materially deceptive invocation of patently invalid compulsory process going to the essential nature of the access. This rule requires knowing procurement, not a merely negligent misunderstanding, ordinary process irregularity or collateral deception; the claimant retains the burden of proving unauthorized access and the statutory mental state, subject to supported authorization and good-faith defenses.

**Controlling explanation.** The Court holds that a provider's act of disclosure and its legal authorization are distinct. On the complaint's account, the requesting parties knowingly used the appearance of compulsory authority to obtain access the process did not authorize. The provider's opportunity to object does not transform such deliberately induced permission into genuine authorization. A categorical rule making every disclosure conclusive would allow the very deception alleged to extinguish the statutory protection.

Konop requires the actual predicates of permission for the access and communication at issue. Its §2701(c)(2) actual-user rule concerns a different exception, but the same provision-specific discipline prevents an act of disclosure from settling §2701(c)(1) without examining the alleged knowing procurement. The amended appellate opinion requires conscious procurement and rejects negligence-based constructive knowledge. That qualification governs this affirmance. The users must establish each access, each defendant's mental state and the remaining §2707 conditions; a failed permission theory does not complete the violation or remedy inquiry.

## Precedent Treatment

- Konop v. Hawaiian Airlines, January 27, 2003: preserves its contemporaneous-interception holding and its actual-user requirements under §2701(c)(2); resolves electronic storage, which Konop assumed, and applies predicate-specific consent analysis to the distinct provider exception.
- Hartford Underwriters Insurance Co. v. Union Planters Bank, 530 U.S. 1: applies its attention to separate enacted actors, permissions and remedy conditions as reflected in Konop; its bankruptcy holding remains unchanged.

## Law After Decision

Delivered or opened messages may remain within the SCA when their provider-retained copies actually serve statutory backup protection. In claims based on knowing, materially deceptive procurement of access through patently invalid compulsory process, physical provider disclosure is not by itself a complete authorization answer. The two constructions govern the defined storage and procurement categories while leaving proof of their factual conditions, the other statutory predicates and authorized remedies to ordinary adjudication.

## Holdings

#### Descriptive fair use does not require disproving the infringement claimant's confusion element

**Holding and operative rule.** The infringement claimant bears the burden of proving likelihood of confusion, including when its registration is incontestable; a defendant invoking §1115(b)(4) must establish by the ordinary civil standard use otherwise than as a mark, fairly and in good faith, only to describe its goods or services or their geographic origin. Park 'N Fly v. Dollar Park and Fly gives incontestability its statutory force while preserving express defenses. Qualitex v. Jacobson Products and TrafFix Devices v. Marketing Displays maintain source-identifying protection together with independent statutory limits. Those principles preserve both Lasting's protection and the enacted room for fair description. Fairness, good faith and use otherwise than as a mark remain real requirements; evidence of confusion may illuminate them without becoming a shifted infringement burden.

KP's role as declaratory plaintiff does not reverse the substantive burdens. The actual manner of presentation must be evaluated under the correct allocation. If undisputed facts establish the defense, judgment must protect that particular fair use; otherwise ordinary proceedings resolve the disputed statutory conditions and corresponding relief.

## Event

Brosseau v. Haugen, No. 03-1261. Merits decision, December 13, 2004, after merits submission. On review of the Ninth Circuit's judgment, 339 F.3d 857, reversing summary judgment for Brosseau on Haugen's individual Fourth Amendment damages claim under 42 U.S.C. §1983.

## Opinion Topology

KENNEDY, J., delivered the opinion of the Court, in which O'CONNOR, SCALIA, SOUTER, THOMAS, GINSBURG and BREYER, JJ., joined. STEVENS, J., and STONE-ZSELA, C. J., each filed a dissenting opinion.

## Holdings

#### The accepted vehicle-escape setting lacked sufficiently particular preconduct notice

**Holding and operative rule.** Brosseau has individual qualified immunity because law existing before February 21, 1999 did not make the illegality of this prospective-risk response sufficiently apparent on the plaintiff-favorable account: determined resistance followed by imminent vehicle escape in a confined setting near occupied cars, with no already moving Jeep, obstructed escape route, identified person in its path or supported immediate weapon threat. The inquiry requires contextual legal notice, permits an obvious application of an established rule without a factual replica, and does not confer a categorical vehicle or flight exception.

**Authority.** Kennedy's opinion of the Court, adopted in full by O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer, supplies seven direct votes for this immunity application and its complete rationale.

**Controlling explanation.** The Court holds that the legal clarity of the governing rule is distinct from the factual reasonableness of the force. Tennessee v. Garner supplies the serious-threat requirement for deadly force against a fleeing suspect; Graham v. Connor supplies the contextual merits inquiry. Harlow v. Fitzgerald and Anderson v. Creighton preserve a separate objective defense where preconduct law did not make the particular legal boundary sufficiently clear. Graham's allowance for urgent circumstances and reasonable factual perceptions does not itself answer that legal-notice question.

On the adverse assumptions, Brosseau's concern was a forecast of danger from an imminent departure, not an established collision or an officer standing in the Jeep's path. Smith v. Freland and Cole v. Bone involved substantially greater demonstrated driving danger. They do not establish this shooting's lawfulness, but their differing applications, together with Estate of Starks, show why Garner's general limit did not plainly settle this anticipatory-force boundary. The Court accepts the favorable escape-path and timing account; it does not manufacture legal uncertainty by choosing disputed facts for the officer.

An established principle may give fair warning through an obvious application. Here the combination of determined resistance, imminent vehicular departure and nearby occupied cars makes the precise prospective-risk question insufficiently clear under the preconduct authorities. Saucier v. Katz retains the existing defense without a mandatory merits-first sequence, and Hope v. Pelzer rejects a demand for a factual twin while preserving context. Those later decisions explain the present inquiry, but neither supplies notice for the 1999 conduct. The limited grant permits resolution of immunity while leaving the ultimate force merits outside this review.

## Precedent Treatment

- Harlow v. Fitzgerald, 457 U.S. 800: applies the existing objective individual-immunity defense to the conduct-date legal question.
- Anderson v. Creighton, 483 U.S. 635: applies particularized legal clarity without demanding an identical prior case.
- Tennessee v. Garner, 471 U.S. 1: preserves the serious-threat condition for deadly force against a fleeing suspect; its general rule does not give sufficiently particular notice for this prospective-risk application.
- Graham v. Connor, 490 U.S. 386: preserves the complete contextual force inquiry and its attention to reasonable factual perceptions; force legality and the clarity inquiry retain separate work.
- Saucier v. Katz, June 18, 2001: applies the retained objective defense and its use of proper factual assumptions; the prior remand established no mandatory merits-first sequence.
- Hope v. Pelzer, June 27, 2002: applies its rejection of a factual-replica requirement and its attention to context; its later date supplies no February 1999 notice.
- Johnson v. Jones, June 12, 1995: preserves the distinction between a separable legal immunity issue on assumed facts and appellate resolution of factual sufficiency.

## Law After Decision

The existing Harlow–Anderson defense resolves this individual claim because the accepted anticipatory vehicle-escape setting lacked sufficiently clear preconduct legal guidance. Lower courts must use the proper plaintiff-favorable facts and assess conduct-date notice in context, including whether established principles make the application obvious. The rule neither requires an identical prior case nor treats vehicle escape as automatic constitutional permission.

## Separate Writings

Justice Stevens dissents. He would affirm the appellate reversal of summary judgment and return the claim for ordinary proceedings under existing law. Garner already gives the governing rule: escape alone does not justify deadly force without the requisite serious-threat basis. On the assumed clear route, stationary Jeep and absence of an identified immediate threat, the proposed future danger does not make that boundary uncertain. Smith and Cole involved materially more dangerous driving and therefore do not erase Garner's notice here. He regards the officer's reasonable assessment of the possible accident and the surrounding circumstances as matters for lawful adjudication, not a reason to end the claim on this assumed account. His position applies the existing defense; it does not make every historical-fact dispute defeat a separable legal immunity ruling.

Chief Justice Stone-Zsela separately dissents. He would expressly replace the broad objective legal-notice defense with a defense of actual reasonable reliance on authoritative law and affirm the removal of summary judgment. An official invoking his proposed defense would have to prove, by a preponderance, identified binding precedent or enacted authority directly authorizing the conduct, genuine reliance on it, and a reasonable basis for treating it as valid and applicable. Legal silence, a department's practice and the absence of a materially identical case would not suffice. A policy or supervisor's instruction would qualify only insofar as it accurately conveyed such identified authority and the official reasonably relied on it.

He reasons that Graham's complete force standard already accommodates urgency, reasonable factual mistakes and lawful discretion. An additional defense that can defeat an established violation without actual reliance on law authorizing the conduct is disconnected from the justification for protecting legal reliance. His proposal would therefore replace the broader Harlow–Anderson rule, preserve Graham and Garner in the force inquiry, and retain Hope's rejection of factual identity. Saucier did not previously adopt that replacement. Brosseau has shown neither directly authorizing authority nor actual reasonable reliance on this record. Further proceedings would apply the complete force rule, resolve genuinely material facts and any supported reliance defense, and require the remaining causation, injury and damages predicates. The proposal concerns the personal defense within the existing cause of action; absolute functional immunity, sovereign immunity and independently sourced institutional liability are outside it. The ultimate force merits remain outside the limited grant.

## Procedure After Action

The case returns for implementation of Brosseau's personal qualified-immunity judgment, ending the reviewed individual federal damages claim. The ultimate force merits remain outside the grant, and the separate municipal and state-law branches retain their existing status.

## Event

Devenpeck v. Alford, No. 03-710. On writ of certiorari to the United States Court of Appeals for the Ninth Circuit; argued November 8, 2004; decided December 13, 2004. The judgment under review, 333 F.3d 972, applied a closely-related-offense requirement to alternative arrest grounds. The question is whether a different offense supported by facts known at arrest may supply objective justification, and what probable-cause and relief questions remain.

## Participation

Chief Justice Stone-Zsela and Justices Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer participated.

## Public Action

The Court rejects the closely-related-offense requirement and remands for assessment of the actual alternative grounds and remaining preserved issues.

## Judgment & Remedy

**Judgment: Reversed and remanded; eight votes for reversal, one for vacatur, and all nine for remand.**

**Supporting reversal:** Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer.

**Supporting vacatur of the challenged ruling and remand:** Stone-Zsela.

All nine agree that the closely-related-offense requirement must be set aside and that the actual alternatives and remaining preserved issues require adjudication on remand. The officers' request for present judgment on their defenses is denied.

## Opinion Topology

O'CONNOR, J., delivered the opinion of the Court, in which STEVENS, SCALIA, KENNEDY, SOUTER, THOMAS, GINSBURG and BREYER, JJ., joined. STONE-ZSELA, C. J., joined the substantive governing-rule and remand portions; he would vacate the challenged ruling rather than join the formal reversal.

| Writing | Author | Joined by | Relationship to judgment | Scope joined |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Opinion of the Court | O'Connor | Stevens, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer in full; Stone-Zsela in the substantive governing-rule and remand portions | Eight-Justice reversal and unanimous remand | Eight Associates join throughout; Stone-Zsela joins the complete legal rule, custody qualification and remand grounds, withholding the formal reversal label |

## Holdings

**Controlling rule.** An arrest may rest on an offense different from, and not closely related to, the officer's announced offense if the facts actually known when the seizure occurred establish probable cause under that offense's elements. The alternative ground must justify the restraint actually imposed; later-acquired evidence cannot supply earlier probable cause, and greater detention remains subject to its independent constitutional limits.

**Authority.** O'Connor's opinion states the common governing rule and remand grounds joined by all nine participating Justices. The formal judgment distinction is stated in Opinion Topology.

**Controlling explanation.** The Court holds that objective legal authority turns on the contemporaneous facts and the applicable offense, rather than the officer's chosen label. *Whren v. United States*, which makes an initial stop on probable cause of an observed violation presumptively reasonable without regard to the officer's motive alone and permits rebuttal only on specific objective evidence that traffic authority was used as an instrument for an unrelated investigation, supplies the governing objective principle; no such instrumental-use claim is presented, because the question here is which offense supplies the objective basis. *Maryland v. Pringle*, which requires a supported inference from facts known at arrest, preserves the actual factual and legal work that principle demands. A closely-related-offense requirement would make identical known facts support one officer's seizure and defeat another's merely because the officers named different offenses.

Alford's objection to post hoc justification identifies a real limit but does not justify the circuit's relation rule. The officers must identify an actual alternative offense and contemporaneously known facts sufficient to establish probable cause for it. A later discovery or a merely conceivable offense will not do. The Ninth Circuit excluded the impersonation and obstruction alternatives under its erroneous legal test; their actual application must now be decided on the proper factual assumptions, with genuinely necessary factual issues returned to the district court.

*Atwater v. City of Lago Vista*, which requires an actual enforcement function for added station transport and confinement for an observed fine-only misdemeanor, and *Ricci v. Village of Arlington Heights*, which supplies the corresponding civil-licensing rule, preserve independent assessment of greater restraint. In those settings, supported identification, appearance, safety, cessation of a continuing violation, protection of evidence or necessary processing may justify added custody; those possibilities are nonexclusive and not presumed, and neither a universal citation duty nor a least-intrusive-means rule follows. Any properly presented custody issue and personal immunity remain ordinary remand work under their governing rules. Immunity's legal-notice inquiry uses the November 22, 1997 conduct date.

## Precedent Treatment

- *Whren v. United States*, 517 U.S. 806: apply its objective-authority principle to alternative offense grounds while preserving independent limits on manner, scope and duration.
- *Maryland v. Pringle*, 540 U.S. 366: apply its requirement of a grounded inference from contemporaneously known facts; the alternate label alone supplies no factual justification.
- *Atwater v. City of Lago Vista*, 532 U.S. 318: preserve the actual-enforcement requirement for added station transport and confinement for observed fine-only criminal offenses.
- *Ricci v. Village of Arlington Heights*: preserve the corresponding actual-enforcement requirement for fine-only civil licensing custody, including its rejection of categorical arrest immunity and universal citation or least-intrusive-means duties.
- *Harlow v. Fitzgerald*, 457 U.S. 800, and *Anderson v. Creighton*, 483 U.S. 635: retain the separate conduct-date personal-immunity inquiry on remand.

## Law After Decision

Courts assessing an alternative ground for arrest examine the actual offense elements and facts known when the seizure occurred, without requiring a relationship to the announced offense. The rule removes a legal-label restriction while retaining the distinct justification required for the restraint imposed. The actual impersonation and obstruction applications, any properly presented additional-custody issue, and personal immunity proceed under those settled standards.

## Procedure After Action

## Event

Florida v. Nixon, No. 03-931. On writ of certiorari to the Supreme Court of Florida; submitted on full merits briefing; decided December 13, 2004. The judgment under review, 857 So. 2d 172, granted a new trial in state postconviction proceedings because counsel conceded guilt without express consent. The question is whether a disclosed capital-trial concession, when the defendant neither agrees nor objects and counsel continues substantive advocacy, automatically establishes ineffective assistance.

## Participation

Chief Justice Stone-Zsela and Justices Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer participated.

## Public Action

The Court reverses the automatic new-trial judgment and remands.

## Judgment & Remedy

**Judgment: Reversed and remanded, 9–0.**

**Supporting:** Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer.

The mandate removes the consent-based automatic new-trial command. The state court must resolve the properly preserved ordinary ineffective-assistance claim, including necessary consultation or preparation questions, and provide the corrective proceeding warranted by any proved constitutional defect.

## Opinion Topology

BREYER, J., delivered the opinion of the Court, in which STONE-ZSELA, C. J., and STEVENS, O'CONNOR, SCALIA, KENNEDY, SOUTER, THOMAS and GINSBURG, JJ., joined.

## Holdings

**Controlling rule.** When capital defense counsel adequately discusses a concession strategy with the defendant, the defendant neither agrees nor objects, and counsel continues meaningful adversarial representation in an actual trial, the absence of express consent alone establishes neither deficient performance nor presumed prejudice. The defendant must prove objectively deficient performance and a reasonable probability of a different result under *Strickland v. Washington*, which supplies the ordinary performance-and-prejudice inquiry.

**Authority.** Breyer's opinion of the Court, joined in full by all other participating Justices, controls the rule, its silent-client limitation, and the remand.

**Controlling explanation.** The Court holds that a disclosed trial strategy must be assessed by what counsel actually did and the trial protections that remained. *Bell v. Cone*, which distinguishes particular choices within substantive advocacy from abandonment of the adversarial role, applies to Corin's continued evidentiary and instruction advocacy and substantial penalty presentation. *United States v. Cronic*, which permits presumed prejudice for recognized complete-denial or complete-failure circumstances, does not turn this concession alone into complete abandonment.

Nixon's argument treats the concession as a guilty plea requiring a personal waiver. *Boykin v. Alabama*, which protects the rights surrendered by a guilty plea, addresses a different legal act. Nixon entered no plea; the prosecution had to prove guilt and the jury had to return its verdict after trial. *Brookhart v. Janis*, which disallows an abbreviated proceeding imposed contrary to the accused's expressed trial choice, likewise differs from this record of consultation and silence. The Court does not resolve a concession over an expressed contrary trial objective, because that question is outside this record and the question decided.

Ordinary review examines the actual investigation, consultation and integrated guilt-and-penalty strategy under trial-era professional standards. Strong prosecution evidence can make a concession intelligible, but neither that evidence nor a strategy label excuses uninformed surrender. *Williams v. Taylor*, which rejects an additional fundamental-unfairness hurdle for lawful-outcome prejudice, leaves the defendant with the ordinary reasonable-probability burden. A court may resolve a properly supported dispositive performance or prejudice prong. Nixon's preserved ordinary allegations, including the challenge concerning unexamined evidentiary inconsistencies, remain for that assessment; the categorical consent rule cannot support the ordered new trial.

## Precedent Treatment

- *Strickland v. Washington*, 466 U.S. 668: apply separate reasonable-performance and reasonable-probability-prejudice requirements to the disclosed capital concession strategy; consultation, investigation and professional judgment retain their independent importance.
- *United States v. Cronic*, 466 U.S. 648: preserve recognized presumed-prejudice categories while rejecting the claim that this concession alone constitutes complete adversarial failure.
- *Bell v. Cone*, 535 U.S. 685: apply the distinction between particular challenged choices and substantive abandonment. Its separate federal-habeas review conditions do not govern this direct review of a state judgment.
- *Williams v. Taylor*, 529 U.S. 362: apply ordinary lawful-outcome prejudice without an additional fundamental-unfairness requirement.
- *Boykin v. Alabama*, 395 U.S. 238: preserve personal waiver requirements for an actual guilty plea; a concession in a continuing trial does not itself enter that plea.
- *Brookhart v. Janis*, 384 U.S. 1: distinguish a materially abbreviated proceeding imposed against the accused's expressed trial choice from the disclosed strategy and unresponsive client presented here.

## Law After Decision

The ordinary ineffective-assistance framework governs disclosed capital concession strategies when the defendant remains unresponsive and counsel continues meaningful adversarial representation. Absence of affirmative consent supplies no automatic deficiency or prejudice rule. Courts assess the real preparation and advocacy, using professional standards applicable when counsel acted, and order the corrective trial or sentencing proceeding that a proved constitutional defect and the applicable postconviction remedy require.

## Procedure After Action

On remand, the state court applies the ordinary performance and reasonable-probability-prejudice requirements to the preserved allegations, using the supported record and trial-era professional standards. A supported dispositive prong may resolve a claim. A proved defect requires the appropriate corrective trial or sentencing proceeding under the applicable state postconviction remedy.

## Event

Kowalski v. Tesmer, No. 03-407. On writ of certiorari to the United States Court of Appeals for the Sixth Circuit; argued October 4, 2004; decided December 13, 2004. The en banc judgment, 333 F.3d 683, permitted attorneys to assert future indigent clients' constitutional rights and invalidated Michigan's appointment restriction. The question is whether these attorneys may assert the rights of unidentified future indigent clients seeking leave to appeal after guilty or no-contest pleas.

## Participation

Chief Justice Stone-Zsela and Justices Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer participated.

## Public Action

The Court reverses the judgment insofar as it rests on the attorneys' third-party claim and remands for dismissal of that claim and conforming relief.

## Judgment & Remedy

**Judgment: Reversed and remanded as to the attorneys' third-party claim, 6–3.**

**Supporting:** Stone-Zsela, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Thomas and Breyer.

**Opposing:** Stevens, Souter and Ginsburg.

The district court must dismiss that claim and remove relief supported only by it, identifying any independently surviving basis before altering other relief. The individual plaintiffs' separate abstention disposition remains undisturbed.

## Opinion Topology

STONE-ZSELA, C. J., delivered the opinion of the Court, in which O'CONNOR, SCALIA, KENNEDY, THOMAS and BREYER, JJ., joined. GINSBURG, J., filed a dissenting opinion, in which STEVENS and SOUTER, JJ., joined.

## Holdings

**Controlling rule.** Attorneys' anticipated representation of unidentified future clients does not establish the close relationship required to litigate those clients' constitutional rights when no concrete existing or specifically prevented representation is shown. Practical barriers faced by indigent litigants require serious consideration, but cannot by themselves supply that missing relationship.

**Authority.** Stone-Zsela's opinion of the Court, joined in full by the other members of the six-Justice coalition of Stone-Zsela, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Thomas and Breyer, controls this relationship holding and its remedy.

**Controlling explanation.** The Court holds that personal injury and entitlement to assert another person's rights are distinct. *Powers v. Ohio*, which requires personal injury, a close relationship and a hindrance to the right-holder's own suit, gives each inquiry independent work. These attorneys allege that Michigan's restriction reduced paid appointments in the regular strict-rotation appellate practice they intend to continue. The Court assumes their personal Article III stake for this decision; their claim fails on the separate relationship requirement.

*Caplin & Drysdale v. United States*, which involved an actual client, and *Department of Labor v. Triplett*, which involved known claimants and enforcement against the attorney, do not equate professional readiness with a concrete client connection. *Campbell v. Louisiana*, which involved a defendant and the excluded jurors connected to his indictment, and *Miller v. Albright*, which permitted a daughter's particular claim concerning her father's citizenship rights, likewise preserve the relationship inquiry.

The contrary position has force: the challenged restriction prevents appointments and makes the right to counsel difficult to assert without counsel. But the obstruction alleged here does not identify a particular representation prevented or burdened by the rule. The prospective-relationship permissions recognized in *Craig v. Boren* and *Singleton v. Wulff*, which turn on the particular relationship and impediment, do not abolish that inquiry for lawyers asserting unascertained future clients' rights. Because this relationship defect suffices, the Court need not decide the full hindrance question. The constitutional entitlement to appointed counsel is not reached because the attorneys fail the antecedent third-party requirement. Existing clients, concrete prevented representations and indigent litigants asserting their own rights remain governed by their applicable standards.

## Precedent Treatment

- *Powers v. Ohio*, 499 U.S. 400: apply distinct personal-injury, relationship and hindrance requirements; assumed personal injury does not establish the relationship.
- *Craig v. Boren*, 429 U.S. 190, and *Singleton v. Wulff*, 428 U.S. 106: retain their contextual prospective-relationship permissions; neither establishes a general professional exception for an unidentified future clientele.
- *Caplin & Drysdale v. United States*, 491 U.S. 617: distinguish the concrete existing client whose rights the attorney asserted.
- *Department of Labor v. Triplett*, 494 U.S. 715: distinguish known claimants and enforcement against the attorney from anticipated future retainers.
- *Campbell v. Louisiana*, 523 U.S. 392: preserve its concrete defendant-juror connection and its separate own-right route.
- *Miller v. Albright*, 523 U.S. 420: preserve the particular daughter-father permission and the distinct relationship and obstacle inquiries.

## Law After Decision

A lawyer's anticipated future representation and a pleaded economic stake in appointments do not alone authorize litigation of unidentified clients' constitutional rights. The relationship requirement remains an independent threshold. The counsel entitlement for Michigan's initial plea-based leave application remains open; a proper claimant's own case proceeds under its own jurisdictional and procedural rules.

## Separate Writings

GINSBURG, J., dissenting, joined by STEVENS and SOUTER, JJ. The dissent would permit these attorneys' third-party route and return only for genuinely unresolved preserved matters within the limited review. A present-versus-future-client distinction does not adequately examine a law that prevents the very representation on which a claim depends. The attorneys regularly accept appellate appointments through strict-rotation rosters, intend to continue that practice, and allege reduced paid assignments caused by the challenged restriction. Taken under the governing pleading standard, those allegations support a concrete personal economic stake as well as the setting in which the prospective representation arises.

The dissent reads *Craig v. Boren* and *Singleton v. Wulff*, which permit sufficiently direct prospective relationships, together with *Campbell v. Louisiana* and *Miller v. Albright*, which require attention to the actual claimant connection and enforcement obstacle. Here the intended assistance and the constitutional right are directly linked: indigent applicants must assert a right to appellate counsel while proceeding without that counsel. That practical barrier gives the attorneys' recurring appointment relationship legal significance beyond generalized professional concern. The dissent would not require new injury allegations merely because future clients remain unidentified. It would leave genuinely unresolved Article III or other preserved matters to their actual procedural standards and preserve the individual plaintiffs' separate abstention disposition. It does not decide the constitutional appointment entitlement.

## Procedure After Action

The case returns through the Court of Appeals for dismissal of the deficient attorney claim and adjustment of its dependent injunction. The district court must identify an independent surviving basis before changing other relief. Proper indigent claimants remain subject to the jurisdictional and procedural rules governing their own cases.
