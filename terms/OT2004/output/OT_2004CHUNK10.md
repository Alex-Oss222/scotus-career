## Decisions and Dispositions

### Eulitt v. Maine Department of Education

Merits decision, June 20, 2005

#### Chronology and Posture

On review of 386 F.3d 344 (First Circuit).

The First Circuit affirmed summary judgment for Maine on October 22, 2004. The questions are whether equal protection requires payment of the ordinary St. Dominic's tuition requested by these parents and whether they may assert the school's separate constitutional rights without an obstacle to its own suit.

Minot contracts for at least ninety percent of its high-school students to attend Poland Regional High School. Up to ten percent may attend another approved nonsectarian school upon showing educational needs the public school cannot meet. The parents seek ordinary tuition at St. Dominic's specifically for Catholic instruction and viewpoint. They did not submit a formal application because the statutory sectarian exclusion would foreclose it. Their own challenge is adjudicated separately from the attempted assertion of the school's rights.

All nine Justices participated.

#### Judgment

The judgment of the Court of Appeals for the First Circuit is affirmed on both components.

| Judgment component | Disposition and vote | Supporting Justices | Remedy and scope |
|---|---|---|---|
| Parents' equal-protection claim | Affirmed, 9–0 | Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer | The integrated-tuition funding claim fails; requested tuition and an injunction are denied. |
| Attempted assertion of St. Dominic's independent rights | Affirmed, 9–0 | Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer | Rejected for lack of a demonstrated obstacle to the school's own suit; the merits of the school's claim are not adjudicated. |

#### Opinion Topology

BREYER, J., delivered the opinion of the Court, in which STONE-ZSELA, C. J., and STEVENS, O'CONNOR, SCALIA, KENNEDY, SOUTER, THOMAS and GINSBURG, JJ., joined.

#### Controlling Propositions and Authority

The opinion of the Court commands all nine votes on both holdings. Its author and complete joins are stated in Opinion Topology; each holding is directly supported at the stated level of generality.

##### 1. Equal treatment depends on the educational work publicly purchased

**Holding and operative rule:** Equal protection does not require a State to purchase integrated devotional instruction when its educational program purchases eligible secular work. Religious identity alone cannot disqualify an independently identifiable, reliably separated secular service that meets lawful neutral program requirements and effective use controls; the parents' request for ordinary St. Dominic's tuition to obtain integrated Catholic instruction falls on the devotional-purchase side of that distinction.

**Controlling explanation:** Agostini requires the Court to identify the government's actual function. Strout applies that inquiry to ordinary integrated tuition at St. Dominic's, while Zelman and McCallum establish that parental choice and payment routing do not change the function a restricted payment buys. The parents identify Catholic instruction and viewpoint as the need the public school does not meet. Their requested tuition purchases that integrated instructional package.

The equality objection is substantial when religious affiliation excludes otherwise eligible secular work. Columbia Union and K.D.M. protect participation on equal terms in such work, and the present rule preserves that protection. Here the financed services differ in the constitutionally material respect established by the governing cases. The program's support for private secular alternatives therefore does not require the requested devotional purchase. The educational-needs condition, school-approval requirements and sectarian exclusion remain distinct; the Court decides the actual funding claim rather than presuming an additional denial under an unapplied neutral criterion.

Strout resolved its granted Religion Clause questions. Its rule supplies substantive authority here; it did not previously adjudicate these different parents' equality claim. Locke's permission concerning an individual ministry-degree scholarship likewise cannot become a general license to exclude religious institutions. These distinctions answer the parents' equal-access argument while retaining equal eligibility for genuinely controlled secular services.

##### 2. A close relationship does not replace a genuine obstacle to the school's own suit

**Holding and operative rule:** Parents seeking to assert a school's separate constitutional rights must establish a genuine hindrance to the school's own assertion, in addition to their own injury and a sufficiently close relationship. These parents have an actual relationship with St. Dominic's, but have not established the independent obstacle required to litigate its institutional claim.

**Controlling explanation:** Kowalski requires examination of the relationship and obstacle as separate prerequisites. Its hypothetical future clients differ from the identified school here. That difference satisfies neither the missing obstacle nor a general entitlement to litigate another legal person's claims. The parents' purchase of the school's services and the financial consequences of the exclusion explain their personal controversy, which the Court has decided. They do not show why the school cannot present its own rights. The institutional claim is therefore rejected at the representative-standing threshold.

#### Precedent Treatment

- Agostini v. Felton supplies the unified governmental-act-and-function inquiry; the decision applies that inquiry to the public purchase actually requested.
- Strout v. Albanese supplies the integrated-tuition rule, applied to a distinct equal-protection claim rather than treated as an earlier adjudication of that claim.
- Zelman v. Simmons-Harris and Freedom From Religion Foundation v. McCallum establish that neutral selection and private routing do not alter the devotional function bought by a restricted public payment; both retain their stated boundaries.
- Columbia Union College v. Oliver and K.D.M. v. Reorganized School District No. 7 preserve equal eligibility for genuine secular services under their separation, accountability and service-specific requirements.
- Locke v. Davey retains its bounded ministry-training holding in the individual-scholarship setting.
- Kowalski v. Tesmer is applied to the independent requirements for third-party assertion, with its absent-client circumstance distinguished from the existing relationship here.

#### Law After Decision

The existing financed-function framework now supplies the direct answer to the equality theory presented by an ordinary integrated devotional-tuition request. Eligible controlled secular work remains open to religious providers on equal terms. The third-party-standing rule continues to require a supported institutional hindrance even when the claimant has a close existing relationship with the rights holder.

#### Source Notes

Eulitt v. Maine Department of Education, 386 F.3d 344 (1st Cir. 2004); Me. Rev. Stat. tit. 20-A, §§2951, 5204; U.S. Const. amends. I, XIV. The request concerns ordinary integrated tuition, rather than an application for a separately controlled secular service. No institutional obstacle to St. Dominic's own suit is established.

#### Mandate, Remedy, and Stage

The affirmed judgment ends this action against the parents' presented funding claim and their attempted assertion of the school's independent rights. Maine may continue refusing this requested integrated-tuition payment under the governing function distinction. The merits of the school's independent claim remain unadjudicated.

**End of entry: Eulitt v. Maine Department of Education, Merits decision, June 20, 2005.**

### Mid-Con Freight Systems, Inc. v. Michigan Public Service Commission, No. 03-1234

Merits decision, June 20, 2005

#### Chronology and Posture

Argued April 26, 2005. On review of the Michigan Court of Appeals, which sustained the annual $100 charge under Michigan Compiled Laws §478.2(2) for Michigan-plated trucks operating entirely interstate. The question is whether the federal Single State Registration System preempts that separate charge under 49 U.S.C. §14504.

The State requires vehicle-identifying information and a decal for the separate program and appears to waive its otherwise available $10 federal-system fee for covered carriers choosing Michigan as their registration State. The carriers seek invalidation and relief from collection. Michigan contends that its interstate and intrastate $100 provisions together form a general neutral program; the carriers dispute that characterization. The distinct local-haul fee and constitutional challenge are presented in American Trucking Associations.

All nine Justices participated.

#### Judgment

**Affirmed, 7–2.**

**Supporting:** Stone-Zsela, Stevens, Scalia, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer.

**Opposing:** O'Connor and Kennedy, who would vacate and remand for consideration of the combined state scheme under their interstate-status construction.

#### Opinion Topology

SCALIA, J., delivered the opinion of the Court, in which STONE-ZSELA, C. J., and STEVENS, SOUTER, THOMAS, GINSBURG and BREYER, JJ., joined. KENNEDY, J., filed a dissenting opinion, in which O'CONNOR, J., joined.

#### Controlling Propositions and Authority

**Holding and operative rule:** Section 14504 preempts additional state registration obligations concerning the federal system's specified subjects—evidence of federal operating authority, insurance, a service agent and associated registration fees—when those obligations exceed the federal standards. It does not displace a genuinely separate state regulatory obligation on another subject merely because it applies to interstate carriers; Michigan's section 478.2(2) charge falls outside the covered subjects on this record.

**Authority:** The signed opinion of the Court, with the seven complete joins stated in Opinion Topology, directly controls the rule, application and remedy.

**Controlling explanation:** Both sentences of section 14504(b) measure registration against the Secretary's standards. Subsection (a) defines those standards by their forms-and-procedures function, and subsection (c) identifies the federal-registration system. The repeated registration language concerns that defined subject. The excess-obligation prohibition therefore protects the federal system against additional covered demands without absorbing every state carrier-registration program.

The competing interstate-status reading would treat every registration requirement triggered by interstate operation as covered, while preserving general neutral registration. That distinction is narrower than a universal fee cap, but the statute identifies the transportation governed by the federal system; it does not say that an obligation on a different subject becomes covered whenever interstate operation triggers it. Dividing the work between subsections (b) and (c) cannot overcome their common reference to the defined system. The replacement system's centralization of relevant filings and fees confirms the enacted structure. Yellow Transportation enforces the benchmark within that system and preserves unrelated taxes and licensing policies.

Michigan's separate program seeks vehicle information and a fee rather than the listed federal permit, insurance or service-agent filings. The apparent $10 waiver creates an administrative connection but does not establish that payment of the $100 is necessary to complete federal registration. A fee's label, age or regulatory purpose alone would not establish independence; the actual obligation supplies the distinction here. Because general neutrality is not the adopted test, the dispute over the combined coverage of Michigan's two provisions does not require a characterization remand under this rule.

**Operative qualifications:** Covered federal-registration demands remain subject to the federal standards despite a different state label. The insurance-proof fee system retains its November 15, 1991 benchmark and $10 ceiling. The prohibition on charging for filing and maintaining evidence of federal registration is distinct. The vehicle-identification restriction governs what the Secretary's standards may require, and the separate inconsistent-fee provision concerns fees under the federal system. These commands do not create a general $10 ceiling for state carrier regulation.

#### Precedent Treatment

Yellow Transportation, Inc. v. Michigan is applied for its enforcement of the delegated registration benchmark, qualifying reciprocity and express system-specific boundary. The present decision resolves which subject section 14504 governs; Yellow's separate fee-calculation holding remains in force.

#### Law After Decision

The federal registration ceiling governs demands within the Single State Registration System's specified subjects. Courts must identify the actual information, payment and conditions a state program imposes. A separate program on a different subject remains outside that ceiling, while relabeling an additional covered demand cannot evade it. Michigan's challenged fee satisfies that distinction on the present record.

#### Separate Writings

**Kennedy, joined by O'Connor, dissenting.** The dissent reads subsection (b) to prohibit additional registration obligations imposed because a carrier conducts interstate transportation, while preserving general neutral registration that only incidentally covers interstate carriers. Subsection (a) defines the permitted federal standards, rather than every additional demand subsection (b) forbids. On that reading, a State cannot avoid the ceiling by asking for different information or spending the fee on safety administration. The information, decal and payment constitute registration even though they serve a regulatory purpose.

The Court's defined-subject construction, in the dissent's view, adds an exemption the excess-obligation sentence does not contain. Applicable subsection (c) commands still independently protect the specified federal demands; the general-neutral qualification does not permit excess covered fees. Yellow's enforcement of the federal benchmark leaves this scope question open.

The dissent would vacate and remand rather than invalidate the fee outright. Michigan argues that sections 478.2(1) and (2) together impose one neutral $100 requirement on Michigan-plated for-hire vehicles; the carriers dispute that account. The lower court's regulatory-purpose rationale did not resolve that characterization under the dissent's rule. The required inquiry concerns the combined scheme's operation, rather than an assumed condition linking the $100 payment to federal registration.

#### Source Notes

Mid-Con Freight Systems, Inc. v. Michigan Public Service Commission, 545 U.S. 440; 49 U.S.C. §14504(a)–(c); Mich. Comp. Laws §§478.2(1), (2). The combined-scheme characterization is disputed. The record does not establish that payment of the $100 is required to complete federal registration.

#### Mandate, Remedy, and Stage

The affirmed judgment concludes this section 14504 challenge. The carriers' requested invalidation and refund relief on that ground are denied. Michigan may continue collecting its separate section 478.2(2) fee, subject to the federal requirements governing any actual covered registration demands.

**End of entry: Mid-Con Freight Systems, Inc. v. Michigan Public Service Commission, Merits decision, June 20, 2005.**

### Exxon Mobil Corp. v. Allapattah Services, Inc., No. 04-70, and del Rosario Ortega v. Star-Kist Foods, Inc., No. 04-79

Merits decision, June 23, 2005

#### Chronology and Posture

Argued March 1, 2005. The Eleventh Circuit sustained supplemental jurisdiction over smaller dealer claims in a Rule 23 class action after a jury verdict. The First Circuit restored an injured child's qualifying diversity claim but excluded the related claims of her relatives, joined under Rule 20, because each fell below the amount requirement. The question in both cases is whether 28 U.S.C. §1367 authorizes those smaller related claims when a named plaintiff presents an independently sufficient claim and the other original-jurisdiction requirements are met.

All nine Justices participated.

#### Judgment

| Judgment component | Disposition and vote | Supporting Justices | Opposing Justices | Remedy or remand |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| No. 04-70: smaller dealer class claims | Affirmed, 5–4 | Stone-Zsela, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter and Thomas | Stevens, O'Connor, Ginsburg and Breyer | The Eleventh Circuit's supplemental-jurisdiction ruling stands; the claims may proceed subject to section 1367 and Rule 23. |
| No. 04-79: joined relatives' claims | Reversed in relevant part and remanded, 5–4 | Stone-Zsela, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter and Thomas | Stevens, O'Connor, Ginsburg and Breyer | Reverse the First Circuit's exclusion of the relatives' claims solely for deficient individual amounts; remand under section 1367 and Rule 20. The child's qualifying claim remains in place. |

The dissenters would reverse the dealer jurisdictional ruling and affirm the relatives' exclusion.

#### Opinion Topology

THOMAS, J., delivered the opinion of the Court, in which STONE-ZSELA, C. J., and SCALIA, KENNEDY and SOUTER, JJ., joined. GINSBURG, J., filed a dissenting opinion, in which STEVENS, O'CONNOR and BREYER, JJ., joined. STEVENS, J., filed a dissenting opinion, in which BREYER, J., joined.

#### Controlling Propositions and Authority

**Holding and operative rule:** When at least one named plaintiff satisfies the diversity amount requirement and the action otherwise meets original-jurisdiction requirements, section 1367(a) authorizes additional claims forming the same Article III case, including claims by additional parties, unless an enacted exception excludes them. Rule 23 class membership and the Rule 20 plaintiff joinder presented here create no such exclusion; the related smaller claims may proceed subject to the statute and applicable procedural rules.

**Authority:** The five Justices joining the complete Opinion of the Court control both docket components and every proposition below.

**Controlling explanation:** The qualifying named claim supplies an action within original jurisdiction. The amount of another plaintiff's claim does not eliminate that independently sufficient claim. Subsection (a) then expressly reaches related claims involving additional parties. Requiring independent jurisdiction over every such claim would defeat the grant's work. City of Chicago applies this distinction between original power, related-claim authority and discretion.

Subsection (b) identifies particular diversity exclusions. It distinguishes claims against persons brought into the case under designated rules from claims by particular added plaintiffs. Congress omitted Rule 23 members and Rule 20 plaintiffs from the latter categories. The Court cannot insert those exclusions to preserve every feature of the earlier amount regime. Finley required legislative authorization for additional-party jurisdiction; Congress supplied it. Zahn and Clark therefore yield for the claims this grant covers.

The competing original-action reading treats every amount deficiency as destructive of the entire action. That approach fails to give the additional-claims language its enacted effect. Complete diversity remains independently necessary: Grupo Dataflux preserves the citizenship requirement, while Caterpillar addresses an actual cure through dismissal of a party. Neither makes a smaller related claim destroy an otherwise sufficient diversity action. Asserted legislative expectations cannot add exclusions absent from the enacted structure.

The dealer and family claims present the same statutory problem. Each must have the required relationship to the qualifying claim and satisfy the applicable procedural rules. Section 1367(c) permits lawful declination after authority is established; it does not make retention automatic.

**Operative qualifications:** The rule preserves complete diversity and requires an independently qualifying original claim; separate claims cannot be aggregated to manufacture that claim. In diversity-only actions, subsection (b) excludes claims by plaintiffs against persons made parties under Rules 14, 19, 20 or 24, and claims by persons proposed as plaintiffs under Rule 19 or seeking to intervene as plaintiffs under Rule 24, when exercising jurisdiction would be inconsistent with section 1332. Subsection (c) permits declination for a novel or complex state-law issue, substantial predominance over the original-jurisdiction claims, dismissal of all claims supplying original jurisdiction, or exceptional circumstances presenting other compelling reasons. Discretion to decline cannot supply absent statutory power.

#### Precedent Treatment

- Finley v. United States required statutory authorization for the additional-party jurisdiction it rejected; section 1367 supplies that authorization within its terms and supersedes the contrary result at that scope.
- Zahn v. International Paper Co. no longer requires every class member to satisfy the amount requirement when section 1367 covers the related claim.
- Clark v. Paul Gray, Inc. no longer requires a separate sufficient amount for each joined plaintiff whose claim falls within the supplemental grant.
- City of Chicago v. International College of Surgeons is applied for the separate inquiries into original jurisdiction, related-claim authority and discretionary retention.
- Grupo Dataflux v. Atlas Global Group retains its citizenship rule; an insufficient amount on a related claim is a distinct statutory issue.
- Caterpillar Inc. v. Lewis retains its rule concerning an actual party dismissal that cures the citizenship defect before trial and judgment.

#### Law After Decision

Federal courts must identify an independently sufficient original claim, verify citizenship and same-case relatedness, apply subsection (b)'s actual exclusions and then consider any proper subsection (c) ground for declining jurisdiction. An otherwise covered class member or Rule 20 plaintiff may litigate a smaller related claim under that sequence. The pre-enactment separate-amount rules yield only to the extent the later grant reaches the claim.

#### Separate Writings

**Ginsburg, joined by Stevens, O'Connor and Breyer, dissenting.** Section 1367 begins with an action already within original jurisdiction. The dissent reads that phrase to incorporate section 1332's settled amount requirements, including Zahn for class members and Clark for joined plaintiffs. Citizenship and amount are both statutory prerequisites; neither may be dispensed with merely because another claim independently qualifies.

The additional-party sentence still performs substantial work under this reading by changing Finley's restriction once an adequate original action exists. Subsection (b) prevents specified attempts to circumvent diversity limits through supplementary additions. Its omission of Rule 23 members and Rule 20 plaintiffs does not itself change the antecedent meaning of original jurisdiction. The dissent acknowledges the force of the broad grant and the omitted categories, but reads the statute as a whole against the established jurisdictional background. It would exclude the smaller dealer claims and sustain exclusion of the relatives' claims.

**Stevens, joined by Breyer, dissenting.** Reliable legislative evidence may corroborate the contextual reading even when the opposing interpretation claims textual clarity. The relevant House report described restoration of pre-Finley supplemental practice and preservation of diversity-only class-action requirements. An earlier study's discussion of changing Zahn does not establish that Congress enacted that different policy. This explanation supports the common dissent's interpretation; it treats legislative material as evidence of meaning rather than an independently enacted jurisdictional rule.

#### Source Notes

Exxon Mobil Corp. v. Allapattah Services, Inc., 545 U.S. 546; 28 U.S.C. §§1332, 1367; Federal Rules of Civil Procedure 20 and 23. The jurisdictional issue assumes the qualifying named claim, complete diversity and same-case relationship identified in the consolidated disputes. Legislative-history references describe the report passages recited in the judicial opinions.

#### Mandate, Remedy, and Stage

The dealer litigation proceeds under the affirmed jurisdictional ruling. The family case returns for consideration of the restored related claims alongside the child's qualifying claim. The lower courts apply the governing joinder rules, resolve any preserved original-jurisdiction and relatedness issues, consider any proper subsection (c) request and conduct the remaining merits proceedings.

**End of entry: Exxon Mobil Corp. v. Allapattah Services, Inc., and del Rosario Ortega v. Star-Kist Foods, Inc., Merits decision, June 23, 2005.**

### Green Party of New York State v. New York State Board of Elections

Merits decision, June 23, 2005

#### Chronology and Posture

On review of 389 F.3d 411 (Second Circuit).

The Second Circuit's November 17, 2004 judgment affirmed preliminary injunctions issued in May and September 2003. The question is whether New York may erase affiliations and exclude future enrollment for organizations that lack statutory party status but demonstrated sufficient support to place statewide candidates on the 2002 ballot.

New York conditions full party status on 50,000 gubernatorial votes. The Green, Liberal and Right to Life organizations lost that status; the Libertarian and Marijuana Reform organizations had not held it. All five nevertheless qualified statewide candidates. The official lists serve organization, contact, mobilization and fundraising as well as primary administration. The plaintiffs challenge the resulting enrollment exclusion and seek continued interim protection. The State seeks dissolution of that protection.

All nine Justices participated.

#### Judgment

**Affirmed and remanded, 7–2.**

**Supporting:** Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Kennedy, Souter, Ginsburg and Breyer.
**Opposing:** Scalia and Thomas.

#### Opinion Topology

SOUTER, J., delivered the opinion of the Court, in which STEVENS, O'CONNOR, KENNEDY, GINSBURG and BREYER, JJ., joined. STONE-ZSELA, C. J., joined Parts II and III and filed an opinion concurring in part and concurring in the judgment. SCALIA, J., filed a dissenting opinion, in which THOMAS, J., joined.

| Writing | Author | Joined by | Relationship to judgment | Scope joined |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Opinion of the Court | Souter | Stevens, O'Connor, Kennedy, Ginsburg and Breyer; Stone-Zsela as specified | Affirms interim protection and remands | Six Associates join Parts I–III; Stone-Zsela joins Parts II–III concerning interim necessity, functional implementation and remedial scope |
| Concurrence in part and in the judgment | Stone-Zsela | — | Supports the disposition | Treats the burden as severe and requiring strict scrutiny; joins Parts II–III and adds concrete implementation directions |
| Dissent | Scalia | Thomas | Would reverse and dissolve the injunction | Ordinary burden and permissible primary-administration classification |

#### Controlling Propositions and Authority

##### I. The substantial enrollment burden lacks the required regulatory fit

**Holding and operative rule:** When a State maintains official affiliation information for political organization as well as primary administration, erasing and refusing the affiliations of organizations with demonstrated statewide ballot support imposes a substantial associational burden. Under the calibrated Anderson–Burdick inquiry, an important state objective must have a demonstrated substantial relationship to that exclusion; the preliminary findings establish a substantial likelihood that New York's categorical exclusion fails that requirement.

**Authority:** Part I receives six direct joins: Stevens, O'Connor, Kennedy, Souter, Ginsburg and Breyer. Its substantial-burden classification and application control without combining them with the Chief Justice's severe-burden rationale.

**Controlling explanation:** Burdick requires examination of the actual burden within the electoral system. Cartwright gives independent work to a substantial burden: serious injury need not make political success impossible, but it requires a substantial connection between the restriction and an important objective. The organizations retain nomination, campaigning and other political activity. Their exclusion from a useful continuing official channel nevertheless causes concrete injury to identification, communication and organization.

Norman requires support rules to fit the electoral function at issue, and Timmons requires a real relationship between an associational restriction and the asserted stability interest. A threshold governing primary and statutory party status does not by itself justify destroying or refusing affiliation information. Accurate instructions can distinguish recorded association from primary eligibility.

The State and dissent regard enrollment as a limited, publicly financed primary service whose incidental organizing value need not be extended. That description overlooks how the existing system operates. The State supplies continuing nonprimary advantages to established parties while removing them from organizations with demonstrated statewide support. The challenged erasure contributes insufficiently to administering primaries or avoiding confusion. The same actual-burden-and-fit inquiry resolves the overlapping First and Fourteenth Amendment claims; unequal treatment does not automatically establish severe burden.

##### II. Ongoing associational loss warrants the existing interim protection

**Holding and operative rule:** The continuing loss of identification and communication with willing affiliates establishes irreparable associational injury, and the preliminary findings satisfy the substantial-likelihood requirement applied to this affirmative injunction. Effective interim protection must preserve existing affiliations and permit new affiliations for the qualifying organizations while the merits proceed.

**Authority:** Part II has seven direct joins: the six Justices joining Part I and Stone-Zsela. Agreement concerns the need and scope of interim protection, with the Chief Justice relying on his separately stated merits ground.

**Controlling explanation:** A static copy of former lists cannot identify new voters or preserve a continuing channel of political organization. The preliminary record establishes both losses. Preserving only old data would therefore leave part of the continuing injury intact. The findings meet the substantial-likelihood standard the lower court used for affirmative relief. This interlocutory determination preserves effective association during litigation; final merits proceedings remain necessary under the same governing constitutional standard.

##### III. Implementation must cure the common exclusion for the qualified category

**Holding and operative rule:** Interim relief retains the Other write-in mechanism with accurate primary-eligibility instructions, continued maintenance and updating for organizations satisfying the established support criterion, best efforts to notify already disenrolled Liberal and Right to Life voters of reenrollment, and equal-form access to Green Party enrollment data. The decree must cure the policy for organizations and voters subject to it who satisfy the support condition, unless the State shows that narrower relief fully cures the same defect; an equally effective administrative arrangement remains permissible under supervision.

**Authority:** The same seven Justices directly join Part III.

**Controlling explanation:** Clingman requires a remedy for the unlawful common policy rather than a limitation justified solely by naming particular plaintiffs. The district court must apply the objective qualification requirement and determine an administrable scope. Continuing data maintenance applies to organizations with sufficient support to place a statewide candidate on the ballot at the most recent gubernatorial election. The form may permit a voter to name any organization without requiring continuing maintenance for every name supplied.

The separate notice and data commands perform distinct work. Best-efforts notice gives already disenrolled Liberal and Right to Life voters an opportunity to reenroll using the new form. The Green Party data order bars publication of its voters as unenrolled and requires the same enrollment data in the same form supplied to statutory parties. Equivalent implementation may replace particular mechanics if it preserves those functions. Existing protection continues while implementation and scope are settled. Separate primary eligibility and full-party requirements remain operative.

#### Precedent Treatment

- Burdick v. Takushi supplies the actual-burden inquiry within the complete electoral system.
- Cartwright v. Barnes supplies the substantial-burden requirement of an important objective and a demonstrated substantial relationship to the restriction.
- Norman v. Reed is applied to the connection between a support requirement and the particular electoral function.
- Timmons v. Twin Cities Area New Party is applied to require an actual connection between a restriction and claimed electoral stability.
- Clingman v. Beaver supplies the common-policy remedy rule and the State's opportunity to show a fully effective narrower cure.
- Belitskus v. Pizzingrilli reinforces examination of actual access burdens; its inability-to-pay application remains distinct.

#### Law After Decision

Qualifying political organizations receive protection against the selective loss of ongoing official affiliation information under the substantial-burden standard. States retain objective support criteria and primary-eligibility rules, but must justify the different enrollment restriction by its actual function. The injunction continues while the district court resolves final merits and applies the governing qualification and common-policy remedy requirements.

#### Separate Writings

**Chief Justice Stone-Zsela, concurring in part and concurring in the judgment.** The Chief Justice regards the demonstrated loss of affiliation records, organizing opportunities and equal access as severe. Burdick's severe-burden rule therefore requires a narrowly drawn restriction serving a compelling interest. The organizations' continuing ability to campaign does not adequately measure the injury to this established channel of political identity and organization. Erasure does not administer the independent primary threshold, and clear instructions address confusion without destroying the association. He would sustain the injunction under that stricter standard.

He joins the Court's interim-protection, functional-implementation and common-policy remedy holdings. To make that relief effective, he would require concrete form, record and publication compliance dates tied to the next registration cycle, immediate preservation of existing data, and reports showing correction. Any alternative administration must provide equivalent protection, and any narrower relief must fully cure the common injury for the objectively qualified category.

**Justice Scalia, dissenting, joined by Justice Thomas.** The State may define official enrollment as part of an administrable primary system. The dissent accepts that political affiliation is protected and that the lists have real organizing value. It nonetheless regards the added benefits of a publicly financed system as an ordinary burden rather than the substantial restriction the Court finds. The organizations remain able to nominate, campaign and associate, and the State may link its enrollment service to the parties whose primaries it administers.

Burdick permits reasonable regulatory administration and does not require the State to await actual confusion or disruption. The dissent acknowledges that these organizations have demonstrated statewide support and that the challenged erasure removes useful information. Those facts do not, in its view, require continuing state recognition and data services beyond the defined primary structure. It would reverse the interlocutory judgment and dissolve the injunction.

#### Source Notes

Green Party of New York State v. New York State Board of Elections, 389 F.3d 411 (2d Cir. 2004), No. 03-7679; U.S. Const. amends. I, XIV. The May 30 and September 18, 2003 injunctions govern the distinct enrollment, notice and data functions described above.

#### Mandate, Remedy, and Stage

The existing injunction remains operative while the district court continues the merits proceedings, makes actual qualification determinations where needed, and applies the common-policy remedy rule to qualifying organizations and voters. It must determine whether any supported narrower cure fully remedies the common defect. Current record preservation, enrollment, notice and data-access protection remains in effect during that work. Recording affiliation does not confer primary eligibility or full statutory party status.

**End of entry: Green Party of New York State v. New York State Board of Elections, Merits decision, June 23, 2005.**

### Halbert v. Michigan, No. 03-10198

Merits decision, June 23, 2005

#### Chronology and Posture

Argued April 25, 2005. On writ of certiorari to the Michigan Court of Appeals, which denied Halbert's uncounseled application for leave to appeal for lack of merit after appointment was refused. The Michigan Supreme Court declined review.

The questions are whether an indigent defendant convicted on a guilty or no-contest plea is entitled to appointed counsel for this first appellate merits examination, and whether Halbert knowingly waived that assistance. He pleaded no contest to two criminal-sexual-conduct counts, twice requested appellate counsel, and identified sentencing and representation issues. He seeks restoration of the first-tier application with assistance; Michigan defends both its selective-review structure and an asserted waiver.

All nine Justices participated.

#### Judgment

**Vacated and remanded, 7–2.**

**Supporting:** Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Kennedy, Souter, Ginsburg and Breyer.
**Opposing:** Scalia and Thomas.

#### Opinion Topology

O'CONNOR, J., delivered the opinion of the Court, in which STONE-ZSELA, C. J., and STEVENS, KENNEDY, SOUTER, GINSBURG and BREYER, JJ., joined. THOMAS, J., filed a dissenting opinion, in which SCALIA, J., joined.

#### Controlling Propositions and Authority

The seven Justices joining the Court's opinion directly support all three holdings at the stated level of generality.

##### I. Counsel must assist the initial appellate merits examination

**Holding and operative rule:** The Fourteenth Amendment requires appointed advocacy for an indigent plea-convicted defendant seeking initial appellate examination of alleged error in the Michigan Court of Appeals. A State may use a genuinely equivalent advocate-assisted screening mechanism and valid frivolousness procedures, but must supply the legal-assistance function before deciding the initial application.

**Controlling explanation:** Douglas protects fair access to initial merits review. Ross permits later discretionary selection after the defendant has received counseled appellate consideration and the materials that consideration produces. Halbert has received no such first presentation. Michigan's intermediate court examines the merits of the claimed error; the leave label cannot replace attention to that function.

Smith v. Robbins requires an appellate advocate's actual legal-screening contribution while allowing equivalent formats. A plea narrows the available claims but leaves potentially difficult questions concerning sentence, plea and representation. Post-grant counsel comes after the initial screening, and an application form cannot identify and present legal issues as an advocate can. Resource conservation and the narrower range of surviving issues therefore do not justify the missing first-review function.

Michigan's statute provides meaningful but bounded exceptions. Appointment is mandatory for an indigent plea defendant when the prosecutor seeks leave, the sentence exceeds the upper limit of the minimum applicable guideline range, an appellate court grants leave, or the defendant challenges a conditional plea under Rule 6.301(C)(2) or its successor. Appointment is discretionary when all three scoring conditions hold: an alleged offense-variable or prior-record-variable scoring error, objection or other preservation, and an upward departure above the upper limit of the minimum range the defendant contends should have applied. Those routes do not supply the assistance denied here. The constitutional protection follows the initial merits function rather than those exceptions alone.

##### II. The whole reported plea record does not establish surrender of this distinct assistance

**Holding and operative rule:** A guilty or no-contest plea does not itself waive counsel for the first appellate review that remains available. The State must establish a knowing, intelligent and voluntary surrender of that distinct assistance from the actual record; Halbert's reported advice, confirmations and general plea finding do not establish that surrender.

**Controlling explanation:** Godinez distinguishes capacity from actual waiver. Tovar requires real understanding without a prescribed judicial formula. Stumpf permits the whole record, including counsel's actual explanation and the defendant's confirmation, to establish informed assent. These rules require weighing the affirmative evidence rather than invalidating a waiver because particular words are absent.

Halbert confirmed that he understood loss of an appeal as of right and the described mandatory and discretionary appointment categories. The court found his plea understandingly made, voluntary and accurate. Counsel gave the reported affirmative responses concerning other promises or considerations and compliance with the no-contest rule. Those facts support Michigan's contextual argument, but they do not identify an explanation and intentional surrender of the distinct assistance needed to prepare the remaining leave application. Stumpf's counsel representation specifically concerned the essential elements whose understanding was disputed; the general confirmations here concern different subjects. The content of any additional promise is unspecified.

The statute commands advice about the ordinary appointment consequence, a determination of understanding and provision of a nontechnical application form. That command is not evidence that every unreported act occurred or a substitute for constitutional waiver. Halbert's later requests and reported learning limitations bear on the inquiry but neither make a valid waiver revocable nor alone prove incapacity. On the combined record, the State has not established the particular informed surrender it invokes.

##### III. Restoration supplies the missing first-review function

**Holding and operative rule:** When required first-tier appellate advocacy was wholly absent and was not validly waived, the defendant must receive renewed adequate consideration with appointed assistance without first proving that an omitted argument probably would prevail. Halbert must receive counsel, relevant-record access and a meaningful renewed application period, subject to ordinary preservation and constitutionally adequate frivolousness screening.

**Controlling explanation:** Robbins restores a missing process whose value lies in finding and presenting potentially arguable issues. Requiring an uncounseled defendant to demonstrate a winning omitted claim would demand the very professional work withheld. The state court must therefore permit an assisted application before treating the first review as complete. Valid screening may conclude that no arguable issue exists; it must include the required advocate function.

#### Precedent Treatment

- Douglas v. California is applied to initial appellate error correction and extended expressly to Michigan's first-tier leave process.
- Ross v. Moffitt is distinguished by the earlier counseled presentation and later selective function on which its ruling rests.
- Smith v. Robbins supplies the actual advocate-assisted screening requirement, equivalent-format permission and restoration remedy for a wholly absent function.
- Godinez v. Moran preserves the independent inquiry into actual knowing, intelligent and voluntary waiver.
- Iowa v. Tovar permits flexible proof of understanding without mandatory ritual warnings.
- Bradshaw v. Stumpf is applied as a whole-record rule; its specific counsel explanation is distinguished from general plea-compliance confirmations.
- Kowalski v. Tesmer retains its third-party relationship holding; Halbert presents his own denial.

#### Law After Decision

Indigent plea defendants seeking initial merits review in the Michigan Court of Appeals are entitled to appointed advocacy before the leave determination, subject to valid waiver and adequate frivolousness procedures. The State must establish an actual informed surrender rather than infer it automatically from the plea. A wholly missing, unwaived function requires restored assisted review under Robbins.

#### Separate Writings

**Justice Thomas, dissenting, joined by Justice Scalia.** The dissent would sustain the judgment on two independently sufficient grounds. First, Douglas governs an appeal as of right, while Ross permits a State to limit counsel at a genuinely selective stage. Michigan affords a leave application after a plea has narrowed the possible issues and supplies appointment in defined circumstances, including after leave is granted. The absence of an earlier counseled appellate presentation is significant, but the dissent does not regard it as sufficient to extend Douglas to this different procedure. Robbins governs the required assistance once that entitlement exists; it does not itself settle this classification.

Second, assuming an entitlement, the dissent finds an actual waiver from the reported record. Halbert repeatedly confirmed the changed appellate rights and finite appointment categories. Counsel's compliance response and the trial court's understanding-and-voluntariness finding reinforce the inference that he understood and accepted the ordinary absence of appointed assistance. The statutory duty supplies the legal context, rather than proof of unreported advice.

The dissent recognizes that surrender of an appeal as of right differs from surrender of help preparing an available application. It nevertheless reads the cumulative advice and confirmations as sufficiently conveying that residual consequence. Under Tovar and Stumpf, demonstrated understanding may be inferred without an express residual exclusion or a prescribed waiver formula. The subsequent requests do not negate the knowing choice the dissent finds. It would affirm on either ground.

#### Source Notes

U.S. Const. amend. XIV; Mich. Comp. Laws §770.3a, enacted by 1999 Mich. Pub. Act 200; Michigan Court Rules 6.301(C)(2) and 6.302. The reported plea colloquy and finding concern the subjects described in the waiver holding; the content of additional promises or advice is unspecified.

#### Mandate, Remedy, and Stage

The judgment denying Halbert's uncounseled application is vacated. Michigan must appoint qualified counsel, provide access to the relevant record and restore a meaningful opportunity to prepare and submit the first-tier application under a renewed filing period. Counsel must be supplied before that period runs. The earlier pro se merits denial cannot serve as the completed review. The renewed process must contain the required legal-screening function, with ordinary preservation and constitutionally adequate frivolousness rules. Halbert's conviction remains in force during restored review.

**End of entry: Halbert v. Michigan, Merits decision, June 23, 2005.**

### Kelo v. City of New London, No. 04-108

Merits decision, June 23, 2005

#### Chronology and Posture

Argued February 22, 2005. On writ of certiorari to the Supreme Court of Connecticut, which sustained the challenged condemnations under federal public-use and state redevelopment law, reversing the trial court's partial relief for some parcels.

The question is whether the Fifth Amendment permits compensated condemnation for an actual comprehensive economic redevelopment plan implemented partly by private parties. New London's Fort Trumbull plan combines commercial, residential and public components in response to prolonged economic distress. The petitioners' homes were not individually blighted. They seek an injunction preventing the acquisitions; the City seeks to proceed with the plan through lawful condemnation and compensation.

All nine Justices participated.

#### Judgment

**Affirmed, 6–3.**

**Supporting:** Stone-Zsela, Stevens, Kennedy, Souter, Ginsburg and Breyer.
**Opposing:** O'Connor, Scalia and Thomas.

#### Opinion Topology

STONE-ZSELA, C. J., delivered the opinion of the Court, in which STEVENS, KENNEDY, SOUTER, GINSBURG and BREYER, JJ., joined. KENNEDY, J., filed a concurring opinion. O'CONNOR, J., filed a dissenting opinion, in which SCALIA and THOMAS, JJ., joined. THOMAS, J., filed a dissenting opinion.

#### Controlling Propositions and Authority

##### Actual public redevelopment, private implementation and favoritism

**Holding and operative rule:** A compensated condemnation implementing an adopted comprehensive redevelopment plan satisfies public use when it serves a rationally supported actual public purpose rather than a pretext for private favoritism, even though private parties will implement portions and the particular homes are not blighted. Courts must meaningfully examine supported claims that the stated public purpose masks a private favor, using the actual plan, process and beneficiary relationships under the ordinary presumption of lawful public action and ordinary proof rules.

**Authority:** The Court's six-Justice opinion, whose author and complete joins appear in Opinion Topology, directly controls the entire rule and its application.

**Controlling explanation:** Berman permits an integrated redevelopment undertaking and private implementation rather than requiring every parcel to be independently harmful. Midkiff makes the public objective, rather than the mechanics of the transfer, decisive. Boston & Maine applies that rule to private reconveyance while retaining an enforceable prohibition on naked private favoritism.

The adopted plan responds to prolonged economic distress through coordinated commercial, residential and public uses. That established undertaking supplies an actual public objective beyond replacing one owner with another. Individual homes need not be blighted for their inclusion in an otherwise public plan. Forecasts of jobs and revenues support the purpose without becoming guarantees; a court need not establish that every expected benefit will materialize.

The owners and common dissent object that every ordinary successor use can promise greater productivity, making all property vulnerable. The answer lies in the public character of the actual undertaking and the independently enforceable private-favor boundary. Reciting economic benefits cannot validate a sham whose actual object is favoring a private recipient and whose public gains are merely incidental. Supported allegations require genuine record examination. On this record, the established planning purpose supports the condemnations and supplies no unresolved pretext issue requiring remand.

The Constitution does not confine privately implemented public acquisitions to removal of already harmful conditions. Public ownership and enforceable public access are sufficient examples of public use under these precedents, but do not exhaust the public-purpose rule. Just compensation and stricter state restrictions retain their independent force. An isolated transfer devised for a favored recipient is outside the rule sustained here.

#### Precedent Treatment

- Berman v. Parker is applied to integrated public redevelopment and private implementation; individual parcel blight is not an indispensable condition.
- Hawaii Housing Authority v. Midkiff is applied to the actual public objective rather than the ultimate titleholder.
- National Railroad Passenger Corp. v. Boston & Maine Corp. is applied to public purpose despite private reconveyance and its surviving prohibition on naked favoritism. Its railroad statutory need presumption does not govern this municipal plan.
- Lingle v. Chevron U.S.A. Inc. retains the distinction between regulatory-taking liability and independent constitutional restraints, including the public-use condition.

#### Law After Decision

The public-purpose rule permits the challenged comprehensive redevelopment condemnations with compensation and lawful authority, while requiring meaningful examination of supported private-favor allegations. The operative inquiry concerns the actual undertaking and its public object; economic forecasts need rational support rather than guaranteed success. State law may impose stricter acquisition limits.

#### Separate Writings

**Justice Kennedy, concurring.** Kennedy joins the Court's opinion in full. Ordinary deference must still examine a supported allegation that private favoritism supplies the actual object of a taking. The rational-basis inquiries in Cleburne and Moreno illustrate that judicial respect for governmental choices can coexist with a genuine examination of an impermissible object. The plan, decision process and beneficiary relationships matter to that inquiry; merely identifying incidental private gain does not establish invalidity.

The record supports this comprehensive plan under that standard. Kennedy leaves open whether a narrowly drawn, exceptionally suspicious class of transfers could warrant stronger review. He identifies no governing heightened standard or set of triggers for that different category in this case.

**Justice O'Connor, dissenting, joined by Justices Scalia and Thomas.** The dissent would prohibit taking ordinary private property for new private users solely because their normal activities are expected to produce greater economic benefits. Public ownership and actual public service remain valid. Berman addressed a harmful area, and Midkiff a harmful concentration of ownership; the acquisition itself removed the identified condition. The petitioners' ordinary homes present a materially different case.

The precedents' broad public-purpose language favors the City's position, but the dissent would confine that language at this economic-successor boundary. A sincere comprehensive plan does not cure the substantive problem because virtually every private activity yields incidental public gains. Meaningful pretext review addresses bad purpose but leaves the lawful-purpose rule too broad. Compensation and possible state-law protection cannot replace an independent federal public-use limit. The dissent would reverse and remand for relief preventing these condemnations.

**Justice Thomas, dissenting.** Thomas also joins the common dissent as an alternative under existing precedents, while proposing a further change to their constitutional rationale. The public-use condition should require governmental ownership or use, or a legally enforceable entitlement of the public to use the property. Common-carrier property may satisfy that rule despite private title; projected jobs and taxes from ordinary private premises do not.

The constitutional text and structure distinguish public use from the independent duty to compensate. Cole and Kohl illustrate public functions and the private-use prohibition without proving an exhaustive historical catalogue. Thomas would reconsider Berman's and Midkiff's broader public-purpose propositions and incompatible private-transfer permission. He would replace Boston & Maine's broader rationale while recognizing that secured passenger service could satisfy the narrower use rule. His alternative reliance on the common dissent does not amount to approval of those precedents' permanent constitutional correctness.

#### Source Notes

U.S. Const. amend. V; Kelo v. City of New London, 268 Conn. 1, 843 A.2d 500 (2004). The challenged parcels are included in the adopted Fort Trumbull plan; projected economic benefits are forecasts. The public-use inquiry concerns that plan and its established purpose.

#### Mandate, Remedy, and Stage

The Connecticut judgment stands against the federal public-use challenge, and the requested injunction is denied. The City may undertake the adjudicated acquisitions through applicable condemnation and compensation proceedings. Preserved compensation and independent state-law claims follow their proper procedures.

**End of entry: Kelo v. City of New London, Merits decision, June 23, 2005.**

### Mayle v. Felix, No. 04-563

Merits decision, June 23, 2005

#### Chronology and Posture

Argued April 19, 2005. On writ of certiorari to the Ninth Circuit. The granted question concerns relation back under Rule 15(c)(2) of an untimely new habeas ground based on different underlying events but directed at the same trial and conviction.

Felix's timely May 8, 1998 petition presented confrontation, instructional-error and insufficient-evidence grounds. Counsel was appointed May 29. His January 28, 1999 amendment, filed after expiration of the limitation, retained confrontation, omitted the other two grounds, and added coercion and related appellate-assistance theories. The new coercion ground concerned his own pretrial interrogation; the confrontation ground concerned Williams's videotaped testimony. The original petition did not attempt the interrogation factual core. The Ninth Circuit sustained relation back on a trial-and-conviction theory and separately affirmed rejection of the confrontation claim.

All nine Justices participated.

#### Judgment

**Reversed in relevant part and remanded, 7–2.**

**Supporting:** Stone-Zsela, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer.
**Opposing:** Stevens and Souter.

#### Opinion Topology

GINSBURG, J., delivered the opinion of the Court, in which STONE-ZSELA, C. J., and O'CONNOR, SCALIA, KENNEDY, THOMAS and BREYER, JJ., joined. STEVENS, J., filed a dissenting opinion, in which SOUTER, J., joined.

#### Controlling Propositions and Authority

##### Relation back requires shared operative facts, not merely the same conviction

**Holding and operative rule:** Under Rule 15(c)(2), an amended habeas ground asserted after limitations expire relates back only when it shares a common core of operative facts set forth or attempted in a timely pleading. A new legal theory, additional detail or fair elaboration of an imperfect pro se account may qualify; the same trial, conviction or requested release alone does not supply the factual connection.

**Authority:** All seven Justices joining the Court's opinion directly adopt the rule, its stated qualifications and its application.

**Controlling explanation:** Section 2242 permits civil-rule amendment, but Rule 15(a)'s permission to amend differs from Rule 15(c)(2)'s effect on an expired limitation. The latter provision links the amendment to conduct or events attempted in the original pleading. Habeas Rule 2(c)'s requirement of supporting facts for each ground gives that link practical content because a prosecution can contain many independent alleged wrongs.

Scarborough permits completion of an already identified timely request. Tiller permits a different legal theory concerning the same identified event. Neither makes an entire criminal judgment the pleaded transaction for every later allegation. Courts must read actual factual allegations fairly, including pro se attempts, rather than demand unchanged legal labels or professional drafting.

The contrary trial-occurrence reading emphasizes that both evidentiary challenges concern one trial and that Rule 15 deliberately accommodates limitations. But the State's possession of the prosecution file is not notice that every act in it is challenged. Treating the whole trial as the occurrence would allow almost any new ground while the first action remains pending, making the effective limitation depend on how long that action lasts. Ordinary amendment controls against prejudice and delay do not perform the separate factual-connection work enacted in Rule 15(c)(2).

Felix's allegedly coerced interrogation and the circumstances of Williams's videotaped testimony are distinct episodes. None of the original grounds attempted the interrogation facts. The amended coercion ground therefore does not relate back. The related assistance theory must be handled under the same rule within the proceedings actually before the lower court. The separately resolved confrontation merits lie outside the grant.

**Operative application:** Timely amendments remain available under the applicable rules. Later amendments sharing the pleaded factual core may relate back even when they change the legal theory. Independent commencement, pendency or equitable grounds require their own established predicates; the remand must enforce the common-core rule and adjudicate any actually preserved independent ground on its own basis.

#### Precedent Treatment

- Scarborough v. Principi is applied to distinguish completing an identified timely matter from adding a different factual wrong; its fee-specific prerequisites do not become habeas rules.
- Tiller v. Atlantic Coast Line Railroad Co. supports changing a legal theory while retaining the same identified factual occurrence.
- Artuz v. Bennett, Duncan v. Walker and Carey v. Saffold retain their distinct rules governing filing and qualifying state pendency.
- Rhines v. Weber retains bounded authority to preserve an existing timely mixed petition; it does not supply relation back for a new factual ground.
- Pliler v. Ford retains its specific judicial-act, causal-loss, diligence and lawful-remedy requirements.
- Pace v. DiGuglielmo and Dodd v. United States retain their separate state-filing and new-right timing holdings.

#### Law After Decision

Post-limitations habeas amendments must be compared with the actual operative events pleaded or attempted on time. Genuine factual elaboration and new legal theories concerning those events may relate back; distinct episodes do not become timely merely because they contributed to the same trial or conviction. The lower court must apply that rule to the added theories within their actual procedural scope and preserve the separate confrontation disposition.

#### Separate Writings

**Justice Stevens, dissenting, joined by Justice Souter.** The dissent treats the trial's use of the challenged evidence as the relevant connected occurrence. Rule 15(c) deliberately permits some amendments despite expiration of limitations; its text does not itself require every habeas ground to be a separate transaction. The State received a timely challenge to the trial's validity, and both the videotape and interrogation theories concern evidence introduced there.

The dissent acknowledges the distinct interrogation facts and does not claim that Felix originally pleaded coercion. It instead reads the transaction broadly in this pending attack on one conviction. Section 2242 provides ordinary civil amendment, and Rule 15(a) allows courts to reject abuse, delay or prejudice. The timely original filing and the different regime governing later actions after final judgment provide additional limits. Those safeguards, in its view, answer the Court's finality concern without narrowing occurrence to each constitutional episode.

That reading also preserves the usefulness of counsel appointed after a prisoner's initial filing: later identification of a trial defect need not be excluded simply because an unrepresented petition used a different legal or factual route. The dissent would affirm the relation-back ruling and permit the remaining proceedings under the ordinary amendment rules.

#### Source Notes

Fed. R. Civ. P. 15(a), 15(c)(2); 28 U.S.C. §§2242, 2244(d); Rule 2(c), Rules Governing Section 2254 Cases. The original pleading and January 28, 1999 amendment concern distinct interrogation and videotape episodes. The amended assistance theory received no separately identified appellate timeliness ruling.

#### Mandate, Remedy, and Stage

The Ninth Circuit must remove the trial-wide relation-back ground and return the case for proceedings consistent with the common-core requirement. The added coercion claim does not relate back and must be treated as time barred unless an independently preserved, legally sufficient timeliness ground applies. The related appellate-assistance theory must be handled under the same rule within its actual procedural scope. The separate Confrontation Clause merits judgment remains in force.

**End of entry: Mayle v. Felix, Merits decision, June 23, 2005.**

### Orff v. United States, No. 03-1566

Merits decision, June 23, 2005

#### Chronology and Posture

Argued February 23, 2005. On writ of certiorari to the Ninth Circuit, which rejected the farmers' reclamation-contract action under the asserted waiver and beneficiary theory.

Westlands Water District receives Bureau of Reclamation water under a 1963 contract. Farmers and farming entities buy water from Westlands and claim intended-beneficiary rights. Following reduced deliveries in 1993, districts sued and the farmers intervened. The districts later stipulated to dismissal. The farmers' surviving action runs directly against the United States and its agents. The question is whether 43 U.S.C. §390uu authorizes that action, independently of the farmers' asserted contract entitlement.

All nine Justices participated.

#### Judgment

**Affirmed, 9–0.**

**Supporting:** Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer.

#### Opinion Topology

THOMAS, J., delivered the opinion of the Court, in which STONE-ZSELA, C. J., and STEVENS, O'CONNOR, SCALIA, KENNEDY, SOUTER, GINSBURG and BREYER, JJ., joined.

#### Controlling Propositions and Authority

##### The statute permits qualifying joinder rather than this stand-alone action

**Holding and operative rule:** Section 390uu permits the United States to be added as a necessary defendant in a qualifying suit adjudicating a contracting entity's and the United States' rights under a federal reclamation contract. It does not itself authorize a stand-alone contract action against the United States and its agents; an asserted benefit under the contract cannot supply the missing procedural condition.

**Authority:** The unanimous Opinion of the Court supplies nine direct joins to the rule and disposition.

**Controlling explanation:** Congress authorized joinder as a necessary party defendant. That language describes adding the sovereign to a qualifying controversy between other parties, rather than creating general consent whenever relief is sought from it. The farmers now pursue a direct federal claim; the districts' earlier participation does not supply a continuing independent nonfederal controversy requiring federal joinder.

The farmers rely on the breadth of the statute's reference to suits adjudicating contractual rights, the second sentence's amenability to judgments and review, and the venue provision. Those words have substantial effect within the consent Congress initially grants. After qualifying joinder, the United States is subject to the specified adjudication and review. The venue sentence identifies a forum for that qualifying suit. Treating each sentence as an independent direct-action waiver would erase the opening procedural condition.

Meyer requires attention to the actual breadth of enacted consent; its broad sue-and-be-sued clause differs from this joinder provision. Blue Fox separates an asserted substantive obligation from consent to the action and requested relief. Idaho likewise distinguishes a defined adjudicatory consent from an additional permission asserted against the Government. These authorities require enforcing Congress's words rather than presuming that every waiver receives the narrowest conceivable construction.

Because the direct action falls outside the asserted waiver, intended-beneficiary entitlement cannot supply jurisdiction. The Court affirms on sovereign consent and leaves that beneficiary question without a merits determination. The sufficient direct-action ground also leaves unresolved whether every qualifying joinder must satisfy all of Rule 19(a), whether section 390uu permits money damages in a suit otherwise within its consent, and whether a noncontracting entity may invoke it in a qualifying joined controversy. Those questions are unnecessary to this threshold disposition; alternative statutory waivers lie outside the granted issue.

#### Precedent Treatment

- FDIC v. Meyer is applied to honor the text and actual breadth of consent while keeping waiver distinct from substantive entitlement; its broader agency clause does not supply the different consent asserted here.
- Department of the Army v. Blue Fox, Inc. is applied to require consent for the actual action and relief, independently of the alleged contractual obligation.
- United States v. Idaho is applied to distinguish a specified adjudicatory waiver from an additional asserted governmental obligation.

#### Law After Decision

Section 390uu remains available for qualifying necessary-party joinder in reclamation-contract controversies. Its amenability and venue provisions operate within that consent. Farmers asserting contract benefits cannot use it alone to maintain the surviving stand-alone suit against the United States and its agents; substantive beneficiary entitlement remains distinct from the threshold waiver.

#### Source Notes

43 U.S.C. §390uu, enacted by Pub. L. 97–293, title II, §221, 96 Stat. 1271 (1982); Fed. R. Civ. P. 19(a). The surviving plaintiffs are the farmers and farming entities, and the defendants are the United States and its agents; the districts previously stipulated to dismissal.

#### Mandate, Remedy, and Stage

The affirmed jurisdictional dismissal concludes the surviving direct action under section 390uu. It leaves intended-beneficiary status undecided and does not reinstate the districts' dismissed complaint. Any independently authorized and properly filed alternative proceeding must satisfy its own claimant, forum and remedy requirements.

**End of entry: Orff v. United States, Merits decision, June 23, 2005.**

### Bell v. Thompson, No. 04-514

Merits decision, June 27, 2005

#### Chronology and Posture

Argued April 26, 2005. Certiorari to the Sixth Circuit, reviewing its amended judgment at 373 F.3d 688. After the announced stays expired at the end of certiorari review, the court withheld its mandate without further notice for more than five months and amended the disposition to permit a hearing on Thompson's mitigation-related ineffective-assistance claim. The question concerns that exercise of mandate authority. Tennessee seeks restoration of the earlier denial; Thompson defends the amended judgment and further hearing.

All nine Justices participated at argument and decision.

#### Judgment

**Reversed and remanded, 5–4.**

**Supporting:** Stone-Zsela, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy and Thomas.
**Opposing:** Stevens, Souter, Ginsburg and Breyer.

#### Opinion Topology

KENNEDY, J., delivered the opinion of the Court, in which STONE-ZSELA, C. J., and O'CONNOR, SCALIA and THOMAS, JJ., joined. STEVENS, J., filed a dissenting opinion, in which SOUTER, GINSBURG and BREYER, JJ., joined.

#### Controlling Propositions and Authority

##### Abuse of assumed authority to withhold the mandate

**Holding and operative rule:** Even assuming exceptional authority to withhold a mandate after completed certiorari review, a court abuses that authority when it leaves the parties without notice for months after its announced stay ends and then reopens the merits without a justification sufficient for that extraordinary course. The Sixth Circuit's combined unexplained delay, expired announced endpoints, earlier opportunities to consider the psychological material, and ensuing merits amendment require reversal here; nonissuance alone cannot justify indefinite reconsideration.

**Authority:** The Opinion of the Court, joined in full by Stone-Zsela, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy and Thomas, controls this rule, its application and the mandate.

**Controlling explanation:** Rule 41 establishes when a mandate issues and makes the mandate effective upon issuance. Carlisle v. United States requires inherent judicial authority to operate within governing procedural rules. Calderon v. Thompson protects the finality of completed habeas review, but its specific mandate-recall rule does not directly govern a mandate that never issued. The Court therefore evaluates the exercise of assumed authority at the actual procedural stage.

The Sixth Circuit expressly tied its stays to the completion of this Court's review. After that endpoint, it withheld the mandate for more than five months without a further order or notice. Tennessee proceeded on the announced endpoint while the appellate court later amended the merits disposition. Earlier rehearing papers prominently presented the Sultan material; its significance was not first raised only after the review process ended.

The omitted report and deposition support a serious mitigation argument. Their importance, and the fact that the mandate remained unissued, supply the strongest grounds for permitting correction. But they do not explain this prolonged silent departure from the announced procedure. The existence of a contested claim requiring further merits assessment differs from a sufficient justification for that exceptional appellate course. The conclusion rests on those circumstances together, rather than treating elapsed time alone as conclusive or requiring a finding that the mitigation claim lacks merit.

The general availability of exceptional post-certiorari withholding is assumed for this exercise-of-discretion ruling. The actual amendment cannot stand even on that favorable assumption.

#### Precedent Treatment

- Carlisle v. United States is applied for rule-consistent inherent authority; its principle prevents a bare appeal to equitable power from displacing the governing procedure.
- Calderon v. Thompson is applied by analogy for finality and state reliance. Its miscarriage-of-justice requirements retain their defined application to actual recalls reopening completed habeas merits; an unissued mandate is a different procedural event.

#### Law After Decision

The Court holds that the asserted exceptional authority to withhold a mandate was abused in the circumstances of this case. The prolonged withholding without notice, expired review endpoints, prior opportunities to present the evidence, limited nature of the proposed correction and the State's justified reliance together establish that abuse. Retained jurisdiction and nonissuance do not justify the course taken here. Rule 41 governs issuance, and the distinct statutory conditions of any further habeas proceeding remain applicable.

#### Separate Writings

**Justice Stevens, joined by Justices Souter, Ginsburg and Breyer, dissenting.** The dissent would affirm the limited correction and hearing direction. Rule 41(b)'s enlargement authority and the absence of an issued mandate leave room to complete a materially deficient first review. The Sultan report and deposition supplied a concrete evidentiary problem bearing on the existing mitigation claim, and the appellate panel's examination of that problem supports its decision to reopen the record before its disposition became effective.

Carlisle requires an authorized procedural basis, while the institutional-process position in Calderon distinguishes correction from reopening completed merits merely to reach a preferred answer. Here the mandate never issued, and the revised judgment directs further adjudication rather than awards the writ. The delay and failure to notify deserve serious criticism, but the dissent does not regard them as sufficient to make this bounded correction unreasonable. Review for abuse of discretion should give weight to the panel's detailed examination of the omitted evidence instead of replacing its judgment about the need for a hearing. The hearing remains subject to independently applicable habeas law. The dissent rests on the documented omission and actual procedural stage, without assuming clerical fault, diligence, innocence or ultimately prejudicial ineffective assistance.

#### Source Notes

No. 04-514; 373 F.3d 688; Fed. R. App. P. 41(b), (c), (d)(2)(D). The Sultan report and deposition were omitted from the district-court summary-judgment record, presented through later procedural papers and prominently discussed in rehearing materials. ### Metro-Goldwyn-Mayer Studios Inc. v. Grokster, Ltd., No. 04-480

Merits decision, June 27, 2005

#### Chronology and Posture

Argued March 29, 2005. On writ of certiorari to the Ninth Circuit, which affirmed summary judgment for Grokster and StreamCast concerning distribution of their then-current peer-to-peer software.

The questions concern purposeful promotion of copyright infringement despite lawful software capability and decentralized operation, the distinct Sony distribution theory, and the independent predicates for secondary liability and relief. Both sides moved for summary judgment. The district court limited the reviewed judgment to the then-current versions, leaving damages arising from past versions and other past activities outside that ruling. It held that the identified users' unauthorized downloads directly infringed the owners' copyrights; that conclusion was uncontested on appeal.

The distributors offer free file-sharing software supported by advertising. The record includes lawful uses, extensive unauthorized sharing, marketing aimed at former Napster users, user assistance and business evidence. Some StreamCast promotional materials were developed as drafts, with publication unestablished. The owners seek vacatur and adjudication of liability and relief; the distributors invoke lawful capability and the limits of their decentralized systems.

All nine Justices participated.

#### Judgment

**Vacated and remanded, 9–0.**

**Supporting:** Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer.

#### Opinion Topology

STEVENS, J., delivered the unanimous opinion of the Court. GINSBURG, J., filed a concurring opinion, in which KENNEDY, J., joined. BREYER, J., filed a concurring opinion, in which STEVENS and O'CONNOR, JJ., joined.

| Writing | Author | Joined by | Relationship to judgment | Scope joined |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Opinion of the Court | Stevens | Stone-Zsela, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer | Vacates the distributors' judgment and remands | Complete inducement rule, current-version and cross-motion instructions, and independent liability and remedial predicates |
| Concurrence | Ginsburg | Kennedy | Joins the Court; adds an independent reason to reject the distributors' motion | Substantial lawful capability remains genuinely disputed on this product-specific record |
| Concurrence | Breyer | Stevens and O'Connor | Joins the Court's inducement disposition; would preserve the favorable Sony determination | Identified lawful uses and plausible development satisfy the protective capability inquiry |

The competing Sony applications do not command a majority. Stone-Zsela, Scalia, Souter and Thomas join neither concurrence's application.

#### Controlling Propositions and Authority

All nine Justices join the Court's inducement and procedural holdings. The separate Sony applications have only the joins stated in Opinion Topology and supply no controlling application or new minimum-use test.

##### I. Affirmative promotion creates responsibility for resulting infringement

**Holding and operative rule:** A distributor that distributes a device with the object of promoting copyright infringement, shown by clear expression or other affirmative steps directed to that object, is responsible for resulting direct infringement when the owner proves the culpable promotion and causal connection. Substantial lawful capability defeats culpability inferred from distribution alone, but does not immunize this proved inducement.

**Authority:** The unanimous Opinion of the Court directly supports this rule, its safeguards and its application to the categorical immunity asserted here.

**Controlling explanation:** Sony protects the distribution of useful technology from liability inferred merely from its capability for misuse. Kalem recognizes responsibility for purposeful promotion of infringing use. Napster likewise distinguishes protected capability from knowing material assistance to identified infringement. Together those principles permit an inducement claim based on the distributor's own affirmative conduct rather than the mere provision of a product.

Decentralized architecture may affect the ability to assist or supervise particular transfers. It does not remove the distributor's responsibility for deliberately promoting infringement resulting from distribution with that object. The record's supported promotion, assistance and business evidence must therefore receive legal effect; lawful capability alone cannot sustain the distributors' judgment.

The distributors' strongest objection is that an intent inquiry could penalize ordinary innovation because users infringe. The rule requires affirmative culpable promotion and resulting infringement under the ordinary civil burden. Ordinary distribution, neutral technical support, product updates, lawful advertising and general awareness of misuse are insufficient by themselves. Infringement volume, advertising revenue and nonfiltering may bear on purpose with other supported affirmative evidence, but none independently supplies the required conduct and connection. The rule creates no freestanding monitoring or redesign duty. Patent inducement provisions illustrate the distinction; the copyright ground rests on the recognized purposeful-promotion principle.

##### II. Earlier evidence may illuminate current distribution without enlarging judgment scope

**Holding and operative rule:** Earlier conduct may show the purpose and character of current software distribution when the record supplies an actual connection; its date alone does not exclude its evidentiary use. Review of the current-version judgment nevertheless remains distinct from adjudication of damages for excluded past versions or activities, and each party's motion and each distributor's conduct must receive separate application of the summary-judgment standard.

**Authority:** All nine Justices adopt this application and remand instruction.

**Controlling explanation:** The distributors argue that a conduct-based theory falls outside review because the lower courts confined the judgment to current software. That argument confuses the scope of liability adjudicated with the evidence relevant to its purpose. An earlier promotional act may illuminate the object of continuing distribution without making damages for that earlier act part of this judgment. The required actual connection prevents old conduct from automatically establishing current culpability or causation.

An unpublished promotional draft can evidence purpose, but its development does not prove publication or a resulting infringing transfer. Nor does the rule require a published message reaching every user: the culpable conduct may consist of distribution with the established infringing object. Resulting infringement must still be causally connected to that conduct.

The district court's direct-infringement conclusion is already established and was uncontested on appeal. The remaining inquiry concerns each defendant's secondary responsibility and its causal and remedial scope. The owners' cross-motion must be reconsidered under the correct rule, with the inferences appropriate to that motion; vacating the distributors' categorical defense neither grants the owners' motion nor requires trial of every issue. Summary judgment remains available where the actual record warrants it.

##### III. Independent secondary theories and effective relief retain their own predicates

**Holding and operative rule:** Knowing material assistance to identified infringement remains a distinct contributory theory, and vicarious liability requires both direct financial benefit and a right and practical ability to supervise. Relief must address proved infringement and culpable conduct through feasible measures that stop any established continuing inducement practice and its supported effects while preserving lawful operation; an equally effective narrower measure can satisfy that obligation.

**Authority:** The unanimous Court applies Napster's separate liability and remedial rules.

**Controlling explanation:** Napster's supervisory ground rested on actual account exclusion and index control. Those capacities cannot be inferred for these decentralized distributors from advertising receipts or theoretical power to cease distributing software. The inducement rule supplies a separate ground for responsibility, not the missing supervisory fact.

The lower court must identify the conduct and infringement attributable to each defendant before setting monetary or injunctive relief. A continuing unlawful practice requires a remedy reaching that practice and its supported effects. Defendants may establish that narrower measures fully cure the injury while preserving lawful uses. Feasibility, effective protection and lawful operation guide the remedial terms; sharing software for lawful and unlawful files alone cannot justify a blanket technology prohibition. Any actually invoked statutory service protection must be considered under its own conditions and remedial limits.

##### IV. The separate Sony application has no controlling resolution

**Existing controlling rule:** Genuine current and prospective substantial lawful capability protects against liability inferred from product distribution alone. Present infringement percentages are not the exclusive measure; Napster's independent knowing-assistance route and today's inducement rule remain available on their own facts.

**Authority and unresolved scope:** The Court unanimously preserves that capability rule. Ginsburg and Kennedy would reject the distributors' favorable Sony determination because material dispute remains; Breyer, Stevens and O'Connor would sustain that determination. Neither product-specific application has five joins, and the opposing applications cannot be combined as narrower versions of one rule. The separate distribution claim therefore remains available without a new Court resolution of this contested showing or a new minimum-use threshold.

**Record limits:** The survey classified available files rather than actual downloads. Its residual ten percent was unknown, not established lawful use; copyrighted status likewise did not alone prove unauthorized use. Identified authorized and public-domain uses and their prospective development must be distinguished from an unsupported numerical conclusion. The differing legal evaluations of those qualified facts appear in the separate writings.

#### Precedent Treatment

- Sony Corp. of America v. Universal City Studios, Inc. retains its protection against imputing culpability from useful product distribution alone, including genuine prospective lawful capability. Affirmative inducement falls outside that protection.
- Kalem Co. v. Harper Brothers supplies the recognized purposeful-promotion principle, applied to software distribution with an infringing object and resulting infringement.
- A&M Records, Inc. v. Napster, Inc. is extended in principle from knowing material assistance to the separately supported inducement route; its particular knowledge-and-assistance, practical-supervision, statutory-protection and feasible-relief rules remain distinct.

#### Law After Decision

A distributor may be held responsible for resulting infringement when affirmative conduct establishes an object of promoting that infringement and the required causal connection. Product capability and network architecture do not immunize that conduct. Ordinary distribution and neutral support remain protected against liability on those facts alone. Independent distribution, knowing-assistance and vicarious theories retain their own predicates; the disputed product-specific Sony application has no controlling resolution. Further proceedings apply the announced rule separately to the parties' motions, defendants, relevant distribution and requested relief.

#### Separate Writings

**Justice Ginsburg, concurring, joined by Justice Kennedy.** The concurrence joins the inducement opinion and would also reject the distributors' summary judgment under Sony. The relevant inquiry concerns these products' reasonable capability for commercially significant lawful use, rather than benefits attributed to peer-to-peer technology in general. Particularized evidence matters even though lawful use need not be predominant and genuine future markets count.

The declarations and anecdotal examples do not, in this view, establish that capability beyond genuine dispute. General statements about authorized distribution, public-domain works or possible future uses may describe real opportunities without proving the commercially significant prospect asserted for these products. The available-file survey does not establish actual-download proportions; its residual is unknown, and the owners continued to dispute the lawful-use showing. A legal assumption that all uncertainty favors sufficient capability would give too little force to Sony's substantive condition.

The concurrence therefore sees an additional reason to set aside the distributors' judgment and would revisit this theory on a fuller record if inducement does not dispose of the case. It would not grant the owners' motion automatically, adopt a numerical requirement, or make infringement percentages, economic dependence or nonfiltering an independent final-liability test.

**Justice Breyer, concurring, joined by Justices Stevens and O'Connor.** This concurrence joins vacatur on inducement while accepting the lower court's favorable Sony capability determination. Concrete authorized and public-domain uses, together with their plausible development, give practical force to the difference between present misuse and the product's lawful capabilities. The survey's unknown residual is not a proved lawful percentage, and the position requires no precise numerical finding from it.

Sony protects technological development when a tool can serve substantial lawful purposes even though current users predominantly infringe. Requiring detailed profitability plans for each future use, or replacing capability with an inquiry into the distributor's present dependence on infringement, would make that protection uncertain and expose useful technologies before lawful markets have developed. The government's business-centered alternative would shift this balance without a sufficiently demonstrated need or administrable advantage.

The concurrence evaluates the proposed change against Sony's protection of innovation, the strength of the demonstrated enforcement need and the comparative costs of alteration. That assessment is a reason to retain Sony, not a new three-step liability test. Inducement and direct-infringement enforcement provide protection for copyright; Napster's distinct knowing-assistance route remains available. Its protective account concerns liability inferred from distribution alone and does not immunize purposeful promotion or proved material assistance. It would preserve the Sony determination while returning the independent inducement claim for adjudication.

Defendant-specific secondary responsibility, causal infringement, any properly remaining independent theories and effective monetary or injunctive relief require adjudication under their respective predicates. Past-version and other past-activity damages remain outside this reviewed judgment.

### National Cable & Telecommunications Association v. Brand X Internet Services, No. 04-277; FCC v. Brand X Internet Services, No. 04-281

Merits decision, June 27, 2005

#### Chronology and Posture

Argued March 29, 2005. On writs of certiorari to the Ninth Circuit, reviewing the same consolidated judgment, 345 F.3d 1120, that set aside the FCC's exclusion of telecommunications service from its cable-modem classification.

The Commission's 2002 declaratory ruling, 17 FCC Rcd. 4798, treats the described retail cable-modem offering as an integrated information service. Subscribers receive transmission together with end-user information functions such as retrieval, storage, domain-name resolution and email. The Ninth Circuit followed its earlier construction in AT&T Corp. v. City of Portland.

The questions are whether Congress delegated the relevant choice to the FCC, whether Portland forecloses the later interpretation, whether the integrated-offering classification is permissible under the statutory definitions, and whether the Commission adequately explained its differing cable and telephone-company DSL treatment. The cable association and federal petitioners seek reversal and restoration of the classification. The challengers seek continued invalidation and telecommunications-service treatment.

All nine Justices participated.

#### Opinion Topology

#### Controlling Propositions and Authority

The five Justices joining the Court's opinion directly support all four holdings in both dockets. Agreement rests on the actual statutory range and agency explanation, not on a conclusion that this classification is compelled.

##### I. The FCC exercised actual delegated authority over this classification

**Holding and operative rule:** A reviewing court accepts a reasonable agency construction within delegated statutory ambiguity only when Congress conferred authority to make the relevant choice with the force of law and the agency issued its interpretation in exercise of that authority. The Communications Act supplies that authority for the FCC's nationwide declaratory classification here; formal procedure alone is neither necessary nor sufficient to establish the delegated range or validate the agency's choice.

**Controlling explanation:** Mead requires identification of the legal assignment and the mechanism actually exercising it. Sections 151 and 201(b), read within the Act and the implementing authority recognized in Iowa Utilities Board, entrust the Commission with administering these service definitions. The nationwide declaratory ruling exercises that authority. It differs from Mead's revisable, transaction-specific Customs letter.

The court must independently enforce the definitions and the delegation's boundaries before sustaining a reasonable administrative construction. Expertise, complexity, official endorsement or formal process cannot supply authority that Congress withheld. Conversely, the absence of one particular procedural form does not categorically defeat an otherwise established exercise of delegated authority. Barnhart and Yellow Transportation preserve this distinction between a genuine administrative choice and a violation of statutory limits. The present classification must still satisfy that second inquiry.

##### II. Portland did not exhaust the interpretive choice Congress left to the Commission

**Holding and operative rule:** An earlier court of appeals' selection of a preferred statutory reading does not foreclose a different reasonable agency interpretation within a choice Congress actually delegated when the earlier holding and statute leave that choice open. A binding determination that the statute requires a particular answer remains a legal boundary; determining which kind of holding exists requires examining its substance rather than searching for an express ambiguity formula.

**Controlling explanation:** Chevron gives effect to Congress's allocation of genuine interpretive choice. A court's preferred answer cannot remove that allocation merely because the court considered the statute before the agency exercised its authority. Portland construed the cable transmission component without deciding the offering question as one having only a single lawful answer. Its treatment therefore does not itself defeat the FCC's later permissible construction.

The dissent argues that independent judicial interpretation fixes law and cannot later become an executive policy preference. That objection correctly distinguishes a judicially settled statutory boundary from approval of one available choice. The Court's rule preserves the boundary: it requires an actual delegation and a substantive examination of what the earlier court decided. It permits lawful administrative choice within the remaining range, rather than revision of the earlier judgment or disregard of a construction establishing the statute's only permissible meaning.

Neal preserved a particular controlling mixture-weight construction while leaving this general relationship open. Today's bounded circuit-precedent rule supplies the answer here. The distinct force of a Supreme Court construction is outside the presented Portland question; such a construction may settle statutory meaning and remove ambiguity. The Court does not decide that different question. Resolving the preserved circuit-precedent objection establishes the legal framework governing this order and the remand.

##### III. The described integrated retail offering may be classified as an information service

**Holding and operative rule:** The FCC may reasonably treat the described retail cable-modem service as an integrated information-service offering rather than a separate public offering of telecommunications service. That permission depends on the actual end-user capabilities and their functional integration with transmission; merely bundling an independently offered transmission service with an unrelated information feature does not remove the transmission service from the statutory category.

**Controlling explanation:** Section 153(43) defines telecommunications as transmission of user-chosen information between user-specified points without changing its form or content as sent and received. Section 153(46) requires an offering of that transmission for a fee to the public, or to a class effectively available to the public, regardless of facilities. Section 153(20) separately covers the offering of information-generating, acquiring, storing, transforming, processing, retrieving, utilizing or availability capabilities through telecommunications, including electronic publishing. It excludes capabilities used to manage, control or operate a telecommunications system or manage a telecommunications service.

The statute thus contemplates information service using telecommunications without necessarily making every transmission input a separate public offering. The Commission's paragraphs 38–41 describe genuine end-user retrieval, storage, email and access functions integrated with cable transmission. Those functions go beyond internal network management. Optional nonuse of a feature does not alone remove it from the service offered.

The dissent identifies a real paid transmission pathway retaining the user's information unchanged. That technical component is material, but it does not conclusively establish the identity of the finished service offered to subscribers. On this account of functional integration, the Commission's construction falls within the statutory range. A telephone service does not escape Title II merely by being packaged with voicemail or another separate feature. The management exclusion likewise prevents routine internal network operations from turning an otherwise offered transmission service into an information service.

Gulf Power left this classification open; its attachment ruling supplies no contrary answer. Under section 153(44), common-carrier treatment attaches only to the extent the provider furnishes telecommunications service. The sustained classification resolves that predicate for this described offer.

##### IV. The Commission gave a reasoned explanation for the cable and DSL distinction

**Holding and operative rule:** The Commission's differential treatment of the described cable offering and telephone-company DSL is not arbitrary and capricious on the reasons it supplied. An agency may address distinct regulatory histories incrementally within its statutory authority when it explains the material differences and its policy choice; a prior policy or technological resemblance does not alone compel identical treatment.

**Controlling explanation:** The Commission's paragraphs 42–44 identify the Computer II framework applied to telephone-company facilities, the historical bottleneck role of those carriers and its decision not to extend that framework automatically to the cable setting. It also addresses emerging broadband alternatives and investment and innovation objectives. Those are the agency's actual reasons for considering cable in a different regulatory setting, rather than an explanation supplied by the reviewing court.

Smiley permits adequately explained reconsideration within lawful authority while retaining review for unexplained change or disregard of legitimate reliance. The Commission's explanation meets that requirement here. The statutory classification must independently be permissible, as the preceding holding establishes; preferred regulatory consequences cannot cure a forbidden classification. The Court resolves the challenged 2002 cable treatment.

#### Precedent Treatment

- Chevron U.S.A. Inc. v. Natural Resources Defense Council, Inc. is applied to a reasonable choice within actual delegated interpretive authority, with independent enforcement of statutory boundaries.
- United States v. Mead Corp. supplies the actual-delegation and actual-exercise requirements; this nationwide declaratory mechanism differs from the revisable Customs letters. Formality remains evidence rather than a necessary or sufficient condition.
- AT&T Corp. v. Iowa Utilities Board supplies the Act's implementing-authority principle while preserving limits on individual rules.
- Barnhart v. Walton and Yellow Transportation, Inc. v. Michigan supply the distinction between a permissible delegated construction and the only construction Congress permits.
- Neal v. United States retains its particular binding mixture-weight construction and its express limit concerning the unresolved broader agency question. Today's prior-circuit rule resolves that separate issue at the stated scope.
- National Cable & Telecommunications Association v. Gulf Power Co. retains its pole-attachment holdings and express reservation of Internet-service classification.
- Smiley v. Citibank preserves lawful, adequately explained interpretive change and the independent arbitrary-and-capricious inquiry.
- AT&T Corp. v. City of Portland does not foreclose the later permissible FCC choice because it did not resolve the offering question as having only one lawful answer.

#### Law After Decision

Courts must first identify the agency's actual delegated authority and independently enforce statutory limits. An earlier circuit interpretation selecting a preferred answer does not foreclose a later reasonable administrative choice where the holding and statute leave that delegated range open; fixed statutory boundaries continue to bind. For the described retail cable-modem service, the Commission may use the integrated-information-service classification, which leaves the offering outside mandatory Title II treatment under that choice. The Commission's actual explanation also sustains its challenged cable/DSL distinction. These holdings permit the specific classification rather than compel it or authorize a general bundling exemption.

#### Separate Writings

**Justice Scalia, dissenting, Part I, joined by Chief Justice Stone-Zsela and Justices Souter and Ginsburg.** The cable subscriber purchases the transmission of chosen information between chosen points without a change in form or content. That paid retail function retains its statutory identity when sold with information capabilities. Congress did not require separate billing or a stand-alone sale before transmission is offered to the public.

The dissent distinguishes transmission delivered to the subscriber from transmission that an information provider merely uses upstream as an input. Its construction therefore does not make every Internet service provider a telecommunications carrier. Genuine information functions can coexist with an identifiable transmission offering; management, routing or incidental processing cannot erase a service satisfying the definition. The Commission's explanation supplies an account of integration, but the dissent finds the actual retail pathway legally distinct within the sold bundle.

Gulf Power left classification open, and Mead preserves the statutory boundary even when an agency acts within an assigned field. Investment or deregulation goals must be pursued through lawful powers, including section 160 forbearance. That provision requires findings that enforcement is unnecessary to ensure just and reasonable charges, practices, classifications or regulations free of unjust or unreasonable discrimination, unnecessary to protect consumers, and that forbearance is consistent with the public interest. The Commission must consider promotion of competitive market conditions, including enhanced competition among telecommunications providers. Section 160(d), subject to section 251(f), also withholds forbearance from sections 251(c) and 271 until their requirements are fully implemented. Potential Title I ancillary authority does not automatically supply every common-carrier obligation after classification outside telecommunications service; section 153(44)'s limit remains material. The dissent would affirm invalidation of the transmission exclusion and return lawful implementation to the FCC. The statutory error makes a separate final cable/DSL arbitrariness ruling unnecessary on this route.

**Justice Scalia, dissenting, Part II.** Scalia additionally distinguishes a court's authoritative independent construction of statutory meaning from judicial approval of one permissible administrative choice. The former fixes law subject to lawful judicial reconsideration; the latter leaves delegated alternatives available. A later agency cannot convert completed independent construction into a nonbinding preference merely because the earlier opinion did not declare the statute unambiguous. Chief Justice Stone-Zsela joins this distinction and its application to Portland.

Scalia alone adds that executive displacement of judicially fixed meaning raises the judicial-finality concern illustrated by Chicago & Southern Air Lines v. Waterman. He also disputes the need for a general prior-court rule here because this Court is not bound by Portland and can examine statutory permissibility directly. The majority's substantive, rather than formula-based, inquiry answers part of his concern but does not persuade him that an independent construction leaves executive revision available. His objection rests on those judicial-authority grounds; Neal's particular holding does not already dictate a universal prohibition. Souter and Ginsburg join neither Part II ground.

**Chief Justice Stone-Zsela, separately dissenting.** The Chief Justice joins the offered-transmission objection and the stated distinction between authoritative statutory meaning and an actually preserved delegated choice. The precedent question requires resolution because it is the Ninth Circuit's ground and the petitioners expressly seek authority to displace that construction. Chevron and Mead preserve Congress's real assignments; lawful judicial reconsideration supplies the route for changing an already adjudicated legal meaning.

On the statutory disposition he supports, the reviewing court should require an FCC implementation decision on a definite, enforceable schedule. The Commission must identify the applicable duties and any properly supported statutory forbearance, explain material technological and market distinctions, and justify a bounded transition if operations require one. A policy preference cannot preserve the rejected categorical exclusion indefinitely. The agency must make the assigned implementation choices within the corrected statutory category rather than receive judicially selected rates, access terms or network design.

### Town of Castle Rock v. Gonzales, No. 04-278

Merits decision, June 27, 2005

#### Chronology and Posture

Argued March 21, 2005. Certiorari to the Tenth Circuit's en banc judgment reinstating a municipal procedural-due-process claim under 42 U.S.C. §1983. Gonzales alleges that police, acting under a Town policy, failed meaningfully to respond after her husband took their daughters contrary to an individualized protective order. The order allowed specified parenting time, including a midweek dinner arranged on reasonable notice; it did not prohibit every contact. The children were later found dead. The Town seeks dismissal; Gonzales seeks adjudication and damages upon proof of the necessary elements. The questions concern the asserted state-created enforcement entitlement and the pleaded procedural claim. Allegations are considered at the dismissal stage.

All nine Justices participated at argument and decision.

#### Judgment

**Reversed, 6–3.**

**Supporting:** O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas and Breyer.
**Opposing:** Stone-Zsela, Stevens and Ginsburg.

#### Opinion Topology

SOUTER, J., delivered the opinion of the Court, in which O'CONNOR, SCALIA, KENNEDY, THOMAS and BREYER, JJ., joined. STEVENS, J., filed a dissenting opinion, in which STONE-ZSELA, C. J., and GINSBURG, J., joined. STONE-ZSELA, C. J., also filed a dissenting opinion.

#### Controlling Propositions and Authority

##### Mandatory enforcement steps do not themselves establish constitutional property

**Holding and operative rule:** A state direction that police take specified enforcement steps upon qualifying violations does not create a beneficiary's constitutional property interest without an independently secured substantive benefit distinct from that enforcement procedure. Colorado's protective-order statute and Gonzales's individualized order prescribe enforcement against the restrained person but do not secure the asserted independent property interest; the municipal procedural-due-process claim therefore fails at its necessary substantive predicate.

**Authority:** The Opinion of the Court receives six complete joins: O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas and Breyer. The ground applies even on the assumption that the statutory enforcement duties are mandatory.

**Controlling explanation:** Board of Regents v. Roth requires an entitlement to a substantive benefit grounded in independent law. Doe v. District of Columbia distinguishes such an entitlement from mandatory investigation or enforcement procedures. Sandin v. Conner directs attention to real substantive constraints rather than mandatory words alone; its prison-liberty application supplies no automatic rule for property in police action.

Colorado directs officers to use every reasonable means to enforce a protective order. Arrest, or pursuit of a warrant when immediate arrest is impractical, is required when information supplies probable cause both that the restrained person violated or attempted to violate the order and that the person was properly served or had actual notice of the order's existence and substance. Those commands have legal force. Yet their object is the executive enforcement mechanism against another person. An identified beneficiary and defined triggers do not by themselves transform the prescribed official steps into a separately secured substantive benefit. The reverse-side notice states enforcement law; it does not make the police parties to a decree guaranteeing the beneficiary a service.

The dissent regards reasonable enforcement itself as the substantive service and correctly distinguishes discretion over means from discretion to refuse a duty. That distinction does not answer the further property question. A binding direction can regulate officials without creating constitutional property in the process they must follow. The Court's conclusion therefore does not rest on giving officers unlimited discretion to disregard the statute. Nor does it turn on whether the claimed interest can be sold or assigned a monetary price. The pleaded policy cannot dispense with the required underlying property interest.

#### Precedent Treatment

- Board of Regents v. Roth is applied: independent law must secure a substantive entitlement before the procedural protection attaches.
- Doe v. District of Columbia is applied to the distinction between prescribed investigative or enforcement steps and independently protected property; its reservation for a genuinely independent entitlement remains operative.
- Sandin v. Conner is applied for its substantive-constraint distinction, without transplanting its prison-liberty application into a property claim.
- DeShaney v. Winnebago County Department of Social Services retains its distinct rule about the absence of a general constitutional duty to protect against private violence. The present decision examines the claimed state-created property interest independently.
- Connecticut Department of Public Safety v. Doe retains its requirement that the proposed process concern facts material under the governing substantive law; its assumed-interest analysis supplies no property interest here.

#### Law After Decision

The prescribed protective-order enforcement mechanism does not itself constitute the property claimed in this procedural-due-process action. Mandatory language, a named beneficiary and substantive enforcement triggers must be distinguished from an independently secured substantive benefit. The judgment does not erase Colorado's enforcement duties. The separate requirements of municipal responsibility and damages continue to govern claims that establish an underlying constitutional deprivation.

#### Separate Writings

**Justice Stevens, joined by Chief Justice Stone-Zsela and Justice Ginsburg, dissenting.** The dissent treats reasonable enforcement as a substantive protective service rather than property in administrative steps alone. The individualized order identifies its beneficiary, and the statute specifies the conditions requiring a reasonable response. Discretion over practicable means and the inability to promise successful rescue do not eliminate that obligation. Roth protects legally secured benefits without confining them to transferable or market-valued interests.

The dissent distinguishes Doe's investigative-procedure limitation from this claimed entitlement to actual enforcement. Sandin requires attention to the whole legal constraint, and DeShaney's rejection of a general duty to protect does not answer a particular state-created claim. The order's parenting permissions and the notice and probable-cause requirements remain essential to proving that the obligation was triggered. The complaint's alleged policy of refusing meaningful consideration is sufficient to proceed under that bounded theory.

The dissent would affirm reinstatement of the municipal claim and remand for proof of the qualifying circumstances, deprivation, constitutionally inadequate process, municipal policy or custom, causal injury and available relief. Mathews calls for process fitted to the urgency, and Zinermon distinguishes an established policy from a random unauthorized failure. Repeated contacts alone do not establish whether the request received meaningful consideration. Nominal relief for a proved process denial and compensation for the children's deaths have different predicates; neither the existence of a duty nor the tragic result establishes compensatory causation.

**Chief Justice Stone-Zsela, also dissenting.** Joining the substantive-service dissent, the Chief Justice would hold that the pleaded policy-level denial states a procedural claim when officials fail to provide a practicable, timely opportunity to establish the statutory conditions and receive an intelligible determination. Receiving the order and material information, genuinely assessing the conditions and communicating a reason for refusal can allow useful correction without delaying emergency action through a formal hearing. Monell still requires proof that the Town's policy caused the procedural deprivation. Any compensatory recovery must correspond to proved causal injury; the children's deaths cannot automatically measure damages merely because an enforcement duty existed.

#### Source Notes

No. 04-278; 42 U.S.C. §1983; Colo. Rev. Stat. §18-6-803.5; the individualized protective order and its enforcement notice. The order's parenting permissions, violation or attempted violation, probable cause, and proper service or actual notice of the order's existence and substance remain distinct requirements. The complaint alleges a policy of nonenforcement; municipal policy and causation are not findings at this stage.

#### Mandate, Remedy, and Stage

The Tenth Circuit's judgment reinstating the municipal procedural-due-process claim is reversed. The lower court must enter a conforming judgment restoring dismissal of that reviewed theory. The separate substantive-due-process and individual-officer dispositions remain undisturbed.

**End of entry: Town of Castle Rock v. Gonzales, Merits decision, June 27, 2005.**

### Van Orden v. Perry, No. 03-1500

Merits decision, June 27, 2005

#### Chronology and Posture

Argued March 2, 2005. Certiorari to the Fifth Circuit, 351 F.3d 173, reviewing the judgment sustaining Texas's Ten Commandments monument on the State Capitol grounds. Van Orden encounters the display while using the grounds and seeks a declaration and removal. Texas relies on the monument's donated origin, civic surroundings and historical presentation. The record includes visitor materials, signs and trial-video recitations supporting that description. The question is whether the complete official presentation explains religious history or adopts the commands as governmental truth.

All nine Justices participated at argument and decision.

#### Judgment

**Reversed and remanded, 5–4.**

**Supporting:** Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Souter and Ginsburg.
**Opposing:** Scalia, Kennedy, Thomas and Breyer.

#### Opinion Topology

STONE-ZSELA, C. J., delivered the opinion of the Court, in which STEVENS, O'CONNOR, SOUTER and GINSBURG, JJ., joined. STONE-ZSELA, C. J., also filed a concurring opinion. BREYER, J., filed a dissenting opinion, in which SCALIA, KENNEDY and THOMAS, JJ., joined.

#### Controlling Propositions and Authority

The Court's two holdings receive five complete joins: Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Souter and Ginsburg.

##### I. The State's presentation adopts the religious commands

**Holding and operative rule:** Government may preserve and explain religious history, but may not present theological commands as its own authoritative civic message. Texas's complete presentation of this monument, including its attribution, surroundings and visitor materials, continues that prohibited governmental adoption and violates the Establishment Clause.

**Authority:** Part I of the Opinion of the Court controls the actual-function rule and its application through the five direct joins stated above.

**Controlling explanation:** Agostini v. Felton requires examination of the governmental act and the function it performs. A secular civic objective cannot justify using public authority to adopt religious doctrine. Robinson v. City of Edmond distinguishes actual historical exposition from making religious content an authoritative civil identifier. Freedom From Religion Foundation v. City of Marshfield requires attention to the continuing public presentation rather than nominal descriptions of ownership or purpose.

Texas selected and maintains this permanent monument on the Capitol grounds. The gift attribution, other civic memorials, visitor brochure, signs and trial-video account are genuine evidence supporting the State's description of an integrated historical presentation. The Court gives that evidence its stated force. Yet general commemoration of civic ideals and identification of a donated artifact do not sufficiently change the present function of these prominently displayed theological commands. In this setting, the State retains them as authoritative civic instruction rather than adequately making their historical place the object of explanation.

The dissent weighs the same complete context differently, treating the donated monument as a preserved source within a civic collection. That is the central disagreement. The surrounding historical apparatus supplies context, but does not resolve how this particular religious text functions in the State's continuing presentation. Neither the age of the monument nor absence of compelled participation removes the independent prohibition on governmental adoption. Capitol Square Review and Advisory Board v. Pinette protects genuinely private expression; the State's permanent selected display has a different source and function.

##### II. The remedy must end the prohibited governmental presentation

**Holding and operative rule:** The district court must provide prospective relief that effectively ends the unconstitutional governmental function. It may select an effective lawful change in presentation rather than require removal if that change fully supplies the relief to which Van Orden is entitled; a nominal adjustment leaving the prohibited function intact is insufficient.

**Authority:** Part II has the same five direct joins.

**Controlling explanation:** Marshfield makes the actual resulting presentation decisive when a government proposes a cure. Removing the monument from the official presentation or incorporating it into a genuinely explanatory historical arrangement can therefore be considered on the remedial record. The judicial task is to test the arrangement's actual function and ensure that the violation ends. Religious content alone does not defeat a genuine historical exhibit, and preserving an object does not establish that the State has ceased adopting its commands. The district court must choose and enforce an effective lawful measure in light of the actual proposal.

#### Precedent Treatment

- Agostini v. Felton is applied as the unified governmental-act-and-function inquiry. Its modification of Lemon remains controlling; civic purpose cannot excuse prohibited governmental adoption.
- Robinson v. City of Edmond is applied for the distinction between historical explanation and official religious identification; its civil-badge application is not converted into a categorical monument rule.
- Freedom From Religion Foundation v. City of Marshfield is applied to the actual continuing presentation and effectiveness of a proposed cure; its private-ownership facts do not decide this State-owned display.
- Capitol Square Review and Advisory Board v. Pinette is distinguished because protection for genuine private expression does not determine the constitutional character of the State's own selected presentation.

#### Law After Decision

Official religious displays remain governed by the actual governmental function. Courts must assess the complete presentation, including real explanatory materials, and distinguish preservation of religious history from adoption of religious propositions through public authority. Texas's present arrangement crosses that boundary. Prospective relief must change that function effectively while permitting a genuinely lawful historical presentation.

#### Separate Writings

**Chief Justice Stone-Zsela, concurring.** The Chief Justice joins the Court's rule, application and effective-cure mandate. He would additionally require Texas to substantiate that any narrower adjustment fully cures the established function. He would require the district court, after receiving a proposed cure, to set a concrete compliance schedule, require evidence of the completed arrangement, retain enforcement authority and enjoin continuing unlawful presentation if Texas fails to comply. A nominal disclaimer alone cannot substitute for a change in what the State actually presents.

**Justice Breyer, joined by Justices Scalia, Kennedy and Thomas, dissenting.** The dissent applies the same prohibition on official adoption of religious doctrine but finds that Texas's complete presentation performs historical commemoration. The donor attribution, ordinary treatment of donated monuments, visitor materials, civic collection and preservation history explain how the religious artifact is used. The commands retain religious meaning, yet that meaning is presented as part of the history commemorated on the grounds rather than as a State demand for religious assent.

Agostini makes that actual function decisive. Robinson permits historical exposition while forbidding a faith's use as a permanent civil badge, and Marshfield requires examination of what the public is actually shown. State ownership distinguishes this monument from private speech, but does not make historical preservation itself an adoption of the artifact's beliefs. The monument's age helps explain its preserved commemorative role; it is not an exemption from review. The dissent would affirm on the full contextual account, without a separate rule allowing religious acknowledgment, favoring monotheism or requiring coercion before governmental adoption can be prohibited.

#### Source Notes

No. 03-1500; 351 F.3d 173. The record includes the State's ownership and selection, the 1961 gift attribution, the surrounding civic collection, visitor brochure and signage, trial-video recitations and the 1993 reinstallation. Their existence is accepted; their effect on the display's present governmental function is the constitutional dispute.

#### Mandate, Remedy, and Stage

The judgment sustaining the display is reversed. The case returns to the district court for remedial proceedings and effective prospective relief ending Texas's prohibited official presentation. The district court must evaluate Texas's actual proposed arrangement under the actual-function rule, select an effective lawful cure and retain authority to enforce compliance with the resulting decree.

**End of entry: Van Orden v. Perry, Merits decision, June 27, 2005.**
