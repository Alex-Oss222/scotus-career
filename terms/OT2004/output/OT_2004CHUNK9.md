### Alaska v. United States, No. 128, Original

Decision on exceptions, June 6, 2005

#### Chronology and Posture

Argued January 10, 2005. On Alaska's exceptions to the Special Master's report in this original title action. Counts I and II assert historic inland waters and juridical bays in the Alexander Archipelago; count IV contests federal retention of Glacier Bay submerged lands. The federal disclaimer resolves the adverse Tongass claim.

All nine Justices participated at argument and decision.

#### Judgment

Alaska's live exceptions are overruled. The Master's recommendations on the disputed Alexander Archipelago and Glacier Bay lands are accepted, and preparation of a conforming decree is directed.

| Judgment component | Disposition and vote | Supporting Justices | Opposing Justices | Operative direction |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Count I: historic waters | Exception overruled, 9–0 | Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer | — | Federal title in the identified extra-coastal pockets |
| Count II: juridical bays | Exception overruled, 9–0 | Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer | — | Claimed bay lines do not establish state title |
| Count IV: Glacier Bay | Exception overruled, 7–2 | Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Kennedy, Souter, Ginsburg and Breyer | Scalia and Thomas | Decree must recognize federal retention |
| Tongass disclaimer and decree preparation | Disclaimer accepted; preparation directed, 9–0 | Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer | — | Use established geographic descriptions and resolve implementation disagreements in the original action |

The parties must submit a proposed title decree through the Special Master. The Court retains jurisdiction pending a conforming decree. Scalia and Thomas agree to that procedure while seeking state title for Glacier Bay.

#### Opinion Topology

KENNEDY, J., delivered the opinion of the Court. SCALIA, J., filed an opinion concurring in part and dissenting in part, in which THOMAS, J., joined.

| Writing | Author | Joined by | Relationship to judgment | Scope joined |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Opinion of the Court, Part I | Kennedy | All other Justices | Overrules counts I and II | Historic waters and juridical bays |
| Opinion of the Court, Part II and related Part III direction | Kennedy | Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Souter, Ginsburg, Breyer | Overrules count IV | Glacier Bay retention and federal-title consequence |
| Opinion of the Court, remaining Part III | Kennedy | All other Justices | Directs proposed decree; accepts disclaimer | Preparation procedure and Tongass disposition |
| Concurrence in part and dissent in part | Scalia | Thomas | Accepts counts I/II and preparation; would sustain count IV | Section 6(e)'s transfer limitation and state title to Glacier Bay |

#### Holdings

##### Historic inland waters

**Holding and operative rule:** A State claiming additional submerged lands as historic inland waters must establish a continuous assertion of authority to exclude foreign navigation, including innocent passage, with foreign acquiescence. Alaska's evidence fails to establish that status for the disputed Alexander Archipelago pockets and enclaves beyond the ordinary coastal and island belts.

**Authority:** Part I of the Court's opinion, joined by all nine Justices, controls this holding and the following juridical-bay holding.

**Controlling explanation:** United States v. Alaska preserves the ordinary Convention coastline and a separate route for genuinely proved historic title. The earlier Cook Inlet decision identifies the relevant sovereignty: authority to exclude even innocent navigation. Regulation of fishing or trading is a different exercise of authority. The Russian treaty and related vessel incidents do not establish the required continuous claim; the 1903 boundary-arbitration assertion and disputed Marguerite seizure do not cure that deficiency. The British protest and the later federal correspondence permitting Canadian fishing outside the three-mile limits undermine the asserted continuity and acquiescence. Geography and governmental activity therefore cannot replace the specific historic-title showing. The Master's recommendation on count I is accepted.

##### The proposed juridical bays

**Holding and operative rule:** An asserted juridical bay must satisfy Article 7(2)'s actual geographic requirements: a well-marked indentation whose penetration in relation to its mouth creates landlocked waters and more than a mere coastal curvature, together with the separate semicircle-area condition. Even assuming Alaska's proposed assimilation of four islands into a constructive peninsula, its proposed North Bay and South Bay fail the well-marked-indentation requirement.

**Controlling explanation:** United States v. Louisiana and United States v. Maine recognize that particular islands can exceptionally form part of the mainland coast when their actual geographic relationship supports that treatment. They do not permit a chosen closing line to create the physical indentation the Convention requires. Assuming the asserted connections among Kuiu, Kupreanof, Mitkof and Dry Islands does not give the resulting expansive waters the distinct enclosed character of the sounds examined in Maine. The independent geographic defect disposes of count II without treating a desirable unified perimeter as the legal coastline. A particular assimilation must still produce waters meeting the Convention; it is not a substitute for those criteria.

##### Retention of Glacier Bay's submerged lands

**Holding and operative rule:** Federal retention of prestatehood submerged lands requires both clear inclusion in the reservation and clear intent to retain federal ownership through statehood. Glacier Bay's established inclusion and the Alaska Statehood Act's treatment of this wildlife reservation satisfy those distinct requirements, so the United States retains the identified submerged lands.

**Authority:** Part II, joined by Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Kennedy, Souter, Ginsburg and Breyer, controls. Scalia and Thomas dissent from this title determination and its decree consequences.

**Controlling explanation:** United States v. Alaska and Idaho v. United States require separate examination of reservation inclusion and retention through admission. The Master's inclusion finding is unexcepted. Retention rests on the Statehood Act and the reservation's actual wildlife-protection purposes. Section 6(e)'s initial clause transfers property used solely for conservation under three named game and fisheries enactments. Its proviso separately retains the described wildlife refuges and reservations, associated facilities, and general fisheries or wildlife research facilities. Reading that proviso with sections 5 and 6(m), as the 1997 Alaska decision requires, gives effect to retention of the described reservation rather than limiting the proviso to the initial clause's named enactments and sole-purpose requirement. Glacier Bay's marine habitat and wildlife protection bring it within that category. Alaska's contrary reading would treat the initial transfer clause as exhausting the proviso's operative retention function. The actual statute supplies continuation of federal ownership; the monument's designation or ecological purpose alone would not do so.

##### Conforming title decree

**Holding and operative rule:** The sovereign parties must submit a proposed decree using the Master's established geographic descriptions and the federal disclaimer of the Tongass claim. The decree must reflect federal title to the adjudicated Archipelago pockets and Glacier Bay lands and resolve the disclaimed controversy consistently with that disclaimer.

**Authority and explanation:** Part III has all nine Justices on decree preparation and the disclaimer; the seven-Justice Part II coalition controls Glacier Bay's substantive title direction. The report identifies the property adjudicated. Original-action supervision permits resolution of any genuine implementation disagreement using the report’s established geographic descriptions. Jurisdiction is retained for that work.

#### Precedent Treatment

- **United States v. Alaska (1997):** Applied to Convention baselines, island belts, independently proved historic waters and the separate inclusion/retention requirements; its construction of section 6(e)'s retention function governs the actual reservation inquiry.
- **United States v. Alaska (1975):** Applied for the sovereignty required to establish historic inland waters, including authority to exclude innocent passage.
- **United States v. Louisiana (1969):** Applied to the Convention and the exceptional possibility of assimilation grounded in actual geography.
- **United States v. Maine (1985):** Distinguished on the physical character of the claimed bays; an assimilated coast must still satisfy the Convention's indentation requirements.
- **Idaho v. United States (2001):** Applied to the reservation-specific examination of inclusion and timely congressional retention; its governing clear-intent inquiry remains in force.

#### Law After Decision

The ordinary Convention coastline and state coastal/island belts remain the starting point. Additional historic-water title requires the established sovereignty, continuity and acquiescence showing. Proposed juridical bays must meet the Convention's geographic criteria even when island assimilation is assumed. For Glacier Bay, the actual wildlife reservation and Statehood Act establish federal retention through admission under the separate clear-inclusion and clear-retention requirements.

#### Separate Writings

Scalia, joined by Thomas, agrees with rejection of the Archipelago claims but would recognize Alaska's Glacier Bay title. He reads section 6(e)'s reference to the particular transfer as confining its proviso to property covered by the initial clause's three specified enactments. Because Glacier Bay was created under the Antiquities Act, he finds that proviso insufficient to defeat the section 6(m) submerged-land transfer. He distinguishes the 1997 Alaska case's assumed coverage by the initial clause and treats the clear-intent requirement as demanding the actual statutory retention that he finds absent here. Wildlife purposes and inclusion establish a reservation but, in his view, do not supply the separate intent to withhold state title.

#### Source Notes

Alaska v. United States, No. 128, Original, 545 U.S. 75; Special Master's Report, counts I, II and IV; federal disclaimer concerning count III. Alaska Statehood Act §§5, 6(e), 6(m), 72 Stat. 339–343; Submerged Lands Act, 43 U.S.C. §§1301–1315; Convention on the Territorial Sea and the Contiguous Zone, art. 7(2), 15 U.S.T. 1606, 1609. The established report descriptions, rather than newly specified coordinates, delimit the decree.

#### Mandate, Remedy, and Stage

The original action remains pending for submission and settlement of the conforming decree. The report's descriptions and the Tongass disclaimer govern that preparation. Federal management of the lands adjudicated federal may continue; any genuine implementation disagreement returns through the Special Master for the Court's resolution.

**End of entry: Alaska v. United States, Decision on exceptions, June 6, 2005.**

### Spector v. Norwegian Cruise Line Ltd., No. 03-1388

Merits decision, June 6, 2005

#### Chronology and Posture

Argued February 28, 2005. On certiorari to the Fifth Circuit. Disabled passengers challenge foreign-flag cruise practices and barriers; the question is whether registry categorically excludes domestic passenger accommodations and transportation from Title III.

All nine Justices participated.

#### Judgment

The categorical foreign-flag exclusion is reversed and the case remanded for claim-specific adjudication.

| Judgment component | Disposition and vote | Supporting Justices | Opposing Justices | Remedy or remand |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Nonstructural passenger claims | Reverse and remand, 7–2 | Stone-Zsela, Stevens, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer | O'Connor and Scalia | Claim-specific adjudication under the applicable duties and defenses |
| Structural claims | Reverse categorical exclusion and remand, 6–3 | Stone-Zsela, Stevens, Kennedy, Souter, Ginsburg and Breyer | O'Connor, Scalia and Thomas | Further adjudication, including appellate consideration of the preserved design-regulations ground |

The lower courts must determine the applicable duty, proved violation and defense for each claim, including the preserved design-regulations ground. Any relief must rest on the adjudicated duty and the applicable cause of action. Thomas would retain structural dismissal while allowing nonstructural claims to proceed.

#### Opinion Topology

SOUTER, J., delivered the opinion of the Court in Parts I, II, III-A and IV, and an opinion in Part III-B. STONE-ZSELA, C. J., filed a concurring opinion concerning implementation of relief. GINSBURG, J., filed a concurring opinion, in which BREYER, J., joined. THOMAS, J., filed an opinion concurring in part and dissenting in part. SCALIA, J., filed a dissenting opinion, in which O'CONNOR, J., joined, and in whose structural-internal-affairs discussion THOMAS, J., joined.

#### Holdings

##### Domestic passenger accommodations and transportation

**Holding and operative rule:** Foreign registry alone does not exempt a cruise operator's public accommodations and covered transportation in United States waters from Title III of the Americans with Disabilities Act. Each passenger claim must be assessed under its particular statutory duty and any applicable maritime limitation.

**Authority:** Part I has seven votes for application-specific coverage: Stone-Zsela, Stevens, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer. Thomas's join permits nonstructural claims only. Parts II and IV's statutory analysis and complete remand are joined by Stone-Zsela, Stevens, Kennedy, Souter, Ginsburg and Breyer.

**Controlling explanation:** The enacted public-accommodation and specified-transportation categories reach these passenger services. Cunard Steamship Co. v. Mellon establishes domestic regulatory authority over foreign vessels in territorial waters, while Benz and McCulloch protect genuinely internal maritime affairs. Selling accommodations and imposing discriminatory passenger charges do not become internal crew governance merely because the vessel has a foreign flag. The Fifth Circuit's categorical rule therefore prevents adjudication of claims within the statute's ordinary reach. Each claimant must establish the particular duty and violation, and the operator must establish the statutory defense it invokes. Application-specific coverage preserves the distinction between domestic services and a particular intrusion on internal maritime affairs.

##### The statute's distinct duties and qualifications

**Holding and operative rule:** Disability-screening eligibility criteria require necessity to the offered service; necessary reasonable policy modifications remain subject to fundamental alteration. Necessary auxiliary aids retain their distinct fundamental-alteration and undue-burden defenses; barrier removal, and alternative access when removal is not readily achievable, retain their own readily-achievable conditions.

**Controlling explanation:** Title III does not collapse these duties into a single design mandate. PGA Tour v. Martin requires examination of the service and the particular requested modification. A substantial international legal conflict caused by a requested barrier removal, or a direct safety threat created by the modification itself, prevents that removal from being readily achievable. The latter inquiry differs from the statutory direct-threat defense concerning a person who poses a significant risk that reasonable modifications or aids cannot eliminate. Actual international obligations and evidence control; a speculative survey of every possible foreign rule does not. These statutory qualifications permit domestic passenger duties while protecting demonstrated safety and maritime interests. Allegations of inaccessible facilities, surcharges and restricted services require their respective predicates.

##### A particular intrusion on internal maritime affairs

**Holding and operative rule:** A particular Title III obligation regulating a foreign ship's genuinely internal affairs requires the congressional clarity appropriate to that interference. The application-specific limit reaches a permanent and significant alteration of basic ship design or construction that interferes with internal maritime affairs; it does not exempt the ship's unrelated passenger services.

**Authority:** Part III-A is joined at this confined level by Stone-Zsela, Stevens, Kennedy, Souter and Thomas. Thomas would exclude all structural claims and disagrees with the remaining remand. Ginsburg and Breyer do not join an additional internal-affairs exclusion after the statute's international-conflict limits are satisfied. 

**Controlling explanation:** Benz and McCulloch distinguish regulation of a vessel's internal order from local commercial transactions; Ariadne preserves domestic protection of persons affected by vessel operations. The required clarity responds to the particular interference. A permanent, significant redesign affecting that order differs from a passenger surcharge and may implicate the rule even when another statutory claim remains enforceable. The agreed rule supplies no conclusion that every physical alteration constitutes the identified interference. 

##### Reinstatement and adjudication of the passenger claims

**Holding and operative rule:** The categorical foreign-flag judgment is reversed and the claims remanded for adjudication under the applicable duties, defenses and maritime rule. The district court's distinct reliance on absent ship-specific design regulations remains for appellate consideration because the Fifth Circuit did not reach it.

**Authority and explanation:** Part IV has the six complete-remand votes identified above. Thomas joins return of nonstructural claims but would retain structural dismissal. The actual judgment rested on a blanket exemption, not findings resolving the requested alterations. A preserved structural claim therefore requires a developed record addressing its necessity, statutory feasibility and safety, any actual international conflict, and the regulations issue. Established violations require relief authorized by the applicable cause of action. This appellate disposition reinstates adjudication and leaves the content of any structural compliance order to the findings supporting the duty.

#### Precedent Treatment

- **Benz v. Compania Naviera Hidalgo (1957) and McCulloch v. Sociedad Nacional de Marineros (1963):** Applied to genuinely internal maritime regulation and distinguished from ordinary domestic passenger commerce; their clear-statement protection remains operative at the particular interference.
- **Cunard Steamship Co. v. Mellon (1923):** Applied for territorial authority over foreign ships in United States waters.
- **Longshoremen v. Ariadne Shipping Co. (1970):** Applied to distinguish local interests from exclusively internal ship affairs.
- **PGA Tour, Inc. v. Martin (2001):** Applied to individualized assessment of the requested service modification and the statutory fundamental-alteration defense.
- **Clark v. Martinez (2005):** The four Justices joining Part III-B distinguish its common statutory detention limitation from an application-specific maritime inquiry. Thomas's different view of Clark is explained in his separate writing.

#### Law After Decision

Foreign-flag status alone does not defeat domestic Title III passenger claims. The statute supplies distinct duty-specific qualifications, including its applicable feasibility, fundamental-alteration, undue-burden and safety limits. Five Justices adopt the confined application-specific maritime rule for genuinely internal affairs; six require further adjudication of structural claims.

#### Separate Writings

**Part III-B, supported by Stone-Zsela, Stevens, Kennedy and Souter.** These Justices permit consideration of either the applicable statutory qualification or the maritime limit first when that resolves the particular claim. They distinguish Clark's common detention provision from duty-specific maritime interference. Thomas rejects both the sequencing discretion and their reconciliation with Clark; the discussion therefore supplies no controlling sequence or new Clark rule.

**Stone-Zsela's concurrence.** Once a challenged charge or policy is proved unlawful and prospective relief is properly before the court, the remedy should govern that policy and those it affects unless the operator establishes that a narrower order fully cures the violation. The district court should identify the proved practice and set an enforceable compliance measure within the applicable cause of action. That proposed allocation of remedial reach does not itself decide liability or specify construction work.

**Ginsburg, joined by Breyer.** The statute's actual international-conflict and safety qualifications adequately protect foreign sovereign interests in this passenger-service dispute. Once those conflicts are avoided, they see no additional internal-affairs exemption. They therefore join coverage, the statutory analysis and complete remand, but withhold assent from a separate exclusion based on permanent structural effects. Their conditional acceptance of an application-specific clear-statement method does not endorse the exclusion itself.

**Thomas.** A duty-by-duty inquiry permits nonstructural passenger claims, but structural obligations intrude on internal affairs without the required clear statement. He would retain dismissal of those claims. He rejects both the Court's expansion of readily-achievable analysis to actual international conflict and the four Justices' discretionary sequencing. Possible international discord is sufficient for his structural rule. His continued disagreement with Clark's uniform-limitation approach supports application-specific treatment, not complete remand.

**Scalia, joined by O'Connor.** Title III's integrated regulation of shipboard services and design requires a clear congressional instruction before application to foreign vessels. Potential international conflict and structural interference trigger that protection without proof of an actual treaty violation. They would therefore affirm dismissal of all present shipboard claims. Thomas joins the structural premise but rejects their extension of it to every nonstructural passenger duty.

#### Source Notes

Spector v. Norwegian Cruise Line Ltd., No. 03-1388, 545 U.S. 119; 356 F.3d 641 (5th Cir. 2004). Americans with Disabilities Act, 42 U.S.C. §§12181–12189, including §§12182(b)(2)(A), 12182(b)(3) and 12184. The unresolved regulations ground and fact-dependent requested alterations remain questions in the judicial record.

#### Mandate, Remedy, and Stage

The case returns for appellate consideration of the remaining design-regulations issue and further claim-specific proceedings consistent with the judgment. The reinstated claims require findings supporting liability and any enforceable compliance measure.

**End of entry: Spector v. Norwegian Cruise Line Ltd., Merits decision, June 6, 2005.**

### United States v. Kincade

Merits decision, June 6, 2005

#### Chronology and Posture

Review of the Ninth Circuit en banc judgment reported at 379 F.3d 813 (August 18, 2004). The question is whether the challenged DNA collection condition and refusal sanction are lawful under the Fourth Amendment.

All nine Justices participated.

#### Judgment

**Disposition:** Reverse in the stated respects and remand, **6–3**.

**Supporting:** Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Souter and Ginsburg.

**Opposing:** Kennedy, Thomas and Breyer.

The supervising authority must cease enforcing the invalid requirement against Kincade. The district court must identify and remove refusal-dependent consequences, correct the operative release judgment, and address deletion and return or destruction of the obtained material and profile sufficient to cure the collection, subject to an independently established lawful basis. It must verify the required correction. Independently supported custody and release obligations continue at their lawful scope.

#### Opinion Topology

STONE-ZSELA, C. J., delivered the opinion of the Court, in which STEVENS, O'CONNOR, SCALIA, SOUTER, and GINSBURG, JJ., joined. KENNEDY, J., filed a dissenting opinion, in which THOMAS and BREYER, JJ., joined.

#### Holdings

##### Compulsory investigative DNA collection during supervised release

**Holding and operative rule:** A qualifying conviction and a supervised-release condition do not by themselves authorize compulsory biological sampling and protected examination for a general criminal-investigation database. The government must establish individualized suspicion together with lawful search authority, valid consent, or a constitutionally sufficient exception whose purpose and scope fit the search; it has established none for Kincade's demand.

**Authority:** The entire opinion of the Court is joined by Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Souter and Ginsburg; that coalition controls both holdings and their operative remedy.

**Controlling explanation:** Rise v. Oregon already requires sufficient authority for this bodily acquisition despite conviction coverage, medically modest extraction, identification benefits and restricted uses. The DNA Act's safeguards reduce aspects of the intrusion but do not provide the missing constitutional justification. United States v. Knights upheld a search on the combined release condition and individualized suspicion; its reservation of suspicionless searches is not permission to undertake this one. City of Indianapolis v. Edmond and Ferguson v. City of Charleston direct attention to the program's actual purpose while preserving properly justified supervisory searches. Matching biological traces to solve ordinary crimes does not acquire a separate administrative purpose through an identification label. The government's continuing correctional responsibility and Kincade's reduced privacy are relevant, but Rise rejects their sufficiency for the offered suspicionless investigative acquisition. The competing defined-offender exception would replace that controlling boundary rather than apply Knights.

##### Correction of the refusal judgment and obtained material

**Holding and operative rule:** The lower courts must remove the unconstitutional collection requirement and any consequence attributable solely to refusal, correct the operative release judgment, and provide effective relief from its continuing effects. Because collection actually occurred, the district court must address the appropriate disposition of the obtained material and database profile under this acquisition ruling and ordinary remedial law, including deletion and return or destruction necessary to cure the violation, subject to an independently established lawful basis.

**Controlling explanation:** The original demand produced the July 15, 2002 refusal judgment and later compulsory collection after a drug event led to incarceration. The later event cannot retroactively supply authority for the original demand. It may independently support a lawful consequence at its proper scope, so implementation must identify the actual judgments and separate their grounds. The constitutional remedy concerns this demand, its dependent sanction and properly presented continuing relief. Collection, source-material retention, database retention and later evidentiary use are distinct subjects; the court must determine and verify the correction actually required. Pennsylvania Board of Probation and Parole v. Scott addresses institutional use of unlawfully obtained evidence and does not itself resolve the disposition of an acquired DNA sample. The present remand therefore requires an effective, record-specific cure rather than treating either that decision or the collection violation as an automatic answer to every later use.

#### Precedent Treatment

- **Rise v. Oregon (1996):** Applied directly to the insufficiency of conviction coverage, reduced privacy, modest medical means and controlled investigative uses to justify compulsory bodily acquisition; its controlling search rule remains in force.
- **United States v. Knights (2001):** Applied to its combined condition-and-suspicion rationale; the reserved suspicionless question supplies no affirmative authorization here.
- **City of Indianapolis v. Edmond (2000) and Ferguson v. City of Charleston (2001):** Applied to the actual program purpose and the distinction between ordinary crime investigation and a sufficient special need.
- **Griffin v. Wisconsin (1987):** Its justified supervisory-search setting remains distinct from the offered categorical biological-identification ground.
- **Pennsylvania Board of Probation and Parole v. Scott (1998):** Distinguished as an exclusionary-rule decision about institutional use; sample and profile correction require the actual acquisition ruling and ordinary remedial law.
- **Illinois v. Caballes (2005):** Distinguished because the bounded contraband examination there does not remove the acknowledged bodily-search character of compulsory blood collection and protected analysis.

#### Law After Decision

The investigative DNA program cannot derive sufficient search authority from conviction coverage and supervision alone. Rise governs the bodily acquisition, with the actual authority, consent or constitutionally sufficient exception required for the search. Courts correcting unconstitutional refusal enforcement must separate dependent consequences from independent grounds and address continuing material or profile effects through effective relief on the actual claims.

#### Separate Writings

Kennedy, joined by Thomas and Breyer, would recognize a defined identification exception for lawfully supervised offenders selected by objective conviction criteria. The continuing correctional relationship, ordinary medical draw, restricted identification profile and authorized-use controls make this program reasonable in their view. They maintain the position taken in their Rise partial dissent and would limit its contrary sufficiency rule in this setting. They acknowledge that Knights did not decide the suspicionless question; its consideration of a lawful supervision relationship supports their proposed extension, not an existing categorical authorization. Their exception depends on the actual collection and use limits rather than a general forfeiture of privacy.

#### Source Notes

United States v. Kincade, 379 F.3d 813 (9th Cir. 2004) (en banc); DNA Analysis Backlog Elimination Act of 2000, Pub. L. 106-546, 114 Stat. 2726. The record includes the March 25, 2002 demand, July 15, 2002 refusal judgment and subsequent compulsory collection after incarceration.

#### Mandate, Remedy, and Stage

The case returns through the Ninth Circuit for correction of Kincade’s operative judgment. The district court must identify consequences traceable to the invalid demand, resolve the continuing material and profile questions, and verify the required correction. The later drug event retains only the consequences it independently supports.

**End of entry: United States v. Kincade, Merits decision, June 6, 2005.**

### Bradshaw v. Stumpf, No. 04-637

Merits decision, June 13, 2005

#### Chronology and Posture

Argued April 19, 2005. Certiorari to the Sixth Circuit, reviewing 367 F.3d 594 in the federal habeas proceeding filed in November 1995. The questions concern Stumpf’s understanding of aggravated murder when he pleaded guilty and the consequences of the State’s differing accounts of who shot Mrs. Stout. Counsel represented that the elements had been explained, and Stumpf confirmed his understanding while denying that he fired the shots. The sentencing panel found him the principal offender. At Wesley’s later trial, the State presented evidence identifying Wesley as the shooter. Ohio seeks reinstatement of the denial of habeas relief; Stumpf seeks conviction and sentence relief or independent consideration of the capital claim.

All nine Justices participated at argument and decision.

#### Judgment

The Court reverses the plea-invalidity ruling, vacates the intertwined prosecutorial-inconsistency disposition, and remands for independent adjudication of the death-sentence claim.

| Judgment component | Disposition and vote | Supporting Justices | Opposing Justices | Remedy or remand |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Conviction relief based on inadequate understanding of intent and the later shooter account | Reversed, 9–0 | Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg, Breyer | — | Plea and conviction preserved against those grounds |
| Independent death-sentence challenge | Intertwined disposition vacated and remanded, 9–0 | Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg, Breyer | — | Separate adjudication under the applicable pre-AEDPA standards |

#### Opinion Topology

O'CONNOR, J., delivered the unanimous opinion of the Court. SOUTER, J., filed a concurring opinion, in which GINSBURG, J., joined. THOMAS, J., filed a concurring opinion, in which SCALIA, J., joined. Each concurrence accompanies a complete join of the Court’s opinion.

#### Holdings

##### The record establishes understanding of purposeful aggravated-murder complicity

**Holding and operative rule:** A guilty plea is knowing as to an essential offense element when the record establishes that competent counsel adequately explained the element and the defendant understood it; the judge need not personally give a prescribed recital. Stumpf’s counsel’s express representation and his own confirmation establish understanding here, and his denial that he fired the fatal shots does not contradict purposeful aggravated-murder complicity under Ohio law.

**Authority:** The common Opinion of the Court coalition stated in Opinion Topology joins this rule and explanation.

**Controlling explanation:** Henderson v. Morgan requires real notice of the offense’s essential elements. Brady v. United States and Boykin v. Alabama require informed, voluntary assent, while Bousley v. United States preserves accurate understanding of the offense rather than a ritual formula. Counsel represented that the elements had been explained, and Stumpf personally acknowledged that advice. His intellectual limitations and counsel’s resistance to identifying him as the shooter require examining that affirmative record; they do not erase it. Ohio’s offense permits liability through purposeful participation with the specific intent to cause death. Personal firing of the shots is a different fact. The Sixth Circuit therefore could not infer inadequate understanding of intent merely from Stumpf’s denial of shooter status. Counsel’s representation matters because this record establishes actual understanding, rather than because it creates an irrebuttable presumption.

##### The later shooter account does not invalidate the informed plea

**Holding and operative rule:** The State’s later presentation identifying Wesley as the shooter does not invalidate Stumpf’s plea to purposeful aggravated-murder complicity. Shooter identity was not essential to the admitted offense, and the later presentation does not establish that Stumpf’s earlier assent lacked the required understanding.

**Authority:** The common Opinion of the Court coalition stated in Opinion Topology joins this rule and explanation.

**Controlling explanation:** The constitutional inquiry concerns what Stumpf understood and admitted when he pleaded guilty. The later evidence concerns which participant personally shot Mrs. Stout, a fact that can differ while both intentionally participate in the murder. Brady’s informed-plea rule does not make an adequately understood plea invalid because later evidence changes a defendant’s assessment of his choice. The sentencing significance of the later account requires the separate inquiry below because punishment depends on an individualized assessment beyond the minimum facts sustaining guilt.

##### The independent death-sentence claim requires its own adjudication

**Holding and operative rule:** An informed aggravated-murder plea does not dispose of a claim that materially false or irreconcilable prosecution accounts affected the defendant’s individual culpability in the selection of death. The reviewing court must separately identify the actual accounts and later evidence, the State’s knowledge, their sentencing use, the applicable constitutional ground, and material prejudice under the governing pre-AEDPA standards.

**Authority:** The common Opinion of the Court coalition stated in Opinion Topology joins this rule and explanation.

**Controlling explanation:** Lindh v. Murphy and the application-date rule preserved in Carriger v. Stewart supply the pre-AEDPA merits regime for this November 1995 capital application. Napue v. Illinois and Giglio v. United States require knowledge and materiality for a knowing-false-evidence claim. Lockett v. Ohio and Eddings v. Oklahoma preserve individualized consideration of responsibility and mitigation. The existence of purposeful complicity thus cannot itself answer whether the State’s use of shooter identity impaired the death determination.

The sentencing panel found Stumpf the principal offender, and the later prosecution used evidence identifying Wesley as the shooter. The Sixth Circuit intertwined the consequence of those accounts with its mistaken plea analysis. Its decision does not supply the independent factual predicates and sentencing rationale necessary for final resolution. The case returns for that adjudication. A general rule for all differing theories across trials exceeds the factual findings available here.

#### Precedent Treatment

- Henderson v. Morgan: applied to actual notice of essential elements; its protection does not require the judge personally to provide a prescribed formula.
- Brady v. United States: applied to informed, voluntary assent; a later change in the evidence does not itself invalidate this informed plea.
- Boykin v. Alabama: applied to the record’s establishment of intelligent and voluntary assent.
- Bousley v. United States: applied to accurate understanding of the offense.
- Lindh v. Murphy: its application-date rule governs the relevant pre-AEDPA treatment.
- Carriger v. Stewart: applies that treatment to the already filed capital application.
- Napue v. Illinois: retains knowledge and materiality as predicates of the knowing-false-evidence route.
- Giglio v. United States: retains the same due-process obligations for material false-evidence claims.
- Lockett v. Ohio: applied to individualized consideration of responsibility apart from the validity of guilt.
- Eddings v. Oklahoma: applied to meaningful consideration of culpability and mitigation in the death decision.

#### Law After Decision

Counsel-provided explanation and the whole plea record may establish understanding of an essential element, while actual misunderstanding remains constitutionally material. Purposeful complicity and personal shooter identity remain distinct. A surviving conviction does not dispose of a preserved independent claim that the State’s factual accounts impaired the individualized selection of death.

#### Separate Writings

Souter, joined by Ginsburg, concurs. He identifies a fundamental-fairness problem if the State continues to defend death for Stumpf by asserting that he alone was the shooter after relying on Wesley’s sole responsibility to seek death there. His concern rests on the prosecutor’s duty to justice and the reliability required for capital selection, without depending on a charge of deliberate deception. The sentencing record must establish the conduct, constitutional significance, prejudice and appropriate correction.

Thomas, joined by Scalia, concurs. Newly obtained evidence and a later prosecution theory do not, in his view, by themselves undermine the fairness or reliability of the earlier proceeding. He stresses the adversarial opportunity to test conflicting evidence and distinguishes a newly discovered-evidence contention from an established constitutional violation. His agreement with the remand preserves that objection to the broader fairness theory.

#### Source Notes

U.S. Const., amends. VIII and XIV; former 28 U.S.C. §2254; Stumpf v. Mitchell, 367 F.3d 594; Henderson v. Morgan, 426 U.S. 637; Brady v. United States, 397 U.S. 742; Boykin v. Alabama, 395 U.S. 238; Napue v. Illinois, 360 U.S. 264; Giglio v. United States, 405 U.S. 150; No. 04-637.

#### Mandate, Remedy, and Stage

The case returns to the Sixth Circuit for independent consideration of the death-sentence claim and any necessary further proceedings on its factual predicates. The reviewing court must separately identify the actual accounts, knowledge where the constitutional ground requires it, sentencing reliance and prejudice. If a material constitutional sentencing violation is established, the habeas court must issue a conditional writ requiring lawful resentencing within a reasonable specified period or removal of the death sentence. The valid aggravated-murder conviction remains effective; a penalty-phase defect does not authorize unconditional release from that conviction.

**End of entry: Bradshaw v. Stumpf, Merits decision, June 13, 2005.**

### Golden v. Golden, Third Circuit No. 03-2184

Merits decision, June 13, 2005

#### Chronology and Posture

Review of the Third Circuit’s September 3, 2004 partial affirmance and reversal of a jurisdictional dismissal. Robert Golden acts for Leah Golden under a power of attorney; Donald Earwood represents Helen Earwood’s estate. They sue David S. Golden and Darlene Koposko over alleged interference with Irene King’s trust and inheritance plans. A purported addendum reduced the claimants’ equal shares to $5,000 and $10,000. The questions concern the probate exception’s application to a will-substitute trust and personal fraud, undue-influence, forgery and fiduciary-duty claims, together with the diversity amount and actual state-judgment effects. The alleged wrongdoing remains unproved.

All nine Justices participated at submission and decision.

#### Judgment

The Court preserves the sufficient jurisdictional amount and the surviving personal claims, vacates broader probate-exception dismissals, and remands for the functional, claim-specific analysis stated in the opinion.

#### Holdings

##### The probate exception follows the act and remedy requested

**Holding and operative rule:** The probate exception reserves probate or annulment of a will, administration of an estate, and control of property in a state court’s custody. Otherwise proper personal claims—including fraud, undue influence, forgery and fiduciary-duty claims—are not excluded merely because a trust functions as a will substitute or deciding them could produce facts inconsistent with a probate determination; a damages label cannot authorize the reserved acts.

**Authority:** The common Opinion of the Court coalition stated in Opinion Topology joins this rule and explanation.

**Controlling explanation:** Markham v. Allen permits adjudication of personal rights while protecting probate and custody of estate property. Waterman v. Canal-Louisiana Bank & Trust Co. and Princess Lida v. Thompson distinguish personal adjudication from administration and control of the property itself. Ankenbrandt v. Richards supplies the corresponding jurisdictional method: a confined exception for identified judicial acts does not become an exclusion of every related tort.

Neither party’s categorical account fits that boundary. A will-substitute trust can be involved in a personal wrong or a request for administration. A request styled as damages can still demand control of the corpus. The requested adjudication and remedy decide which.

The probate court did not directly adjudicate the trust or addendum; the Third Circuit inferred acceptance from probate of the pour-over will. A federal finding concerning fraud or forgery is not itself annulment of probate. The defendants’ concern about undermining a state judgment must be addressed through the actual appellate-review and preclusion rules, rather than a general interference-with-estate-affairs exclusion. Pennsylvania’s choice to assign matters to its Orphans’ Court likewise does not enlarge the federal exception. Its substantive law still determines whether a tort exists and the conditions for recovery.

##### The individual counts must be classified by their requested relief

**Holding and operative rule:** Earwood’s personal slander claim and both claimants’ intentional-interference-with-inheritance claims remain available for ordinary adjudication. Dismissals of the undue-influence, fraud, forgery and fiduciary-duty counts are vacated insofar as they rest on general estate interference; the district court must retain jurisdictionally proper personal claims while excluding actual probate, estate-administration and property-control demands.

**Authority:** The common Opinion of the Court coalition stated in Opinion Topology joins this rule and explanation.

**Controlling explanation:** The two fraud theories identified below concern forging the addendum and signature or inducing King to sign an instrument misrepresented to her. Those theories must receive the same remedy-specific inquiry as undue influence and forgery, rather than categorical dismissal because the alleged facts conflict with an inferred probate determination. The fiduciary-duty count against David Golden likewise requires separation of an individual damages judgment from removal of an executor, estate accounting or restoration and distribution of a state-controlled corpus. The slander claim belongs to Earwood alone; both claimants assert interference with inheritance against David Golden and Koposko. The resolved jurisdictional rule requires ordinary adjudication of those personal claims and application of state substantive law and any established preclusion. The lower court must examine each count and prayer, rather than treat every theory as the same request for relief.

##### The pleaded amount is sufficient under the legal-certainty rule

**Holding and operative rule:** A bona fide diversity claim satisfies the amount requirement when legally available, nonfrivolous punitive damages prevent a legal-certainty conclusion that recovery falls below the statutory threshold. Each claimant’s allegations here satisfy §1332, considering that claimant’s permitted claims and punitive damages without combining different plaintiffs’ demands or counting mutually exclusive recoveries twice.

**Authority:** The common Opinion of the Court coalition stated in Opinion Topology joins this rule and explanation.

**Controlling explanation:** St. Paul Mercury Indemnity Co. v. Red Cab Co. makes legal certainty, rather than proof of eventual recovery, the threshold inquiry. The defendants rely on the $188,946 estate-tax-return figure and the reduced gifts to calculate compensatory losses below $75,000. But the complaint alleges intentional or reckless wrongdoing supporting punitive damages under the Pennsylvania law identified in the record, including the surviving personal claims. That independent basis defeats the legal-certainty objection even on the defendants’ compensatory calculation. The amount ruling therefore remains effective. Ordinary later findings concerning actual loss and punitive entitlement apply the governing law rather than reopen the sufficiency of these allegations by speculation.

##### The parties’ representative capacities preserve diverse citizenship

**Holding and operative rule:** A power of attorney does not substitute the agent’s citizenship for that of the person whose beneficial claim is asserted; §1332(c)(2) attributes the decedent’s citizenship to an estate’s legal representative. Leah Golden’s New York citizenship and Helen Earwood’s Georgia citizenship are diverse from the Pennsylvania defendants.

**Authority:** The Court opinion’s citizenship-and-capacity holding is joined by Stevens, O’Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer. Stone-Zsela agrees with that component’s disposition without joining its rationale.

**Controlling explanation:** Section 1332 requires examination of the actual litigants and legally relevant capacities. Robert Golden asserts Leah Golden’s claim under her power of attorney, while Donald Earwood represents Helen Earwood’s estate. Those distinct relationships identify the applicable citizenship. The pleaded New York and Georgia interests are diverse from David Golden and Darlene Koposko, both Pennsylvania citizens.

##### State judgments receive their actual preclusive effect

**Holding and operative rule:** Section 1738 requires the rendering State’s proper preclusive effect for an actual state adjudication. An inferred acceptance of an instrument or a filed valuation cannot supply a blanket dismissal without identifying what was actually adjudicated and the applicable preclusion predicates.

**Authority:** The common Opinion of the Court coalition stated in Opinion Topology joins this rule and explanation.

**Controlling explanation:** Dodd v. Hood River County requires identity, actual and necessary adjudication, a full and fair opportunity, and the rendering State’s recognition of the binding determination before an issue is precluded. The circuit’s inference from probate of the pour-over will and the filing of a tax return cannot replace those inquiries. On remand, any established state judgment receives its proper effect, and each remaining claim receives the separate state-law sufficiency analysis its elements require.

##### Independent personal injuries are distinct from an appeal of a state judgment

**Holding and operative rule:** The Rooker–Feldman bar applies when a state-court loser brings a later federal action complaining of injury caused by the state judgment and seeking its review and rejection. A personal claim based on the defendants’ independent misconduct does not acquire that appellate character merely because a factual conclusion could conflict with a state determination.

**Authority:** The Court opinion’s state-appellate-review holding is joined by Stevens, O’Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer. Stone-Zsela agrees with that component’s disposition without joining its rationale.

**Controlling explanation:** Exxon Mobil Corp. v. Saudi Basic Industries Corp. requires attention to commencement sequence, the source of injury and the relief sought. Its rule keeps original jurisdiction distinct from the preclusive effect of an actual state judgment. Here the district court must identify the injury and requested remedy of each count under that rule rather than treat inconsistency alone as jurisdictional. An actual request for appellate rejection of a prior state judgment retains the bar’s own requirements.

#### Precedent Treatment

- Markham v. Allen, 326 U.S. 490: applied to confine the probate exception to probate, estate administration and control of property in state custody.
- Waterman v. Canal-Louisiana Bank & Trust Co., 215 U.S. 33: applied to distinguish adjudication of personal rights from administration.
- Princess Lida v. Thompson, 305 U.S. 456: applied to actual control and administration of the corpus rather than every related personal claim.
- Ankenbrandt v. Richards: its method of preserving a confined jurisdictional exception is extended to the probate boundary while its prior domestic-relations holding retains that scope.
- St. Paul Mercury Indemnity Co. v. Red Cab Co., 303 U.S. 283: applied to the good-faith amount and legally available punitive damages.
- Dodd v. Hood River County: applied to actual, necessary and fairly litigated state determinations rather than an inferred blanket bar.
- Exxon Mobil Corp. v. Saudi Basic Industries Corp.: applied to distinguish judgment-caused appellate injury, independent misconduct and preclusion.

#### Law After Decision

A trust’s testamentary function and factual overlap with probate do not themselves exclude diverse personal claims. Federal courts must identify the actual act and remedy requested, reserve probate and estate-property control to the proper state forum, and adjudicate otherwise proper personal claims under state substantive law. Citizenship, the sufficient pleaded amount, actual state-appellate-review predicates and §1738 effects retain their separate functions.

The probate court did not directly adjudicate the trust or addendum; the circuit inferred acceptance from probate of the pour-over will. The estate tax return reported $188,946. The federal complaint was filed March 28, 2001; the lower-court argument was January 22, 2004.

#### Mandate, Remedy, and Stage

The district court must retain the sufficient pleaded amount and proper representative capacities; preserve Earwood’s slander and both claimants’ intentional-interference claims; and classify the undue-influence, both fraud theories, forgery and David Golden fiduciary-duty requests by the actual adjudication and relief sought. Jurisdictionally proper personal claims proceed through the ordinary pleading, evidence and merits stages. Actual probate, annulment, estate-administration and control-of-property demands must be excluded. Any established state judgment receives its proper §1738 effect, and each state-law theory must satisfy its own elements.

### Grable & Sons Metal Products, Inc. v. Darue Engineering & Manufacturing, No. 04-603

Merits decision, June 13, 2005

#### Chronology and Posture

Argued April 18, 2005. Certiorari to the Sixth Circuit, reviewing 377 F.3d 592. After the IRS seized Grable’s Michigan property for unpaid taxes and sold it to Darue, Grable brought a state quiet-title action alleging that certified-mail notice failed to satisfy the federal seizure-notice requirement. Darue removed; the district court sustained jurisdiction and entered summary judgment for Darue, and the Sixth Circuit affirmed. The grant concerns whether the necessary federal notice issue supports jurisdiction despite the absence of a separate federal cause of action.

All nine Justices participated at argument and decision.

#### Judgment

**Affirmed, 9–0.**

**Supporting:** Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg, Breyer.

The existing federal judgment for Darue remains effective. The meaning of the tax-notice provision on its merits falls outside the question granted.

#### Opinion Topology

STEVENS, J., delivered the unanimous opinion of the Court. THOMAS, J., filed an unjoined concurring opinion while joining the Court in full.

#### Holdings

##### A necessary, disputed and substantial federal issue can support jurisdiction over a state claim

**Holding and operative rule:** A state-law claim arises under 28 U.S.C. §1331 when the well-pleaded claim necessarily raises an actually disputed federal issue substantial to the federal system and federal adjudication is consistent with the allocation of judicial business Congress established. Grable’s quiet-title claim satisfies all four conditions, permitting removal under §1441(a), although federal law supplies no separate cause of action.

**Authority:** The common Opinion of the Court coalition stated in Opinion Topology joins this rule and explanation.

**Controlling explanation:** Smith v. Kansas City Title & Trust Co. recognizes jurisdiction over a state claim necessarily turning on a substantial federal issue. Hopkins v. Walker applies that route to a title claim whose required defect turns on federal law; Shulthis v. McDougal requires a real dispute rather than federal law somewhere in the chain of title. Here the complaint’s only alleged defect in Darue’s title is failure to give the seizure notice required by 26 U.S.C. §6335(a). The parties dispute whether the certified mailing, actually received by Grable, satisfies that provision. Its meaning is therefore necessary to Grable’s own claim.

The federal importance extends beyond the parties’ stakes. The Government’s revenue collection and the reliability of titles generated by federal tax sales depend on the legal sufficiency of its sale procedures. The confined category of similar controversies creates little displacement of ordinary state title litigation.

Merrell Dow Pharmaceuticals v. Thompson makes congressional remedial choices and their jurisdictional consequences relevant. There, the absence of a federal action, preservation of state remedies, and the volume of ordinary tort litigation explained the jurisdictional result. Treating the missing action as an absolute bar would erase Smith’s retained route. Treating any federal ingredient as enough would erase Merrell Dow’s limitation. Requiring necessity, genuine dispute, federal-system substantiality and a compatible judicial allocation gives both decisions their proper force.

#### Precedent Treatment

- Smith v. Kansas City Title & Trust Co., 255 U.S. 180: reaffirmed as the route for qualifying embedded federal issues.
- Hopkins v. Walker, 244 U.S. 486: applied to the necessary federal defect asserted in a title claim.
- Shulthis v. McDougal, 225 U.S. 561: applied to require an actual federal dispute in the claim.
- Merrell Dow Pharmaceuticals v. Thompson, 478 U.S. 804: explained as a contextual limitation attending congressional remedies and displacement of ordinary state litigation, rather than an absolute federal-cause-of-action requirement.
- Steel Co. v. Citizens for a Better Environment: applied to establish jurisdiction before any merits adjudication.

#### Law After Decision

Federal courts may hear the small class of state claims satisfying necessity, actual dispute, importance to the federal system, and consistency with Congress’s allocation of judicial responsibilities. The party seeking the federal forum must establish those conditions. A missing separate federal cause of action is relevant within that complete inquiry; merely borrowing a federal standard in an ordinary fact-bound state tort does not establish jurisdiction.

#### Source Notes

28 U.S.C. §§1331, 1441(a); 26 U.S.C. §6335(a); Grable & Sons Metal Products, Inc. v. Darue Engineering & Manufacturing, 377 F.3d 592; No. 04-603. Section 6335(a) concerns notice of seizure; the challenged title arises from the ensuing tax sale.

#### Mandate, Remedy, and Stage

The mandate affirms the Sixth Circuit’s jurisdictional judgment and leaves the federal judgment for Darue in place. The requested jurisdictional remand to state court is denied.

**End of entry: Grable & Sons Metal Products, Inc. v. Darue Engineering & Manufacturing, Merits decision, June 13, 2005.**

### Johnson v. California, No. 04-6964

Merits decision, June 13, 2005

#### Chronology and Posture

Argued April 18, 2005. Certiorari following completion of California appellate review. The California Supreme Court required a strong likelihood of purposeful racial discrimination, construed as more likely than not, before the prosecutor had to explain peremptory strikes. The earlier review was dismissed on May 3, 2004 for want of a final state judgment; review of the completed proceedings was denied October 20, 2004. The question is the first-stage showing required by Batson. All three qualified Black prospective jurors among forty-three eligible jurors were struck, and the trial judge did not require the prosecutor’s reasons.

All nine Justices participated at argument and decision.

#### Judgment

**Reversed and remanded, 8–1.**

**Supporting:** Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O’Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Ginsburg and Breyer.
**Opposing:** Thomas, who would affirm California’s first-stage standard and the reviewed judgment.

#### Opinion Topology

GINSBURG, J., delivered the opinion of the Court, in which STONE-ZSELA, C. J., and STEVENS, O'CONNOR, SCALIA, KENNEDY, SOUTER, and BREYER, JJ., joined in full. BREYER, J., filed an unjoined concurring opinion. THOMAS, J., filed an unjoined dissenting opinion. The eight-Justice Court opinion controls both holdings.

#### Holdings

##### Facts supporting an inference trigger the duty to explain

**Holding and operative rule:** A defendant satisfies the first step of Batson by presenting facts sufficient to permit an inference of purposeful racial discrimination; the defendant need not first establish that discrimination was more likely than not. Striking all three qualified Black prospective jurors from the forty-three-person eligible venire supplies that inference in the circumstances here, requiring the prosecutor’s explanations and an ultimate purpose finding on which Johnson retains the burden of persuasion.

**Authority:** The common Opinion of the Court coalition stated in Opinion Topology joins this rule and explanation.

**Controlling explanation:** Batson v. Kentucky uses the initial showing to obtain information needed to test purposeful discrimination. Requiring probable discriminatory intent before the prosecutor explains a strike demands the ultimate showing before the procedure supplies the evidence needed to make it. The State’s concern about routine demands for explanations is answered by the requirement of a supported inference, rather than mere speculation.

Hernandez v. New York separates a facially neutral explanation from the later determination whether it is credible and reflects the actual purpose. United States v. Franklyn / United States v. Gonzalez likewise requires a real ultimate finding before deference can sustain rejection of a claim. Here the judge’s possible neutral reasons do not supply explanations the prosecutor was never required to give. The three exclusions and their surrounding circumstances satisfy the initial burden. California’s more-likely-than-not threshold therefore cannot sustain the ruling. Thomas’s account of state procedural latitude would permit the State to shift final persuasion ahead of the explanation; Batson’s sequence forecloses that result.

##### The remand must complete a reliable discrimination inquiry

**Holding and operative rule:** After an erroneous refusal to require explanations, the state court must promptly determine whether a reliable Batson adjudication remains possible, obtain the prosecutor’s actual reasons, permit meaningful challenge, and make the ultimate purpose finding. If purposeful discrimination is established, or a reliable adjudication cannot cure the omitted inquiry, the conviction must be set aside and a lawful new trial provided.

**Authority:** The common Opinion of the Court coalition stated in Opinion Topology joins this rule and explanation.

**Controlling explanation:** The remedy follows the inquiry Batson requires. Merely accepting a facially neutral reconstructed reason would leave the credibility question unanswered. A hearing can preserve the conviction only through a reliable adjudication of the actual reasons and purposeful-discrimination claim. Where that adjudication establishes discrimination or can no longer be reliably supplied, a new trial supplies the effective correction. The initial inference triggers this adjudication; it does not substitute for its final finding.

#### Precedent Treatment

- Batson v. Kentucky, 476 U.S. 79: clarified to require an inference at the first stage and applied through its separate explanation and ultimate-purpose stages.
- Hernandez v. New York, 500 U.S. 352: applied to distinguish facial neutrality from credibility and actual discriminatory purpose.
- United States v. Franklyn / United States v. Gonzalez, October 22, 1998: retains its requirement of an actual final purpose finding and review of a supported finding; it supplies no preliminary probability requirement.
- Johnson v. California, May 3, 2004: retains its final-state-judgment rule; completed state proceedings now permit merits review.

#### Law After Decision

A supported inference of racial discrimination requires the strike proponent to explain the challenged peremptory strikes. Final persuasion remains with the objector after the reasons are tested. Courts reviewing a failure to conduct that inquiry must ensure a reliable adjudication or the new-trial correction the holding requires.

#### Separate Writings

Thomas dissents. Equal protection forbids intentional racial jury selection, but he treats Batson’s burden-shifting procedure as an enforcement device leaving States latitude over implementation. Batson’s procedural reservation, Pennsylvania v. Finley’s recognition of flexibility in implementing protections, and Smith v. Robbins’s treatment of alternative procedures support his conclusion that California may demand its stronger prima facie showing. He would affirm on that threshold ground.

#### Source Notes

U.S. Const., amend. XIV; 28 U.S.C. §1257; Batson v. Kentucky, 476 U.S. 79; Hernandez v. New York, 500 U.S. 352; People v. Johnson, 30 Cal. 4th 1302, 71 P.3d 270 (2003); No. 04-6964.

#### Mandate, Remedy, and Stage

The case returns through the California courts. The state court must promptly determine whether a reliable hearing remains possible, obtain the actual prosecutorial explanations, allow meaningful challenge, and decide purposeful discrimination. A finding of discrimination or inability to provide reliable adjudication requires setting aside the conviction and providing a lawful new trial.

**End of entry: Johnson v. California, Merits decision, June 13, 2005.**

### Merck KGaA v. Integra Lifesciences I, Ltd., No. 03-1237

Merits decision, June 13, 2005

#### Chronology and Posture

Argued April 20, 2005. Certiorari to the Federal Circuit, reviewing its statutory-defense ruling in 331 F.3d 860. Integra’s patents concern RGD peptides; Merck sponsored candidate-compound research at Scripps concerning angiogenesis, including work on mechanism, efficacy and safety before human trials. Some candidates or results were not included in an eventual FDA submission. A jury rejected Merck’s §271(e)(1) defense, and the district court denied judgment as a matter of law. The Federal Circuit sustained liability through its restricted regulatory-relationship analysis and remanded damages; the district court subsequently awarded $6.375 million on September 7, 2004. The question is whether qualifying preclinical uses, including unsuccessful or unsubmitted experiments, fall within the statutory protection.

All nine Justices participated at argument and decision.

#### Judgment

**Vacated and remanded, 9–0.**

**Supporting:** Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg, Breyer.

The remand concerns review of the statutory defense and the consequent treatment of liability and the dependent damages judgment through the proper proceedings.

#### Opinion Topology

SCALIA, J., delivered the unanimous opinion of the Court. All nine Justices join the statutory rule, regulatory qualifications and operative remand.

#### Holdings

##### Reasonably directed preclinical compound research falls within the statutory protection

**Holding and operative rule:** Section 271(e)(1) protects the enumerated acts solely for uses reasonably related to developing and submitting information under the covered federal regulatory law. For the compound uses presented here, that relationship exists at least where the drugmaker reasonably believes the compound may act through an identified biological process to produce the intended physiological effect and uses it in experiments that, if successful, would yield information appropriate for FDA submission; the party invoking the defense must establish the relationship for each challenged use as of the time it occurred.

**Authority:** The common Opinion of the Court coalition stated in Opinion Topology joins this rule and explanation.

**Controlling explanation:** The statute protects making, using, offering to sell or selling within the United States, and importation into the United States, of a patented invention for its specified regulatory uses. Its exception concerns a new animal drug or veterinary biological product primarily manufactured using recombinant DNA, recombinant RNA, hybridoma technology or other processes involving site-specific genetic manipulation. The human-drug candidates here fall outside that distinct exception.

Eli Lilly & Co. v. Medtronic gives effect to the statute’s reference to the federal regulatory scheme. Development has work to do apart from ultimate submission: identifying and testing candidates involves trial and error. Qualifying preclinical work can concern safety, efficacy, mechanism of action, pharmacology and pharmacokinetics. An investigational new drug application, or IND, precedes human trials and supplies information relevant to unreasonable risk, including expected therapeutic benefit as well as safety. A new drug application, or NDA, also uses preclinical safety and effectiveness information. Failed candidates and unsubmitted results therefore remain eligible on the same prospective terms.

Integra’s concern that scientific speculation could consume patent rights is answered by the objective, use-specific relationship. A remote prospect of eventual pharmaceutical usefulness is insufficient, and funding a development project does not establish the defense for every activity within it. Commercial aims neither defeat a qualifying regulatory use nor establish the missing connection. The Federal Circuit recognized that information for an IND could qualify, but its restrictive treatment of candidate selection and information not ultimately submitted failed to give reasonable information development its statutory scope.

##### The laboratory-practice objection does not categorically exclude the research

**Holding and operative rule:** Failure to follow good-laboratory-practice rules does not categorically defeat §271(e)(1) protection for the preclinical work at issue. Those rules govern safety studies rather than efficacy, mechanism, pharmacology or pharmacokinetics studies as such, and the IND provision permits a covered safety study to be submitted with a brief explanation of noncompliance; the actual experiment must still satisfy the reasonable regulatory relationship.

**Authority:** The common Opinion of the Court coalition stated in Opinion Topology joins this rule and explanation.

**Controlling explanation:** The defense concerns the reasonable relationship of the use to development and submission of regulatory information. Treating all noncompliant preclinical work as categorically irrelevant would disregard the different studies governed by the laboratory rules and the regulatory allowance for explaining noncompliance in an IND submission. Those qualifications answer Integra’s laboratory-practice objection without making adherence, nonadherence or a later submission conclusive of any particular experiment’s coverage. The admissible evidence must establish the same prospective connection the statute requires.

##### The preserved verdict challenge returns for review under the correct construction

**Holding and operative rule:** The Federal Circuit must review Merck’s preserved challenge to the verdict under the correct construction and the admissible trial record. The consented-to jury instruction was consistent with the governing reasonable-relationship rule; correction of the appellate premises requires renewed review of evidentiary sufficiency and the resulting liability and damages consequences, rather than automatic judgment exempting every challenged experiment.

**Authority:** The common Opinion of the Court coalition stated in Opinion Topology joins this rule and explanation.

**Controlling explanation:** The instruction required Merck to prove by a preponderance an objectively reasonable prospect that the accused activities would contribute relatively directly to kinds of information likely relevant to FDA approval. It required separate consideration of the activities and made actual submission unnecessary. The jury rejected the defense, and the district court denied judgment as a matter of law. The appellate court must now assess that preserved challenge without treating unsuccessful candidates or unsubmitted results as legally excluded.

NCI’s later IND was excluded from the trial evidence. Its illustration of regulatory practice cannot become evidence that the jury considered or replace admissible proof on the verdict challenge. The district court’s September 7, 2004 damages judgment awarded $6.375 million after the earlier damages remand. Its dependence on actionable infringement requires the lower courts to address the consequence of corrected liability review through the proper proceedings.

The question of distinct research-tool patents is outside this candidate-compound dispute. The separate common-law experimental-use ruling is outside the statutory grant, and patent coverage was not challenged here. Experiment-specific coverage and consequential adjustment of damages are ordinary lower-court application of the rule, not unresolved legal standards.

#### Precedent Treatment

Eli Lilly & Co. v. Medtronic, Inc., 496 U.S. 661, is applied to the exemption’s federal regulatory function and coverage. The present decision resolves the distinct reasonable relationship of preclinical candidate-compound uses.

#### Law After Decision

Qualifying preclinical safety, efficacy, mechanism, pharmacology and pharmacokinetics experiments receive the statutory protection on an objectively reasonable regulatory relationship established for each challenged use. That relationship is judged when the use occurs. Failure of a candidate or omission from an ultimate submission does not itself defeat protection, while a merely speculative pharmaceutical prospect supplies no exemption. Courts must apply the rule to the preserved evidence and then identify the actionable uses and resulting damages.

35 U.S.C. §271(e)(1); 21 U.S.C. §§355(b), 355(i); 21 C.F.R. §§312.23(a)(8), 314.50(d)(2), and Part 58; Integra Lifesciences I, Ltd. v. Merck KGaA, 331 F.3d 860; No. 03-1237. The jury instruction assessed an objectively reasonable prospective connection, required separate consideration of the accused activities and made actual submission unnecessary. NCI’s later IND was excluded from trial evidence.

#### Mandate, Remedy, and Stage

The Federal Circuit must review Merck’s preserved evidentiary challenge using the corrected construction and admissible trial record. It must direct any further trial proceedings or judgment adjustment that the preserved record warrants. Liability and damages attributable to uses established as exempt must be eliminated; independently actionable uses retain their legal consequences. The lower courts must address the effect of that review on the dependent $6.375 million damages judgment through the proper proceedings. The research-tool question and separate common-law experimental-use ruling remain outside the statutory grant.

### Miller-El v. Dretke, No. 03-9659

Merits decision, June 13, 2005

#### Chronology and Posture

Argued December 6, 2004. Certiorari to the Fifth Circuit, reviewing 361 F.3d 849 after the appellate proceedings authorized by Miller-El v. Cockrell. The question is whether the state court’s rejection of purposeful racial jury exclusion survives federal habeas review on the lawful state-court record. The prosecutors struck ten of eleven qualified Black prospective jurors. The parties dispute the significance of the actual explanations, juror comparisons, questioning, shuffles and office-practice evidence. Miller-El seeks a conditional writ; Texas invokes the statutory protection of reasonable state findings.

All nine Justices participated at argument and decision.

#### Judgment

**Reversed and remanded, 7–2.**

**Supporting:** Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O’Connor, Kennedy, Souter, Ginsburg and Breyer.
**Opposing:** Scalia and Thomas, who would affirm the denial of habeas relief.

The conditional writ reaches the conviction and consequent death judgment.

#### Opinion Topology

KENNEDY, J., delivered the opinion of the Court, in which STONE-ZSELA, C. J., and STEVENS, O'CONNOR, SOUTER, GINSBURG, and BREYER, JJ., joined in full. BREYER, J., filed an unjoined concurring opinion. THOMAS, J., filed a dissenting opinion, in which SCALIA, J., joined. The seven-Justice Court opinion controls the section 2254(d)(2), section 2254(e)(1) and Batson conclusions.

#### Holdings

##### The combined state record establishes purposeful exclusion and satisfies both habeas provisions

**Holding and operative rule:** A state finding of nondiscriminatory jury selection rests on an unreasonable factual determination under §2254(d)(2) when the full lawful state-court record makes the prosecutor’s actual explanations untenable. Here the permitted record separately rebuts the relevant factual finding by clear and convincing evidence under §2254(e)(1) and establishes purposeful racial exclusion under Batson.

**Authority:** The common Opinion of the Court coalition stated in Opinion Topology joins this rule and explanation.

**Controlling explanation:** Batson v. Kentucky requires examination of actual purpose through all relevant circumstances. Hernandez v. New York and United States v. Franklyn / United States v. Gonzalez preserve deference to supported credibility findings, not acceptance of explanations the record defeats. Miller-El v. Cockrell separated the certificate threshold from these merits requirements; the present conclusions rest on the merits record and the distinct statutory burdens.

The prosecutors struck ten of eleven qualified Black prospective jurors. Their rehabilitation explanation misdescribed Fields’s oral answers and applied differently to accepted non-Black panelists. The family-history explanation, its timing and limited follow-up add credibility evidence. Warren’s stated view that death could be an easy escape was shared in relevant respects by accepted panelists; Jenkins’s acceptance before Warren undermines the retained-challenges explanation. Some accepted comparators were later struck by the defense; their significance is the prosecution’s acceptance.

A comparator need not be identical in every respect. Courts must consider differences material to the reasons actually given and the full combination of those reasons. The State’s arguments about rehabilitation, relatives’ convictions, death-penalty views and strike timing receive that consideration; they do not reconcile the decisive comparisons. Actual oral questioning, the documented shuffles and the state-presented office-history evidence corroborate the trial-specific showing. The latter evidence does not do the work alone.

Thomas and Scalia would credit the differences and the state judge’s assessment. But deference protects a reasonable finding; cumulative proof can make that finding objectively unreasonable and clearly rebut its presumption. The Court relies on the state-record evidence supporting the Fields and Warren comparisons and the corroborating categories. Supplying a new prosecutorial motive would replace the factual inquiry rather than review it.

#### Precedent Treatment

- Batson v. Kentucky: enforced through cumulative examination of the actual reasons and purposeful racial exclusion.
- Hernandez v. New York: preserves the distinction between facial neutrality and actual credibility, with appropriate review of factual findings.
- United States v. Franklyn / United States v. Gonzalez: retains the real-purpose inquiry and deference to a supported finding; the present record defeats the finding under the additional habeas requirements.
- Miller-El v. Cockrell: applied at its express certificate-versus-merits boundary, preserving each provision’s distinct function.
- Michael Wayne Williams v. Taylor: preserves the statutory conditions for additional factual development.
- Holland v. Jackson: applied to the lawful state-record foundation of statutory factual review.

#### Law After Decision

Courts applying Batson on habeas must assess the prosecutor’s actual explanations against the cumulative lawful record and consider materially comparable accepted jurors without demanding identity in every respect. Material differences remain part of the comparison. This record establishes purposeful exclusion, objective factual unreasonableness and clear-and-convincing rebuttal as distinct conclusions, supporting conditional trial relief.

#### Separate Writings

Thomas dissents, joined by Scalia. They would hold that Miller-El has not satisfied the distinct habeas burdens on the permitted record. The State offered combinations of concerns about rehabilitation, future dangerousness, death-penalty answers, relatives’ convictions, strike timing and comparator differences. They regard those differences and the trial judge’s credibility assessment as supporting a reasonable nondiscrimination finding. They read Warren’s actual explanation as concern about inconsistent answers and Fields’s assurances as leaving a genuine rehabilitation concern. Differential questioning, shuffles and office history, in their assessment, do not overcome those individualized reasons. A delayed hearing does not itself withdraw the factual presumption.

28 U.S.C. §2254(d)(2), (e)(1); Batson v. Kentucky, 476 U.S. 79; Hernandez v. New York, 500 U.S. 352; Miller-El v. Cockrell, 537 U.S. 322; Miller-El v. Dretke, 361 F.3d 849; No. 03-9659. #### Mandate, Remedy, and Stage

The district court must enter a conditional writ fixing a definite, reasonable period for Texas to retry Miller-El before a lawfully selected jury after hearing the parties. Failure to comply requires release from custody under this conviction. ### Wilkinson v. Austin, No. 04-495

Merits decision, June 13, 2005

#### Chronology and Posture

Argued March 30, 2005. Certiorari to the Sixth Circuit, reviewing 372 F.3d 346 in a §1983 class action. OSP confinement entails exceptional isolation, indefinite placement and loss of otherwise available parole eligibility. Ohio replaced its earlier system with a New Policy containing substantive placement predicates and notice, rebuttal and review safeguards. The Sixth Circuit sustained the liberty interest and the district court’s procedural additions while rejecting new substantive placement restrictions. The question is what process the identified liberty requires and whether the additional federal procedures are justified.

All nine Justices participated at argument and decision.

#### Judgment

The Court sustains the protected liberty interest, upholds the New Policy’s described procedures, reverses approval of unsupported procedural additions, and remands for a conforming decree.

| Judgment component | Disposition and vote | Supporting Justices | Opposing Justices | Remedy or remand |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Recognition of protected liberty in the OSP placement | Affirmed, 9–0 | Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg, Breyer | — | Ohio’s substantive constraints and significant restraint establish the interest |
| Approval of mandatory procedures beyond the adequate New Policy | Reversed, 9–0 | Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg, Breyer | — | Remove unsupported additions and conform the decree |
| Continuing placements affected by the former policy | Remanded, 9–0 | Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg, Breyer | — | Identify any proved continuing denial of required process and order fitted, prompt correction |

The appellate rejection of new substantive placement restrictions remains effective.

#### Opinion Topology

BREYER, J., delivered the unanimous opinion of the Court. All nine Justices join every holding and the operative remand.

#### Holdings

##### Ohio’s substantive placement limits protect a significant liberty interest

**Holding and operative rule:** State law creates a protected prison liberty interest when it meaningfully restricts officials’ authority to impose a significant deprivation. Ohio’s genuine substantive placement predicates, combined with OSP’s exceptional isolation, indefinite duration and loss of otherwise available parole eligibility, establish that interest in this confinement.

**Authority:** The common Opinion of the Court coalition stated in Opinion Topology joins this rule and explanation.

**Controlling explanation:** Sandin v. Conner requires examination of the real substantive constraint and meaningful restraint, rather than mandatory words alone. Ohio’s grounds include serious assaultive or predatory behavior, riot or gang leadership, escape and other identified security predicates. Officials exercise substantial professional judgment within those grounds, but the grounds constrain assignment authority. Indefinite, highly isolated confinement coupled with loss of parole eligibility supplies the significant deprivation. The State’s retained classification discretion therefore does not dispose of the liberty question. The interest rests on substantive constraints and consequences, rather than on the State’s promise to conduct hearings. Hewitt v. Helms retains the distinction between an actual predicate and a mere procedural direction; the protected interest then requires its own process inquiry.

##### The New Policy supplies adequate process when its safeguards function as designed

**Holding and operative rule:** For the OSP classification at issue, due process is satisfied by usable notice of the actual placement grounds, a meaningful opportunity for oral and written rebuttal, stated reasons and multilevel review in which a negative recommendation terminates placement, objections before final review, review within thirty days after arrival, and genuine annual reconsideration. Those protections make the New Policy adequate without the additional mandatory witness procedures imposed below; security-based restrictions must preserve a meaningful opportunity to contest the actual grounds.

**Authority:** The common Opinion of the Court coalition stated in Opinion Topology joins this rule and explanation.

**Controlling explanation:** Mathews v. Eldridge weighs the prisoner’s substantial interest, the risk of error and likely value of added procedures, and the governmental burden. Notice, rebuttal and reasons address the factual basis for assignment. The three review levels and termination upon a negative recommendation reduce the chance that an unsupported proposal becomes confinement, while prompt and recurring review test continued placement. Hewitt v. Helms and Greenholtz v. Inmates permit informal procedures fitted to correctional decisions. Wolff v. McDonnell’s disciplinary safeguards respond to a different decision and do not mechanically control this predictive security classification.

Mandatory witness presentation adds limited protection to this combination and can expose informants or other prisoners to retaliation. The class’s concern that general allegations or paper review will conceal error is addressed by requiring usable grounds and actual reconsideration. Approval of these designed safeguards depends on their meaningful operation. The former policy’s errors establish the need for an effective cure but do not establish the necessity of every additional procedure prescribed by the district court.

##### The decree must remove unsupported additions and correct proved continuing violations

**Holding and operative rule:** The injunction must conform to the New Policy’s constitutional adequacy while promptly correcting any proved continuing OSP placement that has not received the required process. Each retained command must be necessary to correct the established federal violation, narrowly drawn and the least intrusive necessary means, with substantial weight given to public safety and criminal-justice effects; relief departing from state or local law additionally requires that federal law demand the departure, that it be necessary, and that no other relief correct the violation.

**Authority:** The common Opinion of the Court coalition stated in Opinion Topology joins this rule and explanation.

**Controlling explanation:** Lewis v. Casey makes the proved wrong determine the remedy and gives the State a genuine opportunity to provide an effective cure. Benjamin v. Fraser supplies the requirements for continuing implementation, and Gates v. Cook applies those requirements and the State’s burden to substantiate an adequate narrower alternative. Section 3626 requires independent judicial findings for the actual relief; neither an assurance that a policy exists nor earlier error alone supplies them.

Ohio’s adequate replacement procedures remove the basis for federally mandated additions on the theory rejected here. If established placements remain without adequate review, the district court must identify that continuing injury and require the corrective notices, reasons and review decisions on an enforceable, reasonable schedule. Ohio must substantiate a proposed narrower means as fully correcting the proved common violation. That burden does not replace the court’s statutory findings. The appellate rejection of new substantive classification restrictions remains effective because the grant concerns process.

#### Precedent Treatment

- Sandin v. Conner: applied at its sufficient, nonexclusive substantive-constraint scope to Ohio’s genuine placement predicates and the significant OSP restraint.
- Mathews v. Eldridge: applied separately to the required process, weighing actual interests, error risks, additional value and burdens.
- Hewitt v. Helms: applied to informal review fitted to correctional decisions.
- Greenholtz v. Inmates: applied to process fitted to the kind of correctional judgment at issue.
- Wolff v. McDonnell: its disciplinary setting is distinguished from predictive security classification.
- Lewis v. Casey: applied to connect the decree to the proved violation and preserve Ohio’s opportunity to design an effective cure.
- Benjamin v. Fraser: applied to supported continuing implementation and independent statutory findings.
- Gates v. Cook: applied to the narrower-cure showing and current prison-relief requirements.

#### Law After Decision

Ohio’s constrained OSP assignment implicates protected liberty under Sandin. The New Policy’s functioning combination of notice, rebuttal, reasons and recurring review supplies due process in this setting. Continuing prison relief must correct proved injury through the specific statutory conditions and workable implementation; the adequacy of the designed policy and the correction of deficient continuing placements require distinct determinations.

#### Source Notes

U.S. Const., amend. XIV; 42 U.S.C. §1983; 18 U.S.C. §3626(a)(1); Austin v. Wilkinson, 372 F.3d 346; Mathews v. Eldridge, 424 U.S. 319; No. 04-495.

#### Mandate, Remedy, and Stage

The case returns for a decree removing the unsupported additions and respecting the appellate rejection of new substantive classification restrictions. The district court must identify any continuing placements never given adequate process and require the necessary notices, reasons and genuine review promptly on an enforceable, reasonable schedule. Each additional retained measure must be tied to the proved defect and supported by §3626 findings, with statutory weight given to security evidence. Ohio must substantiate any narrower proposed cure as fully effective. Compliance must be shown by the actual notices, reasons and review decisions the correction requires.

### American Trucking Associations, Inc. v. Michigan Public Service Commission, No. 03-1230

Merits decision, June 20, 2005

#### Chronology and Posture

Argued April 26, 2005. On writ of certiorari to the Court of Appeals of Michigan. The question is whether Michigan’s $100 annual per-truck fee for point-to-point intrastate commercial hauling violates the Commerce Clause.

The fee under Mich. Comp. Laws §478.2(1) applies to every carrier undertaking that local hauling, including carriers also engaged in interstate business. The carriers contend that the flat charge burdens interstate commerce because trucks carrying interstate loads make fewer local miles. The Court of Claims sustained the fee; the Court of Appeals affirmed, subjecting it to Commerce Clause review; the Michigan Supreme Court declined review. The separately litigated §478.2(2) assessment presents a different statutory question.

All nine Justices participated.

#### Judgment

**Affirmed, 9–0.**

**Supporting:** Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O’Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg, and Breyer.

The judgment sustaining §478.2(1) remains effective. The carriers’ requested constitutional invalidation is denied.

#### Opinion Topology

STONE-ZSELA, C. J., delivered the opinion of the Court, in which STEVENS, O'CONNOR, KENNEDY, SOUTER, GINSBURG, and BREYER, JJ., joined. SCALIA, J., and THOMAS, J., each filed an opinion concurring in the judgment.

| Writing | Author | Joined by | Relationship to judgment | Scope joined |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Opinion of the Court | Stone-Zsela | Stevens, O'Connor, Kennedy, Souter, Ginsburg, Breyer | Affirms the judgment | All controlling propositions and remedy |
| Concurrence in the judgment | Scalia | — | Affirms on a separate ground | Facial-discrimination and indistinguishable-precedent test; does not join the Court's rationale |
| Concurrence in the judgment | Thomas | — | Affirms on a separate ground | Rejects negative-Commerce doctrine as a basis for invalidation; does not join the Court's rationale |

#### Holdings

##### Evenhanded charges for a distinct local hauling business

**Operative rule.** An evenhanded annual per-truck fee for undertaking a distinct local hauling business does not violate the Commerce Clause merely because carriers performing fewer local miles pay more per mile. Courts must examine the actual charged event, discriminatory operation, fair apportionment and relation to state services, and any interstate burden clearly excessive relative to local benefits; Michigan’s §478.2(1) fee satisfies those requirements on this record.

**Authority.** The complete Court opinion has seven votes: Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O’Connor, Kennedy, Souter, Ginsburg, and Breyer.

**Controlling explanation.** The Court examines what Michigan charges for. Oklahoma Tax Commission v. Jefferson Lines requires identification of the taxed event while preserving the independent safeguards of nexus, apportionment, nondiscrimination, and fair relation to state services. This fee attaches to point-to-point Michigan hauling, an actual local business with a substantial connection to the State. If every State imposed the same fee, a carrier would pay repeatedly only by undertaking distinct local hauling in those States. That is not repeated taxation of a single interstate passage. The local trigger also keeps the assessment within the activity Michigan regulates.

The fee applies equally to carriers undertaking that activity. Costs of regulating truck size, weight, insurance, and safety may relate to participation by a truck rather than each mile traveled. A fair relation to the services supporting the activity does not require dollar-for-dollar or mile-for-mile equality. The carriers’ fee-per-mile comparison therefore does not establish discrimination. American Trucking Associations v. Scheiner involved charges on use of the highways, including interstate passage, with a demonstrated unequal burden; it does not invalidate every flat local-business fee.

National Electrical Manufacturers Association v. Sorrell preserves Pike review of an evenhanded measure without requiring a preliminary showing of disparate interstate impact. Applying that review, the asserted practical disruption of interstate trade is not substantiated sufficiently to show a burden clearly excessive relative to Michigan’s local regulatory benefits. Calling an activity intrastate does not immunize the charge; its actual event and operation sustain it here.

**Precedent treatment.** Jefferson Lines and NEMA are applied; Scheiner is distinguished by the charged event and demonstrated effects; Complete Auto and Pike retain their independent safeguards.

#### Precedent Treatment

- **Oklahoma Tax Commission v. Jefferson Lines:** Applied to identify the local activity and preserve nexus, fair apportionment, nondiscrimination, and fair relation as independent requirements.
- **Complete Auto Transit, Inc. v. Brady:** Applied through those four requirements; the local label supplies no categorical exemption.
- **National Electrical Manufacturers Association v. Sorrell:** Applied to require actual review of incidental interstate burdens without a disparate-impact threshold.
- **Pike v. Bruce Church, Inc.:** Applied to compare a substantiated interstate burden with the putative local benefits.
- **American Trucking Associations, Inc. v. Scheiner:** Distinguished because its flat highway charges reached interstate passage and their practical discrimination was demonstrated. Its restriction on that burden remains in force.

#### Law After Decision

The existing Commerce Clause framework permits an evenhanded charge for each distinct local hauling business when its actual operation meets the independent constitutional requirements. A higher per-mile cost caused solely by fewer local miles does not establish discrimination. A carrier’s additional local businesses in other States are separate charged events; courts must still examine the practical burden and relation to local regulation.

#### Separate Writings

**Scalia, concurring in the judgment.** Scalia applies the approach reflected in West Lynn Creamery and his prior published Commerce Clause positions: a state measure is invalid under the negative Commerce Clause if it facially discriminates against interstate commerce or is indistinguishable from a type of measure the Court previously invalidated. Michigan’s fee meets neither condition. Scheiner charged interstate trucks even without local hauling, a material difference. This ground supports affirmance without joining Pike balancing, the full Complete Auto framework, or an independent internal-consistency rationale.

**Thomas, concurring in the judgment.** Thomas follows his published Hillside Dairy position that the negative Commerce Clause lacks a constitutional foundation adequate to invalidate state legislation. He would affirm for that reason. His objection reaches the doctrine itself and does not adopt Scalia’s separate precedent-based test.

#### Source Notes

No. 03-1230; Mich. Comp. Laws §478.2(1); Westlake Transportation, Inc. v. Michigan Public Service Commission, 255 Mich. App. 589, 662 N.W.2d 784. The challenged local-hauling assessment is distinct from the assessment in §478.2(2).

#### Mandate, Remedy, and Stage

The affirmed judgment leaves Michigan’s §478.2(1) fee enforceable for covered local hauling. The mandate sustains that judgment on the adjudicated Commerce Clause challenge.

### Dodd v. United States, No. 04-5286

Merits decision, June 20, 2005

#### Chronology and Posture

Argued March 22, 2005. On writ of certiorari to the United States Court of Appeals for the Eleventh Circuit. The question is whether the year under 28 U.S.C. §2255 paragraph 6(3) begins with this Court’s initial recognition of the right or a later decision making the right retroactively applicable.

Dodd moved on April 4, 2001, to set aside his continuing-criminal-enterprise conviction under Richardson v. United States, decided June 1, 1999, which requires jury unanimity on the specific predicate violations. The District Court dismissed the motion as untimely and rejected equitable tolling. The Eleventh Circuit affirmed the initial-recognition clock. Dodd invokes that court’s April 19, 2002 retroactivity decision in Ross.

All nine Justices participated.

#### Judgment

**Affirmed, 5–4.**

**Supporting:** Stone-Zsela, O’Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, and Thomas.
**Opposing:** Stevens, Souter, Ginsburg, and Breyer.

The dismissal of Dodd’s §2255 motion as untimely remains effective.

#### Opinion Topology

THOMAS, J., delivered the opinion of the Court, in which STONE-ZSELA, C. J., and O'CONNOR, SCALIA, and KENNEDY, JJ., joined. STEVENS, J., filed a dissenting opinion, in which SOUTER, GINSBURG, and BREYER, JJ., joined.

#### Holdings

##### Initial recognition starts the special filing year

**Operative rule.** Under §2255 paragraph 6(3), the one-year limitation runs from the Supreme Court’s initial recognition of the asserted right, provided the right is newly recognized and made retroactively applicable on collateral review. A later retroactivity determination does not restart that year; Richardson initially recognized the right Dodd asserts on June 1, 1999, making his April 4, 2001 motion untimely under this trigger even assuming its retroactive qualification.

**Authority.** The complete Court opinion is joined by Stone-Zsela, O’Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, and Thomas.

**Controlling explanation.** The statute names a date and then limits the rights eligible to use it. Treating the retroactivity condition as a second date replaces the event Congress selected. Both conditions must be satisfied for paragraph 6(3) to apply, but satisfying them does not change the initial-recognition date. Richardson supplies that date and the predicate-unanimity right at issue.

Paragraph 6 uses the latest independently qualifying event. Clay v. United States supplies the separate finality rule, and the factual-discovery provision has its own due-diligence condition. Those provisions do not turn a later legal retroactivity decision into a new recognition event. The Court assumes the collateral qualification in Dodd’s favor because the timing defect is independently decisive.

The contrary reading responds to a serious practical concern: the year may expire before retroactivity is established. That concern is especially acute for a successive motion, whose separate authorization provision requires a previously unavailable new constitutional rule made retroactive by this Court. Tyler v. Cain permits express retroactivity or holdings whose necessary propositions jointly compel it; eligibility can therefore be established through existing holdings. But that possibility does not guarantee that every right will qualify within a year. The statute’s strict interaction does not authorize a judicially substituted clock.

The general availability of equitable tolling is outside the granted timing question. The identity of the court competent to establish paragraph 6(3)’s retroactivity qualification, and the predicate-instruction merits, likewise need not be decided to resolve that question on the assumed qualification.

**Precedent treatment.** Richardson is applied to recognition; Clay retains its distinct finality function; Tyler is distinguished as a retroactivity-eligibility rule rather than a timing reset.

#### Precedent Treatment

- **Richardson v. United States:** Applied to identify the predicate-unanimity right and its June 1, 1999 initial recognition.
- **Clay v. United States:** Applied at its finality scope; paragraph 6(1) and paragraph 6(3) remain different triggers.
- **Tyler v. Cain:** Distinguished from the clock question. Its express-holding and necessarily-compelling-multiple-holdings routes to retroactivity retain their force; neither creates a later initial-recognition date.
- **Johnson v. United States:** Its factual-discovery construction concerns a different paragraph and does not replace the enacted new-right trigger.

#### Law After Decision

For initial and successive §2255 motions invoking paragraph 6(3), courts calculate the year from initial Supreme Court recognition and separately test the new-right and retroactivity qualifications. A later retroactivity decision supplies no restart. The latest-event structure continues to permit another statutory trigger only when that trigger’s own conditions are met; successive-motion authorization remains an additional requirement.

#### Separate Writings

**Stevens, joined by Souter, Ginsburg, and Breyer, dissenting.** The dissent would read the new-right and retroactivity conditions together so that the special year begins when both are satisfied. A period that predictably can be consumed before the collateral qualification exists frustrates the opportunity Congress supplied. The completed-condition reading is textually permissible because the subordinate clause describes the qualifying right and can identify the point at which the provision becomes usable. The ordinary concern for a complete claim and the statutory structure support that construction.

Tyler’s multiple-holdings route can establish retroactivity and has been applied to the confined Cage rule, but it does not ensure prompt qualification of every newly recognized right. The dissent’s concern remains the short year expiring before a qualifying collateral route is available, particularly in combination with successive-motion authorization. It would reverse the timing dismissal and remand for any remaining qualification and merits questions. The shared ground rests on the assumed qualification in this case and does not resolve which court may make the retroactivity determination.

#### Source Notes

No. 04-5286; 28 U.S.C. §2255, paragraphs 6(1)–(4) and 8(2), as operative in this case; Dodd v. United States, 365 F.3d 1273; Richardson v. United States, 526 U.S. 813; Ross v. United States, 289 F.3d 677. Retroactive qualification is assumed solely to decide the independently dispositive clock question.

#### Mandate, Remedy, and Stage

The mandate affirms the Eleventh Circuit’s judgment. The limitations dismissal remains in force on the initial-recognition ground.

**End of entry: Dodd v. United States, Merits decision, June 20, 2005.**

#### Chronology and Posture

Argued April 20, 2005. On writ of certiorari to the United States Court of Appeals for the Fourth Circuit. The question is whether the six-year limitation measured from a §3729 violation governs a retaliation action under the then-operative False Claims Act, §3730(h).

Wilson alleged retaliation for reporting suspected false claims and cooperating with a federal investigation, culminating in her March 1997 resignation. She filed qui tam and retaliation claims in January 2001. The District Court dismissed the retaliation claim under a borrowed North Carolina three-year period and certified an interlocutory appeal. The Fourth Circuit reversed, holding that the federal six-year period applied.

#### Judgment

**Reversed and remanded, 7–2.**

**Supporting:** Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O’Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, and Thomas.
**Opposing:** Ginsburg and Breyer.

The Fourth Circuit must select and apply the appropriate borrowed state limitation to Wilson’s retaliation claim, including accrual and any supported tolling. Reversal does not itself reinstate the District Court’s dismissal or approve its selection of a three-year period.

#### Opinion Topology

SOUTER, J., delivered the opinion of the Court, in which STONE-ZSELA, C. J., and O'CONNOR, SCALIA, KENNEDY, and THOMAS, JJ., joined. STEVENS, J., filed an opinion concurring in the judgment. BREYER, J., filed a dissenting opinion, in which GINSBURG, J., joined.

| Writing | Author | Joined by | Relationship to judgment | Scope joined |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Opinion of the Court | Souter | Stone-Zsela, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Thomas | Reverses and remands | Statutory construction, state borrowing and remand |
| Concurrence in the judgment | Stevens | — | Supports reversal, state borrowing and remand | Independently sufficient objection to a period expiring before retaliation; does not join the Court's principal interpretive reasoning in full |
| Dissent | Breyer | Ginsburg | Would affirm the six-year ruling | Six years from the alleged or suspected section 3729 violation |

#### Holdings

##### Retaliation actions use the appropriate borrowed state period

**Operative rule.** The six-year period measured by §3731(b)(1) from a violation of §3729 does not govern the independent retaliation action created by the operative 1986 version of §3730(h). Absent another applicable federal period, courts borrow the most closely analogous state limitation under the established borrowing rules, selecting it by the substantive character of the retaliatory injury and determining accrual and any supported tolling under the applicable rules.

**Authority.** Stone-Zsela, O’Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, and Thomas join the complete Court opinion; Stevens separately supports the reversal and remand.

**Controlling explanation.** The broad reference to an action under §3730 must be read with the event that starts the six years: a violation of §3729. United States ex rel. McKenzie v. BellSouth Telecommunications establishes that protected retaliation liability does not require a completed false claim. A valid retaliation action therefore can lack the very event that the proposed federal clock requires. Reading the sentence as a whole makes its asserted universal scope ambiguous.

The dissent treats a suspected violation as the trigger, observing that limitations rules commonly concern alleged events. But a fraud claimant must ultimately prove the alleged §3729 violation; a successful retaliation claimant need never prove one. The distinction persists after the pleading stage. Section 3731(c)’s government-burden provision likewise shows that a broad reference to §3730 can operate within a confined substantive context. Its reference to the United States explains that context rather than requiring the same unrestricted scope in every neighboring sentence. Section 3731(d)’s more explicit references to subsections (a) and (b) do not overcome the present provision’s trigger and context.

The ordinary expectation that time begins when a complete claim exists favors the construction permitting retaliation-based accrual. The contrary construction may consume the period before the retaliatory injury occurs. North Star Steel supplies ordinary state borrowing when the federal statute supplies no applicable period. Donnelley’s §1658 rule supplies none for this cause created in 1986. The interest in a uniform federal period cannot replace those enacted conditions.

The proper North Carolina analogue was outside the granted question and was not decided by the Court of Appeals. Its selection and application are required remand work under this rule.

**Precedent treatment.** McKenzie’s independent retaliation protection is applied; North Star Steel’s ordinary borrowing rule and Donnelley’s enactment-date rule retain their distinct functions.

#### Precedent Treatment

- **United States ex rel. McKenzie v. BellSouth Telecommunications:** Applied; actionable retaliation does not depend on ultimate proof of a completed false claim.
- **North Star Steel Co. v. Thomas:** Applied to ordinary borrowing of the most closely analogous state period where federal law supplies none for this action.
- **Jones v. R. R. Donnelley & Sons Co.:** Distinguished at its operative enactment-date rule; §1658 does not supply the period for this 1986 cause.

#### Law After Decision

Courts adjudicating retaliation claims under the operative §3730(h) must use the appropriate borrowed state period, with accrual and any supported tolling determined under the applicable rules, rather than §3731(b)(1)’s fraud-triggered six years. Selection follows the character of the retaliatory injury and federal borrowing principles. The cause retains McKenzie’s independent protection even where no completed fraud is proved; the separate fraud action remains governed by its own limitations rules.

#### Separate Writings

**Stevens, concurring in the judgment.** Stevens regards the broad action-under-§3730 language as naturally inclusive of retaliation and recognizes the value of federal uniformity. The decisive contrary consideration is that the fraud-based trigger could exhaust the entire period before an actionable retaliation claim exists. He treats the ordinary accrual principle as sufficient to reject that result without requiring another feature first to establish textual ambiguity. He therefore supports state borrowing and remand without joining the Court’s principal interpretive reasoning in full.

**Breyer, joined by Ginsburg, dissenting.** The dissent would apply six years from the alleged or suspected §3729 violation. Retaliation is an action under §3730, and limitations provisions routinely measure time from alleged events without making ultimate proof of that event a condition of timeliness. This reading preserves independent retaliation liability; it does not require a successful fraud claim or move the federal starting date to the retaliation itself.

The government-burden provision’s own subject, the United States, limits its operation, while Congress expressly named subsections (a) and (b) elsewhere when it wished to narrow coverage. Congress could reasonably select one long period for related fraud and retaliation litigation. That clock can start before retaliation and could expire first, but the dissent considers the resulting risk less consequential than the short, varied periods produced by borrowing. Donnelley’s discussion of the difficulties of varied state periods reinforces the practical value of a uniform period. The dissent would affirm the Fourth Circuit’s six-year ruling.

No. 04-169; 31 U.S.C. §§3729, 3730(h), 3731(b)(1), (c), and (d), as operative in this case; United States ex rel. Wilson v. Graham County Soil & Water Conservation District, 367 F.3d 245. The granted question concerns paragraph (b)(1), not the separate federal-official-knowledge provision in paragraph (b)(2).

#### Mandate, Remedy, and Stage

On remand, the lower court must identify the proper state analogue and calculate timeliness using the applicable accrual and supported tolling rules. A timely retaliation claim proceeds under its independent statutory elements; an untimely claim is dismissed on the established limitations ground. The separate fraud claim is distinct from this limitations judgment.

**End of entry: Graham County Soil & Water Conservation District v. United States ex rel. Wilson, Merits decision, June 20, 2005.**

### Rompilla v. Beard, No. 04-5462

Merits decision, June 20, 2005

#### Chronology and Posture

Argued January 18, 2005. On writ of certiorari to the United States Court of Appeals for the Third Circuit. The questions concern ineffective capital-defense investigation, resulting sentencing prejudice, and entitlement to federal habeas sentence relief.

Rompilla was convicted of murder and sentenced to death in Pennsylvania. The Pennsylvania Supreme Court rejected his investigation claim on December 10, 1998. The Federal District Court granted sentence relief; the Third Circuit reversed. Counsel had interviewed Rompilla and relatives, obtained three mental-health examinations, and acquired the prior victim’s testimony on the eve of sentencing. The prosecution had announced that it would use detailed evidence of the prior violent offense in aggravation; the rest of that readily accessible source had not been adequately investigated.

All nine Justices participated.

#### Judgment

**Reversed and remanded, 6–3.**

**Supporting:** Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O’Connor, Souter, Ginsburg, and Breyer.
**Opposing:** Scalia, Kennedy, and Thomas.

The District Court must grant a writ conditioned on Pennsylvania conducting a constitutionally adequate new penalty proceeding within a definite reasonable period or imposing a lawful noncapital sentence. The conviction and independently lawful custody remain intact. The existing death judgment may not be executed while the conditional remedy operates.

#### Opinion Topology

O'CONNOR, J., delivered the opinion of the Court, in which STONE-ZSELA, C. J., and STEVENS, SOUTER, GINSBURG, and BREYER, JJ., joined. KENNEDY, J., filed a dissenting opinion, in which SCALIA and THOMAS, JJ., joined.

#### Holdings

**Authority common to all four holdings.** Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O’Connor, Souter, Ginsburg, and Breyer join the complete Court opinion.

##### 1. The state performance decision unreasonably applied established law

**Operative rule.** Under the unreasonable-application branch of 28 U.S.C. 2254(d)(1), relief from a state merits decision requires an objectively unreasonable application of the Supreme Court law established at that adjudication, rather than merely an incorrect application. Pennsylvania’s December 10, 1998 approval of this investigation unreasonably applied Strickland because the other inquiries did not supply a reasonable basis for omitting adequate examination of the known accessible source the prosecution intended to use substantially in seeking death.

**Controlling explanation.** Strickland already required a reasonable investigation or a reasonable professional basis for limiting it. The state court credited the client and family interviews and three evaluations, but those efforts did not answer counsel’s separate need to prepare against detailed aggravating proof from an identified file. The prosecution’s announced reliance gave the source value before its mitigating contents became known. Late access to the victim’s testimony did not establish a timely informed inquiry into the remaining material.

Woodford v. Visciotti requires deference to a reasonably supportable application of Strickland, and the dissent correctly identifies that as a distinct threshold. Here, however, the state rationale treated other avenues as an adequate substitute without reasonably accounting for this source’s independent defense-preparation function. This is the concrete basis for objective unreasonableness, not a presumption that an unsuccessful inquiry or a later helpful discovery defeats deference. The state credibility finding that the family had not disclosed the abuse remains intact. Terry Williams and Wiggins explain the established obligation and the present review method; later professional guidance is not treated as new constitutional law already established in 1998.

**Precedent treatment.** Strickland is applied at the state-adjudication date; Visciotti’s statutory deference remains operative; Terry Williams and Wiggins explain the established rule.

##### 2. Counsel’s known-source investigation was constitutionally deficient

**Operative rule.** Capital counsel must examine a readily available prior-case file when counsel knows the prosecution will use its contents as a substantial part of the case for death, unless a reasonable, informed professional judgment makes that examination unnecessary. That duty requires reasonable efforts in the actual circumstances and reasonable pursuit of material leads; counsel’s inadequate timely examination of this source fell below Strickland’s objective standard.

**Controlling explanation.** Counsel needed the identified record to anticipate the prosecutor’s detailed presentation, place the earlier offense in context, and identify responsive evidence. The duty arose from the announced aggravation case, rather than the later discovery of useful mitigation. Other interviews and examinations did not perform that task. The eve-of-sentencing transcript and a criminal-history sheet did not reasonably substitute for examining the accessible source for those purposes.

Wiggins requires an informed foundation for a decision to stop investigating. Practical access, the scope of the materials, available resources, and reasoned allocation of effort remain part of the inquiry. They do not establish an informed justification on this record. The duty is reasonable examination of a known material source, not a mechanical command to read every page of every prior-case file. The deficiency finding follows independently from the performance standard after the statutory gate is satisfied.

**Precedent treatment.** Strickland’s investigation duty and Wiggins’s informed-stopping principle are applied without creating a universal records checklist.

##### 3. The full mitigation record establishes sentencing prejudice

**Operative rule.** Where deficient investigation would have materially changed the mitigation inquiry and presentation, prejudice exists when the supported cumulative evidence, weighed with the full aggravation case and its own adverse aspects, creates a reasonable probability of a different lawful penalty. That probability undermines confidence in Rompilla’s death sentence; because the state court did not adjudicate prejudice, the Court decides this element independently on the developed record.

**Controlling explanation.** The jury heard brief family pleas for mercy, assertions of innocence and good character, and Rompilla’s fourteen-year-old son’s love and willingness to visit. It credited the son’s appearance and possible rehabilitation but found them outweighed by murder during a felony, torture, and a significant history of violent felonies. The repeated stabbing and burning and the earlier violent offense remain part of the comparison.

Reasonable review of the known file would have exposed imprisonment records and the transfer petition’s leads concerning childhood deprivation, alcohol-associated incarceration and very low cognitive testing. Those leads support the ensuing investigation into severe parental alcoholism, drinking during pregnancy, domestic violence, beatings, confinement in a filthy dog enclosure, isolation, inadequate clothing, absent plumbing and an unheated sleeping space. Experts informed by the developed family, school and prison history reported organic brain damage, cognitive impairment, possible prenatal-alcohol effects and substantially impaired functioning at the offense. Those are evidentiary and expert accounts relevant to sentencing.

The causal connection is supported by the accessible file and the investigation its contents reasonably called for. Their unexpected mitigating significance did not entitle counsel to disregard them merely because the initial purpose was to meet aggravation. Pennsylvania’s decision not to contest prejudice does not discharge Rompilla’s burden. Assaultive and alcohol-associated misconduct in the additional history is also weighed; much violent-history aggravation was already before the jury, while the documented explanation of deprivation and impairment was absent.

Under §9711(c)(1)(iv)–(v), death requires unanimous agreement on the statutory weighing, and individual jurors may give effect to mitigation. The supported new presentation creates a reasonable probability that at least one juror would choose life. Strickland requires confidence-undermining probability, not certainty or a more-likely-than-not different outcome.

**Precedent treatment.** Strickland supplies the probability standard; Terry Williams and Wiggins supply the cumulative comparison of the full penalty case.

##### 4. A definite conditional sentence writ implements the violation

**Operative rule.** Pennsylvania must provide an adequately prepared new penalty proceeding within a definite reasonable period set by the District Court or impose a lawful noncapital sentence. The District Court must require notice of the State’s chosen course, verify completion through the resulting sentencing order, and enforce noncompliance against the invalid death sentence and its execution while preserving the conviction and independently lawful custody.

**Controlling explanation.** The constitutional defect concerns selection of the death penalty. The developed record establishes prejudice now; the next proceedings implement sentence relief. Terry Williams and Wiggins supply the conditional-remedy method: a defined compliance period and an actual lawful sentencing disposition, with federal enforcement if the condition fails. A repetition of the original penalty selection without constitutionally adequate defense preparation does not satisfy the condition. This remedy permits the State to choose a lawful new penalty proceeding or a lawful noncapital sentence while preventing execution of the defective death judgment.

**Precedent treatment.** The conditional sentence-remedy rules of Terry Williams and Wiggins are applied.

#### Precedent Treatment

- **Strickland v. Washington:** Applied to reasonable investigation, informed professional judgment, and reasonable-probability prejudice, using the law established when the state court adjudicated performance.
- **Williams v. Taylor (Terry Williams):** Applied to the separate statutory and constitutional inquiries, cumulative mitigation review and conditional sentence remedy.
- **Wiggins v. Smith:** Applied to informed investigation, whole-record prejudice and conditional relief; it explains the preexisting Strickland duty rather than supplying a later constitutional command retroactively.
- **Woodford v. Visciotti:** Preserved and applied as the distinct requirement of objective unreasonableness; an erroneous state decision alone does not authorize relief.

#### Law After Decision

The established Strickland rule requires reasonable examination of an accessible prior-case source known to supply substantial aggravation, absent a reasonable informed basis for omission. Other investigative work does not by itself answer that obligation. In federal habeas review, the state-adjudication statutory threshold remains separate from actual deficiency and prejudice; an unadjudicated prejudice component is assessed independently after the threshold is satisfied. The whole lawful penalty case, including unfavorable material, governs reasonable probability. The remedy corrects the sentence while preserving the conviction.

#### Separate Writings

**Kennedy, joined by Scalia and Thomas, dissenting.** The dissent would affirm because §2254(d)(1) does not authorize disturbing Pennsylvania’s performance adjudication. Strickland leaves room for informed professional judgments about how to allocate limited investigative effort, and Visciotti requires more than disagreement with a state court’s application. Counsel conducted client and family interviews, obtained three examinations, and acquired the aggravating testimony. Those efforts and the limited information they produced permit a reasonable state appraisal even if additional file review would have been preferable.

The prosecution’s announced source is a substantial reason for further work, but the dissent does not regard it as establishing that every reasonable application of Strickland had to condemn counsel’s stopping point. Wiggins involved serious mitigation leads already exposed by the materials counsel possessed; the dissent distinguishes this investigation and warns that reliance on later discoveries can obscure the contemporaneous inquiry. The opinion does not assume that counsel examined the entire file. Its sufficient ground is the statutory limitation, so it does not independently resolve actual deficiency or cumulative prejudice. On that route it would leave the conviction and sentence intact.

#### Source Notes

No. 04-5462; 28 U.S.C. §2254(d)(1), (e)(1); 42 Pa. Cons. Stat. §9711(c)(1)(iii)–(v), (d)(6), (8), (9); Commonwealth v. Rompilla, 554 Pa.378, 721 A.2d 786 (December 10, 1998); Rompilla v. Horn, 355 F.3d 233. Commonwealth v. Frey, 520 Pa.338, 346–348, 554 A.2d 27, explains the individual consideration of mitigation and the unanimity required for death under §9711. The grant concerns the ineffective-investigation and sentencing-prejudice claim.

#### Mandate, Remedy, and Stage

The case returns for entry and enforcement of the conditional sentence writ. The District Court sets the definite reasonable deadline, obtains notice of Pennsylvania’s choice, and verifies the new sentencing order. Compliance requires a constitutionally adequate new penalty proceeding or a lawful noncapital sentence. Failure to comply requires enforcement against the invalid death sentence and execution. The conviction remains intact.

**End of entry: Rompilla v. Beard, Merits decision, June 20, 2005.**

### San Remo Hotel, L.P. v. City and County of San Francisco, No. 04-340

Merits decision, June 20, 2005

#### Chronology and Posture

Argued March 28, 2005. On writ of certiorari to the United States Court of Appeals for the Ninth Circuit. The granted question is whether the full-faith-and-credit statute, 28 U.S.C. §1738, admits a categorical exception for takings claimants who pursued state compensation procedures before returning to federal court.

The hotel owners challenged a $567,000 conversion fee. The federal court abstained as to their facial challenge while the state mandamus proceedings could resolve the predicate state-law issue; their as-applied claims were dismissed as unripe. The owners reserved federal causes but presented broader state takings issues, which the California courts decided. On return to federal court, the lower courts gave those determinations preclusive effect and rejected a categorical takings exception. The particular application of California preclusion law is outside the grant.

All nine Justices participated.

#### Judgment

**Affirmed, 9–0.**

**Supporting:** Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O’Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg, and Breyer.

The federal dismissal remains effective on the issues within the affirmed judgment. The particular California preclusion determinations remain undisturbed under the limited grant.

#### Opinion Topology

STEVENS, J., delivered the opinion of the Court, in which STONE-ZSELA, C. J., and O'CONNOR, SCALIA, KENNEDY, SOUTER, THOMAS, GINSBURG, and BREYER, JJ., joined. O'CONNOR, J., filed a concurring opinion, in which KENNEDY and THOMAS, JJ., joined. The concurrence accompanies a complete join of the Court's opinion.

#### Holdings

##### Required state litigation does not create a takings exception to §1738

**Operative rule.** A takings claimant’s compliance with a state-compensation prerequisite and reservation of federal causes do not exempt issues actually and necessarily decided by the state court from the rendering State’s lawful preclusion rules under §1738. The federal court must identify each binding common issue, with the required identity and full and fair opportunity to litigate; genuinely distinct issues not necessarily adjudicated remain outside that issue-preclusion bar.

**Authority.** All nine Justices join the complete Court opinion.

**Controlling explanation.** Section 1738 requires recognition of a state judgment with the same force it receives in the courts of that State. Dodd v. Hood River County distinguishes reservation of a federal cause from the binding effect of issues necessarily adjudicated in state court. That distinction applies to qualifying legal determinations as well as facts and governs the owners’ claimed compulsory-route exception.

England v. Louisiana State Board of Medical Examiners protects return to federal court after abstention permits a state court to resolve an antecedent state-law issue. It does not allow a litigant to broaden the state proceedings to secure a decision on the same substantive issues and then require their fresh adjudication under a reserved federal label. The owners pursued broader state takings claims beyond the antecedent mandamus issue; the lower courts treated the resolved issues as coextensive with those needed for their federal claims. Review of that particular California-law determination was not granted.

The owners’ strongest objection is that required state compensation litigation, followed by preclusion, may prevent a federal district-court merits determination. Allen v. McCurry rejects an unrestricted entitlement to relitigate state-decided issues merely to obtain a federal forum. Kremer v. Chemical Construction requires a statutory basis for displacing §1738; the proposed takings category supplies none. The obligation to recognize an existing judgment remains distinct from the justification for requiring the preceding state litigation.

Exxon Mobil keeps ordinary preclusion distinct from jurisdiction over an independent federal claim. Lingle’s rejection of regulatory efficacy as an independent takings test supplies no exception to recognition of the state judgment. Wholesale reconsideration of Williamson County’s state-compensation prerequisite lies outside the granted question.

**Precedent treatment.** Dodd is reaffirmed and applied; England retains its abstention-reservation protection at its proper scope; Allen and Kremer supply the recognition rule and the requirement of statutory displacement.

#### Precedent Treatment

- **Dodd v. Hood River County:** Reaffirmed and applied to the asserted compulsory-state-litigation exception; reserving a cause does not erase binding common issues.
- **England v. Louisiana State Board of Medical Examiners:** Applied at its abstention scope. A reservation protects the appropriate return for a distinct federal issue, not a second decision of issues submitted to and decided by the state court.
- **Allen v. McCurry:** Applied to reject an unrestricted federal-forum entitlement to relitigate previously determined issues.
- **Kremer v. Chemical Construction Corp.:** Applied to require statutory grounds for an exception to §1738.
- **Exxon Mobil Corp. v. Saudi Basic Industries Corp.:** Preserved at its distinction between jurisdiction and state-law preclusion.
- **Lingle v. Chevron U.S.A. Inc.:** Its rejection of regulatory efficacy as an independent takings test is respected; it does not displace recognition of the state judgment.
- **Williamson County Regional Planning Commission v. Hamilton Bank:** Its state-compensation prerequisite supplies the asserted route into state court, not an exception to §1738; reconsideration of that prerequisite is outside the grant.

#### Law After Decision

The issue-specific rule in Dodd governs even when the claimant pursued state compensation procedures to satisfy a prerequisite to federal litigation. Federal courts give the state judgment its lawful preclusive effect issue by issue, preserving the distinction between a reserved cause and a binding determination. State litigation alone does not establish that every later federal issue is barred.

#### Separate Writings

**O’Connor, joined by Kennedy and Thomas, concurring.** The concurrence joins the full statutory holding and agrees that the existing state judgment must receive the recognition §1738 commands. It would reconsider Williamson County’s separate state-litigation prerequisite in an appropriate case. That requirement differs from obtaining a final administrative decision identifying how a regulation applies to the property. Once the government has made that decision, the concurrence questions the constitutional or prudential basis for requiring state compensation litigation before a federal takings action.

State courts’ competence to adjudicate federal rights does not explain why takings claims must uniquely begin there. Fair Assessment’s treatment of state taxation rests on a historically grounded comity principle and does not by itself establish a parallel land-use rule. Mandatory state litigation combined with ordinary preclusion can prevent a later federal district-court merits decision. That practical effect calls for examination of the prerequisite, not suspension of §1738. Neither party seeks its reconsideration here, the grant does not include it, and reconsideration would not benefit these petitioners.

#### Source Notes

No. 04-340; 28 U.S.C. §1738; San Remo Hotel, L.P. v. City and County of San Francisco, 364 F.3d 1088; 27 Cal.4th 643, 41 P.3d 87. Review is confined to the categorical-exception question and assumes the ungranted California preclusion determinations.

#### Mandate, Remedy, and Stage

The mandate affirms the Ninth Circuit’s judgment rejecting the categorical exception. The existing federal dismissal remains effective on the issues within that judgment.

**End of entry: San Remo Hotel, L.P. v. City and County of San Francisco, Merits decision, June 20, 2005.**
