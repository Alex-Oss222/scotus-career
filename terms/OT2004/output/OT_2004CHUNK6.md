### California Public Employees’ Retirement System v. WorldCom, Inc.

Plenary merits decision, March 30, 2005

#### Chronology and Posture

On review of the Second Circuit, 368 F.3d 86, No. 04-0219, following full merits submission. The Second Circuit affirmed orders denying remand in an accepted interlocutory appeal under 28 U.S.C. §1292(b). The question is whether bankruptcy-related removal under §1452(a) overrides Securities Act §22(a)’s express protection against removal of the individual Securities Act actions.

All nine Justices participated in the merits submission and decision.

#### Judgment

**Reversed and remanded, 9–0.**

**Supporting:** Chief Justice Stone-Zsela and Justices Stevens, O’Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg, and Breyer.

The lower courts shall identify the covered individual Securities Act actions participating in the reviewed forum judgment and enter orders returning them to their originating state courts.

#### Opinion Topology

STONE-ZSELA, C. J., delivered the opinion of the Court, in which STEVENS, O’CONNOR, SCALIA, KENNEDY, SOUTER, THOMAS, GINSBURG, and BREYER, JJ., joined.

#### Holdings

##### The express nonremoval command governs these individual actions

**Holding and operative rule.** Section 1452(a) does not authorize removal of an individual Securities Act action filed in a state court of competent jurisdiction when §22(a), 15 U.S.C. §77v(a), prohibits its removal. Section 22(a)’s exception concerns §77p(c) removal of covered class actions based on state law that allege a material misstatement or omission, or manipulative or deceptive conduct, in connection with the purchase or sale of a covered security; the present individual actions fall outside that exception.

**Authority.** The unanimous opinion of the Court supplies the controlling rule and the review-channel holding below.

**Controlling explanation.** Congress’s broad bankruptcy-removal permission and its express prohibition for a defined class of securities cases can operate together. Things Remembered, Inc. v. Petrarca supplies that coexistence principle: bankruptcy procedure does not automatically displace independently applicable statutory limits. Section 1452(a) continues to authorize removal within its field; §22(a) prevents removal of these particular cases. A potential effect on a bankruptcy estate establishes, at most, a jurisdictional predicate rather than permission to disregard the forum command.

The absence from §1452(a) of §1441(a)’s introductory reservation for other Acts does not itself repeal §22(a). The removal provisions already differ in who may remove, what may be removed, and the jurisdictional conditions and exclusions. Section 22(a) addresses removal to any federal court. Its retained prohibition is not confined to §1441. The advantages of centralizing bankruptcy-related litigation cannot supply an exception Congress did not enact.

The statutory protection for actions brought by a State, political subdivision or state pension plan on its own behalf remains operative, as does its protection for an authorized class consisting solely of such named public entities. The entity-counting rule treats a pension plan or other qualifying entity as one person unless formed to participate in the action; its beneficiaries do not by themselves convert its individual case into a covered class action. On the individual-action status presented here, state-court return follows even assuming the asserted contribution, indemnification or reimbursement interests satisfy §1334. The actual sufficiency of the estate connection lies outside the certified statutory question, which assumes that connection.

##### The appeal concerns legal removal authority

**Holding and operative rule.** The accepted §1292(b) appeal presents the legal authority to remove these actions. Section 1452(b)’s bar on appellate review of a remand or refusal to remand on equitable grounds does not govern that distinct statutory question.

**Controlling explanation.** Quackenbush v. Allstate Insurance Co. requires attention to the ground of the remand decision and its lawful review channel. Things Remembered preserves the restrictions governing the kinds of remand it addresses. Here the order rejected the asserted statutory prohibition, and the certified appeal concerns that legal ruling. Section 1452(b) expressly protects both equitable remand and equitable refusal; the distinction rests on the ground of decision, not merely whether remand was granted or denied.

#### Precedent Treatment

- Things Remembered, Inc. v. Petrarca is applied for the coexistence of overlapping statutes and preserves its covered-remand review rule.
- Connecticut National Bank v. Germain supplies the coexistence principle adopted in Things Remembered; that principle now resolves this distinct statutory overlap.
- Quackenbush v. Allstate Insurance Co. is applied to distinguish the legal ground and authorized review channel.

#### Law After Decision

The nonremoval protection applies throughout the statutory category of covered individual Securities Act cases brought in competent state courts. Establishing bankruptcy-related jurisdiction cannot alone remove that protection. Section 1452(a) retains its separate operation, including its exclusions for Tax Court proceedings and governmental police-or-regulatory enforcement actions and its requirement of §1334 jurisdiction. Original federal Securities Act actions, bankruptcy claims administration and independently applicable bankruptcy stays remain governed by their own statutory provisions.

#### Source Notes

Judgment below: 368 F.3d 86 (2d Cir. 2004). Securities Act §§16 and 22, 15 U.S.C. §§77p, 77v; 28 U.S.C. §§1292(b), 1334, 1452. The certified appeal assumed related-to jurisdiction; the appellate court expressly declined to resolve the sufficiency of the asserted estate connection.

#### Mandate, Remedy, and Stage

The mandate returns the matter for entry and implementation of the corresponding state-court remand orders. The district court shall transmit the covered actions to their originating courts and cease merits adjudication of those actions after remand. Any independently applicable bankruptcy stay continues according to its own terms.

**End of entry: California Public Employees’ Retirement System v. WorldCom, Inc., plenary merits decision, March 30, 2005.**

### Exxon Mobil Corp. v. Saudi Basic Industries Corp., No. 03-1696

Merits decision, March 30, 2005

#### Chronology and Posture

Argued February 23, 2005. On writ of certiorari to the Third Circuit, 364 F.3d 102. The question is whether entry of judgment in a parallel state action deprives a federal district court of jurisdiction over an independently filed action concerning the same dispute. The Third Circuit dismissed the federal action after the Delaware judgment, while hearing SABIC’s immunity appeal.

All nine Justices participated at argument and decision.

#### Judgment

**Reversed and remanded, 9–0.**

**Supporting:** Chief Justice Stone-Zsela and Justices Stevens, O’Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg, and Breyer.

The federal proceeding is restored for adjudication of the remaining issues under their governing jurisdictional, preclusion and procedural rules.

#### Opinion Topology

KENNEDY, J., delivered the opinion of the Court, in which STONE-ZSELA, C. J., and STEVENS, O'CONNOR, SCALIA, SOUTER, THOMAS, GINSBURG, and BREYER, JJ., joined.

#### Holdings

##### A parallel state judgment does not extinguish an independent federal action

**Holding and operative rule.** A federal district court does not lose original jurisdiction merely because a parallel state action reaches judgment while the federal action is pending. The Rooker–Feldman prohibition on district-court appellate review applies when a state-court loser brings a federal action after the state judgment, complains of injury caused by that judgment and seeks its review and rejection; independent claims remain subject to the applicable jurisdictional statutes, §1738 preclusion and properly applicable abstention rules.

**Authority.** The unanimous opinion of the Court controls the rule and its application.

**Controlling explanation.** Jordahl v. Democratic Party of Virginia distinguishes an injury caused by the asserted legal error of a state judicial order from an independently actionable wrong. It therefore bars a damages claim that would require appellate rejection of an earlier state judgment, while preserving an original claim based on underlying conduct. The present case applies that distinction to the sequence of parallel proceedings.

Exxon filed its federal royalty claims before the Delaware judgment, sought relief for SABIC’s conduct and prevailed in the state trial. The later judgment did not turn that original suit into an appeal. Rooker v. Fidelity Trust Co. and District of Columbia Court of Appeals v. Feldman preserve the appellate boundary reflected in §1257; that allocation does not make original jurisdiction disappear whenever state litigation reaches judgment first. Congress’s express grants of federal review, including habeas review under §2254, retain their own terms.

SABIC’s finality and duplication concerns call for the doctrines that actually govern those concerns. Section 1738 requires the federal court to give the state judgment the preclusive effect it receives under the applicable state law. Colorado River Water Conservation District v. United States supplies the distinct exceptional-circumstances inquiry for parallel-litigation abstention, and Quackenbush v. Allstate Insurance Co. requires an applicable source before surrendering federal adjudication. Overlapping issues, or an independent claim inconsistent with a state court’s legal conclusion, do not alone create an appellate action.

The federal complaint invoked §1330. SABIC’s remaining immunity contentions, the actual effect of the Delaware judgment and any residual mootness issue require adjudication on their own predicates because the court of appeals disposed of the action on the different jurisdictional theory rejected here.

#### Precedent Treatment

- Jordahl v. Democratic Party of Virginia retains its claim-specific distinction between judgment-caused and independent injury; this decision resolves the pending-action timing question it left open.
- Rooker v. Fidelity Trust Co. retains its prohibition on district-court appellate reassessment of state judgments.
- District of Columbia Court of Appeals v. Feldman retains its prohibition on district-court appellate reassessment of state judgments.
- Quackenbush v. Allstate Insurance Co. preserves the separate sources and conditions of abstention.
- Colorado River Water Conservation District v. United States preserves the separate sources and conditions of abstention.
- Marrese v. American Academy of Orthopaedic Surgeons supplies §1738’s direction to examine the State’s preclusion law.

#### Law After Decision

The jurisdictional rule governs civil original actions involving parallel state litigation. A state judgment may determine the fate of particular federal claims through preclusion, while the federal action retains its original character. Courts must examine the source of the asserted injury, the relief requested and the commencement sequence instead of treating overlap as jurisdictional extinction. A complaint styled as damages remains subject to the appellate bar when its actual claim seeks rejection of a preexisting state judgment.

#### Source Notes

Judgment below: 364 F.3d 102 (3d Cir. 2004). 28 U.S.C. §§1257, 1330, 1738. The federal action began before the Delaware trial judgment and alleged injury from the underlying royalty conduct.

#### Mandate, Remedy, and Stage

The case returns for the remaining immunity appeal and consistent further proceedings. The lower courts must identify each live claim and requested relief, apply the Delaware judgment’s preclusive effect under §1738 and address any genuine jurisdictional or mootness defense. The Delaware judgment remains effective, and any remaining recovery must respect its binding effect and the prohibition on duplicate recovery.

**End of entry: Exxon Mobil Corp. v. Saudi Basic Industries Corp., merits decision, March 30, 2005.**

### Rhines v. Weber, No. 03-9046

Merits decision, March 30, 2005

#### Chronology and Posture

Argued January 12, 2005. On writ of certiorari to the Eighth Circuit, 346 F.3d 799. The question is whether a district court may stay a timely habeas petition containing exhausted and unexhausted claims while the prisoner seeks state-court exhaustion, and what limits govern that discretion. The court of appeals vacated the existing stay under its restrictive view of that authority.

All nine Justices participated at argument and decision.

#### Judgment

**Vacated and remanded, 9–0.**

**Supporting:** Chief Justice Stone-Zsela and Justices Stevens, O’Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg, and Breyer.

The pending federal proceeding shall be retained during prompt consideration of whether the actual stay meets the Court’s standard.

#### Opinion Topology

O’CONNOR, J., delivered the opinion of the Court. SOUTER, J., filed an opinion concurring in part and concurring in the judgment, in which GINSBURG and BREYER, JJ., joined. STONE-ZSELA, C. J., filed a concurring opinion.

| Writing | Author | Joined by | Relationship to judgment | Scope joined |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Opinion of the Court | O'Connor | Stone-Zsela, Stevens, Scalia, Kennedy, Thomas, Ginsburg, Breyer; Souter to the extent stated | Vacatur and remand | All propositions receive nine joins except the necessary initial good-cause condition, which receives eight |
| Concurrence in part and in the judgment | Souter | Ginsburg, Breyer | Supports vacatur and remand | Would omit initial cause; accepts the remaining stay limits |
| Concurrence | Stone-Zsela | — | Joins the Court | Ordinary civil proof of the predicate factual showing |

#### Holdings

##### Retaining a timely mixed petition during exhaustion

**Holding and operative rule.** A district court may stay a timely mixed petition while the prisoner exhausts state remedies if the prisoner shows good cause for not exhausting first, the unexhausted claims are not plainly meritless, and the record does not show abusive litigation or intentional delay. Good cause means a reasonable explanation in the actual circumstances, not an extraordinary-circumstances or procedural-default test; the court must set reasonable, definite deadlines for seeking state relief and returning to federal court, accounting for delays outside the prisoner’s control.

**Authority.** Chief Justice Stone-Zsela and Justices Stevens, O’Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer join the complete framework. Justice Souter joins the existence of stay power and the merit, anti-abuse and deadline limits, but not the initial good-cause prerequisite.

**Controlling explanation.** Rose v. Lundy gives state courts the first opportunity to decide federal claims. Congress’s exhaustion and limitations rules can serve that purpose while a federal court retains a timely filed action. Landis v. North American Co. supplies ordinary control over pending cases, exercised here within the habeas statutes. Duncan v. Walker excludes time spent in federal court from statutory tolling under §2244(d)(2); retention preserves an existing action rather than supplying a new filing period. Pliler v. Ford left this stay question open while establishing separate protections against particular court-caused filing losses.

The warden correctly argues that routine, open-ended stays would weaken finality and reward premature filing. Cause, a potential-merit screen, exclusion of abuse and definite deadlines answer that objection. Good cause preserves an incentive to exhaust before filing and permits an individualized explanation. Its reasonable-explanation meaning also avoids turning technical uncertainty about exhaustion into a second, rigid procedural-default obstacle. Section 2254(b)(2) permits denial of an unexhausted claim on its merits; it does not compel denial. Relief remains subject to exhaustion, absence of an available state corrective process, circumstances making that process ineffective to protect the applicant’s rights, or counsel’s express waiver of exhaustion for the State, as the statute provides.

Rhines filed with more than eleven months remaining before limitations expired during federal processing. The district court identified eight unexhausted claims and imposed sixty-day initiation and return conditions. Those facts establish the practical importance of stay authority. Actual cause, potential merit, delay and the reasonableness of those conditions require findings under the announced standard.

##### Protecting timely review when applying that discretion

**Holding and operative rule.** Where good cause, potential merit and absence of intentional delay are established and dismissal would defeat timely federal review, denying a stay and dismissing the entire petition ordinarily abuses discretion; the court should retain the action, and any refusal requires a reason compatible with the statute. If a stay is inappropriate, the prisoner must be allowed to delete unexhausted claims and proceed on exhausted claims when whole-petition dismissal would unreasonably impair federal relief.

**Authority.** All nine Justices join this sufficient case for retention and the deletion protection. Justice Souter’s agreement does not make good cause a necessary condition in his separate view.

**Controlling explanation.** The exhaustion requirement directs claims first to the state courts; it does not require destroying a timely federal action whenever the two processes overlap. The filing date and continued pendency therefore matter independently of statutory tolling. A refusal must apply the governing predicates and account for its actual effect on federal review. If retention is unsuitable, deletion preserves the exhausted claims without demanding adjudication of the others. Pliler’s neutral-warning duty retains its particular triggers—an unrepresented prisoner and a qualifying court-directed dismissal election. Rhines had appointed counsel for his amended federal petition, so that duty does not automatically govern his election.

#### Precedent Treatment

- Rose v. Lundy’s total-exhaustion requirement remains operative with bounded stay-and-abeyance in the limitations setting.
- Duncan v. Walker retains its statutory non-tolling rule; this decision answers the distinct stay question.
- Pliler v. Ford retains its separate notice, causation, diligence and lawful-remedy rules.
- Landis v. North American Co. supplies ordinary management authority subject to the governing habeas statutes.

#### Law After Decision

District courts have a uniform standard for retaining timely mixed petitions while state remedies are pursued. The rule preserves the existing filing and requires prompt exhaustion and return; reopening a terminated action still requires an independently valid procedural ground. The actual stay predicates and reasonable deadlines are matters of application under settled standards, including the deletion protection when whole-petition dismissal would unreasonably impair federal review.

#### Separate Writings

Justice Souter, joined by Justices Ginsburg and Breyer, would omit an initial good-cause requirement and focus on demonstrated intentional delay. Exhaustion can be difficult even for courts, and prisoners often lack legal training; a further threshold dispute may consume time without improving protection against abuse. He accepts retention power, the plainly-meritless screen, anti-abuse limits, reasonable deadlines and the judgment. Justices Ginsburg and Breyer share that practical preference while also accepting and joining the Court’s governing reasonable-explanation requirement.

Chief Justice Stone-Zsela joins the Court and would expressly require the prisoner to establish the predicate factual showing by the ordinary civil standard. That showing concerns justification and the procedural conditions for retaining the case, with the merits inquiry remaining whether the claims are plainly meritless.

#### Source Notes

Judgment below: 346 F.3d 799 (8th Cir. 2003). 28 U.S.C. §§2244(d), 2254(b)–(c). The district court’s July 3, 2002 order used sixty-day initiation and return conditions; the second state petition was filed August 22, 2002.

#### Mandate, Remedy, and Stage

On remand, the lower courts shall assess Rhines’s actual reason for nonexhaustion, the potential merit of the eight claims, any abusive or intentional delay and his state-court progress. They shall evaluate the existing sixty-day conditions and impose reasonable exhaustion-and-return deadlines if a stay remains warranted. If a stay is refused, Rhines must receive a meaningful opportunity to delete the unexhausted claims where wholesale dismissal would unreasonably impair federal relief. Any dismissal must identify its actual effect on timely federal review.

**End of entry: Rhines v. Weber, merits decision, March 30, 2005.**

### Smith v. City of Jackson, No. 03-1160

Merits decision, March 30, 2005

#### Chronology and Posture

Argued November 3, 2004. On writ of certiorari to the Fifth Circuit, 351 F.3d 183. The questions are whether the Age Discrimination in Employment Act permits a disparate-impact challenge to a neutral employment practice and whether the City’s compensation plan satisfies the governing statutory rule. The Fifth Circuit affirmed summary judgment on the impact claim because it regarded that theory as categorically unavailable.

All nine Justices participated at argument and decision.

#### Judgment

**Affirmed as to the disparate-impact claim, 9–0.**

**Supporting:** Chief Justice Stone-Zsela and Justices Stevens, O’Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg, and Breyer.

The Justices differ on whether the statute permits that cause of action, as stated in the opinions.

#### Opinion Topology

STEVENS, J., delivered the opinion of the Court. SCALIA, J., and STONE-ZSELA, C. J., filed concurring opinions. O’CONNOR, J., filed an opinion concurring in the judgment, in which KENNEDY and THOMAS, JJ., joined.

| Writing | Author | Joined by | Relationship to judgment | Scope joined |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Opinion of the Court | Stevens | Stone-Zsela, Souter, Ginsburg, Breyer in full; Scalia except as stated | Affirms on reasonable nonage factors | Six support operative recognition, particular-practice causation, reasonable-factors rule and application; five support independent judicial construction |
| Concurrence | Scalia | — | Joins the stated portions and the judgment | Recognition through the EEOC’s delegated interpretation |
| Concurrence | Stone-Zsela | — | Joins the Court in full | Employer bears the reasonable-factors defense |
| Concurrence in the judgment | O'Connor | Kennedy and Thomas | Affirms because the impact cause is unavailable | No-impact construction and conditional specificity, reasonable-factors and proof views |

#### Holdings

##### The ADEA permits an identified practice’s discriminatory effects to be challenged

**Holding and operative rule.** Section 623(a)(2) permits employees to challenge an identified neutral employment practice that causes protected-age disadvantage in employment opportunities or status, without proving that age motivated the practice. The employees must identify the particular practice and establish the causal adverse effect; an age imbalance alone does not identify its cause.

**Authority.** Chief Justice Stone-Zsela and Justices Stevens, Scalia, Souter, Ginsburg and Breyer support this operative rule. Chief Justice Stone-Zsela and Justices Stevens, Souter, Ginsburg and Breyer join the Court’s independent statutory construction and explanation below. Justice Scalia reaches recognition through the agency ground stated in his concurrence.

**Controlling explanation.** The subsection addresses limitations, segregation or classifications that adversely affect employment opportunities or employee status. Griggs v. Duke Power Co. supplies the established effects-based reading of materially parallel language. The ADEA’s additional reasonable-factors qualification gives that reading a distinct limit suitable to this statute: an adverse effect associated with age does not make a reasonable nonage differentiation unlawful.

The City and the concurrence in the judgment read the because-of-age language as requiring a motivated age decision in every case, with the reasonable-factors clause merely confirming a safe harbor. That reading does not give the effects-oriented subsection and its express qualification their separate work. The necessary causal connection is an age-linked adverse effect from the identified practice; actual age motivation remains the question for disparate treatment.

Hazen Paper Co. v. Biggins requires age motivation for intentional treatment and recognizes the difference between age and years of service, including deliberate use of service as a proxy. It expressly reserved disparate impact. General Dynamics Land Systems, Inc. v. Cline determines the direction of statutory protection, not whether an effects-based mechanism exists. Wards Cove Packing Co. v. Atonio supplies the requirement to identify the actual challenged practice and connect it to the disparity. The 1991 amendments to Title VII did not amend the ADEA; their different provisions cannot be transposed into this Act. The EEOC’s considered recognition of impact liability supports the statutory reading without supplying the Court’s independent ground.

##### Reasonable nonage factors sustain this compensation plan

**Holding and operative rule.** Under §623(f)(1), a differentiation based on reasonable factors other than age is outside disparate-impact liability. Reasonableness concerns the actual nonage factor and its relationship to the employment objective; it does not require business necessity or selection of the least disparate workable alternative. On this record, rank, tenure, existing salary and adjustment toward regional compensation levels supply reasonable nonage factors even assuming that the challenged allocation caused the reported age disparity.

**Authority.** Chief Justice Stone-Zsela and Justices Stevens, Scalia, Souter, Ginsburg and Breyer join this statutory standard and its application in the Court’s opinion.

**Controlling explanation.** Age can correlate with experience, service and compensation without making those characteristics age itself. Congress expressly preserved reasonable nonage differentiation. Griggs supplies the effects analogy, but the ADEA’s enacted qualification controls the justification required here. The EEOC regulation’s business-necessity language cannot replace that statutory standard.

The City sought to bring starting police salaries toward the regional average. A survey of comparable southeastern communities supplied wage ranges for five ranks, divided into steps and half-steps; each employee entered the lowest step that still produced at least a two-percent raise. Employees with lower existing salaries could receive larger percentage increases from the adjustment. Officers in the two highest ranks, all over forty, received larger dollar raises despite smaller percentages. Some officers over forty were also in the shorter-tenure group.

These actual mechanics connect the factors to the compensation objective. The statistics—66.2 percent of officers below forty and 45.3 percent above forty receiving raises exceeding ten percent—do not make that method unreasonable. A demand for equal percentages or another workable allocation does not defeat a reasonable nonage justification. The City therefore prevails on the impact claim on the established plan facts, assuming the claimed causal disparity.

#### Precedent Treatment

- Hazen Paper Co. v. Biggins retains its intentional-treatment and age-proxy rules; its reserved employee disparate-impact question is answered.
- General Dynamics Land Systems, Inc. v. Cline retains the Act’s protection of older employees against disadvantage favoring younger employees.
- Griggs v. Duke Power Co. supplies the effects-language analogy, limited here by the ADEA’s own reasonable-factors text.
- Wards Cove Packing Co. v. Atonio supplies identification and causal-linkage requirements; the ADEA’s enacted qualification governs justification.
- Christensen v. Harris County retains its source-specific rules for assessing agency interpretation.
- United States v. Mead Corp. retains its source-specific rules for assessing agency interpretation.

#### Law After Decision

Employees may pursue effects-based claims under §623(a)(2) by proving an identified neutral practice’s causal protected-age disadvantage. Employers’ actual nonage factors are assessed for reasonableness in relation to their employment objectives, without a universal least-disparate-alternative requirement. Ordinary job criteria are therefore challengeable, while reasonable use of nonage factors remains lawful. Deliberate use of tenure as an age proxy remains governed by intentional-treatment law.

#### Separate Writings

Justice Scalia concurs in the stated portions and the judgment. Congress gave the EEOC authority to implement the ADEA through §628, and its longstanding national interpretation recognizes restrictions on adverse-impact practices. He accepts the Court’s textual analysis as showing that interpretation is permissible, rather than as his independent judicial construction. The delegated national interpretation differs from the advisory response in Christensen and transaction-specific classification letters in Mead. Its recognition of impact liability and its use of business necessity are distinct propositions: the former is reasonable; the latter conflicts with the enacted reasonable-factors standard. He therefore joins the statutory justification rule and its application.

O'Connor, joined by Kennedy and Thomas, concurs in the judgment on the ground that the ADEA prohibits age-based employment decisions, not an additional unintentional-effects wrong. The effects words describe harmful forms of age discrimination; the reasonable-factors clause confirms a safe harbor for nonage criteria. The Act’s structure and the legitimate correlation of age with tenure and compensation counsel against transferring Griggs from Title VII. This writing also rejects binding reliance on the EEOC interpretation because it principally explains the exemption rather than construing the prohibitory subsection, and misstates the exemption through business necessity. Conditionally, if impact claims are recognized, these Justices accept particular-practice specificity and the narrower reasonable-factors justification; under their Wards Cove reading the employee must disprove the employer’s produced nonage explanation.

Chief Justice Stone-Zsela joins the Court and places the statutory reasonable-factors defense on the employer. The employees must prove the particular practice, protected-age disadvantage and causal connection by the ordinary civil standard; the employer must establish its reasonable nonage justification. He concludes that the City establishes that defense on the reviewed plan facts.

#### Source Notes

Judgment below: 351 F.3d 183 (5th Cir. 2003). ADEA §§4(a)(2), 4(f)(1), and 9, 29 U.S.C. §§623(a)(2), 623(f)(1), 628; 29 C.F.R. §1625.7. The challenged plan used rank, steps, existing compensation and regional wage ranges; the reviewed impact judgment concerns that plan.

#### Mandate, Remedy, and Stage

The mandate sustains summary judgment on the disparate-impact branch. The separate intentional-treatment claim continues on its existing discovery remand under its own governing law.

**End of entry: Smith v. City of Jackson, merits decision, March 30, 2005.**

### Consumers Energy Co. v. Federal Energy Regulatory Commission

Merits decision, April 4, 2005

#### Chronology and Posture

Following full merits submission. On review of the D.C. Circuit judgment, 367 F.3d 915, denying a petition for review of FERC's approval and rehearing orders. The question is whether FERC may authorize a foreign utility's affiliate to make market-based electricity sales in the United States when the foreign transmission system uses paired spot-market sales and purchases instead of separately reservable point-to-point transmission. Consumers also invokes Order No. 2000's independence requirements as a distinct objection to comparability.

All nine Justices participated.

#### Judgment

**Affirmed, 9–0.**

**Supporting:** Chief Justice Stone-Zsela and Justices Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer.

Consumers' request to set aside the authorization is denied. The challenged United States sales authorization remains effective.

#### Opinion Topology

GINSBURG, J., delivered the opinion of the Court.

| Writing | Author | Joined by | Relationship to judgment | Scope joined |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Opinion of the Court | Ginsburg | All other Associate Justices in full; Chief Justice Stone-Zsela as to Part I | Affirms denial of review | Part I: comparability and review, all nine; Part II: rehearing prerequisite, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer |

Chief Justice Stone-Zsela joins the judgment and agrees that the separate Order No. 2000 independence-as-comparability objection is not reached; he does not join Part II's reasoning.

#### Holdings

##### Part I — Comparable access turns on actual operation

**Holding and operative rule.** FERC may authorize the covered domestic market-based sales of a foreign utility's affiliate when its reasoned assessment of the administrative record establishes functionally comparable nondiscriminatory access, satisfaction of its market-power requirements and compliance with the Federal Power Act's just-and-reasonable duties. The foreign arrangement need not duplicate every domestic tariff mechanism; section 825l(b) makes findings supported by substantial evidence conclusive, technical application remains subject to review for arbitrary action or legal error, and statutory authority remains independently reviewable.

**Authority.** Part I of the Court's opinion is joined by all nine participating Justices and controls the rule and its application.

**Controlling explanation.** The Court holds that the agency reasonably assessed the combined operation of Ontario's arrangement. FERC corrected its original affiliation conclusion on rehearing and then examined comparable access. Absent congestion, paired sales into and purchases out of Ontario offset at the common market price, leaving the export charge. Separate import and export financial transmission rights hedge the corresponding intertie price differences. These rights provide financial protection; they do not reserve physical capacity.

That difference is Consumers' strongest objection, but comparability concerns the functioning of the whole arrangement. Every participant uses the same transaction rules; at least twelve United States marketers had successfully traded through and out of Ontario; OPG lacked generation market power outside Ontario; and the alleged discriminatory manipulation of congestion lacked supporting instances or costs. Consumers identified no actual discriminatory operation. Refusal of the requested fifty-megawatt reservation therefore did not establish an inadequate access arrangement.

New York v. FERC preserves both lawful transmission authority and the mandatory response to an established section 206 violation. California ex rel. Lockyer v. FERC requires review of the agency's actual reasons. Allentown Mack Sales & Service, Inc. v. NLRB requires evidence under the announced standard, and Timken Co. v. United States distinguishes a supported permissible method from a mandatory one. Those rules sustain this explanation rather than require an identical foreign tariff. The determination concerns this authorization and record; a materially different arrangement requires its own supported assessment. Direct regulatory authority over foreign facilities lies outside the question presented.

##### Part II — The distinct independence objection was not presented on rehearing

**Holding and operative rule.** Under section 825l(b), a reviewing court may not consider an objection omitted from the application for rehearing unless reasonable ground excuses the omission. Raising independence to establish affiliation does not preserve the different claim that a lack of Order No. 2000 independence defeats comparable access.

**Authority.** Part II is joined by Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer; their eight votes directly control this rule and its application. Chief Justice Stone-Zsela agrees with the disposition of this objection without joining that rationale.

**Controlling explanation.** Consumers obtained the affiliation determination it sought. Its rehearing submissions did not separately ask FERC to decide whether the asserted lack of independence itself precluded comparable service. The Commission therefore lacked the opportunity that section 825l(b) requires to address that distinct objection, and no reasonable ground excusing its omission is established. The Court declines to reach that theory. Section 825l(b)'s separate procedure for additional evidence requires materiality and reasonable grounds for not presenting the evidence to the agency; it does not automatically excuse failure to present a legal objection on rehearing.

#### Precedent Treatment

- New York v. FERC is applied to preserve lawful domestic regulatory authority and the duty to correct an established statutory violation.
- California ex rel. Lockyer v. FERC is applied to require the agency's own reasons and the administrative record.
- Allentown Mack Sales & Service, Inc. v. NLRB is applied to require evidentiary support under the agency's announced standard.
- Timken Co. v. United States is applied to distinguish a legally permissible supported method from a method the statute compels.

#### Law After Decision

Foreign-affiliate access arrangements are assessed for supported functional comparability under the domestic authorization requirements. Institutional differences require reasoned assessment of their competitive effects; a demand for identical tariff form does not itself establish unlawful access. Objections to a FERC order must be presented on rehearing, subject to section 825l(b)'s reasonable-ground exception, and each distinct objection must meet that requirement.

#### Source Notes

Judgment below: Consumers Energy Co. v. FERC, 367 F.3d 915 (D.C. Cir. 2004), No. 03-1162. Federal Power Act sections 205, 206 and 313, 16 U.S.C. §§824d, 824e, 825l; FERC Orders Nos. 888 and 2000. The challenged approval and rehearing orders issued in 2002 and 2003.

#### Mandate, Remedy, and Stage

The denial of review stands, and FERC continues administering the authorization under its statutory oversight duties. A later claim of actual discriminatory operation must proceed through the appropriate agency channel on its own record. Under section 825l(c), rehearing or judicial review does not itself stay an order absent a specific agency or court order.

**End of entry: Consumers Energy Co. v. Federal Energy Regulatory Commission, merits decision, April 4, 2005.**

### Johnson v. United States, No. 03-9685

Merits decision, April 4, 2005

#### Chronology and Posture

Argued January 18, 2005. On writ of certiorari to the Eleventh Circuit, 340 F.3d 1219. The question is when §2255’s one-year period begins for a federal prisoner whose sentence was enhanced by a state conviction later vacated in state court. The court of appeals held that the vacatur could not supply a newly discoverable fact under paragraph 6(4).

All nine Justices participated at argument and decision.

#### Judgment

**Reversed and remanded, 5–4.**

**Supporting:** Chief Justice Stone-Zsela and Justices Stevens, Scalia, Kennedy, and Ginsburg.
**Opposing:** Justices O’Connor, Souter, Thomas, and Breyer.

The lower court must apply the date on which due diligence would have disclosed the order and determine the vacatur’s material effect on the enhanced sentence under the governing requirements.

#### Opinion Topology

SCALIA, J., delivered the opinion of the Court. SOUTER, J., filed a dissenting opinion, in which O’CONNOR, THOMAS, and BREYER, JJ., joined.

| Writing | Author | Joined by | Relationship to judgment | Scope joined |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Opinion of the Court | Scalia | Stone-Zsela, Stevens, Kennedy and Ginsburg in full; O’Connor, Souter, Thomas and Breyer as to Part I | Reversal and remand | All nine join vacatur as fact; five join the discovery-only rule, application and remedy |
| Dissent | Souter | O’Connor, Thomas and Breyer | Would affirm | Procurement-and-discovery rule and its application; joins Part I |

#### Holdings

##### An order removing a particular predicate is a supporting fact

**Holding and operative rule.** An order vacating the particular state conviction used to enhance a federal sentence is a fact supporting a challenge to that sentence under §2255 paragraph 6(4). The movant must establish the order and its material connection to the asserted current-sentence claim; an actual change in that conviction’s status differs from a new judicial interpretation, repeated legal argument or unsuccessful collateral filing.

**Authority.** Part I of the Court’s opinion is joined by all nine Justices.

**Controlling explanation.** The state order changes the legal status of a particular predicate judgment. That event is distinct from the older facts underlying the state challenge. The Government’s argument that only those older facts count would start the period before the state order needed for the present claim exists.

Custis v. United States and Daniels v. United States direct covered attacks on state convictions through their proper review channel and preserve an otherwise authorized later federal application after successful invalidation. Treating the resulting order as fact gives that sequence its actual meaning. Those decisions still require the prisoner to satisfy the independent procedural and substantive requirements for relief from the current sentence.

##### Diligence concerns discovery of the existing order

**Holding and operative rule.** The one-year period under paragraph 6(4) begins when the prisoner could discover the vacatur order through due diligence. The statute requires diligent discovery and filing within one year of that discoverability date; it imposes no additional federal deadline for obtaining a state order that did not yet exist.

**Authority.** Chief Justice Stone-Zsela and Justices Stevens, Scalia, Kennedy and Ginsburg join Part II’s rule, application and remedy.

**Controlling explanation.** Congress selected discovery of facts supporting the claim. Discovering an existing event and bringing that event into existence are different acts. An order cannot be discovered before it is entered. Clay v. United States preserves attention to the particular statutory timing trigger; the distinct finality and new-right triggers do not supply an unstated procurement condition here.

The Government and dissent argue that a prisoner who initiates state proceedings should not control federal timing through unexplained delay. The concern for finality is substantial, but Congress addressed it through the one-year discovery period and the other restrictions governing collateral relief. State procedural rules govern the state challenge, and the independent federal requirements govern the later sentencing claim. Custis and Daniels establish the proper channel without adding a federal schedule for creating a state judgment. Estimating when a hypothetical earlier state proceeding would have produced relief substitutes a different condition for discovery.

Johnson obtained an order vacating convictions that included a predicate of his career-offender enhancement, then filed federally just over three months later. The categorical timing dismissal therefore cannot stand. Actual discoverability and the order’s material effect on the enhanced sentence require application of the governing rules on remand.

#### Precedent Treatment

- Custis v. United States retains its proper-channel rule and separate prerequisites for a later authorized federal application.
- Daniels v. United States retains its proper-channel rule and separate prerequisites for a later authorized federal application. This decision determines the previously unresolved vacatur-fact timing question.
- Clay v. United States preserves the distinct statutory triggers; its ordinary-finality rule remains applicable to the trigger it governs.

#### Law After Decision

Actual orders changing the status of particular enhancement predicates qualify under the factual-discovery trigger. Diligence is measured by when the order could be learned of, with the movant required to establish discoverability and the order’s material connection to the claim. A later state vacatur thus permits consideration through the enacted timing provision while the independent requirements governing the federal sentence challenge continue to apply.

#### Separate Writings

Souter, joined by O’Connor, Thomas and Breyer, accepts the vacatur as a supporting fact but would require reasonable diligence in obtaining it after entry of the enhanced federal judgment, as well as diligence in learning of it. The federal judgment gives the prisoner a concrete reason to challenge the predicate. Because the prisoner initiates the process producing the new event, the dissent reads the express diligence condition to include reasonable pursuit; state collateral rules do not replace the federal sentence’s separate finality interest.

That rule includes three qualifications. First, delay in state proceedings not attributable to the prisoner after diligent initiation does not defeat the later trigger. Second, when the underlying grounds for the state challenge could not be discovered through diligence until after federal judgment, pursuit is measured from their discoverability, and diligent pursuit permits time to run from notice of the resulting vacatur. Third, even a prisoner whose pursuit was deficient may qualify if the federal motion was filed within one year of the notice date diligent pursuit would have produced.

The dissent would affirm here. Johnson waited from the November 29, 1994 federal judgment until February 6, 1998 to begin the state challenge. Even crediting all time before AEDPA’s April 24, 1996 enactment leaves more than twenty-one months. Limited education, self-representation and waiting for inmate assistance do not explain reasonable efforts or a specific impediment during that interval; the extension request did not pursue state vacatur. On that sequence the dissent concludes that diligent pursuit would have produced notice more than a year before the eventual federal motion, so the third qualification does not save it. Reasonable diligence permits genuine obstacles and does not demand the fastest conceivable action.

#### Source Notes

Judgment below: 340 F.3d 1219 (11th Cir. 2003). 28 U.S.C. §2255, paragraph 6(4). The federal enhancement was under the career-offender Guideline §4B1.1. The reviewed state order vacated convictions including a predicate used for that enhancement.

#### Mandate, Remedy, and Stage

The case returns for application of the date on which due diligence would have disclosed the order and a reasoned determination of the vacatur’s scope, its connection to the enhancement and the lawful sentencing consequence under §2255. A timing dismissal may not rest solely on failure to obtain the state order earlier. If the authorized claim establishes that the enhancement lacks a required predicate, the sentence must be vacated to that extent and resentencing must proceed under the applicable sentencing law.

**End of entry: Johnson v. United States, merits decision, April 4, 2005.**

### Rousey v. Jacoway, No. 03-1407

Merits decision, April 4, 2005

#### Chronology and Posture

Argued December 1, 2004. On writ of certiorari to the Eighth Circuit, 347 F.3d 689. The question is whether the Rouseys' qualifying individual retirement accounts provide rights to payment under a similar plan or contract on account of age within 11 U.S.C. §522(d)(10)(E). The court of appeals affirmed categorical denial of their claimed exemption and turnover of the accounts because funds could be withdrawn on demand subject to a tax cost.

All nine Justices participated at argument and decision.

#### Judgment

**Reversed and remanded, 9–0.**

**Supporting:** Chief Justice Stone-Zsela and Justices Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer.

The accounts satisfy the similar-plan and age-connection requirements. The lower courts must determine the amount reasonably necessary for the debtors' and their dependents' support and address any remaining properly preserved account-specific objection. Turnover may reach only amounts lawfully found nonexempt.

#### Opinion Topology

THOMAS, J., delivered the opinion of the Court, in which all other Justices joined.

#### Holdings

**Common authority.** The opinion of the Court, joined in full by all nine participating Justices, controls both holdings and their qualifications.

##### Qualified retirement-income accounts are similar plans

**Holding and operative rule.** A qualifying IRA that provides income replacing retirement wages and has the substantial age-related withdrawal restrictions presented here is a similar plan or contract within section 522(d)(10)(E). The exemption covers a right to future payment but remains limited to the amount reasonably necessary to support the debtor and dependents, and remains subject to the statute's conjunctive insider exception.

That exception applies only when all three conditions hold: the plan was established by or under the auspices of an insider that employed the debtor when the debtor's rights arose; the payment is on account of age or length of service; and the plan fails to qualify under Internal Revenue Code sections 401(a), 403(a), 403(b) or 408. The Rouseys' section 408 qualification defeats the third condition.

**Controlling explanation.** The Court holds that similarity concerns the common legal function of the listed plans, not identity in every term. Stock-bonus, pension, profitsharing and annuity arrangements share the provision of income replacing wages, although they differ in sponsorship, funding and payment schedules. The Rouseys rolled employer pension distributions into their IRAs. Their accounts combine tax-supported retirement saving, substantial restrictions on early full access and required later-life distributions. Minimum distributions must begin by April 1 following the year in which they reach age 70½; the then-effective tax on a distribution deficiency is fifty percent. Those features distinguish the accounts from ordinary unrestricted savings.

The trustee's emphasis on individual control overlooks the variation within the statutory list and treats the substantial early-distribution tax as inconsequential. Congress also expressly refers to section 408 in the insider exception. That reference supports inclusion of qualifying IRAs within the broader category, while leaving the support limit independent. A retirement label or subjective saving purpose alone does not establish these characteristics.

Patterson v. Shumate enforces an independently sufficient section 541(c)(2) exclusion for qualifying trust interests with enforceable transfer restrictions. That exclusion and this exemption perform different work. Patterson left the present exemption question open; its exclusion holding remains intact.

##### Substantial age-related restrictions establish the required connection

**Holding and operative rule.** These accounts provide payment on account of age because the ten-percent additional tax ordinarily restricts full withdrawal before age 59½ and ceases to impose that restriction at that age. Age need not be the sole permissible distribution trigger; limited statutory early-distribution routes do not defeat the connection between age and full access.

**Controlling explanation.** The Court holds that a substantial proportional loss of the amount withdrawn is a real restriction. The ability to obtain funds while surrendering ten percent does not create an unrestricted right to receive the entire balance. Removal of that burden at age 59½ materially changes the right to payment and supplies the causal connection the statute requires.

The early-distribution alternatives do not make full access age-independent. For example, substantially equal payments made at least annually over the holder's life or life expectancy, or the joint lives or life expectancies of the holder and designated beneficiary, may qualify. Modification before age 59½ or within five years after payments begin ordinarily recaptures the avoided additional tax and interest, except when modification results from death or disability. Medical-expense withdrawals are limited to the amount allowable under section 213's deduction rule, whether or not deductions are itemized. These bounded routes answer the trustee's contention that any early withdrawal makes an IRA equivalent to ordinary savings.

The amount reasonably necessary for support and any independent preserved account-specific objection require adjudication below. Rule 4003(c) places the burden of proving an improperly claimed exemption on the objector; the debtors must identify the accounts and the exemption asserted sufficiently for adjudication.

#### Precedent Treatment

- Patterson v. Shumate retains its section 541(c)(2) exclusion for an established qualifying trust interest with an enforceable transfer restriction. This decision resolves the distinct section 522(d)(10)(E) exemption question Patterson left open; it does not convert exemption into exclusion from the estate.

#### Law After Decision

Courts evaluating the statutory exemption must examine the account's qualification, wage-replacement function and actual payment restrictions. Qualifying IRAs with the characteristics decided here satisfy the similar-plan and age requirements even though limited early distributions are possible. The support limit determines the exempt amount independently of categorical eligibility, and the insider exception requires all three statutory conditions.

#### Source Notes

Judgment below: In re Rousey, 347 F.3d 689 (8th Cir. 2003). 11 U.S.C. §§541(c)(2), 522(d)(10)(E); Federal Rule of Bankruptcy Procedure 4003(c); 26 U.S.C. §§72(t), 408, 4974(a). The accounts contain rollover pension funds and qualify under section 408(a).

#### Mandate, Remedy, and Stage

The case returns for revision of the exemption and turnover rulings under these holdings. Before distribution to creditors, the bankruptcy court must identify any nonexempt amount and its legal basis after applying the support limit and resolving remaining properly preserved objections under Rule 4003(c).

**End of entry: Rousey v. Jacoway, merits decision, April 4, 2005.**

### Dura Pharmaceuticals, Inc. v. Broudo, No. 03-932

Merits decision, April 19, 2005

#### Chronology and Posture

Argued January 12, 2005. On writ of certiorari to the Ninth Circuit, 339 F.3d 933. The question is whether alleging and proving an inflated purchase price alone satisfies the economic-loss and loss-causation requirements of a private securities action. Review concerns the spray-device claim, which the court of appeals reinstated on an inflation-only theory. The complaint separately alleged misleading profitability statements.

All nine Justices participated at argument and decision.

#### Judgment

**Reversed and remanded, 9–0.**

**Supporting:** Chief Justice Stone-Zsela and Justices Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer.

The existing inflation-only device theory is legally insufficient. Further proceedings, including consideration of any timely proposed amendment, must follow the applicable procedural rules. The separate profitability and scienter branch is outside this review.

#### Opinion Topology

BREYER, J., delivered the opinion of the Court, in which all other Justices joined.

#### Holdings

**Common authority.** The opinion of the Court, joined in full by all nine participating Justices, controls both holdings and their qualifications.

##### Inflation alone does not establish a loss caused by fraud

**Holding and operative rule.** A public-market purchaser seeking private damages under section 10(b) and Rule 10b-5 must establish an actual economic loss proximately caused by the challenged fraud; alleging or proving that the purchase price was artificially inflated does not by itself establish either requirement. Section 78u-4(b)(4) places the proof burden on the plaintiff, who must establish causal loss by a preponderance of the evidence, excluding losses attributable solely to independent causes.

**Controlling explanation.** The Court holds that inducing a purchase and causing a compensable loss are different matters. A purchaser receives a security bearing the prevailing market price and may transfer the same inflation to a later purchaser before it dissipates. A subsequent price decline may instead reflect economic conditions, industry changes or new facts about the company. These possibilities do not prevent recovery for an actual loss attributable to the fraud; they defeat the assertion that inflation alone necessarily supplies that loss and its cause. A later decline alone likewise supplies no conclusive causal link.

The purchasers argue that paying more than the security's true value immediately diminishes their wealth. That argument does not explain what recoverable loss the alleged falsehood produced where the security still carries the market inflation or its value later changes for independent reasons. Basic Inc. v. Levinson addresses reliance connecting deception to a transaction. It does not presume that the deception caused every subsequent loss. Central Bank of Denver, N.A. v. First Interstate Bank of Denver, N.A. preserves actual causation and recoverable loss even within its affirmative-assistance action. The same elements govern the present misstatement claim.

The causal account remains open to supported mechanisms rather than a single compulsory form of disclosure or economic proof. The plaintiffs need not complete a sale, identify one formal corrective announcement or present an event study merely to state a legally available theory. The required inquiry remains whether the challenged fraud caused the claimed economic loss. A comprehensive final damages methodology lies outside the accepted question.

##### The device allegations fail even ordinary fair notice

**Holding and operative rule.** Assuming for this decision that ordinary Rule 8 supplies the entire pleading demand for economic loss and loss causation, the complaint must give a short, intelligible indication of the loss claimed and its causal connection to the challenged fraud. The reviewed allegation that purchasers paid inflated prices and thereby suffered damages fails even that modest requirement; the Court does not need to decide whether Rule 9 or the PSLRA independently adds a particularity requirement for these elements.

**Controlling explanation.** Swierkiewicz v. Sorema N.A. separates fair notice from an evidentiary proof checklist. It prevents a court from demanding trial proof in a complaint, but it does not supply a missing legally adequate causal-loss account. The purchasers need to identify the loss and connection they assert; they need not prove an econometric model or eliminate every competing cause at pleading.

The complaint treats the device representation principally as a source of purchase-price inflation. Its earnings and device allegations have different histories. The February 1998 earnings announcement was followed by a steep decline; the November device announcement produced a temporary decline that almost fully recovered within a week. The earlier earnings response cannot simply be assigned to the device representation. The reviewed allegations give no adequate account beyond the inflation theory the Court rejects.

The device branch therefore cannot proceed on the present rationale. The district court must identify that defect and consider any timely proposed amendment under the applicable rules. Amendment is neither compelled nor forbidden by this decision. A sufficient causal-loss allegation permits ordinary further adjudication; if the claim remains legally insufficient after lawful amendment proceedings, dismissal follows.

#### Precedent Treatment

- Central Bank of Denver, N.A. v. First Interstate Bank of Denver, N.A. is applied for its retained causation, recoverable-loss and ordinary civil proof requirements; its coverage of qualifying affirmative substantial assistance remains in force.
- Basic Inc. v. Levinson retains its transaction-reliance framework, which does not substitute for proof that the fraud caused an economic loss.
- Swierkiewicz v. Sorema N.A. is applied to keep evidentiary proof requirements distinct from modest notice pleading while retaining the claim's substantive elements.

#### Law After Decision

For private public-market securities damages claims, courts must distinguish the fraud's role in inducing a transaction from its role in causing recoverable economic loss. Inflation alone cannot supply either loss or the causal link; a complaint must at least identify the loss and the connection it asserts, and the plaintiff ultimately bears the statutory proof burden. Several supported causal mechanisms may satisfy that requirement. The assumption about ordinary Rule 8 resolves this complaint without establishing an exclusive pleading rule for all securities elements.

#### Source Notes

Judgment below: Broudo v. Dura Pharmaceuticals, Inc., 339 F.3d 933 (9th Cir. 2003). 15 U.S.C. §§78j(b), 78u-4(b)(4); 17 C.F.R. §240.10b-5; Federal Rules of Civil Procedure 8, 9 and 12. The pleaded purchases occurred between April 15, 1997 and February 24, 1998; the earnings and device announcements occurred in February and November 1998, respectively.

#### Mandate, Remedy, and Stage

The case returns for proceedings consistent with the causal-loss rule and the identified pleading defect. The district court must address any timely amendment under the applicable rules and either dismiss a remaining legally insufficient device claim or proceed on a sufficient claim to the proof the statute requires. The separate profitability and scienter branch retains its own procedural scope.

**End of entry: Dura Pharmaceuticals, Inc. v. Broudo, merits decision, April 19, 2005.**

### Monsanto Co. v. McFarling

Plenary merits decision, April 19, 2005

#### Chronology and Posture

Following full merits submission. On review of the Federal Circuit, 363 F.3d 1336, Nos. 03-1177 and 03-1228. The reviewed partial final judgment concerns a one-season seed agreement, categorical saved-seed and patent-scope misuse defenses, and rejection of an independent tying counterclaim. The questions are whether the presented no-replanting restriction improperly enlarges the utility patent and whether patent scope conclusively defeats the §1 tying claim.

All nine Justices participated in the merits submission and decision.

#### Judgment

The Court affirms in part, vacates in part and remands.

| Judgment component | Disposition and vote | Supporting Justices | Opposing Justices | Remedy or remand |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Contract breach and categorical saved-seed/patent-scope misuse defenses | Affirmed, 9–0 | Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O’Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg, Breyer | — | Confined breach and defense rulings stand, subject to any legally established consequence of a separately preserved enforcement defense |
| Independent tying counterclaim | Vacated and remanded, 6–3 | Stone-Zsela, Stevens, Kennedy, Souter, Ginsburg, Breyer | O’Connor, Scalia, Thomas | Apply the independent tying elements to the preserved record |
| Vacatur of the $780,000 liquidated sum and actual-damages remand | Preserved, 9–0 | Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O’Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg, Breyer | — | Actual damages proceed under the surviving lawful basis |

#### Opinion Topology

SOUTER, J., delivered the opinion of the Court. SCALIA, J., filed an opinion concurring in part and dissenting in part, in which O’CONNOR and THOMAS, JJ., joined.

| Writing | Author | Joined by | Relationship to judgment | Scope joined |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Opinion of the Court | Souter | Stone-Zsela, Stevens, Kennedy, Ginsburg and Breyer in full; O’Connor, Scalia and Thomas as to Parts I, II and IV | Affirms in part, vacates in part and remands | Nine join patent/contract, general independent-tying rule and damages mandate; six join the antitrust application and remand |
| Concurrence in part and dissent in part | Scalia | O’Connor and Thomas | Would also affirm antitrust rejection | Joins common parts; rejects further antitrust inquiry on this presented reproduction-license theory |

#### Holdings

##### Part I: The saved-seed exception does not displace utility-patent coverage

**Holding and operative rule.** A plant-variety saved-seed exception does not authorize reproduction of additional seed within independently valid utility-patent claims. A no-replanting promise directed to that reproduction is not misuse merely because it prevents saving and replanting harvested seed; patent and contractual coverage must be established, and independently applicable defenses retain their force.

**Authority.** All nine Justices join Part I, including its confined contract application.

**Controlling explanation.** J. E. M. Ag Supply, Inc. v. Pioneer Hi-Bred International, Inc. permits utility-patent and plant-variety protection to coexist while keeping each statute’s exceptions within its own right. Asgrow Seed Co. v. Winterboer addresses the certificate-based saved-seed provision and supplies no general immunity from an independently applicable utility patent.

The relevant patent claims are assumed in this appeal to cover the additional generations. McFarling accepted one-season planting permission and conceded saving and replanting the harvest. Ownership of the purchased seed does not itself confer permission to make and use additional patented seed. Its reproductive character and his willingness to pay a technology fee do not compel a different reproduction license. The promise therefore survives the presented categorical defenses, and the conceded conduct establishes its breach.

This holding concerns the additional seed and the presented no-replanting promise, rather than every product made with a patented tool or every contract condition. Patent validity lies outside this appeal because the defense was not preserved in the reviewed partial judgment.

##### Part II: Patent scope does not conclusively defeat an independent tie

**Holding and operative rule.** A restriction within a patent grant does not, for that reason alone, defeat a Sherman Act §1 tying claim. The claimant must establish distinct products through actual separate consumer demand, conditioning, sufficient power in the tying market, a substantial amount of affected tied commerce, and the competitive injury and causal requirements for the relief sought; lawful patent exclusion retains substantial justificatory force.

**Authority.** All nine Justices join this general rule in Part II. Its application requiring further proceedings in this case is the separate six-Justice holding in Part III.

**Controlling explanation.** CSU, L.L.C. v. Xerox Corporation holds that patent scope does not conclusively immunize independently unlawful monopoly maintenance. The same coexistence of statutory rights and independent antitrust duties supports rejecting conclusive scope immunity for a properly established §1 tie. This extends CSU’s nonimmunity principle, while the offense remains governed by §1’s own elements.

Eastman Kodak Co. v. Image Technical Services, Inc. and Jefferson Parish Hospital District No. 2 v. Hyde supply the demand-based product inquiry and the ordinary tying requirements. Patent ownership does not prove market power, and a patent, trait or single-product label does not conclusively merge products for which separate demand exists. A preferred licensing arrangement or its royalty expense alone does not establish an independent antitrust wrong. Verizon Communications Inc. v. Law Offices of Curtis V. Trinko, LLP likewise requires the particular offense and causal basis for the requested remedy.

##### Part III: The preserved tying claim requires its own adjudication

**Holding and operative rule.** The rejection of McFarling’s tying counterclaim must be vacated because the appellate court relied on conclusive patent-scope immunity and expressly declined to resolve the separate-product inquiry. On remand, the preserved record must be tested under the independent tying elements and ordinary summary-judgment requirements; actual failure of an element may support renewed judgment.

**Authority.** Chief Justice Stone-Zsela and Justices Stevens, Kennedy, Souter, Ginsburg and Breyer join Part III and its remedy.

**Controlling explanation.** Monsanto and the partial dissent characterize the requested arrangement as a license to reproduce patented second-generation seed while paying a technology fee, rather than a coerced purchase of something independently demanded. That is a substantial merits defense. But the appellate court acknowledged the separate-demand inquiry and expressly left it unresolved, using patent scope instead. This Court cannot substitute a conclusive finding about product identity for that declined inquiry.

Kodak requires actual demand and market conditions, with the remaining elements proved in their own terms. CSU prevents the scope label from replacing that analysis; it does not transplant §2’s offense into §1. The proper course is therefore an element-based adjudication of the preserved claim. Any resulting effect on contract enforcement depends on a proved violation or defense and its own governing requirements.

##### Part IV: The actual-damages mandate remains operative

**Holding and operative rule.** The vacatur of the $780,000 liquidated sum and the existing remand for actual damages are preserved. Actual damages remain subject to surviving lawful liability, any independently established defense or offset, the proper saved-and-replanted measure and the prohibition on double recovery.

**Authority.** All nine Justices join Part IV’s preservation of the damages disposition.

**Controlling explanation.** The lower judgment applied Missouri’s requirement of a reasonable relation to compensable harm and difficulty estimating the loss. It preserved reasonableness measured against either anticipated harm or actual harm; Monsanto did not defend the grossly disproportionate award through the actual-loss alternative. The uniform 120 multiplier, based on purchased rather than saved and replanted bags, lacked the required relation to this breach’s compensable harm. The existing state-law damages disposition therefore remains in place. The question of an appropriate forecast for supplying or selling saved seed to third parties lies outside this replanting dispute and remains as the lower judgment left it.

#### Precedent Treatment

- J. E. M. Ag Supply, Inc. v. Pioneer Hi-Bred International, Inc. is applied to preserve concurrent protection and the separation of statutory exceptions.
- Asgrow Seed Co. v. Winterboer remains confined to the plant-variety right it construed.
- CSU, L.L.C. v. Xerox Corporation’s rejection of conclusive patent-scope immunity is extended from its §2 setting to the presented §1 tying defense; §1 retains its own offense requirements.
- Eastman Kodak Co. v. Image Technical Services, Inc. supplies actual separate-demand and tying rules.
- Jefferson Parish Hospital District No. 2 v. Hyde supplies actual separate-demand and tying rules.
- Verizon Communications Inc. v. Law Offices of Curtis V. Trinko, LLP preserves the distinct requirements for an antitrust offense and its remedy.

#### Law After Decision

The particular patent-covered reproduction promise survives the categorical saved-seed and scope-based misuse attacks. Patent-related conditions nevertheless remain subject to independently established §1 tying liability, with statutory exclusion receiving substantial justificatory weight. Courts must assess the actual products, conditioning, markets, commerce and injury rather than treating patent scope as a conclusive defense. The general nonimmunity rule commands all nine votes; the conclusion that this record requires further tying adjudication commands six.

#### Separate Writings

Scalia, joined by O’Connor and Thomas, joins the patent/contract ruling, the general rule preserving liability for genuine independent tying and the damages mandate. These Justices would also affirm rejection of the antitrust counterclaim on a confined alternative ground.

In their view, the presented transaction is an ungranted right to use patented second-generation seed while paying a technology fee instead of purchasing newly licensed seed. Refusal to offer that reproduction arrangement does not identify a distinct compelled acquisition as the condition of an otherwise authorized use. Their Kodak, CSU and Trinko analysis requires an independent competitive wrong beyond dependence on the protected product or a preference for different license terms. Reconsidering a product label does not cure that defect in the conduct actually asserted. They accordingly reject further inquiry on this claim while preserving the ordinary prohibition of an independently unlawful tie.

#### Source Notes

Judgment below: 363 F.3d 1336 (Fed. Cir. 2004), Nos. 03-1177 and 03-1228. Sherman Act §1; 35 U.S.C. §§101, 154, 271. Review proceeds from a Rule 54(b) contract-and-counterclaim judgment. Later-generation patent coverage is the operative assumption for the preserved questions.

#### Mandate, Remedy, and Stage

The lower courts shall determine whether the preserved tying record independently warrants summary judgment under the stated elements and address any legally established enforcement consequence. Actual contract damages shall be assessed on the surviving lawful basis without reinstating the invalid multiplier. The unfinished patent causes, their damages and the request for injunctive relief remain in their respective proceedings under their own requirements; this reviewed partial contract judgment does not make those claims final.

**End of entry: Monsanto Co. v. McFarling, plenary merits decision, April 19, 2005.**
