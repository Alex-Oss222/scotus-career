## Decisions and Dispositions

### Back v. Hastings on Hudson Union Free School District

Merits decision, March 7, 2005

#### Chronology and Posture

On review of the Second Circuit's April 7, 2004 judgment, 365 F.3d 107, partially vacating summary judgment.

Elana Back, a school psychologist hired on a three-year tenure track, challenges the denial of tenure in 2001. Her evidence includes strong early evaluations and statements linking motherhood to predicted lack of professional commitment. Supervisors Ann Brennan and Marilyn Wishnie dispute that account and identify performance deficiencies. The questions are whether supported sex-stereotyping evidence can establish intentional discrimination without a similarly situated father and whether preexisting law gave these officials fair warning on Back's supported account.

#### Judgment

**Affirmed, 9-0.**

**Supporting:** Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer.

The partial vacatur of summary judgment for Brennan and Wishnie stands. The individual claims return for trial under the governing proof and immunity rules. The judgments for Superintendent Russell and the school district remain undisturbed.

#### Opinion Topology

GINSBURG, J., delivered the opinion of the Court, in which STONE-ZSELA, C. J., and STEVENS, O'CONNOR, SCALIA, KENNEDY, SOUTER, THOMAS and BREYER, JJ., joined in full.

#### Controlling Propositions and Authority

**Common authority:** Both holdings and their complete explanations are adopted by all nine Justices in the Opinion of the Court identified above.

##### 1. Intentional sex stereotyping can be proved without a male comparator

**Holding and operative rule:** A public employee may prove that officials intentionally denied an employment opportunity because of sex through their sex-role statements and related evidence, without identifying a similarly situated employee of the opposite sex. The employee retains persuasion by a preponderance on intentional discrimination and each defendant's causal responsibility; the indirect proof framework shifts only production of a legitimate explanation, and the whole record must support the claimed discriminatory cause.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that an employer cannot turn a general assumption about mothers into a judgment about an individual woman's professional commitment. United States v. Virginia requires attention to the individual rather than unsupported generalizations about women's capacities and roles. Price Waterhouse v. Hopkins identifies the discriminatory work that sex stereotypes can perform in an employment decision. Nevada Department of Human Resources v. Hibbs confirms the constitutional significance of government action grounded in assigned family roles.

Back's supported account connects the supervisors' maternal-role predictions to their tenure recommendation. The contrast between earlier favorable evaluations and later criticism supplies additional evidence for a factfinder. A father comparison could help assess the inference, but making it indispensable would defeat an otherwise supported claim merely because the workplace lacked a comparable man. The performance explanation remains a genuine competing account. St. Mary's Honor Center v. Hicks preserves the employee's ultimate burden, and Reeves v. Sanderson Plumbing Products permits an inference from the whole record without making disbelief of an explanation conclusive. Genuine work requirements applied on equal terms remain lawful. The disputed statements, performance and causal role of each supervisor are therefore matters for trial.

##### 2. The alleged intentional discrimination was clearly prohibited when the supervisors acted

**Holding and operative rule:** Officials are not entitled to summary judgment on qualified immunity when the supported plaintiff-favorable account shows that they intentionally denied an employment opportunity because a woman's maternal role made her unsuitable, and law existing when they acted clearly prohibited that sex-role discrimination. They may contest the alleged conduct and motive through ordinary factfinding, but an identical prior tenure case is unnecessary when the application of the existing prohibition is clear.

**Controlling explanation:** Harlow v. Fitzgerald and Anderson v. Creighton require notice of the constitutional prohibition in its concrete setting. Virginia had already rejected unsupported sex-role assumptions before the alleged statements and recommendation in 2000 and 2001. The asserted uncertainty concerns whether the supervisors acted for the alleged reason, not whether that reason was lawful. Framing the claim as a novel right belonging to caregivers obscures its actual premise: intentional discrimination because of sex.

Hope v. Pelzer explains why clear notice can exist without a factual replica, and Brosseau v. Haugen preserves that principle while requiring attention to the actual circumstances. Those later decisions, and Hibbs, describe the current rule; they do not supply notice missing at the time of conduct. The preexisting prohibition itself resolves the legal immunity issue on Back's supported account. Saucier v. Katz leaves the established defense intact without imposing an additional reasonableness defense or a mandatory merits-first sequence.

#### Precedent Treatment

- United States v. Virginia is applied to individual employment action grounded in unsupported maternal-role assumptions; its requirement of a genuine important justification substantially related to a sex classification retains force.
- Price Waterhouse v. Hopkins supplies the relevance of sex-stereotyping evidence without converting this constitutional action into a Title VII claim.
- Nevada Department of Human Resources v. Hibbs confirms the constitutional significance of state employment stereotypes; its later date does not establish earlier notice.
- St. Mary's Honor Center v. Hicks and Reeves v. Sanderson Plumbing Products preserve plaintiff persuasion, employer production and permissible whole-record inferences. Neither makes an actual opposite-sex comparator indispensable.
- Harlow v. Fitzgerald and Anderson v. Creighton continue to govern personal immunity. Hope v. Pelzer and Brosseau v. Haugen explain contextual fair warning and its obvious applications without replacing that defense.
- Saucier v. Katz retains its existing treatment of sequencing and the independent immunity inquiry.

#### Law After Decision

Intentional public-employment sex discrimination may be proved through decisionmakers' sex-role statements and other evidence even when the workplace supplies no similarly situated man. Courts must assess the whole record, preserve the employee's burden and examine each official's causal role. On the supported explicit maternal-stereotyping account presented here, conduct-date law defeats personal immunity at summary judgment.

#### Source Notes

Back v. Hastings on Hudson Union Free School District, 365 F.3d 107 (2d Cir. 2004); Fourteenth Amendment, Equal Protection Clause; 42 U.S.C. section 1983. The challenged statements and reasons are supported disputed evidence considered in Back's favor at summary judgment.

#### Mandate, Remedy, and Stage

The case returns through the Second Circuit to the district court for trial of Back's claims against Brennan and Wishnie. The factfinder will resolve the disputed statements, performance explanations, intent and causation; any established liability will receive the damages and employment-relief analysis the proved injury supports.

**End of entry: Back v. Hastings on Hudson Union Free School District, merits decision, March 7, 2005.**

### Ballard v. Commissioner, No. 03-184, and Estate of Kanter v. Commissioner, No. 03-1034

Merits decision, March 7, 2005

#### Chronology and Posture

Argued December 7, 2004. On writs of certiorari to the Eleventh and Seventh Circuits, whose judgments are reported at 321 F.3d 1037 and 337 F.3d 833. Ballard and Estate of Kanter are consolidated.

A special trial judge heard extensive evidence concerning alleged unreported income and tax fraud and submitted a report under Tax Court Rule 183. A regular Tax Court judge later entered a decision stating that the special trial judge's opinion was adopted. The original submitted report was withheld from the parties and appellate record. The question is whether that practice permits review of the credibility weight and presumption of correctness required by the rule. Allegations that the original report differed from the final decision remain disputed.

#### Judgment

| Judgment component | Disposition and vote | Supporting Justices | Opposing Justices | Remedy or remand |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Ballard, No. 03-184 | Reversed and remanded, 8-1 | Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Ginsburg, Breyer | Thomas | Return through the Eleventh Circuit for the prescribed report disclosure and review. |
| Estate of Kanter, No. 03-1034 | Reversed and remanded, 8-1 | Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Ginsburg, Breyer | Thomas | Return through the Seventh Circuit for the same process in the related tax judgment. |

The Tax Court must make the submitted report part of the reviewable record and available to the parties, subject to specific lawful confidentiality protections. It must identify material later changes, permit a meaningful response on an orderly schedule, and reconsider or explain the affected decision under Rule 183. The further work depends on what the completed record establishes: demonstrated departures require lawful correction; actual compliance may sustain the substantive determinations. An unexplained failure to supply the operative report cannot sustain an affected judgment.

#### Opinion Topology

STEVENS, J., delivered the opinion of the Court, in which STONE-ZSELA, C. J., and O'CONNOR, SCALIA, KENNEDY, SOUTER, GINSBURG and BREYER, JJ., joined in full. THOMAS, J., filed a dissenting opinion.

#### Controlling Propositions and Authority

##### The submitted Rule 183 report must remain identifiable and reviewable

**Holding and operative rule:** A special trial judge's submitted Rule 183(b) report must be preserved and available to the parties and reviewing court when the final decision is tested for the credibility weight and presumption of correctness that Rule 183(c) requires. The regular judge retains authority to adopt, modify or reject the report, but must make the treatment of its material findings reviewable; exclusion of the legally operative report establishes the need for disclosure without requiring taxpayers first to prove its undisclosed contents.

**Authority:** Stevens's Opinion of the Court, joined in full by Stone-Zsela, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Ginsburg and Breyer, supplies eight direct votes for the complete rule and its remedy in each docket.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that the report submitted after the hearing is the report on which the regular judge must act. Rule 183 gives that completed work legal significance: its factual findings are presumed correct, and the deciding judge must give due regard to the hearing judge's opportunity to evaluate witnesses. Those commands cannot be enforced if an undisclosed replacement erases the starting point and is then attributed entirely to the hearing judge. Freytag v. Commissioner supplies the distinction between the special trial judge's hearing function and the Tax Court's ultimate decisional authority. Preserving both functions requires an identifiable report and an explanation of its treatment.

The deletion of automatic service and party-exception language in 1983 does not answer the continuing weighted-review requirements. The rule's express recommittal procedure also supplies a formal means to return the submitted work for further consideration. Section 7460(b), governing full-court review of a regular Tax Court judge, expressly excludes a different initial decision and concerns a different institutional relationship. That provision cannot silently erase the special trial judge's retained credibility role. The principle recognized in Service v. Dulles requires adherence to the tribunal's governing procedures; interpretive leeway cannot defeat the process their text establishes.

The submitted report is distinct from ordinary preliminary drafts, informal discussions before submission and judicial deliberations generally. Specific confidentiality protection remains available while preserving meaningful party access and appellate review. The disclosure ground does not depend on finding that the original report favored the taxpayers, that material alteration occurred, or that a judge acted improperly. Those competing assumptions are precisely why the actual report must become available. Further reconsideration or explanation must address what that record shows, and a substantive change follows only where the proper application requires it. The rule-specific disposition makes adjudication of the alternative constitutional disclosure theory unnecessary.

#### Precedent Treatment

- Freytag v. Commissioner is applied to preserve the separate hearing/report and final-decision functions; final decisional authority does not eliminate the report's prescribed legal weight.
- Service v. Dulles supplies the requirement that a tribunal follow the procedure its governing rules establish. It is applied to the retained Rule 183 commands.
- The interpretive-deference principle illustrated by Bowles v. Seminole Rock & Sand Co. does not sustain a reading inconsistent with the report's identity and prescribed treatment. The Court construes this rule rather than displacing that general principle.

#### Law After Decision

Under the existing Rule 183, a submitted special trial judge report remains the identifiable basis for weighted review. Parties and appellate courts must be able to assess material departures from it, while the regular Tax Court judge retains final decisional authority and lawful means of recommittal, revision and confidentiality protection. Disclosure enables a determination of actual compliance and an effective remedy for any demonstrated defect.

#### Separate Positions Relevant to the Decision

**Thomas, dissenting.** Thomas would affirm both judgments. The 1983 amendment deliberately removed automatic party service and changed filing to submission. In his view, the Tax Court reasonably reads the amended rule to permit a special trial judge to reconsider a report before the regular judge adopts the final endorsed version. The rule still requires deference to that judge's findings, but it does not expressly require disclosure of every earlier internal version.

Bowles v. Seminole Rock & Sand Co. supports respect for a reasonable interpretation of the institution's own rule. Freytag preserves the special trial judge's work and the regular judge's final authority; neither role proves that an initial report became immutable on submission. Full adoption of the special trial judge's endorsed findings can satisfy the required weight. Thomas also regards ordinary evidentiary adjudication by the authorized judge as adequate against the confined due-process objection absent a demonstrated hearing defect. He rejects treating an unproved account of internal revision as proof that the prescribed review failed. The Court answers that access is required to test the retained legal weight, regardless of whether misconduct or a changed tax result can yet be established.

#### Source Notes

Tax Court Rule 183(b)-(c); 26 U.S.C. sections 7443A, 7460(b), 7482; Ballard v. Commissioner, 321 F.3d 1037 (11th Cir. 2003); Estate of Kanter v. Commissioner, 337 F.3d 833 (7th Cir. 2003). The original report's contents and the existence and extent of material changes require examination of the completed record.

#### Mandate, Remedy, and Stage

Both cases return through their respective Courts of Appeals to the Tax Court for the disclosure, response and review process specified above. Any ensuing appellate review must proceed on a record that permits enforcement of Rule 183's prescribed treatment of the submitted findings.

**End of entry: Ballard v. Commissioner and Estate of Kanter v. Commissioner, merits decision, March 7, 2005.**

### Shepard v. United States, No. 03-9168

Merits decision, March 7, 2005

#### Chronology and Posture

Argued November 8, 2004. On writ of certiorari to the First Circuit, 348 F.3d 308.

After Shepard pleaded guilty to federal firearm possession, the Government sought the Armed Career Criminal Act's fifteen-year minimum based on Massachusetts burglary pleas. The state statutes and complaints extended beyond burglary of buildings. The Government offered police reports and complaint applications describing buildings, although the pleas did not expressly adopt those accounts. The question is whether those materials can establish that the prior convictions necessarily admitted generic burglary and support the First Circuit's unconditional enhancement direction.

#### Judgment

| Judgment component | Disposition and vote | Supporting Justices | Opposing Justices | Remedy or remand |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Rule permitting unadopted police materials to establish a generic-burglary plea | Reversed, 6-3 | Stone-Zsela, Stevens, Scalia, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg | O'Connor, Kennedy, Breyer | Apply the conviction-record restriction in Part I. |
| Unconditional direction to impose the enhanced minimum | Set aside and remanded, 9-0 | Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg, Breyer | — | Identify lawful predicates, the operative federal charging and admission record, preservation and any independent authority for further enhancement proceedings. |

The district court must identify the actual documents and admissions supporting each proposed predicate and the legal basis for the authorized sentencing range. If enhancement prerequisites are absent, it must sentence within the otherwise authorized range under the retained binding Guidelines and applicable departure and sentencing provisions. The firearm conviction remains intact against this sentencing challenge.

#### Opinion Topology

THOMAS, J., delivered the opinion of the Court, in which STONE-ZSELA, C. J., and STEVENS, SCALIA, SOUTER and GINSBURG, JJ., joined, and in Part II of which O'CONNOR, KENNEDY and BREYER, JJ., joined. O'CONNOR, J., filed an opinion concurring in Part II and in the removal of the unconditional enhancement command, and dissenting from Part I, in which KENNEDY and BREYER, JJ., joined.

| Writing | Author | Joined by | Relationship to judgment | Scope joined |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Opinion of the Court, Part I | Thomas | Stone-Zsela, Stevens, Scalia, Souter, Ginsburg | Statutory reversal | Complete conviction-record restriction and application |
| Opinion of the Court, Part II | Thomas | Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Ginsburg, Breyer | Removal of unconditional enhancement command; lawful resentencing | Governing Gatewood and Booker law and bounded remand; the Part I dissenters retain their earlier constitutional objections |
| Concurrence in part and dissent in part | O'Connor | Kennedy, Breyer | Opposes Part I; joins Part II and the present-law remand | Confined reliable shared-understanding inquiry; continuing objections to the prior constitutional rule |

#### Controlling Propositions and Authority

##### I. Qualifying burglary pleas must be established through conclusive judicial plea records

**Holding and operative rule:** In determining whether a plea under a burglary statute broader than generic burglary necessarily admitted that generic offense, an ACCA court may examine the statutory elements, the charging document, written plea terms, the plea colloquy, or a comparable express judicial finding assented to by the defendant. Unadopted police reports and complaint applications cannot turn a broad plea into an admission of the qualifying elements; their reliability as accounts of conduct does not satisfy the conviction-centered inquiry.

**Authority:** Thomas's Part I, joined by Stone-Zsela, Stevens, Scalia, Souter and Ginsburg, supplies six direct votes for the rule and its application.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that what a conviction necessarily established is different from what a later judge finds probably happened. Taylor v. United States ties generic burglary to a conviction establishing the required elements and limits retrospective reconstruction of old conduct. Its reasoning applies equally to convictions entered on pleas. A written agreement or recorded assent to a specific factual basis can establish what the defendant admitted; the investigative reason for bringing a broader charge cannot do so by itself.

The Government's shared-understanding approach would allow a sentencing court to infer an admitted element from a persuasive account that the plea never adopted. Even internally consistent police documents can describe facts that the prosecution did not require the defendant to concede. Differences in state recordkeeping do not justify replacing Taylor's inquiry with another factual trial of the old burglary. Express incorporation of a building-specific charge through an appropriate judicial plea record is different from an unadopted narrative merely present in the file. Here the broader complaints leave the qualifying distinction unresolved, so the police materials cannot support the First Circuit's statutory direction.

##### II. The enhancement and resentencing must satisfy the existing charging and proof rules

**Holding and operative rule:** Any genuinely disputed adult-recidivist fact indispensable to a higher federal sentencing maximum or mandatory minimum must satisfy Gatewood's applicable charging and unanimous jury-proof requirements beyond a reasonable doubt, or rest on a valid admission and any necessary lawful waiver. Courts may legally classify elements already established by qualifying records, but a permissible document does not itself cure an indispensable omitted charge or authorize judicial resolution of a new disputed enhancement fact. A lawful jury waiver permits judicial determination under the same reasonable-doubt standard and charging boundary.

**Authority:** All nine Justices join Part II's application of the governing Gatewood and Booker rules and its remand. The statutory disagreement in Part I remains distinct from agreement that those existing rules control the sentencing court.

**Controlling explanation:** Gatewood governs existence, identity, historical qualification and chronology of adult predicates when those facts authorize increased punishment. Angle and Harris preserve the same protection for mandatory floors as for ceilings. The former constitutional prior-conviction exception therefore cannot justify the First Circuit's direction. Calling disputed conduct an interpretation of a prior plea does not change its punishment-authorizing function.

The federal indictment and the defense's actual charging objection make the operative record material. The district court must examine the governing instruments, the scope and validity of admissions and waivers, and preservation. If an indispensable enhancement allegation was omitted from the federal charge, the objection was preserved and no valid charging waiver supplies authority, sentencing notice and judicial findings cannot authorize the enhancement; sentencing must begin with the independently authorized range. For a genuinely forfeited claim, Rule 52(b) requires error, plainness, the defendant's ordinary showing of outcome-affecting prejudice, and a separate determination that the error seriously affects the fairness, integrity or public reputation of judicial proceedings before discretionary correction. Where settled trial law changes during direct review, plainness is assessed at review. A further enhancement proceeding requires independent lawful authority and its protections for charging, notice, proof, finality and double jeopardy. This remand does not create that authority.

Booker retains the binding Guidelines in their lawful applications. The district court must therefore determine the lawfully supported range and apply the retained sentencing and departure provisions. The earlier thirty-to-thirty-seven-month description is not an automatic resentencing instruction. The statutory evidence limit and constitutional allocation perform separate work, and both govern the proposed predicates.

#### Precedent Treatment

- Taylor v. United States is extended to guilty pleas under broader burglary statutes through judicial records establishing the elements necessarily admitted; its conviction-centered method retains force.
- United States v. Gatewood is applied directly to disputed adult predicates and the operative federal charging and admission record. Legal classification of established elements remains judicial.
- United States v. Angle and Harris v. United States preserve the equivalent treatment of increased maximums and mandatory minimums.
- Almendarez-Torres v. United States supplies no surviving constitutional recidivism exception after Gatewood.
- United States v. Booker governs resentencing through the retained binding Guidelines, lawful factfinding and existing sentencing provisions.

#### Law After Decision

ACCA qualification of a guilty plea under a broader burglary statute turns on conclusive judicial plea records establishing generic elements, with an express distinction between adopted facts and unadopted investigative accounts. The separate Gatewood requirements govern any indispensable disputed enhancement fact, and Booker supplies the ordinary binding sentencing framework on remand.

#### Separate Positions Relevant to the Decision

**O'Connor, concurring in part and dissenting in part, joined by Kennedy and Breyer.** The partial dissent would permit a confined inquiry into the plea's actual shared factual basis using reliable contemporaneous records. In its view, Taylor's examples of trial documents do not establish an exclusive list for pleas. Matching complaints, applications and reports, together with the State's factual-basis requirements, may reliably identify the offense both sides understood was admitted. Equivalent guilty pleas should not have different federal consequences solely because one State records a colloquy differently. The inquiry would remain directed to the conviction rather than an unrestricted retrial of old conduct.

The three Justices nevertheless agree that the unconditional enhancement command must be removed under the governing Gatewood and Booker decisions. Their earlier constitutional objections remain, but those objections do not permit the lower court to impose punishment through a procedure current law forbids. They join Part II's present-law remand and oppose only the categorical statutory restriction in Part I. The Court answers their statutory position by distinguishing reliable proof of conduct from a record establishing what the plea necessarily admitted.

#### Source Notes

18 U.S.C. sections 922(g)(1), 924(e); Fifth and Sixth Amendments; 348 F.3d 308; joint appendix 15-18, 93-94, 100-104. The federal indictment, defense memorandum and affidavit have distinct evidentiary roles; allegations in the affidavit require assessment as part of the actual sentencing record.

#### Mandate, Remedy, and Stage

The case returns through the First Circuit for sentencing under Parts I and II. The district court must make the predicate-by-predicate documentary, charging, admission, preservation and sentencing determinations described above and enter a sentence within the resulting lawful authority.

**End of entry: Shepard v. United States, merits decision, March 7, 2005.**

### Wilkinson v. Dotson, No. 03-287

Merits decision, March 7, 2005

#### Chronology and Posture

Argued December 6, 2004. On writ of certiorari to the Sixth Circuit, sitting en banc, 329 F.3d 463.

Ohio prisoners William Dwight Dotson and William Johnson seek new lawful parole consideration. Dotson challenges application of later guidelines to his earlier sentence. Johnson also challenges the composition of the parole panel and his opportunity to speak. The requested proceedings would leave officials discretion to deny parole. The question is whether these prospective claims may proceed under 42 U.S.C. section 1983 or must be brought exclusively through habeas corpus, the statutory means of challenging unlawful custody.

#### Judgment

**Affirmed and remanded as to both plaintiffs, 9-0.**

**Supporting:** Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer.

Dotson's and Johnson's complaints return for adjudication of independent procedural requirements and their actual constitutional claims. Their existing sentences remain in force.

#### Opinion Topology

O'CONNOR, J., delivered the opinion of the Court, in which STONE-ZSELA, C. J., and STEVENS, SCALIA, KENNEDY, SOUTER, THOMAS, GINSBURG and BREYER, JJ., joined in full.

#### Controlling Propositions and Authority

##### Compatible prospective procedure claims may proceed under section 1983

**Holding and operative rule:** A prisoner may pursue prospective relief under section 1983 when success would require only a new lawful proceeding and would neither invalidate the legal basis of custody nor necessarily require release or a shorter custodial term. The court must examine the challenged procedure, the relief requested and their actual statutory consequences; when lawful official discretion permits the same continued confinement, the possibility of a favorable later decision does not require prior invalidation before the civil claim can be heard.

**Authority:** O'Connor's Opinion of the Court is joined in full by the other eight Justices. The rule governs both complaints and equivalent prospective procedural claims having the same legal effect.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that a chance to obtain earlier release is different from a judgment establishing an entitlement to it. Cavanaugh v. Roller already permits restoration of a legally required occasion for discretionary parole consideration while a valid sentence continues to authorize custody. Heck v. Humphrey protects the continuing legal validity of a conviction or sentence, and Edwards v. Balisok requires attention to the actual consequences of the challenged disciplinary decision and relief. Muhammad v. Close rejects a shortcut based merely on the prison setting. Nelson v. Campbell likewise preserves procedural relief compatible with valid punishment while retaining independent conditions on the remedy.

Both requested parole proceedings leave the responsible officials free to deny release lawfully. Ohio's position substitutes the prisoners' hoped-for outcome for the legal effect of the requested judgments. Its analogy to resentencing overlooks the essential difference: a challenge to an invalid sentence attacks the legal authority for custody even though a later sentence could be equally long; these complaints leave a valid sentence authorizing confinement throughout. A general appeal to state-court exhaustion cannot change that classification.

Preiser v. Rodriguez continues to govern relief necessarily directed to custody. An order restoring credits that automatically shorten confinement, vacating a sentence or directing release has a different legal effect. A procedural label cannot avoid that consequence. Here each plaintiff still must prove a federal constitutional violation and satisfy independently applicable screening, administrative-exhaustion and remedial requirements. Whether Ohio's guideline changes violate the Constitution and whether the plaintiffs possess protected liberty interests lie outside the vehicle question and remain for adjudication in the restored actions.

#### Precedent Treatment

- Cavanaugh v. Roller is applied to prospective restoration of discretionary parole consideration; its opportunity-only rule remains controlling.
- Heck v. Humphrey is applied according to the necessary effect on the legal basis of custody, not the prisoner's hoped-for release prospects.
- Edwards v. Balisok preserves the distinction between relief invalidating a custody-producing credit determination and genuinely compatible prospective procedure.
- Muhammad v. Close rejects confinement alone as a sufficient reason to require prior invalidation.
- Nelson v. Campbell supports compatible procedural relief with independent procedural and remedial requirements.
- Preiser v. Rodriguez retains habeas exclusivity for claims necessarily directed to release, reduced custody or the invalidity of its legal basis.

#### Law After Decision

Courts classify prospective prisoner claims by the necessary legal consequence of success. Discretion-preserving parole reconsideration and equivalent compatible procedures may proceed under section 1983; actual custody-invalidating or custody-shortening relief remains governed by the appropriate habeas and invalidation rules. This classification settles the enforcement vehicle while leaving the plaintiff to establish the asserted federal right and violation.

#### Source Notes

42 U.S.C. section 1983; federal habeas statutes; Dotson v. Wilkinson, 329 F.3d 463 (6th Cir. 2003) (en banc). The complaints seek prospective procedure with discretionary parole decisions remaining available to the State.

#### Mandate, Remedy, and Stage

The district court must resolve the restored claims, applicable defenses and any supported prospective remedy on the developed record. If a violation warrants a new proceeding, the relief must preserve the lawful parole decision entrusted to the responsible officials and remain enforceable through ordinary injunction procedures.

**End of entry: Wilkinson v. Dotson, merits decision, March 7, 2005.**

### Black Political Task Force v. Galvin, lower-court No. 02-11190

Merits decision, March 22, 2005

#### Chronology and Posture

After full merits submission, on direct appeal under 28 U.S.C. §1253 from the three-judge District of Massachusetts's June 21, 2004 final judgment; 300 F. Supp. 2d 291. The questions concern section 2 liability for the challenged Boston-area district arrangement and the continuing effect of the injunction after enactment of Plan C.

#### Judgment

**Count I judgment and permanent relief affirmed, 5–4.**

**Supporting:** Stone-Zsela, Stevens, Souter, Ginsburg and Breyer.

**Opposing:** O'Connor and Kennedy would vacate the affected Count I liability and dependent declaration, injunction and fee basis, preserve supported subsidiary findings, and remand for a corrected totality assessment, with any interim restraint governed by ordinary equity. Scalia and Thomas would reverse and direct dismissal, removing the statutory liability and dependent federal injunction and fee basis.

The June 21, 2004 statutory declaration, permanent injunction covering Suffolk House districts 1–15 and 17–18, and reasonable attorneys' fees and costs as provided by law remain effective.

All nine agree that the continuing injunction presents a live appeal. Plan C remains enacted state law. Counts II and III remain dismissed, and the completed 2004 candidate-remedy issues remain moot. Fee calculation and ordinary implementation proceed under the affirmed judgment.

#### Opinion Topology

SOUTER, J., delivered the opinion of the Court. STONE-ZSELA, C. J., and STEVENS, O'CONNOR, SCALIA, KENNEDY, THOMAS, GINSBURG and BREYER, JJ., joined Part I. STONE-ZSELA, C. J., and STEVENS, O'CONNOR, KENNEDY, GINSBURG and BREYER, JJ., joined Part II. STONE-ZSELA, C. J., and STEVENS, GINSBURG and BREYER, JJ., joined Part III. O'CONNOR, J., filed an opinion concurring in part and dissenting in part, in which KENNEDY, J., joined. THOMAS, J., filed an opinion concurring in part and dissenting in part, in which SCALIA, J., joined.

#### Controlling Propositions and Authority

##### I. The continuing injunction leaves a live appeal

**Holding and authority — Part I, joined by all nine Justices.** Enactment of a replacement districting plan does not end an appeal seeking relief from a still-operative permanent injunction and its statutory liability predicate. The State remains subject to the June 21, 2004 restraint, which this Court can affirm or remove; the separate candidate-remedy issues for the completed 2004 election cycle remain moot.

**Controlling explanation.** The question is the practical effect of the judgment under review. Laidlaw distinguishes a change in conduct from disappearance of a controversy. Here the declaration and permanent prohibition continue to bind the election official even though the legislature has enacted Plan C. Review therefore can alter an existing legal obligation. Growe requires legal effect for a superseding state disposition, but does not require dismissal of an appeal from this continuing federal restraint. Treating the completed candidate requests separately respects the final judgment's actual scope.

##### II. Actual electoral opportunity and independent totality govern the statutory claim

**Holding and authority — Part II, joined by Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Kennedy, Souter, Ginsburg and Breyer.** A districting-based section 2 claimant must establish lawful compact district capacity, political cohesion and majority bloc voting ordinarily sufficient to defeat the group's preferred candidates, subject to supported special circumstances, and must independently prove unequal electoral opportunity by a preponderance under the totality. District capacity may be shown through a lawful compact majority district or Valdespino's rigorous single-group dependable-crossover route; neither another feasible district nor satisfaction of the preliminary conditions creates a presumption of liability.

**Controlling explanation.** Gingles supplies the causal conditions. De Grandy separates those conditions from the ultimate injury and rejects maximization. Valdespino permits a single cohesive group to prove dependable ability to elect with crossover support, while preserving lawful geography, population, causal proof and independent totality; speculative alliances, isolated success and mere influence do not suffice. Martinez requires actual local nomination and election evidence rather than racial labels or a projected vote threshold.

Proportionality is relevant evidence, neither a safe harbor nor an affirmative defense shifting the burden. Demonstrated crossover opportunity belongs in that assessment, and one district may afford opportunity to more than one group. Candidate race is not voter preference. Incumbency and unopposed contests may be relevant special circumstances, but success cannot be discarded merely because a candidate is an incumbent or is white. Opportunity elsewhere cannot offset a proved local impairment, and discriminatory purpose is not an additional element of this results claim.

The supported compact alternative here satisfies the majority-district route under the material demographic measures. The trial findings establish cohesion and the required bloc voting. Its election-specific treatment of exogenous contests, incumbency and cross-racial preferences supports those findings. The lower court nevertheless erred by excluding crossover opportunity from its proportionality numerator and suggesting that these preliminary conditions presumptively establish the ultimate violation.

##### III. The supported local packing findings establish unequal opportunity independently of those errors

**Holding and authority — Part III, joined by Stone-Zsela, Stevens, Souter, Ginsburg and Breyer.** The Count I judgment stands because the supported precinct-transfer, population, voting and feasible-alternative findings independently establish that the challenged configuration concentrated Black voters so as to deny equal electoral opportunity. Protecting incumbents does not excuse that proved injury; racial concentration, racial purpose or the possibility of another majority district alone would not establish it.

**Controlling explanation.** De Grandy forbids using aggregate opportunity to excuse actual local packing. Voinovich requires proof of unequal opportunity rather than condemnation of a heavily minority district by its composition. Martinez likewise asks whether the actual arrangement and a supported lawful alternative establish the local impairment.

The legislature removed six predominantly Black precincts from the 11th and 12th districts, which needed population, and placed them in the already heavily Black 6th, which did not need them. The findings connect those transfers and the Fitzgerald Amendment to protection of white incumbents and impaired electoral opportunity. Supported polarized-voting findings and the workable compact alternative establish the practical effect of that concentration. This is proof of an electoral injury, not merely an unfilled district target.

The reassessment opinion rightly insists that the lower court's numerical and presumptive errors be rejected. But the trial court separately examined actual crossover successes, including minority-preferred white and Hispanic candidates, and explained the relevant election circumstances. Those findings, together with the concrete precinct changes, establish the denial without excluding crossover opportunity from consideration or shifting the voters' burden. Affirmance thus rests on the independent lawful findings, not on treating erroneous arithmetic as harmless wording. Section 2's established districting coverage supplies the legal basis; the access-only construction urged by the other dissent is inconsistent with Gingles and the governing De Grandy, Valdespino and Martinez line.

##### IV. The existing seventeen-district remedy remains fitted to the violation

**Holding and authority — Part III, joined by the same five Justices.** The Count I declaration, permanent prohibition on elections under the 2001 configuration in Suffolk House districts 1–15 and 17–18, and reasonable fees and costs as provided by law are affirmed. Plan C, approved by the district court and enacted unchanged as chapter 74 of the Acts of 2004, remains operative; modification of the continuing injunction requires an ordinary equitable showing that the proposed change fully cures the proved arrangement.

**Controlling explanation.** Growe preserves the State's first opportunity to choose a timely lawful correction. The district court afforded that opportunity, and the legislature exercised it. The violation operates through interrelated boundaries binding all affected voters; relief confined to the named plaintiffs would not cure it. The seventeen-district scope corresponds to the arrangement adjudicated below, and the State has established no narrower cure. The enacted replacement supplies the current implementation rather than a new judicial map. A party seeking a narrower injunction must establish that it remains sufficient to remedy the proved violation. The affirmed fee entitlement retains its statutory qualifications, with calculation left to the proper fee proceeding. Counts II and III remain dismissed. The district court retains ordinary implementation and modification authority consistent with the judgment.

#### Precedent Treatment

- **Thornburg v. Gingles, 478 U.S. 30:** applies the causal voting conditions and contextual totality inquiry; preliminary conditions remain necessary rather than presumptively sufficient, subject to Valdespino's existing modification of the capacity condition.
- **Johnson v. De Grandy, 512 U.S. 997, June 30, 1994:** applies relevant but nonconclusive proportional opportunity, the claimant's independent burden and the prohibition on maximization or geographic offsets. The concrete independent packing findings distinguish this record from a demand for every feasible additional district.
- **Valdespino v. Alamo Heights Independent School District, June 17, 1999:** preserves its rigorous single-group crossover-capacity route and ordinary demographic and disclosure rules. This case meets the retained majority-district route and does not extend the functional alternative.
- **Martinez v. Bush, April 29, 2004:** applies actual local electoral performance, distinct proof quantities, preponderance and the requirement of a supported improving alternative; preserves the separation between racial statutory claims and partisan constitutional claims.
- **Voinovich v. Quilter, 507 U.S. 146, March 2, 1993:** applies the difference between a State's lawful districting choice and a court's remedy for established unequal opportunity; a district's racial composition alone is insufficient.
- **Growe v. Emison, 507 U.S. 25, February 23, 1993:** applies local causal proof and legislative remedial priority. Its superseding-state-judgment rule does not eliminate review of the permanent federal restraint that remains here.
- **Friends of the Earth v. Laidlaw, January 12, 2000:** applies the distinction between changed conduct and disappearance of effective judicial relief; the injunction itself supplies the continuing controversy here.
- **Shaw v. Reno, 509 U.S. 630, June 28, 1993:** preserves the separate constitutional injury inquiry; it adds no constitutional-injury or discriminatory-purpose element to the section 2 results claim.

#### Law After Decision

Section 2 requires independent proof of practical unequal opportunity, with dependable crossover opportunity included in the evidence and the claimant retaining the ultimate burden. Supported local packing can establish the violation despite electoral successes elsewhere, but neither racial population figures nor another feasible majority district creates an entitlement. Here the independently sufficient precinct and electoral findings sustain the final injunction notwithstanding rejection of the lower court's erroneous numerical comparison and presumption. The existing single-group capacity routes, separate constitutional limits and ordinary evidence rules retain their force.

#### Separate Positions Relevant to the Decision

**O'Connor, joined by Kennedy, concurring in Parts I and II and dissenting from Part III and the judgment.** They accept the live appeal, governing section 2 coverage, supported causal findings and the Court's corrections to the legal test. De Grandy makes actual opportunity relevant without establishing either a quota or a safe harbor; Martinez requires applying that rule to the local proof. In their view, expressly excluding crossover opportunity from a principal proportionality comparison and treating the preliminary conditions presumptively could have affected the trial court's ultimate totality assessment. The concrete packing evidence could sustain liability, but the district court must weigh it under the corrected standard. They would vacate Count I liability and its dependent declaration, injunction and fee basis and remand for reassessment, preserving supported subsidiary findings. Plan C remains enacted, and any interim federal restraint must rest on ordinary equitable grounds. Neither apparent aggregate proportionality nor an additional feasible majority district dictates the outcome.

**Thomas, joined by Scalia, concurring in Part I and dissenting from the statutory holdings and judgment.** They would reverse and direct dismissal of Count I, with dissolution of its dependent federal injunction and fee basis. Their continuing construction, stated in their De Grandy, Valdespino and Martinez positions, confines section 2 to equal voting access, casting and counting rather than judicial allocation of electoral strength through district boundaries. They retain a results rule for practices within that coverage; the objection is not a demand for discriminatory intent. The statutory text, in their view, supplies no benchmark for the districting allocation required by the Court's dilution framework. If that framework is applied, the lower court's majority-only comparison and presumption furnish a further reason to reject its analysis, but that conditional objection is not a join in the reassessment mandate or the Court's findings of unequal opportunity. Reversal of the federal statutory judgment would leave enacted Plan C and dismissal of Counts II and III in place.

The [June 21, 2004 final judgment](https://clearinghouse-umich-production.s3.amazonaws.com/media/doc/8380.pdf) identifies Suffolk House districts 1–15 and 17–18, Count I relief, approval and unchanged enactment of Plan C, dismissal of Counts II and III and mootness of the remaining 2004 candidate matters. The separate Meza claims and any combined distinct-minority-group entitlement are outside this reviewed statutory determination. The demographic evidence supports the majority-district route under the material measures here; it establishes no universal population denominator or racial classification method.

#### Mandate, Remedy, and Stage

The affirmed Count I declaration and permanent injunction remain effective. Election officials continue under enacted Plan C, subject to ordinary implementation and lawful modification of the existing judgment. The statutory fee proceeding determines reasonable fees and costs. Counts II and III remain dismissed, and the conceded mootness of the remaining 2004 candidate-remedy issues stands.

### Brown v. Payton, No. 03-1039

Merits decision, March 22, 2005

#### Chronology and Posture

Argued November 10, 2004; 544 U.S. 133. On review of the Ninth Circuit's en banc judgment, 346 F.3d 1204, granting penalty-phase habeas relief after applying AEDPA. The question is whether the California Supreme Court unreasonably applied clearly established mitigation law in its November 23, 1992 rejection of the instructional claim and what relief follows if the statutory condition is satisfied.

#### Judgment

**Penalty-phase habeas relief on the reviewed mitigation-instruction claim reversed, 5–4.**

**Supporting:** O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Thomas and Breyer.

**Opposing:** Stevens, Souter and Ginsburg would affirm sentence-only relief, finding the statutory gateway and constitutional error established and the State's separate beyond-reasonable-doubt actual-effect burden unmet. Stone-Zsela would affirm the gateway and error determinations, vacate the final remedial component, and remand for full-record harmlessness adjudication under the State's burden before conditional sentence relief.

The case returns for proceedings consistent with section 2254(d)(1)'s bar to relief on this claim. The Court does not reach constitutional error or harmlessness. All nine apply the governing review law in Part I. The convictions remain intact. The mandate removes relief on this claim and leaves unrelated habeas claims to their lawful disposition.

#### Opinion Topology

J., filed an opinion concurring in part and dissenting in part.

#### Controlling Propositions and Authority

##### I. The writ application and state merits date determine the applicable review

**Holding and authority — Part I, joined by all nine Justices.** The ordinary AEDPA provisions governing adjudication apply to Payton's post-April 24, 1996 application for the writ, although counsel and interim-protection proceedings began earlier. Section 2254(d)(1) tests the state court's November 23, 1992 merits decision against this Court's holdings established by that date; relief requires a contrary governing rule or an objectively unreasonable application, not merely an independently incorrect result.

**Controlling explanation.** Garceau distinguishes an application requesting adjudication from proceedings preparing for it, with substance rather than caption controlling. Payton's earlier proceedings sought counsel and interim protection; the writ application followed enactment. Williams gives independent work to the statute's contrary-to and unreasonable-application clauses and permits application of clearly established general rules without a factual twin. Lockyer preserves the state-decision-date substantive cutoff and separates statutory eligibility from constitutional entitlement. Early and Visciotti require examination of the state's actual reasoning and complete context, rather than a citation checklist, a count of judges or a factual clear-and-convincing burden imported into the legal question. Later decisions explaining federal review do not turn their later substantive holdings into law established in 1992.

##### II. The state court's contextual conclusion is not objectively unreasonable

**Holding and authority — Part II, joined by O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Thomas and Breyer.** On this complete instructional and advocacy context, the California Supreme Court could reasonably find no reasonable likelihood that jurors understood themselves forbidden to give Payton's postcrime mitigation lawful effect. Its rejection of the claim is neither contrary to nor an objectively unreasonable application of the governing 1992 law, so section 2254(d)(1) bars the habeas relief awarded on this claim.

**Controlling explanation.** Boyde requires the charge and trial to be considered together. Lockett and Eddings distinguish legal exclusion from a sentencer's choice of weight, and Skipper establishes the relevance of postcrime adjustment. Applying those governing protections, the Court acknowledges the substantial objection here: the prosecutor repeatedly argued that the postcrime evidence was legally unavailable; the judge's response did not expressly correct that proposition. The judge's private broad understanding of factor (k) never reached the jury. The direction to consider all evidence was expressly subject to later instructions, which listed the statutory factors, including the crime-centered factor (k), and directed weighing of applicable factors. It was not an unqualified corrective instruction.

The entire presentation nevertheless permits the state court's conclusion. Eight witnesses supplied the substantial conversion and prison-conduct case; the defense forcefully explained why it could support life; the charge identified the court as the source of law; and the prosecutor also contested sincerity and argued that the conversion was outweighed. Admission, defense advocacy or an alternative weight argument is not independently a cure. Together with the full charge, however, these circumstances permit a reasonable adjudicator to conclude that the evidence remained available for the sentencing choice.

The dissent reasonably stresses the risk that the qualified charge reinforced legal exclusion. The statutory question is whether that reading is compelled, not whether it is the reviewing court's preferred constitutional answer. The state decision remains within the objective range allowed by Boyde. The Court therefore resolves the statutory issue without deciding the independent constitutional-error or harmlessness questions; the statutory bar is sufficient to dispose of the claim.

#### Precedent Treatment

- **Woodford v. Garceau, March 25, 2003:** applies the application-versus-preparation distinction to Payton's post-enactment writ application; earlier counsel and protective proceedings retain their own character.
- **Williams v. Taylor (Terry Williams), April 18, 2000:** applies the independent statutory clauses, the Supreme Court-holdings cutoff and objective legal review. Its rule permits a general principle to be clearly established without a factual replica and adds no clear-and-convincing burden to legal unreasonableness.
- **Lockyer v. Andrade, March 5, 2003:** applies the distinction between objective statutory unreasonableness and an independently erroneous constitutional result, without moving the substantive-law date.
- **Early v. Packer and Woodford v. Visciotti, November 4, 2002:** apply substantive assessment of actual state reasoning and the complete supported context; neither citation nor recitation of a correct standard alone resolves the statutory inquiry.
- **Lockett v. Ohio, 438 U.S. 586; Eddings v. Oklahoma, 455 U.S. 104; and Skipper v. South Carolina, 476 U.S. 1:** supply the governing distinction between legal exclusion and permissible evaluation of mitigating weight, including relevant postcrime adjustment. The statutory decision does not adopt the prosecutor's contrary legal reading.
- **Boyde v. California, 494 U.S. 370:** applies its whole-context reasonable-likelihood inquiry. The explicit exclusion argument and qualified all-evidence direction distinguish this trial's circumstances, but do not make the state court's contextual conclusion objectively unreasonable.
- **Penry v. Johnson, June 4, 2001; Beard v. Banks, June 24, 2004; and Bell v. Cone, January 24, 2005:** retain their respective current scope; their later substantive applications do not establish new law available at the November 1992 adjudication. Bell's distinction between actual state legal work and a missing citation is consistent with the review performed here.

#### Law After Decision

Section 2254(d)(1) requires objective review of the state court's actual application of law established at the state merits date. A possible independent constitutional error does not itself satisfy that restriction. The specific combination of the qualified charge, repeated exclusion argument, extensive mitigation presentation and competing advocacy here permits the state court's conclusion within that statutory range. The holding establishes neither a general cure by admission or defense argument nor categorical approval of factor (k). The independent mitigation and actual-effect rules retain their existing force for claims that lawfully reach those questions.

#### Separate Positions Relevant to the Decision

**Souter, joined by Stevens and Ginsburg, concurring in Part I and dissenting from Part II and the judgment; Stone-Zsela joins Part I of this separate opinion.** Part I finds both constitutional error and an objectively unreasonable state application. Skipper made relevant postcrime adjustment legally available, while Eddings distinguished exclusion from weight. The prosecutor repeatedly told this jury that the defense's principal mitigating case could not count. The court declined to correct that legal statement, and the instruction to consider all evidence was qualified by later directions that included factor (k)'s crime-centered wording. Defense advocacy was not authoritative legal authorization. The alternative attack on sincerity did not withdraw the exclusion claim. These features distinguish Boyde and, in the dissent's view, exceed the range of reasonable application rather than merely warrant a different independent answer.

Parts II and III, joined only by Stevens and Ginsburg, separately determine actual effect and would affirm sentence-only habeas relief. Under Brecht, O'Neal, Coleman and Penry II, the State must prove beyond a reasonable doubt that the preserved error did not contribute to the actual death sentence. The dissent considers the murder, sexual violence, other attacks, earlier violence and incriminating statements alongside the eight witnesses' conversion and conduct evidence and both sides' arguments. That aggravation could justify death in a lawful proceeding, but it does not establish what this jury did with its principal reason for choosing life after being told that reason was legally unavailable. Neither the admission of evidence nor the alternative weight argument establishes that the jurors instead weighed it. The State therefore fails its burden on the existing record. This is a separate actual-effect determination, not a structural-error rule or automatic relief from every Boyde violation. The dissent would preserve conditional sentence relief allowing a reasonable court-fixed opportunity for a constitutionally adequate penalty proceeding or a lawful noncapital sentence.

**Stone-Zsela, concurring in Part I and dissenting from Part II and the judgment.** He joins Souter's Part I because the uncorrected legal exclusion of the only affirmative mitigation makes the state application objectively unreasonable and creates a reasonable likelihood that jurors treated the evidence as legally unavailable. The qualified general direction to consider evidence and the alternative prosecution argument about weight do not resolve that legal-use problem. He withholds Souter's completed harmlessness determination and proposed affirmance of final relief. He would affirm the statutory-gateway and constitutional-error determinations, vacate the final remedial component, and require a reasoned whole-record harmlessness adjudication with the State bearing the beyond-reasonable-doubt burden. The reviewing court must obtain and examine the full existing sentencing record and permit focused submissions; strong aggravation does not shift the burden or replace inquiry into the actual sentence. If the State fails, a conditional sentence writ must set and enforce a reasonable court-fixed period for a constitutionally adequate penalty proceeding or replacement of death with a lawful noncapital sentence. The convictions and unrelated habeas claims remain outside that relief.

Woodford, 346 F.3d 1204 (9th Cir. 2003) (en banc), supplies the judgment under review. #### Mandate, Remedy, and Stage

The case returns for implementation of the reversal of penalty habeas relief on this claim. The convictions remain intact, and unrelated habeas claims proceed according to their own lawful posture.

### Muehler v. Mena, No. 03-1423

Merits decision, March 22, 2005

#### Chronology and Posture

Argued December 8, 2004. On review of the Ninth Circuit, 332 F.3d 1255. The case concerns handcuff detention during execution of a weapons warrant and immigration questions asked during that detention. The officers challenge a damages judgment supported by findings of excessive force and duration; Mena relies on the verdict and its independent surviving grounds.

#### Judgment

| Judgment component | Disposition and vote | Supporting Justices | Opposing Justices | Remedy or remand |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Detention and questioning premises; post-search custody and verdict review | Vacated and remanded, 9–0 | Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg, Breyer | — | Correct the detention and questioning premises; separately review post-search custody and the verdict's surviving grounds. |
| Continued handcuffing during the search | Further review under the proper force standard, 5–4 | Stone-Zsela, Stevens, Souter, Ginsburg, Breyer | O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Thomas | The dissenters would resolve the identified search-period force ground for the officers. |

The judgment concerns Mena's damages action against the two petitioning officers. The existing $60,000 award requires the verdict-sensitive review stated below. The defense verdicts on the rejected search and other-officer entry and property claims remain undisturbed.

#### Opinion Topology

GINSBURG, J., delivered the opinion of the Court. STONE-ZSELA, C. J., and STEVENS, O'CONNOR, SCALIA, KENNEDY, SOUTER, THOMAS and BREYER, JJ., joined Parts I and III. STONE-ZSELA, C. J., and STEVENS, SOUTER and BREYER, JJ., joined Part II. KENNEDY, J., filed an opinion concurring in part and dissenting in part, in which O'CONNOR, SCALIA and THOMAS, JJ., joined.

| Writing | Author | Joined by | Relationship to judgment | Scope joined |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Opinion of the Court, Parts I and III | Ginsburg | Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Breyer | Vacatur and remand | Initial detention, questioning, post-search custody, question scope and remedy |
| Opinion of the Court, Part II | Ginsburg | Stone-Zsela, Stevens, Souter, Breyer | Further review of continued handcuffing | Continued-force review under changing conditions |
| Concurrence in part and dissent in part | Kennedy | O'Connor, Scalia, Thomas | Accepts overall vacatur and remand; would resolve the identified search-period force ground for the officers | Joins Parts I and III; finds the identified search-period cuffs reasonable |

#### Controlling Propositions and Authority

**1. A proper premises search permits detention of occupants and reasonable initial safety measures.** Part I controls with all nine Justices. Officers executing this weapons warrant could detain the occupants and initially use handcuffs to secure the premises against the anticipated armed-gang danger. Individual suspicion that each occupant committed an offense is not a prerequisite to that search-related detention.

Summers supplies authority to control occupants while a lawful search is executed, protecting officer safety and orderly completion. Graham separately measures the force used by objective reasonableness. The anticipated weapons risk justified securing four occupants at the outset. Mena's status as an occupant who was not suspected of crime therefore does not itself invalidate the initial control. That detention authority and the reasonableness of its manner remain distinct inquiries.

**2. Continued handcuffing requires continuing objective justification under the actual conditions.** Part II controls with Stone-Zsela, Stevens, Souter, Ginsburg and Breyer. Courts must assess the risks, degree of control, duration, restraint-related harm and available supervision as conditions develop, preserving the historical facts supported by the verdict. The lawful start of restraint cannot conclusively establish its reasonableness throughout the search; the Ninth Circuit must review the continued-force ground on that basis.

Graham supplies objective review of the force actually applied. Holland explains why a reasonable initial measure can become excessive as its justification changes. The weapons search, four adults and limited guards carry substantial weight, as the partial dissent emphasizes. They must be considered together with the supported account of compliance, control, discomfort, requests for release and the course of the search. A general force verdict does not establish every subsidiary fact, but the reviewing court must preserve the historical facts the evidence supports in its favor. Neither an appellate preference for a different tactic nor a categorical approval of all search-period cuffs supplies that review. The Constitution requires reasonable conduct, not the least intrusive feasible arrangement or a fixed handcuff time limit.

**3. The subject of an ordinary question does not alone create an additional seizure.** Part I controls with all nine Justices. Immigration questions during an otherwise lawful detention require no separate reasonable suspicion solely because they concern immigration, provided they add neither detention nor a distinct coercive intrusion. Actual added restraint must have its own adequate justification.

Bostick distinguishes a request for information from a seizure. When a person is already lawfully detained, the relevant inquiry concerns what the questioning adds to that restraint. Caballes preserves the need for independently lawful custody and attention to the protected interest actually invaded; it supplies no general permission for every investigative technique that takes no extra time. The Ninth Circuit's topic-based ground therefore fails. Whether these questions actually extended custody remains part of the factual duration inquiry.

**4. Detention must remain tied to an adequate lawful basis, and the verdict must be reviewed by its surviving grounds.** Part III controls with all nine Justices. The lower court must separately decide the preserved claim of post-search detention, identifying the actual interval, reasonable completion tasks and any independent lawful basis. It must then determine what the legally valid force and duration grounds, supported findings and instructions establish about liability and damages.

Summers links its detention authority to execution of the search; a valid initial detention cannot indefinitely sustain later custody. The separate duration ground was not resolved below. Because compensatory damages were not allocated between force and duration, correction of one theory does not by itself settle the whole award. The court must decide whether supported findings sustain an amount, require reduction or require a limited new trial; punitive damages retain their separate predicates and individual causation remains necessary.

The present questions concern constitutional detention, force and questioning. The officers' requested independent immunity ruling is outside that scope in this case: factual overlap and alternative briefing do not make the distinct conduct-date notice defense necessary to decide those questions. Any defense still properly available below remains subject to preservation, finality and the mandate. Its notice inquiry concerns the particular conduct on February 3, 1998; later decisions cannot supply earlier fair warning.

#### Precedent Treatment

- **Michigan v. Summers:** Applied to occupant detention and initial control during this lawful search; its execution-related authority must remain tied to the detention's actual basis.
- **Graham v. Connor:** Applied to the objective reasonableness of the force used, separately from the authority to detain.
- **Holland ex rel. Overdorff v. Harrington:** Applied to continuing force as circumstances change; its current merits rule does not itself establish notice before its decision.
- **Florida v. Bostick:** Applied to distinguish ordinary questions from an added seizure, with attention to the restraint added when custody already exists.
- **Illinois v. Caballes:** Applied at its lawful-custody and protected-interest limits; absence of extra time alone does not authorize every distinct intrusion.
- **Brosseau v. Haugen** and **Back v. Hastings-on-Hudson:** Preserve the separate, particular conduct-date notice inquiry for any defense properly remaining below.

#### Law After Decision

Search-related detention, the force used to maintain it, and any additional investigative restraint remain separate Fourth Amendment inquiries. Occupant status permits reasonable initial control; continued force depends on the actual changing conditions. An unrelated conversational subject alone supplies no additional seizure, while an extension of custody requires a lawful basis. These rules govern the remand; evaluation of the supported verdict facts and the award is ordinary application and remedial work.

#### Separate Positions Relevant to the Decision

Justice Kennedy, joined by Justices O'Connor, Scalia and Thomas, concurs in Parts I and III and dissents from Part II's continued-force disposition. They would hold the identified two-to-three-hour search-period restraint reasonable given the weapons search, four adults and one or two guards, even accepting the supported unfavorable factual account. Summers permits practical control during execution, and Graham does not require the best possible alternative staffing or restraint arrangement. Those circumstances, in their view, resolve this force ground for the officers.

Their conclusion carries concrete limits. Real pain or serious discomfort from prolonged cuffs requires officers to alter the conditions sufficiently to attend to the detainee's needs. Cuffs must be removed when an objectively reasonable officer would readily recognize that removal would neither compromise safety nor interfere with the search or cause substantial delay. Two to three hours calls for serious attention to continuing justification; it is not a categorical safe period. They join review of the separate post-search custody and damages issues, and the Court's present question-scope limit.

#### Source Notes

U.S. Const. amend. IV; 42 U.S.C. §1983; Michigan v. Summers, 452 U.S. 692; Graham v. Connor, 490 U.S. 386; Florida v. Bostick, 501 U.S. 429; Ninth Circuit judgment, 332 F.3d 1255. The force and duration findings receive the review due a jury verdict; the alleged post-search interval remains for adjudication under the governing standard.

#### Mandate, Remedy, and Stage

The case returns to the Ninth Circuit to perform the force, duration and verdict review stated in the holdings and to order the further district-court proceedings that review requires. The mandate permits a limited new trial only where a material issue cannot be settled through the existing lawful verdict and appropriate appellate review.

### Rancho Palos Verdes v. Abrams, No. 03-1601

Merits decision, March 22, 2005

#### Chronology and Posture

Argued January 19, 2005. On review of the Ninth Circuit, 354 F.3d 1094. The question is whether 42 U.S.C. §1983 supplies an additional action enforcing the Telecommunications Act's wireless-siting restrictions alongside the Act's express expedited judicial remedy. The District Court granted statutory permit relief but rejected the additional action; the Court of Appeals allowed that action.

#### Judgment

**Reversed in the reviewed part and remanded, 9–0.**

**Supporting:** Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer.

Abrams's additional action enforcing the same siting restrictions under section 1983 is foreclosed. Damages and section 1988 fees resting solely on that action must be denied. The direct statutory permit judgment remains in force; remaining properly preserved relief must be considered under its actual legal source.

#### Opinion Topology

THOMAS, J., delivered the opinion of the Court, in which STONE-ZSELA, C. J., and STEVENS, O'CONNOR, SCALIA, KENNEDY, SOUTER, GINSBURG and BREYER, JJ., joined in full.

#### Controlling Propositions and Authority

**1. The specific thirty-day expedited siting action forecloses a parallel section 1983 action enforcing the same statutory restrictions.** The unanimous opinion controls. Assuming a qualifying individual right, the City bears and satisfies the burden of establishing incompatibility between the additional action and the personal judicial mechanism Congress supplied in 47 U.S.C. §332(c)(7)(B)(v). The holding governs enforcement of the restrictions in §332(c)(7)(B).

Blessing separates the existence of a right from Congress's displacement of the ordinary section 1983 route. Here Congress gave the person injured by final action or failure to act a direct action within thirty days and required expedited hearing and decision. An ordinary section 1983 action enforcing the same violation would not inherit those conditions and would make the specified timetable optional. Abrams's proposal to borrow the short period identifies the conflict but supplies no legal basis for creating a hybrid cause of action. This personal judicial arrangement differs from the funding administration that Bruneau found compatible with section 1983. Sea Clammers and Smith v. Robinson support exclusion when the actual statutory remedies conflict. The holding rests on this demonstrated incompatibility, not a general presumption that the existence of any express remedy defeats section 1983.

**2. The saving clause preserves independently existing law without supplying a second, inconsistent route for the new siting entitlement.** The unanimous opinion controls. Section 601(c)(1) preserves other law subject to the Act's express provisions, but it does not require the newly created siting rights to acquire an additional action bypassing their prescribed judicial procedure. Independently established rights remain enforceable subject to their own requirements.

Abrams reads the saving clause to preserve section 1983 and therefore his additional action. Section 1983 remains operative, but its application still includes the statutory-foreclosure inquiry. Giving the new right an incompatible second timetable would defeat the particular enforcement arrangement rather than preserve preexisting law. The separate authority to petition the FCC for the radio-frequency violation specified in clause (iv) retains its own subject; it does not replace the competent-court route applicable to the written-decision and substantial-evidence obligation in clause (iii).

**3. Relief must follow its surviving statutory basis.** The unanimous opinion controls. The failure of this section 1983 predicate defeats damages and section 1988 fees dependent exclusively upon it. The direct TCA permit relief survives, and the lower court must separate that judgment from the requests dependent on the foreclosed action.

The additional remedies Abrams seeks cannot cure the conflict in enforcement procedures. Conversely, denial of that route does not invalidate the direct statutory judgment already obtained. The absence of an express fee provision and the request for additional damages reinforce the statutory context but do not themselves establish universal remedial exclusivity. The complete direct-TCA damages question lies outside the enforcement-channel issue presented and remains open.

#### Precedent Treatment

- **Blessing v. Freestone:** Applied. Identification of an individual right and foreclosure of section 1983 remain distinct inquiries; the defendant must establish the incompatible scheme.
- **Middlesex County Sewerage Authority v. National Sea Clammers Association:** Applied to the demonstrated conflict between enforcement routes, rather than to remedial silence alone.
- **Smith v. Robinson:** Applied for the consequence of an incompatible statutory remedial structure.
- **Bruneau v. South Kortright Central School District:** Distinguished. General funding administration and an implied action there supplied no comparable incompatible personal filing and adjudication procedure.
- **Gonzaga University v. Doe:** Its controlling rejection of the particular FERPA claim remains intact; its broader nonmajority formulation supplies no universal rule here.
- **Alexander v. Sandoval** and **Powell v. Ridge:** Their prospective-enforcement holdings remain in force. The present decision concerns the conflicting mechanism of this specific statute.

#### Law After Decision

The direct action in §332(c)(7)(B)(v) is the judicial enforcement route for the siting restrictions at issue. Its short filing period and mandatory expedition cannot be bypassed through a parallel statutory section 1983 claim. In other statutory settings, courts continue to identify the particular individual right and then test actual remedial incompatibility; general funding oversight alone does not establish the conflict proved here. The existing permit remedy remains operative.

#### Source Notes

47 U.S.C. §332(c)(7)(B)(iii)–(v); Telecommunications Act of 1996, §601(c)(1); 42 U.S.C. §§1983, 1988; Ninth Circuit judgment, 354 F.3d 1094. The court-action and FCC-petition clauses address their respective specified violations.

#### Mandate, Remedy, and Stage

On remand, the court must deny the requests depending exclusively on the foreclosed section 1983 claim, preserve the direct permit judgment and adjudicate any remaining properly preserved relief under the law that supplies it.

**End of entry: Rancho Palos Verdes v. Abrams, merits decision, March 22, 2005.**

### City of Sherrill v. Oneida Indian Nation of New York, No. 03-855

Merits decision, March 29, 2005

#### Chronology and Posture

Argued January 11, 2005. On review of the Second Circuit, 337 F.3d 139. The Nation bought the identified Sherrill parcels in 1997 and 1998 within the reservation acknowledged in 1794. It seeks protection against current local property taxes and dependent enforcement. The City contests that protection in the related Lead, Eviction and Members cases.

#### Judgment

#### Controlling Propositions and Authority

##### I. Ordinary repurchase does not revive the dormant sovereign tax immunity asserted here

**Rule and authority:** Kennedy's Part I, joined by O'Connor, Scalia, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer, controls. After the Nation and its members left these parcels about two centuries ago, state and local government continuously governed and taxed the territory and the community developed under that settled arrangement. In those circumstances, ordinary market reacquisition of discrete parcels does not permit the Nation to revive sovereign immunity from the current property taxes. The bar applies both to an affirmative tax-protection suit and to the same sovereign tax-immunity defense in an enforcement action.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that extraordinary delay, territorial acquiescence and the difficulty of restoring a dormant sovereign arrangement bear on the present assertion of sovereign status itself. Oneida II recognized a federal land claim while leaving equitable limits open. Felix and the territorial-acquiescence decisions supply analogies for the effect of long delay and settled governance; they do not mechanically convert tribal rights into state boundaries.

The Nation already owns these parcels, as the dissent emphasizes. Protecting them would not eject the former willing sellers. But restoring sovereign exclusions parcel by parcel would alter the territorial arrangement maintained for generations. A limited tax decree therefore presents the sovereign-restoration issue even without a broader regulatory decree. The preliminary findings of municipal surpluses and no identified service failure defeat a claim of imminent fiscal collapse; the holding instead rests on the enduring governmental arrangement.

Yakima and Cass require their actual federal disposal sources and parcel chains. No such disposal has been established here, and fee alienability alone supplies no authorization. The present holding adds an independent qualification on assertion of dormant sovereign immunity; it is not an application of a nonexistent disposal statute. Nor is it merely denial of an injunction that leaves the identical tax-immunity defense available. Former §465 offers federal trust review of land use, need, tax-roll effects and jurisdictional conflicts before restored protection. That available orderly route answers the claim that ordinary repurchase must itself accomplish this sovereign restoration; it is not a prerequisite to every retained tribal right.

The tax claim fails even assuming the Nation's underlying reservation and historical-title predicates. The City's alternative diminishment, federal-reservation and recognition-termination theories therefore remain undecided on that sufficient-ground basis, rather than becoming implicit holdings against the Nation.

##### II. The actual amendment defenses must retain their distinct grounds

**Rule and authority:** Part I's seven Justices set aside the futility ruling only insofar as it excludes the independent nonrevival ground adopted above. Part II's eight Associate Justices sustain denial of the other five defenses on their actual theories; Stone-Zsela agrees with those five dispositions without adding a ground. The new merits rule supplies the City its recognized defense without requiring another pleading to obtain its benefit.

**Controlling explanation:** The limitations proposal treats a current-assessment challenge as an ancient recovery claim, identifies no applicable federal limitations period and cannot use a state period to extinguish the asserted federal protection under Oneida II. Purchase-based waiver and estoppel also fail: accepting a seller's fee interest is neither an inconsistent representation about the separate sovereign protection nor a relinquishment of it. The Court's independent territorial-repose ground does not turn the purchase itself into a waiver.

The in pari delicto defense, which would bar recovery based on the claimant's participation in the same unlawful transaction, identifies neither an unlawful present purchase nor a demand to recover proceeds of the Nation's own unlawful bargain. Ratification through market conduct or local practice supplies no legally sufficient federal act surrendering the protection or authorizing the tax. Each theory fails on its stated basis; the Court does not treat all equitable defenses as interchangeable. The accepted sovereign-nonrevival rule operates independently of those unsuccessful grounds.

##### III. Independent procedural grounds sustain the discovery and preliminary-injunction denials

**Rule and authority:** Part II's eight Associates join these grounds; Stone-Zsela agrees with both dispositions without adding a ground. The City failed to support its Rule 56(f) request with particular information, its expected material effect and an explanation of why prior efforts had not obtained it after an adequate opportunity. Its request to restrain further acquisitions also failed to establish irreparable harm, an independent requirement for that preliminary relief.

**Controlling explanation:** The discovery request must be assessed on its actual showing, which identifies no material information requiring delay of judgment. The equitable rule adopted today rests on established territorial history and supplies no missing-evidence premise for reopening discovery. As to preliminary relief, the lower court found the City's projected financial collapse exaggerated, identified several years of surpluses and found no service failure caused by nonpayment. A different tax-merits conclusion cannot supply the absent injury showing. Those preliminary findings decide the requested restraint on this record; they are not a permanent assessment of every later acquisition.

##### IV. Tax immunity and immunity from a collection suit remain distinct

**Rule and authority:** Part II's eight Associates join the stated scope of the mandate; Stone-Zsela agrees in preserving the independent judgments without adding their grounds. Removal of the property-tax-immunity premise does not disturb the independent suit-immunity judgments in the Lead, Eviction and Members cases. Those immunity questions and the Members case's separate sales-tax damages claim lie outside the presented property-tax question.

**Controlling explanation:** The Lead counterclaims were independently barred by tribal immunity. The district court's Eviction judgment and the independently preserved Members dismissal also survive at their actual scope. In the Members sales-tax claim, the official-capacity damages ruling supplies a separate basis for dismissal; the Second Circuit did not reach the additional necessary-party ground. The present tax ruling thus supplies neither a waiver nor congressional abrogation of suit immunity. Conforming proceedings must remove the rejected tax protection while respecting the independent judgments that continue to govern the City's requested enforcement and damages relief.

#### Precedent Treatment

- **County of Yakima v. Confederated Tribes and Bands of the Yakima Indian Nation:** Its actual federal disposal and incidence requirements remain. Governmental impact and ordinary fee alienability do not replace the required statutory source; the present independent nonrevival qualification is additional legal work.
- **Cass County v. Leech Lake Band of Chippewa Indians:** Its qualifying Nelson Act disposal, parcel chain and independent-protection requirements remain. No such disposal is inferred here, and Cass did not decide this extraordinary dormant-sovereignty claim.
- **County of Oneida v. Oneida Indian Nation (Oneida II), 470 U.S. 226:** Its federal common-law claim and existing damages judgment remain; its reservation of equitable limits permits consideration of the distinct current sovereign claim. Its limits on using state limitations periods retain their force.
- **Felix v. Patrick, 145 U.S. 317:** Supplies an analogy for equitable consequences of extraordinary delay and changed property arrangements, rather than a congressional disposal rule.
- **Ohio v. Kentucky, 410 U.S. 641:** Supplies a territorial-acquiescence analogy concerning long-established governance; its state-boundary holding is not mechanically transferred to tribes.
- **Hagen v. Utah; South Dakota v. Yankton Sioux Tribe; Minnesota v. Mille Lacs Band of Chippewa Indians:** Preserve their requirements for legally sufficient federal alteration of reservation or treaty rights. The Court's sufficient equity ground assumes the relevant predicates without deciding the City's alternative alteration theories.
- **Alaska v. Native Village of Venetie Tribal Government:** Its dependent-community inquiry retains its distinct statutory category; it does not erase the reservation category.
- **Idaho v. Coeur d'Alene Tribe of Idaho:** Its prospective officer-suit route does not itself determine the asserted right's merits or guarantee the requested equitable relief.

#### Law After Decision

Ordinary reacquisition cannot itself restore the sovereign property-tax immunity asserted after the extraordinary territorial dormancy established here. The rule reaches both affirmative and defensive use of that immunity and operates independently of statutory taxability through a completed congressional disposal. The federal-source rules of Yakima and Cass retain their own applications. A tax liability premise and the legal availability of a particular collection suit remain distinct; the independent immunity judgments preserved in these cases continue to operate.

#### Separate Positions Relevant to the Decision

**Stevens, concurring in Part II and dissenting from Part I; Stone-Zsela joins Part I of the dissent.** The two Justices would affirm effective protection of the identified Sherrill parcels against the challenged taxes and dependent enforcement. They require the Nation to establish the retained reservation and parcel connection and the City to identify a legally sufficient federal alteration or taxable disposal. The Treaty of Canandaigua recognizes the particular retained reservation and federal protection. Venetie's separate dependent-community criteria do not defeat that reservation status merely because the original reservation arose under state law.

The actual Buffalo Creek instrument supplies no completed Oneida surrender here. Articles 1 and 2 exchange Wisconsin interests for western lands; Article 3 conditions that new western interest, and Article 13 conditions Oneida removal on satisfactory arrangements for New York purchases. The separate Seneca and Tuscarora transactions cannot be attributed to the Oneidas. Existing recognition and succession likewise cannot be displaced by demographic dispersal alone. Yakima and Cass require the actual federal source, which this parcel chain lacks. Section 233 preserves existing tax protection; its distinct execution qualification for a tribal member's land-use or possession suit against another member is not a tax exception.

They reject the majority's independent qualification for the same reason. The Nation bought from willing sellers and resists a new governmental exaction on land it now owns. Effective prospective protection neither recovers ancient rents nor removes other private owners. Stevens's Oneida II concern about disruptive ancient recovery therefore does not decide this case. Equity may shape lawful relief, but reliance and elapsed time cannot create taxing power that federal law withholds. The majority's status formulation, in their view, permits the judicial extinction of protection while avoiding the federal act needed to remove it. Trust acquisition remains available; it is not a prerequisite to this existing parcel protection.

They would preserve the declaration and injunction and require ordinary implementation to identify the affected parcels, tax periods and enforcement measures. Sale or eviction consequences necessary to effective protection must be addressed according to their actual record status, independent legal requirements and legally required parties. That is bounded relief against these current assessments.

In Part II of his dissent, Stevens alone also objects to the Court's late formulation of the independent equitable bar. The majority regards that subsidiary ground as included in the tax-immunity question and its underlying substance as addressed by the competing arguments. Stone-Zsela's substantive position expressly reaches and rejects the equitable objection; he does not join Stevens's separate procedural objection.

The independent Lead counterclaim, Eviction and Members immunity judgments remain operative, including the separate sales-tax damages dismissal. The other five amendment denials, discovery denial and preliminary-injunction denial remain effective. The separate Madison County proceedings continue under their existing remand.

### Jackson v. Birmingham Board of Education, No. 02-1672

Merits decision, March 29, 2005

#### Chronology and Posture

Argued November 30, 2004. On review of the Eleventh Circuit judgment, 309 F.3d 1333, affirming dismissal of a coach's Title IX retaliation claim. The question is whether the established action reaches a recipient's retaliation for reporting sex discrimination when the personally injured reporter was not its original victim.

#### Judgment

**Reversed and remanded, 6–3.**

**Supporting:** Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Souter, Ginsburg and Breyer.

**Opposing:** Scalia, Kennedy and Thomas, who would affirm the categorical statutory dismissal.

The Eleventh Circuit's judgment is reversed, and the case returns for proceedings on Jackson's individual Title IX retaliation claim under the Court's coverage, attribution, causation and remedial rules.

#### Opinion Topology

STEVENS, J., delivered the opinion of the Court, Parts I–III, in which STONE-ZSELA, C. J., and O'CONNOR, SOUTER, GINSBURG and BREYER, JJ., joined in full. THOMAS, J., filed a dissenting opinion, in which SCALIA and KENNEDY, JJ., joined.

#### Controlling Propositions and Authority

##### I. Title IX protects a personally injured reporter of sex discrimination

**Holding and authority — Part I.** A Title IX funding recipient engages in intentional discrimination on the basis of sex when it materially retaliates against a person because that person reports or opposes sex discrimination in the recipient's educational program or activity. The personally injured reporter may invoke the established Title IX action although the reported unequal treatment targeted others. The claimant must prove covered-recipient status, legally attributable recipient conduct, a complaint concerning sex discrimination, materially adverse treatment because of that complaint and the causal injury supporting relief. These propositions command the six Justices joining the Court's opinion.

**Controlling explanation.** Section 1681 protects persons against discrimination on the basis of sex in covered education. A recipient that deliberately punishes a demand for sex equality uses retaliation to suppress the very protection Congress supplied. The sex-discrimination subject of the complaint and the recipient's purposeful response connect the adverse treatment to that statutory prohibition. The reporter's own sex need not be the trait used against him. Sullivan's protection of a personally injured advocate for another's equal property rights supplies a pre-enactment example of this understanding of discrimination; it concerns substantive protection as well as the advocate's ability to sue.

The Board and the dissent distinguish treatment because of sex from treatment because of complaint activity. That distinction overlooks what makes the alleged complaint a target: its demand for equal educational treatment without regard to sex. Protecting only the original victim would permit a recipient to punish the teacher who can identify and oppose the deprivation. Title VII's detailed enumeration of discrimination and retaliation does not require the same drafting in Title IX's broader prohibition. The statutory subject supplies the boundary; an unrelated employment grievance does not become sex discrimination merely because the employee suffers an adverse decision. Jackson alleges complaints about the girls' team's unequal funding, equipment and facility access, followed by removal from coaching and loss of pay. Those allegations present the statutory category decided here.

##### II. The established action and adequate funding notice reach the intentional statutory wrong

**Holding and authority — Part II.** Cannon's established Title IX action reaches the retaliation described in Part I. The statutory prohibition, the pre-enactment Sullivan background and the longstanding implementing interpretation together give funding recipients adequate notice of that obligation. Franklin permits appropriate compensation for a proved intentional violation, subject to recipient responsibility, causation and compensable injury. The same six Justices join.

**Controlling explanation.** Cannon supplies an existing action for the statutory protection; the Court now expressly decides that this intentional retaliation falls within that protection. Franklin supplies appropriate relief after the intentional wrong is proved. It does not substitute a desirable remedy for an enacted duty. Pennhurst requires fair notice of a funding condition, and that requirement is met by the statute read in its legal setting. Sullivan preceded Title IX, and the implementing rule has long forbidden retaliation for complaints of discrimination. Sections 106.71 and 100.7(e) confirm the statutory construction and notice; the damages duty rests on the statute itself.

The dissent correctly separates an existing action from the scope of the right, but the direct construction in Part I answers that scope objection. The absence of an earlier decision involving this coach's precise circumstances does not eliminate notice of the intentional statutory wrong. Powell, Sandoval and South Camden preserve a bounded prospective action under Title VI effects regulations. Their distinct regulatory setting neither supplies nor defeats this Title IX statutory claim. Gebser and Davis likewise concern different attribution problems involving third-party harassment. Their particular conditions cannot require a second complaint as a prerequisite to responsibility for the recipient's own intentional retaliatory decision. Jackson must still establish that the challenged conduct is legally the recipient's conduct; its importance to enforcement does not establish that fact.

##### III. The categorical dismissal must be reversed and the individual claim adjudicated

**Holding and authority — Part III.** Jackson's allegations of removal from coaching and lost supplemental pay because he complained of sex discrimination state a claim against the categorical grounds adopted below. The Eleventh Circuit's judgment is reversed. The District Court must determine coverage, recipient responsibility, the complaint's statutory subject, retaliatory causation, supported defenses and causal injury; appropriate compensation and prospective employment relief depend on that proof and their lawful requirements. The same six Justices join.

**Controlling explanation.** Jackson asserts his own job and pay injury. He need not first obtain a completed adjudication of the girls' discrimination claim to survive this dismissal. Pleading sufficiency accepts the material allegations for review; it does not establish their truth. The parties must develop the Board's decisionmaking, the asserted connection between the complaints and removal, and any independent lawful explanation. Sequence alone does not compel a finding of retaliatory causation.

Franklin allows compensation for proved causal pay loss and other legally recoverable injury. Prospective employment relief must address a proved continuing consequence within the court's lawful authority. If retaliation is established, the remedy must repair supported monetary loss and remaining adverse consequences within those limits. Ordinary proceedings will apply the adopted rule to the actual evidence and defenses.

#### Precedent Treatment

- **Cannon v. University of Chicago, 441 U.S. 677:** extends the established Title IX action to the intentional retaliation now held to violate the statute.
- **Sullivan v. Little Hunting Park, Inc., 396 U.S. 229:** applies its protection of an advocate personally injured for pressing another's statutory equality as pre-enactment support for the coverage and notice holdings, while preserving the distinct statutory settings.
- **Franklin v. Gwinnett County Public Schools, February 26, 1992:** applies and extends appropriate compensation for proved intentional Title IX discrimination to the statutory retaliation held actionable here, preserving recipient responsibility, causation and compensable loss.
- **Pennhurst State School and Hospital v. Halderman, 451 U.S. 1:** applies its fair-notice requirement; the enacted prohibition, Sullivan background and longstanding interpretation together satisfy it for this intentional wrong.
- **Gebser v. Lago Vista Independent School District, June 22, 1998:** preserves the teacher-attribution holdings and their actual authority limits; their third-party setting is distinguished from the recipient's own intentional retaliation.
- **Davis v. Monroe County Board of Education, May 24, 1999:** preserves its controlled-peer-harassment and culpable-recipient-response rules; their particular attribution and severity conditions are not transferred mechanically to the direct retaliatory decision alleged here.
- **Powell v. Ridge, April 3, 2000:** preserves its bounded Title VI prospective action for valid effects regulations and its distinct section 1983 holding; those rules remain separate from the intentional Title IX wrong decided here.
- **Alexander v. Sandoval, April 24, 2001:** preserves its application of Powell to the valid Department of Justice effects regulation. That prospective regulatory action supplies no separate source of the Title IX damages duty, which rests here on the statute.
- **South Camden Citizens in Action v. New Jersey Department of Environmental Protection, May 28, 2002:** preserves its application of the bounded Title VI action to valid EPA effects regulations and its separate statutory and section 1983 components. The present right and damages duty arise from Title IX's intentional-discrimination prohibition.

#### Law After Decision

Title IX now expressly reaches a covered recipient's materially adverse intentional retaliation because a person reports or opposes sex discrimination in its educational program. A personally injured reporter may use the established action even when the original discrimination targeted others. The statutory claim requires recipient coverage and responsibility, the sex-discrimination subject, retaliatory causation and causal injury; the adopted notice holding permits Franklin compensation upon proof, with prospective employment relief under its lawful requirements. The distinct teacher- and peer-harassment attribution rules retain their scopes.

#### Separate Positions Relevant to the Decision

**Thomas, joined by Scalia and Kennedy, dissenting.** The dissent would affirm the categorical statutory dismissal. Section 1681 prohibits discrimination against a person on the basis of that person's sex. Intentional punishment for complaint activity is a distinct prohibition, even when the complaint alleges sex discrimination against someone else. Jackson's alleged loss of coaching duties and pay establishes personal injury; it does not establish that the enacted right protects the activity that allegedly caused that injury. The dissent's beneficiary and statutory-causation objections therefore remain distinct from standing.

Title VII's express retaliation provision illustrates that Congress can separately protect complaint activity. Sullivan construed section 1982's property-right protection, and neither its standing analysis nor its substantive setting compels the asserted Title IX funding obligation. Cannon supplies an action for rights Congress enacted; Franklin permits remedies after a statutory wrong is established. Neither decides this disputed expansion of coverage. Pennhurst adds a notice objection to imposing the asserted condition on recipients without a sufficiently clear statutory command. The dissent accepts the actual scopes of the existing Title IX and Title VI enforcement precedents but finds no enacted retaliation right here. It would conclude the appealed Title IX theory.

#### Source Notes

20 U.S.C. §1681(a); 34 C.F.R. §§106.71 and 100.7(e); Jackson v. Birmingham Board of Education, 309 F.3d 1333 (11th Cir. 2002); the United States' merits brief in No. 02-1672. The complaint alleges unequal treatment, complaints, removal from coaching and loss of supplemental pay while Jackson remained a teacher. Those allegations are accepted for review of the categorical dismissal and require proof in further proceedings.

#### Mandate, Remedy, and Stage

The case returns through the Eleventh Circuit for ordinary adjudication of Jackson's individual retaliation claim. The District Court must permit development of the decisionmaking and causation record, assess the Board's supported lawful explanation and other defenses, and identify relief repairing any proved injury. A proved violation permits appropriate compensation for causal pay loss and other legally recoverable injury, together with justified prospective employment relief addressing continuing consequences.

**End of entry: Jackson v. Birmingham Board of Education, merits decision, March 29, 2005.**

### Sabree ex rel. Sabree v. Richman

Merits decision, March 29, 2005

#### Chronology and Posture

On full merits submission. On review of the Third Circuit's May 11, 2004 judgment, 367 F.3d 180, reversing dismissal of claims to covered Medicaid assistance with reasonable promptness. The questions concern the identified individual rights and whether Medicaid's hearing and funding provisions foreclose section 1983 enforcement.

#### Judgment

**Affirmed, 9–0.**

**Supporting:** Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer.

The Third Circuit's judgment reversing dismissal is affirmed. The named beneficiaries' claims proceed against the responsible Secretary for prospective enforcement under the identified assistance rights, the dated statutory definition and the ordinary requirements for breach and lawful relief. Further proceedings determine any class and the proper scope of relief.

#### Opinion Topology

SCALIA, J., delivered the opinion of the Court, Parts I–III, in which STONE-ZSELA, C. J., and STEVENS, O'CONNOR, KENNEDY, SOUTER, THOMAS, GINSBURG and BREYER, JJ., joined in full.

#### Controlling Propositions and Authority

##### I. The identified assistance and promptness commands create individual rights

**Holding and authority — Part I, joined by all nine Justices.** Sections 1396a(a)(10) and 1396a(a)(8) give eligible beneficiaries individual rights to the covered medical assistance their participating State has undertaken to provide and to that assistance with reasonable promptness. The claimant must identify the adopted benefit and satisfy its governing financial, categorical and clinical conditions. For the intermediate-care benefit here, the operative section 1396d(a) definition concerns payment of part or all of covered costs; paragraph (15) identifies the covered category in conjunction with those commands. Pennsylvania's adoption of the benefit and these plaintiffs' eligibility are accepted at this stage.

The category covers intermediate care outside an institution for mental diseases for persons determined under section 1396a(a)(31) to need it. That provision requires a written care plan before admission or authorization of benefits and regular independent professional review of continuing need. Section 1396d(d) requires a qualifying institution or distinct part with the prescribed health or rehabilitative purpose and standards, and active treatment for the payment sought. Those conditions operate at their statutory stages; an eligible waiting-list claimant need not already receive the allegedly withheld treatment before challenging delay. The public-institution expenditure-maintenance condition by its terms governs quarters before January 1, 1975.

**Controlling explanation.** Blessing asks whether the provision identifies an intended individual beneficiary, imposes a binding obligation and defines a right sufficiently definite for judicial enforcement. These provisions do so: they direct assistance to eligible people and require its reasonably prompt provision. The identified benefit, eligibility and evidence of delay make that qualitative timing requirement judicially manageable. Clinical and fiscal questions inform its application rather than erase it.

The Secretary emphasizes state-plan form and program administration. Suter's rejection of the particular reasonable-efforts and reporting claims establishes no general immunity for plan provisions. Sections 1320a-2 and 1320a-10 remove plan placement alone as a ground of nonenforceability, without creating a right independently or disturbing Suter's preserved section 671(a)(15) result. Wright and Wilder exemplify enforceable, defined benefit obligations rather than a general promise of program success. Gonzaga rejects the particular FERPA entitlement; its four-Justice general reformulation does not displace Blessing.

The dated payment definition fixes the obligation's scope. It reaches covered care provided in or after the third month before the application month; its distinct qualified-Medicare-beneficiary cost-sharing rule begins after the month that status is acquired. The pertinent benefit remains subject to the actual statutory categories and conditions. The command and definition together supply the personal right. The State's accepted adoption and the plaintiffs' accepted eligibility make the claimed assistance concrete without turning every statutory definition into an independent entitlement.

##### II. Medicaid's hearing and funding mechanisms do not foreclose this section 1983 action

**Holding and authority — Part II, joined by all nine Justices.** The personal assistance rights recognized in Part I are enforceable through section 1983. The Secretary has shown neither an express exclusion nor a sufficiently comprehensive remedial scheme incompatible with that route. Section 1396a(a)(3)'s individual fair hearing and section 1396c's federal funding supervision, considered together, do not establish the asserted foreclosure.

**Controlling explanation.** Blessing separates an individual right from Congress's displacement of section 1983. Once the right is identified, the defendant must establish the asserted exclusion or actual incompatibility. Section 1396a(a)(3) gives an individual whose assistance claim is denied or not acted on promptly an opportunity for a state-agency fair hearing. That personal corrective remedy deserves consideration in its own right. Section 1396c separately requires reasonable notice and an opportunity for hearing before the Secretary finds that a changed state plan no longer complies with section 1396a or that administration fails substantially to comply with its provisions. Upon that finding, the Secretary must notify the state agency and withhold further payments until satisfied that the failure will no longer occur; the Secretary may instead limit payments to unaffected categories or parts. That aggregate enforcement measure does not replace an individual's timing entitlement.

Rancho distinguishes a specific personal judicial action, its thirty-day filing period and expedited adjudication, whose restrictions a parallel section 1983 action would bypass. Here, neither the fair hearing nor funding supervision, alone or together, identifies a comparable incompatibility or another congressional remedial restriction this action would evade. Their presence and detail do not establish exclusivity. The inquiry concerns the actual combined design, not a rule that administrative remedies can never displace section 1983. Wright, Wilder, Blessing and Bruneau preserve that distinction between particular rights, funding administration and an incompatible remedy. The State's comprehensiveness argument therefore fails on this statutory structure.

##### III. Prospective enforcement proceeds through findings and effective lawful relief

**Holding and authority — Part III, joined by all nine Justices.** The Third Circuit's reversal of dismissal is affirmed. The named beneficiaries may proceed for prospective enforcement against the responsible Secretary. The District Court must determine the assistance owed, unreasonable delay, causation, official responsibility and lawful means of compliance, and adjudicate any proposed representative scope under Rule 23. If a violation is proved, an injunction must identify the lawful assistance, compliance steps and reasonable deadlines supported by the findings, with reporting adequate to test performance and enforcement upon noncompliance.

**Controlling explanation.** Blessing requires the particular right, breach and remedy to remain distinct, and the prospective-officer framework permits effective enforcement of the federal obligation against the responsible official. Accepted eligibility and alleged years of waiting permit adjudication; they do not supply the missing findings about the assistance withheld, cause of delay or lawful compliance arrangement. The District Court must develop those matters and decide the request for prospective relief.

The Third Circuit expressly reserved whether this dated obligation requires financing rather than furnishing services, and the precise compliance means. That legal question remains open because the operative assistance and remedial facts are undeveloped. The payment-based definition governs its resolution; neither nominal willingness to pay nor an assumed general service entitlement resolves the actual duty. Budgetary administration cannot erase the entitlement, but relief must remain authorized by the operative statute.

The named claims proceed. Broader relief requires a properly established class and common unlawful practice. If proved, the remedy must cure that practice for those it governs unless a narrower cure is demonstrated. Findings must support the assistance obligations and reasonable timetable, so the remand produces a decision on workable compliance rather than an indefinite placeholder.

#### Precedent Treatment

- **Blessing v. Freestone, April 21, 1997:** applies its provision-specific rights inquiry, separate presumption of section 1983 enforcement and requirement of actual congressional incompatibility. Its distinction between personal assistance and aggregate administration governs both the rights and remedy analysis.
- **Wright v. Roanoke Redevelopment and Housing Authority, 479 U.S. 418:** applies its recognition of a defined individual benefit obligation and its separate foreclosure inquiry; a program's administrative structure alone does not defeat the identified right.
- **Wilder v. Virginia Hospital Association, 496 U.S. 498:** applies its specific Medicaid-obligation and enforcement principles, without converting every administrative requirement into a beneficiary right.
- **Suter v. Artist M., March 25, 1992:** preserves its particular reasonable-efforts, reporting and implied-action rulings. The state-plan form is not a categorical bar; sections 1320a-2 and 1320a-10 retain their enacted correction and express preservation of the section 671(a)(15) result.
- **Gonzaga University v. Doe, June 20, 2002:** preserves its rejection of the asserted FERPA entitlement and its fractured authority. The four-Justice general rights formulation supplies no controlling replacement for Blessing.
- **Bruneau v. South Kortright Central School District, June 3, 1999:** preserves the distinction between funding administration and an actually incompatible remedy; that distinction informs the combined Medicaid-mechanism analysis.
- **City of Rancho Palos Verdes v. Abrams, March 22, 2005:** applies its separate rights and channel inquiries and distinguishes the specific personal judicial action, thirty-day filing period and expedited adjudication that supported foreclosure there. The holding depends on the concrete statutory structure rather than the mere existence of an express remedy.
- **Ex parte Young, 209 U.S. 123:** supplies the prospective-officer framework for enforcement of a proved ongoing federal obligation against the responsible Secretary; relief must fit that obligation and official responsibility.

#### Law After Decision

Eligible beneficiaries of the adopted intermediate-care benefit possess individual rights to the covered payment-based medical assistance and its provision with reasonable promptness under sections 1396a(a)(10) and (8), read with the operative section 1396d definition and conditions. Section 1983 supplies an enforcement channel because the combined Medicaid hearing and funding mechanisms do not establish congressional incompatibility. Courts must identify the benefit and eligibility, apply the definition in force when performance was required, and fit effective prospective relief to proved delay and official responsibility. The financing-versus-furnishing question remains for resolution on the developed remedial record under that dated definition.
