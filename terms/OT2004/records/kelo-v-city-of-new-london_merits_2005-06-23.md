## Controlling holdings

### Actual public redevelopment, private implementation and favoritism

**Holding and operative rule:** A compensated condemnation implementing an adopted comprehensive redevelopment plan satisfies public use when it serves a rationally supported actual public purpose rather than a pretext for private favoritism, even though private parties will implement portions and the particular homes are not blighted. Courts must meaningfully examine supported claims that the stated public purpose masks a private favor, using the actual plan, process and beneficiary relationships under the ordinary presumption of lawful public action and ordinary proof rules.

**Authority:** The Court's six-Justice opinion, whose author and complete joins appear in Opinion Topology, directly controls the entire rule and its application.

**Controlling explanation:** Berman permits an integrated redevelopment undertaking and private implementation rather than requiring every parcel to be independently harmful. Midkiff makes the public objective, rather than the mechanics of the transfer, decisive. Boston & Maine applies that rule to private reconveyance while retaining an enforceable prohibition on naked private favoritism.

The adopted plan responds to prolonged economic distress through coordinated commercial, residential and public uses. That established undertaking supplies an actual public objective beyond replacing one owner with another. Individual homes need not be blighted for their inclusion in an otherwise public plan. Forecasts of jobs and revenues support the purpose without becoming guarantees; a court need not establish that every expected benefit will materialize.

The owners and common dissent object that every ordinary successor use can promise greater productivity, making all property vulnerable. The answer lies in the public character of the actual undertaking and the independently enforceable private-favor boundary. Reciting economic benefits cannot validate a sham whose actual object is favoring a private recipient and whose public gains are merely incidental. Supported allegations require genuine record examination. On this record, the established planning purpose supports the condemnations and supplies no unresolved pretext issue requiring remand.

The Constitution does not confine privately implemented public acquisitions to removal of already harmful conditions. Public ownership and enforceable public access are sufficient examples of public use under these precedents, but do not exhaust the public-purpose rule. Just compensation and stricter state restrictions retain their independent force. An isolated transfer devised for a favored recipient is outside the rule sustained here.

## Precedent treatment and resulting law

- Berman v. Parker is applied to integrated public redevelopment and private implementation; individual parcel blight is not an indispensable condition.
- Hawaii Housing Authority v. Midkiff is applied to the actual public objective rather than the ultimate titleholder.

- Lingle v. Chevron U.S.A. Inc. retains the distinction between regulatory-taking liability and independent constitutional restraints, including the public-use condition.

The public-purpose rule permits the challenged comprehensive redevelopment condemnations with compensation and lawful authority, while requiring meaningful examination of supported private-favor allegations. The operative inquiry concerns the actual undertaking and its public object; economic forecasts need rational support rather than guaranteed success. State law may impose stricter acquisition limits.

## Material published noncontrolling positions

**Justice Kennedy, concurring.** Kennedy joins the Court's opinion in full. Ordinary deference must still examine a supported allegation that private favoritism supplies the actual object of a taking. The rational-basis inquiries in Cleburne and Moreno illustrate that judicial respect for governmental choices can coexist with a genuine examination of an impermissible object. The plan, decision process and beneficiary relationships matter to that inquiry; merely identifying incidental private gain does not establish invalidity.

The record supports this comprehensive plan under that standard. Kennedy leaves open whether a narrowly drawn, exceptionally suspicious class of transfers could warrant stronger review. He identifies no governing heightened standard or set of triggers for that different category in this case.

**Justice O'Connor, dissenting, joined by Justices Scalia and Thomas.** The dissent would prohibit taking ordinary private property for new private users solely because their normal activities are expected to produce greater economic benefits. Public ownership and actual public service remain valid. Berman addressed a harmful area, and Midkiff a harmful concentration of ownership; the acquisition itself removed the identified condition. The petitioners' ordinary homes present a materially different case.

The precedents' broad public-purpose language favors the City's position, but the dissent would confine that language at this economic-successor boundary. A sincere comprehensive plan does not cure the substantive problem because virtually every private activity yields incidental public gains. Meaningful pretext review addresses bad purpose but leaves the lawful-purpose rule too broad. Compensation and possible state-law protection cannot replace an independent federal public-use limit. The dissent would reverse and remand for relief preventing these condemnations.

**Justice Thomas, dissenting.** Thomas also joins the common dissent as an alternative under existing precedents, while proposing a further change to their constitutional rationale. The public-use condition should require governmental ownership or use, or a legally enforceable entitlement of the public to use the property. Common-carrier property may satisfy that rule despite private title; projected jobs and taxes from ordinary private premises do not.

The constitutional text and structure distinguish public use from the independent duty to compensate. Cole and Kohl illustrate public functions and the private-use prohibition without proving an exhaustive historical catalogue. Thomas would reconsider Berman's and Midkiff's broader public-purpose propositions and incompatible private-transfer permission. He would replace Boston & Maine's broader rationale while recognizing that secured passenger service could satisfy the narrower use rule. His alternative reliance on the common dissent does not amount to approval of those precedents' permanent constitutional correctness.

## Holdings

### Actual public redevelopment, private implementation and favoritism

**Holding and operative rule:** A compensated condemnation implementing an adopted comprehensive redevelopment plan satisfies public use when it serves a rationally supported actual public purpose rather than a pretext for private favoritism, even though private parties will implement portions and the particular homes are not blighted. Courts must meaningfully examine supported claims that the stated public purpose masks a private favor, using the actual plan, process and beneficiary relationships under the ordinary presumption of lawful public action and ordinary proof rules.

**Authority:** The Court's six-Justice opinion, whose author and complete joins appear in Opinion Topology, directly controls the entire rule and its application.

**Controlling explanation:** Berman permits an integrated redevelopment undertaking and private implementation rather than requiring every parcel to be independently harmful. Midkiff makes the public objective, rather than the mechanics of the transfer, decisive. Boston & Maine applies that rule to private reconveyance while retaining an enforceable prohibition on naked private favoritism.

The adopted plan responds to prolonged economic distress through coordinated commercial, residential and public uses. That established undertaking supplies an actual public objective beyond replacing one owner with another. Individual homes need not be blighted for their inclusion in an otherwise public plan. Forecasts of jobs and revenues support the purpose without becoming guarantees; a court need not establish that every expected benefit will materialize.

The owners and common dissent object that every ordinary successor use can promise greater productivity, making all property vulnerable. The answer lies in the public character of the actual undertaking and the independently enforceable private-favor boundary. Reciting economic benefits cannot validate a sham whose actual object is favoring a private recipient and whose public gains are merely incidental. Supported allegations require genuine record examination. On this record, the established planning purpose supports the condemnations and supplies no unresolved pretext issue requiring remand.

The Constitution does not confine privately implemented public acquisitions to removal of already harmful conditions. Public ownership and enforceable public access are sufficient examples of public use under these precedents, but do not exhaust the public-purpose rule. Just compensation and stricter state restrictions retain their independent force. An isolated transfer devised for a favored recipient is outside the rule sustained here.

## Precedent Treatment

- Berman v. Parker is applied to integrated public redevelopment and private implementation; individual parcel blight is not an indispensable condition.
- Hawaii Housing Authority v. Midkiff is applied to the actual public objective rather than the ultimate titleholder.
- National Railroad Passenger Corp. v. Boston & Maine Corp. is applied to public purpose despite private reconveyance and its surviving prohibition on naked favoritism. Its railroad statutory need presumption does not govern this municipal plan.
- Lingle v. Chevron U.S.A. Inc. retains the distinction between regulatory-taking liability and independent constitutional restraints, including the public-use condition.

## Law After Decision

The public-purpose rule permits the challenged comprehensive redevelopment condemnations with compensation and lawful authority, while requiring meaningful examination of supported private-favor allegations. The operative inquiry concerns the actual undertaking and its public object; economic forecasts need rational support rather than guaranteed success. State law may impose stricter acquisition limits.

## Separate Writings

**Justice Kennedy, concurring.** Kennedy joins the Court's opinion in full. Ordinary deference must still examine a supported allegation that private favoritism supplies the actual object of a taking. The rational-basis inquiries in Cleburne and Moreno illustrate that judicial respect for governmental choices can coexist with a genuine examination of an impermissible object. The plan, decision process and beneficiary relationships matter to that inquiry; merely identifying incidental private gain does not establish invalidity.

The record supports this comprehensive plan under that standard. Kennedy leaves open whether a narrowly drawn, exceptionally suspicious class of transfers could warrant stronger review. He identifies no governing heightened standard or set of triggers for that different category in this case.

**Justice O'Connor, dissenting, joined by Justices Scalia and Thomas.** The dissent would prohibit taking ordinary private property for new private users solely because their normal activities are expected to produce greater economic benefits. Public ownership and actual public service remain valid. Berman addressed a harmful area, and Midkiff a harmful concentration of ownership; the acquisition itself removed the identified condition. The petitioners' ordinary homes present a materially different case.

The precedents' broad public-purpose language favors the City's position, but the dissent would confine that language at this economic-successor boundary. A sincere comprehensive plan does not cure the substantive problem because virtually every private activity yields incidental public gains. Meaningful pretext review addresses bad purpose but leaves the lawful-purpose rule too broad. Compensation and possible state-law protection cannot replace an independent federal public-use limit. The dissent would reverse and remand for relief preventing these condemnations.

**Justice Thomas, dissenting.** Thomas also joins the common dissent as an alternative under existing precedents, while proposing a further change to their constitutional rationale. The public-use condition should require governmental ownership or use, or a legally enforceable entitlement of the public to use the property. Common-carrier property may satisfy that rule despite private title; projected jobs and taxes from ordinary private premises do not.

The constitutional text and structure distinguish public use from the independent duty to compensate. Cole and Kohl illustrate public functions and the private-use prohibition without proving an exhaustive historical catalogue. Thomas would reconsider Berman's and Midkiff's broader public-purpose propositions and incompatible private-transfer permission. He would replace Boston & Maine's broader rationale while recognizing that secured passenger service could satisfy the narrower use rule. His alternative reliance on the common dissent does not amount to approval of those precedents' permanent constitutional correctness.

## Procedure After Action

The Connecticut judgment stands against the federal public-use challenge. The City may undertake the adjudicated acquisitions through applicable condemnation and compensation proceedings. Preserved compensation and independent state-law claims follow their proper procedures.
