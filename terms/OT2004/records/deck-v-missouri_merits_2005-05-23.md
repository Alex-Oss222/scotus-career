## Controlling holdings

### Visible restraint during capital punishment selection

**Holding and operative rule:** A court may not require a defendant to appear visibly shackled before a capital sentencing jury unless circumstances particular to the defendant and proceeding establish an essential security or comparable courtroom need. The State must justify the measure; the trial judge retains reasonable discretion to address actual danger, escape risk and courtroom order, considering supported history and reasonable less prejudicial alternatives without a prescribed hearing format or proof quantum.

**Authority:** Part I of the Court's opinion, joined in full by Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Kennedy, Souter, Ginsburg and Breyer, supplies seven direct joins. That same coalition controls Parts II and III.

**Controlling explanation:** Allen preserves the court's power to maintain order, while Holbrook distinguishes ordinary security from an inherently prejudicial display. Riggins shows why a compelled courtroom condition requires actual justification and why its effects on participation may resist reconstruction. Their principles extend to the jury's capital punishment decision. Conviction settles guilt; it does not settle punishment. Visible chains can convey the court's own judgment of present dangerousness at that remaining choice, interfere with participation and consultation, and compromise the dignity and reliability of the proceeding. The dissent's distinction between an accused and a convicted defendant therefore does not answer the due-process concern. The Court preserves real security authority: a judge may use the defendant's supported history and need not await a new incident. Necessity, rather than a routine practice tied to conviction, supplies the constitutional justification.

### The restraints in this proceeding lacked the required justification

**Holding and operative rule:** Conviction alone and generalized juror reassurance do not justify deliberately imposed visible shackling throughout a capital penalty proceeding. Deck's preserved challenge establishes unconstitutional restraint because the trial court identified no individualized necessity and this record supplies no exceptional, indisputable need curing that omission.

**Authority:** Part II, joined by the seven-Justice Court coalition.

**Controlling explanation:** The objections and the trial court's responses establish that the jury saw Deck's restraints. Estelle's distinction between compelled appearance and an unobjected choice therefore presents no preservation obstacle. The court's reasons addressed the general condition of a convicted defendant and reassurance, rather than circumstances showing why these restraints were needed for Deck. A conclusory reference to security cannot replace the actual judgment Part I requires.

### The State bears the direct-review harmlessness burden

**Holding and operative rule:** Unjustified visible shackling in capital jury sentencing is constitutional trial error. On a preserved direct-review claim, the State must establish beyond a reasonable doubt from the full penalty record that the error did not contribute to the death sentence; absent that showing, the defendant receives a lawful new sentencing proceeding or a lawful noncapital sentence.

**Authority:** Part III, joined by the seven-Justice Court coalition, adopts the rule and remand.

**Controlling explanation:** Chapman supplies the governing burden for preserved constitutional trial error. Smith v. Texas applies that burden to a preserved capital sentencing restriction and distinguishes correction of the penalty proceeding from disturbance of guilt. Deck need not reconstruct the jury's unshackled deliberations or prove each juror's actual response before relief becomes available. The inquiry remains a genuine full-record harmlessness review, rather than automatic reversal. Riggins's medication-specific holding supports attention to unreconstructable effects but did not previously decide this restraint error's Chapman treatment. Missouri's contrary demand that Deck establish actual prejudice placed the burden on the wrong party. The reviewing court must apply the proper burden to the actual sentencing record.

## Precedent treatment and resulting law

- Illinois v. Allen preserves necessary courtroom control and supplies the individualized security authority applied at capital sentencing.
- Holbrook v. Flynn supplies the distinction between ordinary security arrangements and inherently prejudicial measures; that distinction governs the visible restraints here.
- Riggins v. Nevada is extended by analogy concerning actual justification and effects that cannot reliably be reconstructed; its medication holding remains confined to that subject.
- Estelle v. Williams preserves the distinction between compelled appearance and a measure accepted without timely objection; Deck repeatedly objected.
- Chapman v. California now governs the preserved visible-restraint error on direct review.
- Smith v. Texas supplies the existing capital-error allocation and sentence-specific remedy; its mitigation rule remains distinct from the restraint prohibition adopted here.

Due process now expressly protects capital punishment selection against unjustified visible restraints. Particular necessity permits appropriate security; routine reliance on conviction does not. A preserved violation receives State-borne Chapman review, with sentence-specific relief if harmlessness is not established. The rule separates the trial judge's security determination from the reviewing court's inquiry into the error's effect.

**Opposing:** Scalia and Thomas.

The reviewed affirmance of the death sentence is reversed. Missouri must establish harmlessness beyond a reasonable doubt on the full penalty record; otherwise the death sentence must be vacated and the State must provide a lawful new penalty proceeding or a lawful noncapital sentence. The convictions remain intact.

## Holdings

### Visible restraint during capital punishment selection

**Holding and operative rule:** A court may not require a defendant to appear visibly shackled before a capital sentencing jury unless circumstances particular to the defendant and proceeding establish an essential security or comparable courtroom need. The State must justify the measure; the trial judge retains reasonable discretion to address actual danger, escape risk and courtroom order, considering supported history and reasonable less prejudicial alternatives without a prescribed hearing format or proof quantum.

**Authority:** Part I of the Court's opinion, joined in full by Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Kennedy, Souter, Ginsburg and Breyer, supplies seven direct joins. That same coalition controls Parts II and III.

**Controlling explanation:** Allen preserves the court's power to maintain order, while Holbrook distinguishes ordinary security from an inherently prejudicial display. Riggins shows why a compelled courtroom condition requires actual justification and why its effects on participation may resist reconstruction. Their principles extend to the jury's capital punishment decision. Conviction settles guilt; it does not settle punishment. Visible chains can convey the court's own judgment of present dangerousness at that remaining choice, interfere with participation and consultation, and compromise the dignity and reliability of the proceeding. The dissent's distinction between an accused and a convicted defendant therefore does not answer the due-process concern. The Court preserves real security authority: a judge may use the defendant's supported history and need not await a new incident. Necessity, rather than a routine practice tied to conviction, supplies the constitutional justification.

### The restraints in this proceeding lacked the required justification

**Holding and operative rule:** Conviction alone and generalized juror reassurance do not justify deliberately imposed visible shackling throughout a capital penalty proceeding. Deck's preserved challenge establishes unconstitutional restraint because the trial court identified no individualized necessity and this record supplies no exceptional, indisputable need curing that omission.

**Authority:** Part II, joined by the seven-Justice Court coalition.

**Controlling explanation:** The objections and the trial court's responses establish that the jury saw Deck's restraints. Estelle's distinction between compelled appearance and an unobjected choice therefore presents no preservation obstacle. The court's reasons addressed the general condition of a convicted defendant and reassurance, rather than circumstances showing why these restraints were needed for Deck. A conclusory reference to security cannot replace the actual judgment Part I requires.

### The State bears the direct-review harmlessness burden

**Holding and operative rule:** Unjustified visible shackling in capital jury sentencing is constitutional trial error. On a preserved direct-review claim, the State must establish beyond a reasonable doubt from the full penalty record that the error did not contribute to the death sentence; absent that showing, the defendant receives a lawful new sentencing proceeding or a lawful noncapital sentence.

**Authority:** Part III, joined by the seven-Justice Court coalition, adopts the rule and remand.

**Controlling explanation:** Chapman supplies the governing burden for preserved constitutional trial error. Smith v. Texas applies that burden to a preserved capital sentencing restriction and distinguishes correction of the penalty proceeding from disturbance of guilt. Deck need not reconstruct the jury's unshackled deliberations or prove each juror's actual response before relief becomes available. The inquiry remains a genuine full-record harmlessness review, rather than automatic reversal. Riggins's medication-specific holding supports attention to unreconstructable effects but did not previously decide this restraint error's Chapman treatment. Missouri's contrary demand that Deck establish actual prejudice placed the burden on the wrong party. The reviewing court must apply the proper burden to the actual sentencing record.

## Precedent Treatment

- Illinois v. Allen preserves necessary courtroom control and supplies the individualized security authority applied at capital sentencing.
- Holbrook v. Flynn supplies the distinction between ordinary security arrangements and inherently prejudicial measures; that distinction governs the visible restraints here.
- Riggins v. Nevada is extended by analogy concerning actual justification and effects that cannot reliably be reconstructed; its medication holding remains confined to that subject.
- Estelle v. Williams preserves the distinction between compelled appearance and a measure accepted without timely objection; Deck repeatedly objected.
- Chapman v. California now governs the preserved visible-restraint error on direct review.
- Smith v. Texas supplies the existing capital-error allocation and sentence-specific remedy; its mitigation rule remains distinct from the restraint prohibition adopted here.

## Law After Decision

Due process now expressly protects capital punishment selection against unjustified visible restraints. Particular necessity permits appropriate security; routine reliance on conviction does not. A preserved violation receives State-borne Chapman review, with sentence-specific relief if harmlessness is not established. The rule separates the trial judge's security determination from the reviewing court's inquiry into the error's effect.

## Separate Writings

Justice Thomas, joined by Scalia, dissents. The guilt-stage restraint cases protected the presumption of innocence, and the medication cases addressed distinct liberty and defense effects. A convicted capital defendant stands in a different posture. In the dissent's view, those authorities do not establish that visible restraints at punishment are constitutionally prejudicial without proof of actual unfairness or serious impairment of assistance in the defense. The writing acknowledges that chains can suggest danger but rejects treating that possible incremental effect as a sufficient constitutional violation on this showing. It would affirm. Its disagreement precedes harmlessness: it does not excuse an acknowledged constitutional error under a different Chapman burden.

## Procedure After Action

The case returns to the Missouri courts for full-record harmless-error review under the State's burden. If that showing fails, the State must provide lawful resentencing or the lawful noncapital alternative. At a new penalty proceeding the judge must determine actual security needs before exposing the jury to restraints and reconsider changed circumstances; the prior general rationale cannot substitute for that determination.
