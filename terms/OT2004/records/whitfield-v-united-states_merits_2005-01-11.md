## Controlling holdings

### Section 1956(h) requires the unlawful agreement, without an additional overt act

**Holding and operative rule:** Section 1956(h) independently defines conspiracy to commit an offense specified in §1956 or §1957 and requires neither allegation nor proof of an additional overt act. The Government must prove the actual unlawful agreement, the defendant's knowing participation and the required unlawful object beyond a reasonable doubt; association alone is insufficient, and the requested overt-act instruction was properly refused in both cases.

**Authority:** Ginsburg's opinion of the Court, joined in full by the other eight Justices identified in Opinion Topology. All nine adopt the complete statute-specific construction and both docket applications.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that Congress made the agreement itself the conspiracy offense. Section 1956(h) directly identifies the persons who conspire and the specified laundering offenses that are their object. It contains no additional-act requirement and no cross-reference incorporating §371. The latter statute expressly requires an act in furtherance of its conspiracy. That enacted difference must be given effect.

Shabani supplies the close analogy: its construction of §846 preserves proof of the knowing unlawful agreement without adding an overt act. The Court independently reaches the same conclusion for §1956(h)'s materially equivalent agreement-and-penalty language. Nash supplies the traditional meaning under which the agreement completes conspiracy, and Singer explains the significance of Congress's omission of a further-act condition.

Petitioners contend that §1956(h) merely increases punishment for a conspiracy otherwise defined by §371. A sentence can define the prohibited conduct and prescribe its punishment together. The same-penalties language therefore does not supply the missing incorporation. The penalty-oriented title and placement apart from the substantive laundering offenses do not negate the operative agreement offense. Legislative silence about eliminating an act requirement cannot insert one into that text.

The later venue provision identifies geographic connections for prosecution. Its reference to an act in furtherance does not itself make such an act an element of guilt under the earlier offense. That distinction answers petitioners' attempt to turn a venue provision into an element of guilt. Ratzlaf preserves lenity when ordinary construction leaves genuine ambiguity; the text, structure and settled agreement meaning resolve the present question. The refusal to add an overt-act instruction thus supplies no ground for reversal or a new trial in either docket.
