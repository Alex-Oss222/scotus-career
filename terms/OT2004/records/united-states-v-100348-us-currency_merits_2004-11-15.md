## Holdings

**A protected possessor may vindicate his own interest.** A lawful possessor whose property rights or enforceable obligations are impaired by punitive forfeiture may challenge the exaction against all property to which that established interest extends. The claimant must establish the interest under applicable property law and the record; another person's title does not by itself defeat the claim. Part I is unanimous.

Mayzel's actual possession and California bailment duties give this claim legal substance beyond physical custody. The entrusted amount is $100,000 within a total fund of $100,348; the full-fund challenge rests on his protected possessory interest, including that bailment. Delivery and care duties survive the unproved purported disclaimer. Austin v. United States directs attention to punitive operation rather than the civil form: deprivation of this claimant's own interest answers the Government's objection that only the titled owner is punished. The interest exists without a completed damages judgment against Mayzel. The owner's procedural default governs the owner's claim while leaving Mayzel's independently established interest subject to adjudication.

**Punitive reporting forfeiture is governed by gross disproportionality.** A partly punitive reporting forfeiture violates the Excessive Fines Clause when the claimant proves that it is grossly disproportionate to the actual offense. Courts compare supported culpability, harm, the property's relation to the wrongdoing and authorized sanctions where relevant, with substantial respect for legislative punishment judgments. Constitutional proportionality receives independent appellate review; supported subsidiary facts receive clear-error review. Part II is unanimous.

Austin supplies coverage; United States v. Bajakajian supplies the constitutional comparison and distinct review standards. United States v. Wagoner County Real Estate supplies the supported-finding discipline and claimant burden, which the Court extends from its real-property setting to this reporting exaction. These considerations retain their contextual operation rather than becoming an exhaustive checklist. A statutory maximum deserves weight but cannot make the separate constitutional inquiry conclusive in the Government's favor.

**The full demand is excessive; the actual $10,000 forfeiture is permissible.** On the supported findings, forfeiture of $100,348 is grossly disproportionate to the reporting offense, while the $10,000 exaction is not. Part III is joined by Stone-Zsela, Stevens, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer.

The district court credited lawful source and found no governmental financial loss. The reporting system suffered informational harm, and Mayzel's false statements support meaningful culpability; the civil forfeiture nevertheless rests on the reporting violation. His criminal reporting acquittal turned on failure to prove knowledge of the reporting duty beyond reasonable doubt and leaves the civil forfeitability judgment intact. The supported wrongdoing and harm do not justify confiscating the full fund. The dissent's reliance on the amount withheld and Congress's strong preventive interest does not authorize measuring that wrong against unproved laundering or other criminal activity.

The hypothetical fine maximum is $5,000 at level 6 or $30,000 at level 12. The lower level requires no knowledge or belief of unlawful proceeds, no reckless disregard of source, lawful proceeds, and intended lawful use; only lawful source was expressly found. The $10,000 exaction survives the constitutional comparison even under the lower benchmark, while full forfeiture fails under either. Thus the missing bracket findings do not require another proportionality hearing. The Court judges the actual exaction, without adopting the lower court's arithmetic compromise as a constitutional formula or a fixed ratio for other cases.

## Law After Decision

A legally substantive possessory interest may support a first-party challenge throughout the property it covers despite another person's title. In punitive reporting forfeiture, the claimant bears the gross-disproportionality burden under an actual-offense comparison and independent constitutional review. Here that comparison sustains $10,000 and requires return of the $90,348 balance.

## Separate Writings

Kennedy, joined by O'Connor and Scalia, agrees that Mayzel may protect his own interest and that the common proportionality and review rules govern. He would reverse the reduction and permit full forfeiture. The unreported amount measures the scale of a serious reporting wrong; the information system prevents offenses before the Government can identify their proceeds, and Congress may make forfeiture operate alongside ordinary fines. The smaller amount than in Bajakajian, Mayzel's supported deception and the unsettled entitlement to the lowest hypothetical fine distinguish the earlier application. In the dissent's view those matters leave gross disproportionality unproved, without assuming another crime or treating the $30,000 bracket as a finding.
