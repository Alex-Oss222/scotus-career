## Controlling holdings

**Controlling rule.** An arrest may rest on an offense different from, and not closely related to, the officer's announced offense if the facts actually known when the seizure occurred establish probable cause under that offense's elements. The alternative ground must justify the restraint actually imposed; later-acquired evidence cannot supply earlier probable cause, and greater detention remains subject to its independent constitutional limits.

**Authority.** O'Connor's opinion states the common governing rule and remand grounds joined by all nine participating Justices. The formal judgment distinction is stated in Opinion Topology.

**Controlling explanation.** The Court holds that objective legal authority turns on the contemporaneous facts and the applicable offense, rather than the officer's chosen label. *Whren v. United States*, which rejects investigative-motive and customary-enforcement additions to objective initial-stop authority, supplies the governing objective principle. *Maryland v. Pringle*, which requires a supported inference from facts known at arrest, preserves the actual factual and legal work that principle demands. A closely-related-offense requirement would make identical known facts support one officer's seizure and defeat another's merely because the officers named different offenses.

Alford's objection to post hoc justification identifies a real limit but does not justify the circuit's relation rule. The officers must identify an actual alternative offense and contemporaneously known facts sufficient to establish probable cause for it. A later discovery or a merely conceivable offense will not do. The Ninth Circuit excluded the impersonation and obstruction alternatives under its erroneous legal test; their actual application must now be decided on the proper factual assumptions, with genuinely necessary factual issues returned to the district court.

*Atwater v. City of Lago Vista*, which requires an actual enforcement function for added station transport and confinement for an observed fine-only misdemeanor, and *Ricci v. Village of Arlington Heights*, which supplies the corresponding civil-licensing rule, preserve independent assessment of greater restraint. In those settings, supported identification, appearance, safety, cessation of a continuing violation, protection of evidence or necessary processing may justify added custody; those possibilities are nonexclusive and not presumed, and neither a universal citation duty nor a least-intrusive-means rule follows. Any properly presented custody issue and personal immunity remain ordinary remand work under their governing rules. Immunity's legal-notice inquiry uses the November 22, 1997 conduct date.
