## Controlling holdings

The five Justices joining the Court's opinion directly support all four holdings in both dockets. Agreement rests on the actual statutory range and agency explanation, not on a conclusion that this classification is compelled.

### I. The FCC exercised actual delegated authority over this classification

**Holding and operative rule:** A reviewing court accepts a reasonable agency construction within delegated statutory ambiguity only when Congress conferred authority to make the relevant choice with the force of law and the agency issued its interpretation in exercise of that authority. The Communications Act supplies that authority for the FCC's nationwide declaratory classification here; formal procedure alone is neither necessary nor sufficient to establish the delegated range or validate the agency's choice.

**Controlling explanation:** Mead requires identification of the legal assignment and the mechanism actually exercising it. Sections 151 and 201(b), read within the Act and the implementing authority recognized in Iowa Utilities Board, entrust the Commission with administering these service definitions. The nationwide declaratory ruling exercises that authority. It differs from Mead's revisable, transaction-specific Customs letter.

The court must independently enforce the definitions and the delegation's boundaries before sustaining a reasonable administrative construction. Expertise, complexity, official endorsement or formal process cannot supply authority that Congress withheld. Conversely, the absence of one particular procedural form does not categorically defeat an otherwise established exercise of delegated authority. Barnhart and Yellow Transportation preserve this distinction between a genuine administrative choice and a violation of statutory limits. The present classification must still satisfy that second inquiry.

### II. Portland did not exhaust the interpretive choice Congress left to the Commission

**Holding and operative rule:** An earlier court of appeals' selection of a preferred statutory reading does not foreclose a different reasonable agency interpretation within a choice Congress actually delegated when the earlier holding and statute leave that choice open. A binding determination that the statute requires a particular answer remains a legal boundary; determining which kind of holding exists requires examining its substance rather than searching for an express ambiguity formula.

**Controlling explanation:** Chevron gives effect to Congress's allocation of genuine interpretive choice. A court's preferred answer cannot remove that allocation merely because the court considered the statute before the agency exercised its authority. Portland construed the cable transmission component without deciding the offering question as one having only a single lawful answer. Its treatment therefore does not itself defeat the FCC's later permissible construction.

The dissent argues that independent judicial interpretation fixes law and cannot later become an executive policy preference. That objection correctly distinguishes a judicially settled statutory boundary from approval of one available choice. The Court's rule preserves the boundary: it requires an actual delegation and a substantive examination of what the earlier court decided. It permits lawful administrative choice within the remaining range, rather than revision of the earlier judgment or disregard of a construction establishing the statute's only permissible meaning.

Neal preserved a particular controlling mixture-weight construction while leaving this general relationship open. Today's bounded circuit-precedent rule supplies the answer here. The distinct force of a Supreme Court construction is outside the presented Portland question; such a construction may settle statutory meaning and remove ambiguity. The Court does not decide that different question. Resolving the preserved circuit-precedent objection establishes the legal framework governing this order and the remand.

### III. The described integrated retail offering may be classified as an information service

**Holding and operative rule:** The FCC may reasonably treat the described retail cable-modem service as an integrated information-service offering rather than a separate public offering of telecommunications service. That permission depends on the actual end-user capabilities and their functional integration with transmission; merely bundling an independently offered transmission service with an unrelated information feature does not remove the transmission service from the statutory category.

**Controlling explanation:** Section 153(43) defines telecommunications as transmission of user-chosen information between user-specified points without changing its form or content as sent and received. Section 153(46) requires an offering of that transmission for a fee to the public, or to a class effectively available to the public, regardless of facilities. Section 153(20) separately covers the offering of information-generating, acquiring, storing, transforming, processing, retrieving, utilizing or availability capabilities through telecommunications, including electronic publishing. It excludes capabilities used to manage, control or operate a telecommunications system or manage a telecommunications service.

The statute thus contemplates information service using telecommunications without necessarily making every transmission input a separate public offering. The Commission's paragraphs 38–41 describe genuine end-user retrieval, storage, email and access functions integrated with cable transmission. Those functions go beyond internal network management. Optional nonuse of a feature does not alone remove it from the service offered.

The dissent identifies a real paid transmission pathway retaining the user's information unchanged. That technical component is material, but it does not conclusively establish the identity of the finished service offered to subscribers. On this account of functional integration, the Commission's construction falls within the statutory range. A telephone service does not escape Title II merely by being packaged with voicemail or another separate feature. The management exclusion likewise prevents routine internal network operations from turning an otherwise offered transmission service into an information service.

Gulf Power left this classification open; its attachment ruling supplies no contrary answer. Under section 153(44), common-carrier treatment attaches only to the extent the provider furnishes telecommunications service. The sustained classification resolves that predicate for this described offer.

### IV. The Commission gave a reasoned explanation for the cable and DSL distinction

**Holding and operative rule:** The Commission's differential treatment of the described cable offering and telephone-company DSL is not arbitrary and capricious on the reasons it supplied. An agency may address distinct regulatory histories incrementally within its statutory authority when it explains the material differences and its policy choice; a prior policy or technological resemblance does not alone compel identical treatment.

**Controlling explanation:** The Commission's paragraphs 42–44 identify the Computer II framework applied to telephone-company facilities, the historical bottleneck role of those carriers and its decision not to extend that framework automatically to the cable setting. It also addresses emerging broadband alternatives and investment and innovation objectives. Those are the agency's actual reasons for considering cable in a different regulatory setting, rather than an explanation supplied by the reviewing court.

Smiley permits adequately explained reconsideration within lawful authority while retaining review for unexplained change or disregard of legitimate reliance. The Commission's explanation meets that requirement here. The statutory classification must independently be permissible, as the preceding holding establishes; preferred regulatory consequences cannot cure a forbidden classification. The Court resolves the challenged 2002 cable treatment.

## Material published noncontrolling positions

#### B. Offered-service classification

The governing definitions concern different things. Section153(43) defines telecommunications as transmission of user-chosen information between user-specified points without changing its form or content as sent and received. Section153(46) requires an offering for a fee directly to the public or to a class effectively available to the public, regardless of facilities. Section153(20) covers the offered information-processing capabilities via telecommunications while excluding capabilities used for managing, controlling or operating a telecommunications system or managing a telecommunications service. Section153(44)'s carrier definition and qualifications remain as supplied in the independent freeze; common-carrier treatment extends only to providing telecommunications services. Neither a facilities label nor marketing alone supplies the answer.

The five reversal supporters sustain a permissible construction of the described retail service. The Commission's paragraphs38–41 describe genuine end-user information capabilities and their integration with transmission: retrieval, storage, email, access and related functions. The statute expressly contemplates information service provided via telecommunications; it does not necessarily make every such input a distinct public telecommunications offering. The management exception remains enforceable: internal network management alone does not transform transmission into information service. The described offer includes end-user capabilities beyond that exception.

The strongest contrary argument identifies a real paid transmission function whose information arrives unchanged even though optional processing accompanies it. The five answer that functional integration of this retail service permits the Commission's finished-offering classification; mere technical identification of a transmission input is not conclusive about the public service offered. Conversely, packaging an independent telephone transmission service with voicemail or an unrelated information feature would not erase the independent service. The rule is tied to the actual described offering rather than a categorical bundling exemption. Optional nonuse of a feature does not by itself remove that feature from what the provider offers.

Scalia, Souter and Ginsburg regard the actual transmission pathway sold to this subscriber as retaining an independent identity under the statute. They distinguish delivery of the finished information to the consumer from telecommunications an information provider merely uses upstream. Thus their rule does not classify every information service as a common carrier. A standalone sale is not a statutory prerequisite; selling a defined function with another function still offers it when its independent identity remains. Management, routing or incidental processing cannot erase a telecommunications service that otherwise satisfies the definition. This is the shared statutory ground in historical Part I, including its answer to the claim that the dissent would regulate every ISP.

The three also preserve Part I's remedial and statutory caution: any permissible deregulation through section160 must satisfy that provision's own conditions, and potential Title I ancillary powers cannot automatically be treated as an alternative authority to impose every common-carrier obligation on an entity classified outside telecommunications service. The carrier limitation in section153(44) remains relevant. They identify the difficulty without adjudicating a future access order that is not before the Court.

## Holdings

The five Justices joining the Court's opinion directly support all four holdings in both dockets. Agreement rests on the actual statutory range and agency explanation, not on a conclusion that this classification is compelled.

### I. The FCC exercised actual delegated authority over this classification

**Holding and operative rule:** A reviewing court accepts a reasonable agency construction within delegated statutory ambiguity only when Congress conferred authority to make the relevant choice with the force of law and the agency issued its interpretation in exercise of that authority. The Communications Act supplies that authority for the FCC's nationwide declaratory classification here; formal procedure alone is neither necessary nor sufficient to establish the delegated range or validate the agency's choice.

**Controlling explanation:** Mead requires identification of the legal assignment and the mechanism actually exercising it. Sections 151 and 201(b), read within the Act and the implementing authority recognized in Iowa Utilities Board, entrust the Commission with administering these service definitions. The nationwide declaratory ruling exercises that authority. It differs from Mead's revisable, transaction-specific Customs letter.

The court must independently enforce the definitions and the delegation's boundaries before sustaining a reasonable administrative construction. Expertise, complexity, official endorsement or formal process cannot supply authority that Congress withheld. Conversely, the absence of one particular procedural form does not categorically defeat an otherwise established exercise of delegated authority. Barnhart and Yellow Transportation preserve this distinction between a genuine administrative choice and a violation of statutory limits. The present classification must still satisfy that second inquiry.

### II. Portland did not exhaust the interpretive choice Congress left to the Commission

**Holding and operative rule:** An earlier court of appeals' selection of a preferred statutory reading does not foreclose a different reasonable agency interpretation within a choice Congress actually delegated when the earlier holding and statute leave that choice open. A binding determination that the statute requires a particular answer remains a legal boundary; determining which kind of holding exists requires examining its substance rather than searching for an express ambiguity formula.

**Controlling explanation:** Chevron gives effect to Congress's allocation of genuine interpretive choice. A court's preferred answer cannot remove that allocation merely because the court considered the statute before the agency exercised its authority. Portland construed the cable transmission component without deciding the offering question as one having only a single lawful answer. Its treatment therefore does not itself defeat the FCC's later permissible construction.

The dissent argues that independent judicial interpretation fixes law and cannot later become an executive policy preference. That objection correctly distinguishes a judicially settled statutory boundary from approval of one available choice. The Court's rule preserves the boundary: it requires an actual delegation and a substantive examination of what the earlier court decided. It permits lawful administrative choice within the remaining range, rather than revision of the earlier judgment or disregard of a construction establishing the statute's only permissible meaning.

Neal preserved a particular controlling mixture-weight construction while leaving this general relationship open. Today's bounded circuit-precedent rule supplies the answer here. The distinct force of a Supreme Court construction is outside the presented Portland question; such a construction may settle statutory meaning and remove ambiguity. The Court does not decide that different question. Resolving the preserved circuit-precedent objection establishes the legal framework governing this order and the remand.

### III. The described integrated retail offering may be classified as an information service

**Holding and operative rule:** The FCC may reasonably treat the described retail cable-modem service as an integrated information-service offering rather than a separate public offering of telecommunications service. That permission depends on the actual end-user capabilities and their functional integration with transmission; merely bundling an independently offered transmission service with an unrelated information feature does not remove the transmission service from the statutory category.

**Controlling explanation:** Section 153(43) defines telecommunications as transmission of user-chosen information between user-specified points without changing its form or content as sent and received. Section 153(46) requires an offering of that transmission for a fee to the public, or to a class effectively available to the public, regardless of facilities. Section 153(20) separately covers the offering of information-generating, acquiring, storing, transforming, processing, retrieving, utilizing or availability capabilities through telecommunications, including electronic publishing. It excludes capabilities used to manage, control or operate a telecommunications system or manage a telecommunications service.

The statute thus contemplates information service using telecommunications without necessarily making every transmission input a separate public offering. The Commission's paragraphs 38–41 describe genuine end-user retrieval, storage, email and access functions integrated with cable transmission. Those functions go beyond internal network management. Optional nonuse of a feature does not alone remove it from the service offered.

The dissent identifies a real paid transmission pathway retaining the user's information unchanged. That technical component is material, but it does not conclusively establish the identity of the finished service offered to subscribers. On this account of functional integration, the Commission's construction falls within the statutory range. A telephone service does not escape Title II merely by being packaged with voicemail or another separate feature. The management exclusion likewise prevents routine internal network operations from turning an otherwise offered transmission service into an information service.

Gulf Power left this classification open; its attachment ruling supplies no contrary answer. Under section 153(44), common-carrier treatment attaches only to the extent the provider furnishes telecommunications service. The sustained classification resolves that predicate for this described offer.

### IV. The Commission gave a reasoned explanation for the cable and DSL distinction

**Holding and operative rule:** The Commission's differential treatment of the described cable offering and telephone-company DSL is not arbitrary and capricious on the reasons it supplied. An agency may address distinct regulatory histories incrementally within its statutory authority when it explains the material differences and its policy choice; a prior policy or technological resemblance does not alone compel identical treatment.

**Controlling explanation:** The Commission's paragraphs 42–44 identify the Computer II framework applied to telephone-company facilities, the historical bottleneck role of those carriers and its decision not to extend that framework automatically to the cable setting. It also addresses emerging broadband alternatives and investment and innovation objectives. Those are the agency's actual reasons for considering cable in a different regulatory setting, rather than an explanation supplied by the reviewing court.

Smiley permits adequately explained reconsideration within lawful authority while retaining review for unexplained change or disregard of legitimate reliance. The Commission's explanation meets that requirement here. The statutory classification must independently be permissible, as the preceding holding establishes; preferred regulatory consequences cannot cure a forbidden classification. The Court resolves the challenged 2002 cable treatment.

## Precedent Treatment

- Chevron U.S.A. Inc. v. Natural Resources Defense Council, Inc. is applied to a reasonable choice within actual delegated interpretive authority, with independent enforcement of statutory boundaries.
- United States v. Mead Corp. supplies the actual-delegation and actual-exercise requirements; this nationwide declaratory mechanism differs from the revisable Customs letters. Formality remains evidence rather than a necessary or sufficient condition.
- AT&T Corp. v. Iowa Utilities Board supplies the Act's implementing-authority principle while preserving limits on individual rules.
- Barnhart v. Walton and Yellow Transportation, Inc. v. Michigan supply the distinction between a permissible delegated construction and the only construction Congress permits.
- Neal v. United States retains its particular binding mixture-weight construction and its express limit concerning the unresolved broader agency question. Today's prior-circuit rule resolves that separate issue at the stated scope.
- National Cable & Telecommunications Association v. Gulf Power Co. retains its pole-attachment holdings and express reservation of Internet-service classification.
- Smiley v. Citibank preserves lawful, adequately explained interpretive change and the independent arbitrary-and-capricious inquiry.
- AT&T Corp. v. City of Portland does not foreclose the later permissible FCC choice because it did not resolve the offering question as having only one lawful answer.

## Law After Decision

Courts must first identify the agency's actual delegated authority and independently enforce statutory limits. An earlier circuit interpretation selecting a preferred answer does not foreclose a later reasonable administrative choice where the holding and statute leave that delegated range open; fixed statutory boundaries continue to bind. For the described retail cable-modem service, the Commission may use the integrated-information-service classification, which leaves the offering outside mandatory Title II treatment under that choice. The Commission's actual explanation also sustains its challenged cable/DSL distinction. These holdings permit the specific classification rather than compel it or authorize a general bundling exemption.

## Separate Writings

**Justice Scalia, dissenting, Part I, joined by Chief Justice Stone-Zsela and Justices Souter and Ginsburg.** The cable subscriber purchases the transmission of chosen information between chosen points without a change in form or content. That paid retail function retains its statutory identity when sold with information capabilities. Congress did not require separate billing or a stand-alone sale before transmission is offered to the public.

The dissent distinguishes transmission delivered to the subscriber from transmission that an information provider merely uses upstream as an input. Its construction therefore does not make every Internet service provider a telecommunications carrier. Genuine information functions can coexist with an identifiable transmission offering; management, routing or incidental processing cannot erase a service satisfying the definition. The Commission's explanation supplies an account of integration, but the dissent finds the actual retail pathway legally distinct within the sold bundle.

Gulf Power left classification open, and Mead preserves the statutory boundary even when an agency acts within an assigned field. Investment or deregulation goals must be pursued through lawful powers, including section 160 forbearance. That provision requires findings that enforcement is unnecessary to ensure just and reasonable charges, practices, classifications or regulations free of unjust or unreasonable discrimination, unnecessary to protect consumers, and that forbearance is consistent with the public interest. The Commission must consider promotion of competitive market conditions, including enhanced competition among telecommunications providers. Section 160(d), subject to section 251(f), also withholds forbearance from sections 251(c) and 271 until their requirements are fully implemented. Potential Title I ancillary authority does not automatically supply every common-carrier obligation after classification outside telecommunications service; section 153(44)'s limit remains material. The dissent would affirm invalidation of the transmission exclusion and return lawful implementation to the FCC. The statutory error makes a separate final cable/DSL arbitrariness ruling unnecessary on this route.

**Justice Scalia, dissenting, Part II.** Scalia additionally distinguishes a court's authoritative independent construction of statutory meaning from judicial approval of one permissible administrative choice. The former fixes law subject to lawful judicial reconsideration; the latter leaves delegated alternatives available. A later agency cannot convert completed independent construction into a nonbinding preference merely because the earlier opinion did not declare the statute unambiguous. Chief Justice Stone-Zsela joins this distinction and its application to Portland.

Scalia alone adds that executive displacement of judicially fixed meaning raises the judicial-finality concern illustrated by Chicago & Southern Air Lines v. Waterman. He also disputes the need for a general prior-court rule here because this Court is not bound by Portland and can examine statutory permissibility directly. The majority's substantive, rather than formula-based, inquiry answers part of his concern but does not persuade him that an independent construction leaves executive revision available. His objection rests on those judicial-authority grounds; Neal's particular holding does not already dictate a universal prohibition. Souter and Ginsburg join neither Part II ground.

**Chief Justice Stone-Zsela, separately dissenting.** The Chief Justice joins the offered-transmission objection and the stated distinction between authoritative statutory meaning and an actually preserved delegated choice. The precedent question requires resolution because it is the Ninth Circuit's ground and the petitioners expressly seek authority to displace that construction. Chevron and Mead preserve Congress's real assignments; lawful judicial reconsideration supplies the route for changing an already adjudicated legal meaning.

On the statutory disposition he supports, the reviewing court should require an FCC implementation decision on a definite, enforceable schedule. The Commission must identify the applicable duties and any properly supported statutory forbearance, explain material technological and market distinctions, and justify a bounded transition if operations require one. A policy preference cannot preserve the rejected categorical exclusion indefinitely. The agency must make the assigned implementation choices within the corrected statutory category rather than receive judicially selected rates, access terms or network design.

## Procedure After Action

Both petitions return to the Ninth Circuit under the reversed common judgment. The FCC's challenged integrated-information-service classification and its stated cable/DSL rationale are sustained. Further proceedings must implement these holdings for the reviewed 2002 order.
