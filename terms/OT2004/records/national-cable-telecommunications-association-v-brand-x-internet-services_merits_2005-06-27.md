## Controlling holdings

The five Justices joining Parts II through IV of the Court's opinion directly support those holdings and the judgment in both dockets; eight Justices directly support Holding I. Agreement rests on the enacted definitions, the actual delegation and the force of the prior judicial construction, not on a policy preference about broadband regulation.

### I. Chevron and Mead govern only within actual delegated authority

**Holding and operative rule:** A reviewing court gives controlling force to an agency's construction of a statute it administers only when Congress delegated the relevant interpretive choice, the agency exercised that authority through a mechanism Congress authorized to carry the force of law, and the construction stays within the statutory limits. The court decides independently whether a delegation exists and how far it extends, what the statutory limits are, whether the agency actually exercised the delegated authority, and whether procedural and reasoned-decisionmaking requirements are met. The Communications Act delegates to the FCC the administration of these service definitions, and its nationwide declaratory ruling exercises that authority; whether the classification stays within the statutory limits is decided in Holdings II and III.

**Controlling explanation:** Mead requires identification of the legal assignment and the mechanism actually exercising it. Sections 151 and 201(b), read within the Act and the implementing authority recognized in Iowa Utilities Board, entrust the Commission with administering these service definitions. The nationwide declaratory ruling exercises that authority. It differs from Mead's revisable, transaction-specific Customs letter, and formal procedure is evidence of exercise, neither necessary nor sufficient by itself.

Expertise, responsibility for a complex field or the presence of ambiguity does not alone authorize every interpretation an agency prefers. Barnhart and Yellow Transportation sustain genuine delegated choices within legal boundaries, and MCI enforces the limits of a delegated power against a reading that would remove a statutory requirement's essential operation. Chevron is retained on those terms: a court gives effect to a reasonable choice Congress actually left to the agency and enforces the line Congress drew. Mead, Barnhart and MCI remain governing law. The present classification must still satisfy the statutory limits.

### II. An authoritative judicial construction binds within the judicial hierarchy

**Holding and operative rule:** An authoritative judicial construction of a statute's legal content binds within the judicial hierarchy that issued it until Congress changes the statute or a court with authority to do so reconsiders the construction: a court of appeals' construction binds within its circuit, and this Court's construction binds throughout the federal system. The absence of an express statement that the statute is unambiguous does not strip the construction of that authority. A binding construction of statutory meaning differs from a judicial recognition that Congress delegated a range of choice: the agency must comply with the former and may change policy within the latter. Where a court of appeals has actually settled a classification, an agency that departs from it must show that the earlier decision left an identifiable delegated choice open. The remedy for a mistaken precedent is judicial reconsideration, by the court of appeals en banc or by this Court; the agency cannot extinguish its force.

**Controlling explanation:** Chevron gives effect to Congress's allocation of genuine interpretive choice; it does not make a court's completed construction of statutory meaning provisional. The question is what the earlier court actually decided, examined by substance rather than by a search for a formula. If the decision recognized a range Congress left to the agency, the agency may choose within it. If the decision fixed the statute's legal content, the agency is bound within that hierarchy and must seek judicial reconsideration to change it. Lechmere requires a later agency approach to respect a governing judicial statutory construction, and Neal held that a later administrative measure did not displace Chapman's construction of §841. Today's rule extends that principle to agency interpretations generally, at the reach stated, while preserving actual delegated choice.

Portland construed §153(46) and held that the cable provider's transmission to its subscribers is a telecommunications service. It identified no delegated choice left open on that classification, and the Commission has not shown that one was. The Ninth Circuit therefore correctly treated Portland as binding. The question requires decision because Portland is the Ninth Circuit's ground and the petitioners expressly seek authority to displace it. This Court is not bound by Portland and reaches the same classification independently in Holding III.

The dissent contends that this rule lets the first court to construe a statute exhaust a choice Congress gave the agency. It does not. A decision that leaves a delegated range open constrains no choice within that range, and a decision that fixes statutory meaning remains subject to reconsideration by the courts Congress empowered to review it. The contrary rule would make adjudicated meaning yield to each later administrative preference and would make a circuit's law depend on whether its earlier court used a particular adjective.

### III. The publicly offered transmission function is telecommunications service

**Holding and operative rule:** Telecommunications is transmission, between or among points the customer specifies, of information of the customer's choosing, without change in form or content; a telecommunications service is the offering of telecommunications for a fee directly to the public or to a class effectively available to the public. An information service is the offering of information capabilities via telecommunications, and its exclusion of capabilities used to manage, control or operate a telecommunications system or manage a telecommunications service applies. Bundling information capabilities with an independently offered transmission function does not extinguish the transmission's statutory character; the question is what the provider actually offers. Email, storage or similar features do not transform the transmission, and separate itemization is not required. The decisive question is whether customers are substantively offered public transmission for a fee. Cable-modem providers offer their subscribers that transmission, so the FCC's classification is set aside insofar as it excludes the publicly offered transmission function from telecommunications-service treatment.

**Controlling explanation:** Sections 153(43) and (46) focus on transmission, the customer's choice of content and destination, and an offering to the public for a fee, not on separate billing or a stand-alone sale. The subscriber pays for the ability to send and receive information of the subscriber's choosing between points the subscriber selects across the cable network, and that information arrives unchanged. The Commission's paragraphs 38–41 describe genuine retrieval, storage, email and access capabilities, and those capabilities are information services under section 153(20). They coexist with the offered transmission; they do not change the form or content of what the subscriber sends and receives. A telephone service does not escape Title II by being packaged with voicemail, and the cable transmission function does not escape it by being packaged with email. The management exclusion keeps routine network operations on the telecommunications side; it cannot turn an offered transmission service into an information service.

AT&T Corp. v. City of Portland described the offering as an information-service function provided together with a telecommunications pipeline, and the Court adopts that account. The line between an offering and an input preserves Congress's distinction: an information-service provider that merely uses telecommunications obtained from another carrier does not thereby offer telecommunications to the public. The holding classifies the transmission a facilities-based provider sells to its subscribers, not every information service that uses transmission.

The Commission's construction cannot be sustained as a choice within delegated ambiguity. The delegation recognized in Holding I does not extend to excluding from the statutory category a function the definitions cover, and complexity and the Commission's policy objectives do not enlarge the range Congress left open. Gulf Power reserved this classification; this holding resolves it. Under section 153(44), common-carrier treatment attaches only to the extent the provider furnishes telecommunications service, which here means the transmission function; the accompanying information capabilities remain information services.

### IV. The Commission retains forbearance and implementation authority within the statutory category

**Holding and operative rule:** Classifying the transmission function as telecommunications service does not apply every Title II obligation permanently. Section 160(a) directs the Commission to forbear from applying a regulation or provision of the Act to a telecommunications carrier or service, or a class of them, in any or some geographic markets, when it determines that enforcement is not necessary to ensure just, reasonable and not unjustly or unreasonably discriminatory charges, practices, classifications or regulations, is not necessary to protect consumers, and that forbearance is consistent with the public interest, considering competitive effects under section 160(b). The Commission may forbear on those findings and must forbear when it makes them, subject to section 160(d)'s limitation for sections 251(c) and 271, itself subject to section 251(f). It may not redefine a covered service out of the statutory category to avoid those conditions.

**Controlling explanation:** Congress addressed the regulatory consequences of classification through forbearance, not through an exemption from the definitions. MCI holds that a power to modify statutory requirements does not permit removing their essential operation for a regulated class; a classification removing a covered service from Title II would accomplish without findings what section 160 permits only on them. Iowa Utilities Board preserves the Commission's national authority to implement the Act, and that authority remains available within the statutory category. Potential Title I ancillary authority does not automatically supply every common-carrier obligation for a service classified outside telecommunications service, and section 153(44)'s limit remains material.

Because the classification fails at the statutory boundary, the Court does not decide the challengers' separate objection that the Commission inadequately explained its different treatment of cable and telephone-company DSL. The Commission may address material technological and market distinctions in lawful classification, implementation and forbearance proceedings, on its own findings. The judgment orders no particular access regime, rates, net-neutrality rules or penalties. The Court resolves the challenged 2002 cable classification and the authority governing its implementation.

No access regime, rates, net-neutrality rules or penalties are ordered.

## Material published noncontrolling positions

**Justice Breyer, concurring.** Breyer joins the Court's opinion in full. Congress's assignment, not expertise or complexity, supplies an agency's interpretive authority; formal procedure is neither necessary nor sufficient to establish it, and the particular question must be one Congress left to the agency. The definitions turn on what the provider actually offers. This classification lies close to the boundary, but subscribers are offered transmission of their chosen information for a fee, and the Commission's account of integration does not move that function outside the definition. Within the statutory category the Commission keeps broad discretion: it decides which duties apply and how they operate, makes the technical and market findings section 160 requires, and must forbear when it makes them. Part II respects actual delegation because an earlier decision that left a choice open does not constrain it. The opinion adopts no exception based on describing a question as important and no rule that an agency loses authority whenever a court speaks first.

**Justice Scalia, concurring in part.** Scalia joins Parts II, III and IV and the judgment, but not Part I. As his Mead dissent explained, he would give controlling reasonable-choice treatment to an agency's authoritative official interpretation within an assigned statutory field rather than ask whether Congress authorized the particular mechanism to carry the force of law. That difference does not affect this case: the Commission's nationwide declaratory ruling is its authoritative position on any view, and the statutory definitions, which bind an agency even within an assigned field, exclude the classification. He adds that executive displacement of a judicially fixed statutory meaning would raise the judicial-finality concern illustrated by Chicago & Southern Air Lines v. Waterman: a completed construction by an Article III court may not be overturned by the Executive, and its force rests on judicial power as well as on precedent. Neal's particular holding does not itself dictate a universal prohibition, and he joins Part II's bounded rule; the finality ground is his alone.

**Justice Thomas, dissenting, joined by Justices Stevens, O'Connor and Kennedy.** The dissenters join Part I and would reverse in both dockets. An earlier court of appeals' selection of a preferred statutory reading does not, in their view, foreclose a different reasonable agency interpretation within a choice Congress actually delegated when the earlier holding and the statute leave that choice open. A binding determination that the statute requires a particular answer remains a legal boundary, and which kind of holding exists depends on its substance rather than on an express ambiguity formula. Portland construed the transmission component without deciding the offering question as one with a single lawful answer, so it did not foreclose the Commission's construction; placing the burden on the agency gives the first circuit to rule a choice Congress assigned to the Commission. The dissent's position concerns circuit precedent; the different force of this Court's constructions, which may settle statutory meaning and remove ambiguity, is not at issue.

On classification, the statute contemplates information service provided via telecommunications without making every transmission input a separate public offering. The Commission's paragraphs 38–41 describe genuine retrieval, storage, email and access functions integrated with cable transmission and going beyond internal network management; on that account the finished offering is an information service within the statutory range. A telephone service packaged with voicemail would keep its separate identity, but this offering is functionally integrated, and Gulf Power left the question open. The dissenters would also reject the cable/DSL arbitrariness objection: the Commission's paragraphs 42–44 rely on the Computer II framework for telephone-company facilities, those carriers' bottleneck history, emerging broadband alternatives and investment objectives, and Smiley permits that explained distinction within lawful authority. They would sustain the classification as permissible, not compelled, and adopt no general bundling exemption.

## Event

National Cable & Telecommunications Association v. Brand X Internet Services, No. 04-277; FCC v. Brand X Internet Services, No. 04-281. Argued March 29, 2005; decided June 27, 2005. On writs of certiorari to the Ninth Circuit, reviewing the same consolidated judgment, 345 F.3d 1120, that set aside the FCC's exclusion of telecommunications service from its cable-modem classification.

The Commission's 2002 declaratory ruling, 17 FCC Rcd. 4798, treats the described retail cable-modem offering as an integrated information service. The cable association and federal petitioners seek reversal and restoration of the classification. The challengers seek continued invalidation and telecommunications-service treatment.

## Holdings

The five Justices joining Parts II through IV of the Court's opinion directly support those holdings and the judgment in both dockets; eight Justices directly support Holding I. Agreement rests on the enacted definitions, the actual delegation and the force of the prior judicial construction, not on a policy preference about broadband regulation.

### I. Chevron and Mead govern only within actual delegated authority

**Holding and operative rule:** A reviewing court gives controlling force to an agency's construction of a statute it administers only when Congress delegated the relevant interpretive choice, the agency exercised that authority through a mechanism Congress authorized to carry the force of law, and the construction stays within the statutory limits. The court decides independently whether a delegation exists and how far it extends, what the statutory limits are, whether the agency actually exercised the delegated authority, and whether procedural and reasoned-decisionmaking requirements are met. The Communications Act delegates to the FCC the administration of these service definitions, and its nationwide declaratory ruling exercises that authority; whether the classification stays within the statutory limits is decided in Holdings II and III.

**Controlling explanation:** Mead requires identification of the legal assignment and the mechanism actually exercising it. Sections 151 and 201(b), read within the Act and the implementing authority recognized in Iowa Utilities Board, entrust the Commission with administering these service definitions. The nationwide declaratory ruling exercises that authority. It differs from Mead's revisable, transaction-specific Customs letter, and formal procedure is evidence of exercise, neither necessary nor sufficient by itself.

Expertise, responsibility for a complex field or the presence of ambiguity does not alone authorize every interpretation an agency prefers. Barnhart and Yellow Transportation sustain genuine delegated choices within legal boundaries, and MCI enforces the limits of a delegated power against a reading that would remove a statutory requirement's essential operation. Chevron is retained on those terms: a court gives effect to a reasonable choice Congress actually left to the agency and enforces the line Congress drew. Mead, Barnhart and MCI remain governing law. The present classification must still satisfy the statutory limits.

### II. An authoritative judicial construction binds within the judicial hierarchy

**Holding and operative rule:** An authoritative judicial construction of a statute's legal content binds within the judicial hierarchy that issued it until Congress changes the statute or a court with authority to do so reconsiders the construction: a court of appeals' construction binds within its circuit, and this Court's construction binds throughout the federal system. The absence of an express statement that the statute is unambiguous does not strip the construction of that authority. A binding construction of statutory meaning differs from a judicial recognition that Congress delegated a range of choice: the agency must comply with the former and may change policy within the latter. Where a court of appeals has actually settled a classification, an agency that departs from it must show that the earlier decision left an identifiable delegated choice open. The remedy for a mistaken precedent is judicial reconsideration, by the court of appeals en banc or by this Court; the agency cannot extinguish its force.

**Controlling explanation:** Chevron gives effect to Congress's allocation of genuine interpretive choice; it does not make a court's completed construction of statutory meaning provisional. The question is what the earlier court actually decided, examined by substance rather than by a search for a formula. If the decision recognized a range Congress left to the agency, the agency may choose within it. If the decision fixed the statute's legal content, the agency is bound within that hierarchy and must seek judicial reconsideration to change it. Lechmere requires a later agency approach to respect a governing judicial statutory construction, and Neal held that a later administrative measure did not displace Chapman's construction of §841. Today's rule extends that principle to agency interpretations generally, at the reach stated, while preserving actual delegated choice.

Portland construed §153(46) and held that the cable provider's transmission to its subscribers is a telecommunications service. It identified no delegated choice left open on that classification, and the Commission has not shown that one was. The Ninth Circuit therefore correctly treated Portland as binding. The question requires decision because Portland is the Ninth Circuit's ground and the petitioners expressly seek authority to displace it. This Court is not bound by Portland and reaches the same classification independently in Holding III.

The dissent contends that this rule lets the first court to construe a statute exhaust a choice Congress gave the agency. It does not. A decision that leaves a delegated range open constrains no choice within that range, and a decision that fixes statutory meaning remains subject to reconsideration by the courts Congress empowered to review it. The contrary rule would make adjudicated meaning yield to each later administrative preference and would make a circuit's law depend on whether its earlier court used a particular adjective.

### III. The publicly offered transmission function is telecommunications service

**Holding and operative rule:** Telecommunications is transmission, between or among points the customer specifies, of information of the customer's choosing, without change in form or content; a telecommunications service is the offering of telecommunications for a fee directly to the public or to a class effectively available to the public. An information service is the offering of information capabilities via telecommunications, and its exclusion of capabilities used to manage, control or operate a telecommunications system or manage a telecommunications service applies. Bundling information capabilities with an independently offered transmission function does not extinguish the transmission's statutory character; the question is what the provider actually offers. Email, storage or similar features do not transform the transmission, and separate itemization is not required. The decisive question is whether customers are substantively offered public transmission for a fee. Cable-modem providers offer their subscribers that transmission, so the FCC's classification is set aside insofar as it excludes the publicly offered transmission function from telecommunications-service treatment.

**Controlling explanation:** Sections 153(43) and (46) focus on transmission, the customer's choice of content and destination, and an offering to the public for a fee, not on separate billing or a stand-alone sale. The subscriber pays for the ability to send and receive information of the subscriber's choosing between points the subscriber selects across the cable network, and that information arrives unchanged. The Commission's paragraphs 38–41 describe genuine retrieval, storage, email and access capabilities, and those capabilities are information services under section 153(20). They coexist with the offered transmission; they do not change the form or content of what the subscriber sends and receives. A telephone service does not escape Title II by being packaged with voicemail, and the cable transmission function does not escape it by being packaged with email. The management exclusion keeps routine network operations on the telecommunications side; it cannot turn an offered transmission service into an information service.

AT&T Corp. v. City of Portland described the offering as an information-service function provided together with a telecommunications pipeline, and the Court adopts that account. The line between an offering and an input preserves Congress's distinction: an information-service provider that merely uses telecommunications obtained from another carrier does not thereby offer telecommunications to the public. The holding classifies the transmission a facilities-based provider sells to its subscribers, not every information service that uses transmission.

The Commission's construction cannot be sustained as a choice within delegated ambiguity. The delegation recognized in Holding I does not extend to excluding from the statutory category a function the definitions cover, and complexity and the Commission's policy objectives do not enlarge the range Congress left open. Gulf Power reserved this classification; this holding resolves it. Under section 153(44), common-carrier treatment attaches only to the extent the provider furnishes telecommunications service, which here means the transmission function; the accompanying information capabilities remain information services.

### IV. The Commission retains forbearance and implementation authority within the statutory category

**Holding and operative rule:** Classifying the transmission function as telecommunications service does not apply every Title II obligation permanently. Section 160(a) directs the Commission to forbear from applying a regulation or provision of the Act to a telecommunications carrier or service, or a class of them, in any or some geographic markets, when it determines that enforcement is not necessary to ensure just, reasonable and not unjustly or unreasonably discriminatory charges, practices, classifications or regulations, is not necessary to protect consumers, and that forbearance is consistent with the public interest, considering competitive effects under section 160(b). The Commission may forbear on those findings and must forbear when it makes them, subject to section 160(d)'s limitation for sections 251(c) and 271, itself subject to section 251(f). It may not redefine a covered service out of the statutory category to avoid those conditions.

**Controlling explanation:** Congress addressed the regulatory consequences of classification through forbearance, not through an exemption from the definitions. MCI holds that a power to modify statutory requirements does not permit removing their essential operation for a regulated class; a classification removing a covered service from Title II would accomplish without findings what section 160 permits only on them. Iowa Utilities Board preserves the Commission's national authority to implement the Act, and that authority remains available within the statutory category. Potential Title I ancillary authority does not automatically supply every common-carrier obligation for a service classified outside telecommunications service, and section 153(44)'s limit remains material.

Because the classification fails at the statutory boundary, the Court does not decide the challengers' separate objection that the Commission inadequately explained its different treatment of cable and telephone-company DSL. The Commission may address material technological and market distinctions in lawful classification, implementation and forbearance proceedings, on its own findings. The judgment orders no particular access regime, rates, net-neutrality rules or penalties. The Court resolves the challenged 2002 cable classification and the authority governing its implementation.

## Precedent Treatment

- Chevron U.S.A. Inc. v. Natural Resources Defense Council, Inc. is retained, subject to actual delegation: courts give effect to a reasonable choice Congress actually left to the agency and independently enforce statutory boundaries.
- United States v. Mead Corp. is preserved: controlling force requires actual delegation and an authorized force-of-law mechanism; this nationwide declaratory mechanism differs from the revisable Customs letters, and formality remains evidence rather than a necessary or sufficient condition.
- Barnhart v. Walton and Yellow Transportation, Inc. v. Michigan are preserved for the distinction between a permissible delegated construction and the only construction Congress permits.
- MCI Telecommunications Corp. v. American Telephone & Telegraph Co. is applied: a power to modify statutory requirements does not permit the Commission to remove a covered service from the statutory category.
- FDA v. Brown & Williamson Tobacco Corp. is preserved: an agency cannot obtain authority by construing broad terms to reach what the connected statutory requirements do not permit.
- AT&T Corp. v. Iowa Utilities Board is preserved for the Commission's national implementing authority, subject to the limits on particular rules.
- Lechmere, Inc. v. NLRB is preserved: a later agency approach must respect a governing judicial statutory construction.
- Neal v. United States is extended: its holding that a later administrative measure did not displace Chapman's construction of §841 supports the binding force of an authoritative judicial construction within the judicial hierarchy, subject to an identifiable delegated choice the construction left open. Neal's particular mixture-weight construction is unchanged.
- National Cable & Telecommunications Association v. Gulf Power Co. retains its pole-attachment holdings; its reserved Internet-service classification is resolved for the offered transmission function.
- Smiley v. Citibank preserves adequately explained interpretive change within lawful authority; the challengers' cable/DSL explanation objection is not decided.
- AT&T Corp. v. City of Portland binds within the Ninth Circuit, and its account of an information-service function provided with a telecommunications pipeline is adopted.

## Law After Decision

Controlling force for an agency's statutory construction requires an actual delegation of the relevant choice, its exercise through an authorized force-of-law mechanism and a construction within the statutory limits, each decided independently by the reviewing court. An authoritative judicial construction of statutory meaning binds within the judicial hierarchy that issued it, a court of appeals' construction within its circuit and this Court's throughout the federal system, until lawfully displaced; an agency departing from an appellate court's settled classification must show that the earlier decision left an identifiable delegated choice open, and the remedy for a mistaken precedent is judicial reconsideration. A provider offers telecommunications service when customers are substantively offered public transmission of information of their choosing between points they specify, for a fee; bundling information capabilities with that independently offered transmission does not change its statutory character, and separate itemization is not required. The FCC's classification of cable-modem service is set aside insofar as it excludes the publicly offered transmission function. The Commission keeps its implementation authority and must forbear under section 160 when it makes the required findings, but it cannot redefine a covered service out of the statutory category. No access regime, rates, net-neutrality rules or penalties are ordered.

## Separate Writings

**Justice Breyer, concurring.** Breyer joins the Court's opinion in full. Congress's assignment, not expertise or complexity, supplies an agency's interpretive authority; formal procedure is neither necessary nor sufficient to establish it, and the particular question must be one Congress left to the agency. The definitions turn on what the provider actually offers. This classification lies close to the boundary, but subscribers are offered transmission of their chosen information for a fee, and the Commission's account of integration does not move that function outside the definition. Within the statutory category the Commission keeps broad discretion: it decides which duties apply and how they operate, makes the technical and market findings section 160 requires, and must forbear when it makes them. Part II respects actual delegation because an earlier decision that left a choice open does not constrain it. The opinion adopts no exception based on describing a question as important and no rule that an agency loses authority whenever a court speaks first.

**Justice Scalia, concurring in part.** Scalia joins Parts II, III and IV and the judgment, but not Part I. As his Mead dissent explained, he would give controlling reasonable-choice treatment to an agency's authoritative official interpretation within an assigned statutory field rather than ask whether Congress authorized the particular mechanism to carry the force of law. That difference does not affect this case: the Commission's nationwide declaratory ruling is its authoritative position on any view, and the statutory definitions, which bind an agency even within an assigned field, exclude the classification. He adds that executive displacement of a judicially fixed statutory meaning would raise the judicial-finality concern illustrated by Chicago & Southern Air Lines v. Waterman: a completed construction by an Article III court may not be overturned by the Executive, and its force rests on judicial power as well as on precedent. Neal's particular holding does not itself dictate a universal prohibition, and he joins Part II's bounded rule; the finality ground is his alone.

**Justice Thomas, dissenting, joined by Justices Stevens, O'Connor and Kennedy.** The dissenters join Part I and would reverse in both dockets. An earlier court of appeals' selection of a preferred statutory reading does not, in their view, foreclose a different reasonable agency interpretation within a choice Congress actually delegated when the earlier holding and the statute leave that choice open. A binding determination that the statute requires a particular answer remains a legal boundary, and which kind of holding exists depends on its substance rather than on an express ambiguity formula. Portland construed the transmission component without deciding the offering question as one with a single lawful answer, so it did not foreclose the Commission's construction; placing the burden on the agency gives the first circuit to rule a choice Congress assigned to the Commission. The dissent's position concerns circuit precedent; the different force of this Court's constructions, which may settle statutory meaning and remove ambiguity, is not at issue.

On classification, the statute contemplates information service provided via telecommunications without making every transmission input a separate public offering. The Commission's paragraphs 38–41 describe genuine retrieval, storage, email and access functions integrated with cable transmission and going beyond internal network management; on that account the finished offering is an information service within the statutory range. A telephone service packaged with voicemail would keep its separate identity, but this offering is functionally integrated, and Gulf Power left the question open. The dissenters would also reject the cable/DSL arbitrariness objection: the Commission's paragraphs 42–44 rely on the Computer II framework for telephone-company facilities, those carriers' bottleneck history, emerging broadband alternatives and investment objectives, and Smiley permits that explained distinction within lawful authority. They would sustain the classification as permissible, not compelled, and adopt no general bundling exemption.

## Procedure After Action

Both petitions return to the Ninth Circuit under the affirmed common judgment. The FCC's classification is set aside insofar as it excludes the publicly offered transmission function from telecommunications-service treatment. The Commission may undertake lawful classification, implementation and forbearance proceedings for the described offering; no access regime, rates, net-neutrality rules or penalties are ordered, and the challengers' separate cable/DSL explanation objection is not decided.
