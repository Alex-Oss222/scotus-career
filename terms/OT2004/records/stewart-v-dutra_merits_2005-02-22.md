## Holdings

### Practical water-transport capability determines vessel status

**Holding and operative rule:** A watercraft is a vessel under the LHWCA's integrated vessel provisions and the linked seaman inquiry when it is used, or practically capable of being used, to transport people or things over water; transportation need not be its primary purpose, the craft need not propel itself, and motion at the moment of injury is unnecessary. Permanently fixed or withdrawn structures whose transportation capacity is merely theoretical fall outside this rule, but the Super Scoop's actual waterborne movements carrying equipment and workers satisfy it.

**Authority:** Scalia's opinion, joined in full by all eight other Justices, controls this vessel rule and its application.

**Controlling explanation:** Section 3 of Title 1 covers use or capability as a means of water transportation. Its terms do not restrict a vessel to a transportation business or require a voyage at the instant of injury. The LHWCA identifies the vessel's relevant owners and operators without supplying a different physical-characteristics definition. Practical capability gives the statutory term a concrete limit: flotation or an imagined possibility of moving a permanently fixed structure is insufficient.

The Super Scoop regularly moved thirty to fifty feet while dredging, carrying its machinery and crew, and tugs moved it over long distances. Its anchors and cables supplied shorter movement. Its construction function and reliance on external propulsion therefore do not remove its actual transport capacity. The stop associated with a scow's repair was temporary, rather than withdrawal from service.

Dutra argues that this reading erases the distinction between maritime crews and people employed on floating workplaces. The distinct worker and claim requirements preserve that distinction. Southwest Marine, Inc. v. Gizoni separates occupational labels from actual Jones Act status. Chandris, Inc. v. Latsis directs attention to the employment relationship and distinguishes temporary repair from withdrawal; it supplies no primary-purpose exclusion for this operational dredge. Harbor Tug & Barge Co. v. Papai likewise separates the vessel relationship from a job label or momentary docking. Those precedents retain their respective worker-status functions while the Court now resolves the craft-definition question. The vessel premise supporting the Jones Act judgment is legally erroneous.

### The mandate follows the ground of each claim judgment

**Holding and operative rule:** The Jones Act judgment must be reversed because it rests on the erroneous vessel exclusion, and the lower court must decide the genuinely remaining employment-connection, proper defendant-capacity, negligence and remedial issues under the corrected classification while honoring Dutra's summary-judgment acknowledgments. The independent section 905(b) judgment remains operative because it assumed vessel status and its separate capacity ground lies outside the granted question.

**Authority:** The same unanimous Court opinion directly controls this disposition and remand.

**Controlling explanation:** Harbor Tug requires separate treatment of seaman-dependent claims and a distinct vessel-negligence judgment. Its vessel-negligence appeal remained unaddressed below; here the First Circuit already decided that separate appeal. Correcting the vessel premise therefore reopens the Jones Act branch while leaving the independent capacity judgment in place.

For its Jones Act motion, Dutra acknowledged Stewart's crew membership, ninety-nine percent service aboard the dredge and contribution to its function. The lower court must give those acknowledgments their procedural effect when identifying what remains to be adjudicated. A renewed vessel exclusion or invented dispute contradicting those motion facts cannot support summary judgment. Any remaining independent legal ground may be addressed, and genuinely disputed liability or remedy facts receive ordinary adjudication. This gives Stewart the further proceedings warranted by the identified error while preserving the separate judgment's actual scope.
