## Holdings

### The ADEA permits an identified practice’s discriminatory effects to be challenged

**Holding and operative rule.** Section 623(a)(2) permits employees to challenge an identified neutral employment practice that causes protected-age disadvantage in employment opportunities or status, without proving that age motivated the practice. The employees must identify the particular practice and establish the causal adverse effect; an age imbalance alone does not identify its cause.

**Authority.** Chief Justice Stone-Zsela and Justices Stevens, Scalia, Souter, Ginsburg and Breyer support this operative rule. Chief Justice Stone-Zsela and Justices Stevens, Souter, Ginsburg and Breyer join the Court’s independent statutory construction and explanation below. Justice Scalia reaches recognition through the agency ground stated in his concurrence.

**Controlling explanation.** The subsection addresses limitations, segregation or classifications that adversely affect employment opportunities or employee status. Griggs v. Duke Power Co. supplies the established effects-based reading of materially parallel language. The ADEA’s additional reasonable-factors qualification gives that reading a distinct limit suitable to this statute: an adverse effect associated with age does not make a reasonable nonage differentiation unlawful.

The City and the concurrence in the judgment read the because-of-age language as requiring a motivated age decision in every case, with the reasonable-factors clause merely confirming a safe harbor. That reading does not give the effects-oriented subsection and its express qualification their separate work. The necessary causal connection is an age-linked adverse effect from the identified practice; actual age motivation remains the question for disparate treatment.

Hazen Paper Co. v. Biggins requires age motivation for intentional treatment and recognizes the difference between age and years of service, including deliberate use of service as a proxy. It expressly reserved disparate impact. General Dynamics Land Systems, Inc. v. Cline determines the direction of statutory protection, not whether an effects-based mechanism exists. Wards Cove Packing Co. v. Atonio supplies the requirement to identify the actual challenged practice and connect it to the disparity. The 1991 amendments to Title VII did not amend the ADEA; their different provisions cannot be transposed into this Act. The EEOC’s considered recognition of impact liability supports the statutory reading without supplying the Court’s independent ground.

### Reasonable nonage factors sustain this compensation plan

**Holding and operative rule.** Under §623(f)(1), a differentiation based on reasonable factors other than age is outside disparate-impact liability. Reasonableness concerns the actual nonage factor and its relationship to the employment objective; it does not require business necessity or selection of the least disparate workable alternative. On this record, rank, tenure, existing salary and adjustment toward regional compensation levels supply reasonable nonage factors even assuming that the challenged allocation caused the reported age disparity.

**Authority.** Chief Justice Stone-Zsela and Justices Stevens, Scalia, Souter, Ginsburg and Breyer join this statutory standard and its application in the Court’s opinion.

**Controlling explanation.** Age can correlate with experience, service and compensation without making those characteristics age itself. Congress expressly preserved reasonable nonage differentiation. Griggs supplies the effects analogy, but the ADEA’s enacted qualification controls the justification required here. The EEOC regulation’s business-necessity language cannot replace that statutory standard.

The City sought to bring starting police salaries toward the regional average. A survey of comparable southeastern communities supplied wage ranges for five ranks, divided into steps and half-steps; each employee entered the lowest step that still produced at least a two-percent raise. Employees with lower existing salaries could receive larger percentage increases from the adjustment. Officers in the two highest ranks, all over forty, received larger dollar raises despite smaller percentages. Some officers over forty were also in the shorter-tenure group.

These actual mechanics connect the factors to the compensation objective. The statistics—66.2 percent of officers below forty and 45.3 percent above forty receiving raises exceeding ten percent—do not make that method unreasonable. A demand for equal percentages or another workable allocation does not defeat a reasonable nonage justification. The City therefore prevails on the impact claim on the established plan facts, assuming the claimed causal disparity.

## Law After Decision

Employees may pursue effects-based claims under §623(a)(2) by proving an identified neutral practice’s causal protected-age disadvantage. Employers’ actual nonage factors are assessed for reasonableness in relation to their employment objectives, without a universal least-disparate-alternative requirement. Ordinary job criteria are therefore challengeable, while reasonable use of nonage factors remains lawful. Deliberate use of tenure as an age proxy remains governed by intentional-treatment law.

## Separate Writings

Justice Scalia concurs in the stated portions and the judgment. Congress gave the EEOC authority to implement the ADEA through §628, and its longstanding national interpretation recognizes restrictions on adverse-impact practices. He accepts the Court’s textual analysis as showing that interpretation is permissible, rather than as his independent judicial construction. The delegated national interpretation differs from the advisory response in Christensen and transaction-specific classification letters in Mead. Its recognition of impact liability and its use of business necessity are distinct propositions: the former is reasonable; the latter conflicts with the enacted reasonable-factors standard. He therefore joins the statutory justification rule and its application.

O'Connor, joined by Kennedy and Thomas, concurs in the judgment on the ground that the ADEA prohibits age-based employment decisions, not an additional unintentional-effects wrong. The effects words describe harmful forms of age discrimination; the reasonable-factors clause confirms a safe harbor for nonage criteria. The Act’s structure and the legitimate correlation of age with tenure and compensation counsel against transferring Griggs from Title VII. This writing also rejects binding reliance on the EEOC interpretation because it principally explains the exemption rather than construing the prohibitory subsection, and misstates the exemption through business necessity. Conditionally, if impact claims are recognized, these Justices accept particular-practice specificity and the narrower reasonable-factors justification; under their Wards Cove reading the employee must disprove the employer’s produced nonage explanation.

Chief Justice Stone-Zsela joins the Court and places the statutory reasonable-factors defense on the employer. The employees must prove the particular practice, protected-age disadvantage and causal connection by the ordinary civil standard; the employer must establish its reasonable nonage justification. He concludes that the City establishes that defense on the reviewed plan facts.
