## Controlling holdings

### Evenhanded charges for a distinct local hauling business

**Operative rule.** An evenhanded annual per-truck fee for undertaking a distinct local hauling business does not violate the Commerce Clause merely because carriers performing fewer local miles pay more per mile. Courts must examine the actual charged event, discriminatory operation, fair apportionment and relation to state services, and any interstate burden clearly excessive relative to local benefits; Michigan’s §478.2(1) fee satisfies those requirements on this record.

**Authority.** The complete Court opinion has seven votes: Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O’Connor, Kennedy, Souter, Ginsburg, and Breyer.

**Controlling explanation.** The Court examines what Michigan charges for. Oklahoma Tax Commission v. Jefferson Lines requires identification of the taxed event while preserving the independent safeguards of nexus, apportionment, nondiscrimination, and fair relation to state services. This fee attaches to point-to-point Michigan hauling, an actual local business with a substantial connection to the State. If every State imposed the same fee, a carrier would pay repeatedly only by undertaking distinct local hauling in those States. That is not repeated taxation of a single interstate passage. The local trigger also keeps the assessment within the activity Michigan regulates.

The fee applies equally to carriers undertaking that activity. Costs of regulating truck size, weight, insurance, and safety may relate to participation by a truck rather than each mile traveled. A fair relation to the services supporting the activity does not require dollar-for-dollar or mile-for-mile equality. The carriers’ fee-per-mile comparison therefore does not establish discrimination. American Trucking Associations v. Scheiner involved charges on use of the highways, including interstate passage, with a demonstrated unequal burden; it does not invalidate every flat local-business fee.

National Electrical Manufacturers Association v. Sorrell preserves Pike review of an evenhanded measure without requiring a preliminary showing of disparate interstate impact. Applying that review, the asserted practical disruption of interstate trade is not substantiated sufficiently to show a burden clearly excessive relative to Michigan’s local regulatory benefits. Calling an activity intrastate does not immunize the charge; its actual event and operation sustain it here.

**Precedent treatment.** Jefferson Lines and NEMA are applied; Scheiner is distinguished by the charged event and demonstrated effects; Complete Auto and Pike retain their independent safeguards.

## Precedent treatment and resulting law

- **Oklahoma Tax Commission v. Jefferson Lines:** Applied to identify the local activity and preserve nexus, fair apportionment, nondiscrimination, and fair relation as independent requirements.
- **Complete Auto Transit, Inc. v. Brady:** Applied through those four requirements; the local label supplies no categorical exemption.
- **National Electrical Manufacturers Association v. Sorrell:** Applied to require actual review of incidental interstate burdens without a disparate-impact threshold.
- **Pike v. Bruce Church, Inc.:** Applied to compare a substantiated interstate burden with the putative local benefits.
- **American Trucking Associations, Inc. v. Scheiner:** Distinguished because its flat highway charges reached interstate passage and their practical discrimination was demonstrated. Its restriction on that burden remains in force.

The existing Commerce Clause framework permits an evenhanded charge for each distinct local hauling business when its actual operation meets the independent constitutional requirements. A higher per-mile cost caused solely by fewer local miles does not establish discrimination. A carrier’s additional local businesses in other States are separate charged events; courts must still examine the practical burden and relation to local regulation.

## Material published noncontrolling positions

**Scalia, concurring in the judgment.** Scalia applies the approach reflected in West Lynn Creamery and his prior published Commerce Clause positions: a state measure is invalid under the negative Commerce Clause if it facially discriminates against interstate commerce or is indistinguishable from a type of measure the Court previously invalidated. Michigan’s fee meets neither condition. Scheiner charged interstate trucks even without local hauling, a material difference. This ground supports affirmance without joining Pike balancing, the full Complete Auto framework, or an independent internal-consistency rationale.

**Thomas, concurring in the judgment.** Thomas follows his published Hillside Dairy position that the negative Commerce Clause lacks a constitutional foundation adequate to invalidate state legislation. He would affirm for that reason. His objection reaches the doctrine itself and does not adopt Scalia’s separate precedent-based test.
