## Durable decision kernel

The Eleventh Circuit's judgment is **reversed, 6–3**, and the case is remanded for proceedings on Jackson's individual Title IX retaliation claim under the Court's coverage, attribution, causation and remedial rules.

**Supporting:** Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Souter, Ginsburg and Breyer. **Opposing:** Scalia, Kennedy and Thomas, who would affirm the categorical statutory dismissal.

### I. Title IX protects a personally injured reporter of sex discrimination

**Holding and authority — Part I.** A Title IX funding recipient engages in intentional discrimination on the basis of sex when it materially retaliates against a person because that person reports or opposes sex discrimination in the recipient's educational program or activity. The personally injured reporter may invoke the established Title IX action although the reported unequal treatment targeted others. The claimant must prove covered-recipient status, legally attributable recipient conduct, a complaint concerning sex discrimination, materially adverse treatment because of that complaint and the causal injury supporting relief. These propositions command the six Justices joining the Court's opinion.

**Controlling explanation.** Section 1681 protects persons against discrimination on the basis of sex in covered education. A recipient that deliberately punishes a demand for sex equality uses retaliation to suppress the very protection Congress supplied. The sex-discrimination subject of the complaint and the recipient's purposeful response connect the adverse treatment to that statutory prohibition. The reporter's own sex need not be the trait used against him. Sullivan's protection of a personally injured advocate for another's equal property rights supplies a pre-enactment example of this understanding of discrimination; it concerns substantive protection as well as the advocate's ability to sue.

The Board and the dissent distinguish treatment because of sex from treatment because of complaint activity. That distinction overlooks what makes the alleged complaint a target: its demand for equal educational treatment without regard to sex. Protecting only the original victim would permit a recipient to punish the teacher who can identify and oppose the deprivation. Title VII's detailed enumeration of discrimination and retaliation does not require the same drafting in Title IX's broader prohibition. The statutory subject supplies the boundary; an unrelated employment grievance does not become sex discrimination merely because the employee suffers an adverse decision. Jackson alleges complaints about the girls' team's unequal funding, equipment and facility access, followed by removal from coaching and loss of pay. Those allegations present the statutory category decided here.

### II. The established action and adequate funding notice reach the intentional statutory wrong

**Holding and authority — Part II.** Cannon's established Title IX action reaches the retaliation described in Part I. The statutory prohibition, the pre-enactment Sullivan background and the longstanding implementing interpretation together give funding recipients adequate notice of that obligation. Franklin permits appropriate compensation for a proved intentional violation, subject to recipient responsibility, causation and compensable injury. The same six Justices join.

**Controlling explanation.** Cannon supplies an existing action for the statutory protection; the Court now expressly decides that this intentional retaliation falls within that protection. Franklin supplies appropriate relief after the intentional wrong is proved. It does not substitute a desirable remedy for an enacted duty. Pennhurst requires fair notice of a funding condition, and that requirement is met by the statute read in its legal setting. Sullivan preceded Title IX, and the implementing rule has long forbidden retaliation for complaints of discrimination. Sections 106.71 and 100.7(e) confirm the statutory construction and notice; the damages duty rests on the statute itself.

The dissent correctly separates an existing action from the scope of the right, but the direct construction in Part I answers that scope objection. The absence of an earlier decision involving this coach's precise circumstances does not eliminate notice of the intentional statutory wrong. Powell, Sandoval and South Camden preserve a bounded prospective action under Title VI effects regulations. Their distinct regulatory setting neither supplies nor defeats this Title IX statutory claim. Gebser and Davis likewise concern different attribution problems involving third-party harassment. Their particular conditions cannot require a second complaint as a prerequisite to responsibility for the recipient's own intentional retaliatory decision. Jackson must still establish that the challenged conduct is legally the recipient's conduct; its importance to enforcement does not establish that fact.

### III. The categorical dismissal must be reversed and the individual claim adjudicated

**Holding and authority — Part III.** Jackson's allegations of removal from coaching and lost supplemental pay because he complained of sex discrimination state a claim against the categorical grounds adopted below. The Eleventh Circuit's judgment is reversed. The District Court must determine coverage, recipient responsibility, the complaint's statutory subject, retaliatory causation, supported defenses and causal injury; appropriate compensation and prospective employment relief depend on that proof and their lawful requirements. The same six Justices join.

**Controlling explanation.** Jackson asserts his own job and pay injury. He need not first obtain a completed adjudication of the girls' discrimination claim to survive this dismissal. Pleading sufficiency accepts the material allegations for review; it does not establish their truth. The parties must develop the Board's decisionmaking, the asserted connection between the complaints and removal, and any independent lawful explanation. Sequence alone does not compel a finding of retaliatory causation.

Franklin allows compensation for proved causal pay loss and other legally recoverable injury. Prospective employment relief must address a proved continuing consequence within the court's lawful authority. If retaliation is established, the remedy must repair supported monetary loss and remaining adverse consequences within those limits. Ordinary proceedings will apply the adopted rule to the actual evidence and defenses.

### Material precedent treatment

- **Cannon v. University of Chicago, 441 U.S. 677:** extends the established Title IX action to the intentional retaliation now held to violate the statute.
- **Sullivan v. Little Hunting Park, Inc., 396 U.S. 229:** applies its protection of an advocate personally injured for pressing another's statutory equality as pre-enactment support for the coverage and notice holdings, while preserving the distinct statutory settings.
- **Franklin v. Gwinnett County Public Schools, February 26, 1992:** applies and extends appropriate compensation for proved intentional Title IX discrimination to the statutory retaliation held actionable here, preserving recipient responsibility, causation and compensable loss.
- **Pennhurst State School and Hospital v. Halderman, 451 U.S. 1:** applies its fair-notice requirement; the enacted prohibition, Sullivan background and longstanding interpretation together satisfy it for this intentional wrong.
- **Gebser v. Lago Vista Independent School District, June 22, 1998:** preserves the teacher-attribution holdings and their actual authority limits; their third-party setting is distinguished from the recipient's own intentional retaliation.
- **Davis v. Monroe County Board of Education, May 24, 1999:** preserves its controlled-peer-harassment and culpable-recipient-response rules; their particular attribution and severity conditions are not transferred mechanically to the direct retaliatory decision alleged here.
- **Powell v. Ridge, April 3, 2000:** preserves its bounded Title VI prospective action for valid effects regulations and its distinct section 1983 holding; those rules remain separate from the intentional Title IX wrong decided here.
- **Alexander v. Sandoval, April 24, 2001:** preserves its application of Powell to the valid Department of Justice effects regulation. That prospective regulatory action supplies no separate source of the Title IX damages duty, which rests here on the statute.
- **South Camden Citizens in Action v. New Jersey Department of Environmental Protection, May 28, 2002:** preserves its application of the bounded Title VI action to valid EPA effects regulations and its separate statutory and section 1983 components. The present right and damages duty arise from Title IX's intentional-discrimination prohibition.

### Resulting law and procedure

Title IX now expressly reaches a covered recipient's materially adverse intentional retaliation because a person reports or opposes sex discrimination in its educational program. A personally injured reporter may use the established action even when the original discrimination targeted others. The statutory claim requires recipient coverage and responsibility, the sex-discrimination subject, retaliatory causation and causal injury; the adopted notice holding permits Franklin compensation upon proof, with prospective employment relief under its lawful requirements. The distinct teacher- and peer-harassment attribution rules retain their scopes.

The case returns through the Eleventh Circuit for ordinary adjudication of Jackson's individual retaliation claim. The District Court must permit development of the decisionmaking and causation record, assess the Board's supported lawful explanation and other defenses, and identify relief repairing any proved injury. A proved violation permits appropriate compensation for causal pay loss and other legally recoverable injury, together with justified prospective employment relief addressing continuing consequences.

## Published noncontrolling positions and continuity

**Thomas, joined by Scalia and Kennedy, dissenting.** The dissent would affirm the categorical statutory dismissal. Section 1681 prohibits discrimination against a person on the basis of that person's sex. Intentional punishment for complaint activity is a distinct prohibition, even when the complaint alleges sex discrimination against someone else. Jackson's alleged loss of coaching duties and pay establishes personal injury; it does not establish that the enacted right protects the activity that allegedly caused that injury. The dissent's beneficiary and statutory-causation objections therefore remain distinct from standing.

Title VII's express retaliation provision illustrates that Congress can separately protect complaint activity. Sullivan construed section 1982's property-right protection, and neither its standing analysis nor its substantive setting compels the asserted Title IX funding obligation. Cannon supplies an action for rights Congress enacted; Franklin permits remedies after a statutory wrong is established. Neither decides this disputed expansion of coverage. Pennhurst adds a notice objection to imposing the asserted condition on recipients without a sufficiently clear statutory command. The dissent accepts the actual scopes of the existing Title IX and Title VI enforcement precedents but finds no enacted retaliation right here. It would conclude the appealed Title IX theory.

## Holdings

### I. Title IX protects a personally injured reporter of sex discrimination

**Holding and authority — Part I.** A Title IX funding recipient engages in intentional discrimination on the basis of sex when it materially retaliates against a person because that person reports or opposes sex discrimination in the recipient's educational program or activity. The personally injured reporter may invoke the established Title IX action although the reported unequal treatment targeted others. The claimant must prove covered-recipient status, legally attributable recipient conduct, a complaint concerning sex discrimination, materially adverse treatment because of that complaint and the causal injury supporting relief. These propositions command the six Justices joining the Court's opinion.

**Controlling explanation.** Section 1681 protects persons against discrimination on the basis of sex in covered education. A recipient that deliberately punishes a demand for sex equality uses retaliation to suppress the very protection Congress supplied. The sex-discrimination subject of the complaint and the recipient's purposeful response connect the adverse treatment to that statutory prohibition. The reporter's own sex need not be the trait used against him. Sullivan's protection of a personally injured advocate for another's equal property rights supplies a pre-enactment example of this understanding of discrimination; it concerns substantive protection as well as the advocate's ability to sue.

The Board and the dissent distinguish treatment because of sex from treatment because of complaint activity. That distinction overlooks what makes the alleged complaint a target: its demand for equal educational treatment without regard to sex. Protecting only the original victim would permit a recipient to punish the teacher who can identify and oppose the deprivation. Title VII's detailed enumeration of discrimination and retaliation does not require the same drafting in Title IX's broader prohibition. The statutory subject supplies the boundary; an unrelated employment grievance does not become sex discrimination merely because the employee suffers an adverse decision. Jackson alleges complaints about the girls' team's unequal funding, equipment and facility access, followed by removal from coaching and loss of pay. Those allegations present the statutory category decided here.

### II. The established action and adequate funding notice reach the intentional statutory wrong

**Holding and authority — Part II.** Cannon's established Title IX action reaches the retaliation described in Part I. The statutory prohibition, the pre-enactment Sullivan background and the longstanding implementing interpretation together give funding recipients adequate notice of that obligation. Franklin permits appropriate compensation for a proved intentional violation, subject to recipient responsibility, causation and compensable injury. The same six Justices join.

**Controlling explanation.** Cannon supplies an existing action for the statutory protection; the Court now expressly decides that this intentional retaliation falls within that protection. Franklin supplies appropriate relief after the intentional wrong is proved. It does not substitute a desirable remedy for an enacted duty. Pennhurst requires fair notice of a funding condition, and that requirement is met by the statute read in its legal setting. Sullivan preceded Title IX, and the implementing rule has long forbidden retaliation for complaints of discrimination. Sections 106.71 and 100.7(e) confirm the statutory construction and notice; the damages duty rests on the statute itself.

The dissent correctly separates an existing action from the scope of the right, but the direct construction in Part I answers that scope objection. The absence of an earlier decision involving this coach's precise circumstances does not eliminate notice of the intentional statutory wrong. Powell, Sandoval and South Camden preserve a bounded prospective action under Title VI effects regulations. Their distinct regulatory setting neither supplies nor defeats this Title IX statutory claim. Gebser and Davis likewise concern different attribution problems involving third-party harassment. Their particular conditions cannot require a second complaint as a prerequisite to responsibility for the recipient's own intentional retaliatory decision. Jackson must still establish that the challenged conduct is legally the recipient's conduct; its importance to enforcement does not establish that fact.

### III. The categorical dismissal must be reversed and the individual claim adjudicated

**Holding and authority — Part III.** Jackson's allegations of removal from coaching and lost supplemental pay because he complained of sex discrimination state a claim against the categorical grounds adopted below. The Eleventh Circuit's judgment is reversed. The District Court must determine coverage, recipient responsibility, the complaint's statutory subject, retaliatory causation, supported defenses and causal injury; appropriate compensation and prospective employment relief depend on that proof and their lawful requirements. The same six Justices join.

**Controlling explanation.** Jackson asserts his own job and pay injury. He need not first obtain a completed adjudication of the girls' discrimination claim to survive this dismissal. Pleading sufficiency accepts the material allegations for review; it does not establish their truth. The parties must develop the Board's decisionmaking, the asserted connection between the complaints and removal, and any independent lawful explanation. Sequence alone does not compel a finding of retaliatory causation.

Franklin allows compensation for proved causal pay loss and other legally recoverable injury. Prospective employment relief must address a proved continuing consequence within the court's lawful authority. If retaliation is established, the remedy must repair supported monetary loss and remaining adverse consequences within those limits. Ordinary proceedings will apply the adopted rule to the actual evidence and defenses.

## Separate Writings

**Thomas, joined by Scalia and Kennedy, dissenting.** The dissent would affirm the categorical statutory dismissal. Section 1681 prohibits discrimination against a person on the basis of that person's sex. Intentional punishment for complaint activity is a distinct prohibition, even when the complaint alleges sex discrimination against someone else. Jackson's alleged loss of coaching duties and pay establishes personal injury; it does not establish that the enacted right protects the activity that allegedly caused that injury. The dissent's beneficiary and statutory-causation objections therefore remain distinct from standing.

The dissent accepts the actual scopes of the existing Title IX and Title VI enforcement precedents but finds no enacted retaliation right here. It would conclude the appealed Title IX theory.
