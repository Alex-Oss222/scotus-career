#### Components and provisional result

Stevens, O'Connor, Kennedy, Souter, Ginsburg and Breyer would reverse both appellate judgments insofar as they uphold concealment of the original submitted special-trial-judge report, and remand for disclosure, inclusion in the reviewable record, and fresh consideration under Rule 183. Scalia and Thomas would affirm both judgments against the report-withholding challenge. No Justice treats the undisclosed report as proved favorable to the taxpayers or decides the ultimate deficiency and fraud findings in this review. The two consolidated components receive the same procedural treatment within each Justice's commitment.

For the six supporting reversal, Rule 183 requires a genuine two-stage process. The special trial judge submits findings and opinion; the deciding regular judge may adopt, modify or reject them but must give the prescribed regard to the hearing judge's credibility opportunity and the specified presumption of correctness. The submitted report must be preserved and disclosed so that parties and the reviewing court can test that legally required treatment. This requirement concerns the submitted Rule 183 report, not every draft, discussion, note or internal communication. It leaves final decisional authority with the regular judge and permits supported rejection of proposed findings. The rule does not make the report evidence of its own correctness or require adoption merely because the special judge heard the witnesses.

#### Shared alternative-path comparison

**Disclosure/remand path.** Freytag's unanimous statutory discussion distinguishes a hearing officer's submitted findings from the regular judge's final decision. Rule 183 assigns specific legal weight to the former. Neither final decisional authority nor permissible consultation can erase the object of the required review. The strongest contrary argument is the 1983 deletion of automatic party-service and exception provisions: an omitted requirement ordinarily should not be reinserted. The answer is limited. The Court need not restore the old automatic-service timetable or exceptions procedure; the surviving report and deference commands, combined with actual appellate review under section 7482, require access to the submitted report used in the disputed adjudication. Disclosure allows review of the existing commands rather than restoring every deleted mechanism. Targeted confidentiality protection remains available.

**Withholding/affirmance path.** Sections 7443A and 7482 leave the regular Tax Court judge with final decisional responsibility, and Rule 183 no longer expressly orders service of the special judge's report. An internal proposed decision may be discussed and revised without giving litigants every prior judicial view. The strongest contrary argument is that presumptive correctness and special credibility regard become unreviewable if the original report is hidden. This path answers that appellate review addresses the final decision and the regular judge's obligation to follow the rule, and that a presumption of regular judicial performance is not displaced by unproved allegations about the initial report's contents. The disagreement is whether those safeguards are adequate under the retained rule, not whether a special trial judge may secretly become the final statutory decisionmaker.

Neither path requires a broad constitutional ruling granting or rejecting access to every internal judicial document. For the six supporting reversal, the rule ground fully supplies the remedy. For Scalia and Thomas, ordinary due process permits the final legally authorized judge to decide after full evidentiary proceedings; a provisional judicial report is not additional secret evidence or a new adversarial factual submission merely because it contains proposed credibility findings. This conclusion is confined to the established process and does not approve an actually proved denial of the required hearing, unauthorized adjudicator or substitution of undisclosed evidence.

## Holdings

### The submitted Rule 183 report must remain identifiable and reviewable

**Holding and operative rule:** A special trial judge's submitted Rule 183(b) report must be preserved and available to the parties and reviewing court when the final decision is tested for the credibility weight and presumption of correctness that Rule 183(c) requires. The regular judge retains authority to adopt, modify or reject the report, but must make the treatment of its material findings reviewable; exclusion of the legally operative report establishes the need for disclosure without requiring taxpayers first to prove its undisclosed contents.

**Authority:** Stevens's Opinion of the Court, joined in full by Stone-Zsela, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Ginsburg and Breyer, supplies eight direct votes for the complete rule and its remedy in each docket.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that the report submitted after the hearing is the report on which the regular judge must act. Rule 183 gives that completed work legal significance: its factual findings are presumed correct, and the deciding judge must give due regard to the hearing judge's opportunity to evaluate witnesses. Those commands cannot be enforced if an undisclosed replacement erases the starting point and is then attributed entirely to the hearing judge. Freytag v. Commissioner supplies the distinction between the special trial judge's hearing function and the Tax Court's ultimate decisional authority. Preserving both functions requires an identifiable report and an explanation of its treatment.

The deletion of automatic service and party-exception language in 1983 does not answer the continuing weighted-review requirements. The rule's express recommittal procedure also supplies a formal means to return the submitted work for further consideration. Section 7460(b), governing full-court review of a regular Tax Court judge, expressly excludes a different initial decision and concerns a different institutional relationship. That provision cannot silently erase the special trial judge's retained credibility role. The principle recognized in Service v. Dulles requires adherence to the tribunal's governing procedures; interpretive leeway cannot defeat the process their text establishes.

The submitted report is distinct from ordinary preliminary drafts, informal discussions before submission and judicial deliberations generally. Specific confidentiality protection remains available while preserving meaningful party access and appellate review. The disclosure ground does not depend on finding that the original report favored the taxpayers, that material alteration occurred, or that a judge acted improperly. Those competing assumptions are precisely why the actual report must become available. Further reconsideration or explanation must address what that record shows, and a substantive change follows only where the proper application requires it. The rule-specific disposition makes adjudication of the alternative constitutional disclosure theory unnecessary.

## Precedent Treatment

- Freytag v. Commissioner is applied to preserve the separate hearing/report and final-decision functions; final decisional authority does not eliminate the report's prescribed legal weight.
- Service v. Dulles supplies the requirement that a tribunal follow the procedure its governing rules establish. It is applied to the retained Rule 183 commands.
- The interpretive-deference principle illustrated by Bowles v. Seminole Rock & Sand Co. does not sustain a reading inconsistent with the report's identity and prescribed treatment. The Court construes this rule rather than displacing that general principle.

## Law After Decision

Under the existing Rule 183, a submitted special trial judge report remains the identifiable basis for weighted review. Parties and appellate courts must be able to assess material departures from it, while the regular Tax Court judge retains final decisional authority and lawful means of recommittal, revision and confidentiality protection. Disclosure enables a determination of actual compliance and an effective remedy for any demonstrated defect.

## Separate Writings

**Thomas, dissenting.** Thomas would affirm both judgments. The 1983 amendment deliberately removed automatic party service and changed filing to submission. In his view, the Tax Court reasonably reads the amended rule to permit a special trial judge to reconsider a report before the regular judge adopts the final endorsed version. The rule still requires deference to that judge's findings, but it does not expressly require disclosure of every earlier internal version.

Bowles v. Seminole Rock & Sand Co. supports respect for a reasonable interpretation of the institution's own rule. Freytag preserves the special trial judge's work and the regular judge's final authority; neither role proves that an initial report became immutable on submission. Full adoption of the special trial judge's endorsed findings can satisfy the required weight. Thomas also regards ordinary evidentiary adjudication by the authorized judge as adequate against the confined due-process objection absent a demonstrated hearing defect. He rejects treating an unproved account of internal revision as proof that the prescribed review failed. The Court answers that access is required to test the retained legal weight, regardless of whether misconduct or a changed tax result can yet be established.

## Procedure After Action

Both cases return through their respective Courts of Appeals to the Tax Court for the disclosure, response and review process specified in Judgment & Remedy. Any ensuing appellate review must proceed on a record that permits enforcement of Rule 183's prescribed treatment of the submitted findings.
