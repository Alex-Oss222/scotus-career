## Law of the decision

#### Descriptive fair use does not require disproving the infringement claimant's confusion element

**Holding and operative rule.** The infringement claimant bears the burden of proving likelihood of confusion, including when its registration is incontestable; a defendant invoking §1115(b)(4) must establish by the ordinary civil standard use otherwise than as a mark, fairly and in good faith, only to describe its goods or services or their geographic origin. The defendant need not negate likely confusion: some confusion can coexist with the defense, while facts bearing on confusion may also inform whether the actual use is fair, in good faith or source-identifying.

**Authority.** Scalia's opinion of the Court, adopted in full by Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer, supplies nine direct votes for the complete allocation, its stated qualifications and remand.

**Controlling explanation.** The Court holds that the infringement requirement and the affirmative defense perform distinct statutory work. Section 1114 places likely confusion within infringement; §1115(b)(4) states its own conditions without requiring the defendant to disprove that element. Making absence of confusion a defense prerequisite would make the defense unavailable precisely when a claimant had established the need for it. Lasting's categorical argument that any confusion defeats fairness cannot be reconciled with that allocation.

Park 'N Fly v. Dollar Park and Fly gives incontestability its statutory force while preserving express defenses. Qualitex v. Jacobson Products and TrafFix Devices v. Marketing Displays maintain source-identifying protection together with independent statutory limits. Those principles preserve both Lasting's protection and the enacted room for fair description. Fairness, good faith and use otherwise than as a mark remain real requirements; evidence of confusion may illuminate them without becoming a shifted infringement burden.

KP's role as declaratory plaintiff does not reverse the substantive burdens. The actual manner of presentation must be evaluated under the correct allocation. If undisputed facts establish the defense, judgment must protect that particular fair use; otherwise ordinary proceedings resolve the disputed statutory conditions and corresponding relief.

### Treatment of precedent

- Park 'N Fly, Inc. v. Dollar Park and Fly, Inc., 469 U.S. 189: applies incontestability subject to the Lanham Act's express defenses; the protected registration does not erase §1115(b)(4).
- Qualitex Co. v. Jacobson Products Co., March 28, 1995: applies the distinction between qualifying source identification and independent limits on commercial exclusion; this decision resolves the different descriptive-defense allocation.
- TrafFix Devices, Inc. v. Marketing Displays, Inc., March 20, 2001: applies the independence of statutory limitations from source-identifying value; its functionality rule retains its own scope.

### Resulting law

Across claims invoking §1115(b)(4), the infringement claimant retains proof of likelihood of confusion and the defense claimant bears the enacted fair-use conditions. Likely confusion is compatible with the defense and may be evidence bearing on the actual statutory conditions. The rule supplies the governing burden allocation; presentation-specific factual and remedial work proceeds under it.
