## Controlling holdings

### Mandatory enforcement steps do not themselves establish constitutional property

**Holding and operative rule:** A state direction that police take specified enforcement steps upon qualifying violations does not create a beneficiary's constitutional property interest without an independently secured substantive benefit distinct from that enforcement procedure. Colorado's protective-order statute and Gonzales's individualized order prescribe enforcement against the restrained person but do not secure the asserted independent property interest; the municipal procedural-due-process claim therefore fails at its necessary substantive predicate.

**Authority:** The Opinion of the Court receives six complete joins: O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas and Breyer. The ground applies even on the assumption that the statutory enforcement duties are mandatory.

**Controlling explanation:** Board of Regents v. Roth requires an entitlement to a substantive benefit grounded in independent law. Doe v. District of Columbia distinguishes such an entitlement from mandatory investigation or enforcement procedures. Sandin v. Conner directs attention to real substantive constraints rather than mandatory words alone; its prison-liberty application supplies no automatic rule for property in police action.

Colorado directs officers to use every reasonable means to enforce a protective order. Arrest, or pursuit of a warrant when immediate arrest is impractical, is required when information supplies probable cause both that the restrained person violated or attempted to violate the order and that the person was properly served or had actual notice of the order's existence and substance. Those commands have legal force. Yet their object is the executive enforcement mechanism against another person. An identified beneficiary and defined triggers do not by themselves transform the prescribed official steps into a separately secured substantive benefit. The reverse-side notice states enforcement law; it does not make the police parties to a decree guaranteeing the beneficiary a service.

The dissent regards reasonable enforcement itself as the substantive service and correctly distinguishes discretion over means from discretion to refuse a duty. That distinction does not answer the further property question. A binding direction can regulate officials without creating constitutional property in the process they must follow. The Court's conclusion therefore does not rest on giving officers unlimited discretion to disregard the statute. Nor does it turn on whether the claimed interest can be sold or assigned a monetary price. The pleaded policy cannot dispense with the required underlying property interest.

- Doe v. District of Columbia is applied to the distinction between prescribed investigative or enforcement steps and independently protected property; its reservation for a genuinely independent entitlement remains operative.

The prescribed protective-order enforcement mechanism does not itself constitute the property claimed in this procedural-due-process action. **Holding and operative rule:** A state direction that police take specified enforcement steps upon qualifying violations does not create a beneficiary's constitutional property interest without an independently secured substantive benefit distinct from that enforcement procedure. Colorado's protective-order statute and Gonzales's individualized order prescribe enforcement against the restrained person but do not secure the asserted independent property interest; the municipal procedural-due-process claim therefore fails at its necessary substantive predicate.

**Authority:** The Opinion of the Court receives six complete joins: O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas and Breyer. The ground applies even on the assumption that the statutory enforcement duties are mandatory.

**Controlling explanation:** Board of Regents v. Roth requires an entitlement to a substantive benefit grounded in independent law. Doe v. District of Columbia distinguishes such an entitlement from mandatory investigation or enforcement procedures. Sandin v. Conner directs attention to real substantive constraints rather than mandatory words alone; its prison-liberty application supplies no automatic rule for property in police action.

Colorado directs officers to use every reasonable means to enforce a protective order. Arrest, or pursuit of a warrant when immediate arrest is impractical, is required when information supplies probable cause both that the restrained person violated or attempted to violate the order and that the person was properly served or had actual notice of the order's existence and substance. Those commands have legal force. Yet their object is the executive enforcement mechanism against another person. An identified beneficiary and defined triggers do not by themselves transform the prescribed official steps into a separately secured substantive benefit. The reverse-side notice states enforcement law; it does not make the police parties to a decree guaranteeing the beneficiary a service.

The dissent regards reasonable enforcement itself as the substantive service and correctly distinguishes discretion over means from discretion to refuse a duty. That distinction does not answer the further property question. A binding direction can regulate officials without creating constitutional property in the process they must follow. The Court's conclusion therefore does not rest on giving officers unlimited discretion to disregard the statute. Nor does it turn on whether the claimed interest can be sold or assigned a monetary price. The pleaded policy cannot dispense with the required underlying property interest.

## Precedent Treatment

- Board of Regents v. Roth is applied: independent law must secure a substantive entitlement before the procedural protection attaches.
- Doe v. District of Columbia is applied to the distinction between prescribed investigative or enforcement steps and independently protected property; its reservation for a genuinely independent entitlement remains operative.
- Sandin v. Conner is applied for its substantive-constraint distinction, without transplanting its prison-liberty application into a property claim.
- DeShaney v. Winnebago County Department of Social Services retains its distinct rule about the absence of a general constitutional duty to protect against private violence. The present decision examines the claimed state-created property interest independently.
- Connecticut Department of Public Safety v. Doe retains its requirement that the proposed process concern facts material under the governing substantive law; its assumed-interest analysis supplies no property interest here.

## Law After Decision

The prescribed protective-order enforcement mechanism does not itself constitute the property claimed in this procedural-due-process action. Mandatory language, a named beneficiary and substantive enforcement triggers must be distinguished from an independently secured substantive benefit. The judgment does not erase Colorado's enforcement duties. The separate requirements of municipal responsibility and damages continue to govern claims that establish an underlying constitutional deprivation.

## Separate Writings

**Justice Stevens, joined by Chief Justice Stone-Zsela and Justice Ginsburg, dissenting.** The dissent treats reasonable enforcement as a substantive protective service rather than property in administrative steps alone. The individualized order identifies its beneficiary, and the statute specifies the conditions requiring a reasonable response. Discretion over practicable means and the inability to promise successful rescue do not eliminate that obligation. Roth protects legally secured benefits without confining them to transferable or market-valued interests.

The dissent distinguishes Doe's investigative-procedure limitation from this claimed entitlement to actual enforcement. Sandin requires attention to the whole legal constraint, and DeShaney's rejection of a general duty to protect does not answer a particular state-created claim. The order's parenting permissions and the notice and probable-cause requirements remain essential to proving that the obligation was triggered. The complaint's alleged policy of refusing meaningful consideration is sufficient to proceed under that bounded theory.

The dissent would affirm reinstatement of the municipal claim and remand for proof of the qualifying circumstances, deprivation, constitutionally inadequate process, municipal policy or custom, causal injury and available relief. Mathews calls for process fitted to the urgency, and Zinermon distinguishes an established policy from a random unauthorized failure. Repeated contacts alone do not establish whether the request received meaningful consideration. Nominal relief for a proved process denial and compensation for the children's deaths have different predicates; neither the existence of a duty nor the tragic result establishes compensatory causation.

**Chief Justice Stone-Zsela, also dissenting.** Joining the substantive-service dissent, the Chief Justice would hold that the pleaded policy-level denial states a procedural claim when officials fail to provide a practicable, timely opportunity to establish the statutory conditions and receive an intelligible determination. Receiving the order and material information, genuinely assessing the conditions and communicating a reason for refusal can allow useful correction without delaying emergency action through a formal hearing. Monell still requires proof that the Town's policy caused the procedural deprivation. Any compensatory recovery must correspond to proved causal injury; the children's deaths cannot automatically measure damages merely because an enforcement duty existed.
