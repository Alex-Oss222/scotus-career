## Controlling holdings

### I. DeShaney's limit is retained, and this claim rests on a state-created entitlement

**Holding and operative rule:** The Due Process Clause imposes no general constitutional duty on the State to protect individuals against private violence. That limit is retained, together with the distinct doctrines governing custody, an official's affirmative deprivation of available protection and discriminatory denial of protection. DeShaney does not decide whether state law has created an individualized entitlement to protective services; procedural due process protects such an entitlement on its own terms.

**Authority:** Part I of Breyer's Opinion of the Court, joined by Stone-Zsela, Stevens, Souter and Ginsburg; five direct joins.

**Controlling explanation:** DeShaney v. Winnebago County Department of Social Services rejects a constitutional duty to protect against private harm outside custody, and nothing here enlarges that duty. S.S. ex rel. Jervis v. McMullen's actual-custody rule, Pinder v. Johnson's distinction between omission and an official's own deprivation, the affirmative-interference rule of Saenz v. Heldenfels Brothers, and the prohibition on discriminatory denial of protection applied in Nabozny v. Podlesny and recognized in United States v. Morrison keep their separate predicates. Gonzales does not rest this claim on any of them. She alleges that Colorado law secured to her a defined protective service and that the Town, by policy, deprived her of it without due process. Doe v. District of Columbia recognized that DeShaney does not mean that no noncustodial person can possess an independently created entitlement. The question is therefore the one Board of Regents v. Roth asks of any state-created interest: whether independent law secures the benefit claimed.

### II. A qualifying individualized protective entitlement is property, and Colorado's statute and order establish one

**Holding and operative rule:** State law creates a property interest in protective services, protected by procedural due process, when it establishes all four of these elements: (1) an identified individual or specifically defined beneficiary; (2) a sufficiently definite legal entitlement to protective services, rather than an expectation of ordinary law enforcement; (3) objective triggering conditions; and (4) material legal constraints on officials' authority to withhold the benefit once those conditions exist. The statute, any individualized order and authoritative state constructions are read together, and a protective order is not automatically sufficient. The benefit need not be transferable, monetizable or a guarantee of outcome. A general duty to investigate, discretionary promises and internal guidelines ordinarily create no such entitlement, and no entitlement guarantees prevention of every injury. Colorado's protective-order statute and Gonzales's individualized order establish each element; the entitlement's legal existence is resolved.

**Authority:** Part II of Breyer's Opinion of the Court, joined by Stone-Zsela, Stevens, Souter and Ginsburg; five direct joins.

**Controlling explanation:** Board of Regents v. Roth protects a legitimate claim of entitlement that independent law secures, not a unilateral expectation. Sandin v. Conner, applied this Term in Wilkinson v. Austin, asks whether substantive law meaningfully restricts officials' authority rather than whether its words are mandatory; that sufficient, nonexclusive principle informs state-created interests generally, although its prison application does not transfer. An identified beneficiary and objective triggers distinguish a secured individual benefit from the public's general interest in enforcement. Definiteness distinguishes a defined protective service from ordinary policing or a general duty to investigate. Material constraints on withholding supply the substantive limit Sandin requires.

Colorado's scheme meets each element. The order names the protected person. The statute requires every reasonable means to enforce the order, and it requires arrest, or a warrant when arrest is impractical, when officers have information amounting to probable cause both that the restrained person violated or attempted to violate the order and that the person was properly served or had actual notice of its existence and substance. Those predicates are objective, and the commands constrain refusal once they exist. The protected object is the protective service itself: enforcement of the order on the beneficiary's behalf. That benefit is distinct from the procedures used to decide whether it is owed, so the claim does not seek process for its own sake. Doe v. District of Columbia's investigative-procedure holding concerned steps preceding any commanded protection, and its reservation of independently created entitlements describes this scheme.

The order's parenting permissions and reasonable-notice condition define when a violation occurs; they do not dissolve the entitlement. The reverse-side notice does not itself bind police; the statute supplies the obligation. Gonzaga University v. Doe concerns statutory rights enforceable under §1983, and that inquiry remains distinct from constitutional property. No dispositive unresolved Colorado construction requires certification.

### III. Enforcement discretion is preserved, and a required response is not a guaranteed outcome

**Holding and operative rule:** The entitlement leaves officers' enforcement judgment intact. Under Colorado's statute officers still determine, using reasonable means, whether a violation or attempted violation occurred, whether probable cause exists as to the violation and as to service or actual notice, and whether immediate arrest is impractical amid competing emergencies, in which case they must seek a warrant. They lack only unrestricted discretion to disregard the obligation once its conditions exist. The beneficiary is entitled to the required response, not to a particular tactic, a successful arrest or the prevention of harm.

**Authority:** Part III of Breyer's Opinion of the Court, joined by Stone-Zsela, Stevens, Souter and Ginsburg; five direct joins.

**Controlling explanation:** Discretion over means differs from discretion to refuse a duty. Colorado's reasonable-means command preserves practical judgment about how and when to act, and the warrant alternative accommodates circumstances that make arrest impractical. The probable-cause predicates require officers to assess the information presented, including the order's parenting permissions and notice condition, before the duty attaches. Recognizing the entitlement therefore does not make officers insurers, convert every enforcement choice into a constitutional question or allow a beneficiary to demand prosecution of every offense. A general tradition of police discretion cannot nullify a statute that removes the choice to provide no reasonable enforcement at all once its conditions are met. Where no comparable entitlement exists, ordinary enforcement discretion is untouched. Whether the predicates existed on the night in question is a matter of proof under this rule, not a reason to deny the entitlement.

### IV. Mathews governs the process due, without a mandatory list or emergency hearing

**Holding and operative rule:** The process due for this entitlement is determined under Mathews v. Eldridge by weighing the beneficiary's interest, the risk of erroneous deprivation and the value of additional safeguards, and the governmental interest, including the burdens of emergency policing. Adequate process may include an accessible means of requesting enforcement, prompt consideration, supervisory review, communication of a refusal and timely reconsideration, but none of these is constitutionally required in every case, and no formal predeprivation hearing is required in an emergency. Parratt v. Taylor and Hudson v. Palmer continue to govern random, unauthorized failures for which predeprivation process is impracticable and adequate postdeprivation remedies exist. An alleged established municipal policy of refusing meaningful consideration of qualifying requests states a procedural due-process claim.

**Authority:** Part IV of Breyer's Opinion of the Court, joined by Stone-Zsela, Stevens, Souter and Ginsburg; five direct joins.

**Controlling explanation:** Wilkinson v. Austin approved designed safeguards only as they function meaningfully, and it declined to require procedures whose added value did not justify their cost. Mathews calls for the same practical inquiry here. Protective enforcement is time-sensitive, and a hearing that delayed action would defeat the interest it protects. The safeguards with real value let a beneficiary present the order and the information bearing on its conditions, receive a genuine assessment and learn of a refusal in time to seek reconsideration. Zinermon v. Burch distinguishes an established procedure or foreseeable policy from an unauthorized departure: when the deprivation results from the municipality's own policy, predeprivation safeguards are feasible and a postdeprivation remedy does not satisfy due process. Connecticut Department of Public Safety v. Doe requires that additional process concern facts material under the governing rule; the statutory conditions are such facts. Repeated contacts with officers neither establish nor disprove meaningful consideration under a pleaded policy that prevents it. The district court determines what the Town's procedures provided and what additional safeguards were practicable.

### V. Monell, causation and Carey govern municipal liability and damages

**Holding and operative rule:** The Town is liable only if its own policy or custom caused an actual deprivation of the entitlement without the process due; respondeat superior does not apply. Causation of the children's deaths is not presumed from the existence of the duty or from a procedural violation. Under Carey v. Piphus, a proved denial of process supports nominal damages, and compensatory damages require proof of actual injury caused by that denial.

**Authority:** Part V of Breyer's Opinion of the Court, joined by Stone-Zsela, Stevens, Souter and Ginsburg; five direct joins.

**Controlling explanation:** Monell v. Department of Social Services requires the municipality's own policy or custom to be the moving force behind the constitutional deprivation, and Saenz v. Heldenfels Brothers kept that attribution requirement separate from individual defenses. Gonzales alleges a policy tolerating nonenforcement; that allegation is assumed at the dismissal stage and must be proved. Carey separates the constitutional violation from compensable injury. A denial of process is actionable for nominal damages without proof of other harm, but compensation requires proof that the injury resulted from the denial of process rather than from a deprivation that would have occurred with adequate process. The deaths cannot automatically measure damages merely because an enforcement duty existed, and an unsuccessful protective effort does not by itself establish either the violation or the injury.

## Precedent treatment and resulting law

- DeShaney v. Winnebago County Department of Social Services is preserved: there is no general constitutional duty to protect against private violence, and its rule does not address a state-created entitlement.
- Pinder v. Johnson and Saenz v. Heldenfels Brothers are preserved: their affirmative-deprivation rules keep their own predicates, and Saenz's separation of municipal attribution from individual defenses is applied.
- Doe v. District of Columbia is preserved: its holding that mandatory investigative procedures alone create no substantive entitlement is unchanged, and its reservation of independently created entitlements is developed by the qualifying-entitlement rule.
- Sandin v. Conner is preserved and applied for its sufficient, nonexclusive substantive-constraint principle, without transplanting its prison application; Wilkinson v. Austin is applied for safeguards that function meaningfully.
- Board of Regents v. Roth, Mathews v. Eldridge and Monell v. Department of Social Services are applied.
- Parratt v. Taylor and Hudson v. Palmer are preserved for random, unauthorized deprivations; Zinermon v. Burch is applied to the pleaded established policy, and Carey v. Piphus to damages.
- Connecticut Department of Public Safety v. Doe retains its requirement that additional process concern facts material under the governing substantive law.
- Gonzaga University v. Doe is distinguished: statutory rights enforceable under §1983 and constitutional property remain distinct inquiries.

A qualifying individualized protective entitlement is property protected by procedural due process when state law identifies the beneficiary, defines a sufficiently definite entitlement to protective services, sets objective triggers and materially constrains officials' authority to withhold the service once triggered; Colorado's protective-order statute and an individualized order establish such an entitlement. DeShaney's limit, the custody, affirmative-deprivation and discriminatory-denial doctrines, and Doe's investigative-procedure holding remain. Officers retain discretion over reasonable means and over the probable-cause and practicability judgments the statute commits to them, but not discretion to disregard the obligation; the entitlement is to the required response, not a guaranteed outcome. Mathews determines the process due without a mandatory list of safeguards or a formal emergency hearing, and Parratt and Hudson govern random, unauthorized failures. Municipal liability requires the municipality's own policy or custom causing the procedural deprivation, and Carey separates the violation from compensable injury.

## Material published noncontrolling positions

**Chief Justice Stone-Zsela, concurring.** The Chief Justice joins the Court's opinion in full. He would further specify that a beneficiary plausibly denied the secured protective service by municipal policy states a procedural claim when officials fail to provide a practicable, timely opportunity to establish the statutory conditions and receive an intelligible determination. Receiving the order and material information, genuinely assessing the conditions and communicating a reason for refusal can allow a mistaken refusal to be reconsidered in time without delaying emergency action through a formal hearing. Monell still requires proof that the Town's policy caused the procedural deprivation. Any compensatory recovery must correspond to proved causal injury; the children's deaths cannot automatically measure damages merely because an enforcement duty existed.

**Justice Stevens, joined by Justice Ginsburg, concurring.** The concurrence joins the Court's opinion in full and adds that property interests protected by due process are created and defined by state law. A State that deliberately replaces general nonenforcement discretion with a specific protective duty owed to named beneficiaries creates the kind of entitlement Roth protects. Enforcement is itself the tangible service, so a beneficiary who insists on it does not claim a right to procedure alone, and refusal of the service differs from an incorrect assessment of its conditions.

The Colorado courts have the final word on the meaning of Colorado law. When a dispositive state-law question is genuinely unresolved, federal courts should obtain or await the State's authoritative construction rather than supply one of their own. No such question is dispositive here: the statute's commands and the order supply the entitlement, and a later compilation label creates no uncertainty requiring certification.

**Justice Souter, concurring.** Justice Souter joins the Court's opinion in full and writes to explain why the claim does not seek process to obtain process. A procedural due-process claim cannot rest on an interest consisting only of the procedure itself, and Doe v. District of Columbia's investigative-procedure holding rests on that principle. The benefit here is different. Colorado secures to the protected person the protective assistance itself, enforcement of the order when its conditions exist, much as state law may secure utility service, public education or benefits. The procedures by which officers determine whether those conditions exist are distinct from that assistance, and the federal process claimed concerns those determinations. Because the Court's opinion identifies the protected object in that way, preserves the exclusion of general investigative duties and promises no outcome, its rule does not convert enforcement procedure into property.

**Justice Scalia, joined by Justices O'Connor, Kennedy and Thomas, dissenting.** The dissent would reverse and restore dismissal of the reviewed municipal procedural-due-process theory. In its view, a state direction that police take specified enforcement steps upon qualifying violations does not create a beneficiary's constitutional property interest without an independently secured substantive benefit distinct from that enforcement procedure. Colorado's commands have legal force, but their object is the executive enforcement mechanism against another person. An identified beneficiary and defined triggers do not transform the prescribed steps into a separately secured benefit, and the reverse-side notice does not make the police parties to a decree. Doe v. District of Columbia distinguishes such an entitlement from mandatory investigation or enforcement procedures, and Sandin's attention to substantive constraints supplies no rule for property in police action. The order's parenting permissions also show that the claimed interest cannot be an unconditional right to the children's return.

The dissent assumes that the statutory duties are mandatory and does not rest on unlimited police discretion or on whether the interest can be sold or assigned a monetary value. It concludes that a binding direction can regulate officials without creating constitutional property in the process they must follow, so the pleaded policy cannot supply the missing interest, and it does not reach the adequacy of the process provided.

## Holdings

### I. DeShaney's limit is retained, and this claim rests on a state-created entitlement

**Holding and operative rule:** The Due Process Clause imposes no general constitutional duty on the State to protect individuals against private violence. That limit is retained, together with the distinct doctrines governing custody, an official's affirmative deprivation of available protection and discriminatory denial of protection. DeShaney does not decide whether state law has created an individualized entitlement to protective services; procedural due process protects such an entitlement on its own terms.

**Authority:** Part I of Breyer's Opinion of the Court, joined by Stone-Zsela, Stevens, Souter and Ginsburg; five direct joins.

**Controlling explanation:** DeShaney v. Winnebago County Department of Social Services rejects a constitutional duty to protect against private harm outside custody, and nothing here enlarges that duty. S.S. ex rel. Jervis v. McMullen's actual-custody rule, Pinder v. Johnson's distinction between omission and an official's own deprivation, the affirmative-interference rule of Saenz v. Heldenfels Brothers, and the prohibition on discriminatory denial of protection applied in Nabozny v. Podlesny and recognized in United States v. Morrison keep their separate predicates. Gonzales does not rest this claim on any of them. She alleges that Colorado law secured to her a defined protective service and that the Town, by policy, deprived her of it without due process. Doe v. District of Columbia recognized that DeShaney does not mean that no noncustodial person can possess an independently created entitlement. The question is therefore the one Board of Regents v. Roth asks of any state-created interest: whether independent law secures the benefit claimed.

### II. A qualifying individualized protective entitlement is property, and Colorado's statute and order establish one

**Holding and operative rule:** State law creates a property interest in protective services, protected by procedural due process, when it establishes all four of these elements: (1) an identified individual or specifically defined beneficiary; (2) a sufficiently definite legal entitlement to protective services, rather than an expectation of ordinary law enforcement; (3) objective triggering conditions; and (4) material legal constraints on officials' authority to withhold the benefit once those conditions exist. The statute, any individualized order and authoritative state constructions are read together, and a protective order is not automatically sufficient. The benefit need not be transferable, monetizable or a guarantee of outcome. A general duty to investigate, discretionary promises and internal guidelines ordinarily create no such entitlement, and no entitlement guarantees prevention of every injury. Colorado's protective-order statute and Gonzales's individualized order establish each element; the entitlement's legal existence is resolved.

**Authority:** Part II of Breyer's Opinion of the Court, joined by Stone-Zsela, Stevens, Souter and Ginsburg; five direct joins.

**Controlling explanation:** Board of Regents v. Roth protects a legitimate claim of entitlement that independent law secures, not a unilateral expectation. Sandin v. Conner, applied this Term in Wilkinson v. Austin, asks whether substantive law meaningfully restricts officials' authority rather than whether its words are mandatory; that sufficient, nonexclusive principle informs state-created interests generally, although its prison application does not transfer. An identified beneficiary and objective triggers distinguish a secured individual benefit from the public's general interest in enforcement. Definiteness distinguishes a defined protective service from ordinary policing or a general duty to investigate. Material constraints on withholding supply the substantive limit Sandin requires.

Colorado's scheme meets each element. The order names the protected person. The statute requires every reasonable means to enforce the order, and it requires arrest, or a warrant when arrest is impractical, when officers have information amounting to probable cause both that the restrained person violated or attempted to violate the order and that the person was properly served or had actual notice of its existence and substance. Those predicates are objective, and the commands constrain refusal once they exist. The protected object is the protective service itself: enforcement of the order on the beneficiary's behalf. That benefit is distinct from the procedures used to decide whether it is owed, so the claim does not seek process for its own sake. Doe v. District of Columbia's investigative-procedure holding concerned steps preceding any commanded protection, and its reservation of independently created entitlements describes this scheme.

The order's parenting permissions and reasonable-notice condition define when a violation occurs; they do not dissolve the entitlement. The reverse-side notice does not itself bind police; the statute supplies the obligation. Gonzaga University v. Doe concerns statutory rights enforceable under §1983, and that inquiry remains distinct from constitutional property. No dispositive unresolved Colorado construction requires certification.

### III. Enforcement discretion is preserved, and a required response is not a guaranteed outcome

**Holding and operative rule:** The entitlement leaves officers' enforcement judgment intact. Under Colorado's statute officers still determine, using reasonable means, whether a violation or attempted violation occurred, whether probable cause exists as to the violation and as to service or actual notice, and whether immediate arrest is impractical amid competing emergencies, in which case they must seek a warrant. They lack only unrestricted discretion to disregard the obligation once its conditions exist. The beneficiary is entitled to the required response, not to a particular tactic, a successful arrest or the prevention of harm.

**Authority:** Part III of Breyer's Opinion of the Court, joined by Stone-Zsela, Stevens, Souter and Ginsburg; five direct joins.

**Controlling explanation:** Discretion over means differs from discretion to refuse a duty. Colorado's reasonable-means command preserves practical judgment about how and when to act, and the warrant alternative accommodates circumstances that make arrest impractical. The probable-cause predicates require officers to assess the information presented, including the order's parenting permissions and notice condition, before the duty attaches. Recognizing the entitlement therefore does not make officers insurers, convert every enforcement choice into a constitutional question or allow a beneficiary to demand prosecution of every offense. A general tradition of police discretion cannot nullify a statute that removes the choice to provide no reasonable enforcement at all once its conditions are met. Where no comparable entitlement exists, ordinary enforcement discretion is untouched. Whether the predicates existed on the night in question is a matter of proof under this rule, not a reason to deny the entitlement.

### IV. Mathews governs the process due, without a mandatory list or emergency hearing

**Holding and operative rule:** The process due for this entitlement is determined under Mathews v. Eldridge by weighing the beneficiary's interest, the risk of erroneous deprivation and the value of additional safeguards, and the governmental interest, including the burdens of emergency policing. Adequate process may include an accessible means of requesting enforcement, prompt consideration, supervisory review, communication of a refusal and timely reconsideration, but none of these is constitutionally required in every case, and no formal predeprivation hearing is required in an emergency. Parratt v. Taylor and Hudson v. Palmer continue to govern random, unauthorized failures for which predeprivation process is impracticable and adequate postdeprivation remedies exist. An alleged established municipal policy of refusing meaningful consideration of qualifying requests states a procedural due-process claim.

**Authority:** Part IV of Breyer's Opinion of the Court, joined by Stone-Zsela, Stevens, Souter and Ginsburg; five direct joins.

**Controlling explanation:** Wilkinson v. Austin approved designed safeguards only as they function meaningfully, and it declined to require procedures whose added value did not justify their cost. Mathews calls for the same practical inquiry here. Protective enforcement is time-sensitive, and a hearing that delayed action would defeat the interest it protects. The safeguards with real value let a beneficiary present the order and the information bearing on its conditions, receive a genuine assessment and learn of a refusal in time to seek reconsideration. Zinermon v. Burch distinguishes an established procedure or foreseeable policy from an unauthorized departure: when the deprivation results from the municipality's own policy, predeprivation safeguards are feasible and a postdeprivation remedy does not satisfy due process. Connecticut Department of Public Safety v. Doe requires that additional process concern facts material under the governing rule; the statutory conditions are such facts. Repeated contacts with officers neither establish nor disprove meaningful consideration under a pleaded policy that prevents it. The district court determines what the Town's procedures provided and what additional safeguards were practicable.

### V. Monell, causation and Carey govern municipal liability and damages

**Holding and operative rule:** The Town is liable only if its own policy or custom caused an actual deprivation of the entitlement without the process due; respondeat superior does not apply. Causation of the children's deaths is not presumed from the existence of the duty or from a procedural violation. Under Carey v. Piphus, a proved denial of process supports nominal damages, and compensatory damages require proof of actual injury caused by that denial.

**Authority:** Part V of Breyer's Opinion of the Court, joined by Stone-Zsela, Stevens, Souter and Ginsburg; five direct joins.

**Controlling explanation:** Monell v. Department of Social Services requires the municipality's own policy or custom to be the moving force behind the constitutional deprivation, and Saenz v. Heldenfels Brothers kept that attribution requirement separate from individual defenses. Gonzales alleges a policy tolerating nonenforcement; that allegation is assumed at the dismissal stage and must be proved. Carey separates the constitutional violation from compensable injury. A denial of process is actionable for nominal damages without proof of other harm, but compensation requires proof that the injury resulted from the denial of process rather than from a deprivation that would have occurred with adequate process. The deaths cannot automatically measure damages merely because an enforcement duty existed, and an unsuccessful protective effort does not by itself establish either the violation or the injury.

## Precedent Treatment

- DeShaney v. Winnebago County Department of Social Services is preserved: there is no general constitutional duty to protect against private violence, and its rule does not address a state-created entitlement.
- Pinder v. Johnson and Saenz v. Heldenfels Brothers are preserved: their affirmative-deprivation rules keep their own predicates, and Saenz's separation of municipal attribution from individual defenses is applied.
- Doe v. District of Columbia is preserved: its holding that mandatory investigative procedures alone create no substantive entitlement is unchanged, and its reservation of independently created entitlements is developed by the qualifying-entitlement rule.
- Sandin v. Conner is preserved and applied for its sufficient, nonexclusive substantive-constraint principle, without transplanting its prison application; Wilkinson v. Austin is applied for safeguards that function meaningfully.
- Board of Regents v. Roth, Mathews v. Eldridge and Monell v. Department of Social Services are applied.
- Parratt v. Taylor and Hudson v. Palmer are preserved for random, unauthorized deprivations; Zinermon v. Burch is applied to the pleaded established policy, and Carey v. Piphus to damages.
- Connecticut Department of Public Safety v. Doe retains its requirement that additional process concern facts material under the governing substantive law.
- Gonzaga University v. Doe is distinguished: statutory rights enforceable under §1983 and constitutional property remain distinct inquiries.

## Law After Decision

A qualifying individualized protective entitlement is property protected by procedural due process when state law identifies the beneficiary, defines a sufficiently definite entitlement to protective services, sets objective triggers and materially constrains officials' authority to withhold the service once triggered; Colorado's protective-order statute and an individualized order establish such an entitlement. DeShaney's limit, the custody, affirmative-deprivation and discriminatory-denial doctrines, and Doe's investigative-procedure holding remain. Officers retain discretion over reasonable means and over the probable-cause and practicability judgments the statute commits to them, but not discretion to disregard the obligation; the entitlement is to the required response, not a guaranteed outcome. Mathews determines the process due without a mandatory list of safeguards or a formal emergency hearing, and Parratt and Hudson govern random, unauthorized failures. Municipal liability requires the municipality's own policy or custom causing the procedural deprivation, and Carey separates the violation from compensable injury.

## Separate Writings

**Chief Justice Stone-Zsela, concurring.** The Chief Justice joins the Court's opinion in full. He would further specify that a beneficiary plausibly denied the secured protective service by municipal policy states a procedural claim when officials fail to provide a practicable, timely opportunity to establish the statutory conditions and receive an intelligible determination. Receiving the order and material information, genuinely assessing the conditions and communicating a reason for refusal can allow a mistaken refusal to be reconsidered in time without delaying emergency action through a formal hearing. Monell still requires proof that the Town's policy caused the procedural deprivation. Any compensatory recovery must correspond to proved causal injury; the children's deaths cannot automatically measure damages merely because an enforcement duty existed.

**Justice Stevens, joined by Justice Ginsburg, concurring.** The concurrence joins the Court's opinion in full and adds that property interests protected by due process are created and defined by state law. A State that deliberately replaces general nonenforcement discretion with a specific protective duty owed to named beneficiaries creates the kind of entitlement Roth protects. Enforcement is itself the tangible service, so a beneficiary who insists on it does not claim a right to procedure alone, and refusal of the service differs from an incorrect assessment of its conditions.

The Colorado courts have the final word on the meaning of Colorado law. When a dispositive state-law question is genuinely unresolved, federal courts should obtain or await the State's authoritative construction rather than supply one of their own. No such question is dispositive here: the statute's commands and the order supply the entitlement, and a later compilation label creates no uncertainty requiring certification.

**Justice Souter, concurring.** Justice Souter joins the Court's opinion in full and writes to explain why the claim does not seek process to obtain process. A procedural due-process claim cannot rest on an interest consisting only of the procedure itself, and Doe v. District of Columbia's investigative-procedure holding rests on that principle. The benefit here is different. Colorado secures to the protected person the protective assistance itself, enforcement of the order when its conditions exist, much as state law may secure utility service, public education or benefits. The procedures by which officers determine whether those conditions exist are distinct from that assistance, and the federal process claimed concerns those determinations. Because the Court's opinion identifies the protected object in that way, preserves the exclusion of general investigative duties and promises no outcome, its rule does not convert enforcement procedure into property.

**Justice Scalia, joined by Justices O'Connor, Kennedy and Thomas, dissenting.** The dissent would reverse and restore dismissal of the reviewed municipal procedural-due-process theory. In its view, a state direction that police take specified enforcement steps upon qualifying violations does not create a beneficiary's constitutional property interest without an independently secured substantive benefit distinct from that enforcement procedure. Colorado's commands have legal force, but their object is the executive enforcement mechanism against another person. An identified beneficiary and defined triggers do not transform the prescribed steps into a separately secured benefit, and the reverse-side notice does not make the police parties to a decree. Doe v. District of Columbia distinguishes such an entitlement from mandatory investigation or enforcement procedures, and Sandin's attention to substantive constraints supplies no rule for property in police action. The order's parenting permissions also show that the claimed interest cannot be an unconditional right to the children's return.

The dissent assumes that the statutory duties are mandatory and does not rest on unlimited police discretion or on whether the interest can be sold or assigned a monetary value. It concludes that a binding direction can regulate officials without creating constitutional property in the process they must follow, so the pleaded policy cannot supply the missing interest, and it does not reach the adequacy of the process provided.
