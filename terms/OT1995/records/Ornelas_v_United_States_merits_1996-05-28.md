## Public Projection

## Event

Ornelas v. United States, 517 U.S. 690, No. 95-5257. Decided May 28, 1996; October Term 1995. On certiorari under 28 U.S.C. §1254(1), following federal appeals under §1291, reviewing the Seventh Circuit's decisions at 16 F.3d 714 and 52 F.3d 328 (table). The question concerns appellate review of ultimate reasonable suspicion for a stop and probable cause for a nonwarrant search, preserved through conditional guilty pleas.

On December 11, 1992, detectives investigated an older two-door General Motors car with California plates, motel and travel circumstances, and two NADDIS entries thought related to the men. The entries were not established to identify petitioners. Detectives approached as petitioners entered the car, obtained identification and consent, and eventually removed a vent cover and panel, recovering approximately two kilograms of cocaine. The officer's gun was not visible. The Government conceded petitioners could challenge the search and that a reasonable person would not have felt free to leave. It did not contend that the stop or consent independently authorized dismantling.

The first appellate opinion accepted reasonable suspicion under clear-error review, held inevitable discovery waived and remanded the loose-panel credibility question. On the stipulated existing record, the magistrate credited the loose-panel testimony, retained the adverse assessment of testimony about rust indicating recent removal, and found the scratches inconclusive. He recommended suppression. The district judge accepted the loose-panel credibility finding but found probable cause; the second appellate order affirmed for want of clear error. The remaining disagreement is not an unresolved conflict over that credited observation.

Both petitioners and the United States seek independent ultimate review and appellate remand, while disagreeing about suppression. Amici defend deferential review. The separate database-discovery claim and initial determination by this Court of the stop/search merits are outside the question presented.

## Holdings

### I. Review includes both preserved ultimate thresholds

**Controlling proposition:** Sections 1291 and 1254(1), following the supported conditional pleas, permit review of the appellate standard for both ultimate reasonable suspicion and probable cause. The parties' agreement does not determine that standard, and neither the separate database-discovery claim nor initial Supreme Court resolution of suppression's merits enters this review.

**Authority:** Part I of O'Connor's Opinion of the Court; all nine participating Justices join.

**Controlling explanation:** The first appellate decision applied clear-error review to reasonable suspicion; the second sustained probable cause under the same formulation after the limited remand. The independent-review contention remained preserved in both appeals. Considering only the final probable-cause discussion would leave the earlier preserved threshold governed by the contested allocation.

Rule 11(a)(2) permits a conditional plea with court approval and Government consent, reserving in writing review of a specified adverse pretrial determination. The selected orders and docket entries establish the suppression reservation here; incomplete copies of the full agreements create neither an additional reservation nor a finding of noncompliance. The Government's concession concerning petitioners' ability to challenge this search resolves no other issue by implication.

Although the parties agree on independent review, suppression remains contested and legal classification is the Court's responsibility. The Court decides the review standard and returns its application to the Court of Appeals. No database-disclosure ruling, new waiver or ultimate Fourth Amendment result follows from accepting this channel.

### II. Ultimate constitutional sufficiency is independently reviewed, with factual protection and genuine due weight

**Controlling proposition:** Appellate courts independently decide whether established circumstances, objectively considered in their totality, supply reasonable suspicion for the stop and probable cause for the nonwarrant search; no finding of clear error in the ultimate conclusion is required. Historical facts, credibility and genuinely factual subsidiary findings or inferences receive clear-error review, while grounded contextual inferences of resident judges and experienced local officers receive genuine due weight without controlling the ultimate constitutional judgment.

**Authority:** Part II of O'Connor's Opinion of the Court; the eight Justices identified for Parts II–III in Opinion Topology join.

**Controlling explanation:** Reasonable suspicion and probable cause demand different degrees of justification, but both require applying a national constitutional boundary to established circumstances. Brinegar, Gates, Sokolow and White preserve practical, objective totality analysis rather than mechanical formulas. That practical character does not answer who independently determines constitutional sufficiency.

Miller's functional approach and Thompson's treatment of ultimate custody distinguish historical findings from constitutional application without making every mixed question independently reviewable. First Options likewise preserves ordinary legal/factual review but does not itself classify these questions. Applications give constitutional standards practical content; appellate comparison supplies guidance and error correction even when a particular combination yields little general precedent. Ultimate deference could permit materially similar established conduct to receive different constitutional treatment merely because different trial conclusions survived review.

The competing argument properly emphasizes trial experience, local conditions and fact-specific probabilities. Those advantages remain protected. Courts review what happened, physical conditions and credibility for clear error. They also give genuine due weight to grounded inferences from resident judges' local knowledge and officers' experience, including supported trial assessments of credibility and inferential reasonableness. This is real contextual deference, not a direction merely to notice and disregard the assessment. But experience cannot establish an unproved observation or create a presumption that a search was lawful. Ultimate constitutional sufficiency remains independently decided.

Gates's substantial-basis review of an issuing magistrate's warrant decision serves the preference for advance judicial authorization. That institutional relationship differs from appellate review of a suppression decision after a nonwarrant search; its deference is not transplanted here. Pierce and Cooter & Gell demonstrate that other tasks can justify ultimate deference, not that all concrete applications share one allocation. Salve Regina's appellate advantages likewise require issue-specific analysis. The Court adopts no numerical weight, police-credibility preference, new evidentiary burden or intermediate standard insulating the ultimate conclusion.

### III. Both appellate judgments require reconsideration under that allocation

**Controlling proposition:** Both reviewed appellate judgments are vacated so the Court of Appeals can apply independent review to reasonable suspicion and probable cause on the established record, with the qualifications in Part II. This remand neither changes accepted historical findings nor supplies an alternative search justification, additional evidentiary proceeding or immediate plea-withdrawal entitlement.

**Authority:** Part III of the same eight-Justice Opinion of the Court.

**Controlling explanation:** Whether the panel was loose is a historical finding; its significance to an experienced officer deserves due weight; whether that fact and the remaining established circumstances satisfy the constitutional threshold is independently decided. The district judge accepted the remand credibility finding. No new hearing is needed to resolve an invented conflict about it, and neither the rejected rust testimony nor inconclusive scratches becomes additional support by invoking experience. Nor does due weight establish that the NADDIS entries identified petitioners.

Section 636(b)(1)(B) governs a magistrate's suppression recommendation, followed by the district judge's de novo determination of specifically objected-to portions. The judge may accept, reject or modify, receive evidence or recommit. Raddatz distinguishes that determination from an invariably required fresh hearing and reserves the serious question of rejecting dispositive credibility findings without hearing witnesses. Acceptance of the credited loose-panel finding does not present that reserved question. The nondispositive review provision in §636(b)(1)(A) supplies no different final suppression standard, and neither provision fixes appellate review.

For this decision the Court assumes the circuit's limitation on the consent given; it announces no universal consent rule. The existing consent supplies no ground for dismantling, and inevitable discovery was waived. Neither may be revived as an alternative affirmance ground. The database-discovery issue remains excluded.

Rule 11(a)(2) permits withdrawal when a defendant ultimately prevails on the preserved issue. Obtaining a new appellate standard and remand alone does not establish that condition. The Court orders no suppression, release, acquittal, new trial, inspection or additional evidentiary hearing. Proper challenges to historical findings remain governed by ordinary clear-error review and preservation, not new disputes created by this mandate.

## Law After Decision

Effective May 28, 1996, ultimate reasonable suspicion and probable cause in this nonwarrant appellate setting receive independent review, while historical findings receive clear-error review and grounded contextual inferences receive genuine due weight. The lower court called its ultimate review clear error. Abuse of discretion more precisely describes that rejected deferential mixed-question alternative; clear error remains the factual-review term. Neither label creates an intermediate rule replacing independent ultimate judgment.

The allocation governs otherwise authorized Government suppression appeals as well, without expanding their availability. Section 3731 retains its bar on appeals from suppression orders made after the defendant has been put in jeopardy and before verdict or finding, the United States attorney's certifications of no delay purpose and substantial proof of a material fact, thirty-day filing and diligent prosecution. Its distinct dismissal/new-trial and release provisions, double-jeopardy bar and inapplicable pre-January 2, 1971 savings provision remain unchanged. The present case proceeds through defendants' appeals, not that Government channel.

No habeas factual presumption, clear-and-convincing burden or civil Rule 52 command governs this direct criminal appeal. The ruling does not decide suppression's merits, create facts, restore waived inevitable discovery, enlarge consent or alter Rule 11(a)(2)'s condition for plea withdrawal.

## Separate Writings

Justice Scalia joins Part I but would affirm the reviewed allocation. Courts should independently correct legal definitions and legal-rule errors while deferring to the district court's concrete reasonable-suspicion and probable-cause applications under the circuit's clear-error formulation. Practical probabilities depend heavily on interacting circumstances and trial experience; many applications offer little reusable guidance. Pierce and Cooter & Gell demonstrate why that institutional comparison can favor ultimate deference even when a legal standard is applied.

Thompson's custody question and Salve Regina's state-law exposition do not require the same allocation for every mixed issue. Gates's practical observations remain relevant even though its warrant-issuance test does not directly govern this appeal. Magistrate referral does not eliminate the district judge's institutional responsibility or power to supplement the record.

Scalia regards the majority's combination of independent ultimate review and deference to contextual inferences as unstable: its factual and evaluative parts are often difficult to separate. He would preserve correction of legal errors and clear factual mistakes without independently repeating each final probability estimate. Deference would run to the district court, not a presumption that officers were correct. His response to the majority's warrant-incentive rationale also invokes Leon's distinct treatment of searches under warrants; it supplies no alternative good-faith holding on this search.

His proposed ultimate deference is noncontrolling. He does not decide suppression in the first instance, reopen inevitable discovery, resolve database disclosure or supply an immunity-style reasonable-error defense.
