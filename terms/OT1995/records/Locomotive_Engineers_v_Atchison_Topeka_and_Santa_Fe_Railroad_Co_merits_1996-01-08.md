### Waiting incident to completed-assignment return is neither duty nor rest

**Operative rule:** Waiting genuinely incident to transportation from a completed duty assignment to final release falls within 49 U.S.C. §21103(b)(4)'s return-transportation interval and counts as neither on-duty time nor required off-duty rest. Time engaged in or connected with movement of a train remains duty time under §21103(b)(2), as does any other service for the carrier during a twenty-four-hour period in which the employee is engaged in or connected with train movement under §21103(b)(3); transportation to a new duty assignment remains on duty.

**Authority:** Justice Kennedy's Opinion of the Court, joined by Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg, and Breyer. All nine adopt the rule, its limits, and affirmance.

**Controlling explanation:** Transportation describes the undertaking of returning the crew, including awaiting the vehicle that carries it. Nothing in the specific return provision confines that undertaking to minutes of physical movement. The general reporting-to-final-release rule in §21103(b)(1) must leave room for the express return category in §21103(b)(4). Otherwise the general language would defeat the exception Congress placed beside it.

The unions emphasize that waiting crews remain subject to the railroad's direction and may become fatigued. Those circumstances do not distinguish the wait from the return journey Congress expressly excluded from duty. Nor does the exclusion declare the time restorative: Congress also withheld off-duty credit. The separate provisions counting actual service and certain interim rest as duty continue to govern their own predicates. An employer's transportation label cannot remove work from those provisions.

MCI Telecommunications requires an independent determination of the statutory boundary; Town & Country permits agency construction only within the statutory range. The enacted categories resolve this question without reliance on an agency's litigation position or administrative history. No general deference doctrine is changed.

The question concerns waiting after assignment completion without further train-movement responsibility or other service. It does not require a judicial maximum waiting duration, a finding that waiting causes no fatigue, or a decision about a particular worker's compensation, penalty, or rest entitlement. The Seventh Circuit's classification is affirmed.

**Operative qualifications:** Section 21103(a)'s ordinary eight-consecutive-hours-off requirement within the preceding twenty-four hours, and its ten-consecutive-hours-off requirement after twelve consecutive hours on duty, remain. Section (c)'s distinct emergency permission for a wreck or relief crew allows up to four additional hours in twenty-four for emergency-related work and ends when the track is cleared and the line reopened; that permission is not invoked here. Sections (b)(5)–(6) count interim rest outside a designated terminal, or shorter than four hours at a designated terminal, as duty. Section (b)(7) separately excludes an interim period available for at least four hours of rest at a place with suitable food and lodging facilities when a casualty, track obstruction, act of God, or derailment or major equipment failure prevents the employee from reaching the designated terminal; for derailment or major equipment failure, the cause must have been unknown and unforeseeable to the carrier or its officer or agent in charge of that employee when the employee left the designated terminal. Those predicates are neither found nor excused by this decision.

**Precedent treatment:** MCI's statutory-boundary inquiry and Town & Country's bounded agency-construction rule remain intact; neither supplies an independent deference ground or a contrary classification here.
