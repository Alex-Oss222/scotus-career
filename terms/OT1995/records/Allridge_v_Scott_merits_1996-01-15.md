## Holdings

### Ordinary parole availability defeats the asserted release-prediction entitlement

**Operative rule:** Simmons's right to explain legal parole ineligibility does not require a favorable prediction about eventual release when the noncapital alternative permits ordinary parole. The rejection of that asserted entitlement is affirmed; this conclusion neither categorically bars truthful parole evidence nor authorizes a misleading description of the lawful sentencing alternative.

**Authority:** Part I-A of Justice Souter's Opinion of the Court, joined by Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Thomas, Ginsburg, and Breyer. All nine adopt this eligibility-versus-prediction ground.

**Controlling explanation:** Simmons v. South Carolina protects an effective opportunity to tell the jury that the only lawful noncapital sentence excludes ordinary parole when the State raises future dangerousness. That legal fact answers the State's sentencing case; speculative clemency does not change it. Here ordinary parole was legally available. A prediction that release is improbable therefore cannot be substituted for Simmons's absent predicate. The Court does not adopt the lower court's suggestion that danger to inmates universally defeats Simmons. The claim fails because of ordinary parole availability, without reaching harmlessness.

**Precedent treatment:** Simmons retains its legal-ineligibility rule and is distinguished on its express predicate, not confined by a new prison-danger exception.

### Admitted mitigation must be assessed by its possible independent culpability significance

**Operative rule:** A court may not reject a preserved claim for effective consideration of admitted capital mitigation merely because the evidence fails to establish a uniquely severe permanent disability to which the crime is conclusively attributable. When the actual evidence supports material mitigation of culpability independent of deliberateness or future danger, the court must determine whether, considering the charge, evidence, and trial as a whole, there is a reasonable likelihood that jurors could not give it effect while answering the special issues truthfully; mere possibility is insufficient.

**Authority:** Part II of Souter's Opinion of the Court, joined by Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Thomas, Ginsburg, and Breyer. All nine adopt this correction of the reviewing rule and bounded remand, without finding an actual instructional violation.

**Controlling explanation:** Graham v. Collins requires an effective sentencing route for record-supported mitigation with independent culpability significance and rejects a uniquely severe permanent-handicap restriction. Its background holding does not require that the evidence lack all relevance to future danger. Johnson v. Texas applies the whole-charge reasonable-likelihood inquiry: admission and partial future-danger relevance do not suffice if truthful special-issue answers prevent the supported mitigating use. Those rules preserve Jurek's conditional approval of the Texas scheme while enforcing Lockett and Eddings's requirement that relevant mitigation receive lawful effect.

The father testified about alleged mental illness and prior prison abuse. The lower court rejected the claim because his nonexpert account failed to establish the crime-attribution and severe-disability linkage. That categorical requirement is no longer a lawful reviewing premise. His lack of diagnostic expertise limits what the testimony proves; it does not erase admitted lay evidence or establish that no abuse occurred.

The competing contention—that the testimony may still be weak, unsupported, or adequately usable under the actual charge—remains for proper review. The Court has not found psychiatric illness, credibility, causation, material independent mitigating force, or the absence of an effective sentencing route. The jury need not assign favorable weight. No categorical additional special issue, facial invalidity, or new general mitigation category follows. Because the stated lower ground is legally insufficient, the rejection of this admitted-evidence claim is vacated and remanded.

**Precedent treatment:** Graham's youth and background rules and prefinality analysis are applied; its fractured positive-character component is not enlarged. Johnson and Boyde supply the functional reasonable-likelihood inquiry. Tuggle preserves the distinction between correcting an appellate rationale and finding a present entitlement to a writ.

### Unoffered medical evidence supplies no independent instruction entitlement

**Operative rule:** A defendant cannot obtain a mitigation instruction on the assumed content of medical evidence never presented merely because presenting it might also support future dangerousness. The independent instruction claim based on that unoffered evidence fails, while a supported challenge to an actual unconstitutional exclusion or legal impediment remains distinct and is not decided here.

**Authority:** Part III of Souter's Opinion of the Court, joined by Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Thomas, Ginsburg, and Breyer. Eight adopt the rationale; Stone-Zsela agrees with rejection of this component in the judgment only and adds no ground.

**Controlling explanation:** Delo v. Lashley permits a requirement of some evidence supporting a requested mitigating circumstance while protecting meaningful use of mitigation actually offered. Evidence carrying both mitigating and aggravating implications can create a difficult strategic choice. That concern alone does not establish the missing diagnosis, its content, admissibility, or mitigating significance, and does not require jurors to act on assumed proof. The separate admitted-evidence inquiry continues under Part II. This ruling does not compel a defendant to present evidence formally excluded by an unconstitutional rule, foreclose a supported challenge to an actual legal barrier, or adjudicate a new ineffective-assistance claim.

**Precedent treatment:** Delo's evidentiary predicate is applied without an expert-only rule, an adverse inference from silence, or a reduction of the State's aggravation burden. Graham's protection of supported mitigation remains intact.

## Law After Decision

Ordinary parole availability does not become legal ineligibility through a prediction of unlikely release. Admitted mitigation must receive the functional inquiry required by Graham and Johnson; an expert diagnosis or uniquely severe disability is not a categorical entrance requirement. Unoffered medical material establishes no independent instruction entitlement merely because its introduction would present strategic risks. The actual instructional violation, available habeas gates, any error-effect standard and finding, and relief remain unresolved. The proposed Chapman extension has no controlling force.

## Separate Writings
