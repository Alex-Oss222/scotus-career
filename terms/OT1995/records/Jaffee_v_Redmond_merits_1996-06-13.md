## Event

**Jaffee v. Redmond, 518 U.S. 1, No. 95-266 — June 13, 1996.** Argued February 26, 1996, on certiorari to the Seventh Circuit, 51 F.3d 1346. ## Holdings

**Authority:** Stevens's opinion controls on each rule below, joined by all eight judgment supporters. The future-contours illustration is not necessary to any holding.

### I. Confidential psychotherapy communications are privileged

**Operative rule:** Federal common law protects confidential communications between a patient and a licensed psychotherapist in the course of diagnosis or treatment, including confidential psychotherapy provided by a licensed clinical social worker. The protection covers the treatment conversations and counseling notes at issue; a professional title alone does not protect administrative conversations, ordinary workplace counseling or communications outside a confidential treatment relationship.

**Controlling explanation:** Rule501 directs courts to interpret common-law privilege through reason and experience, subject to the Constitution, Acts of Congress and rules prescribed under statutory authority. Trammel supplies the responsibility to examine a privilege's present justification; University of Pennsylvania requires a defensible benefit and boundary rather than an assumption that secrecy is always useful. Effective psychotherapy depends on frank disclosure, which predictable confidentiality makes possible. Its benefit extends beyond the patient to the treatment of mental illness.

Widespread state recognition corroborates that judgment, without proving that every State uses identical definitions or exceptions. Proposed Rule504 reflects considered professional judgment but was not enacted and does not govern. The loss of relevant evidence is serious, especially in a dispute about a killing. Rule501 nevertheless permits a defined relationship privilege where its public function justifies that cost.

Beyer's license, training and actual psychotherapy bring this treatment within the rule. Clinical social workers perform the same protected function and provide access for patients unable to afford other providers; the record supplies no confidentiality distinction warranting exclusion of this relationship. The holding creates no general physician-patient privilege or privilege for every conversation with a social worker.

Rule501's separate state-law sentence remains operative for an element of a civil claim or defense governed by state law. Recognition here makes it unnecessary to decide which privilege law would govern evidence relevant to both federal and state claims if the two laws conflict. The Court adopts no general federal-preemption rule for that situation.

### II. Protection does not depend on routine balancing in each lawsuit

**Operative rule:** A communication within this privilege does not lose protection merely because a judge finds the opposing party's evidentiary need more important in the particular lawsuit. A valid assertion of the privilege cannot support the adverse inference and lack-of-legal-justification instruction given here.

**Controlling explanation:** A patient must know the protection's character when speaking. If disclosure turns on an unpredictable later assessment of usefulness, the uncertainty can deter the very communication that makes treatment effective. The Seventh Circuit's reliance on the availability of other eyewitnesses cannot establish a general balancing test within the privilege.

United States v. Nixon concerns a presumptive executive privilege and demonstrated criminal-trial need; it does not require routine balancing inside every recognized confidential relationship. Daubert and Williamson concern admissibility and reliability under different evidence rules, not this relationship's protection under Rule501.

Rejecting general balancing does not make the privilege exceptionless. A patient may waive it. The account of partial responses and three redacted pages supplies no adjudicated waiver of all treatment communications; whether any waiver occurred remains undecided. The Court decides no waiver rule, court-ordered-examination rule, litigation-use exception, criminal constitutional override or serious-threat dispute. No adverse inference may rest on a valid assertion of the protected core. Because the actual instruction penalized withholding protected treatment material, the appellate new-trial judgment stands without its balancing rationale.

**Reserved contours in the Court's opinion.** Stevens, O'Connor, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer observe that the privilege must yield in a hypothetical situation where a serious threat to the patient or others can be averted only by therapist disclosure. No such threat is found here; the illustration decides no operative exception, procedure or ordinary evidentiary-need claim. Stone-Zsela does not join that affirmative illustration. All eight leave the actual boundaries for a case presenting them.

## Law After Decision

Federal common law now protects the defined confidential psychotherapy relationship, including licensed clinical social-work psychotherapy. Evidentiary usefulness alone does not defeat the privilege, and valid withholding cannot be penalized by the instruction used here. State-law privilege obligations under Rule501 remain distinct. Waiver and other particular limits require their own supported questions; this decision supplies no comprehensive code of exceptions and overrules no earlier decision.

## Separate Writings

**Scalia, dissenting.** Rule501 authorizes common-law development, but the dissent finds the asserted benefit insufficient to justify withholding reliable evidence about disputed conduct. The possibility that confidentiality assists treatment does not by itself establish how much this evidentiary privilege adds to ordinary professional secrecy, while lost evidence directly impairs the adjudicative task. University of Pennsylvania supplies the need for a supported justification, not a categorical prohibition on creating privileges.

The dissent also disputes the professional boundary. Proposed Rule504 did not include social workers and was never enacted; differences in training, functions and state exceptions caution against treating all such treatment as one established category. Scalia would reverse the federal-privilege basis for the new-trial judgment, leaving unresolved matters in their proper channels. He does not find wholesale waiver or hold that every note must be disclosed, and his objections create no controlling balancing alternative.
