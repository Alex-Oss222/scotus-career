## Event, participation and material record

Review under 28 U.S.C. §1254(1) concerns the Seventh Circuit's November 10, 1994 judgment, 40 F.3d 879, insofar as it sustains Rutledge's separate convictions and concurrent life sentences for conspiracy under 21 U.S.C. §846 and conducting a continuing criminal enterprise under §848. The question is whether the conspiracy is included in CCE and whether both judgments and punishments may remain. The petitioner's reply requests vacatur of either challenged conviction and corresponding sentence, initially at the sentencing court's selection; the Government's preference to retain CCE is not a contrary concession by petitioner. No companion Supreme Court matter is consolidated here.

Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer participate at argument and decision. No recusal or other nonparticipation is established. Nine participate, satisfying the six-Justice quorum; five votes suffice for a judgment or proposition majority. Ordinary merits review is available. The Court reaches the elements, cumulative-conviction, assessment and count-specific remedial questions. It does not reopen the other defendants' consolidated appeals or Rutledge's unrelated rejected objections.

The second superseding indictment charges Count 1's enterprise from early 1988 through late 1990, comprising a continuing series of §§841(a)(1)/846 violations, concert with five or more other persons whom Rutledge organized, supervised or managed, and substantial income or resources. Count 2 charges the same-period knowing cocaine-distribution agreement. The same agreement supports both counts. The Government's unwitting-participant theory challenges the meaning of the concert element, not that record premise.

The December 29, 1992 judgment followed six guilty verdicts. Count 1 carries life imprisonment; Count 2 carries a concurrent life-without-possible-release term. Count 3 independently carries a concurrent life term; Count 4 carries ten years; the firearm terms are consecutive. The total special assessment is $300, six times $50. No fine, incarceration costs or restitution was imposed. Correction of Counts 1–2 neither releases Rutledge nor disturbs Counts 3–6. The existing assessment establishes a monetary sanction without a finding about payment, collection, refund amount or expiration.

## Holdings

### I. The CCE concert requirement includes agreement

**Holding:** Section 848(c)'s requirement of action in concert with five or more other persons requires mutual agreement in a common criminal plan or enterprise. In the narcotics setting presented, §846 conspiracy is therefore a lesser included offense of CCE; the same agreement supports both challenged counts.

**Authority:** Part I, joined by all eight Associates. Stone-Zsela additionally joins the application only at the level of these offenses as charged and tried, where the CCE verdict necessarily establishes every conspiracy element.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court compares statutory elements, not the mere overlap of evidence. Shabani requires a knowing narcotics agreement proved beyond a reasonable doubt, without an additional overt act. CCE requires the agreement and adds the continuing series, five-other-person, managerial and substantial-income-or-resource requirements. The concert language requires mutual commitment to the unlawful plan. An organizer cannot satisfy it merely by using people wholly unaware of that plan; individual agreements with the necessary participants can supply the requisite conspiracy without a universally prescribed organizational form.

Jeffers supplied the textual reasoning but assumed the included-offense conclusion. The Court now resolves it. The Government's unwitting-participant hypothetical does not give §846 an element absent from the statutory concert requirement. Here the same agreement supplied Count 2 and the concert element of Count 1. This construction does not place a subchapter-II-only enterprise within §846's distinct subchapter-I coverage. A distinct agreement, different offense or separate substantive predicate requires its own analysis; successive-prosecution questions remain separate.

### II. Congress did not authorize both judgments for this agreement

**Holding:** Congress did not authorize cumulative convictions and punishment under §§846 and 848 for the included agreement at issue. Concurrent imprisonment does not cure the unauthorized second conviction.

**Authority:** Part II, joined by all eight Associates. Stone-Zsela additionally joins Ball's second-conviction and concurrency propositions and the conclusion that these two judgments cannot remain, on his charged-and-tried ground.

**Controlling explanation:** Under Hunter, the single-prosecution inquiry concerns the punishment Congress authorized. Blockburger's elements comparison and Whalen's presumption weigh against cumulative punishment for an included offense. Jeffers's rejection of cumulative punishment and Garrett's distinction between conspiracy and substantive predicates confirm the absence of authorization here. Separate section numbers, CCE's additional elements and severe punishment, and the usefulness of a backup conviction do not overcome that conclusion.

Jeffers's retention of judgments does not decide this question otherwise. Only the later CCE judgment was under review; the plurality relied on the defendant's separate-trial election, while Justice White treated the offenses differently. Silence about an unreviewed judgment creates no controlling authorization for two present convictions.

Ball establishes that a second conviction remains a sanction despite concurrent imprisonment. Potential collateral consequences cannot be erased by combining labels, and the actual assessment supplies a concrete additional penalty. Harris and Bishop/Stokes apply §924(c)'s express additional-and-consecutive direction to their different statutory pairing; §§846 and 848 contain no corresponding authorization here. Garrett's separate substantive-predicate rule and Felix's distinction between conspiracy and its substantive objects remain intact. This holding requires no constitutional punishment rule independent of congressional authorization.

### III. The second assessment is a present consequence of the challenged judgment

**Holding:** The mandatory $50 assessment attached to the second felony conviction is actual additional punishment and may be considered in resolving the preserved challenge to the dual judgments. Vacatur must remove the selected conviction's assessment.

**Authority:** Part III, joined by all eight Associates. Stone-Zsela additionally joins the actual-sanction and assessment-removal propositions, without joining the separate presentation ruling.

**Controlling explanation:** Received §3013 imposes a $50 assessment for each individual felony conviction. Unlike concurrent prison time, these separate monetary sanctions accumulate. Considering that automatic consequence answers the Government's assertion that the second judgment imposes no additional punishment; it does not introduce an unrelated monetary claim. The Government's objection that the assessment was not separately challenged below therefore does not require ignoring it for this purpose. The Court adopts no general exception to preservation rules.

Ball also forecloses treating the second conviction as nonexistent. No conclusive prediction about parole, future recidivist treatment, impeachment or marginal stigma is necessary given the actual assessment. Removing one $50 assessment reduces the existing six-count total from $300 to $250. The Court makes no finding concerning payment, collection, a refund amount or an expired obligation, and decides no freestanding refund claim or assessment on another count.

### IV. One conviction must be vacated, with the initial choice left to the District Court

**Holding:** The District Court must vacate either Count 1 or Count 2, its entire corresponding life sentence and its $50 assessment, choosing initially within lawful discretion. Retaining both convictions while vacating only one sentence, staying execution or applying a combined label is insufficient.

**Authority:** Part IV, joined by all eight Associates. Stone-Zsela additionally joins the requirement to remove one judgment, sentence and assessment without reopening Counts 3–6; he dissents from permission to select CCE for vacatur.

**Controlling explanation:** Ball requires removal of an unauthorized conviction and leaves selection to the sentencing court. Rutledge's own requested relief permits either count; the Government's narrower preference to preserve CCE does not bind him or require a conspiracy-only mandate. The Court therefore corrects the dual-judgment error while preserving the District Court's lawful initial selection.

A second conviction does not become permissible through a different label or a stay of its execution. The selected judgment, its sentence and assessment must be vacated. The surviving challenged conviction and Counts 3–6 remain undisturbed, including their existing sentences and assessments and Count 3's independent life term. The order does not erase the fact that the jury returned the verdicts, require a prospective stay-of-entry system, reopen unpresented objections or direct general resentencing. It orders neither release nor a new trial.

### V. Qualified appellate correction answers the asserted need for a backup conviction

**Holding:** An appellate court may, in an appropriate case, direct entry of judgment on a valid lesser included offense when reversal rests on a defect affecting only the greater offense. That existing power does not require two present convictions and does not make later reinstatement automatic.

**Authority:** Part V, joined by all eight Associates. Stone-Zsela does not join this proposition.

**Controlling explanation:** The Government's backup concern does not justify retaining an unauthorized second judgment now. Section 2106 supplies corrective appellate authority. Tinder illustrates that authority through correction of a sentence after felony treatment failed, while retaining the underlying conviction; it did not decide revival of a vacated conspiracy conviction. Ward provides the closer example: the CCE conviction failed for insufficient five-person supervisory proof, the conspiracy conviction had been vacated, and the defendant conceded reinstatement before remand. That concession is part of the example, not a rule that consent is always required or irrelevant.

A valid conspiracy verdict may thus support appropriate lesser-offense relief after a CCE-specific failure. But a ground invalidating the agreement or otherwise tainting that verdict cannot be ignored. The precise limits, procedural vehicle and relief in a future contested application remain open. The Court orders no restoration now, adopts no automatic-revival or general finality exception, and decides no entitlement to retrial, compelled lesser judgment or harmlessness of a future error. Present vacatur for duplication is not an evidentiary acquittal. An earlier separate conspiracy prosecution, previously unknown CCE facts and a noncoterminous enterprise present questions this case does not decide.

## Precedent Treatment

- **Blockburger v. United States, 284 U.S. 299:** Applies the statutory-elements inquiry to the included agreement; does not replace it with an evidentiary same-conduct test.
- **Whalen v. United States, 445 U.S. 684, and Missouri v. Hunter, 459 U.S. 359:** Apply the presumption against unauthorized cumulation and Congress's authority over punishment imposed in one prosecution. Their distinct roles remain intact.
- **Jeffers v. United States, 432 U.S. 137:** Resolves its assumed included-offense premise and applies its no-cumulative-punishment conclusion; its procedural disposition supplies no contrary dual-judgment holding.
- **Ball v. United States, 470 U.S. 856:** Applies its recognition of a second conviction's consequences and its requirement to vacate one conviction, with initial selection left to the sentencing court. No new multiple-charging prohibition is adopted.
- **Garrett v. United States, 471 U.S. 773:** Preserves its substantive-predicate rule and distinguishes the shared conspiracy agreement; its successive-prosecution questions are not resolved here.
- **United States v. Shabani, 513 U.S. 10:** Applies its knowing-agreement requirement and absence of an overt-act element under §846.
- **United States v. Harris and United States v. Bishop and Stokes:** Preserve their source-specific express-authorization holdings. Their §924(c) direction does not authorize this statutory pairing.
- **United States v. Felix, 503 U.S. 378, and United States v. Dixon, 509 U.S. 688:** Preserve the agreement/substantive-offense distinction and the retained Grady rule within successive-prosecution law. This single-prosecution disposition changes neither.
- **Witte v. United States, 515 U.S. 389:** Distinguishes authorized relevant-conduct sentencing from entry of an additional conviction; leaves its separate conditional sentence-coordination rule unchanged.
- **Tinder v. United States, 345 U.S. 565:** Uses its bounded §2106 corrective authority without treating its sentence correction as a prior decision reinstating a vacated conspiracy conviction.

## Law After Decision

The statutory concert requirement now receives a definite agreement construction, and the included §846 narcotics agreement cannot support both challenged judgments absent congressional authorization. Existing single-prosecution authorization and second-conviction principles remain governing; a second $50 assessment is a concrete sanction despite concurrent prison terms. One conviction, its sentence and assessment must be removed, with lawful initial count selection left to the District Court.

Qualified appellate entry of a valid lesser conviction may answer a later greater-only reversal; it is not automatic restoration. The exact future procedure, defects affecting the lesser verdict, distinct agreements, subchapter-II-only offenses, separate substantive predicates and successive prosecutions retain the limits stated above. The eight-Justice general holdings have direct support; the additional narrower joins do not reduce their scope.

## Separate Writings

Chief Justice Stone-Zsela concurs in part and dissents in part. He begins with Blockburger's element comparison and the offenses as charged and tried. Here the CCE verdict necessarily incorporated the same agreement and concerted conduct supporting conspiracy, so every element of the §846 count was necessarily established. His conclusion is confined to that elements-and-trial structure; it does not bar convictions for distinct conspiracies or offenses requiring an element not necessarily included in the CCE count.

Ball makes the second judgment consequential even though imprisonment runs concurrently. The separate assessment, stigma and other collateral effects prevent treating the included conviction as an empty entry. On that basis, the Double Jeopardy Clause requires removal of the included judgment. His partial joins adopt the corresponding case-specific application, second-conviction consequences and assessment correction; they do not adopt the Court's broader statutory construction, fuller congressional-intent analysis, preservation ruling or future appellate-entry proposition.

His remedial disagreement is concrete. Because conspiracy is the included offense established by this CCE verdict, he would direct vacatur of Count 2 and its special assessment and leave the CCE conviction and sentence undisturbed. He therefore agrees that the dual-conviction ruling must be reversed but dissents from allowing the District Court to vacate CCE instead. No other Justice joins his separate writing.

## Procedure After Action

The case returns through the Seventh Circuit for the District Court to choose and vacate one of Counts 1 and 2, its corresponding sentence and its $50 assessment. The other challenged conviction and Counts 3–6 remain undisturbed. No later challenge or restoration is ordered. The precise procedure and relief in any future contested lesser-offense application remain open, subject to the limits stated in Part V.
