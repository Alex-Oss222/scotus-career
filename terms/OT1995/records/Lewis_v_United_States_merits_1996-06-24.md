## Holdings

### The common rule forbids aggregate imprisonment above six months after this form of nonjury prosecution

**Operative rule:** In a single prosecution of joined petty statutory offenses, when the defendant's timely jury demand has not been waived, aggregate imprisonment exceeding six months may not be imposed after a nonjury trial. This prohibition does not establish majority agreement about when the jury right attaches, whether an advance sentencing cap permits trial without a jury, or what mechanism makes such a cap valid.

**Authority:** Stone-Zsela's concurrence, Kennedy's concurrence joined by Breyer, and Stevens's dissent joined by Ginsburg directly support this exact prohibition. These five votes establish the proposition, although they do not share a disposition or a complete theory of jury entitlement. The rule is not an aggregation of the seven votes to affirm or a synthesis of supposed narrower affirmance grounds under Marks.

**Controlling explanation:** Five Justices agree that dividing a prosecution into individually petty counts cannot permit aggregate imprisonment above six months following a nonjury trial over an unwaived timely jury demand. Their disagreement concerns the earlier consequences of authorized exposure and whether this advance limitation permits the convictions.

Kennedy and Breyer directly prohibit the greater aggregate term as the constitutional limit of such a nonjury trial. Stone requires an irrevocable limit before trial and forbids lifting it through disposition. Stevens and Ginsburg would require a jury from the commencement of this prosecution; their broader rule necessarily and expressly forbids imposing the same greater term following its nonjury adjudication. No supporter treats eventual leniency as a retrospective cure.

Codispoti's protection against aggregate serious confinement extends to this minimum prohibition for joined statutory petty counts. The writings do not agree that every feature of contempt procedure or its actual-sentence measure transfers to statutory offenses. Baldwin, Blanton and Nachtigal retain the independent rule for an individually serious offense and the complete additional-penalty inquiry. The present six-month imprisonment prohibition cannot be used to declare an otherwise serious offense petty.

Lewis received concurrent probation, so the judgment requires no enforcement remedy for a sentence exceeding this limit. Whether his jury entitlement nevertheless arose before trial divides the Justices. The Court's affirmance resolves his case without establishing a common rule validating caps or categorical nonaggregation.

## Precedent Treatment

- **Duncan v. Louisiana, 391 U.S. 145, and Baldwin v. New York, 399 U.S. 66:** Preserve the jury guarantee for serious offenses and the rule that a single offense authorizing more than six months' imprisonment is serious. The present fracture does not permit reducing an independently serious offense merely through promised leniency.
- **Blanton v. North Las Vegas, 489 U.S. 538, and United States v. Nachtigal, February 22, 1993:** Preserve the rebuttable petty-offense presumption. Additional authorized penalties, considered with maximum imprisonment, can rebut it when sufficiently severe to clearly reflect a legislative judgment of seriousness. Government-imposed penalties, including authorized fines and probation conditions, remain relevant; speculative private consequences do not supply the statutory seriousness inquiry. Neither case had decided this statutory aggregation question.
- **Codispoti v. Pennsylvania, 418 U.S. 506:** Its protection against aggregate serious confinement informs the five-Justice prohibition here. The four-Justice plurality would confine its aggregate actual-sentence analysis to the distinct contempt setting; that limitation does not command a majority.
- **Taylor v. Hayes, 418 U.S. 488:** Preserves the distinct contempt setting in which actual punishment can supply the measure absent a legislative maximum. Its treatment does not produce a majority statutory-cap doctrine here.
- **International Union, United Mine Workers of America v. Bagwell, June 30, 1994:** Preserves jury safeguards for serious criminal contempt and its limits concerning petty and direct contempt. It supplies no universal statutory aggregation or sentencing-cap rule.
- **United States v. Gaudin, June 19, 1995:** Preserves jury resolution of every element when the jury right applies. Its allocation holding does not settle the petty-offense threshold.

No precedent is overruled.

## Separate Writings

### O'Connor's plurality, joined by Scalia, Souter and Thomas

The plurality would affirm because legislative seriousness is measured offense by offense. Each mail-obstruction count is petty under Blanton's full test, and joinder alone does not alter Congress's classification. The additional fine, assessment and probation package supplies no independent ground for treating either offense as serious.

Codispoti's aggregate actual-sentence analysis concerns contempt without a legislative maximum and the concentration of judicial power in that setting. Statutory crimes supply Congress's own seriousness measure. The plurality would therefore reject aggregation here without deciding whether the magistrate's cap was effective. The concurrent-sentence default does not establish that consecutive sentences were legally unavailable. This four-Justice rationale does not become Court law, and it announces no universal approval of prosecutorial evasion, new waiver or harmlessness rule.

### Kennedy's concurrence, joined by Breyer

Kennedy and Breyer reject a rule allowing unlimited cumulative imprisonment in one nonjury prosecution simply because each count is petty. In their view, a bench trial of joined petty charges constitutionally cannot yield aggregate imprisonment above six months. The magistrate's advance ruling made clear that this trial would remain within that limit; the constitutional analysis itself supplies the ceiling, without an invented independent statutory cap.

They distinguish an individually serious offense, which carries legislative stigma and a jury right that a short-sentence promise cannot remove, from the aggregate liberty loss created by multiple petty counts. Codispoti and Taylor are not irrelevant merely because they involved contempt. Here the advance announcement supports affirmance; eventual probation is not its retrospective justification. Their reasoning supplies no finding of charging abuse or rule for every sequence of separate prosecutions.

### Stone-Zsela's concurrence

The Chief Justice first aggregates the exposure and then asks whether a lawful, irrevocable limitation actually removes serious punishment from the prosecution. The jury guarantee concerns the punishment the accused legally faces, not the labels on the counts.

Here the magistrate's unqualified announcement before trial operates with the constitutional prohibition on greater aggregate nonjury imprisonment. It binds the court through disposition and cannot be lifted after the jury demand is denied. That constitutional limit supplies enforceability; no separate statutory reduction, sentencing order or waiver is assumed. The other applicable penalties do not independently establish seriousness under Blanton.

An informal promise, revocable prediction, limitation imposed only after trial or cap omitting independently serious punishment would fail this analysis. Stone does not join categorical nonaggregation or rely on the probation actually imposed to cure a prior denial. His requirement of an effective advance cap remains distinct from Kennedy and Breyer's complete rationale.

### Stevens's dissent, joined by Ginsburg

Stevens and Ginsburg would reverse and remand because the joined prosecution authorized a year of imprisonment. The legislative measure remains controlling, but it must be applied to the prosecution as a whole. Blanton's single-offense facts did not require choosing between the offense and prosecution as the relevant unit.

Codispoti supports aggregation of serious confinement. Where contempt lacks a legislative maximum, actual punishment supplies a proxy; that feature does not confine the jury's protection to contempt. The right here attached when prosecution began. A judge's promise of leniency cannot alter the statutory exposure or remove that attached right, and the eventual probation terms do not cure its denial.

The dissent rejects the proposed distinction between the stigma of one serious offense and the serious liberty exposure produced by several petty counts. Its broader entitlement necessarily prohibits aggregate imprisonment above six months following this nonjury trial, but its proposed remedy is more extensive: reversal for lawful jury-compliant proceedings. It orders no acquittal and makes no finding about guilt.
