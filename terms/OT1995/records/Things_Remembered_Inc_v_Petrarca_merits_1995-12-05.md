## Controlling holdings

### The ordinary covered-remand bar applies in bankruptcy removals

**Operative rule:** When a removed case is remanded to the State court from which it came because of a timely raised defect in removal procedure or lack of subject-matter jurisdiction, §1447(d), read with §1447(c), bars appellate review even if removal also invoked §1452. An order remanding a case removed pursuant to §1443 may be reviewed on appeal or otherwise; that then-applicable exception is not implicated here. The rule does not declare every bankruptcy remand unreviewable.

**Authority:** Thomas's opinion, joined in full by all eight other Justices identified in Opinion Topology, supplies nine direct votes for this holding and its application. The two concurrences add no limitation to those joins and no independent majority ground.

**Controlling explanation:** Thermtron requires reading the review restriction with the grounds listed in §1447(c). A procedural-remand motion must be made within thirty days after removal; lack of subject-matter jurisdiction is a distinct covered ground. The district court remanded this action for untimely removal, and the remand request was timely raised. The actual stated ground thus falls within the ordinary bar. Gravitt makes clear that an asserted error in a covered remand ground does not open an appeal to decide its correctness.

Rice applies the ordinary remand-review restriction across removal statutes absent contrary congressional direction. Section 1452(b) separately permits equitable remand and restricts review of the decisions it covers. It grants no entitlement to review forbidden by §1447(d). Germain requires giving overlapping statutes effect where they do not positively conflict. The specialized bankruptcy provision can perform its additional work without displacing the ordinary restriction. Petitioner's exclusivity theory mistakes an additional statutory regime for a repeal of a compatible rule.

The Sixth Circuit therefore properly dismissed the attempted appeal. The Court does not determine the ultimate scope of equitable grounds under §1452(b), the merits of bankruptcy jurisdiction or removal, the rent claim, or reviewability of remands outside the present statutory grounds.

**Precedent treatment:** Thermtron supplies the covered-ground boundary; Rice supplies application across removal statutes; Gravitt preserves the restriction despite asserted error; Germain supplies coexistence rather than implied exclusivity.

- **United States v. Rice, 327 U.S. 742:** Applied to the ordinary restriction's operation across removal statutes absent contrary direction.

The §1447 covered-ground review restriction applies cumulatively with the bankruptcy-remand regime where their conditions overlap. Classification of the stated remand ground permits the appellate-jurisdiction decision; it does not permit review of that ground's correctness. Ginsburg and Stevens's additional §1452(b) construction remains noncontrolling, and no universal rule for all remands results.

## Published separate positions and future continuity

**Kennedy, joined by Ginsburg, concurring.** They join the Court's opinion with the express understanding that the present covered-ground holding does not decide reviewability of a remand under Carnegie-Mellon University v. Cohill. Thermtron held both that a particular unauthorized remand was improper and that the ordinary review bar did not cover remands outside §1447(c). Cohill later recognized discretion to remand remaining state-law claims after elimination of the sole federal claim, despite the absence of express statutory authorization. Cohill did not decide whether §1447(d) bars review of that distinct remand. Neither today's affirmance nor these two Justices' reservation resolves that issue in either direction.

**Ginsburg, joined by Stevens, concurring.** They also find an independent review bar in §1452(b). Equity there means fairness or appropriateness, not the former division between legal and equitable pleading. Failure to remove promptly fits that meaning. Making a statutory time bar reviewable while equivalent delay labeled laches is immune would make appellate jurisdiction depend on a label. Hernandez v. Brakegate supplies the modern sense of equity; Sykes v. Texas Air explains the incoherence of label-dependent review. The chapter's structure and the 1977 House report's parallel treatment of remand review support a supplementary bankruptcy-remand regime, while Timbers directs attention to the coherent statutory scheme.

They give an alternative explanation as well: even if a procedural or jurisdictional impediment were called legal rather than equitable, equity may describe the choice to remand rather than dismiss. Section 1452(b) permits that fair disposition without requiring the impediment itself to fit an old equitable category. Their position concerns remands of claims related to bankruptcy. It does not insulate lack of subject-matter jurisdiction in a retained case: a decision not to remand may lack a separate immediate appeal while jurisdiction remains open to district-court reconsideration and eventual review through an otherwise proper channel. Their additional ground neither answers the separate Cohill question outside its statutory coverage nor commands a majority of the Court.
