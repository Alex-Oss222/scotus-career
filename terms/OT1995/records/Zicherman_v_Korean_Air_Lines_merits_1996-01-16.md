## Holdings

### The Convention leaves compensable loss to applicable domestic law

**Operative rule:** The Warsaw Convention's reference to damage does not supply an independent, comprehensive code identifying every compensable loss or beneficiary. Domestic law supplies those matters, subject to the Convention's own conditions and limits; the parties' accepted use of United States law permits decision here without choosing a general international conflicts rule.

**Authority:** Justice O'Connor's Opinion of the Court, joined in full by all eight other Justices, controls this holding and the following holding.

**Controlling explanation:** Article 17 identifies the accident and passenger injury giving rise to carrier liability. Article 24(2) leaves questions concerning the persons entitled to sue and their respective rights without prejudice. Neither provision establishes a worldwide measure of damages. Air France v. Saks requires attention to the treaty's actual terms and their shared legal meaning; Eastern Airlines v. Floyd likewise enforces the injury conditions the treaty contains rather than adding recovery from a general compensatory purpose.

The relatives argue that a treaty action requires a uniform federal common-law damages measure. That argument confuses uniform conditions of carrier liability with a comprehensive rule governing every resulting loss. A different domestic measure in a different location does not authorize the Court to write a missing treaty provision. Nor does removal of a Convention liability cap create a category of loss that applicable domestic law excludes. The Court decides neither the treaty's exclusivity in every setting nor which country's law would govern a disputed choice-of-law question.

**Precedent treatment:** Saks and Floyd are applied for treaty interpretation bounded by the Convention's actual conditions; neither supplies a general damages code.

### DOHSA excludes loss-of-society damages for both relatives

**Operative rule:** When United States law governs damages for a death on the high seas beyond a marine league from a United States shore, within the Death on the High Seas Act's geographical field, the Act's restriction to pecuniary loss excludes loss-of-society recovery. Dependency cannot revive that excluded category, and general maritime law cannot supplement the Act to permit it.

**Controlling explanation:** The Death on the High Seas Act, or DOHSA, governs the accepted high-seas location and limits recovery to pecuniary loss. Loss of society compensates the absence of companionship, a nonpecuniary loss. Mobil Oil Corp. v. Higginbotham holds that courts may not supplement DOHSA where Congress has spoken to the damages question. Offshore Logistics v. Tallentire likewise preserves the Act's exclusive high-seas regime against a contrary state-law recovery.

Miles v. Apex Marine Corp. requires respect for Congress's occupied maritime remedial fields. Yamaha Motor Corp. v. Calhoun preserves supplementary state remedies for an uncovered nonseafarer in territorial waters precisely because no governing federal statute occupied that field. It therefore does not authorize supplementation here. Sea-Land Services v. Gaudet's territorial-water society remedy cannot supersede DOHSA. American Dredging's treatment of forum administration supplies no different substantive damages measure.

The mother's exclusion is correct. Further findings about the sister's dependency cannot affect the result because the category itself is unavailable. The Court leaves other properly preserved damage components undisturbed and decides no general dependency rule, survival claim, predeath suffering claim, mental-distress claim, beneficiary-standing issue, or jury-trial issue.

**Precedent treatment:** Higginbotham and Offshore Logistics govern the statutory exclusion; Miles and Yamaha preserve its field. Gaudet is distinguished by its territorial setting, and American Dredging by its procedural subject.

## Law After Decision

The Convention does not itself make loss of society compensable. Under the accepted United States-law premise, DOHSA excludes that category for this high-seas death regardless of dependency. The Court announces no rule eliminating territorial-water remedies or resolving other damages categories.
