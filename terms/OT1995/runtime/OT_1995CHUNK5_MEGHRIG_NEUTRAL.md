#### C. DECISION-MATERIAL LITIGATION POSITIONS

##### Issue 1: Endangerment at commencement

* **Controlling Reasoning Below / Institutional Posture:** The Ninth Circuit permitted the suit despite completion of remediation before filing.
* **Petitioner or Applicant’s Position:** The enacted trigger concerns a threat existing at suit, not one removed years earlier.
* **Respondent or Competing Position:** The statute refers to past contributors and should permit recovery from those responsible even when responsible cleanup has eliminated the immediate threat.

##### Issue 2: Completed cleanup costs

* **Controlling Reasoning Below / Institutional Posture:** The Ninth Circuit read the power to order other necessary action as reaching reimbursement of past cleanup expense.
* **Petitioner or Applicant’s Position:** Congress expressly authorized cost recovery elsewhere but used prospective abatement language here.
* **Respondent or Competing Position:** Restitution can be equitable, and excluding past costs can discourage an owner from promptly removing contamination.

* **Alternative or Unreached Grounds and Limits:** Citizen-suit coverage, present endangerment, permissible relief, notice, and governmental-prosecution limits are distinct. This case does not concern costs incurred after a properly instituted ongoing-endangerment action.

**Key Tronic**

**Current rule:** Sections 107(a)(4)(B) and 101(25) do not shift private litigation attorney’s fees; a reference to enforcement activities is not the required authorization for that private award. Work performed by a lawyer can nevertheless qualify when the actual task independently constitutes a necessary response activity, such as identifying other responsible parties in a way that advances cleanup, and the private claimant proves necessity and consistency with the National Contingency Plan. **Department of Energy v. Ohio**

**Current rule:** The four statutory routes retain distinct limits:

- The Clean Water Act §505, 33 U.S.C. §1365, and RCRA §7002, 42 U.S.C. §6972, citizen-suit authorizations permit proceedings against the United States but do not unequivocally authorize punitive civil penalties incorporated from their separate enforcement provisions. Incorporation includes the penalty provisions' limits, whose generally defined liable persons exclude the United States.
- Clean Water Act §313, 33 U.S.C. §1323, does not unequivocally waive punitive fines for completed violations through its submission to requirements, process and sanctions. In that context, sanctions enforcing process support coercive compliance relief without clearly adding retrospective punishment.
- Section 313's qualified reference to civil penalties does not independently provide the missing punitive waiver. State-law penalties do not arise under federal law merely because EPA approved the state program, and the incorporated federal penalty definition still excludes the United States from liable persons.
- RCRA §6001, 42 U.S.C. §6961, submits facilities to substantive and procedural requirements, including permits, reporting, injunctive relief and sanctions enforcing that relief, without unequivocally authorizing punitive fines for past violations.

Enacted compliance duties and conceded prospective or coercive compliance remedies remain effective and distinct; no general immunity from every environmental remedy follows.

**Lujan**

**Current rule:** A plaintiff invoking federal jurisdiction must establish a concrete and particularized, actual or imminent injury; a causal connection fairly traceable to the defendant's challenged conduct rather than to independent third parties' own decisions; and a likelihood, not mere speculation, that the requested relief will redress the injury. Authorization for any person to sue does not supply standing for only the public's undifferentiated interest in lawful administration. A procedural right must protect the plaintiff's own threatened concrete interest; when that connection exists, the plaintiff need not prove that the required procedure will certainly alter the ultimate agency decision. Aesthetic, recreational or professional wildlife interests may qualify, but past visits and indefinite return intentions without concrete plans or another supported basis for imminent renewed exposure do not establish threatened interference with personal use. At summary judgment the plaintiff must support challenged standing elements with specific facts by affidavit or other evidence, with favorable reasonable inferences available; allegations cannot replace missing evidence.

**Authority by component:**
- Stage-specific evidentiary requirement and existing injury, traceability and redressability elements: [Lujan v. Defenders of Wildlife, “1. Article III elements must be supported at the litigation stage”](https://github.com/Alex-Oss222/scotus-career/blob/87e62b7612c15409039d524fd66f743d0ad75e2a/terms/OT1991/records/Lujan_v_Defenders_of_Wildlife_merits_1992-06-12.md), June 12, 1992; Scalia Part II, joined by White, Kennedy, Souter and Thomas (five).
