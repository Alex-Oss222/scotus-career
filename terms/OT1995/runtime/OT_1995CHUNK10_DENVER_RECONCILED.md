## Shared operative scope

All eight reach the June 6, 1995 en banc judgment through §1254(1) review of the challenged 1992 statute/FCC rules. The supplied case presents facial programmer/viewer claims, not an adjudicated removal of a named program, damages, or a general constitutional challenge to all compelled access.

- **Section 10(a)** permits prospective enforcement of a written and published operator policy covering programming reasonably believed to depict or describe sexual or excretory activities or organs in a patently offensive way under contemporary community standards. The FCC specifies the cable medium. Permission is not an obligation to ban. Context matters; neither Miller's complete obscenity test nor a categorical serious-value exemption is supplied.
- **Section 10(b)** requires, where the operator has not voluntarily prohibited the covered leased-access material, identification/notification, concentration on a channel and initial blocking unless the subscriber requests access in writing. The agency requires an eighteen-or-older statement and compliance within thirty days, not an obligatory thirty-day wait. Another blocked channel may be used if capacity is insufficient. The government's attributed accommodation for systems without premium/pay-per-view channels is an operator-installed lockbox with its key/code retained until unblocking is requested; it is not a parent-controlled device or a verified numerical small-system exception.
- **Section 10(c)** directs enabling regulations allowing operator prohibitions on PEG capacity for obscenity, sexually explicit conduct, and material soliciting or promoting unlawful conduct. The FCC construes the latter categories as indecency and otherwise proscribed material respectively. It supplies no (a) express policy/reasonable-belief qualifications and no (b) compulsory subscriber-blocking mechanism. An invalidity position below reaches its authorization concerning protected nonobscene indecency; lawful obscenity and otherwise-unprotected/unlawful-material controls are preserved. No Justice treats the provision as ordering operators to prohibit programs.
- **Section 10(d)** excepts obscenity from access-programming immunity; it does not generally impose liability for protected indecency. No independent invalidity commitment concerns (d).

Congress's enactment is reviewable separately from whether a particular operator's refusal is attributable to government. Private ownership alone does not answer legislative content discrimination; a franchise or PEG label alone does not establish a public forum or governmental speaker. Hurley protects an actual private expressive presentation. It neither establishes that every access program is the operator's own presentation nor makes statutory access constitutionally immutable.

ACT's March 4 holding governs protected indecency and independent review of necessity and means in light of actual medium characteristics. Child protection and parental assistance are compelling, but that fact is not sufficient by itself. ACT neither imposes Turner's intermediate cable test nor decides these provisions. Turner's eight-Justice general content rule, five-Justice full-power classification, limited common predictive-review principle, and four-Justice record-insufficiency application remain distinct. Its evidentiary remand is not a universal requirement. Pacifica's narrow contextual holding survives; neither its result nor cable's accessibility to children supplies an automatic answer.

## Reconciled individual commitments

### Stevens

**(a): Affirm.** Sustain the confined, prospective, reasonably administered private permission after detaching (b). A government-created access arrangement can retain reasonable subject limits without constitutionally entrenching every previous statutory allowance. His own historical separate discussion at 768–773 confirms the bounded forum-definition position; it does not authorize viewpoint discrimination or an absolute exemption for legislative changes. His narrower actual Turner join preserves attention to the distribution arrangement. His ACT dissent supports contextual evaluation but cannot displace ACT's controlling adult-speech rule.

**(b): Reverse and remove the compulsory structure.** The collective effect of forced regrouping, default denial and written adult election burdens willing speakers and recipients beyond adequately tailored parental assistance. The adult's ability to obtain later access is relevant but not dispositive. Available requested household controls and the distinction between mixed access programs and an entire blocked channel answer the claim that the mandatory design is indispensable; no numerical usage finding or perfect alternative is required. This is consistent with his Sable Part IV join and differs from his ACT vote because that vote rested on broadcast scheduling and a meaningful existing harbor.

**(c): Reverse as to protected indecency.** A federal veto over locally arranged access expression is materially different from (a)'s confined restoration of operator choice. Title alone does not establish an operator message; (c) supplies neither (a)'s safeguards nor a mechanism preserving adult access. No final universal PEG-forum classification is necessary. His historical separate writing at 773–774 and principal-opinion join support this scope. Contrary private-editor arguments remain available in a proper concrete claim; they do not sustain this general federal authorization. He may join bounded contextual reasoning on all three components, not a lower-value speech hierarchy or categorical operator immunity.

### O'Connor

**(a): Affirm on the corrected contextual ground.** Her actual Turner separate opinion protects the operator's editorial selection; Hurley gives that interest concrete constitutional weight. Congress's permission leaves the private operator free to carry the program and does not itself exclude every adult from a willing speaker's message. The prospective policy and reasonable-belief terms confine the accommodation. The contrary integrated-coercion objection is answered by removal of (b), not a finding that economic pressure is universally absent. She accepts review of Congress's legal alteration and the ACT requirement of careful means review.

**(b): Reverse and remove the combined compulsory scheme.** ACT Parts I–III and her Turner's alternative breadth analysis support final relief where the enacted combination itself unduly burdens adult access. An age screen alone or an ordinary subscription request is not held unconstitutional. The thirty-day term remains a maximum compliance interval. The operator-controlled agency accommodation does not remove default denial or written opt-in. A parent may use available household controls after obtaining access; that answers an absolute claim that access necessarily leaves the entire channel uncontrollable, but does not cure the government's burdensome initial structure.

**(c): Affirm against this facial claim.** On scrutiny of the permissive law itself, she finds the shared protection of children and accommodation of independent operator editorial choice more significant than the differing origins of leased and PEG capacity. She does not import (a)'s absent policy terms into (c); she finds those differences insufficient to make this uncoerced permission facially invalid. Actual ACT's rejection of a general mandatory schedule does not make every permission equivalent to such exclusion. Her historical separate opinion at 779–780 directly confirms this weighing. An established governmental facility, particular enforceable local obligation, or coercive administration remains distinguishable. She can join a bounded contextual (a) rationale and a strict-tailoring (b) rationale; she cannot join final protected-indecency invalidity under (c), categorical no-state-action language, or a rule making every PEG channel a public forum.

### Scalia

**(a) and (c): Affirm on the distinct private-editor/access-entitlement ground.** An access programmer has protected expression and a right to seek a willing outlet, but no freestanding constitutional entitlement to compel another private editor to transmit it. Partial relaxation of the imposed access scheme is not itself a direct government prohibition on that underlying expression. His own Sable concurrence at 133 distinguishes governmental prohibition from a constitutional utility duty to carry; his actual Turner dissent joins and Hurley join support the same private editorial interest. Regulatory designation alone does not create a public forum. He preserves challenges involving genuinely governmental institutions or independently established obligations, without finding their existence in a particular franchise here. This is a merits ground, not lack of Article III standing. He will not join an ad hoc contextual balancing framework as the sufficient reason to sustain these permissions.

**(b): Affirm under strict scrutiny.** Unlike the permissions, it regulates transmission by a willing operator and thus burdens the programmers' speech. Mixed and unpredictable leased-access schedules make subscriber-initiated program blocking a materially less effective default protection; segregation preserves the remaining basic package while a real adult election remains available. Written rather than oral requests can reduce unauthorized child access. Compliance within thirty days is not mandatory delay, and the adult may still use household controls after opting in. His ACT dissent supplies continuity on practical parental burdens, not a lesser standard. The opposing combination-of-burdens argument remains substantial, but he finds this default/opt-in structure sufficiently fitted rather than a total adult ban. Historical Thomas Part III at 831–838, which Scalia joined, expressly uses strict scrutiny; it does not decide (b) solely by denying programmer rights. No foolproof-alternative requirement, presumed official registry, or universal affordability finding is adopted.

### Kennedy

**(a) and (c): Reverse as to protected indecency under strict scrutiny.** Congress selects protected content for loss of an otherwise supplied access protection. That legislative burden can be reviewed without deeming every later private refusal governmental. His actual Turner general content analysis, ACT join and Rosenberger program-specific viewpoint reasoning supply continuity; his historical writing at 782–783 and 799–803 also expressly identifies strict scrutiny as the baseline, with forum analysis testing an exception rather than creating the only possible speech claim. It is therefore unnecessary to declare every PEG channel a public forum. Where a local government actually dedicates contracted capacity to all-comer private expression, his forum rationale remains available; this handoff makes no new franchise finding and does not reject that conditional rationale.

Child protection is compelling. But discretionary exclusion can suppress protected programming from adults altogether while leaving comparable exposure to the operator's unrestricted choice. A general permission to exclude this content does not establish that the statutory access expression is the operator's own message. Household choice and narrower protection remain relevant; neither mere alternative availability elsewhere nor permission rather than mandatory prohibition establishes narrow tailoring. His Hurley join protects an actual private expressive presentation, not an unproved equation of all access transmission with one.

**(b): Reverse and remove the compulsory combination.** It directly burdens willing communication and adult access; the identification, segregation, default-blocking and written-request machinery is evaluated together. No independent invalidation of all age checks or notices follows. He can join strict-tailoring invalidity, but not a rationale treating protected indecency as categorically lower value or replacing legal standards with an undefined balance. On (c) he agrees in final relief but need not join a theory assigning local managers an unqualified power to censor lawful expression. No independent §506 judgment or guaranteed carriage follows.

### Souter

**(a): Affirm through bounded contextual scrutiny.** Preserve the modeled confined accommodation of private choice, with the written/published prospective policy and reasonable-belief limits doing substantive work. He recognizes Congress's own action and the programmers' interests, while giving weight to Hurley's actual private-expression protection. The historical concurrence at 774–778 supports context and caution about premature medium categories; it does not license an unbounded standard. His ACT Part I join requires protected-speech and independent means review now. He joins no automatic no-state-action rule or blanket claim that statutory access is the operator's own expression.

**(b): Reverse and remove the combined compulsory design.** His ACT remand was tied to evaluation of a particular timetable, not a demand to defer every final decision while additional data could be collected. The burden here follows from the combination of statutory mechanisms even accepting the lower court's mixed-programming account and the described accommodation. Existing household control remains relevant without a universal effectiveness finding. His historical full Part III join confirms the modeled distinction. Parent controls remain usable after opt-in, but that does not remove the initial forced structure.

**(c): Reverse as to protected indecency; corrected from vacatur alone.** The challenged federal permission can be held insufficiently fitted to child protection without first deciding every local institution's forum classification. Its additional content-based veto reaches access expression not established as an operator's private presentation, lacks (a)'s limiting architecture, and permits total denial to adults. Actual Chabad requires attention to selection, financing, control and attribution; it does not require a new forum inquiry before any facial statutory judgment. Actual Hurley requires a concrete expressive burden rather than title alone. The historical opinion he joined already preserved categorical questions while granting final (c) relief, so the model's caution alone supplies no changed premise for a different remedy. He can join this bounded contextual ground and its final relief. He will not join an assertion that all PEG is governmental, that no private-editor claim could succeed, or that every local access administrator may suppress protected expression.

### Thomas

**(a) and (c): Affirm on the private-editor ground described for Scalia.** His actual Turner joins in O'Connor's Parts I and III, rather than her intermediate alternative Part II, distinguish a content-based imposition on editorial speech from permission to exercise editorial discretion. His Hurley and Rosenberger joins protect actual private expression without constitutionalizing all statutory access. Ordinary regulatory creation of a channel supplies no governmental property interest or forum by itself. He reserves an actually different governmental institution rather than making a new historical absence-of-easement finding. The underlying access regime's full constitutionality and any taking are not decided. His historical Parts I–II at 812–831 confirm the sufficiently specific ground; he does not join contextual balancing merely because it yields affirmance.

**(b): Affirm under strict scrutiny.** The willing-operator distinction makes (b) genuine content regulation. He finds the default, segregation and real adult election carefully fitted to random mixed access programming; routine subscriber-requested blocking and schedule monitoring do not serve that parental-assistance function equally. Written requests and the actual maximum compliance period are permissible safeguards, not a permanent adult prohibition. Household controls remain usable after opt-in. His actual ACT vote for removing the sixteen-hour broadcast restraint does not require invalidity here: the relevant changed design provides an adult election to obtain the speech instead of forbidding willing broadcasters to transmit it throughout the specified hours. No lesser-value-speech rule or indiscriminate deference follows. His historical Part III confirms this merits analysis. He does not rely on a new factual finding about confidentiality, registry creation, or §505 implementation. A statutory comparison is not proof of technical equivalence.

### Ginsburg

**(a) and (c): Reverse on the strict legislative-content ground, with Kennedy's limitations.** Her actual ACT opinion requires the justification of necessity and means; her Turner separate opinion rejects the premise that benign regulatory objectives neutralize content selection. Her Chabad join and Rosenberger dissent distinguish the actual institution and attribution rather than adopting a universal forum category. She agrees that Congress's content-defined withdrawal can burden protected access expression whether or not an individual operator is a state actor. Her historical join in Kennedy's opinion strongly confirms the result and strict ground. The separate public-access forum rationale remains conditional on the actual arrangement, not a finding about every PEG channel. Hypothetical private compelled-expression claims do not themselves establish a sufficient basis for this broad statutory permission.

**(b): Reverse and remove the compulsory structure.** Her ACT rule directly requires examining the burden on willing adults and speakers; the available requested controls and statutory design sustain final relief rather than indefinite evidentiary remand. She takes seriously unpredictable programming and parental difficulties but rejects making all adults undergo the combination of segregation, initial denial and written election for the entire channel. She does not adopt a generic definition-based invalidity, automatic merit exception, assumed actual disclosure, or universal technical solution. She can join strict invalidity on all three components, not a rationale in which the importance of child protection alone supplies tailoring or historical Pacifica language erases the actual ACT standard.

### Breyer

**(a): Affirm through explicit contextual means review.** The defined permission, private editorial interest, prospective policy and reasonable-belief conditions give Congress a limited way to address child access while retaining a willing operator's option to carry the programming. Invalidating (b) removes the compulsory pressure mechanism considered here. His actual ACT dissent shows attention to broadcast realities but cannot displace that decision's governing protection; neither evolving technology nor institutional complexity is a reason to leave the operative review standard undefined. He was not a participant in Turner; its current holdings bind, but its historical votes are not his own writings.

**(b): Reverse and remove the combined compulsory scheme.** The mandatory channel-wide arrangement and written election impose avoidable adult burdens beyond justified parental assistance. The 1984 household-control route is an affirmative existing alternative; the exact 1996 text is a distinct legal comparison subject to the admission receipt, not proof of implementation. Future V-chip technology supplies no present factual solution. His ACT vote is consistent because its timed broadcast rule and actual contextual defenses differ from mandatory sorting and default denial on cable. He accepts practical child access without requiring flawless protections.

**(c): Reverse as to protected indecency.** Local access arrangements and the absence of an established operator expressive presentation distinguish the added federal veto from (a)'s limited permission. The statutory design and narrower parental/local arrangements support final relief without a universal public-forum holding or an assertion that the entire record lacks evidence. The historical principal opinion confirms this distinction but supplies no new neutral factual finding. He may join the bounded contextual grounds on all three components, not a blanket private-choice exemption, an unsupported medium-wide category, or a rule making compelling purpose sufficient.

## Comparison conclusions, barriers and remedy

After the two express corrections, no Associate component outcome materially departs from the verified historical result. This conclusion follows examination of grounds and changed current law, not copying the historical disposition. The model's Souter remedy departure lacked a concrete Justice-specific changed premise and has been corrected. The current ACT ruling is a real intervening simulated premise. For its author Ginsburg and joiners O'Connor, Kennedy, Souter and Thomas, their own protected-speech/means-review commitments are direct evidence; for dissenters Stevens, Scalia and Breyer, the holding binds while their separate practical judgments remain relevant. The reconciliation does not silently reproduce a hypothetical broadcast rule sustaining any indecency restriction. Accordingly, no unsupported material historical-departure line remains to be exported.

Shared judgments do not erase sufficient-ground differences. O'Connor's contextual affirmance remains distinct from Scalia/Thomas's rights-ordering ground. No Marks holding or new join is inferred from the component results.

Those sustaining (a) regard it as independently operable after (b)'s removal under the frozen severability analysis; permission remains usable and child-protection purposes remain served without compulsory sorting. Each Justice invalidating (c) supports relief against the protected-indecency authorization and implementing rule, preserving ordinary obscenity and otherwise-unlawful-material authority. Remand implements the component resolution; it does not order a new FCC hearing, universal franchise investigation, replacement broadcast hour, mandatory damages, carriage of a named program, or automatic interim relief.

Independent 1996 §506 authority remains distinct. Its public-access subsection is not silently extended to every E/G category; leased access has its separate subsection. Invalidating 1992 authority does not adjudicate the 1996 provisions, establish automatic repeal or mootness, or guarantee carriage. The 1996 Act's §561 facial-challenge route and twenty-day appeal condition remain separate from 47 U.S.C. §561. Section 504's no-charge subscriber-requested full audio/video blocking text has no imported sexual-content trigger; §505 has its separate sexually oriented programming condition and transition.
