- **Question Presented:** Whether a federal court has ancillary jurisdiction over a new action seeking to impose an earlier ERISA judgment against a corporation on a person not liable under that judgment — here, a corporate officer and shareholder who was a defendant in the earlier action but was held not to be a plan fiduciary — alleged to have diverted corporate assets to defeat collection.
- **Question Granted:** The stated question describes the submitted review scope; no additional question or plenary grant is inferred beyond the identified procedural stage.
- **Legal Area:** Federal courts — ancillary jurisdiction and judgment enforcement
- **Court and Judgment Under Review:** The Fourth Circuit (39 F.3d 493, decided November 4, 1994) sustained ancillary jurisdiction, affirmed the District Court's judgment allowing the plaintiff class to pierce Tru-Tech's corporate veil and recover the earlier judgment from Peacock personally under a federal common-law veil-piercing rule, and vacated and remanded the attorneys'-fee award for further proceedings.
- **Requested Supreme Court Disposition / Relief:** The party seeking review asks for relief from the adverse judgment identified below; the opposing party seeks its preservation. Any decision must stay within the presented issues and source-authorized remedy.
- **Material Facts and Record:** In 1987 Thomas, for a class of former Tru-Tech employees, sued Tru-Tech and Peacock, an officer and shareholder of Tru-Tech, for pension benefits and breach of fiduciary duty under ERISA. The District Court held Tru-Tech, but not Peacock, a plan fiduciary and entered judgment for the class against Tru-Tech only (November 28, 1988); the Fourth Circuit affirmed in all respects (1990). After failing to collect from Tru-Tech, Thomas filed a second federal action against Peacock individually and Peacock's attorney, pleading civil conspiracy, fraudulent transfer of assets and corporate veil-piercing under ERISA. The District Court found that Peacock, as Tru-Tech's controlling shareholder and later sole director, carried out a strategy of diverting Tru-Tech assets to favored creditors he owned or controlled in order to defeat collection of the Thomas judgment. It rejected the claim against the attorney and Thomas's other theories, but held Peacock personally liable for the earlier judgment on the veil-piercing theory, and awarded attorneys' fees for both suits.
- **Preservation / Threshold / Vehicle Issues:** There is no diversity of citizenship. Below, it was undisputed that no basis for federal-question jurisdiction existed other than the fact that the suit sought to enforce a judgment obtained under ERISA (39 F.3d at 499 n.6); the Fourth Circuit rested jurisdiction on ancillary enforcement jurisdiction. Whether any independent federal basis, including ERISA's civil-enforcement provisions, supports the action is part of the jurisdictional question. Peacock also argued that the District Court should have applied a state rather than a federal veil-piercing standard.
- **Relevant Dates:** Fourth Circuit decision November 4, 1994; argument November 6, 1995; decision February 21, 1996.

**Current rule:** Enforcement of the settlement is a separate contract controversy requiring an independent jurisdictional basis unless the dismissal order lawfully incorporates the settlement obligation or expressly retains jurisdiction to enforce it. **Central Bank**

**Current rule:** A person who knowingly or with severe recklessness substantially assists an underlying securities fraud through affirmative conduct can be privately liable. The defendant must know or severely recklessly disregard both the activity’s fraudulent character and its conduct’s assisting role. Severe recklessness is an extreme departure from ordinary care presenting a specific danger of deception known to the defendant or so obvious that the defendant must have appreciated it. An independent disclosure duty is unnecessary for affirmative assistance. The plaintiff must prove the underlying manipulation or deception, qualifying assistance and culpability, legally sufficient reliance on the underlying fraud, causation and recoverable loss by the ordinary civil preponderance standard. Investors need not have known of or relied on concealed assistance itself; no reliance presumption follows merely from this rule.

**Denton**

**Current rule:** A court may dismiss a claim proceeding without prepayment when its factual allegations are clearly baseless—irrational or wholly incredible—even without judicially noticeable contrary facts. **Current rule:** Congress may not require an unwilling State to choose between regulating private waste generators under federal instructions and accepting ownership and possession of their waste with associated liability.
