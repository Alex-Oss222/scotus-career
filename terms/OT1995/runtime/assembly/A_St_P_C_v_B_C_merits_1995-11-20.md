## Record, competing requests and scope

The November 1992 child-in-need-of-care adjudication found abuse by a bare preponderance. The December disposition permitted weekly supervised visits of up to an hour, subject to shortening in light of the child's reaction and contemplated supervision by a neutral relative when possible. The mother's January 1993 request sought the different, mandatory all-contact prohibition. The Louisiana rehearing opinion expressly states that the father lacked notice at the earlier hearing that its finding could produce those additional consequences; his failure to appeal that finding therefore did not make it conclusive for this request.

Section 9:364(D), as reproduced in the date-eligible Louisiana opinions, required prohibition of all visitation and contact after a finding of parental sexual abuse until a contradictory hearing established both successful completion of treatment designed for sexual abusers and that supervised visitation was in the children's best interests. Section 9:362(6) described treatment as evaluation and psychotherapy specifically designed for perpetrators of family violence and conducted by licensed mental-health professionals. The subsection specified no proof burden. Its further command to give greater weight to the child's therapist was separately invalidated on state separation-of-powers grounds; that state ruling is outside the federal question here. The actual OCS plan sought an admission, and an expert considered continued denial an obstacle to benefit then; neither fact makes admission a statutory requirement of every program.

The decree under review sustained invalidation on the federal proof ground. It did not make a new abuse finding, prescribe a new visitation schedule, adjudicate all fees or establish an unrestricted right to contact.

## Controlling holdings

### Clear and convincing proof must precede this statutory all-contact restriction

**Operative rule:** Before La. Rev. Stat. 9:364(D)'s severe restriction may be imposed, it must be proved by clear and convincing evidence that the parent sexually abused his or her child or children. A bare-preponderance finding cannot trigger mandatory loss of all visitation and contact until a contradictory (adversarial) hearing establishes both successful completion of a program designed for sexual abusers and that supervised visitation is in the children's best interests. The statutory treatment definition requires evaluation and psychotherapy specifically designed for perpetrators of family violence and conducted by licensed mental-health professionals.

**Authority:** Chief Justice Stone-Zsela's Opinion of the Court, joined in full by Stevens, Souter, Ginsburg and Breyer; five Justices directly adopt the rule, application and limits.

**Controlling explanation:** Mathews requires examination of the actual loss, the risk of error and the State's protective interest. The consequence here is complete exclusion from a child's life with no fixed endpoint, followed, if two conditions are met, only by supervised contact. Santosky explains why ordinary preponderance may allocate too much risk to a parent facing a grave destruction of family relations. Although the legal relationship survives here, the scope and potentially indefinite duration of this particular exclusion warrant intermediate proof before it begins. The Court extends that method to this statutory consequence, not to every custody or visitation decision.

Protection of the child remains essential. Existing authority for investigation, emergency intervention, temporary separation and supported supervised restrictions permits protective action under the requirements governing each measure while the severe statutory predicate is established. Clear and convincing proof is not a prerequisite to every protective act. The contrary view correctly identifies danger from erroneous permission, but it does not explain why imposing this complete, potentially indefinite regime requires only ordinary preponderance when those independent protections remain available. Foucha and Heller reinforce attention to the grounds and reliability of serious deprivation within their distinct commitment-law settings; they do not already prescribe this parental-contact rule.

The earlier bare-preponderance finding, entered without notice of these additional consequences, cannot supply the required predicate. That conclusion is not a finding that abuse did not occur. The Louisiana judgment is affirmed on this proof-burden ground; the Court neither rewrites the statute nor orders contact. Lawful state implementation, including any available conforming construction, remains for the state courts. No general periodic-review schedule, restoration procedure, treatment-admission condition, fee ruling or proof standard for lesser restrictions is created.

**Precedent treatment:** Mathews is applied to the specific deprivation; Santosky's error-allocation method is extended to this statutory cutoff while its termination holding remains intact. Foucha and Heller are applied only by the stated analogy; their distinct operative rules are unchanged.
