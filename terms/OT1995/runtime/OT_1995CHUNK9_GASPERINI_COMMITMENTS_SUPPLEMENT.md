## The additional state-law premise

Inya v. Ide Hyundai, Inc., 209 A.D.2d 1015, 619 N.Y.S.2d 440 (1994), expressly states that the New York Supreme Court—the state's trial court—may set aside a jury damage award when it materially deviates from reasonable compensation. It holds use of the shocks-the-conscience test erroneous on that inadequacy motion. The court then directs a conditional new trial unless defendants agree to increased awards.

Cochetti v. Gralow, 192 A.D.2d 974, 975–976, 597 N.Y.S.2d 234 (1993), describes trial-court intervention as discretionary and to be used sparingly, while calling trial application of the material-deviation standard settled law. It sustains the trial court's intervention in a verdict denying future damages and expands the new trial to all damages because of the potentially inconsistent or compromised verdict.

Shurgan v. Tedesco, 179 A.D.2d 805, 806 (1992), also preserves sparing exercise of trial-court discretion and approves setting aside an inadequate award under §5501(c)'s material-deviation measure.

These are authoritative state judicial applications, not an inference that federal courts may rewrite a statute's named actor. The enacted direction names the Appellate Division, but state law as construed also governs trial judges' review of damage awards. The prior comparison must therefore no longer describe the state's measure as exclusively appellate, or suggest that applying it at trial necessarily creates a function unknown to New York law. This clarification is material even though it does not determine the federal questions.

The sources preserve permission and discretion: trial judges may set aside qualifying awards, and intervention remains sparing. They do not establish that every identified deviation compels one particular remedy. The legal measure is distinct from whether it is met and which lawful remedy follows. Their personal-injury findings and amounts supply no valuation evidence for Gasperini's transparencies. Inya's conditional increase is a state procedure; it does not authorize federal additur or displace Dimick. The literal text's itemized-verdict setting and the actual Gasperini lower record remain part of the original packet.

## Fresh comparison of the affected alternatives

**Accommodation path.** The state standard can operate in federal trial court through Rule 59, while federal law governs allocation and appellate review. The new cases strengthen this path: selecting the district judge to apply the material-deviation measure parallels an established state-law trial function, rather than creating a new trial role from a statute confined to appellate judges. Inya also confirms that material deviation and shocks-the-conscience are distinct operative measures at trial. The original Erie concern about materially different compensation depending on forum remains substantial.

**Strongest counterargument after the return.** State trial application does not prove that the measure is substantive for Erie purposes. It may instead demonstrate a state procedural standard governing the judicial reassessment that Rule 59 already addresses. Nor does state practice establish what reexamination the Seventh Amendment permits in a federal system. Hanna still requires examining the Federal Rule's actual coverage; state-law characterization cannot bypass that inquiry.

**Federal-standard path.** Scalia and Thomas's original path remains legally available only on its federal-rule and constitutional grounds. It cannot rely on an assertion that New York has reserved the material-deviation measure to appellate courts or that trial use contradicts settled New York law. They regard the measure as governing the degree of judicial reexamination, and read Rule 59's historical-federal-practice reference as controlling that function. The new state cases do not decide that federal construction.

**Strongest counterargument to this retained path.** Rule 59 may preserve the reason for a new trial—excessiveness—without prescribing an immutable federal degree of excessiveness. The state's settled use of a different compensation constraint in both trial and appellate courts supports treating the constraint as substantive while preserving federal method. That argument is materially stronger after the return. Scalia and Thomas nevertheless retain the original federal-standard conclusion because the source of their barrier is Rule 59's coverage and the federal jury structure, not the name of the state reviewing court. Their conclusion is a fresh application, not a claim that the additional cases are immaterial.

The six other Associates retain the accommodation path for the reasons stated in their original rows, now supplemented by the authoritative state trial applications. Those cases answer the particular objection that trial application would itself invent a state role. They do not by themselves answer the separate direct-collision objection or constitutional appellate-review question; the original reasoning on those questions remains necessary.
