**Central Bank**

**Current rule:** A person who knowingly or with severe recklessness substantially assists an underlying securities fraud through affirmative conduct can be privately liable. The defendant must know or severely recklessly disregard both the activity’s fraudulent character and its conduct’s assisting role. Severe recklessness is an extreme departure from ordinary care presenting a specific danger of deception known to the defendant or so obvious that the defendant must have appreciated it. An independent disclosure duty is unnecessary for affirmative assistance. The plaintiff must prove the underlying manipulation or deception, qualifying assistance and culpability, legally sufficient reliance on the underlying fraud, causation and recoverable loss by the ordinary civil preponderance standard. Investors need not have known of or relied on concealed assistance itself; no reliance presumption follows merely from this rule.

**Nordic**

**Current rule:** Original §106(c) does not unequivocally waive immunity from an affirmative personal money judgment recovering an unauthorized transfer under §550. A plausible reading under which its application-and-binding language governs nonmonetary or claim determinations defeats clear consent to that remedy; legislative history cannot supply missing enacted clarity. Sections 106(a) and (b) retain their own claim-linked and offset operations. General estate-property jurisdiction under §1334(d), an asserted trust interest, and the money's origin in estate assets do not supply the missing consent. An order requiring payment of a sum, rather than adjudicating identified property under the court's control, cannot be preserved by calling it a property proceeding or defensive equitable recoupment.

- Original §106(c) monetary-waiver construction: [United States v. Nordic Village — Original §106(c) does not clearly authorize this monetary recovery](https://github.com/Alex-Oss222/scotus-career/blob/87e62b7612c15409039d524fd66f743d0ad75e2a/terms/OT1991/records/United_States_v_Nordic_Village_merits_1992-02-25.md), February 25, 1992.

Scalia's opinion joined by Stone, White, O'Connor, Kennedy, Souter, and Thomas controls both.

**Open question:** Genuinely distinct specific-property proceedings require their actual facts and independent authority; their validity is not decided. State immunity and a different express statutory waiver remain outside the decision.
