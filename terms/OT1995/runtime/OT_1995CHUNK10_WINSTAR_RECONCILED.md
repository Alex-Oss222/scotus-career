## Exact historical comparison

The historical judgment affirmed, 7–2. Souter's opinion was joined fully by Stevens and Breyer, and by O'Connor **except Parts IV-A and IV-B**. Breyer separately concurred. Scalia concurred in the judgment, joined by Kennedy and Thomas. Rehnquist dissented, and Ginsburg joined **Parts I, III and IV only**: she did not join his sovereign-acts Part II or his final Part V statement of outright reversal or alternative remand. Historical authorship is not an assignment for this Court.

The historical positions have distinct content:

- **Souter, Stevens and Breyer:** Actual contracts and authorized financial-risk undertakings; unmistakability protects against effective surrender of sovereign authority and does not defeat these undertakings. A demand for money is not automatically outside the doctrine: repayment effectively conferring a tax or comparable exemption can still implicate it. Their sovereign-acts analysis has two independent grounds. Parts IV-A/B reject the defense where release from the Government's own contracts is a substantial effect of the challenged action, rather than merely incidental. Part IV-C separately rejects ordinary impossibility even assuming a public and general act because the relevant risk was allocated to the Government. Footnote 46 rejects a predominant-purpose/effect threshold; footnote 50 expressly disclaims a legislative-intent test. Their ground cannot accurately be replaced by a test of subjective bad faith.
- **O'Connor:** The same contract, authority and limited financial-undertaking grounds, and the independent impossibility/assumed-risk ground, without the IV-A/B substantial-effect discussion. Her omitted joins are substantive limits, not an inference that she considered the entire Act non-general.
- **Scalia, Kennedy and Thomas:** Unmistakability **applies and is satisfied**. Sovereign contracting reverses the ordinary presumption that a promisor undertakes not to disable its performance through its own acts, but the specific future regulatory treatment was the bargain's essential promised subject. No second promise to keep that promise is required. Actual delegation supports these agreements; retained sovereign powers do not cancel the assumed commercial liability. Clear assumption of the change-of-law risk likewise defeats the sovereign-acts defense. This is not mere reservation of the doctrine for a later case.
- **Ginsburg's actual joins:** The lower courts' findings did not resolve the promise under the required sovereign-contract construction rule; describing the undertaking as risk shifting could not replace that inquiry. Immediate regulatory permission supplies consideration even if no longer-term legislative-risk promise was made. Damages and the bare possibility of a genuine warranty do not establish the further alleged undertaking. Her joins do not establish agreement with Rehnquist's broad sovereign-acts defense, a missing-delegation ruling, or his precise alternative remedy.

Historical references to additional legislative statements, an oral-argument concession, industry-wide relative incidence, or authorities not already admitted as current law are not added to the neutral record through this comparison. The broad public/general alternative was already an express neutral issue; the operative measures and their disallowance of the particular bargained capital benefits were already supplied. The limited additional support below uses those existing premises, not newly imported legislative facts.
