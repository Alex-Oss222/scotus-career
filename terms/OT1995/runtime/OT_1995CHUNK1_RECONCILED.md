## Doe v. Taylor Independent School District, Fifth Circuit No. 90-8431

### Final components and law

Four Associates — Stevens, Souter, Ginsburg and Breyer — affirm denial of Lankford's immunity on the supported assumption of his own knowing, causal acquiescence. **Factual sufficiency is not an immediate qualified-immunity appeal** (Johnson, June 12, 1995) confines review to separable legal immunity on the supported nonmovant assumptions. **Appellate review must consider all relevant law on the preserved immunity issue** (Elder, February 23, 1994) requires the full relevant conduct-date landscape, not retrospective notice. Hafer preserves personal-capacity liability and personal immunity. Collins separates a federal wrong from attribution, rejects an extra freestanding governmental-power-abuse element, and leaves deliberate bodily injury and different affirmative-deprivation claims outside its voluntary-workplace holding. Farmer supplies a current actual-awareness proof analogy, not a retroactive school duty. Albright has no controlling rationale that eliminates the independent bodily-integrity claim or establishes a universal state-remedy defense.

**D1's rule and limits:** Actual abuse of the teacher's school authority can infringe bodily integrity; off-campus location alone neither proves nor disproves that nexus. The asserted supervisor's own liability requires actual awareness of abuse or substantial abuse risk, deliberately unreasonable nonresponse despite authority and opportunity to act, and causation of injury or its continuation. Circumstantial proof may support awareness, but warning signs are not conclusive and what an official merely should have known is insufficient. Knowing causal acquiescence on Lankford's assumed pattern of dismissed or suppressed reports is personal conduct, not respondeat superior, a general guarantee of school safety, or liability from state law alone.

**D2's opposing ground:** The teacher's obvious wrong does not by itself clearly establish every supervisor's constitutional damages obligation. Rizzo's affirmative-link requirement and the contemporaneous Ford/Reimer/Vela/Chinchello line make the step from omission to personal responsibility materially uncertain. Wanger/Bowen and related cases supply the strongest contrary notice argument, but their actual facts and municipal/custodial distinctions matter. D2 grants immunity without announcing a comprehensive present school-duty rule or denying pupils bodily-integrity protection. A fact-identical precedent is not required; the dispute concerns the content of the then-established responsibility rule.

The January/February Caplinger notice discrepancy remains unresolved. His knowledge is not combined with the principal's undisclosed history. His inquiries, reprimand and warning are evaluated on the supported assumed record; the failure to hold the wider meeting and continued injury remain contrary facts. All eight's legal conclusion is that the particular additional federal duty asserted was not clearly established, not that his responses were necessarily prudent or that ineffective warnings always suffice.

D1 treats their actual later holdings as reinforcing an already established restricted knowing-acquiescence principle; its earlier denial does not depend on them. D2 considers them but finds that the direct teacher restraint in Jefferson and the bus-assault supervisory analysis/rejection in Lopez did not clearly resolve the particular noncustodial supervisory and postwarning-monitoring obligation alleged here. ### Reconciled components and grounds

**L1 — Rule11(f): all eight Associates reject the claimed rule-specific duty.** Rule11(f) requires a factual basis for the guilty plea to the substantive offense. **L2 — constitutional source: O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Thomas and Breyer reject the asserted Sixth Amendment entitlement for this forfeiture determination; Stevens, Souter and Ginsburg do not decide it.** The five distinguish a sentencing determination of property extent from the elements whose jury adjudication Gaudin protects. **L3 — waiver: all eight accept waivability; seven uphold the waiver on this record, with two grounds and an express reservation by Stevens.**

Actual supported challenges to understanding, voluntariness, coercion and agreement scope remain distinct.

Advising defendants of the unusual special verdict at the plea hearing is a readily available safeguard, but the omission of that additional warning does not require remand when the record otherwise establishes informed relinquishment. ## American Life League v. Reno ? reconciled commitments

**Assigned merits event November 13, 1995.** The authorized vehicle is merits review of the Fourth Circuit's affirmance of dismissal with prejudice under Rule 12(b)(6), not a historical petition action. No grant, argument date, Supreme Court merits docket, private petition tally or additional order is invented. Historical-source petition No.94-1867 identifies research materials only. ### Material text and pleading premises retained in every commitment

Section 248(a)(1) requires force, threat of force, or physical obstruction and intentional injury, intimidation, interference or attempt. It separately reaches acts **because** someone is or has been obtaining/providing reproductive health services and acts **in order to intimidate** that person, another or a class from obtaining/providing them. Interference means restricted freedom of movement, intimidation reasonable apprehension of bodily harm to self or another, and obstruction impassable or unreasonably difficult/hazardous passage. Section (a)(3)'s challenged branch covers intentional reproductive-facility property damage/destruction or attempt because of the services. The defined services include medical, surgical, counseling and referral services relating to reproduction, pregnancy and termination; they are not confined to abortions or a single viewpoint.

The parent/legal-guardian exception removes **any penalties under the section** for the described conduct directed **exclusively at that minor**. It is neither a general secular-obstruction exception nor a nonexistent qualification. The constitutional and RFRA analyses below explicitly retain it. The worship provisions exist but are outside the presented issues.

The criminal, government civil and private-remedy schedules remain distinct. The private eligible claimant **may elect** $5,000 per violation before final judgment **in lieu of actual compensatory damages**; this does not mandate that award in all cases or replace every other remedy. The amount's reasonableness, actual number of violations, particular liability, punitive damages and criminal sentence are not before the Court. Section 248(d) preserves constitutionally protected expressive conduct, including peaceful demonstrations/picketing; it is a construction limit, not a premise that every covered act is already unprotected. Severability extends to provisions and applications. The enactment covers conduct from May 26, 1994.

The pleaded future conduct includes prayer, counseling, demonstrations and peaceful physical obstruction. “Peaceful” does not establish unobstructed passage, and the plaintiffs are not convicted violent offenders. The circuit assumed a substantial religious burden; the government disputes that threshold. No Justice resolves sincerity, doctrinal necessity, an individual's exact physical method, or private enforcement history as a fact. The RFRA alternatives below expressly assume the pleaded substantial burden for purposes of evaluating the dismissal.

RFRA supplies a separate claim: after substantial burden, the government bears both production and persuasion that applying the burden **to the person** furthers a compelling interest by the least restrictive means. Later federal law is subject unless it expressly excludes RFRA by reference; FACE does not. A constitutional neutral-law conclusion and intermediate speech scrutiny do not satisfy RFRA by substitution. Ordinary standing, appropriate relief, belief protection and the Establishment Clause remain.

### Shared constitutional and statutory conclusions

**A1 — Commerce power: all eight reject the presented commerce invalidity claim.** The challenged provisions directly protect the provision and receipt of medical services and the facilities through which those services are supplied against targeted coercive obstruction, violence and destruction. Congress regulates interference with an actual interstate services market, supported here by the reported legislative evidence of interstate patients, personnel and supplies and actual disruption/closure of service. The direct relation to service transactions differs from the remote chain from stand-alone school firearm possession through education and productivity rejected in the controlling Lopez holding. The legal object is not converted into noneconomic activity merely because the obstructing person's purpose is ideological or his act is local.

The common sufficient ground is the substantial commercial relationship of this regulated class, within the bounded economic-class reasoning retained in Lopez and applied in Harris. No individual crossing or amount is required by an element that Congress did not enact. Harris's actual vehicle-history element remains essential to that statute; it is not made a universal condition for all class regulation. Robertson's direct-enterprise holding confirms the significance of actual commercial inputs, labor and output in its own setting, but does not itself classify every protester as an interstate enterprise. The present common ground neither adopts an exhaustive commerce formula nor validates every local offense with remote economic costs. Stevens, Souter, Ginsburg and Breyer retain their broader Lopez dissenting substantial-effects view, but join this narrower sufficient ground.

The alternative persons/things-in-interstate-commerce theory is not needed to cover every statutory application. No assertion that some patients cross state lines is transformed into a universal individual nexus. Because the commerce ground suffices, all eight reserve the unadjudicated Fourteenth Amendment §5 alternative. Direct regulation of private conduct does not command state legislation or administration; all eight reject the independent Tenth Amendment objection on that bounded basis, without diminishing New York's anti-compulsion rule.

**A2 — Content/viewpoint classification and expressive obstruction: all eight reject the presented facial speech invalidity claim on a common conduct-based, intermediate-scrutiny ground.** The service-related motive elements identify the conduct's target and the access-related harm; they do not require an antiabortion message, religious belief, or hostility to a particular viewpoint. An actor can violate the relevant branch without communicating an idea. Political disagreement, criticism, prayer, counseling and persuasion alone do not meet the physical-act and harm definitions. A statute selecting the content of protected expression would not become neutral through a benign label; this statute's selection is tied to interference with medical access and service facilities.

Mitchell permits legally relevant motive to accompany independently proscribable conduct and protects abstract belief; it does not by itself establish that every peaceful obstruction is unprotected. For potentially expressive but physically obstructive conduct, the O'Brien/Ward inquiry therefore remains necessary. Protecting access to medical services and persons from physical restriction or apprehended bodily harm is substantial and unrelated to suppressing their opponents' message. The act directly advances that interest by addressing impassable or unreasonably difficult/hazardous access, force, threats and intentional destruction, rather than requiring listeners to accept counseling or banning advocacy throughout a broad area. Its definitions, intentional-act/motive predicates and protected-expression limitation confine the burden to that harm. Nonobstructive demonstration, prayer, signs, leafleting and willing conversation remain usable; no numerical buffer, advance invitation rule or compulsory silence is imposed here.

This is ordinary statute review, not automatic adoption of Madsen's heightened injunction standard or its distances. Turner requires review of actual selection and fit, with supported legislative predictions receiving deference rather than conclusiveness. Here the relevant connection between a physical blockade and denied access is direct; the constitutional conclusion does not depend on an unverified numerical legislative statistic or a forecast that all clinics will fail. The Act does not compel private speakers to include another message, as in Hurley. It preserves speech while regulating physical interference; the right to refuse another's message does not include physical dominion over that person's route to a medical service.

The comparison with labor or environmental protest does not establish a statutory exemption for those views. Their conduct falls within FACE only if the enacted service-related purpose and other predicates are established; a different motive or object may fall outside. Congress's selection of this physical service harm differs from selecting favored messages for scarce editorial space. The limited parent/minor exception is relational and confined to acts directed exclusively at the actor's own minor; it does not select abortion viewpoints or permit a general blockade against other service-seekers. These classifications answer the facial speech challenge, without deciding discriminatory enforcement or every mixed-motive application.

**A3 — Vagueness/overbreadth: all eight reject the presented facial claims.** The actual passage, movement and bodily-harm definitions, coupled with the required intentional conduct and service-related purpose, supply ascertainable conduct boundaries and do not criminalize mere offense, resentment or successful persuasion. That differs from the substantial protected coverage remaining in the controlling R.A.V. decision. Section 248(d)(1) reinforces a textually available limitation; it does not save any future application that actually punishes protected advocacy. True force threats remain distinct from harsh political hyperbole. The Court need not prescribe a new universal true-threat mental-state rule or decide an uncharged speaker's words. Speculative use of protected statements as motive evidence does not establish substantial overbreadth under Mitchell, while relevance and the bar on punishment of abstract belief remain.

**A4 — Private statutory damages: all eight reject the categorical objection.** Congress may provide elected violation-based statutory damages without requiring proof of the same amount as actual compensatory loss; Claiborne does not categorically forbid that remedy. Liability must still rest on this defendant's legally attributable violation, not shared views, membership, another person's acts, or protected persuasion that reduces demand. An injunction's actual-concert doctrine is not a substitute for the liability predicates of a damages claim. The holding neither decides whether $5,000 is reasonable nor imposes an award, counts violations, validates punitive damages, or measures damages from a mixed lawful/unlawful campaign. The statute's election is permissive and replaces actual compensatory damages, not automatically every form of relief.

**A5 — Constitutional religion, with a genuine scope difference.** All eight reject the facial claim that the statutory text establishes religious targeting: its predicates concern acts and service access, not the actor's religion, and religious prominence among opponents is not an objective targeting finding. The Act does not create a general exemption for comparable secular blockades while excluding religious ones. The parent/minor qualification must be considered; it concerns an exclusively parent/guardian-to-own-minor relation rather than collective outsiders' obstruction of others' access. That limited distinction does not itself establish the substantial secular underinclusion presented by the Lukumi scheme.

Stevens, Scalia, Kennedy, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer additionally reject the asserted constitutional exemption under the governing Smith/Lukumi neutral-law rule on the pleaded frame. O'Connor and Souter **reserve the broader constitutional substantial-burden exemption question** in light of their respective Lukumi positions and the statutory remand they support. They can join the rejection of demonstrated facial targeting and the speech holdings without endorsing Smith's full account. Neither adopts a new constitutional exemption or overrules Smith. No Justice's RFRA position is obtained by collapsing it into this constitutional component.

### A6 — RFRA: two supported dispositions

**O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter and Thomas would vacate the dismissal of the RFRA prospective-enforcement claim and remand for the separate statutory inquiry.** They assume the sufficiently pleaded substantial burden for purposes of the dismissal review, without establishing it at trial. Under their own Clearwater and Swanner commitments, a general invocation of access, health and safety, coupled with a successful intermediate-scrutiny speech analysis, does not by itself demonstrate least restrictive means for these persons. The present dismissal treats the intended physical obstruction at a general level; it does not resolve the plaintiffs' actual claimed religious conduct, the legally material application and accommodation possibilities, or the significance of the statute's own parent/minor qualification under RFRA's more demanding test.

Their remand does not grant a right to block a clinic, require an exemption that deprives another person of service, diminish the compelling weight of reliable medical access and bodily safety, or force patients to travel to another clinic. The government can show that a proposed accommodation would leave precisely the harm Congress may prevent. But it must carry the specified burdens for the persons and conduct at issue. The district court must identify which claimant and threatened application presents a live claim, determine substantial burden if genuinely contested, and if necessary require the government's claimant-specific compelling-interest/least-restrictive-means showing. It must consider concrete alternatives and any legally comparable exemptions without deciding religious truth or presuming that commerce strips protection. A mere desire to persuade rather than physically obstruct may fall outside the prohibition, but it may not be attributed to a claimant in place of the pleaded burden.

**Stevens, Ginsburg and Breyer would affirm the RFRA dismissal on the bounded assumed-burden application and thus affirm the entire judgment within the presented scope.** Their own Swanner dissent joins distinguish a general statutory label from an application that necessarily imposes the protected injury on an identified third party. Assuming the alleged religious obligation is to engage in the statutory physical obstruction, the requested immunity would leave other service-seekers facing impassable or unreasonably difficult/hazardous access because of the objector's religious decision. Allowing prayer, counseling, demonstrations and persuasion without that physical restriction preserves communication; permitting the very obstruction would remove the access protection in the affected encounter. Notice, referral to another facility, or reliance on a patient's ability to find another route does not preserve the same protected access. Those are reasons about this asserted exemption, not a declaration that entering commerce forfeits RFRA.

The three treat the parent/minor exception as materially different for this requested outsider obstruction: it is restricted to the parent's or guardian's own minor and cannot cover interference with other patients, providers or a general entrance. They do not decide all legal powers within a family, validate abuse, or declare a religious parent unprotected by RFRA. The limited relational exception does not supply a comparable secular entitlement to impose these plaintiffs' alleged blockade on others. Their conclusion applies to the assumed request to perform the defined obstructive conduct; it does not foreclose a materially different supported religious practice or an enforcement threat against nonobstructive expression. They find the direct statutory harm and the requested exemption sufficient on the pleading assumption, without finding each person's sincerity, exact acts, violence, interstate travel or historical guilt. They retain the government's production and persuasion burden and do not impose on claimants a universal duty to prove an alternative.

### Enhanced alternative-path tests

**Commerce.** The strongest invalidation path treats the regulated activity as local ideological protest or ordinary crime without an individual commerce element, and views aggregate medical-market consequences as Lopez's forbidden causal chain. It properly resists a federal police power and judicial addition of a missing element. The common eight-Justice answer identifies a direct target — service provision/receipt and the facilities providing it — rather than a remote general productivity effect. Harris preserves class regulation within an actual economic setting; its particular enacted nexus is not a freestanding rule for all statutes. The conclusion does not decide the §5 alternative, or use a reported finding as conclusive proof of every application. A broader rationale that any locally committed offense with medical costs is commercial would lose the Lopez-majority Justices' joins.

**Speech.** The strongest invalidation path compares the covered service-related motives with other motives producing identical obstruction and invokes Turner/R.A.V. against selective suppression of antiabortion expression. It is especially substantial for peaceful expressive obstruction: Mitchell's independently criminal battery does not answer that issue by label. The common answer evaluates the physical target and defined harm, both service-related motive branches and the availability of protected advocacy; it applies intermediate scrutiny rather than declaring all protest conduct outside the First Amendment. No protected message is required to violate the law, no disagreement is itself punishable, and the qualifying physical access harm supplies the fit. Scalia, Kennedy, Souter and Thomas's R.A.V. separate theory would itself preserve genuine conduct and category-related injury distinctions; their present join does not make that theory controlling. A proposed rule that motive is never constitutionally significant, or that a savings clause alone defeats overbreadth, would not retain these joins.

**RFRA.** The strongest affirmance path is the three-Justice application above: a religious entitlement to cause the defined access deprivation leaves the protected person bearing the objector's choice, and suggested nonblocking alternatives preserve expression without sacrificing access. The five remanding Justices accept the force of that showing in a properly identified application but reject treating it as complete at the generalized dismissal stage. Their Swanner commitments preserve an application-specific inquiry even when the law protects important direct third-party interests. The strongest objection to remand is that it becomes a mandatory fact hearing whenever a claimant says religion requires the prohibited injury. The five adopt no such rule: a claim can fail on its actual pleadings and legally sufficient government showing, and a genuine substantial-burden failure ends RFRA without compelling-interest review. They conclude only that this dismissal record and reasoning do not complete the governing statutory task. No preliminary or permanent injunction follows automatically from their remand.

The three affirming Justices confront the contrary Swanner majority rather than substituting their dissent for its law. They apply its required person/conduct inquiry on an express assumption and find the requested immunity logically defeats the protected access in that encounter. They reserve materially different accommodations and enforcement threats. Their disagreement with the five concerns the sufficiency of this pleading-level application, not RFRA's force, burden sequence, or the existence of an obligation to consider material exceptions.
