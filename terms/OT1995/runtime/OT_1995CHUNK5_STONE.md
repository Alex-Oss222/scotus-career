#### B. JUDGMENT AND REMEDY

* **Stone’s Controlled Judgment or Disposition:** Reverse the Ninth Circuit’s judgment and remand for dismissal of the RCRA claim as pleaded. This is Stone's approved vote.
* **Stone’s Remedy and Remand Position:** No reimbursement is available through this citizen-suit claim. Preserve only independently pleaded or otherwise lawfully available alternative claims; do not imply that another statute necessarily authorizes recovery.

#### C. ISSUE POSITIONS

##### Issue 1: Endangerment at commencement

* **Threshold and Merits Reach:** Reach the temporal coverage question on the complaint as pleaded; do not find a remaining danger not alleged.
* **Stone’s Legal Position:** Apply the current-threat language without converting imminence into a demand for completed present injury.
* **Stone’s Proposed Holding:** Section 6972(a)(1)(B) requires an endangerment that may presently exist when suit is filed. The reference to past contribution identifies responsible persons; it does not eliminate the current-threat condition. The pleaded completed cleanup does not satisfy that condition.
* **Essential Reasoning and Record Application:** Past disposal can create a present threat, which explains coverage of a former contributor. That is different from treating a fully removed threat as presently imminent. The text gives both propositions work, and the statute’s prospective abatement design reinforces that reading. [P1, 485–486]
* **Boundary or Reserved Question:** No demand that injury already have occurred; a supported ongoing or threatened exposure may qualify despite the age of the disposal.

##### Issue 2: Completed cleanup costs

* **Threshold and Merits Reach:** Reach the remedy demanded, while reserving expense allocation arising during a properly commenced abatement suit.
* **Stone’s Legal Position:** Apply the statute’s remedial design rather than derive reimbursement from the desirability of cleanup.
* **Stone’s Proposed Holding:** RCRA’s citizen-suit provision does not authorize this action solely to recover the costs of a completed cleanup. The claim must be dismissed without adjudicating independently available remedies.
* **Essential Reasoning and Record Application:** The broad phrase concerning necessary action serves the elimination of a qualifying endangerment; it does not create the retrospective cost action Congress expressly formulated in CERCLA. Policy concerns about prompt cleanup cannot supply omitted liability, particularly in a scheme with notice and public-enforcement limitations not designed for a damages action. The United States’ support for broader equitable authority in some properly filed cases does not establish this particular remedy after the endangerment has ended. [P1, 483–488]
* **Boundary or Reserved Question:** State-law claims, any independently available federal cost action, and costs associated with relief in a properly pending RCRA case remain outside this holding.

## OT1995-043 — Varity Corp. v. Howe

**Event:** 1996-03-19 | **Category:** MERITS | **Action:** Argued merits decision

**Citation:** 516 U.S. 489

**Docket(s):** No. 94-1471

### SECTION II — STONE POSITION SUPPLEMENT

**Position status:** Approved by the user as written on October 2, 2026; no Court vote or outcome entered.

- **Stone’s Controlled Judgment:** Affirm the Eighth Circuit.
- **Stone’s Remedy / Remand Position:** Preserve the source-bound equitable relief awarded to restore the affected beneficiaries to the position they would have occupied absent the fiduciary breach, while excluding legal damages or duplicative relief available through another adequate ERISA channel.
- **Stone’s Threshold Position:** Decide first whether Varity acted in a fiduciary capacity when making the challenged representations and then whether the requested relief is appropriate equitable relief under §502(a)(3).
- **Supporting Authorities:** ERISA §§3(21)(A), 404, and 502(a)(1)(B)–(3); *Massachusetts Mutual Life Insurance Co. v. Russell*, 473 U.S. 134; *Mertens v. Hewitt Associates*, 508 U.S. 248; *Curtiss-Wright Corp. v. Schoonejongen*, 514 U.S. 73.
- **Stone’s Proposed Holding for This Case:** A person acts as an ERISA fiduciary to the extent it exercises discretionary plan administration or communicates plan information in that capacity, and §502(a)(3) authorizes appropriate individual equitable relief for a fiduciary breach when no other ERISA provision provides an adequate remedy for that injury.
- **Stone’s Reasoning:** Varity did more than announce a corporate restructuring: it used its plan-administration authority to explain existing benefit security and deliberately induced beneficiaries to surrender protected plan interests through materially false assurances. ERISA defines fiduciary status by the function performed, so the same employer may act as a settlor in one act and a fiduciary in another. Section 502(a)(3) operates as a source-bounded equitable safety net where the statute supplies no other adequate remedy, but *Mertens* prevents conversion of that language into general legal damages.
- **Stone’s Operative Limit:** The holding does not make every employer communication fiduciary conduct, authorize legal or punitive damages under §502(a)(3), or permit duplicative relief where another ERISA provision adequately remedies the same injury.

**Stone’s reasoning and application:** When an educational injury caused by the State’s segregated system remains unremedied, the absence of predicted increases in white enrollment cannot by itself justify refusing necessary, practicable, educationally sound repair. Students’ educational opportunities matter independently of their usefulness in attracting other students. Fordice requires dismantlement of traceable policies with continuing segregative effects and places the dismantlement burden on the State. Actual simulated Jenkins recognizes necessary educational repair as an independently sufficient remedial ground; improvement need not be justified solely by voluntary racial attraction. Applying that principle in this university setting remains tied to the established violation and educational injury, not to a general guarantee of educational quality or equal institutional budgets. Milliken v. Bradley (Milliken II), 433 U.S. 267, which simulated Jenkins and Fordice apply, sustains educational remedies beyond pupil assignment where the record connects them to the violation. Extending it to university library resources develops that doctrine; Milliken II did not decide the question.

**Equipment and libraries:** The 1995 findings identify unequal historical investment and continuing equipment and library conditions, and connect those policies to inherited missions to the stated extent. Preserve supported findings. Determine whether the challenged deficiency perpetuates an educational injury attributable to the unconstitutional system and whether the proposed expenditure would repair it practicably and consistently with sound education. A racial comparison of budgets alone does not answer that question. Where a laboratory deficiency has that established relationship, a necessary improvement may qualify as relief even if enrollment remains predominantly Black. This example illustrates the rule; it does not invent an additional laboratory finding in this record.

**Library ruling:** The district court declined to enlarge the historically Black institutions’ libraries on two grounds. It attributed the differences in volume holdings to historical mission assignments and found racial comparison across broadly disparate missions of little use; and, absent proof that the holdings precluded those institutions as viable choices for white students, it found enlargement beyond the level consistent with their missions not educationally sound. It also noted the library expansions underway at Jackson State and Alcorn. 879 F. Supp. at 1456–58. By conditioning enlargement on proof about white students’ choices, the second ground makes white-student attraction a prerequisite to repairing an educational injury the opinion otherwise connects to the dual system; under the stated principle, it cannot by itself support the denial. The first ground does not resolve the question on this record. Different missions can justify different collections, but an inherited mission restriction cannot conclusively justify limited resources if the restriction itself remains an unlawful feature of the former dual system. The same opinion found mission policies traceable to de jure segregation and still fostering separation, and library allocations traceable to the degree they follow those missions, 879 F. Supp. at 1477; simulated Fordice requires examination of inherited mission assignments with their combined segregative effects and educational justifications. The library ruling did not decide whether the mission limit it applied is justified apart from that history, what library resources the institutions’ missions require, including the program changes ordered in decree paragraphs 3–10, or whether the expansions underway repair the holdings and space conditions the opinion found. Had the findings established that the libraries adequately support lawful missions and that the improvements underway remedy any traceable injury, the principle would justify no additional relief; the analysis leaves both points unresolved.

**Programs and missions:** Preserve the supported Jackson State and Alcorn improvements reflected in decree paragraphs 3–10. Examine challenged mission limits and program arrangements for their continuation of the established violation. Historically Black institutions are not themselves the constitutional wrong. Equal opportunity does not require identical programs at every institution or justify discarding an effective educational institution merely because of its enrollment. Any separately challenged racial eligibility term retains its own governing equality requirements; support for institutional repair does not automatically decide such a term.

**Admissions and scholarships:** Apply Fordice directly to the identified barriers and their interaction, using the operative admissions provisions, including the fourth full-admission alternative the February 16, 1996 order made equally applicable to all institutions, rather than assuming the original March 1995 formulation remained unchanged. The State’s dismantlement burden, supported findings, educational justifications and practicable alternatives govern. AY-2 supplies no independent holding that a particular ACT threshold or scholarship criterion is unlawful. A policy with no established connection to the violation is not condemned merely because outcomes differ by race. Existing admissions reform is not discarded solely to substitute a preferred admissions formula.

Supp. at 1456–57 and 1477. The vacatur reaches only the portion of the judgment denying additional library relief. On remand, the court reconsiders additional library relief under the stated principle, using the existing findings and crediting the Jackson State and Alcorn expansions already underway: whether a library condition attributable to the dual system remains unremedied; what library resources each affected institution needs to carry out its legally justified mission, including the program changes already ordered in decree paragraphs 3–10; and what practicable, educationally sound repair, if any, remains necessary. The remand does not reopen the decree’s mission, program or admissions provisions or the equipment determinations, and does not require every university to acquire an equally large collection. The preparation does not assume any other denial rested on the enrollment premise, and the remand creates no new universal proof protocol.
