## Decisions and Dispositions

### Doctor's Associates, Inc. v. Casarotto, No. 95-559

Merits decision, May 20, 1996

517 U.S. 681. Argued April 16, 1996; October Term 1995. On certiorari under 28 U.S.C. §1257(a) to the Montana Supreme Court, 274 Mont. 3, 901 P.2d 596, reviewing its August 31, 1995 judgment sustaining a statutory notice bar to arbitration. The question presented is whether §2 of the Federal Arbitration Act (FAA) preempts the arbitration-specific first-page notice condition.

Paul Casarotto signed DAI's April 25, 1988 franchise agreement. Paragraph 10(c), on page nine, requires arbitration of controversies arising out of or relating to the contract or its breach under AAA Commercial Arbitration Rules in Bridgeport, Connecticut. Commencement of arbitration is a condition precedent to legal action, and costs are shared equally. The agreement selects Connecticut law, provides severability and acknowledges reading, understanding and consent. Its integration clause preserves representations in the agreement, offering circular and franchisor advertising. The offering circular disclosed arbitration; the agreement has no first-page arbitration notice. Paul's affidavit describes a nonnegotiable contract, absent explanation and lack of understanding of the lost Montana forum. The affidavit and signed acknowledgment are evidence, not findings of informed assent or ignorance.

The district court found actual interstate commerce, clause coverage of claims against DAI and its alleged agent Lombardi, and an arbitration demand. Invoking FAA §3, it stayed those claims June 2, 1993, and later certified its order under state Rule 54(b). The Montana majority rejected the stay because §27-5-114(4) requires notice that a contract is subject to arbitration pursuant to the chapter, typed in underlined capital letters on its first page, and precludes arbitration without that notice. It selected Montana law under conflicts principles and relied on *Volt*, reasoning that informed consent advances arbitration. It announced no independently sufficient general contract-defense ruling.

The dispute concerns subsection (4). Subsection (2) separately excludes personal-injury claims; contracts for an individual's acquisition of property, services, money or credit with total consideration of $5,000 or less; insurance or annuity agreements except contracts between insurance companies; and workers' compensation claims. Its express subsection (3) qualification concerns written agreements among trade or professional organization members to arbitrate future disputes among members. Those are not exceptions written into subsection (4), and their validity or application is not decided here.

All nine Justices participated at argument and decision.

#### Judgment

**Reversed and remanded, 8–1.**

**Supporting:** Chief Justice Alex-Lamar Stone-Zsela and Justices Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Ginsburg and Breyer.

**Opposing:** Justice Thomas, who would affirm because he concludes that the FAA does not command enforcement in state court.

The state courts must resolve the stay request without the preempted notice condition. Existing commerce, clause-scope and demand determinations remain undisturbed.

#### Controlling holdings

##### I. The finally decided federal notice bar is reviewable

**Holding and authority.** Part I, joined by all nine Justices, holds that §1257(a) permits review of Montana's finally resolved federal notice-preemption question despite continuing merits proceedings. The finality doctrine applied in *Southland* and *Cox Broadcasting* governs, and no independently sufficient lower contract-defense ground is established. State Rule 54(b) certification alone does not establish federal finality, and this conclusion creates no universal right to review interlocutory arbitration orders.

**Controlling explanation.** *Southland* applied *Cox Broadcasting* to a state judgment finally rejecting arbitration despite further litigation below. Delaying review risks both losing later review if the arbitration proponent prevails on nonfederal grounds and eroding the claimed federal forum right through the very litigation arbitration would avoid. Montana conclusively sustained the notice bar; reversal removes that adjudicated obstacle to the existing stay request.

Neither Rule 54(b) nor unadjudicated damages claims supplies the answer by itself. A genuinely sufficient independent ground defeating arbitration would require its own finality analysis, but no such lower ground is established. Possible defenses are not adjudicated defenses. Montana's conflicts ruling selects the notice rule whose federal validity is challenged; it does not independently sustain that rule against federal law. The Court therefore need not resolve a separate state-law conflicts challenge to reach preemption.

*Southland*'s earlier appeal route under former §1257(2) is not the present jurisdictional provision. Its finality reasoning applies to this §1257(a) certiorari review. Thomas's disagreement about §2's substantive reach does not remove jurisdiction to decide that federal question.

##### II. Section 2 forbids this arbitration-only notice condition

**Holding and authority.** Part II, adopted by the eight Justices joining Parts II–III, holds that Montana may not deny arbitration under this FAA-covered agreement because the contract lacks the notice typed in underlined capital letters on its first page required by §27-5-114(4). Generally applicable formation and revocation rules remain available. *Volt* protects actual contractual selection of state arbitration procedure, not this imposed condition invalidating the clause; no selection of Montana's condition is established.

**Controlling explanation.** Section 2 makes covered written arbitration agreements valid, irrevocable and enforceable, subject to grounds at law or equity for revoking any contract. *Allied-Bruce* retained *Southland*'s application in state courts and actual-commerce coverage. The district court's commerce finding stands; no new coverage hearing or outer constitutional ruling is needed. Retained statutory precedent governs despite the dissent's original-construction objection.

*Perry* preserves ordinary contract law while rejecting disabilities keyed to arbitration. Montana's typography, placement and nonenforcement requirements attach because the contract selects arbitration. Allowing enforcement after compliance does not make that additional condition a ground for revoking any contract.

Respondents invoke informed consent, adhesion, reasonable expectations and Restatement §211. Those concerns may support genuinely applicable contract doctrines; they do not transform this categorical special condition into general law. Modest compliance cost, physical compatibility with federal law and a consent-protective purpose do not answer §2's saving-clause limit. No subjective hostility finding is required.

*Volt* enforced an actual selection of state arbitration procedure. This agreement selects Connecticut law and AAA arbitration in Bridgeport, not Montana's first-page condition. *Mastrobuono* requires attention to the integrated agreement and genuine contractual restrictions. Neither decision shields an imposed invalidity rule or requires deciding every Connecticut-law or conflicts question. The lower dissent's construction of the statute's “pursuant to this chapter” language is not an independent ground adopted here.

The Montana majority announced no independently sufficient reasonable-expectations, fraud or unconscionability holding. Properly available general defenses survive; arbitration's uniqueness cannot itself become unconscionability under another label. Respondents' asserted *Chor* actual-knowledge exception is unestablished: its majority addressed a common-law defense on its evidence, not an express statutory exception. Paul's affidavit and acknowledgment supply no new finding of knowledge, ignorance or invalidity. No general conspicuousness requirement or broader disclosure rule is adjudicated. The enacted equal-footing command, not a free-standing preference for arbitration, controls.

##### III. Remand preserves actual contractual and party limits

**Holding and authority.** Part III of the same eight-Justice opinion requires resolution of the stay request without the notice bar, subject to genuinely available, properly preserved independent grounds and the actual parties' contractual rights. Existing commerce, clause-scope and demand determinations remain intact. Reversal requires neither unconditional compulsion nor a new evidentiary proceeding.

**Controlling explanation.** Petitioners seek immediate reinstatement of the stay, but invalidating the notice condition does not decide every enforcement predicate. Paul signed; Pamela's entire position is unresolved. Lombardi's alleged agency is not an actual agency finding, and his contested nonsignatory enforcement right is not independently decided. Existing scope findings do not expand into every enforcement theory. Claims against the Hudson defendants and D&D Subway remain outside this stay and appeal.

*First Options* requires courts independently to determine whether a party agreed to arbitrate arbitrability. Only clear and unmistakable delegation established under ordinary contract law permits arbitrators to decide the underlying arbitrability question; one party's assent supplies no other person's assent to that delegation. The broad clause and AAA reference establish no adjudicated delegation here; independent nonsignatory theories remain unresolved. Ordinary appellate review remains independent for legal questions and for clear error as to factual findings. No new conditional award-review holding is made.

The lower majority relied on the notice statute. The parties dispute whether an ordinary adhesion defense remains preserved; neither abandonment nor a successful defense is found. General formation, fraud, duress, unconscionability, lack of assent and other evenhanded defenses retain their own requirements. Remand creates no defense, revives none already waived and orders no hearing merely because a defense is conceivable.

The district court's invocation of §3 does not import §§3–4 wholesale into state courts. Section 3 addresses courts of the United States and requires a written agreement, a referable issue, the court's satisfaction, a party's application, arbitration according to the agreement and no applicant default in proceeding. These conditions remain distinct if invoked; no default or mandatory default inquiry is established. State courts must implement §2 through lawful procedure.

The forum, cost, severability and integration terms are neither rewritten nor independently upheld against every defense. No liability, damages, actual arbitral expenses, award or constitutional jury-waiver rule is decided. Absent an available preserved independent ground, the notice objection cannot prevent restoration of the existing stay.

#### Precedent treatment

- *Southland Corp. v. Keating*, 465 U.S. 1, supplies its finality reasoning at pages 6–8 and its retained substantive state-court §2 rule. Its former appellate subsection is not the current review channel.
- *Cox Broadcasting Corp. v. Cohn*, 420 U.S. 469, supplies finality doctrine for this conclusively resolved federal obstacle despite further merits proceedings. No general interlocutory-review rule follows.
- *Perry v. Thomas*, 482 U.S. 483, requires equal enforcement while preserving grounds applicable to contracts generally. A general consent label does not save an arbitration-specific disability.
- *Allied-Bruce Terminix Cos. v. Dobson*, January 18, 1995, retains state-court coverage and actual commerce, preserving ordinary defenses, assent, scope and the reservation of wholesale §§3–4 incorporation.
- *Volt Information Sciences, Inc. v. Board of Trustees*, 489 U.S. 468, protects actual contractual selection of state arbitration procedure. Invalidation by an unselected forum notice rule is different.
- *Mastrobuono v. Shearson Lehman Hutton, Inc.*, March 6, 1995, requires attention to the actual integrated agreement and genuine contractual restrictions, without resolving every conflicts question.
- *First Options of Chicago, Inc. v. Kaplan*, May 22, 1995, preserves antecedent assent, actual clear-and-unmistakable delegation, ordinary legal/factual review and unresolved nonsignatory theories. Its conditional review rule remains governing in its own setting; neither delegation nor a new award-review question is decided here.

No precedent is overruled. Thomas's proposed rejection of *Southland*'s state-court construction does not alter its controlling force.

#### Law after the decision

Effective May 20, 1996, Montana §27-5-114(4) cannot defeat an FAA-covered arbitration agreement for lack of the prescribed first-page notice. The decision applies §2's state-court command to this special formality. It does not invalidate all of §27-5-114 or decide its separate exclusions.

Facially neutral, generally applicable contract law remains effective; merely affecting arbitration does not displace it. Ordinary formation, scope, fraud, duress, unconscionability, lack of assent and other defenses retain their genuine evenhanded operation and preservation requirements. Arbitration's special character cannot itself supply a disguised invalidity rule. Existing actual-commerce coverage stands, while the outer Commerce Clause boundary and wholesale state incorporation of §§3–4 remain unaddressed. No unconditional enforcement entitlement against every person or claim follows.

#### Dissent

Justice Thomas reads the FAA's structure and its federal-court judicial directions as failing to create §2's substantive command to state tribunals. He would overrule *Southland* to that extent and affirm the Montana judgment on federal nonapplication, maintaining the ground stated in his *Allied-Bruce* dissent. Statutory stare decisis and reliance do not, in his view, cure that original construction error.

Participation in federal arbitration cases concerning assent or other statutes does not adopt *Southland*'s state-court rule. The dissent does not conditionally uphold the notice law under a state-applicable §2, find a successful general contract defense, decide commerce coverage, announce an independently sufficient §§3–4 specific-performance rule or restrict Congress's constitutional power to enact a different command. It supplies no liability, damages or party-wide arbitration judgment. These proposed changes are noncontrolling.

#### Mandate, remedy, and stage

The Montana Supreme Court's judgment sustaining the arbitration-specific notice bar is reversed. The case returns to the Montana courts for further proceedings consistent with Parts II–III. The notice condition cannot again sustain denial of the existing stay. If no genuinely available, properly preserved independent ground defeats the stay, that objection cannot prevent its restoration, subject to the actual parties' contractual rights.

The Court does not unconditionally reinstate the stay, direct arbitration of all persons and claims, revive waived defenses or order a new hearing. No new commerce inquiry or mandatory defense hearing is required. Supreme Court merits review is complete, subject to ordinary mandate procedures; no later Supreme Court event is prescribed.

### Henderson v. United States, No. 95-232

Merits decision, May 20, 1996

517 U.S. 654. Argued March 19, 1996; October Term 1995. On certiorari under 28 U.S.C. §1254(1) to the Fifth Circuit, 51 F.3d 574, which affirmed dismissal without prejudice under the Suits in Admiralty Act's direction to serve the complaint forthwith. The questions are whether Rule 4 displaces that timetable and, alternatively, whether the statutory period may be extended.

Henderson filed on April 8, 1993, after administrative disallowance, alleging injury on or about August 27, 1991 aboard the United States-owned DELMONTE. His negligence, unseaworthiness, maintenance, cure and damages claims remain unadjudicated. The Attorney General received the mailed papers May 25, day 47. The original 120-day period expired August 6. The district court signed a fifteen-day service extension August 31; it was filed September 1, and the United States Attorney was served September 3, day 148. The order contains no express finding of good cause, diligence or clerk fault. Counsel's account of problems with forms, seals and mail and the Government's challenge to his diligence remain opposed submissions.

The Government sought dismissal under §742, without a distinct Rule 4 dismissal ground, and described service as properly effected September 3. The district court dismissed under Rule 12(b)(1) in June 1994. The Fifth Circuit treated forthwith service as a jurisdictional condition of consent applying to both recipients and held 148 days insufficient even assuming good cause and a reasonable-time construction. It did not decide whether day-47 receipt independently satisfied the statute. Dismissal without prejudice did not establish that a new suit would be timely.

All nine Justices participated at argument and decision.

#### Judgment

**Reversed and remanded, 7–2.**

**Supporting:** Chief Justice Alex-Lamar Stone-Zsela and Justices Stevens, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Ginsburg and Breyer.

**Opposing:** Justices O'Connor and Thomas, who would affirm dismissal under the surviving statutory service condition.

The action was timely commenced by filing. Neither day-148 service nor day-47 receipt can sustain dismissal under the displaced statutory timetable.

#### Opinion of the Court

Justice Scalia delivered the Opinion of the Court, Parts I–III, joined throughout by Chief Justice Stone-Zsela and Justices Stevens, Kennedy, Souter, Ginsburg and Breyer. These seven Justices directly adopted each holding and explanation below. Justice Thomas dissented, joined throughout by Justice O'Connor.

##### I. The service direction implements an existing consent to suit

**Holding and authority.** Part I holds that §742's direction to serve the filed complaint forthwith regulates procedure in an already authorized Suits in Admiralty Act action. It does not independently condition this waiver of sovereign immunity or remove subject-matter jurisdiction. Congress may make a procedural requirement a jurisdictional condition of consent, but this timetable's placement, function and surrounding provisions establish that Congress did not do so here.

**Controlling explanation.** Section 742 first authorizes the specified nonjury in-personam admiralty proceeding against the United States. Its later sentences direct delivery to the United States Attorney, registered mailing to the Attorney General and a sworn return. These steps notify the designated recipients of the filed action. Consent, venue, service and transfer perform separate functions within the section; proximity to the waiver does not make every direction jurisdictional.

Section 743's instruction to follow principles and practice applicable between private parties reinforces this procedural reading. It does not erase that section's separate prescriptions concerning costs, interest, admiralty review, in-rem principles concerning proceedings against property, or government security. Nor does procedure's subject alone settle the question: Congress may attach jurisdictional consequences to procedure. This holding interprets the particular scheme and announces no categorical immunity exception.

The Government argues that placing the service command in §742, apart from §743's general practice provision, specially conditions consent. That structure gives the service directions work without making their timetable a substantive waiver limit. *Nordic Village* requires clear consent to the particular monetary remedy and distinguishes consent from general jurisdiction; this decision supplies no new monetary remedy. *McNeil* enforces a prefiling exhaustion sequence, and *Keene* a filing-time jurisdictional bar. Neither determines this postfiling direction's function. *Carlisle* likewise distinguishes procedural restrictions from subject-matter jurisdiction without classifying all procedural provisions alike.

The two-year commencement limit and substantive boundaries of consent remain intact. Whether the timetable survives as a nonjurisdictional command is the separate question addressed in Part II.

##### II. Congress's later Rule 4 scheme displaces the competing timetable

**Holding and authority.** Part II holds that, in this timely commenced domestic Suits in Admiralty Act action, the applicable Rule 4 timing scheme, including authorized enlargement, governs service. Section 742 cannot independently require dismissal for failure to serve sooner. Congress's direct enactment of former Rule 4(j) displaces the incompatible procedural timetable without enlarging substantive consent or dispensing with the designated-recipient requirements.

**Controlling explanation.** Congress enacted former Rule 4(j) in Pub. L. 97-462. It required dismissal without prejudice when service was not made within 120 days and good cause was not shown, on motion or on the court's initiative after notice; foreign service under former subdivision (i) was excepted. This extendable scheme governs the same postfiling service event as §742.

The Government argues that prompt service satisfies both texts and that a general outer deadline does not repeal a special command. But physical compliance is not the whole inquiry. A separately fatal, shorter timetable would deny the service allowance and authorized enlargement the later scheme supplies. Former Rule 4(a)'s prompt-service responsibility creates no such independent statutory dismissal condition; the holding nevertheless permits no deliberate disregard of service responsibilities.

Section 2072 authorizes procedural regulation, protects substantive rights and addresses conflicting procedural laws. Its framework supports this construction, but its clause concerning rules prescribed under the Act does not directly nullify earlier law through a rule Congress itself enacted. The operative route is the later congressional enactment, construed with this specific conflict. The Court adopts no second independently sufficient ground based on Court promulgation. Rule 82 is respected because neither substantive consent nor jurisdiction is enlarged.

*Hanna* and *Mississippi Publishing Corp. v. Murphree* support treating service mechanisms as procedure; they do not replace the conflict inquiry. *Walker* and *Ragan* preserve a state-created right's distinct state-law commencement condition: Rule 3's filing provision did not address that different limitations question. Here both provisions govern the same service event. Section 743 reinforces limited displacement without erasing other special Suits in Admiralty Act provisions.

The holding concerns timing, not every service statute, all government/private procedural differences or an unpresented registered-versus-certified-mail dispute. The 1993 amendment's qualified pending-case application remains governed by Part III.

##### III. Remand removes the statutory dismissal without deciding a new service dispute

**Holding and authority.** Part III requires reversal of the statutory dismissal as to both designated recipients and application of the governing Federal Rules to any properly presented issue in ordinary further proceedings. The decision neither validates the existing extension nor orders a new service hearing. Any necessary application of amended Rule 4 must honor its express pending-case qualification.

**Controlling explanation.** September 3 fell outside the original 120 days but within the district court's fifteen-day direction. No distinct Rule 4 defect supplied the dismissal under review. The absence of express good-cause findings does not itself invalidate the order or require renewed factfinding. Any genuinely available later objection remains subject to its own authority, preservation and procedural conditions. Neither recipient's timing sustains the displaced §742 ground; whether either service was forthwith need not be decided.

Former Rule 4(j) governed the April–September 1993 acts. The April 22, 1993 order made amended Rule 4 effective December 1 for new cases and, insofar as just and practicable, proceedings in then-pending cases. That direction controls if a properly presented issue concerning the 1994 dismissal or further proceedings requires choosing the applicable Rule. The Court does not automatically relabel the 1993 order or hold that amended Rule 4(m) necessarily supplies relief.

Under Rule 4(m), after notice, the court must dismiss without prejudice or direct service within a specified time when service exceeds 120 days; good cause requires an appropriate extension. Without good cause, enlargement is permitted, not compelled. Foreign service under subdivisions (f) and (j)(1) is excepted. A limitations bar may support discretion but does not require relief. *Pioneer* preserves flexibility authorized by the governing rule, not a Suits in Admiralty Act tolling power. *Landgraf* requires attention to express temporal direction and does not automatically reopen completed acts.

The district-court Rule 12 waiver contention supplies no independently sufficient reversal ground: the transfer motion's legal effect and appellate preservation are not established. No new waiver ruling, negligence finding, maintenance or cure award, damages judgment, immediate trial entitlement or separate due-process decision follows. Section 745's two-year limit and narrowly dated 1950 savings provision create no general new refiling opportunity.

#### Precedent treatment

- *United States v. Nordic Village, Inc.*, 503 U.S. 30, retains its requirement of clear consent to the actual monetary remedy and its distinction between consent and jurisdiction. Its original §106(c) construction does not classify this service timetable.
- *McNeil v. United States*, 508 U.S. 106, concerns prefiling administrative exhaustion. That statutory sequence and its express alternatives and exceptions remain unchanged.
- *Keene Corp. v. United States*, 508 U.S. 200, concerns a particular filing-time bar; later service presents a different operative event.
- *Carlisle v. United States*, April 29, 1996, supplies the distinction between procedural authority and subject-matter jurisdiction, while preserving rule-consistent discretion, express transition limits and independently supported avenues of relief.
- *Hanna v. Plumer*, 380 U.S. 460, supplies recognition of federal service procedure; actual conflict and lawful authority remain necessary.
- *Mississippi Publishing Corp. v. Murphree*, 326 U.S. 438, supports service's procedural role in implementing an existing right without enlarging substantive rights or jurisdiction.
- *Walker v. Armco Steel Corp.*, 446 U.S. 740, concerns state limitations and commencement, unlike the competing provisions governing the same federal postfiling service event here.
- *Ragan v. Merchants Transfer & Warehouse Co.*, 337 U.S. 530, preserves the state-law commencement condition for the state-created right. Rule 3 does not universally displace such conditions.
- *Pioneer Investment Services Co. v. Brunswick Associates Limited Partnership*, 507 U.S. 380, preserves rule-specific enlargement authority and its limits. It supplies neither former Rule 4's good-cause test nor a general extension of Suits in Admiralty Act conditions.
- *Landgraf v. USI Film Products*, 511 U.S. 244, requires respect for express temporal direction. Neither a pending case nor procedural characterization automatically reopens completed acts.

No Supreme Court precedent is overruled. The Fifth Circuit's premise that the forthwith-service timetable remains a jurisdictional condition enforceable independently of Rule 4 is rejected.

#### Law after the decision

Effective May 20, 1996, §742's competing forthwith timetable cannot defeat service allowed by the governing Rule 4 scheme in a timely commenced domestic Suits in Admiralty Act action. This procedural direction does not define substantive consent. Congress retains power to impose jurisdictional procedural conditions, and an enacted command requires a lawful superseding source for displacement.

The designated-recipient requirements, §745's two-year commencement period, substantive waiver limits and compliance with the applicable Rules remain. The extension's validity, other possible service issues, the Clarification Act/PVA classification, maritime liability and relief are undecided. The decision neither extends a surviving statutory deadline nor creates a general refiling period. Further use of amended Rule 4 remains subject to the express just-and-practicable transition and the distinction between permitted and mandatory enlargement.

#### Dissent

Justice Thomas's dissent would affirm. Congress may make procedure a condition of sovereign consent, and the dissent reads §742's particular service command, separately from §743's general private-party practice instruction, as doing so here. *Sherwood*, *Block* and *Nordic Village* require respect for the actual scope of consent; the argument is not that every sentence in a waiver statute is jurisdictional. *Meyer*'s broad agency clause and *Carlisle*'s criminal procedural mechanism do not determine the differently structured Suits in Admiralty Act waiver.

A judicial rule cannot enlarge that consent under §2072(b) and Rule 82. Congress enacted former Rule 4(j) and could change the waiver, but its general dismissal timetable does not sufficiently establish repeal of the retained special condition. Prompt service can satisfy both, and general authorized enlargement need not displace the special statute. The 1993 transition supplies no independent substantive enlargement.

Service on the United States Attorney after 148 days fails the surviving requirement even under a reasonable-dispatch reading and assuming good cause. The Rule 4 extension does not extend that separate condition. No universal number of days, adverse diligence finding or conclusion about day-47 Attorney General receipt is required. *Pioneer* and the *American Pipe*/*McCormick* analogy establish no general authority to extend this consent condition. The dissent creates no new Rule 4 defect and decides neither injury merits, separate due process nor a right to timely refiling. The unestablished Rule 12 waiver route supplies no alternative reversal of the jurisdictional condition. These propositions are noncontrolling.

#### Mandate, remedy, and stage

The Fifth Circuit's judgment affirming dismissal under §742's forthwith-service direction is reversed. The case returns through that court for ordinary further proceedings consistent with Parts I–III. The displaced statutory timetable cannot again sustain dismissal. ### Pinder v. Johnson

Merits decision, May 21, 1996

October Term 1995. On certiorari under 28 U.S.C. §1254(1) to the Fourth Circuit in No. 93-2125, reviewing its May 30, 1995 en banc judgment, 54 F.3d 1169. The question is Officer Johnson's individual qualified immunity on the substantive-due-process claim arising from his alleged conduct and assurances before Pittman's attack.

On March 10, 1989, Johnson responded after Pittman broke into Pinder's home, assaulted her, threw objects and threatened her and her children. Johnson was told of prior threats and an earlier attempt to burn the house. After arresting Pittman, Johnson assured Pinder that Pittman would remain detained overnight. Pinder alleges that this induced her to return to work instead of staying with or arranging protection for her children. Johnson charged trespass and malicious destruction of property under $300. Commissioner Ames released Pittman that evening; Pittman returned, set the house on fire and killed Pinder's three children. No warning of release is alleged.

The district court denied dismissal and immunity. A divided panel affirmed, but its opinion was vacated on rehearing. The en banc court granted immunity, 9–4 in judgment. Its principal opinion classified the account as nonprotection and found inadequate notice while recognizing a different affirmative-danger paradigm; the supporting judges did not all adopt the same reasoning. The competing view treated the assurance-induced withdrawal of private protection as affirmative causation. Review concerns those legal characterizations on proper favorable assumptions, not a finding that Pinder proved the alleged causal chain.

All nine Justices participated in the decision.

#### Judgment

**Affirmed, 5–4.**

**Supporting:** Chief Justice Alex-Lamar Stone-Zsela and Justices O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy and Thomas.

**Opposing:** Justices Stevens, Souter, Ginsburg and Breyer, who would vacate the reviewed immunity disposition and remand on a bounded affirmative-danger theory.

Johnson receives judgment on the individual damages claim within this immunity issue. The Court does not decide that the assumed affirmative conduct was constitutional or that custody is indispensable to every possible affirmative-deprivation claim.

#### Opinion topology

| Writing | Author | Joined by | Relationship to judgment | Scope joined |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Opinion of the Court, Parts I–II | O'Connor | Stone-Zsela, Stevens, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg, Breyer | Unanimous common grounds | All nine adopt legal-review authority and the common constitutional and notice limits. |
| Opinion of the Court, Part III | O'Connor | Stone-Zsela, Scalia, Kennedy, Thomas | Supports affirmance | Five adopt the sufficient notice-only ground. |
| Dissent from the judgment | Ginsburg | Stevens, Souter, Breyer | Would vacate and remand | Joins Parts I–II; adopts a bounded affirmative-deprivation and preconduct-notice conclusion. |

#### Controlling holdings

##### I. Review reaches the legal immunity issue on favorable assumptions

**Holding and authority.** Part I, joined by all nine Justices, holds that §1254(1) review and the underlying §1291 legal-immunity appeal permit determination of the separable legal question on the properly assumed favorable account. They do not permit deciding factual sufficiency, selecting disputed intent or causation, or adjudicating the separate excluded claims.

**Controlling explanation.** *Mitchell* permits appeal of the separable legal immunity issue. *Johnson v. Jones* and *Kimberlin* distinguish that question from whether the plaintiff can prove the facts. *Doe* and *Behrens* preserve the same boundary: an official may obtain the legal consequences of the favorable account, but cannot obtain appellate findings that the alleged warnings, contribution or causal effect did not occur.

The district court expressly used Rule 12(b)(6); the appellate opinions describe summary judgment. Neither description authorizes an adverse factual determination here. The asserted assurance-induced withdrawal of private protection can be assumed for legal review without deciding credibility, knowing falsity or whether that withdrawal actually caused the deaths. No second pretrial immunity appeal is established, and no ruling on successive-appeal conditions is necessary.

Official-capacity or municipal claims, equal protection, §1985 and state tort counts do not enter this appeal merely because they concern the same events. *Behrens* preserves their distinct posture. No new procedural-due-process claim is supplied. The legal question can therefore be decided without resolving factual disputes or extending review to every claim.

##### II. Private nonprotection, affirmative deprivation and historical notice remain distinct

**Holding and authority.** Part II of the same unanimous opinion holds that knowledge of danger, promises of protection and custody of Pittman create no custodial special relationship with Pinder or her children. The alleged affirmative loss of otherwise available private protection must be considered separately from a failure to supply public protection. Individual immunity requires particularized objective notice from all relevant preconduct law, without an identical-case requirement. Neither judicial disagreement nor the official's subjective belief in legality independently decides that inquiry.

**Controlling explanation.** *DeShaney* generally rejects a due-process duty to protect against private violence. Its custodial rule arises from state restraint of the injured person's ability to act, not knowledge, assistance or a promise. It also emphasized that the State had neither created the danger nor increased Joshua's vulnerability; it did not announce a complete test for every affirmative-danger claim. Johnson's alleged alteration of available private protection therefore cannot be discarded simply by identifying Pittman as the assailant.

That distinction does not establish liability. Section 1983 supplies a remedy for an attributable federal deprivation, not a general constitutional duty to perform state protective functions. *Daniels* excludes merely negligent injury. Culpability must concern the deprivation, not merely the intentional utterance of an assurance; its reservation concerning recklessness and gross negligence does not establish that any nonnegligent reassurance is unconstitutional.

*Anderson* requires unlawfulness sufficiently apparent in pre-existing law at the appropriate level of specificity. The exact act need not already have been held unlawful. *Elder* requires the entire relevant preconduct landscape, including adverse decisions and their limits. A subjective element of the underlying claim differs from the official's belief about legality; *Kimberlin* permits competent circumstantial proof without inventing a substantive mental-state requirement.

March 10, 1989 fixes notice. *Collins*, *Doe*, *Hodge*, *Love* and *Sheets* inform present analysis but cannot supply notice retroactively. *Love* permits a dispositive notice inquiry without mandating either merits-first or immunity-first sequencing. These common principles do not decide whether the affirmative-danger theory succeeds on its constitutional merits or whether its particular wrong was sufficiently established then.

##### III. Contemporaneous law did not sufficiently establish this particular obligation

**Holding and authority.** Part III, adopted by the five Justices joining that part, holds that Johnson has individual qualified immunity on the reviewed claim. Even accepting the alleged assurance-induced withdrawal of private protection for legal review, pre-March 10, 1989 authorities did not make sufficiently apparent that this noncoercive undertaking, ensuing release and uncorrected exposure constituted his own constitutional deprivation. The Court reserves the distinct affirmative-danger merits.

**Controlling explanation.** The alleged loss of private protection is consequential and more specific than a general failure to prevent crime. The question remains whether existing law sufficiently identified the combination of assurances, charging decisions, third-party release and reliance as this officer's constitutional wrong. *DeShaney* had rejected knowledge-and-promise protective duties sixteen days earlier, leaving affirmative increases in danger incompletely defined.

*Bowers* distinguished an active state tortfeasor but rejected liability for releasing a dangerous person. *Archie* discussed increased risk and interference with private aid, yet found neither culpable deprivation nor state creation of danger or hindrance of aid. Its discussion of intentional deprivation and extreme indifference to known mortal risk cannot be detached from those limits. *Jensen*'s distinct danger discussion remains relevant, but its broader knowledge-and-promise relationship ground cannot survive *DeShaney*. The adverse outcomes do not erase these cases' reasoning; neither does general recognition of affirmative injury settle this noncoercive reliance mechanism.

The contrary argument identifies the qualifying wrong as culpable displacement of otherwise available protection against a known immediate mortal danger. Even on that favorable legal account, the extension from earlier affirmative-injury and private-aid principles to this undertaking was not sufficiently apparent. The uncertainty concerns constitutional attribution, not a requirement that an earlier case reproduce every fact or award a plaintiff relief. Judicial disagreement is not itself the test.

As *Hodge* and *Doe*'s Caplinger holding illustrate in their own settings, an individual notice judgment can leave constitutional sufficiency unresolved. Those later decisions provide no retrospective notice here. The conclusion rests neither on finding negligence nor on rejecting alleged reliance. Greater charges are not established to have ensured detention, and unlawful preventive confinement is not the prescribed solution. No freestanding warning entitlement, endorsement of affirmative injury, general custody prerequisite or new actual-reliance immunity defense is adopted.

#### Precedent treatment

- *Mitchell v. Forsyth*, 472 U.S. 511, supplies the separable legal-immunity collateral-order channel. It does not bring factual sufficiency or all related claims into review.
- *Johnson v. Jones* and *Kimberlin v. Quinlan*, June 12, 1995, supply the legal/factual appellate boundary. *Kimberlin* preserves circumstantial proof subject to actual constitutional elements and ordinary burdens.
- *DeShaney v. Winnebago County Department of Social Services*, 489 U.S. 189, limits private-protection and custodial duties while preserving the distinction concerning state-created or increased vulnerability. Its foster-care, culpability, individual-immunity and municipal-responsibility reservations remain unresolved.
- *Daniels v. Williams*, 474 U.S. 327, excludes mere negligence. Its reservation concerning recklessness and gross negligence remains open.
- *Anderson v. Creighton*, 483 U.S. 635, requires particularized apparent unlawfulness without an identical prior case.
- *Elder v. Holloway*, February 23, 1994, requires independent review of all relevant preconduct authority on the preserved question. It supplies no retrospective notice or mandatory merits-first sequence.
- *Collins v. Harker Heights*, February 26, 1992, preserves the distinction between a federal deprivation and state duties or municipal attribution. Its reservation of a different affirmative-danger claim remains.
- *Doe v. Taylor Independent School District*, October 16, 1995, supplies the review boundary and preserves its distinct personal-causation and Caplinger notice holdings. Official-authority abuse is not treated as the same as this private attack.
- *Hodge v. Jones*, October 23, 1995, supplies the method of individualized notice adjudication with constitutional merits reserved. Its confidential-record claim does not become a danger-creation rule.
- *Love v. Pepersack*, December 11, 1995, preserves lawful sequencing and the distinction between state illegality and a federal wrong. No mandatory order of decision or general merits test is inferred.
- *Behrens v. Pelletier*, February 21, 1996, supplies favorable legal-review assumptions and separate-claim limits. No second-appeal premise is supplied.
- *Sheets v. Salt Lake County*, April 29, 1996, retains its confined affirmative-disclosure rule. It neither defines this danger theory nor supplies March 1989 notice.

*Bowers v. DeVito*, *Archie v. City of Racine* and *Jensen v. Conrad* are considered as preconduct circuit authority with their adverse outcomes and limited affirmative-danger discussions intact. They are neither treated as Supreme Court holdings nor overruled. No Supreme Court precedent is overruled; *Albright*'s separate positions do not become a general bodily-integrity bar or state-tort cure.

#### Law after the decision

Effective May 21, 1996, Johnson is individually immune on the reviewed due-process claim because the particular constitutional attribution was insufficiently established on March 10, 1989. The ruling applies existing objective notice law. It creates no identical-facts prerequisite and adopts no defense requiring actual reliance on authoritative law.

The outer substantive boundary of affirmative state-created danger remains unresolved. A private assailant does not categorically exclude an attributable affirmative official deprivation, but neither promises, serious injury nor intentionally speaking establishes one. The decision announces no minimum culpability standard for every lesser account, general warning duty, mandatory detention rule or expanded remedial entitlement. Constitutional merits, personal causation, historical notice and appellate authority retain their separate functions.

#### Dissent

Justice Ginsburg's dissent would vacate the reviewed immunity disposition and remand. Injured-party custody is not indispensable where an official's own sufficiently culpable conduct actually removes otherwise available protection and thereby exposes the victims to a known immediate mortal danger. The dissent concludes now that pre-March 10, 1989 law sufficiently identified the qualifying form of this alleged deprivation to defeat immunity disposing of the entire theory at this stage.

*Bowers*'s active-tortfeasor distinction, *Archie*'s private-aid reasoning and *Jensen*'s distinct danger discussion, read with *DeShaney*'s no-increased-vulnerability limit, supply that particular principle. Their adverse outcomes and culpability restrictions remain material. Purposefully giving reassurance alone is insufficient: culpability must attach to the deprivation, and actual causal displacement must be shown. The dissent neither restores a knowledge-and-promise special relationship nor infers notice from the deaths' severity. It disagrees with the en banc application to this account, not with an invented universal custody rule.

The favorable pleading possibility includes intentional or conscience-shocking deprivation without establishing that mental state. The dissent selects no minimum standard for every lesser account, finds no knowing falsity or favoritism, and does not assume greater charges would have prevented release. A later factual finding cannot create historical legal notice. Materially different or less culpable conduct retains its own constitutional and immunity analysis.

The requested remand would proceed through the Court of Appeals under ordinary preservation, pleading and proof rules, addressing culpability, actual causal predicates and genuinely available defenses only as needed. It would not unconditionally restore the entire district order or vacated panel opinion, mandate discovery, a hearing or trial, award damages, or adjudicate excluded counts. This bounded merits-and-notice position is noncontrolling.

### Ornelas v. United States, No. 95-5257

Merits decision, May 28, 1996

517 U.S. 690. October Term 1995. On certiorari under 28 U.S.C. §1254(1), following federal appeals under §1291, reviewing the Seventh Circuit's decisions at 16 F.3d 714 and 52 F.3d 328 (table). The question concerns appellate review of ultimate reasonable suspicion for a stop and probable cause for a nonwarrant search, preserved through conditional guilty pleas.

On December 11, 1992, detectives investigated an older two-door General Motors car with California plates, motel and travel circumstances, and two NADDIS entries thought related to the men. The entries were not established to identify petitioners. Detectives approached as petitioners entered the car, obtained identification and consent, and eventually removed a vent cover and panel, recovering approximately two kilograms of cocaine. The officer's gun was not visible. The Government conceded that petitioners could challenge the search and that a reasonable person would not have felt free to leave. It did not contend that the stop or consent independently authorized dismantling.

The first appellate opinion accepted reasonable suspicion under clear-error review, held inevitable discovery waived and remanded the loose-panel credibility question. On the stipulated existing record, the magistrate credited the loose-panel testimony, retained the adverse assessment of testimony about rust indicating recent removal, and found the scratches inconclusive. He recommended suppression. The district judge accepted the loose-panel credibility finding but found probable cause; the second appellate order affirmed for want of clear error. The remaining disagreement is not an unresolved conflict over that credited observation.

Both petitioners and the United States seek independent ultimate review and appellate remand, while disagreeing about suppression. Amici defend deferential review. The separate database-discovery claim and initial determination by this Court of the stop/search merits are outside the question presented. All nine Justices participated in the decision.

#### Judgment

**Vacated and remanded, 8–1.**

**Supporting:** Chief Justice Alex-Lamar Stone-Zsela and Justices Stevens, O'Connor, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer.

**Opposing:** Justice Scalia, who would affirm the reviewed allocation under deferential ultimate review.

Both reviewed Court of Appeals judgments, 16 F.3d 714 and 52 F.3d 328, are vacated. The case is remanded to that court for independent review of both ultimate constitutional determinations, with clear-error review of historical findings and genuine due weight for grounded contextual inferences. The Court does not decide whether the existing suppression result ultimately changes.

#### Opinion topology

| Writing | Author | Joined by | Relationship to judgment | Scope joined |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Opinion of the Court, Part I | O'Connor | Stone-Zsela, Stevens, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg, Breyer | Unanimous threshold ruling | All nine adopt review authority and the preserved question's scope. |
| Opinion of the Court, Parts II–III | O'Connor | Stone-Zsela, Stevens, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg, Breyer | Supports vacatur of both appellate judgments and remand | Eight adopt independent ultimate review, factual protection and genuine due weight. |
| Dissent from the judgment | Scalia | Unjoined | Would affirm the reviewed allocation | Joins Part I; favors deferential ultimate application with independent correction of legal errors. |

#### Controlling holdings

##### I. Review includes both preserved ultimate thresholds

**Holding and authority.** Part I, joined by all nine Justices, holds that §§1291 and 1254(1), following the supported conditional pleas, permit review of the appellate standard for both ultimate reasonable suspicion and probable cause. The parties' agreement does not determine that standard. Neither the separate database-discovery claim nor initial Supreme Court resolution of suppression's merits enters this review.

**Controlling explanation.** The first appellate decision applied clear-error review to reasonable suspicion; the second sustained probable cause under the same formulation after the limited remand. The independent-review contention remained preserved in both appeals. Considering only the final probable-cause discussion would leave the earlier preserved threshold governed by the contested allocation.

Rule 11(a)(2) permits a conditional plea with court approval and Government consent, reserving in writing review of a specified adverse pretrial determination. The selected orders and docket entries establish the suppression reservation here; incomplete copies of the full agreements create neither an additional reservation nor a finding of noncompliance. The Government's concession concerning petitioners' ability to challenge this search resolves no other issue by implication.

Although the parties agree on independent review, suppression remains contested and legal classification is the Court's responsibility. The Court decides the review standard and returns its application to the Court of Appeals. No database-disclosure ruling, new waiver or ultimate Fourth Amendment result follows from accepting this channel.

##### II. Ultimate constitutional sufficiency receives independent review, with factual protection and genuine due weight

**Holding and authority.** Part II, adopted by the eight Justices joining Parts II–III, holds that appellate courts independently decide whether established circumstances, objectively considered in their totality, supply reasonable suspicion for the stop and probable cause for the nonwarrant search. No finding of clear error in the ultimate conclusion is required. Historical facts, credibility and genuinely factual subsidiary findings or inferences receive clear-error review. Grounded contextual inferences of resident judges and experienced local officers receive genuine due weight without controlling the ultimate constitutional judgment.

**Controlling explanation.** Reasonable suspicion and probable cause demand different degrees of justification, but both apply a national constitutional boundary to established circumstances. *Brinegar*, *Gates*, *Sokolow* and *White* preserve practical, objective totality analysis rather than mechanical formulas. That practical character does not determine who independently decides constitutional sufficiency.

*Miller*'s functional approach and *Thompson*'s treatment of ultimate custody distinguish historical findings from constitutional application without making every mixed question independently reviewable. *First Options* preserves ordinary legal/factual review but does not itself classify these questions. Applications give constitutional standards practical content; appellate comparison supplies guidance and error correction even when a particular combination yields little general precedent. Ultimate deference could give materially similar established conduct different constitutional treatment merely because different trial conclusions survived review.

The competing argument properly emphasizes trial experience, local conditions and fact-specific probabilities. Those advantages remain protected. What happened, physical conditions and credibility receive clear-error review. Courts also give genuine due weight to grounded inferences from resident judges' local knowledge and officers' experience, including supported trial assessments of credibility and inferential reasonableness. This is real contextual deference, not merely a direction to notice and disregard the assessment. But experience cannot establish an unproved observation or create a presumption that a search was lawful. Ultimate constitutional sufficiency remains independently decided.

*Gates*'s substantial-basis review of an issuing magistrate's warrant decision serves the preference for advance judicial authorization. That relationship differs from appellate review of suppression after a nonwarrant search; its deference is not transplanted here. *Pierce* and *Cooter & Gell* show that other tasks can justify ultimate deference, not that all concrete applications share one allocation. *Salve Regina*'s appellate advantages likewise require issue-specific analysis. The Court adopts no numerical weight, police-credibility preference, new evidentiary burden or intermediate standard insulating the ultimate conclusion.

##### III. Both appellate judgments require reconsideration under that allocation

**Holding and authority.** Part III of the same eight-Justice opinion vacates both reviewed appellate judgments so the Court of Appeals can independently review reasonable suspicion and probable cause on the established record, with Part II's qualifications. Remand neither changes accepted historical findings nor supplies an alternative search justification, additional evidentiary proceeding or immediate plea-withdrawal entitlement.

**Controlling explanation.** Whether the panel was loose is a historical finding; its significance to an experienced officer deserves due weight; whether that fact and the remaining established circumstances satisfy the constitutional threshold is independently decided. The district judge accepted the remand credibility finding. No new hearing is needed to resolve an invented conflict about it. Neither rejected rust testimony nor inconclusive scratches becomes additional support by invoking experience. Nor does due weight establish that the NADDIS entries identified petitioners.

Section 636(b)(1)(B) governs a magistrate's suppression recommendation, followed by the district judge's de novo determination of specifically objected-to portions. The judge may accept, reject or modify, receive evidence or recommit. *Raddatz* distinguishes that determination from an invariably required fresh hearing and reserves the serious question of rejecting dispositive credibility findings without hearing witnesses. Acceptance of the credited loose-panel finding does not present that question. The nondispositive review provision in §636(b)(1)(A) supplies no different final suppression standard, and neither provision fixes appellate review.

The Court assumes the circuit's limitation on the consent given; it announces no universal consent rule. Existing consent supplies no ground for dismantling, and inevitable discovery was waived. Neither may be revived as an alternative affirmance ground. The database-discovery issue remains excluded.

Rule 11(a)(2) permits withdrawal when a defendant ultimately prevails on the preserved issue. Obtaining a new appellate standard and remand alone does not establish that condition. The Court orders no suppression, release, acquittal, new trial, inspection or additional evidentiary hearing. Proper challenges to historical findings remain governed by ordinary clear-error review and preservation, not new disputes created by this mandate.

#### Precedent treatment

- *Miller v. Fenton*, 474 U.S. 104, supplies a functional allocation between historical findings and ultimate constitutional application. Its own statutory setting is not transplanted.
- *Thompson v. Keohane*, November 29, 1995, supplies an issue-specific method, not a universal mixed-question rule or its §2254(d) factual presumption and burden.
- *First Options of Chicago, Inc. v. Kaplan*, May 22, 1995, preserves ordinary independent legal and clear-error factual review. Its arbitration holding does not itself classify suspicion or probable cause.
- *Illinois v. Gates*, 462 U.S. 213, preserves practical totality analysis and warrant-issuance substantial-basis review. That reviewing relationship differs from this nonwarrant appellate inquiry.
- *Brinegar v. United States*, 338 U.S. 160, preserves probable cause's practical-probability character without making it conclusive on appellate allocation.
- *United States v. Sokolow*, 490 U.S. 1, preserves objective totality assessment of reasonable suspicion. Its substantive application supplies no previously settled answer to this review question.
- *Alabama v. White*, 496 U.S. 325, preserves the distinction between reasonable suspicion and the more demanding probable-cause showing without requiring different appellate standards.
- *Pierce v. Underwood*, 487 U.S. 552, concerns the distinct EAJA substantial-justification task. Comparative advantage and limited generalizability remain relevant functional considerations.
- *Cooter & Gell v. Hartmarx Corp.*, 496 U.S. 384, concerns a distinct litigation-management allocation while preserving correction of erroneous law and clearly erroneous factual assessment.
- *Salve Regina College v. Russell*, 499 U.S. 225, supports attention to appellate legal responsibility without making every mixed question independently reviewed.
- *United States v. Raddatz*, 447 U.S. 667, preserves the distinction between a district determination and a fresh hearing, and its credibility-rejection reservation. Accepted credibility findings do not trigger that reservation here.

*Stansbury*'s objective custody rule remains within its substantive *Miranda* setting. *Markman*'s trial-allocation holding leaves appellate subsidiary findings open. *Sheets* and *Pinder* do not authorize factual redetermination or transfer civil pleading assumptions into these suppression proceedings. No precedent is overruled.

#### Law after the decision

Effective May 28, 1996, ultimate reasonable suspicion and probable cause in this nonwarrant appellate setting receive independent review, while historical findings receive clear-error review and grounded contextual inferences receive genuine due weight. The lower court called its ultimate review clear error. Abuse of discretion more precisely describes that rejected deferential mixed-question alternative; clear error remains the factual-review term. Neither label creates an intermediate rule replacing independent ultimate judgment.

The allocation also governs otherwise authorized Government suppression appeals, without expanding their availability. This case proceeds through defendants' appeals, not that Government channel.

No habeas factual presumption, clear-and-convincing burden or civil Rule 52 command governs this direct criminal appeal. The ruling does not decide suppression's merits, create facts, restore waived inevitable discovery, enlarge consent or alter Rule 11(a)(2)'s condition for plea withdrawal.

Magistrate referral does not eliminate the district judge's institutional responsibility or power to supplement the record.

He would preserve correction of legal errors and clear factual mistakes without independently repeating each final probability estimate. Deference would run to the district court, not a presumption that officers were correct. His response to the majority's warrant-incentive rationale also invokes *Leon*'s distinct treatment of searches under warrants; it supplies no alternative good-faith holding on this search.

This proposed ultimate deference is noncontrolling. The dissent does not decide suppression in the first instance, reopen inevitable discovery, resolve database disclosure or supply an immunity-style reasonable-error defense.

### Auciello Iron Works, Inc. v. NLRB, No. 95-668

Merits decision, June 3, 1996

#### Chronology and Posture

Argued April 22, 1996; reported at 517 U.S. 781. On certiorari under 28 U.S.C. §1254(1) to the First Circuit, 60 F.3d 24, which enforced the National Labor Relations Board's order under §10(e) of the National Labor Relations Act. The question is whether the Board may bar an employer from repudiating an accepted bargaining agreement on good-faith majority-status doubts based on information known before acceptance, despite its asserted need for a reasonable assessment period.

The Board certified Shopmen's Local No. 501 in 1977. The latest agreement expired September 25, 1988, and employees struck October 14 while bargaining continued. Auciello made a proposal on November 17 and left it open. On November 27, the Union telegraphed its negotiating committee's acceptance of the entire proposal and ended the strike. The following day, Auciello denied that a contract had formed, asserted doubt about the Union's continued majority, and refused further bargaining or signature.

The Board found that the offer remained open and membership ratification was unnecessary. The First Circuit sustained those findings in 980 F.2d 804 but remanded for an explanation of the refusal to consider the doubt defense. The Board's supplemental decision, 317 N.L.R.B. 364, explained the acceptance cutoff, and the First Circuit enforced the order in full.

Auciello relied on asserted picket-line crossings, union resignations, employee statements and job changes, emphasizing information received during the Thanksgiving-shortened week. The Board found the relevant information was known while the offer remained outstanding. Neither the Board nor the court found sufficient objective doubt or actual minority status, and the Board did not concede sufficiency. Union membership, strike participation and preference for representation are distinct.

#### Judgment

**Affirmed, 9–0.**

**Supporting:** Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg, Breyer.

The First Circuit's judgment enforcing the Board's order in its entirety stands. On the Union's request, Auciello must put the accepted agreement in writing, sign and effectuate it retroactive to November 27, 1988. The existing make-whole and cease-and-desist requirements remain enforceable.

#### Opinion of the Court

Justice Ginsburg delivers the opinion of the Court, Parts I–IV, joined throughout by Chief Justice Alex-Lamar Stone-Zsela and Justices Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas and Breyer. All nine directly adopt each corresponding holding, explanation and qualification below and the existing remedy. No separate writing is filed.

#### Controlling Propositions and Authority

##### I. The statute leaves this timing question open

At Chevron's first step, the Court holds that the Act does not unambiguously entitle an employer to leave an offer open and then repudiate the accepted agreement on previously known, unasserted good-faith majority-status doubt. This administrative timing question remains for a permissible Board choice. Section 10(e) bars the separate actual-minority objection because it was not urged before the Board and no extraordinary circumstances excuse that omission.

Sections 7 and 9 protect employee choice and majority representation. Sections 8(a)(1), (2) and (5) prohibit interference, unlawful employer support and refusal to bargain, but do not establish the claimed postacceptance grace period. Section 8(d) requires good-faith bargaining and, on request, execution of an agreement reached; it compels neither assent to an unaccepted proposal nor a concession.

MCI, Lechmere, Town & Country and Holly Farms require independent examination of those statutory boundaries before deference. Auciello substantially argues that acceptance alone cannot give a union authority when employee-choice conditions prohibit recognition. But good-faith doubt differs from actual loss of majority. This established incumbent retained a rebuttable presumption after expiration. Brooks and Fall River allow continuity and orderly challenge without fresh numerical proof for every bargaining act. Neither that presumption nor silence proves actual support, and the 1977 certification creates no new conclusive year in 1988.

Garment Workers addressed initial recognition of an actually minority union. Its employee-choice and §8(a)(2) limits survive, but that factual premise cannot be assumed here. The settled open-offer and ratification findings follow the Board's formation criteria, which need not coincide with general contract law; they establish neither majority support nor an independent answer to the statutory objection.

Preserving doubt did not preserve actual-minority merits. Holly Farms applies §10(e)'s extraordinary-circumstances qualification, but none excuses this omission. The Board separately reserves postacceptance actual-minority claims resting on previously known events. The Court decides neither that hypothetical defense nor whether parties both aware of actual minority may continue bargaining.

##### II. Acceptance is a permissible cutoff for this known-information defense

At Chevron's second step, the Court holds that the Board may ordinarily require an employer to act on majority-status doubt based on previously known information before the Union accepts its outstanding offer. The explained stability policy is reasonable and consistent with the Act, subject to Part III's safeguards. This is not a finding that Auciello lacked sufficient doubt or acted in bad faith.

An accepted agreement would remain uncertain if the employer could invoke previously uncommunicated doubt whenever management finished assessing it or disliked the agreement's consequences. The Board may use acceptance to prevent that unilateral leverage and post hoc litigation. These are prospective policy risks, not findings that Auciello strategically concealed information or that employees supported the Union.

Auciello's concern about premature action on insufficient doubt and time to assess reports does not require leaving an accepted agreement defeasible. The employer controls its outstanding offer and may use legally available routes before acceptance: seek an election, assert legally sufficient doubt in defense of a refusal-to-bargain charge, or continue good-faith bargaining while investigating. Refusal may produce a charge whose defense must satisfy applicable requirements. Investigation does not suspend bargaining; an election is neither automatically available nor ordered.

Discussion of withdrawing the offer assumes without deciding that withdrawal here would not itself support an unfair-labor-practice charge implicating the blocking-charge rule. It gives no categorical immunity for withdrawing an offer or recognition. The cutoff does not depend on treating that assumption as an adjudicated right.

Fall River and Curtin Matheson support reasonable policy within the Act. Curtin Matheson's striker-replacement rule establishes neither this cutoff nor sufficient doubt here. Its distinction between policy and evidence matters: the supplemental decision explains policy rather than purporting to find employee sentiment. ABF Freight preserves statutory connection, explanation and arbitrariness review, without a majority-status rule. Health Care and Holly Farms require actual predicates and agency reasons. No substitute judicial rationale or explanatory remand is needed. The statute does not compel this policy for every future Board.

##### III. Presumptions and challenge routes retain their qualifications

The acceptance cutoff preserves the distinct certification-year, contract-term and postexpiration presumptions, recognized unusual-circumstances qualifications, and lawful representation procedures. The Board separately reserves case-by-case consideration of new doubt-producing information first received virtually simultaneously with acceptance. Later assessment of earlier-known information is different.

Ordinary certification protection lasts one year; ordinary contract protection lasts the term, up to three years. Illustrative unusual circumstances include dissolution or defunctness of a certified union, or a schism in which substantially all members and officers transfer affiliation to another local or international. The examples are not exhaustive. No dissolution or schism claim is adjudicated here, and advocacy that none occurred adds no finding.

After protection ends, actual nonmajority requires its own numerical showing, while sufficient objective good-faith doubt may rest on circumstantial evidence under the received framework. Neither lawfully available route changes. The Court does not decide whether the Board could abolish the doubt defense generally.

The petition window for representation or decertification is separate. For agreements of three years or less, the stated ordinary window is more than 60 but less than 90 days before expiration. Healthcare and seasonal operations receive different treatment; no substitute window is decided here. Permission to petition does not authorize unilateral refusal to bargain during a contract. No petition or employee vote is found here.

Virtually simultaneous information might include employees first presenting signed anti-representation petitions when acceptance arrives. That reservation requires case-specific consideration, not a predetermined hypothetical result. The First Circuit accepted the Board's conclusion that this case falls outside it. The reservation differs from both protected-period unusual circumstances and §10(e)'s excuse for unpreserved objections.

The Board's separate statement that parties aware of actual loss of majority may not continue bargaining concerns another setting; it supplies neither an actual-minority finding nor another holding here. The Court does not foreclose a timely lawful challenge, validate a fraudulently procured agreement, or decide relief for material postacceptance events. Each issue retains its own lawful predicates and procedures.

##### IV. The existing enforcement judgment stands

Because the relevant evidence was known before acceptance and the simultaneous-information reservation does not apply, the lawful timing rule defeats the preserved defense without a decision on evidence sufficiency or a reasonable assessment period. The entire enforcement judgment stands, with its existing requested-execution, retroactive-performance, make-whole and cease-and-desist relief.

The accepted findings establish that the November 17 offer remained open, the negotiating committee could accept without membership ratification, and management already possessed the information later invoked. Management's earlier list and post-telegram review support the timing determination, without establishing actual employee preferences or legally sufficient doubt.

The holiday-week assessment account does not turn earlier-known evidence into virtually simultaneous first receipt. Applying the permissible rule therefore requires no decision whether nine picket-line crossings, thirteen union resignations, asserted disaffection or reported new jobs furnished sufficient objective doubt. Those events do not mean the same thing as opposition to representation, and no actual minority is found.

The Board alternatively argues that Auciello had enough time even under its proposed reasonable-period approach. The Court adopts no calendar ground, fixes no assessment period, and makes no finding that adequate doubt existed by November 18, 21 or 23. The lawful policy ground suffices.

On Union request, Auciello must write, sign and effectuate the accepted agreement retroactively to November 27, 1988, and comply with the existing make-whole and cease-and-desist order. The Court prescribes no payment amount, election, doubt-sufficiency hearing, compliance hearing or new liability determination. Any genuinely remaining implementation issue retains its ordinary lawful scope; the affirmance creates no pending proceeding.

### Loving v. United States, No. 94-1966

Merits decision, June 3, 1996

#### Chronology and Posture

Argued January 9, 1996; reported at 517 U.S. 748. The Court reviews under 28 U.S.C. §1259(1) the judgment of the Court of Appeals for the Armed Forces, 41 M.J. 213, affirming Loving's convictions and sentence. The Army Court of Military Review affirmed at 34 M.J. 956 and denied reconsideration at 34 M.J. 1065. The court below rejected the delegation objection under Curtis and Matthews.

The questions are whether Congress authorized the President to prescribe the challenged military capital factors, whether further express authorization was required, whether the task could constitutionally be assigned, and whether Congress supplied adequate guidance. The United States also advances independent Article II power as an alternative ground. Loving seeks reversal; the United States seeks affirmance.

Loving, an Army private at Fort Hood, was convicted of premeditated murder of Bobby Sharbino, felony murder of Christopher Fay, attempted murder and four robbery specifications. Fay, an active-duty soldier driving a taxi for additional income, was killed on Fort Hood. Sharbino, a retired Army sergeant, was killed in Killeen. Overlapping specifications dismissed as multiplicitous are not additional retained convictions.

On April 3, 1989, the eight-member court-martial panel unanimously imposed death, dishonorable discharge and forfeiture of all pay and allowances. It unanimously found three factors: premeditated murder during robbery under RCM 1004(c)(7)(B), another Article 118 murder in the same case under (c)(7)(J), and actual perpetration of the felony-murder killing under (c)(8).

#### Judgment

**Affirmed, 9–0, on the presented authority challenges.**

**Supporting:** Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg, Breyer.

The reviewed convictions and sentence remain undisturbed against those challenges. The judgment does not resolve unpresented individual-factor or collateral claims.

#### Opinion Topology

| Writing | Author | Joined by | Relationship to judgment | Scope joined |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Opinion of the Court | Kennedy | Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Souter, Ginsburg, Breyer | Supports affirmance | Entire confined statutory and constitutional rationale, including the four holdings below |
| Concurrence | Stevens | Souter, Ginsburg, Breyer | Joins the Court's opinion and judgment | Preserves the substantial open question of a capital service-connection requirement |
| Concurrence | Scalia | O'Connor | Joins the Court's opinion and judgment | Distinguishes assigned executive implementation from transfer of legislative power |
| Concurrence in the judgment | Thomas | No other Justice | Supports affirmance on his own ground | Military-specific basis; joins no part of the Court's rationale |

Kennedy's opinion and its seven joiners directly support every holding and explanation below. These are eight-Justice holdings; Thomas's separate ground is not counted toward them, and no combination of separate rationales is necessary.

#### Governing Statutory and Sentencing Terms

Articles 18 and 56 of the Uniform Code of Military Justice govern the President's punishment-limiting role. Article 118 makes premeditated murder under clause (1), and murder during perpetration or attempted perpetration of burglary, sodomy, rape, robbery or aggravated arson under clause (4), punishable by death or life imprisonment. The intentional-killing or great-bodily-harm offense in clause (2), and the inherently dangerous/wanton-disregard offense in clause (3), are not capital.

Article 18, subject to Article 17, permits general courts-martial to adjudge punishment not forbidden by the Code under presidential limitations, including death only when the Code specifically authorizes it. Its separate law-of-war jurisdiction permits punishment allowed by that law. Under Article 16(1)(B), a judge-alone general court-martial lacks jurisdiction over a death-eligible offense unless it was previously referred as noncapital; this panel trial does not invoke that exception. Article 56 bars punishment exceeding the President's prescribed limits for the offense.

Article 36, in its governing pre-1990 form, permits the President to prescribe pretrial, trial and post-trial procedures, including modes of proof, for courts-martial, military commissions and other military tribunals, and procedures for courts of inquiry. Generally recognized federal criminal-trial principles of law and evidence apply insofar as the President considers practicable. The regulations may neither contradict nor be inconsistent with the Code, must be uniform insofar as practicable, and must be reported to Congress.

Article 106a separately identifies capital espionage categories and aggravators: another qualifying espionage or treason conviction, grave risk of substantial national-security harm, grave risk of another person's death, and another factor prescribed by the President under Article 36. It requires unanimous proof of a statutory factor beyond reasonable doubt and unanimous agreement that aggravation substantially outweighs mitigation. Evidence may come from guilt or sentencing, and mitigation receives broad latitude. That provision does not govern Loving's murders. Article 55 prohibits flogging, branding, marking, tattooing and other cruel or unusual punishment; irons are permitted only for safe custody.

RCM 1004, in its governing pre-1991 form, requires conviction of an expressly capital Manual offense or a separately authorized law-of-war offense, unanimous conviction by all members present when voted, and compliance with the applicable factor and procedure requirements. Written aggravator notice precedes arraignment. Untimeliness does not foreclose later notice and proof unless the accused shows specific prejudice that a continuance or recess cannot cure. At least one listed factor must be proved beyond reasonable doubt and unanimously found with the required notice. All members must separately agree that admissible aggravation, including the eligibility factor, substantially outweighs extenuation and mitigation. Broader sentencing aggravation cannot substitute for eligibility.

The accused has broad mitigation and extenuation latitude. The military judge instructs on applicable factors, procedures and consideration of all mitigation. In closed session, members vote separately by secret written ballot on each instructed factor before voting on sentence; when death is adjudged, the court-martial president announces the factors found. Evidence may come from before or after the guilt findings, or both.

Paragraph (d) excepts Article 106 spying from paragraphs (a)(2), (b), (c) and RCM 1006/1007, supplying a separate sentencing procedure and operation-of-law announcement. Except for Article 106, paragraph (e) permits other RCM 1003 punishments, including life instead of death, subject to specific Manual limits. Death includes dishonorable discharge or dismissal as appropriate; confinement is its necessary incident, not a component of that sentence. These provisions do not make the present murders spying offenses.

RCM 1004(c)(7) applies to Article 118(1), while (c)(8) requires actual perpetration under Article 118(4). Other factor categories retain their own offense limits; no uncharged factor replaces those found here. Executive Order 12460, issued January 24, 1984, made its amendments immediately effective for trials of capital offenses committed on or after that date and directed transmission to Congress under Article 36. The Court determines neither actual transmission nor a reporting violation. These historical versions govern; later amendments do not replace them.

#### Controlling Propositions and Authority

##### Congress authorized these limits on capital murder punishment

Articles 18 and 56 authorize the President to restrict the availability of the death punishment Congress selected for Article 118(1) and (4). Article 36 supplies additional statutory confirmation and procedural authority. Together they authorize the challenged RCM 1004 restrictions, without authorizing new capital offenses, death under Article 118(2) or (3), or punishment exceeding or contradicting Congress's choice.

Congress defined the relevant murders and chose death or life imprisonment as their punishment. Article 18 subjects general courts-martial punishment to presidential limitations and permits death only when the Code specifically authorizes it. Article 56 likewise permits presidential punishment limits. Withholding death from part of an already capital class is a limit even though it is not a numerical maximum.

The challenged factors perform that function; they neither add murder to the capital code nor enlarge its penalty. Article 36 further authorizes implementing procedures, subject to practicability, consistency, qualified uniformity and reporting requirements. Calling a factor procedural cannot itself establish substantive authority, and the Court need not decide whether Article 36 alone would suffice.

Loving's reading confines Articles 18 and 56 too narrowly to maximum quantities. The relevant distinction is between implementing Congress's penalty within an assigned limiting role and replacing Congress's choice. Neal v. United States preserves the separate operation of statutory sentencing law and valid delegated rules and is consistent with that distinction. The delegation authorizes neither civilian crimes nor vague or otherwise unconstitutional capital factors.

##### No additional capital-specific wording is required

The enacted punishment-limiting authority suffices without a further formula expressly naming capital aggravating factors. Article 106a's later express espionage provisions do not withdraw that authority for these murders.

Capital eligibility requires an actual lawful source of authority. Its seriousness does not allow the Court to disregard Congress's enacted language merely because Congress did not use the later terminology of aggravating factors. Articles 18 and 56 directly assign punishment limitations; they do not invite the Executive to fill any omission in criminal law.

Article 106a specifies espionage aggravators and allows an additional presidential factor under Article 36. More explicit treatment of that distinct offense neither repeals the general limiting provisions nor excludes murder from their scope. Existing authorization also did not expire when, on the assumption governing this case, further narrowing became constitutionally necessary. A lawful exercise remains within its terms when a later constitutional requirement makes its use necessary.

The Court therefore rejects an additional express-factor requirement. Executive practice, congressional silence and usefulness cannot replace enacted authority, and the Court does not resolve every criminal-law clear-statement question.

##### The assignment has adequate constitutional boundaries

Congress may assign the President the identification of narrowing factors for military capital sentencing when Congress defines the capital offenses and punishment, supplies intelligible legal boundaries, and confines implementation to the President's assigned military-administration role. Congress's offense selection, death-or-life alternatives, express limiting assignment and consistency restraints adequately bound this delegation, even assuming that criminal and capital delegations require greater specificity than the ordinary standard.

For this authority question, the Court assumes that these military murders require additional eligibility narrowing and that the three factors would adequately narrow if Congress enacted them. That assumption does not determine who may prescribe them. Neither Article I's military rulemaking clause nor the assumed capital requirement categorically requires Congress personally to prescribe each limiting factor.

Mistretta v. United States permits guided sentencing implementation while requiring Congress to retain legislative responsibility; its capital discussion did not decide these presidential factors. Touby v. United States rejects categorical invalidity of criminal rulemaking but leaves open whether criminal delegations require greater specificity. This assignment survives even that assumption: Congress chose the offenses and punishment, assigned a limiting role, and prohibited inconsistent implementation within an existing military system.

The President's executive and Commander-in-Chief responsibilities place the assignment within his actual functions. They reinforce the enacted role without enlarging it. The Court neither transfers Congress's entire legislative responsibility nor exempts the military from structural review. Weiss v. United States and Ryder v. United States retain their distinct enforcement of constitutional adjudicator requirements in military proceedings.

Loving correctly distinguishes the factors' adequacy from authority to assign their selection. But the enacted limits and the President's actual military responsibilities adequately guide this task without another express factor-selection principle. Executive safeguards and panel unanimity could not cure absent congressional authority. The Court establishes no general rule for a newly created actor, an unrelated executive sphere, or wholesale application of civilian capital doctrine to military proceedings. Enacted authority suffices, so independent Article II power remains undecided.

##### The authority challenges require no sentence relief

The statutory-authorization, additional-clear-authorization, categorical-nondelegability and inadequate-guidance objections provide no ground to disturb the judgment below. The Court affirms without harmless-error review, a new sentencing proceeding, or resolution of unpresented factor-specific and collateral claims.

The panel found three factors under an authorized rule: premeditated murder during robbery, another Article 118 murder in the same case, and actual perpetration of the felony-murder killing. The Court sustains the rule's source against the submitted objections. It does not independently adjudicate every factor's validity, the application of every sentencing protection, or all underlying conviction claims.

The capital precedents address distinct questions. Arave v. Creech requires sufficiently narrowed eligibility in its setting; Tuilaepa and Proctor permit relevant individualized selection following unchallenged lawful eligibility. Graham v. Collins and Johnson v. Texas protect effective consideration of supported mitigation. Stringer v. Black, Sochor v. Florida and Espinosa v. Florida require an adequate cure for established weighing error. Tuggle v. Netherland prevents surviving eligibility from disposing of an independent assistance injury. None establishes a delegation defect here, and survival of an aggravator cannot replace correction of a separately established injury.

Congress's offense boundaries constrain the delegation without necessarily satisfying the separate assumed eligibility requirement. The Court finds no authority error; it does not excuse one because the verdict was unanimous or Congress could have enacted identical factors. Article 71's finality and presidential-approval conditions remain independent. This affirmance orders no execution or execution date and supplies no presidential approval.

#### Precedent Treatment and Current Law

- **Mistretta v. United States, 488 U.S. 361:** Its rule for guided sentencing implementation applies; its conditional capital discussion does not independently resolve these factors.
- **Touby v. United States, 500 U.S. 160:** Its approach supports evaluation of this actual criminal delegation while preserving the general question of enhanced specificity. Its drug-scheduling criteria do not become a mandatory checklist for every assignment.
- **Neal v. United States, 516 U.S. 284:** The boundary between statutory punishment and lawful delegated sentencing rules remains; the presidential restrictions operate within Congress's chosen punishment.
- **Weiss v. United States, 510 U.S. 163, and Ryder v. United States, 515 U.S. 177:** Constitutional review of military adjudicator arrangements remains, with each decision's appointment, tenure and remedial limits. Neither creates a military exemption from structural review.
- **Arave v. Creech:** Genuine narrowing and the distinction between a factor's formulation and application remain. **Tuilaepa and Proctor** retain their bounded selection holding after unchallenged eligibility; no broader framework is attributed to their partial joiners.
- **Graham v. Collins and Johnson v. Texas:** Effective consideration of supported independent culpability mitigation remains protected. **Stringer v. Black, Sochor v. Florida and Espinosa v. Florida** retain their distinct established-error and adequate-cure requirements. **Tuggle v. Netherland** retains its independent-assistance-claim rule and unresolved error-effect standard. None permits a harmlessness shortcut here, and those remedies are not transferred to an unestablished error.
- **Solorio v. United States, 483 U.S. 435:** It remains a noncapital jurisdiction decision; the constitutional need for a service connection in capital military offenses remains undecided.

Effective June 3, 1996, the decision establishes a confined military capital-delegation holding. Articles 18 and 56, with Article 36's additional statutory and procedural support, authorize the challenged limits. The enacted boundaries and the President's assigned military responsibilities sustain the delegation without another express aggravator formula or factor-selection principle. No offense, authorized punishment, sentencing safeguard or defect-specific remedy changes.

The decision does not establish that Article 118 alone supplies every necessary eligibility narrowing or that every RCM factor is valid. It leaves undecided whether Article 36 alone suffices, whether criminal delegations generally require enhanced specificity, whether additional narrowing is constitutionally indispensable throughout military capital proceedings, and whether independent Article II power could authorize factors without congressional delegation. It creates no military exception to constitutional review, general criminal-code authority, or permission for vague or otherwise unconstitutional factors. A capital service-connection requirement remains open, not newly imposed. The limited assumptions about narrowing and hypothetical congressional enactment do not establish the constitutionality of every military capital rule or application.

#### Separate Positions Relevant to the Decision

Justice Stevens, joined by Justices Souter, Ginsburg and Breyer, joins the authority analysis while preserving the substantial question whether capital military prosecutions constitutionally require a connection to military service. Solorio's noncapital jurisdiction holding does not resolve that extension. The actual military connections suffice here: Fay was an active-duty soldier killed on Fort Hood, and Sharbino was a retired Army sergeant. The concurrence neither dismisses for want of jurisdiction nor adopts a new eligibility condition. Agreement with this disposition does not decide every military capital offense.

Justice Scalia, joined by Justice O'Connor, distinguishes implementation ancillary to the President's genuine executive and Commander-in-Chief functions from an impermissible transfer of legislative power. The extent of an assignment and absence of constraints can cross that boundary. Congress's actual offense, penalty and limiting provisions keep this task within execution of enacted military law. Scalia's distinction in Mistretta between ancillary rulemaking and lawmaking detached from an existing executive or judicial function explains his agreement. He rejects English Parliament–Crown arrangements as a basis for allocating powers under this Constitution; historical jurisdiction inquiries such as Solorio address a different question. That objection withholds no join from the Court's opinion, which contains no English-history ground, and supplies no independent Article II authority to disregard Congress.

Justice Thomas concurs in the judgment on the distinct constitutional responsibilities of Congress and the President concerning the armed forces. He assumes without deciding that additional aggravators are constitutionally necessary, and adopts neither the Court's use of nonmilitary delegation doctrine nor a general capital-delegation framework. If the added protection were unnecessary, that premise would remove the corresponding complaint; he does not hold the factors unnecessary. His military-specific deference operates within Congress's offense and punishment choices, consistently with the constitutional and statutory scrutiny required by Weiss and Ryder, whose requirements remain binding. He reserves any view of congressional authority to alter separation of powers outside this setting and rejects speculative English-history inferences. His separate sufficient ground supports the judgment alone.

#### Mandate, Remedy, and Stage

Review of the submitted authority challenge is complete, and the Court of Appeals for the Armed Forces' judgment stands. The Court orders no resentencing, sentence recalculation, replacement life sentence, new guilt trial, acquittal, release or execution.

Article 71 independently requires a final judgment as to legality through the applicable military and Supreme Court review routes where review has not been waived or withdrawn. The death part cannot be executed without presidential approval. The President may commute or remit it but may not suspend it. Article 71(c)(2)'s separate waiver-or-withdrawal route concerns dismissal or punitive discharge, and subsection (d)'s suspension power excludes death. The Court neither determines that every execution prerequisite is complete nor directs an execution, an execution date, or a new military sentencing proceeding.

### Quackenbush v. Allstate Insurance Co., No. 95-244

Merits decision, June 3, 1996

#### Chronology and Posture

Argued February 20, 1996, on writ of certiorari to the Ninth Circuit, 47 F.3d 350. California's Insurance Commissioner, acting as liquidator and trustee of the insolvent Mission companies, seeks a declaration of reinsurance obligations and recovery of money due under the contracts. Allstate removed the action on diversity grounds and moved to compel arbitration and stay the litigation. The District Court accepted jurisdiction but remanded under Burford abstention without deciding that motion. The Ninth Circuit vacated the remand and returned the action for further proceedings.

The questions are whether the abstention remand is appealable under §1291 rather than reviewable only by mandamus, and whether abstention permits relinquishing this action for legal damages. The Court resolves both questions without deciding whether the circumstances otherwise satisfy Burford.

#### Judgment

**Affirmed, 9–0.**

**Supporting:** Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg, Breyer.

All nine support appellate review and affirmance of the judgment vacating the abstention remand. The Court does not adopt the Ninth Circuit's broader prohibition of every form of abstention in actions at law.

#### Opinion of the Court

Justice O'Connor delivers the opinion of the Court, joined in full by Chief Justice Stone-Zsela and Justices Stevens, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer. Part I addresses the statutory review restriction and two independently sufficient grounds of appealability; Part II addresses abstention authority, the remedy and its limits. Justice Kennedy and Justice Scalia each file a concurrence, joined by no other Justice. Both join the entire opinion and judgment.

O'Connor's Parts I–II supply nine direct votes for every holding and qualification below. The concurrences establish no controlling exception or alternative holding.

#### Controlling Propositions and Authority

##### The statutory remand-review bar

Section 1447(d), read with §1447(c), does not bar review of this remand because it rests on Burford abstention, not a timely raised removal-procedure defect or lack of subject-matter jurisdiction. A court may identify the stated remand ground, but may not use that classification to review the correctness of a covered ground.

Thermtron and Things Remembered require the review restriction to be read with the grounds specified in §1447(c). The District Court accepted jurisdiction and invoked abstention, placing this order outside the covered category. The Commissioner's continued opposition to review supplies no reason to disregard that construction; the Court attributes no concession to him. It does not decide the separate reviewability of discretionary remands under Carnegie-Mellon University v. Cohill.

A removal-procedure objection must be raised within thirty days after removal; subject-matter jurisdiction is a distinct ground. The then-applicable §1443 exception to §1447(d) is not implicated. The Court creates no exception to the covered-remand bar. Falling outside that bar does not itself establish an appellate route.

##### Complete surrender of the federal forum

When an abstention remand completely disassociates the District Court from the action and conclusively surrenders federal adjudication, §1291 permits an appeal on Moses H. Cone's limited ground that the litigant is effectively out of federal court, provided no statutory review bar applies. This ground independently sustains the appeal.

Moses H. Cone recognizes finality when the federal court effectively surrenders the controversy to a state forum. This remand leaves nothing on the federal docket to adjudicate; it is more than an ordinary delay or revocable case-management stay. The Commissioner's reliance on the usual final-judgment rule overlooks that complete surrender. The remand does not resolve the contract merits and is not final in Catlin's traditional sense. Cone supplies the specific basis for appealability. The Court does not classify every remand or stay as a conventional final judgment, and appealability does not determine whether abstention was lawful.

##### The independent collateral-order ground

This remand independently satisfies Cohen's collateral-order doctrine: it conclusively decides the important question of surrendering statutory federal jurisdiction, separate from the contract merits, and that surrender cannot effectively be reviewed after a later federal final judgment. Thermtron's statement that remands are reviewable only by mandamus is disavowed to the extent inconsistent with this application of the later Moses H. Cone holding.

Each requirement is satisfied. The remand conclusively ends federal adjudication. Whether jurisdiction may be surrendered is separate from Allstate's contractual liability and setoff defenses. The category implicates the public allocation of adjudicative responsibility and federal-state relations. Once the case returns to state court, no later federal final judgment permits review of that surrender.

Digital Equipment requires genuine importance and effective unreviewability. Its ordinary private-settlement category lacks the necessary public importance and independently remains adequately protected by final review despite litigation costs. Here the forum ruling forecloses later federal review. Neither an asserted right to avoid trial nor litigation expense alone supports appeal.

The limited disavowal leaves Thermtron's construction of §§1447(c)–(d) intact. Appeal requires neither §1292(b) certification nor an extraordinary writ; the Court finds no certification or entitlement to mandamus. Section 1292(b) remains a separate route requiring written district certification of a controlling legal question, substantial ground for difference of opinion and possible material advancement of termination, followed by application within ten days and appellate permission. An application alone does not stay proceedings.

##### Burford and independent damages claims

Outright dismissal or remand on abstention grounds requires equitable or otherwise discretionary relief under the governing abstention precedents. Burford does not authorize surrender of these independent coercive contract-damages claims. An otherwise justified stay may postpone damages litigation while retaining federal jurisdiction, but the Court approves or requires no stay here.

Burford and NOPSI recognize bounded discretion to withhold discretionary relief against Congress's assignment of federal jurisdiction. They confer no general power to abandon an obligatory damages action. Merger of legal and equitable procedure did not make legal recovery discretionary, and California's substantial interest in orderly insurance liquidation cannot supply the missing remedial authority.

The complaint includes a declaratory count. The Ninth Circuit declined the Commissioner's distinct declaratory-character theory because he first presented it on rehearing. In any event, the independent contract claim prevents treating the entire action as Wilton's declaration-only suit. Wilton draws discretion from §2201 and Brillhart for a declaration-only action with adequate parallel state proceedings and preserves federal jurisdiction during a stay. Abuse-of-discretion review still corrects legal-source errors and unsupported adequacy findings. The Court leaves open the handling of a genuinely separable declaratory claim.

Allstate's stay request, anticipated setoff defenses and participation in liquidation do not convert the Commissioner's contract recovery into discretionary equitable relief. The state court's asset-control orders coexist with permission for the liquidator to sue elsewhere; they establish no exclusive-jurisdiction ground displacing the District Court's accepted jurisdiction.

A properly supported stay can protect state administrative interests while preserving federal responsibility. The District Court declined that alternative and chose remand. A different, conditional remedy cannot validate the remand or establish that a stay's conditions exist. This narrower remedy ground suffices to affirm; the Court does not adopt a categorical exclusion of abstention in actions at law.

#### Limits and Questions Not Reached

The Court does not decide whether either Burford branch is satisfied. Subject to timely and adequate state review, NOPSI identifies alternative grounds: difficult state-law questions of substantial public importance transcending the immediate result, or federal review disruptive of state efforts to establish coherent policy on a matter of substantial public concern. Those grounds are not cumulative. A complex regulatory scheme or potential conflict alone is insufficient. Prudential, 3 Cal. 4th 1118, and Imperial, 41 Cal. App. 4th 828, 837–840, resolve particular issues without establishing that every setoff dispute is settled. Imperial's settlement ground neither settles this action nor makes it moot. Disputed assertions about the relevance and supersession of Allstate's liquidation proofs of claim are not adjudicated facts.

The damages-surrender holding does not decide abstention in a genuinely equitable action or settle every source-specific abstention doctrine. Fair Assessment's state-tax and §1983 setting supplies no adjudicated general damages-dismissal exception here; its distinct comity rule remains undisturbed. The Court does not decide the unpresented application of Thibodaux or reopen the unchallenged Colorado River ruling. Forum non conveniens rests on a distinct pedigree and considerations, including its surviving application to a foreign alternative forum; this abstention holding does not overrule it.

No lower federal court decided Allstate's Federal Arbitration Act motion. Its substantial federal interest does not establish coverage, assent, scope, enforceability or an arbitration right. A §3 stay requires a written agreement covering a referable issue, the court's satisfaction, a party application, arbitration according to the agreement and no applicant default in proceeding. The Court decides none of those conditions, presumes no default and requires no new default inquiry. Formation, fraud, duress, unconscionability, lack-of-assent and other evenhanded defenses retain their preservation requirements. Actual contract selection, delegation, nonsignatory rights and the interaction with state insurance-liquidation law, including McCarran-Ferguson, remain undecided. The Court neither directs arbitration nor requires it to be ordered first, and mandates no new evidentiary proceeding or abstention hearing.

#### Precedent Treatment and Current Law

- **Thermtron Products, Inc. v. Hermansdorfer, 423 U.S. 336:** Its construction of §§1447(c)–(d) remains controlling; only its mandamus-only language is disavowed to the extent inconsistent with the present application of Cone.
- **Things Remembered, Inc. v. Petrarca, December 5, 1995:** Its covered-ground restriction applies without review of a covered remand's correctness. Its bankruptcy-remand setting and separate Cohill reservation do not decide this Burford appeal.
- **Moses H. Cone Memorial Hospital v. Mercury Construction Corp., 460 U.S. 1:** Both its effective-surrender finality rule and independent collateral-order ground apply to an order completely ending federal adjudication.
- **Cohen v. Beneficial Industrial Loan Corp., 337 U.S. 541:** Its cumulative collateral-order requirements are applied to this conclusive surrender of the federal forum.
- **Catlin v. United States, 324 U.S. 229:** Traditional merits finality remains intact; the distinct appealability grounds here do not make the remand a merits-ending judgment.
- **Digital Equipment Corp. v. Desktop Direct, Inc., June 6, 1994:** Its private-settlement limits remain intact; this category differs in public importance and the availability of final review.
- **Burford v. Sun Oil Co., 319 U.S. 315, and New Orleans Public Service, Inc. v. Council of City of New Orleans, 491 U.S. 350:** Their bounded remedial discretion and actual alternative state-policy predicates remain; neither authorizes the outright damages remand entered here.
- **Wilton v. Seven Falls Co., June 12, 1995, and Brillhart v. Excess Insurance Co., 316 U.S. 491:** Their source-specific discretion over declaration-only relief does not encompass this entire action with its independent coercive claim. Wilton's rule for reviewing a lawful discretionary stay remains unchanged.
- **Ankenbrandt v. Richards, June 15, 1992:** The duty to exercise ordinary diversity damages jurisdiction remains absent an applicable exception or concrete abstention basis; its particular record did not settle every damages-abstention question.
- **Colorado River Water Conservation District v. United States, 424 U.S. 800:** Its distinct conditions and underlying obligation to exercise jurisdiction remain; the ruling rejecting that ground below is not reopened.
- **Fair Assessment in Real Estate Ass'n v. McNary, 454 U.S. 100, and Louisiana Power & Light Co. v. City of Thibodaux, 360 U.S. 25:** Their source-specific settings remain without a decision on their unpresented application here.

Effective June 3, 1996, a conclusive abstention surrender of the federal forum is appealable on the two independent Part I grounds when no statutory review bar applies. The covered-remand bar remains intact. The decision adds a remedial boundary to Burford: independent legal-damages claims may not be dismissed or remanded merely to relinquish federal adjudication, while a retained-jurisdiction stay requires its own justification. It leaves the actual Burford predicates undecided, does not extend declaration-only discretion to an entire mixed action, and preserves distinct source-specific doctrines. There is no newly recognized extraordinary-federalism exception to the damages-surrender limit.

Casarotto's arbitration nondiscrimination rule and preserved ordinary defenses do not adjudicate the pending motion. No arbitration entitlement, insurance-law preemption result, setoff right or damages liability follows from this judgment.

#### Separate Positions Relevant to the Decision

Justice Kennedy joins the Court's opinion in full but would leave open a future extension permitting dismissal of a damages action where a serious affront to federalism could be avoided in no other way. In his view, comity and federalism matter beyond the historic discretion to withhold equitable relief, and California has a substantial interest in administering insurance insolvencies. A stay preserving federal jurisdiction is the preferred accommodation. This case presents no occasion to decide whether extraordinary circumstances could permit dismissal. He recognizes no current exception and makes no finding that either a stay or exceptional dismissal is warranted on this record.

Justice Scalia also joins the entire opinion, understanding its restriction to foreclose Kennedy's suggested discretionary surrender of lawfully imposed nondiscretionary jurisdiction. Congress's lawful conferral of jurisdiction cannot itself be the federalism affront authorizing refusal to exercise it. There is therefore no need to decide whether this record otherwise presents extraordinary circumstances. His response does not reject every retained-jurisdiction stay or disturb independently governed state-tax comity and forum-non-conveniens doctrines. Both concurrences remain noncontrolling.

#### Mandate, Remedy, and Stage

The Ninth Circuit's judgment vacating the District Court's remand is affirmed. The action returns for further federal proceedings under that judgment and the Court's abstention rule. The pending arbitration motion and any properly available stay request receive lawful initial treatment below; no particular result, priority command or new hearing is prescribed. The Court orders no arbitration or stay, awards no contract recovery and directs no distribution of estate assets. Supreme Court merits review is complete, subject to ordinary mandate procedures.

### Smiley v. Citibank (South Dakota), N.A., No. 95-860

Merits decision, June 3, 1996

#### Chronology and Posture

Argued April 24, 1996; reported at 517 U.S. 735. On writ of certiorari to the Supreme Court of California, 11 Cal. 4th 138, 900 P.2d 690, which affirmed judgment on the pleadings for Citibank. The questions concern whether federal law includes contractual credit-card late fees within interest under 12 U.S.C. §85, whether South Dakota instead supplies that federal definition, and the resulting statutory and constitutional objections to applying the home State's allowance outside its borders.

Citibank is a national bank located only in South Dakota. Barbara Smiley, a California resident, challenges its late fees under California Civil Code §1671. That provision invalidates a consumer liquidated-damages term unless the parties agree on presumed damages where fixing actual damages would be impracticable or extremely difficult.

Smiley's Classic Card charged $15 when the minimum payment was more than twenty-five days overdue. Her Preferred Card charged $6 after fifteen days; if payment remained delinquent at thirty days, it added the greater of $15 or 0.65 percent of the current balance, assessed again each succeeding month in which the minimum payment remained more than thirty days overdue. The agreements also imposed monthly finance charges on certain balances, calculated by percentage or a fifty-cent minimum. Those charges are not challenged.

South Dakota's allowance of the agreed charges and Citibank's location there are undisputed. The complaint alleges that the late fees lack a relationship to the balance, amount due, days overdue or processing costs, accompany continuing finance charges, and do not waive contractual default. Those allegations are accepted for this pleading judgment. There are no findings about actual costs, credit-market effects or benefits to other borrowers.

#### Judgment

**Affirmed, 9–0.**

**Supporting:** Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg, Breyer.

The judgment sustaining judgment on the pleadings for Citibank against the challenged California late-fee theory remains in force.

#### Opinion Topology

Justice Souter delivers one opinion of the Court. No separate opinion is filed.

| Writing | Author | Joined by | Relationship to judgment | Scope joined |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Opinion of the Court | Souter | Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Thomas, Ginsburg, Breyer | Supports affirmance | Entire opinion |
| Partial join in the same opinion | Souter | Stone-Zsela | Joins the judgment | Parts I, II, IV and VI; does not join Parts III-A, III-B or V |

Parts I, II, IV and VI have nine votes. Parts III-A, III-B and V have the eight Associate Justices' votes. Every part is an opinion of the Court, with the respective majority directly supporting its holdings, explanations and qualifications below. There is no fractured controlling rationale.

#### Controlling Propositions and Authority

##### I. Federal statutory meaning and the Comptroller's authority

Federal law determines which charges are interest under §85; home-state law then supplies the permitted rate within the statute's terms. Congress did not unambiguously exclude these late-payment charges, and the Comptroller has delegated responsibility supporting authoritative interpretation of that substantive banking obligation.

The Court first identifies the statutory boundary and the agency's actual responsibility. MCI Telecommunications Corp. v. AT&T requires that inquiry before deference. Chevron U.S.A. Inc. v. Natural Resources Defense Council, Inc. then respects a permissible construction within the range Congress left. NationsBank of North Carolina, N.A. v. Variable Annuity Life Insurance Co. applies that sequence to reasoned banking classifications without authority to override enacted conditions.

Sections 93, 1818 and 1813(q) establish enforcement responsibilities concerning national banks' substantive obligations, including §85. Section 93a authorizes rules carrying out the Comptroller's responsibilities except where authority is expressly and exclusively assigned to another agency; its grant does not apply to §36 or national banks' Glass-Steagall securities activities. Enforcement retains its statutory institutional scope, predicates, notice, hearing, findings or consent, review and remedy conditions. This private action enters no enforcement order. Section 86's judicial remedy does not make all §85 administration exclusively judicial. Adams Fruit Co. v. Barrett rejects agency control over a judicial remedy outside its delegation, but therefore does not bar this substantive interpretation.

South Dakota cannot enlarge the federal category by calling an exaction interest. Its permission matters after federal classification. Daggs v. Phoenix National Bank allows a State to permit agreed rates without specifying a numerical maximum; it does not classify every contractual charge as interest.

##### II. The challenged late fees fall within a reasonable interpretation

The Comptroller reasonably includes these contractual charges for late repayment of credit within §85 interest, and that interpretation controls under Chevron's second step. Fixed form, contingency, an incentive for prompt payment, continuing finance charges, or lack of proportionality to balance, time or administrative loss does not categorically exclude them.

Section 85's reference to interest at a rate does not require each charge to be a periodic percentage. Its Federal Reserve percentage alternative does not make that form exclusive. Section 86's twice-paid-interest remedy does not require each component to be calculable when credit is first extended.

Smiley accepts some flat initial charges and default charges measured by time and balance. Her objection that these contingent fees cannot be reckoned at inception receives a distinct answer: Congress imposed no such prerequisite. The contractual contingency makes the charge ascertainable when it occurs. The Office of the Comptroller of the Currency reasonably distinguishes payments for extending credit, making it available or defaulting on its conditions from reimbursement for other services. Each fee need not equal actual loss.

Citizens' National Bank v. Donnell rejects a penalty label as a categorical way to exclude default charges from interest restrictions. Its percentage-overdraft and compounding holding did not decide flat credit-card fees. Meilink v. Unemployment Reserves Commission concerned a statute expressly separating penalties from interest; it does not impose that distinction on §85.

The pleaded allegations do not defeat the permissible classification of either card's charges. The Court adopts no independently sufficient judicial best reading, approves no entire nonexclusive regulatory list, and prescribes no cost study or new fee-reasonableness test. Attorneys' fees, litigation costs, prepayment charges, unrelated services and other breach charges remain undecided. Classification does not validate an undisclosed, unconscionable or contractually unauthorized charge; compatible contract and consumer-protection rules remain.

##### III-A. Interpretive change does not automatically foreclose deference

An interpretation does not lose eligibility for deference merely because it changes or addresses an old statute during litigation. A lawful, adequately explained change within delegated ambiguity may receive deference; unexplained reversal or disregard of legitimate reliance may be arbitrary and capricious. This independently answers the categorical inconsistency objection without validating every policy change.

Good Samaritan Hospital v. Shalala permits reasoned interpretive change subject to lawful authority, statutory fit and adequate explanation. Neither statutory age nor litigation impetus creates a categorical contrary rule. The OCC publicly proposed its interpretation on March 3, 1995, at 60 Fed. Reg. 11924, received 112 comment letters, and explained its consideration of comments and reasons in the final rule published February 9, 1996, at 61 Fed. Reg. 4849, 4850, 4858, 4869–4870.

That instrument differs from an unsupported position invented by litigation counsel. Its stated purpose was to clarify interpretive uncertainty. The Court evaluates the agency's actual explanation without supplying economic findings or an invented reliance rationale. Those procedural facts do not establish compliance with every statutory rulemaking requirement.

##### III-B. The earlier letters established no contrary settled policy

Independently, the cited 1964 and 1988 letters do not establish clear, settled official guidance contrary to the current interpretation. Their existence does not itself make the rule an unexplained reversal of a binding prior position. This is a second, independent answer to the claimed prior-policy barrier.

The 1964 informal statement on consumer automobile financing listed late charges as items not properly characterized as interest. The 1988 deputy-counsel response proceeded on a home-state-definition approach. The letters differ in authority and context and conflict about whether the relevant category is federally or state defined. They do not establish the clear prior interpretation Smiley assumes.

The formal rule's explanation that it clarifies uncertainty supplies a reason for the federal definition. This conclusion concerns the identified documents' legal force, not a borrower's actual reliance. Good Samaritan requires attention to agency reasons without making every informal formulation controlling. Bowen v. Georgetown University Hospital denies automatic weight to unsupported litigation positions. The separately published rule is assessed on its own stated grounds.

##### IV. The federal permission displaces this conflicting California restriction

Federal classification as interest and South Dakota's undisputed permission bring these charges within §85's interstate allowance. California may not prohibit them through the conflicting application of §1671. The holding neither displaces compatible state regulation nor invalidates §1671 generally.

The Court assumes without deciding that courts must always determine statutory preemption independently. The OCC reasonably interprets a substantive federal term; it gains no freestanding preemption power. Marquette National Bank of Minneapolis v. First of Omaha Service Corp. permits a national bank under §85 to charge home-state-allowed interest to out-of-state borrowers. Lending to California customers does not itself relocate this South Dakota bank.

Invoking §1671 to forbid agreed charges unless they meet California's different liquidated-damages restriction withdraws the particular federal permission. A general contract-law label does not remove the conflict, and no empirical finding about burdens on the bank's overall business is needed. Barnett Bank of Marion County, N.A. v. Nelson protects a specifically conferred federal banking permission while retaining its conditions and compatible state law. Cipollone and Gade require attention to statutory scope; neither lets a State negate this federal option merely because the bank could abandon it.

The home-state allowance does not let South Dakota define federal interest, so the Court need not decide an abstract Article I or Article IV objection premised on that supposed delegation. It reaches neither an independent §24 incidental-powers ground nor compliance with California's 1995 credit-card legislation. Contract formation, disclosure, unconscionability and other generally applicable defenses remain available insofar as their application does not conflict with §85.

##### V. Applying the interpretation to antecedent transactions

In determining the statute's meaning for antecedent transactions without clear prior agency guidance, the Court may use the current authoritative interpretation. This does not backdate the rule's April 1, 1996 effective date. The materially different case of a later rule displacing a clear earlier interpretation remains reserved.

The Court determines what §85 means for the transactions before it. As Part III-B establishes, the letters supplied no clear contrary guidance. Part III-A's general allowance for policy changes would not itself establish lawful retrospective use. An objection to applying a new rule to prior conduct could have substance if it displaced a clear earlier interpretation.

Bowen forbids unauthorized retrospective legislative rulemaking; it does not require disregard of the current authoritative statutory interpretation in these circumstances. The rule contains no special transition or retroactivity clause, no settled judgment is reopened, and no factual reliance finding is made. The Court gives no universal permission to change completed conduct's legal consequences and makes no waiver or preservation ruling on this objection.

##### VI. Statutory and procedural limits

The statutory-merits deference ground does not adjudicate every independent challenge to the rule's validity or excuse a demonstrated procedural violation. Part VI preserves the following statutory conditions and limits.

Section 43 covers agency opinion letters or interpretive rules, issued on request or the agency's own motion, concluding that federal law preempts application to a national bank of state community-reinvestment, consumer-protection, fair-lending or intrastate-branch law, and the specified §36(f)(1)(A)(ii) determinations. Subsection (a) requires advance Federal Register notice describing the issue and each state law, at least thirty days for written comments, and consideration of comments received. Subsection (b) separately requires publication of the specified final action.

Its exceptions differ in scope. Subsection (c)(1) excludes the entire section for essentially identical preemption issues previously resolved by courts or addressed in an agency opinion or interpretive rule, or a response to a request containing no significant legal basis for a preemption determination. Subsection (c)(2) excludes the entire section for materials prepared for judicial proceedings, submission to Congress or a Member, or intragovernmental use.

Subsection (c)(3) permits an exception only to subsection (a), on a written necessity determination to avoid a serious and imminent threat to a national bank's safety and soundness, or for an opinion or rule connected with acquiring banks in statutory default or danger of default, or an acquisition receiving FDIC assistance under §1823(c). Final publication remains required. Institutional default requires an official custodial appointment. Danger of default requires the specified agency advice or determination of inability to meet demands or obligations, or depletion of substantially all capital, and no reasonable prospect of recovery without federal assistance. Borrower delinquency establishes none of these conditions.

**Controlling explanation.** A published proposal and comments do not prove every procedural requirement. Interpretive form, later litigation use, §93a authority and absence of clear prior guidance establish neither an exception nor compliance. Distinguishing substantive interpretation from resulting preemption does not make §43 categorically inapplicable. No finding resolves applicability, satisfaction, violation, waiver, preservation or an exception. No independently dispositive procedural defect is established here; missing documentary proof creates no presumption for either side or alternative disposition.

Section 85 retains the allowed home-state rate or one percent above the specified Federal Reserve ninety-day commercial-paper discount rate, whichever is greater and no more, subject to the special state-bank-rate qualification. Where no state rate is fixed, the greater of seven percent or that discount-rate alternative applies, with advance interest reckoned by the instrument's days to run. The foreign-branch local-law maximum, bona fide bill-of-exchange qualification for the current sight-draft exchange rate in addition to interest, and savings limited to historical business or agricultural transactions do not expand permission for these accounts.

Section 86 retains its knowing excess-rate predicate, interest forfeiture, and separate recovery of twice paid interest in an action begun within two years; no such claim or remedy is adjudicated. The regulation's home-state allowance retains material loan-class restrictions, including loan-size restrictions without a required state license. Solely state-law definitions and calculations remain distinct from federal classification. The corporate-borrower provision does not govern Smiley. Non-interest-charge rules remain separate; the Court approves neither every paragraph (c) application nor every listed category. Ordinary exclusions for appraisal, repayment-insurance, finder, document-preparation, notarization and credit-report fees remain ordinary, not absolute.

#### Precedent Treatment and Current Law

- **Chevron** supplies controlling deference within an identified statutory ambiguity and delegation; its two-step framework remains intact.
- **MCI** preserves independent enforcement of enacted boundaries before deference; no banking condition is dispensed with.
- **NationsBank** applies to reasonable classification within actual national-banking responsibility, without general preemption or retroactive-rulemaking power.
- **Adams Fruit** is distinguished because §§93, 1818, 1813(q) and 93a confer relevant substantive administrative responsibility. Agencies still may not redefine exclusively judicial remedies outside their delegation.
- **Good Samaritan** permits reasoned interpretive change while retaining authority, explanation and reliance limits.
- **Bowen** remains controlling against unauthorized retroactive legislative rulemaking and unsupported litigation rationales; Part V's qualified judicial use of current interpretation is distinguished.
- **Daggs** supplies the home-state allowed-rate mechanism, without deciding flat-fee classification.
- **Donnell** rejects a categorical penalty exclusion within the limits of its percentage-overdraft and compounding holding; it is not a flat-fee decision.
- **Meilink** is distinguished by its express statutory separation of penalties and interest.
- **Marquette** supplies interstate application after federal interest classification; bank location and home-state permission remain prerequisites.
- **Barnett Bank** protects the particular federal permission while preserving statutory conditions and compatible state regulation.
- **Cipollone and Gade** retain their statute-specific scope. Neither establishes national-bank field immunity or requires surrender of a federal option to avoid conflict.

The decision adds a controlling §85 application: the OCC's reasonable federal classification encompasses the challenged contractual credit-card late fees and permits their interstate collection under the undisputed home-state allowance despite California's conflicting liquidated-damages restriction. Courts first identify statutory boundaries and delegated responsibility, then apply the permissible agency interpretation. Neither state labels nor banking status alone determines the category.

Both independent answers to the asserted policy-change barrier remain controlling, as does the qualified use of a current interpretation for antecedent transactions without clear prior guidance. The rule's effective date and statutory limits on retroactive legislation remain unchanged. The decision does not settle every agency-procedure objection, validate every fee, displace compatible contract or consumer-protection law, or alter §§85–86's surviving alternatives, predicates and remedies.

#### Mandate, Remedy, and Stage

The California Supreme Court's judgment sustaining judgment on the pleadings stands affirmed. No evidentiary or agency remand, damages, restitution, injunction, class certification, special mandate date or special transition is ordered. No further evidentiary proceedings or agency action is directed, and no related petition or distinct claim is disposed of.

Contract formation, disclosure, unconscionability and other generally applicable defenses remain open to the extent they do not conflict with §85. The Court does not adjudicate a distinct claim asserting them or validate a charge unauthorized by the agreements.

### Degen v. United States, No. 95-173

Merits decision, June 10, 1996

517 U.S. 820. Argued April 22, 1996; October Term 1995. On certiorari under 28 U.S.C. §1254(1) to review the Ninth Circuit's judgment, 47 F.3d 1511, sustaining the District of Nevada's exclusion of Brian Degen's claims and defenses in civil forfeiture. The district ruling is reported at 755 F. Supp. 308. The question is the existence and permissible scope of the court's asserted inherent or supervisory authority to impose that bar because Brian has not returned for a related criminal prosecution.

Brian is a dual United States and Swiss citizen who left before indictment. He disputes an intent to evade prosecution at departure; the Government and lower court rely on his later knowing refusal to return. The extradition treaty does not require a party to surrender its own nationals. The Government seeks forfeiture of real and personal property in California, Nevada and Hawaii as alleged drug proceeds or facilitating property. The court controls that property and can enforce its eventual judgment. Brian timely filed verified claims and answers through counsel, denying substantive forfeiture allegations and raising defenses including limitations, retroactivity and probable cause. Neither indictment nor absence establishes guilt or the merits of the property dispute.

The District Court struck Brian's claim in December 1990. The Ninth Circuit approved the bar, identifying but treating as waived his objection to the District Court's mistaken view that disentitlement was mandatory rather than discretionary. That subsidiary preservation ruling differs from the preserved challenge to the asserted power. Karyn Degen's claims initially survived. Her later failure to oppose a supported renewed summary-judgment motion, after extensions and reopened discovery, produced a separate judgment and affirmance. She did not seek this review.

Swiss authorities arrested Brian in November 1992. The Ninth Circuit refused supplementation with Justice Department correspondence and rejected the inadequately developed contention that Swiss custody ended fugitivity. Here the Government acknowledges the correspondence's authenticity and its request for Swiss prosecution principally concerning the same conduct, correcting its misleading descriptions below. The parties dispute preservation, the consequences of custody, unclean hands and responsibility for the deficient record. These admitted developments establish neither present freedom nor impossibility of return.

The governing forfeiture provisions retain separate coverage and conditions:

- Section 881(a)(6) covers money, negotiable instruments, securities or other things of value furnished or intended for a prohibited controlled-substance exchange, traceable proceeds, and money, negotiable instruments and securities used or intended to facilitate a covered violation. Its facilitating-property clause does not extend to all property.
- Section 881(a)(7) covers real-property interests, including leaseholds, in the whole lot or tract and its appurtenances or improvements, used or intended, in any manner or part, to commit or facilitate a covered offense punishable by more than one year's imprisonment.
- Each paragraph protects the extent of an owner's interest for an act or omission that the owner establishes occurred without that owner's knowledge or consent. The Court determines no claimant's satisfaction of those qualifications.
- Section 881(d) incorporates customs forfeiture procedures insofar as applicable and consistent. Under incorporated §1615, the Government must first show probable cause for instituting the action, to be judged by the court, before the claimant bears the statutory burden. This decision supplies no new probable-cause or property-liability finding.

All nine Justices participated at argument and decision.

#### Judgment

**Reversed and remanded, 9–0, on the reviewed defense-bar issue.**

**Supporting:** Chief Justice Stone-Zsela and Justices Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer.

The Ninth Circuit's judgment is reversed insofar as it sustains Brian's categorical civil-defense bar. Brian must receive a lawful opportunity to contest forfeiture, and the dependent forfeiture result cannot rest on the rejected exclusion. The Court decides no merits defense or separate sufficient ground outside this review.

#### Opinion of the Court

Justice Scalia delivered the Opinion of the Court, joined in full by Chief Justice Stone-Zsela and Justices Stevens, O'Connor, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer. All nine directly adopted Holdings I–III, their explanations and qualifications, including the inherent-power ground, conditional-management limits and reversal/remand. The decision rests on no constitutional alternative holding. No separate opinion is filed.

##### I. The asserted inherent power does not support this complete defense bar

**Holding and authority.** The unanimous Court holds that, when the court controls the forfeiture property and can enforce its eventual judgment, a claimant's failure to return for a related criminal prosecution alone does not justify striking his timely civil-forfeiture claims and defenses. Inherent authority must respond reasonably to the actual need provoking its exercise. This complete exclusion lacks the necessary connection and justification in the forfeiture proceeding.

**Controlling explanation.** The existence of inherent authority does not establish the permissible extent of a sanction. *Chambers v. NASCO* recognizes power to protect judicial proceedings while requiring restraint. *Ortega-Rodriguez v. United States* requires a sufficient connection to appellate proceedings in its preappeal-flight setting. That principle informs this case without transferring the particular appellate rule wholesale to civil forfeiture.

The court controls the property and can enforce its judgment. Brian timely filed verified claims and answers through counsel. His absence creates no enforcement difficulty of the kind that can support disentitlement in a criminal appeal. Denying affirmative appellate assistance also differs from foreclosing a defense to the Government's requested property deprivation.

The Government identifies genuine concerns: civil discovery could circumvent criminal restrictions, absence could impede litigation, and selective submission to judicial authority can undermine deterrence and judicial dignity. But anticipated misuse is not an established discovery violation or inevitable inability to adjudicate. Those interests do not justify this excessively blunt and arbitrary exclusion. Refusal to return supplies neither drug guilt nor forfeiture liability. Conditional tools remain available for supported litigation problems.

*McVeigh*, *Hovey*, *Hammond Packing* and *Societe Internationale* distinguish an opportunity to defend from punitive exclusion and properly grounded litigation sanctions or evidentiary consequences. *United States v. James Daniel Good Real Property* separately protects process in its real-property seizure setting. These considerations reinforce restraint, but the sufficient ground is the scope of the asserted inherent power. The Court does not decide Congress's constitutional ability to authorize a different disentitlement rule or whether enforcing a rule supplied under proper authority would violate due process. No present statute authorizes this categorical sanction. The decision neither requires fresh express enactment for every inherited judicial power nor abolishes criminal-appellate disentitlement within its actual scope.

##### II. Lawful management and properly supported sanctions remain available

**Holding and authority.** The unanimous Court holds that the District Court may address a supported litigation problem through applicable statutes, Rules and bounded inherent authority, subject to each source's predicates, process and justified scope. The decision neither establishes those predicates nor orders a stay, protective order, proof restriction or sanction. It imposes no universal exhaustion, mandatory sequencing or least-restrictive-means requirement.

**Controlling explanation.** Under 21 U.S.C. §881(i), filing an indictment or information alleging a violation of the federal drug provisions in subchapter I or II—or a state or local violation chargeable under those provisions—shall stay a related §881 civil-forfeiture proceeding upon a United States motion and good cause shown. The qualifying charge, relationship, Government motion and good cause are separate conditions. None is found satisfied here; an indefinite stay pending a prosecution that cannot begin is not presumed adequate.

Rule 26(c) permits supported discovery protection. Courts may also control the form of proof and, in an extreme justified case, permissible theories to prevent improper exploitation of the civil/criminal asymmetry. These possibilities do not authorize eliminating an identified defense now. If absence produces noncompliance with a legitimate pleading, discovery or evidence order, ordinary sanctions remain possible, including dismissal when appropriate under Rules 37 or 41(b), or properly bounded inherent authority. The actual source, predicate, notice and process, and scope must support the response. Perjury, discovery refusal, contempt, fraudulent transfer or other established misconduct gains no immunity from a lawfully tailored sanction.

*Carlisle v. United States* preserves inherent power while rejecting its use to supply relief that specific Rules foreclose. *Kokkonen v. Guardian Life* likewise connects authority to function; this court, unlike the postdismissal court there, already has jurisdiction. *Quackenbush v. Allstate* requires authority for the remedy actually used; availability of a different conditional remedy is insufficient. These decisions do not create a default or compel one management sequence. The tools explain why categorical exclusion is unjustified without guaranteeing an easy solution to every future difficulty or requiring courts to await completed abuse before taking justified preventive action.

##### III. Reversal restores an opportunity to defend, not entitlement to the property

**Holding and authority.** The unanimous Court reverses the Ninth Circuit's judgment insofar as it sustains striking Brian's claims and defenses on the asserted disentitlement ground, and remands for lawful further proceedings. The dependent forfeiture result cannot stand on that bar. No merits defense, independent sufficient ground outside this review, ownership right or entitlement to return is adjudicated.

**Controlling explanation.** The defect is categorical exclusion from defending this civil action. Removing that exclusion does not decide whether Brian succeeds on limitations, retroactivity, innocent ownership, probable cause or another defense. Section 881's separate forfeiture categories and owner protections remain operative. Through §881(d)'s qualified incorporation, §1615 requires the Government's antecedent probable-cause showing before placing the statutory burden on the claimant. The Court neither newly finds probable cause nor declares an otherwise established showing absent.

The preserved power question can be resolved even assuming the Government's fugitive characterization for this inquiry. The Court need not determine Brian's status in every disputed sense, his present ability to return from Switzerland, unclean hands or responsibility for disputed record deficiencies. Nor does it overturn the Ninth Circuit's adverse preservation treatment of the separate objection to calling disentitlement mandatory.

Karyn's separate judgment is outside review. Her failure to oppose the renewed summary-judgment motion directed to her claims is not Brian's misconduct. The criminal prosecution and Swiss proceedings remain undisturbed. *Republic National Bank v. United States* preserves lawful effectual relief and a conditional return/payment route upon a sufficient judgment; procedural success here supplies no immediate property return or payment. No damages, compensation, criminal dismissal, release, stay, new evidentiary hearing or full retrial is ordered. The District Court must proceed under existing lawful forfeiture procedure, without the rejected bar and without any promise that Brian will prevail.

#### Precedent treatment

- *Chambers v. NASCO, Inc.*, 501 U.S. 32, retains genuine, restrained litigation-sanction authority. Recognition of that power does not establish the scope or necessity of this bar.
- *Ortega-Rodriguez v. United States*, 507 U.S. 234, supplies by analogy the required connection between a sanction and the proceeding it protects. Its preappeal-flight rule, actual-disruption qualifications and absence of a least-restrictive-sanction requirement remain intact. Criminal-appellate disentitlement is not abolished.
- *McVeigh v. United States*, *Hovey v. Elliott*, *Hammond Packing Co. v. Arkansas* and *Societe Internationale v. Rogers* retain their distinctions between opportunity to defend, punitive exclusion and justified litigation sanctions or evidentiary consequences. They inform the inherent-power limit without supplying an independent constitutional disposition.
- *United States v. James Daniel Good Real Property*, 510 U.S. 43, retains its meaningful prior-process rule for civil seizure of real property, its justified-exigency qualification and its separate refusal to infer an unstated dismissal sanction from administrative timing duties. Its exigency inquiry does not become a universal sanction test. It supplies no automatic dismissal, title transfer or compensation remedy here.
- *Kokkonen v. Guardian Life Insurance Co. of America*, 511 U.S. 375, retains the need for an actual jurisdictional basis to enforce a separate settlement after dismissal. This pending forfeiture already lies within the court's jurisdiction; the analogy concerns function and extent, not absence of jurisdiction.
- *Carlisle v. United States*, April 29, 1996, preserves inherent powers while barring circumvention of specific Rules controlling the same relief. No identical Rule 29 conflict is found here, and its dissent's distinct retained pre-sentence duty supplies no civil-defense sanction.
- *Quackenbush v. Allstate Insurance Co.*, June 3, 1996, preserves its source-specific abstention/remedy boundary. An otherwise available conditional remedy neither validates the act ordered nor proves the alternative's predicates.
- *Republic National Bank of Miami v. United States*, 506 U.S. 80, preserves jurisdiction when lawful effectual relief remains possible and its conditional statutory return/payment route. It supplies no immediate return or money judgment upon this procedural reversal.

#### Law after the decision

Effective June 10, 1996, the asserted inherent power does not authorize this complete forfeiture-defense bar solely because Brian has not returned for the related criminal prosecution, where property control and judgment enforcement are secure. That civil-forfeiture limit leaves statutory forfeiture categories, owner protections, burdens and properly supported litigation sanctions intact.

Statutes and Rules may constrain or override inherent powers; fresh express legislation is not required for every inherited power. The Court reserves both Congress's constitutional ability to authorize a different disentitlement rule and the due-process validity of enforcing a rule supplied under proper authority. No later statute supplies present authority. The judgment establishes no universal constitutional invalidity of disentitlement, new fugitive definition, general appellate-review standard or mandatory sequence of lesser measures.

The Swiss status and preservation disputes, property defenses and separate mandatory-versus-discretionary preservation ruling are not resolved anew. Rejection of this categorical bar neither immunizes actual misconduct nor withdraws otherwise lawful preventive management supported by a real litigation problem.

#### Source notes

The [filed record and briefs in No. 95-173](https://archive.org/details/micro_IA40385013_0637) supply petition appendix 1a–14a, Joint Appendix 29–41 and 53–87, petitioner's merits brief 2–5 and 40–43, and the Government's brief 2–9 and note 4 for the proceedings, allegations and separate judgments. Government brief 43–47 and notes 25–27, together with reply 16–20, supply the qualified Swiss admissions and competing preservation arguments. These submissions establish neither the merits of unclean hands nor a present unrestricted ability to return.

The governing 1994 texts of [21 U.S.C. §881](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/USCODE-1994-title21/pdf/USCODE-1994-title21.pdf) and [19 U.S.C. §1615](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/USCODE-1994-title19/pdf/USCODE-1994-title19.pdf) supply the separate forfeiture coverage, innocent-owner qualifications, consistent customs incorporation, conditioned stay and probable-cause/burden provisions. No subsequent forfeiture-disentitlement enactment is applied. The decision rests on secure property control and limits of the asserted power, not a new finding of drug guilt, forfeiture probable cause or civil discovery misconduct.

#### Mandate, remedy, and stage

The case returns through the Ninth Circuit for lawful further forfeiture proceedings concerning Brian without the rejected defense bar. Properly available requests for discovery protection, a statutory stay or a supported sanction remain subject to their own legal conditions. No request is presumed made or meritorious, no new default is found, and no evidentiary hearing, full retrial or particular resolution of a defense is prescribed.

Karyn's separate judgment and Brian's criminal and Swiss proceedings remain undisturbed. Eventual return of property or proceeds requires a legally sufficient entitlement and applicable return or payment authority; this reversal supplies neither. No immediate return, payment, damages, compensation, release, criminal dismissal or stay is ordered. Supreme Court merits review of the power issue is complete, subject to ordinary mandate procedures.

**End of entry: Degen v. United States, merits decision, June 10, 1996.**

### Exxon Co., U.S.A. v. Sofec, Inc., 517 U.S. 830, No. 95-129

Merits decision, June 10, 1996

#### Chronology and Posture

Argued March 19, 1996. On writ of certiorari to the United States Court of Appeals for the Ninth Circuit, 54 F.3d 570, which affirmed a Rule 54(b) judgment against Exxon for stranding-related damages after the first phase of a maritime bench trial. The submitted questions concern whether comparative maritime fault abolished superseding cause and whether Exxon may recover the stranding loss under the asserted contractual warranties. A separate challenge to this particular bifurcation as an abuse of discretion or denial of due process is outside the questions presented. Claims concerning oil spilled when the vessel broke from its mooring remain pending.

The tanker broke free during severe conditions. A connected hose continued to cause concern, but the district court found that by 6:30 p.m. the vessel had reached a safe position from which it could continue backing out. The master failed for an extended period to plot position fixes, failed to use available personnel, and began the final shoreward turn without knowing the vessel's position. Both lower courts characterized that navigation as extraordinary, independent, and unforeseeable, and as the superseding and sole proximate cause of the stranding. Exxon contests the legal consequences and argues that grounding remained within the original risk.

For phase one, Exxon was relieved of proving defendants' fault or strict liability for the breakout, which was assumed to contribute in fact to the sequence. That collective premise established no individual respondent's negligence, breach, corporate obligation, or share of responsibility.

The safe-berth undertaking promised a berth to which vessels could proceed or depart and where they could always lie safely afloat; an amendment included single point mooring. Its express exclusion denied any warranty of the safety of public channels, fairways, their approaches, anchorages, and other publicly maintained areas inside or outside the port. No finding places the grounding within that exclusion. The lower court quoted the agreement in relevant part without establishing every term of the complete executed agreement.

Joint Trial Exhibit 92 is a separate General Instructions, Discharging/Loading Orders and Indemnification instrument. Its service-personnel provisions treat the supplied mooring master, tug and launch operators and crews, and vessels as servants of the vessel and owners while rendering services. They provide indemnity for resulting liabilities, losses, claims, or damages whether or not arising from the stated personnel's or indemnitees' fault. The master's legal responsibilities and final decisions remain expressly his. The lower court qualified imputation where the same conduct breached the safe-berth undertaking. The complete interaction of the two instruments is not decided here.

All nine Justices participated at argument and decision.

#### Judgment

| Judgment component | Disposition and vote | Supporting Justices | Remedy |
|---|---|---|---|
| Tort and comparative-fault claim for stranding damages | Affirmed, 9–0 | Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg, Breyer | Judgment against Exxon remains in force; no apportionment or remand. |
| Submitted safe-berth and workmanlike-service warranty claim for the same loss | Affirmed, 9–0 | Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg, Breyer | Judgment against Exxon remains in force on the distinct contractual causation ground; no contract-construction proceeding is ordered. |

No damages award, contribution order, allocation percentage, new hearing, or individual corporate-liability determination is entered. The untried breakout oil-spill claims are not disposed of.

#### Opinion and Authority

Justice Stevens delivers the opinion of the unanimous Court. Chief Justice Stone-Zsela and Justices O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg, and Breyer join Parts I–V in full. The same coalition supplies authority for every holding below. No separate opinion is filed.

#### Controlling Propositions

##### I. Review of the legal rule and concurrent causation findings

**Holding:** The Court independently reviews the governing legal rule while protecting historical findings under Rule 52. Application of proximate and superseding cause to the found circumstances ordinarily rests with the factfinder subject to limited review. Under *Graver Tank & Manufacturing Co. v. Linde Air Products Co.*, concurrent lower-court findings are not disturbed without a very obvious and exceptional showing of error, which Exxon has not made here.

**Authority:** Part I of the unanimous Opinion of the Court.

*Graver Tank* supplies restrained treatment of concurrent findings. It does not immunize a mistaken legal definition, require acceptance of unsupported new facts, or establish a universal standard for every mixed question of law and fact. *Ornelas v. United States* assigns independent ultimate review in its particular Fourth Amendment setting and expressly supplies no contrary universal rule.

Exxon identifies tension between the continuing hose concern and the finding that safety had been restored. The Court acknowledges that tension and its relevance to the claimed continuation of the original peril. The safe-position finding, available time and personnel, missing fixes, and final turn remain findings made below. The lower courts nevertheless agreed that the navigation was independent and unforeseeable. The required exceptional showing for disturbing their causal conclusion has not been made. Leaving that conclusion undisturbed does not necessarily ratify their particular application of the doctrine.

The Court neither independently recalculates the navigational sequence nor adds an alternative holding that fresh appellate review would produce the same causal result. It does not adopt HIRI's asserted abandonment of a factual challenge as a separate waiver ground.

##### II. Comparative fault and legal causation

**Holding:** Comparative maritime fault apportions a loss among actors who legally caused it. A later independent and unforeseeable cause may supersede an earlier factual contribution. A plaintiff whose conduct is the superseding and sole proximate cause cannot recover part of that loss merely because others' blameworthy conduct helped produce the sequence in fact.

**Authority:** Part II of the unanimous Opinion of the Court.

*United States v. Reliable Transfer Co.* replaced automatic equal division of maritime collision or stranding damages with proportional allocation when multiple parties' fault contributes legally to the loss. Equal division remains when fault is equal or cannot fairly be measured. The decision changed allocation without eliminating the antecedent requirement of legal causation.

Its rejection of the major-minor doctrine remains important: a court cannot excuse one legally contributing actor merely because another was much more negligent. Severity of fault alone therefore does not establish supersession. The later conduct must have the independence and unforeseeability necessary to break the legal causal connection. Ordinary foreseeable efforts to escape the first actor's danger do not automatically have that effect. The Court does not hold that grounding can never be a foreseeable consequence of mooring failure.

*McDermott, Inc. v. AmClyde* and *Boca Grande Club, Inc. v. Florida Power & Light Co.* require reliable allocation for their settlement-credit and contribution rules after legal responsibility is established; they do not make every factual contributor liable. *City of Milwaukee v. Cement Division, National Gypsum Co.* concerns prejudgment interest on a compensable loss and likewise presupposes liability.

The Court retains the term comparative fault without deciding whether allocation should reflect comparative culpability alone or also relative causal contribution, assuming a meaningful distinction exists. No settlement-credit, contribution-protection, or prejudgment-interest rule changes.

##### III. The consequence of the undisturbed tort-causation determination

**Holding:** The undisturbed finding that Exxon's navigation was the superseding and sole proximate cause defeats tort recovery for the stranding loss. No comparative allocation is required because the accepted determination leaves no other legally operative cause of that injury.

**Authority:** Part III of the unanimous Opinion of the Court.

The mooring failure explains the initial emergency and remains an assumed factual contributor. The lower courts relied on the later safe position, available opportunity to depart and assistance, and the master's final navigation as separating the stranding from the original peril. Their analysis thus purported to identify an independent causal intervention rather than merely compare degrees of negligence or count elapsed time.

Exxon's strongest contrary argument, supported by its invocation of Restatement §442B and *Kinsman*, is that the later navigation and grounding remained within the risk created by the breakout. That objection prevents a categorical rule treating every negligent emergency response as superseding. The continuing hose concern makes the application contestable; the Court does not declare that concern nonexistent or independently find complete freedom from danger. Under Part I's limited review, however, the lower courts' concurrent ultimate conclusion remains undisturbed.

The legally permissible distinction between factual contribution and legal responsibility sustains this tort judgment. Phase one's assumed fault or strict liability does not become a finding that respondents were blameless, that every respondent breached a duty, or that an already established comparative share has been extinguished. No present percentage or fresh finding about each respondent is needed.

##### IV. The distinct causal requirement for contractual warranty damages

**Holding:** Contractual duties may exceed negligence duties, but recovery under the submitted safe-berth and workmanlike-service warranties still requires legal causation of the claimed loss. Assuming the stricter undertaking and relevant breach, the undisturbed determination that Exxon's navigation alone proximately caused the stranding defeats recovery of that loss without deciding strict warranty versus due diligence.

**Authority:** Part IV of the unanimous Opinion of the Court.

*Italia Societa per Azioni di Navigazione v. Oregon Stevedoring Co.* recognizes breach of workmanlike service through nonnegligently supplied defective equipment, but does not remove the causal limitation on warranty damages. *East River Steamship Corp. v. Transamerica Delaval Inc.* and contract foreseeability principles distinguish promised performance from recoverable losses. Contractual causation can be more restrictive in some circumstances; it is not held always stricter than, or categorically identical to, tort causation.

The asserted strict promise is assumed, not replaced with reasonable care. The district court chose due diligence before finding no post-breakout breach. Exxon's concession that diligence was satisfied does not concede performance of a stricter promise; the Court of Appeals expressly reserved that choice. The safe-departure and available-assistance findings retain causal significance independently of the disputed duty standard. Under the undisturbed sole-proximate-cause determination, an assumed breach contributing only in fact does not establish the causal connection required for these warranty damages.

The duty continued after breakout: an earlier superseding force cannot by itself excuse a later breach. That later-breach theory remains subject to contractual causation rather than being erased through the diligence concession. Neither the clause's public-area exclusion nor blanket imputation or indemnity under Exhibit 92 supplies an alternative holding. Their complete interaction and individual corporate responsibility remain reserved. No unquoted promise insuring every later navigational loss is inferred, and no unquoted exclusion is invented.

HIRI's avoidable-consequences contention need not be decided as an independently sufficient ground that every stranding-loss item was wholly avoidable. The holding creates neither automatic forfeiture for ordinary promisee negligence nor a rule that mitigation must always be proportional. A materially different express allocation of later losses would require consideration of its own supported terms and preserved theory.

##### V. Bifurcation and the stranding-only judgment

**Holding:** Comparative fault does not invariably require assessment of every party's relative fault before a court can determine a genuinely superseding cause. The phase-one assumptions permit that antecedent inquiry here. The separate claim that this particular bifurcation was an abuse of discretion or denied due process is not decided.

**Authority:** Part V of the unanimous Opinion of the Court.

Legal responsibility precedes apportionment. Because phase one assumed factual contribution and relieved Exxon of proving defendants' fault or strict liability for the breakout, the submitted legal-causation questions could be decided on that basis. This rejects a categorical legal bar to separate consideration, not every objection to the management of this trial.

Supreme Court Rules 14.1(a) and 24.1(a) preserve the question limits. *Yamaha Motor Corp., U.S.A. v. Calhoun* addresses issues within a certified interlocutory order. *Quackenbush v. Allstate Insurance Co.* addresses appealability and abstention. Neither enlarges the questions submitted here. Exclusion of a free-standing bifurcation challenge does not erase Exxon's argument that the available evidence was insufficient to resolve the submitted issues; that argument has been considered within their actual scope.

The affirmed Rule 54(b) judgment concerns stranding damages alone. This decision requires no further contract-construction proceeding, allocation, hearing, or damages determination. The breakout oil-spill proceedings retain their existing posture and undecided liability and damages issues.

#### Precedent Treatment and Current Law

*Reliable Transfer* remains a comparative-allocation rule preserving legal causation, its equal-fault and unmeasurable-fault exception, and its rejection of the major-minor shortcut. *Graver Tank* supplies limited review of concurrent findings without immunity from review for legal error. *Ornelas* remains confined to its stated review setting and establishes no universal independent review of mixed questions.

*Italia Societa* retains warranty responsibility beyond negligence without eliminating legal causation of contract damages. *East River* supports distinct treatment of contractual responsibility and recoverable loss; no universal equivalence between contractual and tort causation is adopted. *McDermott* and *Boca Grande* retain their settlement-credit and contribution-protection rules, including reliable allocation where those rules operate. *City of Milwaukee* retains its rule governing compensatory prejudgment interest on otherwise recoverable loss; no compensable principal is created here.

*Yamaha* retains its certified-order review rule and reservations concerning causation and liability standards; it supplies no broader certiorari scope. *Quackenbush* retains its appealability and abstention holdings. An obligation to adjudicate a contract claim guarantees no recovery and does not expand the submitted questions.

Comparative maritime fault and superseding cause remain complementary. Courts identify legal responsibility before allocating loss; relative severity of negligence alone cannot remove an otherwise operative legal cause. The accepted sole-proximate-cause determination leaves no stranding loss to apportion among these defendants. The warranty claims fail on their separately examined contractual causation requirement even assuming the stricter duty and breach. The decision neither adopts due diligence as the safe-berth promise's meaning nor adjudicates wholesale avoidability, comprehensively construes Exhibit 92, excuses later breaches through an earlier superseding event, or determines a related corporation's individual obligation. The particular causal application remains undisturbed under limited review without necessarily being ratified in every respect.

#### Mandate, Remedy, and Stage

The Ninth Circuit's judgment sustaining the stranding-related Rule 54(b) judgment is affirmed. Ordinary mandate procedures apply. No special mandate date, remand, new hearing, apportionment, further contract-construction proceeding, damages determination, or further Supreme Court event is ordered. The pending breakout oil-spill claims and their unadjudicated liability and damages issues remain for the proceedings below.

**End of entry: Exxon Co., U.S.A. v. Sofec, Inc., merits decision, June 10, 1996.**

### Richards v. Jefferson County, 517 U.S. 793, No. 95-386

Merits decision, June 10, 1996

#### Chronology and Posture

Argued March 26, 1996. On certiorari under 28 U.S.C. §1257(a) to the Alabama Supreme Court, 662 So. 2d 1127. The question is whether due process permits the earlier taxpayer judgment in *Bedingfield*, 527 So. 2d 1270, to preclude Richards and Hill's federal claims. The March 15, 1996 order removed the separate question concerning the occupational tax's substantive equal-protection validity. The Court does not restore that question or review the separate federal employees' Buck Act claims.

*Bedingfield* combined challenges by the City of Birmingham and its finance director with those of individual employees. Its complaints included federal and state theories; the appellate opinion discussed state authorization, population classification, and collection authority. Richards and Hill were not parties, and *Bedingfield* was not a class action. Their present complaint challenges their own tax obligations and seeks relief including amounts already paid.

The Alabama majority found adequate representation from essentially identical interests and relied on revenue finality and tax-supported bond financing. Respondents also emphasize the City's resources and adversarial incentive. Petitioners assert that they received no notice that their own claims could be impaired. That assertion is not an appellate finding. No finding of their participation or control supplies the representative basis used below. The Court addresses that basis's legal sufficiency without finding every possible connection absent. All nine Justices participated at argument and decision.

#### Judgment

**Reversed and remanded, 9–0.**

**Supporting:** Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg, and Breyer.

The Alabama Supreme Court's dispositive preclusion judgment is reversed as applied to Richards and Hill's reviewed federal claims. The case is remanded to that court for further proceedings. The identified representative basis cannot bar these nonparties' reviewed claims; the Court awards no relief on the tax itself.

#### Opinion and Authority

Chief Justice Alex-Lamar Stone-Zsela delivers the Opinion of the Court, Parts I–IV, joined throughout by Justices Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg, and Breyer. That unanimous coalition directly adopts each of the following controlling propositions, explanations, and qualifications, together with the bounded reversal and remand. No separate writing is filed.

#### Controlling Propositions

##### I. Finality for the limited federal review

**Holding:** Section 1257(a) permits review under the first finality category of *Cox Broadcasting Corp. v. Cohn*, 420 U.S. 469: the preclusion ruling conclusively bars Richards and Hill's remaining federal claims and leaves its implementation preordained, despite the state interlocutory label. Review concerns only nonparty due process; the separate Buck Act proceedings and removed tax-merits question do not enlarge it.

**Authority:** Part I of the unanimous Opinion of the Court.

*Cox* permits review when the federal issue is finally determined and further proceedings are preordained, leaving the matter practically concluded. State Counts 3 and 4 had already been rejected. The Alabama Supreme Court's preclusion judgment disposes of these petitioners' remaining federal claims. Its implementation is not an open merits trial that might eliminate their objection to being bound. The remaining Buck Act claim belongs to a separate federal-employee group outside this review.

The state's permissive interlocutory procedure therefore does not defeat federal finality, but state certification alone does not establish it. The actual effect of the orders controls; no later judgment is presumed. *Southland Corp. v. Keating*, 465 U.S. 1, and *Doctor's Associates, Inc. v. Casarotto*, decided May 20, 1996, concern *Cox*'s different fourth category, under which federal reversal of an arbitration bar removes a dispute from court. Reversal here permits litigation to continue, so that rationale does not supply jurisdiction. *Quackenbush v. Allstate Insurance Co.*, decided June 3, 1996, concerns federal appellate routes under §1291, which do not govern a state judgment under §1257. This first-category route decides neither tax constitutionality, Buck Act liability, nor entitlement to a particular tax remedy.

##### II. Authority to bind absent claimants

**Holding:** Ordinary state preclusion rules remain subject to due process. Essentially identical interests, vigorous advocacy, resources, and public importance alone do not authorize adjudication of absent persons' own legal rights. Binding may rest on constitutionally sufficient consent, control, genuine privity, adequate class or representative litigation, or another recognized relationship or special remedial arrangement consistent with due process; finality policy and a virtual-representation label cannot replace that prerequisite.

**Authority:** Part II of the unanimous Opinion of the Court.

*Hansberry v. Lee*, 311 U.S. 32, protects an opportunity to be heard while permitting genuine representative adjudication. *Martin v. Wilks*, 490 U.S. 755, preserves adequate representation, control-based binding, and special arrangements, including bankruptcy or probate, that may lawfully foreclose successive litigation. Guardian–ward and trustee–beneficiary relationships illustrate genuine privity. Each person need not appear individually, but common objectives cannot alone confer a representative's authority.

*Matsushita Electric Industrial Co. v. Epstein*, decided February 27, 1996, distinguishes ordinary state recognition from constitutional power to bind an absent member. Notice, objections, or opt-out opportunities do not independently cure inadequate representation. Section 1738 does not separately command Alabama to bind these strangers to its earlier judgment; the constitutional minimum remains operative.

The Court assumes without deciding that adequate representation might cure absence of notice in some class or representative suits. Even on that assumption, the offered representative basis fails. The assumption makes representation no automatic cure and requires no individual notice in every representative proceeding. Conversely, mere notice of another person's suit establishes no authority to bind and imposes no general duty on strangers to intervene.

States retain ordinary preclusion autonomy and their interest in genuine finality. The Court corrects an extreme application extinguishing personal claims without a sufficient binding basis; it authorizes no general retrial of ordinary state-law errors. The full scope of collateral review reserved in *Matsushita* remains undecided, and *Bedingfield* remains effective between persons properly bound. The absence of a Rule 23 class matters here but does not itself invalidate every judgment entered through an authorized form of representative litigation.

##### III. These personal tax claims and the public-interest rationale

**Holding:** The representative rationale used below cannot constitutionally bind Richards and Hill to *Bedingfield*'s disposition of their own tax liability and claims for paid sums. Shared taxpayer objectives and fiscal reliance do not establish the required relationship. Genuine representative public-right litigation and ordinary reliance on precedent remain distinct and preserved.

**Authority:** Part III of the unanimous Opinion of the Court.

The County emphasizes the earlier litigants' shared constitutional objective, substantial resources, and vigorous advocacy, together with public revenue and bond financing. Those considerations show the importance of valid finality, but not authority to adjudicate these absent taxpayers' personal rights conclusively. The Court need not find deficient advocacy or resolve every possible connection to reject the rationale actually used.

A taxpayer's challenge to misuse of public funds or government action affecting that taxpayer only indirectly differs from a challenge to a direct levy of the taxpayer's own funds. The Court assumes wide state latitude over standing and repeated proceedings in the former setting. This case concerns the latter. Genuine public-right representative judgments remain possible. Neither refund relief nor lack of formal class treatment independently supplies a universal invalidating test, and federal class-action requirements are not wholesale constitutional commands to the States.

The finance director's participation establishes no rule that public officials always adequately represent constituents' personal rights; that broader question remains open. Supposed representation of city taxpayers cannot, without more, become established representation of these county taxpayers.

*Brinkerhoff-Faris Trust & Savings Co. v. Hill*, 281 U.S. 673, protects the opportunity to challenge a direct levy. *Logan v. Zimmerman Brush Co.*, 455 U.S. 422, recognizes the protected status of an otherwise available individual claim—a chose in action. Extinguishing that personal claim reinforces the hearing concern. The identified Alabama rationale establishes no exclusive public-action remedial scheme, and common interests and revenue reliance cannot create one by assertion. This reasoning creates no cause of action or tax-merits holding. A court may use precedent to decide a later claim without conclusively binding its owner to another person's judgment; those are different operations.

##### IV. Issue preclusion and the limited relief

**Holding:** Respondents' narrower issue-preclusion theory cannot sustain this judgment because it too requires a constitutionally sufficient basis to bind these nonparties. The dispositive bar on the reviewed Richards–Hill federal claims is reversed and the case remanded, without deciding whether the exemption issue was actually and necessarily determined or awarding relief on the tax.

**Authority:** Part IV of the unanimous Opinion of the Court.

Claim preclusion, which bars a claim, and issue preclusion, which bars relitigation of an issue, have different requirements. Both first require a lawful basis to bind the person affected. Narrowing the bar to an exemption issue supplies no missing representative relationship.

The earlier complaints pleaded federal theories. The Alabama majority reasoned that those theories could have been, and arguably were, adjudicated; Justice Maddox disputed the identity of the cause and actual federal adjudication. Pleading an issue, having an opportunity to litigate it, and actually deciding it as necessary to judgment are distinct predicates. Because the asserted binding basis fails first, the Court need not resolve that dispute or find that no relevant issue was ever determined.

Uncertainty about every possible notice, authorization, or control fact does not require an exploratory remand. The Court corrects the identified legal ground without adverse factual findings or discovery directed at constructing a different bar. A genuinely available defense or constitutionally valid relationship remains subject to its own proof, preservation, and procedural conditions. The mandate guarantees neither a new theory nor a finding that one exists.

Ordinary proceedings on the reviewed claims may resume. No state-count ruling, earlier Tax Injunction Act dismissal, properly binding judgment, or separate Buck Act claim is reopened. The decision orders no immediate trial, refund, damages, injunction, tax invalidation, or representative-adequacy hearing, and guarantees no eventual recovery. No special deadline or new Supreme Court event is prescribed.

#### Precedent Treatment and Current Law

*Cox*'s first finality category applies to the practically concluded claims and preordained implementation despite the state procedural label. *Southland*'s fourth-category arbitration route is distinguished. *Casarotto*'s focus on actual proceedings rather than certification alone is preserved; neither its arbitration rationale nor its substantive Federal Arbitration Act holding decides this case. *Quackenbush* retains its distinct §1291 and abstention holdings and the separate state-tax comity setting; it supplies no nonparty relationship or basis to reopen the earlier federal tax dismissal.

The Court applies *Hansberry*'s constitutional prerequisite of genuine adequate representation while preserving lawful representative adjudication. It applies *Martin*'s nonparty hearing protection while preserving adequate representation, litigation control, and special remedial schemes consistent with due process; notice alone creates no general intervention duty. *Matsushita*'s distinction between ordinary recognition and constitutional authority to bind applies, including the requirement of adequate representation independent of notice and opt-out protections. Its complete collateral-review reservation remains unresolved. *Brinkerhoff-Faris* supports an opportunity to contest a direct levy without deciding this tax's validity. *Logan* supplies the protected personal-claim principle where an individual claim is otherwise available; it creates no new cause of action or universal state-remedy scheme.

No precedent is overruled. *Bedingfield* retains its proper force among persons lawfully bound and as precedent; only its constitutionally unsupported preclusive use against these petitioners is rejected.

Effective June 10, 1996, the identified Alabama virtual-representation rationale cannot extinguish these nonparties' personal tax claims merely because earlier litigants shared their objectives and the County relied on fiscal finality. Tax validity and any right to recovery remain undecided. Ordinary state preclusion, genuine privity, actual litigation control, valid class and representative judgments, and historically established exceptions consistent with due process remain available. The possibility that adequate representation might cure absent notice is assumed only; mere notice supplies neither representation nor a general intervention duty. Indirect public-right suits retain the assumed wide state latitude described above. No universal rule concerning public officials, every taxpayer action, individual notice, or collateral review is adopted.

#### Mandate, Remedy, and Stage

The case returns to the Alabama Supreme Court for proceedings consistent with rejection of the identified nonparty bar on Richards and Hill's reviewed federal claims. Ordinary legally available defenses, constitutionally valid preclusion, and remedy conditions retain their scope. The Court mandates no representative-adequacy hearing, automatic discovery, or opportunity to develop another preclusion theory, and awards no tax invalidation, refund, damages, or injunction. The separate state-count rulings, federal tax dismissal, Buck Act proceedings, and judgments binding other litigants remain untouched. Supreme Court merits review is complete, subject to ordinary mandate procedures; no special deadline or additional Supreme Court event is ordered.

**End of entry: Richards v. Jefferson County, merits decision, June 10, 1996.**

### Whren v. United States, No. 95-5841

Merits decision, June 10, 1996

#### Chronology and Posture

Argued April 17, 1996, on writ of certiorari to the D.C. Circuit; reported at 517 U.S. 806. Petitioners challenge the initial traffic restraint by plainclothes officers investigating drugs. They contend that reasonable officers ordinarily would not have stopped them for the traffic purpose alone. The later search is not independently presented.

The district judge acknowledged discrepancies but credited Officer Soto's account. The vehicle waited unusually long at a stop sign, turned without signaling and traveled at unreasonable speed. Officers approached after it had stopped at another intersection, directed it to park and then saw drugs in plain view. The Court of Appeals sustained probable cause under the full-attention, signaling and speed provisions, 18 D.C.M.R. §§2213.4, 2204.3 and 2200.3.

The Court of Appeals rejected suppression on this ground but remanded by agreement to correct lesser-included §841 convictions and resentence under §860. That correction has occurred: the lesser convictions were vacated, the original prison terms were generally reimposed, Whren's fine was not, and the new sentencing appeals were held pending this review.

Petitioners' updated conflict discussion recognizes that Botero-Ospina, 71 F.3d 783, moved the Tenth Circuit to the objective-probable-cause approach. The cited Ninth and Eleventh Circuit decisions represented the remaining contrary line.

#### Judgment

**Affirmed on the initial-stop issue, 9–0.**

**Supporting:** Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg, Breyer.

The rejection of suppression on that ground remains intact. The completed correction of the lesser convictions and sentences is undisturbed; the Court neither repeats that correction and resentencing nor decides the later sentencing appeals.

#### Opinion of the Court

Justice Thomas delivers the opinion of the Court, joined in full by Chief Justice Stone-Zsela and Justices Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Ginsburg and Breyer. No separate opinion is filed. The unanimous opinion supplies direct authority for each rule, application and qualification below.

#### Controlling Propositions and Authority

##### Objective probable cause supports this ordinary initial traffic stop

An ordinary initial traffic stop supported by objective probable cause of a traffic violation does not become unreasonable under the Fourth Amendment because officers also seek to investigate an unrelated offense. Validity does not additionally depend on whether reasonable officers ordinarily would have enforced the violation without that other aim. Independent limits on manner, scope and duration remain.

Scott requires objective assessment of the action actually justified. Robinson supports that distinction within its lawful-arrest and search setting. Prouse restricts suspicionless discretionary vehicle stops; the known violations here supply its missing individualized basis. An additional drug-investigation aim does not remove that basis.

Petitioners propose an objective would-have test, not merely an inquiry into personal intent. They argue that pervasive minor violations otherwise permit arbitrary selection. But customary enforcement propensity is not another element of an ordinary probable-cause stop. Local priorities, uniforms, assignments and vehicle markings cannot change constitutional authority for the same known violation. Their test would require choosing a relevant custom and resolving its variable exceptions without explaining why custom controls the constitutional boundary. Police manuals can supply evidence where legally material; ordinary assignments do not establish this additional condition.

Bertine and Wells require standardized criteria or established routine and genuine inventory purposes when that exception supplies authority. An inventory cannot be a ruse for general investigatory rummaging. Burger likewise preserves the qualifying purpose and limits of administrative inspections. Those source-specific conditions do not impose an enforcement-propensity requirement on this individualized stop. Neither Bannister's no-pretext observation nor Abel's account of regular procedure makes departure from practice invalidate an otherwise justified seizure. Robinson's reservation of different facts does not decide them.

Balancing traffic-safety gains against anxiety or confusion from plainclothes intervention adds no condition for this ordinary stop. Prouse, Martinez-Fuerte and Brignoni-Ponce addressed intrusions without probable cause and retain their distinct requirements. Broad traffic regulation does not authorize courts to rank observed violations by deserved enforcement. Actual execution remains reviewable; probable cause does not authorize every intrusive seizure.

##### The credited record requires no new factual inquiry

The accepted traffic observations supply probable cause for the initial restraint. Ornelas preserves clear-error review of historical facts, credibility and genuinely factual subsidiary findings or inferences, together with genuine due weight for grounded contextual inferences. The governing legal standard and any ultimate constitutional sufficiency properly at issue receive independent judgment.

The district judge's acceptance of Soto's account remains protected by the factual standard. Genuine due weight requires substantive consideration of supported trial and local-officer context. It is not a numerical weight, a presumption favoring officer credibility or an intermediate standard insulating the ultimate constitutional conclusion. Neither a habeas presumption nor a clear-and-convincing burden governs this direct review.

This case selects the legal rule for an accepted traffic basis. Independent judgment supplies neither another violation nor supposed antecedent drug probable cause. Officer experience does not establish the General Order's safety exception. Ornelas creates no new factual conflict, required hearing or automatic remand. Applying the objective rule leaves the suppression rejection intact within the issue presented.

#### Policy, Execution and Discrimination Limits

General Order 303.1, in its April 30, 1992 form, contains qualified enforcement assignments. It permits traffic enforcement by on-duty uniformed officers in marked vehicles; off-duty uniformed officers in marked vehicles participating in the take-home cruiser program; and on-duty uniformed Public Vehicle Enforcement Unit officers in unmarked vehicles. Other plainclothes or unmarked-vehicle enforcement requires a violation so grave that it poses an immediate threat to others' safety.

A traffic stop ordinarily requires a Notice of Infraction or Warning Notice of Infraction. An oral warning is allowed in extreme circumstances, such as an immediate-response radio assignment or emergency transport of a sick or injured passenger to a hospital. The Court makes no finding of compliance or violation and does not equate unreasonable speed with the immediate-threat exception. Because policy compliance is not the selected constitutional prerequisite, its unresolved application requires no hearing or remand.

The rule authorizes no detention or search materially exceeding what the traffic violation reasonably justifies without independent grounds. Actual manner, scope and duration remain subject to constitutional review. Wilson makes announcement part of reasonable home entry, retaining supported danger, futility, escape and evidence-destruction qualifications; the exact suspicion quantum and automatic-suppression question remain open. Garner, Welsh and Winston preserve their distinct constraints on deadly force, home entry and bodily intrusion. Plainclothes status alone establishes no unusual execution, and missing duration or force details establish no affirmative finding. No distinct supported, preserved execution ground changes this initial-stop judgment. The subsequent search remains outside the independently presented issue.

Purposeful racial selection remains independently forbidden by Equal Protection. Rejecting the proposed Fourth Amendment test neither approves discrimination nor establishes its absence. Soto's denial and the district judge's credit retain their procedural status; the Court enters no new discrimination finding. Kimberlin preserves competent circumstantial proof where motive is an underlying element. Armstrong's federal charging and discovery rules are not transferred to police-stop claims. The Court decides no discovery entitlement, immunity, suppression consequence or damages remedy for discriminatory selection. The supposed adequacy of another remedy is not a ground for the judgment.

#### Precedent Treatment and Current Law

- **Scott v. United States, 436 U.S. 128:** Objective assessment of the justified action applies, without making motive irrelevant to every constitutional claim.
- **United States v. Robinson, 414 U.S. 218, and Gustafson v. Florida, 414 U.S. 260:** Their lawful-arrest and search settings remain. An additional investigative purpose does not itself remove objective authority; no new arrest or search power arises here.
- **United States v. Villamonte-Marquez, 462 U.S. 579:** Its objectively authorized customs-inspection setting supports the same distinction while retaining the authority's statutory and constitutional predicates.
- **Delaware v. Prouse, 440 U.S. 648:** Suspicionless discretionary selection differs from a stop based on observed violations. Its constraints remain in force.
- **Colorado v. Bertine, 479 U.S. 367, and Florida v. Wells, 495 U.S. 1:** Standardized or routine inventory constraints, purpose-related discretion and the prohibition of an investigatory ruse remain.
- **New York v. Burger, 482 U.S. 691:** Administrative-inspection requirements survive. A general rule disregarding motive does not displace a justification limited by its purpose.
- **United States v. Martinez-Fuerte, 428 U.S. 543, and United States v. Brignoni-Ponce, 422 U.S. 873:** Their distinct treatment of intrusions without probable cause does not require an additional case-by-case balance for this ordinary probable-cause stop.
- **Wilson v. Arkansas, May 22, 1995:** The distinction between authority and reasonable execution applies, with its home-entry qualifications and unresolved remedy intact.
- **Tennessee v. Garner, 471 U.S. 1; Welsh v. Wisconsin, 466 U.S. 740; and Winston v. Lee, 470 U.S. 753:** Their distinct constraints on unusually harmful execution remain. Plainclothes traffic intervention is not categorically immune from manner review.
- **Ornelas v. United States, May 28, 1996:** Independent constitutional judgment applies with protected historical findings and genuine contextual due weight; no new factual dispute is created.
- **Kimberlin v. Quinlan, June 12, 1995:** Circumstantial proof remains available where motive is an element, without adding that element to this stop rule.
- **United States v. Armstrong, May 13, 1996:** Its federal charging and discovery setting and unresolved discrimination remedy remain distinct. No police-stop discovery rule is imported.
- **Abel v. United States, 362 U.S. 217, and Colorado v. Bannister, 449 U.S. 1:** Their regular-procedure and no-pretext observations do not establish the proposed would-have rule.

No precedent is overruled. Effective June 10, 1996, the validity of an ordinary initial traffic stop founded on known probable cause does not additionally depend on an unrelated investigative motive or customary willingness to enforce the violation. The rule answers the objective would-have proposal while preserving the individualized justification that distinguishes this stop from suspicionless selection.

Inventory and administrative justifications retain their purposes and procedural limits. Execution, duration, scope and purposeful discriminatory selection retain independent constitutional treatment. The decision supplies no universal authorization for searches or seizures whenever probable cause exists, blanket exclusion of motive evidence, transfer of Armstrong's discovery rule or new suppression rule. Ornelas's allocation remains unchanged.

#### Mandate, Remedy, and Stage

The rejection of suppression on the initial-stop ground is affirmed. The completed vacatur of the lesser-included §841 convictions and resentencing under §860 remain undisturbed, including nonreimposition of Whren's fine. The later sentencing appeals remain subject to their own lawful disposition; the Court prescribes no outcome or special timing.

No new trial, acquittal, release, evidentiary hearing or sentencing proceeding or remand is ordered. The holding supplies no independent determination of the subsequent search or of every ground of evidence admissibility. Supreme Court merits review is complete, subject to ordinary mandate procedures.

**End of entry: Whren v. United States, merits decision, June 10, 1996.**
