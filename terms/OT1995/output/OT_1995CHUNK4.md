### Peacock v. Thomas, No. 94-1453, 516 U.S. 349

Merits decision, February 21, 1996

#### Chronology and Posture

Argued November 6, 1995. On writ of certiorari to the United States Court of Appeals for the Fourth Circuit, 39 F.3d 493. The questions concern original jurisdiction under ERISA and ancillary jurisdiction over a later action imposing Tru-Tech's earlier judgment on Peacock. Although Peacock was a defendant in the original action, he was held not to be a fiduciary and was not made a judgment debtor. The later judgment imposed the whole $187,628.93 corporate obligation, with derivative assessments; it was not a separable award returning identified debtor property. Chief Justice Stone-Zsela and Justices Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer participated at argument and decision.

#### Judgment

**Reversed and remanded for jurisdictional dismissal, 8–1.**

**Supporting:** Stone-Zsela, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer.

**Opposing:** Stevens, who would vacate the unqualified personal-liability and derivative-fee affirmance but preserve ancillary jurisdiction for a bounded restorative-enforcement remand.

The judgment sustaining jurisdiction and imposing Tru-Tech's obligation on Peacock is reversed. The later personal liability and its dependent interest, attorneys' fees and expenses cannot stand. The direction to recalculate the derivative assessment falls with its jurisdictional basis; the already vacated $205,182.01 combined fees-and-expenses amount is not reinstated. The original $187,628.93 judgment against Tru-Tech remains intact.

#### Opinion Topology

| Writing | Author | Joined by | Relationship to judgment | Scope joined |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Opinion of the Court | Thomas | O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Ginsburg and Breyer throughout; Stone-Zsela in part | Supports reversal and jurisdictional dismissal | Seven Justices adopt the original-ERISA-jurisdiction analysis. Stone-Zsela joins ancillary jurisdiction, its application and limits, and disposition, giving those propositions eight votes; he does not join the separate ERISA analysis. |
| Dissent | Stevens | No other Justice | Opposes subject-matter dismissal; supports vacating the excessive whole-debt and derivative-fee affirmance | Would preserve possible restorative relief on remand. |

#### Controlling Propositions and Authority

##### ERISA supplies no original jurisdiction on the asserted basis

**Holding and authority:** Thomas's opinion, joined by O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Ginsburg and Breyer, holds that this later collection action alleges no underlying ERISA or plan violation supporting the asserted federal jurisdiction. Veil piercing is a means of imposing liability, not an independent statutory violation.

Section 502(a)(3) provides relief for the statutory or plan obligations it identifies, not equitable relief at large. Later control and collection obstruction do not themselves erase the original nonfiduciary adjudication or establish fiduciary responsibility during the earlier breaches. Calling an unrestricted compensation award equitable supplies neither a new duty nor a jurisdictional source. The Court assumes without deciding that ERISA may permit veil piercing in an appropriately founded action. It does not decide every possible equitable monetary remedy or treat every unsuccessful federal merits theory as a jurisdictional defect.

##### Ancillary jurisdiction does not support the new full-debt action

**Holding and authority:** The eight Justices joining Thomas's ancillary-jurisdiction analysis hold that a court's power to identify, preserve and execute against its judgment debtor's assets does not authorize a later independent action imposing that debtor's whole obligation on another person without an independent jurisdictional basis.

Kokkonen distinguishes authority to effectuate a decree from jurisdiction based on factual interdependence; neither is unlimited. The later collection conduct differs materially from the original pension violations. A new lawsuit cannot borrow the earlier action's jurisdiction merely through connection to its result. Making Peacock a new debtor requires adjudicating new facts, duties and defenses. Beecher's limit on creating a new obligation applies. Riggs and Labette County enforced existing official duties rather than transferring judgment debt to officials. Rule 69 and §1367 do not independently create original jurisdiction. The original suit predates §1367's effective boundary, and the later filing supplies no original claim to which supplemental jurisdiction can attach. Remedial need does not replace statutory authority.

##### Ordinary enforcement remains available

**Holding and authority:** The same eight Justices require dismissal on the asserted grounds while preserving the original judgment. This jurisdictional disposition does not restrict contempt, asset discovery, ordinary attachment or garnishment, fraudulent-transfer process directed to debtor property, or an independently founded federal action.

The Court neither affirms a nonexistent separable property award nor adjudicates particular assets, recipients or values. The transfer findings and disputed valuations do not establish an asset award equal to the pension judgment. The Court does not decide personal jurisdiction or service, the governing South Carolina enforcement rule, federal displacement of that rule, or the merits of later-conduct preclusion. The preserved service objection is neither rejected nor deemed waived; ancillary subject-matter jurisdiction alone would not establish nationwide-service authority. No general prohibition on all alter-ego actions or all proceedings involving persons other than the debtor follows.

#### Precedent Treatment and Current-Law Effect

Kokkonen's independent-authority requirement and distinct decree-effectuation branch govern the later new-debtor action. Beecher's limit on creating a new obligation applies; Riggs and Labette County retain their existing-duty premise. Mertens's bounded equitable-category rule and antecedent-action reservation remain intact. Neither O'Melveny nor Travelers supplies a general federal collection rule, and the Fourth Circuit's categorical federal-preemption rationale is not approved. No earlier holding is overruled.

A federal judgment does not itself confer jurisdiction over a separate action creating another person's full liability for that judgment. The new-debtor limit leaves traditional execution against debtor property and independently founded federal actions available, without deciding the merits or governing law of a particular restorative property proceeding. The decision creates no new ERISA collection cause and no general bar to equitable monetary relief.

#### Separate Position

Stevens concludes that a court's continuing authority to effectuate its judgment permits consideration of restoration when a person controlling the debtor deliberately diverts its assets to defeat collection. Wayman, Riggs and Labette County support that enforcement function; Beecher's attempted addition of independently liable persons does not resolve this distinct interference with execution. But the full corporate debt cannot simply be imposed on Peacock.

Stevens would vacate the unqualified liability and derivative-fee affirmance and remand to determine whether restorative relief remains legally and procedurally available. The existing findings do not establish a ready-made asset award, that every transfer occurred after judgment, that Peacock received every asset, or a particular recoverable value. State enforcement law ordinarily supplies the Rule 69 rule; federal uniformity alone does not justify displacement. Preserved personal-jurisdiction and service objections must be resolved before renewed personal relief, and fees or expenses require their own lawful basis. He reserves the alternative ERISA cause and would not direct immediate payment, reinstate a rejected count or guarantee recovery. His approach also leaves the original Tru-Tech judgment undisturbed.

#### Mandate, Remedy, and Stage

The case returns to the lower courts for dismissal of the later action for want of subject-matter jurisdiction and removal of the dependent assessments against Peacock. Dismissal does not extinguish otherwise available state-law rights or an action supported by an independent jurisdictional grant. The original Tru-Tech judgment remains enforceable through lawful proceedings. No special mandate date, new filing or particular asset-recovery order is directed.

**End of entry: Peacock v. Thomas, merits decision, February 21, 1996.**

### Jones v. ABC-TV, No. 95-7186, 516 U.S. 363

Filing-management order, February 26, 1996

#### Chronology and Posture

The Court considers the financial-leave motion accompanying Jones's petition for certiorari and a proposed condition on future noncriminal certiorari filings. The petition seeks review of the Eighth Circuit's August 8, 1995 summary affirmance of the district court's June 14 dismissal of a media-related civil action. Certiorari itself is not decided by this order.

Chief Justice Stone-Zsela and Justices Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas and Ginsburg participate. Justice Breyer takes no part; no reason is stated.

#### Public Action

All eight participating Justices support keeping the financial-leave motion pending, reserving financial eligibility, declining a present denial under Rule 39.8, and refusing the proposed future noncriminal-certiorari fee-and-form condition. This is neither a grant nor a denial of financial leave or certiorari. The motion and petition remain pending; no petition poll is announced.

#### Opinion Topology

| Writing | Author | Joined by | Relationship to judgment | Scope joined |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Opinion of the Court | Ginsburg | Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter and Thomas throughout; Stone-Zsela in part | Supports pending financial treatment and refusal of present and prospective restrictions | Eight Justices adopt the financial and current-paper propositions. Stone-Zsela joins pending financial treatment and the present Rule 39.8 showing; seven Justices adopt the record-specific prospective-control ground. |
| Concurrence in part and in the result concerning prospective control | Stone-Zsela | No other Justice | Agrees that no future condition should issue | Joins the financial/current-paper explanation; rests prospective refusal on a broader individual-consideration ground. |

#### Controlling Propositions and Authority

##### Financial eligibility remains reserved

**Holding and authority:** Ginsburg's opinion, joined on this proposition by all seven other participating Justices, keeps the financial motion pending without finding eligibility or ineligibility.

Rule 39.1 requires the prescribed motion and notarized affidavit or declaration under 28 U.S.C. §1746, unless a qualifying appointed-counsel exception applies; no such exception is established here. References to monthly benefits, copying loans and an accompanying affidavit do not establish the required financial particulars or the affidavit's contents. An incomplete evidentiary showing also does not establish that Jones can pay, failed to submit a required document or made a false statement. Pending treatment adds no refiling duty, deadline or default consequence. A later financial ruling requires its own supported basis.

##### The present showing does not establish frivolousness or malice under Rule 39.8

**Holding and authority:** The same eight Justices decline a present Rule 39.8 denial. Martin, Anderson and Attwood require assessment of the particular paper; ordinary pleading failure is not automatically frivolousness.

The media-agreement, compulsory-publication and derivative §1986 theories have substantial defects. Formal assignment and actual appellate review contradict broad allegations of self-assignment and no review. But the petition also identifies distinct objections to actual leave requirements affecting paid filings by litigants representing themselves and to denial of requested appellate briefing. Defects in the media theories do not dispose of those objections. The petition and its appended June 14 district memorandum, transfer order and August 8 Eighth Circuit order distinguish those questions. The alleged proof in nineteen separate appendix volumes is not established by the record described here.

The Court finds neither that the access allegations are true nor that the lower restrictions, recusal ruling or appellate procedure were unlawful. A supported Rule 39.8 denial remains available; the Rule permits rather than compels it. Refusal of this proposed denial establishes neither indigence nor entitlement to review.

##### The proposed future condition lacks independent support

**Holding and authority:** Ginsburg's opinion, joined by Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter and Thomas, applies Martin, Day, Sassower, Anderson and Attwood to require support tied to the applicant, the relevant filing category and the inadequacy of individual treatment before imposing a prospective condition.

The lower courts' abuse findings do not themselves establish those predicates for future noncriminal certiorari submissions here. Preserving paid and criminal routes narrows the proposal but supplies no missing justification. The Court declines the condition on this record. It does not categorically invalidate all prospective controls, require a warning in every case, fix a numerical threshold or demand exhaustion of every conceivable lesser measure.

#### Precedent Treatment and Current-Law Effect

Martin's distinction between the present paper and the independent justification for prospective restrictions is applied with Anderson, Day, Sassower and Attwood. Zatko continues to permit supported denial of a particular frivolous or malicious filing. McDonald and Sindram retain their bounded authority for targeted protection of the judicial process. No prior filing-control holding is overruled or enlarged into categorical immunity.

Assessment of the current paper, financial eligibility, justification for future controls and certiorari remain separate inquiries. The order applies existing filing law and creates no merits holding concerning the underlying suit or its procedural restrictions. The governing provisions are the Rules effective October 2, 1995, and 28 U.S.C. §1915 as then in force. The Court does not adopt the concurrence's broader future-filing rule.

#### Separate Position

Stone-Zsela concurs in the financial and current-paper treatment and in refusal of the prospective condition. He would require individual consideration of future ordinary civil-certiorari submissions by an otherwise financially eligible litigant. Rule 39.8 addresses a frivolous or malicious submission; past abuse does not establish the contents of an unwritten petition. Automatic fee denial based on identity and history risks excluding a later legitimate claim solely because its author cannot pay. Inherent docket authority should implement the Rules' current-paper inquiry while permitting prompt rejection of a submission actually shown defective.

His position goes beyond the Court's record-specific ground. It concerns ordinary civil-certiorari fee treatment, does not decide every extraordinary-writ restriction, vacate unreviewed orders or establish unlimited fee-free litigation in every court, and preserves jurisdictional, timeliness, form and current-paper controls. Individual consideration requires no merits opinion, hearing or elaborate explanation for every filing. Financial eligibility must still be established; once it is established and current frivolousness or malice is not shown, he would grant financial leave and consider certiorari separately. Missing financial proof is not itself ineligibility, and pending treatment grants neither review nor relief against respondents.

#### Mandate, Remedy, and Stage

The financial-leave motion and certiorari petition remain pending. Eligibility is reserved for a supported later determination, with no date set. No direction to refile, payment interval, new warning, sanction, Rule 33.1 payment/form condition, merits remand, terminal petition action or alteration of an existing lawful filing order follows. Future submissions remain subject to ordinary rules and governing law.

**End of entry: Jones v. ABC-TV, filing-management order, February 26, 1996.**

### United States v. Maine et al. (Massachusetts Boundary Case), No. 35, Original, 516 U.S. 365

The United States and Massachusetts jointly seek entry of their January 1996 proposed decree implementing the October 6, 1975 decree, 423 U.S. 1, and the February 25, 1986 boundary decision, 475 U.S. 89. The question is whether the submitted classification and coastal closing lines conform to that existing determination. The motion is within retained original jurisdiction; no lower-court judgment is under review and no new argument date is supplied.

Chief Justice Stone-Zsela and Justices Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer participate. Justice Souter takes no part in consideration or decision of the motion and supplemental decree; no reason is stated.

#### Judgment

#### Controlling Authority and Explanation

The Court's unsigned supplemental decree in No. 35, Original gives the surviving 1975 decree and 1986 Massachusetts boundary determination operative geographic form through the classifications and lines above. The Court retains jurisdiction to effectuate and supplement the decree and the parties' rights.

The 1986 decision accepted Vineyard Sound's inland status, rejected Massachusetts's exception claiming the whole of Nantucket Sound as historic or ancient inland waters, and directed submission of a conforming decree. The parties' joint submission supplies implementing lines for that adjudication. Entry gives the existing determination geographic effect; it does not adjudicate a renewed historic-waters claim or replace approximate coordinates with a new survey. The decree concerns Massachusetts alone and decides no other State's boundary or unrelated private ownership.

#### Precedent Treatment and Current-Law Effect

The October 6, 1975 decree, 423 U.S. 1, is supplemented through its retained implementation authority. The February 25, 1986 Massachusetts boundary decision, 475 U.S. 89, supplies the surviving classifications and direction to submit a conforming decree; its merits determination remains intact. The Court does not reopen those classifications or decide the validity, limits or universal burden of proving ancient title.

The five-line description now governs the Massachusetts coastline for purposes of the 1975 decree, subject to the approximate endpoints and Nantucket Sound indentation exceptions. No new historic-waters test, general ancient-title proof rule, new title or precise-survey finding is adopted.

The January 1996 [joint motion, memorandum and proposed supplemental decree](https://www.supremecourt.gov/pdfs/recordsandbriefs/1000202729/1000202729_019.pdf), proposed-decree paragraphs 1–3, supplies the classification, lines, approximate endpoints and retained-jurisdiction terms. The [1986 Massachusetts boundary opinion](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/USREPORTS-475/pdf/USREPORTS-475-89.pdf), 475 U.S. 89, 105, directed a decree conforming to the Special Master's recommendations. The filed coordinates are approximations, without an independent geographic survey.

#### Mandate, Remedy, and Stage

Entry resolves the joint motion in No. 35, Original. The Court retains jurisdiction to entertain further proceedings, enter orders and issue writs as may from time to time be necessary or advisable to effectuate and supplement the decree and the rights of the respective parties. The original action is not dismissed. No new hearing, survey or filing deadline is directed.

**End of entry: United States v. Maine et al. (Massachusetts Boundary Case), supplemental decree, February 26, 1996.**

### Voting Rights Coalition v. Wilson, petition identifier No. 95-673

Merits decision, February 26, 1996

#### Chronology and Posture

On review of the Ninth Circuit's July 24, 1995 judgment, No. 95-15449, 60 F.3d 1411, affirming a March 2 permanent injunction requiring California to comply with the National Voter Registration Act of 1993 (NVRA) and remanding for implementation. The district court consolidated the injunction hearing with trial under Rule 65(a)(2).

The questions concern Congress's authority to require federal-election registration through motor-vehicle and designated public agencies despite California's Tenth Amendment and retained-qualification objections, including assignment of duties to employees previously performing other work. Presidential-election coverage requires its own constitutional ground. The Governor had directed implementation only to the extent federal funding was available; the resulting noncompliance produced this enforcement dispute. The United States asserts its own §11(a) claim for prospective relief. Chief Justice Stone-Zsela and Justices Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer participate.

#### Judgment

**Affirmed in full, 9–0.**

**Supporting:** Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer.

The permanent injunction, implementation remand and retained appellate jurisdiction stand. California must comply with the Act's federal-election requirements. The district court must impose no burden unauthorized by the Act that impairs California's retained conduct of state elections and must approach implementation with due sensitivity to federalism.

#### Opinion and Authority

O'Connor writes the Opinion of the Court, joined throughout by Stone-Zsela, Stevens, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer. All nine adopt each controlling proposition and its limits. No separate writing issues.

#### Controlling Propositions

##### Congress may require the challenged congressional-election registration duties

The unanimous Court holds that Article I, §4 permits the NVRA's actual registration commands, including distribution, assistance, acceptance and transmittal duties assigned to motor-vehicle, public-assistance and disability-service personnel.

Smiley includes registration within the constitutional power over the manner of congressional elections. Siebold permits partial federal alteration through shared state administration; a wholly federal apparatus is unnecessary. California correctly identifies real commands on employees whose prior duties differed, with consequences for resources and accountability. But Congress's express authority to make or alter registration procedures cannot depend on which office a State previously used. Receiving and transmitting applications remains electoral administration at multipurpose agencies. The in-home requirement likewise concerns registration services where the designated disability office already serves the person at home.

The duties are not upheld as costless, trivial or voluntary. California's disputed estimates of $18 million and 58 additional employees are not findings adopted by the Court; enacted legislative findings remain distinct from findings about California. New York's bar against the ordinary-commerce command to regulate private waste generators or assume their obligations remains intact. The distinct constitutional allocation for congressional elections supplies the authority absent there. California's preference for reimbursement does not excuse compliance. No general power to direct state administration follows.

##### Presidential registration rests on a separate constitutional basis

The same unanimous opinion sustains the challenged safeguards for California's chosen popular presidential election under the national protective authority recognized by Burroughs, implemented through necessary-and-proper legislation. Article I, §4 alone does not cover presidential elections.

Burroughs upheld corrupt-practices regulation of political committees, not these agency assignments. Its principle applies here because registration opportunities, eligibility attestations and reliable rolls protect eligible participation in selection of the national executive. The Act neither chooses the State's method of appointing electors nor selects or allocates them. The holding concerns these registration safeguards for the chosen popular election. It establishes neither unlimited authority to redesign presidential selection nor identical scope for the congressional and presidential grants.

##### Registration procedure is distinct from substantive voter qualifications

The same unanimous opinion rejects the facial contention that the prescribed registration methods themselves redefine who is qualified to vote. Article I, §2 and the Seventeenth Amendment retain their distinct qualification rules.

The Act requires eligibility statements, including citizenship, and signed attestations under penalty of perjury; state election officials continue assessing eligibility. The statute nonetheless regulates permissible form information and maintenance procedures, and its federal mail form may not require notarization or other formal authentication. Term Limits preserves the distinction between genuine election administration and additional personal qualifications, but its candidate holding does not decide every voter-qualification dispute. Concrete controversies about required information, eligibility decisions or list maintenance remain open.

##### The injunction has an independent public-enforcement foundation

The same unanimous opinion holds that §11(a) authorizes the Attorney General's declaratory and injunctive action against the established categorical noncompliance. Private §11(b) notice conditions do not govern that action.

California's immunity objection cannot erase the United States' independently authorized prospective enforcement by treating it as a private damages suit. The Court decides no damages liability or general private-enforcement immunity question and makes no finding that separate private notice conditions were met. Implementation's complexity requires fidelity to the statute and existing remand limits; it does not justify vacating a permanent injunction without an identified unauthorized command. None is established here. The Fourteenth/Fifteenth Amendment enforcement alternative is preserved but not reached because the distinct congressional and presidential grounds suffice.

##### Operative statutory limits

The affirmance preserves these enacted limits of the sustained duties; it does not create new conditions. The enacted duties and exceptions appear in Pub. L. 103-31, 107 Stat. 77–89. California's applicable effective date was January 1, 1995.

- **Federal-election scope and exemptions.** Section 4(b) exempts a State with no federal-election registration requirement under law continuously effective on and after March 11, 1993. It also exempts a State permitting every voter to register at the polling place during a general federal election under law continuously effective on and after that date, or enacted by that date to take effect on enactment of the NVRA and continuously maintained thereafter. These conditions provide no present opt-out for California.
- **Driver's-license applications and address changes.** Under §5(a), a driver's-license or renewal application serves as a federal voter-registration application unless the applicant fails to sign the registration application; a submitted registration application updates prior registration. Under the distinct §5(d) exception, a driver's-license change-of-address form serves as a voter-registration address change unless the registrant states it is not for that purpose. The license-registration portion may not demand duplicate license information except a second signature or information necessary for the required eligibility statement and attestation. It may require only the minimum information needed to prevent duplicates and permit eligibility assessment and administration.
- **Designated offices and assistance.** Section 7 requires designation of all public-assistance offices and offices providing state-funded programs primarily serving persons with disabilities, as well as other registration offices. Federal and nongovernmental offices may be designated with their agreement. Designated offices must distribute, assist with and accept forms for transmittal; a designated disability office providing services at home must provide registration services there. Applicants may refuse assistance. Distribution with a public-assistance or disability-service application, recertification, renewal or address-change form may be omitted when the applicant declines registration in writing. Applicants who do not decline receive the same degree of assistance as with the office's own forms unless they refuse it. Failure to mark either registration-choice box counts as declination for the assistance provision. Staff may not seek to influence or display political preference, discourage registration, or suggest that registration choice affects services or benefits. Declination and source information retain statutory confidentiality protections.
- **Transmittal and retained decisions.** Sections 5(e) and 7(d) ordinarily require transmittal to the proper state election official within ten days after acceptance. When an application is accepted within five days before the last registration day for an election, transmittal is due within five days after acceptance. These statutory duties are distinct from the district court's implementation-plan direction. State officials retain registration and roll decisions within the statute. The decision does not require identical federal and state rolls or determine a particular implementation practice.

#### Precedent Treatment and Current-Law Effect

Smiley and Siebold apply to registration and shared administration under the express congressional-election allocation. New York's ordinary-commerce structural holding remains effective, distinguished by that allocation without converting every federal interest into authority to command States. Burroughs's national protective principle separately sustains the challenged presidential-registration safeguards while preserving the State's choice of appointment method. Term Limits supplies the administration/qualification distinction within its actual candidate-rule scope; it is not expanded into a comprehensive voter-qualification decision. No prior holding is overruled.

The challenged agency-registration duties are valid within their federal-election scope, with distinct constitutional grounds for congressional and presidential coverage. California must perform the prescribed work despite its reimbursement condition. Substantive eligibility and retained state-election administration remain distinct from federally regulated registration procedure. The facial decision preserves concrete statutory and constitutional implementation challenges; it does not adjudicate a later implementation controversy or decide the alternative Fourteenth/Fifteenth Amendment theory.

#### Mandate, Remedy, and Stage

The permanent injunction stands, and the case proceeds through the already ordered implementation remand. The Ninth Circuit panel retains jurisdiction over subsequent proceedings and appeals. No provision is severed or new administrative process required. The lower order's original ten-day plan requirement is not restarted, and compliance with that requirement is not presumed.

Separately reserved coalition claims concerning implementation remain for their proper proceedings. Dismissal without prejudice of the Federal Election Commission and its chair is not converted into a merits judgment. The lower judgment's denial of appellate costs to every party remains undisturbed. No new plan, costs award, state-election redesign or finding about a later administrative practice is ordered.

**End of entry: Voting Rights Coalition v. Wilson, merits decision, February 26, 1996.**

### Matsushita Electric Industrial Co. v. Epstein, No. 94-1809

Merits decision, February 27, 1996

#### Chronology and Posture

516 U.S. 367. Argued November 27, 1995; decided February 27, 1996. On writ of certiorari to the Ninth Circuit, 50 F.3d 644. The question is whether §1738 requires recognition of a Delaware class-settlement judgment releasing securities claims within exclusive federal jurisdiction under Exchange Act §27.

The Delaware acquisition litigation began in September 1990; the federal Epstein action began December 3, 1990, before either proposed settlement. The first settlement was rejected. The second provided a $2 million gross fund reduced by fees and an opt-out opportunity; the final award for fees and expenses was $250,000. The state judgment expressly incorporated a release identifying the pending federal litigation, found adequate representation and due process, and was affirmed by the Delaware Supreme Court. Notice, discovery, objections and an adversarial hearing occurred; the constitutional adequacy and binding effect of that process remain disputed.

The Ninth Circuit refused recognition because the federal claims rested on different underlying facts and could not have been extinguished by adjudication of the state claims. It reserved releases sharing the same predicate facts and did not decide the fact-specific constitutional alternative. Petitioners request immediate dismissal of covered non-opt-out complaints; respondents defend the appellate judgment and invoke representative-authority, due-process and binding-operation objections. Valid opt-outs, including the identified opt-out litigants outside this review, remain excluded.

Chief Justice Stone-Zsela and Justices Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer participate at argument and decision.

#### Judgment

**Reversed and remanded, 7–2.**

**Supporting:** Stone-Zsela, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas and Breyer.

**Opposing:** Stevens and Ginsburg, who would vacate and remand for an initial determination of Delaware preclusive effect as well as the unresolved constitutional and collateral-review questions.

Ordinary Delaware recognition is established, but the Court does not direct immediate dismissal of the federal complaints. The lower federal court must give the judgment its ordinary Delaware effect, subject to properly presented unresolved constitutional and binding objections and the antecedent extent to which those objections remain available collaterally. The Court orders no wholesale invalidation of the settlement.

#### Controlling Propositions and Authority

##### Section 1738 governs this kind of judgment

A state judicial judgment incorporating a class settlement falls within §1738 even when it releases claims subject to exclusive federal adjudicatory jurisdiction. The federal court begins with the effect the rendering State gives that judgment; class form, consent disposition and exclusive jurisdiction alone supply no categorical exemption. All nine Justices adopt this rule in Part I.

**Controlling explanation:** Marrese requires the rendering-state-law inquiry and expressly leaves settlement releases distinct from the competency limit governing adjudicated claims. The Ninth Circuit could not replace that inquiry with a federal rule equating inability to adjudicate a claim with inability to approve its release. Exclusive federal jurisdiction likewise does not by itself establish that a lawfully authorized representative can never compromise a related federal claim. But an owner's capacity to settle and a representative's authority to bind absent persons remain different. Silence following notice is not individually negotiated consent, and expansive release language cannot supply constitutionally adequate representation where it is lacking. This conclusion neither grants state courts jurisdiction to adjudicate Exchange Act liability nor validates every asserted release.

##### This judgment receives ordinary Delaware preclusive effect

Part II, joined by Stone-Zsela, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas and Breyer, determines the ordinary state-law recognition question in petitioners' favor. The incorporated release and affirmed judgment cannot lose that effect merely because a federal litigant alleges state-law error in approving the settlement; federal constitutional binding limits remain unresolved.

**Controlling explanation:** Nottingham permits an authorized release of exclusively federal claims within its identical-factual-predicate and representative limits. The judgment here does not merely acknowledge a separate private bargain: it incorporates the release and expressly covers the identified federal actions. Final approval and state affirmance have the ordinary force Delaware gives them. The objection that the lower federal court should first examine state law does not require treating an affirmed judgment as open to renewed collateral correction of alleged state-law errors. Marrese supplies the correct inquiry, not an inflexible requirement that every application begin below.

This holding does not replace Nottingham's identical-factual-predicate requirement with a rule permitting release of anything connected to the same acquisition. Prezant's actual Rule 23 and express adequacy requirements remain part of Delaware law. The Court makes no new finding about tendering shareholders, counsel's incentives, or full and fair determination of constitutional adequacy. Ordinary state-law error and a legally available objection that a person was never constitutionally bound remain distinct.

##### Section 27 does not impliedly partially repeal §1738 here

Part III, joined by Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas and Breyer, rejects the asserted statutory exception for this category of judgment. Exclusive federal authority to adjudicate Exchange Act liability does not prohibit a state court with jurisdiction over the state action from approving an authorized representative release.

**Controlling explanation:** Marrese and Kremer require a supported basis for displacing §1738. Section 27's exclusive-jurisdiction language and the interest in uniform federal adjudication do not establish incompatibility with recognition of a lawful settlement. A settlement court may assess federal claim value without adjudicating federal securities liability. The release/adjudication distinction therefore preserves both statutes.

Recognition is also distinct from direct coercive interference with a pending federal case. Nottingham's refusal to enjoin such litigation does not invalidate every release, and the present judgment's incorporation does not validate every injunction or contempt remedy. No state wording removes federal jurisdiction. An independently preserved objection to the order's coercive operation remains subject to its own legally supported treatment; the Court neither decides it nor guarantees that it defeats recognition. Ginsburg agrees that exclusivity alone does not negate settlement capacity but reserves this further statutory-exception determination.

##### Constitutional representation and unresolved review questions limit the remand

All nine Justices adopt Part IV-A: §1738 cannot bind an absent member through constitutionally inadequate representation, and notice, objection opportunities and opt-out rights do not independently cure that failure. The actual constitutional adequacy and binding application remain undecided, so no immediate dismissal is ordered. In Part IV-B, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer leave the complete permissible scope of collateral review, including the asserted full-and-fair determination of adequacy, for lawful consideration below; the Chief Justice states his own structural-failure standard separately.

**Controlling explanation:** Hansberry and Shutts make representation a constitutional prerequisite, distinct from notice and exclusion rights. The state court's express findings, discovery and adversarial hearing matter; they are not erased by respondents' criticism. But the Ninth Circuit did not decide the fact-specific constitutional alternative, and a recital does not itself answer every disputed constitutional dimension. Properly presented unresolved grounds and the antecedent extent of their collateral availability may be considered. This remand finds neither that representation failed nor that every adequacy question may be relitigated despite a full and fair prior determination.

The Court decides no underlying tender-offer liability, private right under §§14(d)(6)–(7), new federal class, damages or unpresented securities count. Traditional contract consent as an independent dispositive theory remains unadjudicated; any legally applicable state contract approach would operate through §1738, without a federal fiction of individually negotiated assent. Valid opt-outs remain outside the judgment's release.

#### Precedent Treatment and Current-Law Effect

- Marrese v. American Academy of Orthopaedic Surgeons, 470 U.S. 373: applies state law first and preserves its settlement-law reservation; the adjudicated-claim competency limit is not mechanically transferred to a release.
- Kremer v. Chemical Construction Corp., 456 U.S. 461: applies §1738 finality subject to due process and the actual scope of matters determined; no blanket authorization to retry state-law error follows.
- Hansberry v. Lee, 311 U.S. 32, and Phillips Petroleum Co. v. Shutts, 472 U.S. 797: preserve adequate representation as a constitutional prerequisite distinct from notice and opt-out protections; actual inadequacy is not found here.
- Kokkonen v. Guardian Life Insurance Co. of America, 511 U.S. 375: its distinction between a separate settlement and an obligation lawfully incorporated into an order remains intact. Its federal postdismissal-enforcement holding does not resolve state representative authority or make incorporation self-validating.
- Peacock v. Thomas, 516 U.S. 349: its independent-authority limit for new-debtor liability remains distinct from recognition of an existing representative judgment.
- Central Bank of Denver, N.A. v. First Interstate Bank of Denver, N.A., 511 U.S. 164: the confined affirmative-assistance action remains governing securities law. It establishes neither a tender-rule private right nor the value, liability or retrospective invalidity at issue here.

The incorporated judgment receives ordinary Delaware recognition notwithstanding exclusive federal jurisdiction over adjudicating the released Exchange Act claims. Section 27 supplies no implied partial repeal for this judgment category. No universal same-transaction release rule, automatic adequacy finding or state power to enjoin federal adjudication follows.

The Chief Justice's complete structural-failure standard is separate and noncontrolling. The three-Justice fuller representation discussion does not enlarge the Court's common constitutional minimum or resolve the reserved full-and-fair-litigation question.

#### Separate Positions Relevant to the Decision

**Stone-Zsela, concurring.** The Chief Justice joins reversal, ordinary Delaware recognition, the §27 holding, and the constitutional minimum and unresolved case application. He would give the judgment the effect §1738 requires unless respondents establish a fundamental defect in jurisdiction, notice, representation or binding operation. Hansberry and Shutts permit collateral attack where constitutionally deficient notice, a disabling conflict, collusion, or representation tantamount to no representation deprived absent members of a genuine adversarial representative. Ordinary disagreement with strategy, valuation or settlement terms does not establish that structural failure. Exclusive federal jurisdiction bars state adjudication of the federal cause, but not its settlement through a judgment the State was competent to enter. He therefore does not join the Court's complete reservation of the collateral-attack standard. He finds neither actual adequacy nor inadequacy here and supports the remand within those limits. No other Justice joins this complete standard.

**Ginsburg, concurring in part and dissenting in part.** Ginsburg and Stevens would vacate and remand for the lower federal courts to determine Delaware preclusive effect in the first instance, without affirming the Ninth Circuit's overbroad federal rule. They accept the judgment's incorporated release but would first examine Nottingham's identical-factual-predicate limit, Prezant's representative requirements and the judgment's finality. General state power to approve releases does not settle that application. Ginsburg additionally reserves the further §27 exception question until the state-effect predicate is established; Stevens does not share that reservation and joins the Court's nonrepeal holding.

In the representation discussion, joined by Stevens and Souter, Ginsburg explains that constitutionally adequate representation must exist throughout the litigation and that a final class judgment remains susceptible to a legally proper collateral challenge for failure of that prerequisite. Notice and opt-outs are distinct protections. Hansberry, Shutts and Prezant support attention to actual representative authority and interests. The existing findings and hearing do not establish the absence of a possible constitutional objection; neither do the objections prove failure. Respondents' inadequacy contention and petitioners' response that the issue was fully and fairly litigated both remain open. Souter joins this explanation while supporting ordinary Delaware recognition and reversal; he does not join the first-instance state-law remand or Ginsburg's §27 reservation.

The settlement history and state disposition appear in *In re MCA, Inc. Shareholders Litigation*, 598 A.2d 687 and 1993 WL 43024, and the Delaware Supreme Court's September 21, 1993 affirmance. *Nottingham Partners v. ### Norfolk & Western Railway Co. v. Hiles, No. 95-6

Merits decision, February 27, 1996

#### Chronology and Posture

516 U.S. 400. Argued January 8, 1996; decided February 27, 1996. On writ of certiorari to the Illinois Appellate Court, Fifth District, 268 Ill. App. 3d 561, 644 N.E.2d 508, after the Illinois Supreme Court denied review. The question is whether the Safety Appliance Act makes the railroad liable as a matter of law for Hiles's injury while manually realigning an off-center drawbar before coupling.

Hiles injured his back on July 18, 1990 while he and a coworker moved the drawbar. The other car remained four or five car lengths away; no impact had been attempted. The trial court directed statutory liability and excluded nondefect evidence as irrelevant under its rule. A jury awarded $492,500, and the appellate court affirmed. The railroad seeks reversal and remand. No independent ordinary-negligence count under FELA was pleaded.

Chief Justice Stone-Zsela and Justices Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer participate at argument and decision.

#### Judgment

**Reversed and remanded, 9–0.**

**Supporting:** Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer.

The Court reverses the Illinois Appellate Court's judgment. The directed statutory-liability ruling and its dependent $492,500 damages foundation cannot stand. Further proceedings must apply the statutory preparation/performance distinction. The Court orders neither judgment for the railroad nor a guaranteed new trial or recovery; it supplies no new negligence count and revives no forfeited issue.

#### Opinion and Controlling Propositions

Justice Thomas writes the Opinion of the Court. Chief Justice Stone-Zsela and Justices Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Ginsburg and Breyer join in full. No separate opinion is issued.

**Common authority:** Thomas's opinion and all eight complete joins identified above directly support each following holding, its application and limits. No combination of separate rationales is required.

##### Ordinary preparation is distinct from failure of required performance

Former 45 U.S.C. §2 imposes an absolute duty that coupling equipment perform its statutory function when properly set; ordinary, nondefective lateral displacement requiring drawbar alignment does not alone violate that duty. The Act does not categorically prohibit every preparatory task between cars or separately require automatic-realignment equipment.

**Controlling explanation:** The governing text required couplers coupling automatically by impact and capable of uncoupling without workers going between the car ends. Affolder recognizes that automatic coupling presupposes proper setting; Carter preserves that qualification while rejecting an ordinary-care defense. The same distinction applies to preparing a nondefective drawbar as to opening a knuckle. O'Donnell's absolute-performance holding remains effective when an engaged coupler fails.

Hiles correctly emphasizes that the Act protects workers from manual-coupling dangers and that mechanically sound parts can still fail to perform. But that principle does not make every injury during preparatory work a statutory violation. Lateral play accommodates curves, and the enacted duty does not itself require a mechanism eliminating every manual alignment task. Neither customary practice nor a general appeal to safety decides whether this appliance was awaiting ordinary preparation or had failed its required function. The 1994 recodification at 49 U.S.C. §20302(a)(1)(A) adds no substantive requirement governing this 1990 injury.

##### Performance-based proof and ordinary-setting proof remain distinct

An employee may establish a violation by failure to perform the required function without proving negligence or identifying a particular mechanical defect. The railroad may establish that the complained-of condition instead resulted from ordinary nondefective misalignment before proper setting; once a statutory violation contributing to injury is proved, due care, inspection, later successful operation, contributory negligence or assumption of risk does not erase it.

**Controlling explanation:** Affolder and Carter make actual performance decisive within the proper-setting qualification. The railroad's permissible explanation concerns whether the statutory duty was breached; it is not a safe harbor for careful inspection after a breach. Nor can calling a malfunction preparation excuse it. A statutory violation must have contributed to the employee's injury within the applicable FELA remedial framework, but causation does not supply a missing violation. No attempted impact is invariably required: the Act does not demand a futile or damaging collision before an otherwise provable failure may be shown. Conversely, an attempted coupling would not by itself establish Hiles's right to a directed liability ruling on this record.

##### The automatic liability direction requires reversal without an appellate finding for the railroad

Injury during realignment does not establish statutory liability as a matter of law. The reason, extent and legal classification of this displacement remain for lawful consideration under the correct distinction, together with any properly preserved performance and causation issues.

**Controlling explanation:** The general need for lateral movement does not prove that this particular condition was ordinary or nondefective. The inspection account and asserted AAR compliance were excluded affidavit and proffer evidence, not findings. The difficulty moving the bar, the uncoupling evidence and the disputed curve inference also cannot be resolved merely from the trial court's erroneous rule. Wagner and Parker preserve assessment of the actual displacement and performance evidence. The absence of a prior impact and the absence of an identified broken component do not justify this Court's entering final judgment for the railroad. Reversal removes the directed-liability foundation of the damages judgment while leaving only procedurally available issues for further proceedings. It neither manufactures the unpleaded negligence claim nor guarantees another trial.

#### Precedent Treatment and Current-Law Effect

- Affolder v. New York, Chicago & St. Louis Railroad Co., 339 U.S. 96: applies its proper-setting qualification to ordinary nondefective drawbar alignment, retaining absolute performance once the appliance is properly set.
- Carter v. Atlanta & St. Andrews Bay Railway Co., 338 U.S. 430: preserves performance-based proof without negligence or identification of a particular defect and the separate causal requirement; ordinary FELA comparative-negligence principles do not excuse a proven statutory violation.
- O'Donnell v. Elgin, Joliet & Eastern Railway Co., 338 U.S. 384: leaves intact the absolute duty when an engaged coupler breaks; preparation supplies no defense to such an established failure.
- San Antonio & Aransas Pass Railway Co. v. Wagner, 241 U.S. 476, and Atlantic City Railroad Co. v. Parker, 242 U.S. 56: preserve consideration of the actual evidence bearing on drawbar displacement and failure, rather than making every realignment injury conclusive.
- Consolidated Rail Corp. v. Gottshall, 512 U.S. 532: its distinct negligent-emotional-injury rule remains unchanged; its negligence and zone-of-danger requirements do not govern this statutory-performance claim.

The proper-setting qualification extends to ordinary nondefective drawbar alignment. An injury during that preparation is insufficient by itself; proven failure of required performance remains actionable without negligence or identification of a particular mechanical defect. Violation and causal contribution remain separate. The decision establishes no general exemption for misaligned drawbars, inspection safe harbor, universal impact prerequisite, new technology mandate or broader operating prohibition.

The [Illinois appellate decision, 644 N.E.2d 508](https://cite.case.law/ne2d/644/508/), describes the accident and directed-verdict posture. Joint Appendix 10–11 and 21 contain the excluded inspection affidavit and proffer, not jury findings of compliance; pages 15–16 describe movement difficulty and the other car's distance, and pages 22–25 contain the ruling and remaining proffer. The [1994 codification of §20302](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/USCODE-1994-title49/pdf/USCODE-1994-title49-subtitleV-partA-chap203-sec20302.pdf) preserves the distinct statutory formulation and exceptions without changing the rule governing this injury.

#### Mandate, Remedy, and Stage

The case returns through the Illinois Appellate Court for proceedings under the corrected statutory rule. The directed-liability ruling and dependent damages foundation fall. Remaining evidence, statutory-performance and causal issues are preserved only insofar as properly presented and procedurally available. No particular further hearing, new trial, damages award or final railroad judgment is ordered, and no further Supreme Court date is set.

**End of entry: Norfolk & Western Railway Co. v. Hiles, Merits decision, February 27, 1996.**

### United States v. Chesapeake & Potomac Telephone Co. of Virginia, and National Cable Television Assn., Inc. v. Bell Atlantic Corp., Nos. 94-1893 and 94-1900

Post-argument statutory-change disposition, February 27, 1996

#### Chronology and Posture

516 U.S. 415. Argued December 6, 1995. On writs of certiorari to the Fourth Circuit, 42 F.3d 181. The two dockets are disposed of together.

The Fourth Circuit affirmed a judgment declaring former 47 U.S.C. §533(b)'s telephone-company cable restriction unconstitutional. The amended October 7, 1993 District Court order enjoined enforcement against the named plaintiffs. After argument, Congress repealed the challenged provision. The immediate question is whether the litigation remains live and what disposition should precede any constitutional ruling.

The Government seeks vacatur and dismissal, asserting that repeal ends the prospective dispute and that ordinary cable service is immediately available even over telephone wires. Respondents contend that their proposed Alexandria open-video service remains impeded pending FCC rules; alternatively, they oppose vacatur because the Government procured repeal and seek to retain the injunction for a proposed Rule 60(b) clarification. The Government disputes the asserted continuing prohibition and treats the successor dispute as distinct. No clarification has been granted. The complaint does not expressly demand damages, and the Government describes it as seeking no retrospective relief.

All nine Justices participate. A per curiam opinion states the Court's limited procedural ground. No separate writing or individual join is announced.

#### Public Action

The Court vacates the Fourth Circuit judgment in both dockets and remands to that court to consider whether the cases are moot in light of the Telecommunications Act of 1996.

|---|---|---|---|
| No. 94-1893, United States v. Chesapeake & Potomac Telephone Co. of Virginia | Fourth Circuit judgment vacated | No individual lineup or numerical tally disclosed | Remand to the Fourth Circuit to consider mootness. |

The Court neither dismisses the actions now nor vacates the District Court judgment. It does not extend the old injunction to the new statute, guarantee amendment or relief, or affirm either statute's constitutionality.

#### Controlling Proposition and Authority

##### Intervening repeal requires reconsideration of the surviving controversy

Under 28 U.S.C. §2106, the material statutory change and unresolved effect on these claims justify vacating the appellate judgment and remanding for consideration of mootness before constitutional adjudication. The per curiam opinion of the Court supplies authority for this case-specific disposition; it establishes neither final mootness nor a categorical entitlement to equitable vacatur.

**Controlling explanation:** Section 302(b)(1) repealed the provision adjudicated below. Church of Scientology requires attention to meaningful remaining relief; Northeastern Florida asks whether a successor actually continues the challenged injury. Neither makes repeal conclusive or treats every dispute about the same service as the same controversy.

The parties dispute the new regime's effect. Communications Act §653(a)(1), 47 U.S.C. §573(a)(1), permits a local exchange carrier to offer cable service through a complying open video system. Its separate permission for a cable operator or other person is qualified by permissible Commission regulations. Reduced regulatory burdens require certification of compliance with subsection (b) regulations and Commission approval. Section 651(a)(3), 47 U.S.C. §571(a)(3), applies ordinary Title VI to nonradio, non-common-carriage programming unless provided through an open video system with approved certification. The Commission has six months to complete the specified rulemaking, including reconsideration. It must publish notice of receipt of a certification and act to approve or disapprove it within ten days after receipt. Section 302(b)(3)'s termination of video-dialtone rules does not require termination of an already approved system. These distinctions establish neither a six-month blanket prohibition nor this proposed system's present compliance.

Bancorp separates authority to dispose of a judgment from equitable entitlement to erase it. Respondents' voluntary-surrender argument and the Government's repeal argument therefore require consideration in their actual setting. Today's appellate vacatur rests on the enacted change and unresolved threshold application, not on an assumed final mootness finding or automatic Munsingwear relief. The Fourth Circuit can assess the remaining controversy and determine what further proceedings are necessary. The former statute's constitutional merits, the successor scheme's constitutionality, ultimate treatment of the trial judgment, and entitlement to any proposed relief remain undecided.

#### Precedent Treatment and Current-Law Effect

- Church of Scientology of California v. United States, 506 U.S. 9: applies the requirement to examine meaningful partial relief, without establishing entitlement to relief.
- Northeastern Florida Chapter, AGC v. Jacksonville, 508 U.S. 656: applies the distinction between actual continuing injury and a replacement that ends it; the old injunction does not automatically extend.
- U.S. Bancorp Mortgage Co. v. Bonner Mall Partnership, 513 U.S. 18: preserves the separate equitable inquiry. Its ordinary voluntary-settlement rule, exceptional-circumstances qualification and distinction for events outside the movant's choice remain intact.
- United States v. Munsingwear, Inc., 340 U.S. 36: its judgment-clearing remedy does not establish the antecedent mootness finding missing here.
- Anderson v. Green, 513 U.S. 557: its lost-approval and contingent-program setting does not establish mootness or trial-judgment vacatur in these cases.
- Lawrence v. Chater, 516 U.S. 163: preserves independently justified reconsideration and the receiving court's judicial responsibility; its petition-stage rule is not mechanically extended to this disposition.

The repealed restriction cannot be treated as unchanged governing law. The appellate judgment no longer stands, and the Fourth Circuit must assess whether the original controversy and meaningful lawful relief survive under the enacted regime. The Court creates no new speech tier, successor-statute constitutional rule, or automatic rule awarding or denying vacatur after legislative change. Existing mootness and equitable-vacatur law remains applicable within its stated limits.

The [Fourth Circuit opinion, 42 F.3d 181](https://openjurist.org/42/f3d/181/chesapeake-and-potomac-telephone-company-of-virginia-v-united-states), the amended final order and the complaint describe the original claims and named-plaintiff injunction. The post-repeal positions appear in the Government's February 8 suggestion of mootness, respondents' February 8 response and the Government's February reply. [Public Law 104-104, §302](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/PLAW-104publ104/html/PLAW-104publ104.htm), contains the repeal and new regulatory provisions. The complaint's description of a proposed system establishes neither current operation nor approved certification; the Court makes no such finding.

#### Mandate, Remedy, and Stage

Both cases return to the Fourth Circuit for consideration of mootness. That court may obtain appropriate further District Court proceedings if needed; the Court does not require a further remand. If no live controversy remains, the Fourth Circuit must direct appropriate dismissal and address the remaining orders under applicable vacatur doctrine. Otherwise, further proceedings must remain confined to the surviving dispute. The trial judgment and injunction receive no present Supreme Court vacatur, extension or merits affirmance. No hearing, amended claim, FCC approval or further Supreme Court date is prescribed.

### Action for Children's Television v. FCC, with Pacifica Foundation v. FCC, D.C. Circuit Nos. 93-1092 and 93-1100

Merits decision, March 4, 1996

#### Chronology and Posture

On review under 28 U.S.C. §1254(1) of the June 30, 1995 en banc D.C. Circuit judgment, 58 F.3d 654.

The questions concern the safe-harbor structure, unequal treatment of broadcasters and administrative support for restrictions on protected indecent broadcasting. Section 16(a) of the Public Telecommunications Act of 1992 required prohibition from 6 a.m. to 10 p.m. on any day for public radio or television stations signing off at or before midnight, and from 6 a.m. to midnight on any day for every other radio or television broadcasting station. It required APA §553 procedures and final regulations within 180 days of enactment. Section 16(b) repealed the earlier all-day provision; §16 took effect August 26, 1992.

The court of appeals rejected the distinction and ordered a uniform 10 p.m.–6 a.m. harbor, leaving a 6 a.m.–10 p.m. restriction. The Government did not petition or cross-petition to restore midnight. Both petitioners challenge the surviving sixteen-hour rule. ACT also presented an independent vagueness argument; this decision addresses the schedule and its contextual burden without independently invalidating the generic definition or deciding any particular broadcast sanction.

All nine Justices participate: Chief Justice Stone-Zsela and Justices Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer.

#### Judgment

| Judgment component | Disposition and vote | Supporting Justices | Other positions | Remedy |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Existing uniform nighttime protection and rejection of the favored-station distinction | Preserved, 9–0 | Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg, Breyer | No opposition | No restoration of the midnight restraint or original distinction. |
| Final invalidity and mandatory removal of the 6 a.m.–10 p.m. schedule | Approval set aside; mandatory set-aside relief supported 5–4 | Stone-Zsela: vacate approval; O'Connor, Kennedy, Thomas, Ginsburg: reverse approval. All five require removal of the rule. | Souter: vacate approval and reconsider without deciding validity. Stevens, Scalia, Breyer: affirm. | Remand through the D.C. Circuit to set aside the corresponding FCC rule and prevent enforcement of §16(a)'s dependent command to the extent it requires this invalid schedule. |

Souter's separate vacatur is not a sixth vote for invalidity or mandatory removal. The Court selects no replacement hour and revives no repealed all-day prohibition. The remainder of the 1992 Act and independently lawful FCC authority are not invalidated; the agency may not reproduce the invalid restriction under another label. The mandate preserves the nighttime protection and decides no individual sanction, damages claim, new technology implementation or broadcaster's entitlement to a defense.

#### Opinion Topology

| Writing | Author | Joined by | Relationship to judgment | Scope joined |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Opinion of the Court, Part I | Ginsburg | Stone-Zsela, O'Connor, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas | Six Justices join | Scope, protected-speech standard, independent review and continuing-precedent propositions. |
| Opinion of the Court, Parts II–III | Ginsburg | Stone-Zsela, O'Connor, Kennedy, Thomas | Five Justices join final invalidity and mandatory removal | The mandatory schedule and direction to remove it, with the stated limits; common operative relief preserves the distinct vacatur/reversal descriptions. |

| Dissent | Stevens | Scalia, Breyer | Would affirm the actual uniform schedule on the existing record | Contextual qualifications and the favorable nighttime protection remain preserved. |

#### Controlling Propositions and Authority

##### Protected broadcasting and the applicable review

Nonobscene indecent expression is protected. A restriction selecting that content must be carefully tailored to a compelling interest, with independent review of its necessity and means in light of broadcasting's actual characteristics. Protecting children and assisting parental supervision are compelling interests; their importance does not establish that this schedule is necessary. Ginsburg's Part I, joined by Stone-Zsela, O'Connor, Kennedy, Souter and Thomas, supplies six votes.

**Controlling explanation:** Pacifica permits the particular contextual regulation it sustained; it does not validate every general daily restriction. Sable requires protection of adult access and careful tailoring while preserving material differences between broadcasting and telephone services. Legislative predictive judgments receive substantial consideration, but asserted harm and the selected response remain subject to independent review. Neither scientific certainty nor a particular legislative report is indispensable. Turner preserves that judicial responsibility; its intermediate test for content-neutral cable carriage is not the test for this content-selected rule.

##### The mandatory sixteen-hour schedule fails on this record

The prescribed 6 a.m.–10 p.m. exclusion is insufficiently tailored to justify its broad burden on protected adult broadcasting. Ginsburg's Part II, joined by Stone-Zsela, O'Connor, Kennedy and Thomas, supplies five direct votes for final invalidity.

**Controlling explanation:** The record establishes substantial child access, including late audiences, and difficulties of supervision and rapid tuning. It does not adequately connect those circumstances to excluding this broad category of protected expression across sixteen hours, media and contexts. A child somewhere in the audience cannot by itself settle the necessary degree of adult exclusion. Late-night audiences, taping and purchased alternatives do not adequately answer practical access for adults relying on free broadcasting or the burden on parental choice. The FCC considered alternatives; the defect is their insufficient treatment and the sweep of the resulting rule, not the absence of any evidence or analysis.

The agency's contextual definition, attention to reasonable licensee judgment and clinical or bona fide news applications limit the burden but create no blanket news or serious-merit exemption. Its tentative defense permits market-wide evidence of no appreciable child audience at the relevant time; ordinary station/program ratings remain inadequate until convincing evidence establishes relevance despite grazing. Those qualifications do not cure the schedule's general fit, establish an automatic low-rating exemption or decide any individual defense.

Audience measures remain distinct. Receiver ownership shows opportunity; aggregate viewing does not establish actual indecent-program exposure. Radio quarter-hour audiences are not cumulative reach, and limited under-twelve radio data cannot be supplied by inference. Adult presence is not effective supervision; public radio's small teen listening share and weekly reach are not zero child audience. The holding requires neither proof of harm from every program nor flawless alternatives, and does not declare all broadcasting interchangeable with other media.

##### The relief removes this rule while preserving lawful authority

The appellate approval must be set aside and the D.C. Circuit must remove the corresponding rule and prevent §16(a)'s dependent command from requiring the invalid schedule. Ginsburg's Part III, joined by Stone-Zsela, O'Connor, Kennedy and Thomas, supplies five votes for that operative direction. The existing 10 p.m.–6 a.m. protection remains intact with all nine Justices' agreement.

**Controlling explanation:** Extending the 10 p.m. opening removed the unjustified classification but did not answer petitioners' independent breadth challenge. The Government's absence of a petition forecloses restoring the harsher decree through an alternative defense. The constitutional defect calls for removing the challenged mandate, not judicial selection of a new national opening hour. Section 16(b)'s repeal remains effective. Section 1464, Pacifica and ordinary obscenity enforcement retain their lawful scope; more confined FCC action must satisfy those authorities and today's ruling. No unexamined severability provision or statutory exception creates a substitute schedule. A renewed-record remand leaving validity open does not command five votes, and no Marks aggregation makes Souter's separate position the governing remedy.

The Court does not independently resolve facial vagueness, grant an automatic serious-merit exemption, adjudicate any enforcement sanction, overrule Pacifica or Red Lion, require station-by-station permission hearings, or prescribe a new multifactor test. Section 551 creates no present device-based factual ground for the judgment and does not automatically repeal §16, moot the case or require remand.

#### Precedent Treatment and Current-Law Effect

- FCC v. Pacifica Foundation, 438 U.S. 726: preserves its narrow contextual broadcast holding and distinguishes it from this general mandatory schedule. Its three-Justice discussion of speech value is not made controlling; the Court does not overrule it or Red Lion's separate broadcast holding.
- Sable Communications of California, Inc. v. FCC, 492 U.S. 115: applies protection of adult nonobscene speech, careful tailoring and independent review; preserves the distinction between telephone services and broadcasting.
- Butler v. Michigan, 352 U.S. 380: applies its protection against reducing adults to material suitable for children without deriving a broadcast timetable from it.
- Turner Broadcasting System, Inc. v. FCC, 512 U.S. 622: applies content classification and the limited controlling principle of deferential but independent evidentiary review. Its content-neutral cable test and four-Justice record-insufficiency application do not determine this case.
- United States v. X-Citement Video, Inc., 513 U.S. 64: applies the boundary allowing alternative defenses of an existing favorable judgment without enlarging respondents' rights under it.
- United States v. Chesapeake & Potomac Telephone Co., with National Cable Television Assn. v. Bell Atlantic Corp., February 27, 1996: its repeal-based remand rests on actual statutory repeal and unresolved continuing injury; it supplies no automatic remand from §551's enactment here.

Effective March 4, 1996, the mandatory uniform 6 a.m.–10 p.m. indecency schedule may not be enforced, and the D.C. Circuit must implement its removal. The already favorable 10 p.m.–6 a.m. protection continues. The Court adds a controlling, record-based limit on the mandatory schedule; it does not withdraw all authority to protect children through lawful, more confined broadcast regulation. Adult speech remains protected, Pacifica retains its actual contextual force and ordinary obscenity enforcement survives. No new general definition or replacement safe-harbor hour is prescribed.

Section 551's parental-choice provisions remain subject to their enacted conditions. Its findings are legislative findings, not proof of a presently effective alternative. The rating amendment takes effect one year after February 8, 1996 only upon the FCC's determination, after consultation with appropriate public-interest groups and interested private-sector individuals, that acceptable voluntary ratings and voluntary transmission agreements have not been established. The rating paragraph does not authorize political or religious ratings. The advisory committee must comprise parents, television broadcasters, television programming producers, cable operators, appropriate public-interest groups and other interested private-sector individuals, fairly balanced in political affiliation, represented viewpoints and committee functions. The FCC must supply staff and resources necessary for efficient and prompt performance and require a final recommendations report within one year of initial appointments.

The manufacturing requirement applies on an FCC-selected date, after consultation with the television manufacturing industry, no earlier than two years after enactment to covered apparatus with screens at least thirteen inches diagonally manufactured in the United States or shipped in interstate commerce. Carriers merely transporting without trading are excepted. FCC rules must provide Commission oversight of industry's adoption of blocking-technology standards and require covered apparatus to receive line-21 rating signals conforming to industry specifications established under Commission supervision. An FCC determination that alternative technology enables parental blocking by identifying programs without ratings, is comparably priced and blocks broadly across a multichannel system as effectively and easily requires permitting either technology. No such determination, implemented nationwide ratings/blocking system, household penetration, cost/effectiveness finding or radio counterpart is established. These provisions neither repeal §16 nor independently resolve this challenge.

#### Separate Positions Relevant to the Decision

**Souter, concurring in part and dissenting in part.** Souter joins the content-based standard, independent review and Pacifica's continued limits. He would vacate approval of the actual schedule for renewed examination of its boundary, contextual reach, practical adult opportunities and alternatives. The lower majority did address 10 p.m. by reasoning from its midnight conclusion and removing unequal treatment; he finds that analysis insufficiently focused, not wholly absent. Turner supports a careful remand without turning present uncertainty into final constitutional invalidity. Actual evidence exists, and further analysis may show adequate support or require relief. He therefore withholds assent to final invalidity and mandatory removal, preserves nighttime protection, and prescribes neither a new agency hearing nor automatic interim relief. Section 551 alone requires no remand, and future equipment is no present factual premise.

**Stevens, dissenting, joined by Scalia and Breyer.** The dissent would affirm the actual uniform decree under demanding review. Pacifica permits contextual broadcast channeling; Sable's rejection of wholesale adult exclusion does not establish an effective telephone-like means of segregating this audience. Substantial child access, rapid tuning, multiple receivers, the meaningful 10 p.m. harbor and evidence-sensitive contextual defenses together justify this schedule. The dissent accepts protected adult speech and independent review, but finds the majority gives insufficient weight to the remaining opportunities and the practical difficulty of protecting children without timed channeling. It requires neither a formal legislative report nor scientific certainty, makes no finding that every program harms children, and adopts no general lower-value hierarchy. No foolproof-alternative condition, operational V-chip or automatic station-rating exemption supplies its conclusion. Its proposed affirmance preserves the favorable nighttime protection, actual defenses and properly supported future challenges.

The [en banc opinion, 58 F.3d 654](https://openjurist.org/58/f3d/654/action-for-childrens-television-v-federal-communications-commission), contains the majority and distinct Edwards and Wald dissents and the uniform 10 p.m. decree. [Public Law 102-356, §§16 and 22, 106 Stat. 954, 957](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/STATUTE-106/pdf/STATUTE-106-Pg949.pdf), contains the original schedules, APA procedure, 180-day direction, repeal and effective date. [18 U.S.C. §1464 (1994)](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/USCODE-1994-title18/html/USCODE-1994-title18-partI-chap71-sec1464.htm) remains the statutory basis for the surviving authority discussed above.

The challenge and competing arguments appear in the ACT petition, filing No. 95-520, pp. i, 20–29; Pacifica petition, filing No. 95-509, pp. i, 10–30; common Government response, pp. 8–20; and Pacifica reply, pp. 1–2 n.3, 2–10. These numbers identify the submissions, not separate dockets for this decision. The contextual definition, record explanations and qualified defense appear in the FCC Report and Order, 8 FCC Rcd 704, reproduced at Pacifica App. 102a–133a, ¶¶37–38. Characterizations of underlying comments and studies through that record are not independent findings.

The opinion relationships and limits appear in [Pacifica, 438 U.S. 726](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/USREPORTS-438/pdf/USREPORTS-438-726.pdf), and [Sable, 492 U.S. 115](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/USREPORTS-492/pdf/USREPORTS-492-115.pdf). [Public Law 104-104, §551, 110 Stat. 139–142](https://www.congress.gov/104/plaws/publ104/PLAW-104publ104.htm), contains the enacted parental-choice provisions and transitions; its text establishes neither present nationwide implementation nor an equivalent radio system.

The cases return to the D.C. Circuit for implementation of the five-Justice mandatory-set-aside direction. The FCC rule corresponding to the invalid 6 a.m.–10 p.m. schedule must be set aside and the dependent statutory command may not be enforced to require that schedule. Uniform nighttime protection remains. ### Bennis v. Michigan, No. 94-8729

Merits decision, March 4, 1996

#### Chronology and Posture

Argued November 29, 1995. On review under 28 U.S.C. §1257 of the Michigan Supreme Court's final judgment, 447 Mich. 719, 527 N.W.2d 483 (1994), reversing the Court of Appeals and reinstating nuisance abatement of a jointly owned automobile. The questions concern due-process protection of an uninvolved co-owner's interest and whether the Takings Clause independently requires compensation. Tina Bennis seeks protection of her interest or constitutionally required relief; Michigan seeks affirmance. All nine Justices participated at argument and decision.

John Bennis used the car to convey the participants and as the place of the prohibited sexual act in an area with an existing prostitution nuisance. The state lead opinion assumes Tina neither knew of nor consented to the illegal use, while treating ordinary shared use as voluntary. It does not establish all reasonably expected preventive action. Earlier encounters do not establish repeated prohibited use of this car. Officers observed no payment; the state lead opinion relies on lewdness associated with prostitution and alternatively circumstantial proof. The Court makes no additional state-law offense finding.

Riley's lead rationale had three votes; Griffin's concurrence in the result supplied the fourth vote for the Michigan judgment. The Court reviews that judgment without attributing all the lead reasoning to a state-court majority. A contested hearing and equitable consideration of alternatives occurred. The record establishes no precise ownership fraction, valuation, sale accounting, complete preventive-measures findings or oral-argument concession. The qualitative sale-cost concern and reference to another family car establish neither expenses nor available substitute transportation; no additional waiver, preservation, preventive-act or monetary-entitlement finding follows.

#### Judgment

**Vacated in part and remanded, 5–4.**

**Supporting:** Stone-Zsela, Stevens, Kennedy, Souter and Breyer.

**Opposing:** O'Connor, Scalia, Thomas and Ginsburg, who would affirm rejection of both reviewed constitutional objections.

The vacatur reaches only the judgment sustaining forfeiture of Tina's ownership interest. Her entitlement remains unresolved pending determination of the qualified due-process defense.

#### Opinion Topology

Chief Justice Stone-Zsela delivers the Opinion of the Court, Parts I–III, joined throughout by Stevens, Kennedy, Souter and Breyer. Those five directly adopt the qualified due-process rule and claimant burden, the lawful-forfeiture/takings distinction and its application to the unresolved defense, and the limited vacatur and conditional remedy.

O'Connor dissents, joined by Scalia, Thomas and Ginsburg. Thomas and Ginsburg each also file a separate dissent, joined by no other Justice. All four dissenters accept the premise that an independently lawful forfeiture does not require additional compensation, but reject the defense and remand here. That agreement does not join them to Part II's application, the Court's opinion or its judgment.

#### Controlling Propositions and Authority

##### A qualified owner defense

**Controlling proposition:** Due process forbids forfeiture of a co-owner's separately held interest in an ordinarily lawful automobile because of another's prohibited use when she establishes nonparticipation, lack of knowledge and all prevention reasonably expected in the circumstances. Ordinary co-ownership and permission for shared lawful use do not conclusively defeat protection; knowingly tolerated misuse, culpable entrustment and omitted reasonable precautions fall outside it.

**Authority:** Part I of the Court's opinion, with the five direct joins identified above, controls the rule, claimant burden, application and limits.

The owner's interest cannot be taken solely because she shared lawful use if she proves uninvolvement and all reasonably expected prevention. Goldsmith-Grant and Van Oster sustain forfeiture despite ignorance in their unlawful-use and voluntary-entrustment settings. Calero-Toledo likewise sustains forfeiture against an unaware entrusting owner, but reserves the materially different case of an owner proving additional preventive circumstances. The Court now adopts that reserved protection; the reservation was not an earlier holding.

Deterrence and anti-collusion interests justify placing the showing on the claimant. They do not make ordinary shared use conclusive against an owner who proves she reasonably could have done no more. The car's transport and use permit invocation of forfeiture law, but do not resolve each owner's constitutional protection. A neighborhood nuisance differs from personal knowledge and preventability.

Reasonable prevention requires practical precautions in the actual circumstances, not constant supervision, omniscience or prevention at any imaginable cost. Ignorance, marriage and joint title do not presume success; joint title does not establish an omitted precaution. Ordinary state factfinding rules apply, with a meaningful opportunity to prove protection. The Court creates no numerical or heightened proof standard and no universal duty for the State to disprove innocence.

Austin concerns punitive forfeitures under the Excessive Fines Clause; Good concerns seizure process; Buena Vista applies a statutory defense. None already resolves this substantive question. The rule does not immunize every unaware owner or decide materially different contraband, maritime or involuntary-taking cases, or a comprehensive constitutional instrumentality test.

##### Lawful forfeiture and compensation

**Controlling proposition:** A constitutionally and statutorily lawful forfeiture does not automatically require additional compensation under the Takings Clause. That premise cannot establish the lawfulness of forfeiting Tina's interest before her due-process defense is resolved and supplies no independent compensation judgment here.

**Authority:** Part II of the Court's opinion controls with the same five direct joins. The four dissenters independently accept the lawful-forfeiture premise but disagree with its application.

Calero-Toledo rejects the uncompensated-taking challenge to the forfeiture it sustains. Fuller concerns value attributable to revocable federal grazing privileges; it creates no compensation exemption for every exercise of governmental power. Michigan correctly rejects automatic compensation for every forfeiture, but cannot establish the validity of taking Tina's interest merely by naming the proceeding a forfeiture. If she proves the defense, lawful property relief must protect an interest that cannot be forfeited on this ground; that is not compensation after authorizing a taking. If she fails and forfeiture is otherwise lawful, forfeiture alone creates no additional payment. The Court fixes no amount and does not finally sustain retention of her interest before the required determination.

##### Conditional property relief

**Controlling proposition:** The state court must afford Tina a meaningful opportunity to prove actual ownership, nonparticipation, lack of knowledge and all reasonably expected prevention, and must decide those predicates. Success requires preservation or restoration of her proven interest through lawful limitation of forfeiture or corresponding property relief; failure leaves otherwise valid abatement undefeated by this defense.

**Authority:** Part III of the Court's opinion supplies the same five direct joins for application, remedy and limits.

The state lead opinion's assumptions about knowledge and consent do not establish every element of the new defense. The existing hearing also does not establish available precautions that Tina omitted. Recognition of the defense makes those unresolved matters material. The Court neither finds entitlement to return nor treats hypothetical control of joint property as a finding against her.

Existing usable evidence may support the determination; further proceedings are required only as needed. The record need not be discarded or the entire hearing automatically repeated. Buena Vista distinguishes eligibility from proven entitlement, although its defense is statutory. Good's prior-process holding supplies no automatic final title remedy. Libretti distinguishes lawful forfeiture authority and protected third-party interests from consent and procedural labels, without importing its federal statutory scheme.

Release, division, sale accounting or another lawful property remedy must reflect ownership and value actually established. The Court prescribes no immediate return, ownership fraction or half share, gross or net sum, mandatory sale, particular expense allocation or eminent-domain award. It neither transfers John's interest to Tina nor disturbs his conviction or lawful forfeiture of his own interest. State equitable authority may implement relief, but discretion cannot replace protection once the constitutional predicates are proved. No new state-law statutory ruling or independent Excessive Fines adjudication follows.

#### Precedent Treatment and Current-Law Effect

- **Calero-Toledo v. Pearson Yacht Leasing Co., 416 U.S. 663:** Its reasonable-prevention reservation becomes substantive due-process protection here. Its entrustment holding and lawful-forfeiture/takings distinction survive; the separate involuntary-taking question and materially different maritime circumstances remain open.
- **J.W. Goldsmith, Jr.-Grant Co. v. United States, 254 U.S. 505, and Van Oster v. Kansas, 272 U.S. 465:** Their ignorant-owner, unlawful-use and voluntary-entrustment holdings survive subject to the newly protected category. Reserved theft and remote-incidental-use questions are neither adjudicated defenses nor grounds decided here.
- **Austin v. United States, 509 U.S. 602:** Its covered-forfeiture and historical partly punitive rules remain intact. Its reserved innocence question is answered only to today's extent; no new Excessive Fines, state-incorporation or comprehensive instrumentality holding follows.
- **United States v. James Daniel Good Real Property, 510 U.S. 43:** Its prior-process rule and movable-property qualifications remain distinct; a hearing neither resolves this substantive defense nor requires automatic return.
- **United States v. 92 Buena Vista Avenue, 507 U.S. 111:** Its statutory defense, claimant showing and qualified relation-back holdings survive. Eligibility differs from entitlement; its statute does not supply today's constitutional defense.
- **Libretti v. United States, 516 U.S. 29:** Consent cannot enlarge forfeiture authority or transfer interests beyond the defendant's lawful power. Its sentencing, waiver and third-party statutory rules remain intact and are not transplanted into Michigan law.
- **United States v. Fuller, 409 U.S. 488:** Its valuation rule for revocable federal grazing privileges supplies no general compensation exemption.

Effective March 4, 1996, the previously reserved protection controls in this automobile setting. Mere ignorance is insufficient, ordinary sharing is not conclusive against protection, and the claimant must prove the protective circumstances under ordinary state factfinding rules with a meaningful opportunity to do so. Existing punitive-forfeiture, statutory innocent-owner, seizure-process and criminal-forfeiture rules retain their scope. No categorical immunity, universal property-nexus formula, special proof standard, state-incorporation holding, automatic second hearing, fixed share or automatic compensation right results. Successive punishment of a culpable owner is not decided, and the Court announces no independent construction of Michigan's nuisance statute.

#### Separate Positions Relevant to the Decision

**O'Connor, joined by Scalia, Thomas and Ginsburg, dissenting.** Goldsmith-Grant, Van Oster and Calero-Toledo sustain forfeiture despite ignorance when an owner voluntarily makes property available and it is actually used unlawfully. This car transported the participants and provided the place of the act; its association was not remote or fortuitous. Assumed ignorance does not prove reasonable prevention, and Calero-Toledo's reservation does not require adoption of a new ground for remand.

Austin's punitive-forfeiture treatment, Buena Vista's enacted defense and Good's process protection do not already create the asserted constitutional immunity. The dissent preserves those limits, statutory authority and required process. It finds no omitted available precaution, decides no proved all-reasonable-prevention case and authorizes no forfeiture of every property loosely associated with wrongdoing. Sustaining this forfeiture defeats the separate compensation claim; it does not exempt invalid forfeitures or every governmental acquisition from constitutional scrutiny.

**Thomas, separately dissenting and joining O'Connor.** Entrusted-property precedent supports affirmance, but the absence of personal-fault requirements makes historical instrumentality limits especially important. Calling property a nuisance cannot erase them. The car's admitted transport and use permit application of Van Oster without an instrumentality concession. Thomas creates no comprehensive nexus test, adopts no earlier exclusive excessiveness formula of Scalia's and does not characterize every historical forfeiture as personal punishment. Possible remedial costs establish neither an exclusively remedial forfeiture nor the absence of constitutional cost to Tina.

**Ginsburg, separately dissenting and joining O'Connor.** Voluntary sharing and actual equitable consideration distinguish this case from unrestricted punishment of innocent third parties. The trial court considered alternatives, but qualitative cost concerns and another family automobile establish no expense calculation or substitute transportation. Ginsburg would apply the inherited entrustment rule to assumed ignorance while reserving the owner who proves all reasonably expected prevention and materially different exorbitant applications.

A hearing does not cure every substantive defect, and low value does not erase ownership rights. Equitable discretion supplies context, not a mandatory federal offset or an answer to established constitutional protection. Her conclusion rests on the existing rule, without a precise ownership fraction, vehicle concession or newly found valuation.

#### Mandate, Remedy, and Stage

The case returns to the Michigan Supreme Court, including such proceedings in the appropriate state court as are needed to determine the defense and implement lawful property relief. Vacatur reaches only forfeiture of Tina's interest; no outcome on her protective showing is directed. Supreme Court merits review is complete and state proceedings remain open. Ordinary mandate procedure applies, with no special issuance date or filing deadline.

**End of entry: Bennis v. Michigan, Merits decision, March 4, 1996.**

### Hercules, Inc. v. United States, No. 94-818, 516 U.S. 417

Merits decision, March 4, 1996

#### Chronology and Posture

Argued October 30, 1995. On writ of certiorari to the Federal Circuit, 24 F.3d 188, reviewing its May 4, 1994 judgment affirming summary judgments dismissing the separate government-contract suits of Hercules and Thompson. The questions concern whether the government-contractor tort defense precludes the asserted specifications-warranty claims and whether procurement circumstances, including Defense Production Act compulsion, establish contractual responsibility for third-party tort settlements and defense expenses. The manufacturers seek further proceedings; the United States seeks affirmance.

The manufacturers supplied Agent Orange under federal specifications, settled veterans' tort litigation in 1984 and incurred defense expenses. No express governmental indemnity was included. Hercules's fifteen contracts and Thompson's two had distinct production and knowledge histories. Thompson initially declined to supply; the Government identifies initial competitive awards to Hercules. Alleged later compulsion, control and superior knowledge are distinct from established contract terms. The Federal Circuit used the government-contractor defense to reject settlement causation and separately rejected defense expenses. Absence of contract coverage is the Government's alternative ground here.

Justice Stevens takes no part in consideration or decision; no reason is stated. The other eight Justices participate.

#### Judgment

**Affirmed, 6–2.** The Court resolves the preserved contract claims on the absence of the asserted reimbursement undertaking, without deciding the veterans' tort claims.

| Judgment component | Disposition and vote | Supporting Justices | Opposing Justices | Remedy or remand |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Both petitioners' warranty-based settlement recovery | Denial affirmed, 6–2 | Stone-Zsela, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg | O'Connor, Breyer | Summary judgments remain in place; no payment or further merits remand. |
| Both petitioners' associated tort-defense expenses | Denial separately affirmed, 6–2 | Stone-Zsela, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg | O'Connor, Breyer | No expense award or damages determination. |
| Thompson's contextual implied-indemnity claim | Rejection affirmed, 6–2 | Stone-Zsela, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg | O'Connor, Breyer | No new equitable reimbursement action or further merits remand. |

**Not participating:** Stevens. O'Connor and Breyer would vacate in part and remand the preserved contract claims. Other disposed claims are not reopened.

#### Opinion Topology

Justice Souter delivers the Opinion of the Court, Parts I–IV, joined throughout by Chief Justice Stone-Zsela and Justices Scalia, Kennedy, Thomas and Ginsburg. Part I addresses actual contracts and authorization; Part II the specifications warranty and distinct settlement and expense dispositions; Part III Thompson's contextual undertaking and §707's absence of a payment promise; Part IV the scope, reservations and affirmance. Six direct joins establish every controlling proposition and its stated limits. Breyer dissents in Parts I–III, joined throughout by O'Connor; neither joins the Court's opinion.

#### Controlling Propositions and Authority

##### An actual authorized undertaking

**Controlling proposition:** Under 28 U.S.C. §1491(a)(1), the claim must rest on an express or implied-in-fact undertaking the Government was authorized to make. The Tucker Act supplies jurisdiction, not a substantive reimbursement promise; fairness cannot supply an obligation implied only in law.

**Authority:** The Court's opinion, Parts I–IV, with the six direct joins identified above, supplies the authority for this and the following holdings.

Sutton applies governmental contracting-authority limits to implied and express agreements alike. Hatzlachh preserves the independent point that absence of a tort remedy does not defeat an actual contract claim. The Government cannot evade a genuine contractual obligation through tort immunity, and a claimant cannot create a promise by labeling equitable reimbursement contractual. An ordinary authorized warranty is not invalid merely because possible breach damages were uncertain at contracting. The decision concerns the undertaking's scope, without finding a missing appropriation, delegation or required approval in these contracts.

##### Specifications warranty, settlements and defense costs

**Controlling proposition:** A warranty implied from government specifications assures satisfactory performance of the procurement undertaking. Without more, it does not promise reimbursement for these manufacturers' settlements of veterans' tort claims or the expense of defending them. Both petitioners' warranty-based settlement and expense claims fail on that ground.

Spearin ties the specifications warranty and breach consequences to the performance the purchaser prescribed. Protection against defective specifications does not itself make the purchaser an insurer of the supplier's outside liabilities. Ryan Stevedoring and Weyerhaeuser recognize that a particular service duty can carry foreseeable third-party consequences without express indemnity. They require identification of that duty; specification and purchase alone do not establish this additional promise. Hatzlachh preserves contract remedies but supplies no missing term.

The manufacturers correctly distinguish contract loss from tort liability, but that distinction does not establish coverage. Even taking material allegations favorably, the specifications implication does not undertake these losses. Defense expenses receive a separate disposition: they may persist despite a successful tort defense, but the same absent undertaking defeats recovery here. The Court does not limit every contractual damage to delivery, require express indemnity for all third-party losses or adopt Kania as a categorical expense bar. A different demonstrated, authorized undertaking remains possible. The Federal Circuit's equation of a warranty claim with a successful government-contractor defense is unnecessary to the judgment and is not decided.

##### Compulsion and contextual indemnity

**Controlling proposition:** Thompson's compulsory procurement, specifications, alleged control and superior knowledge, and statutory liability protection, considered together, do not establish the asserted authorized agreement to bear third-party settlement and defense costs. Defense Production Act §707 itself promises no affirmative reimbursement.

Section 101 authorizes priorities and compelled performance; §707 protects against specified damages or penalties arising from compliance. A production command and liability protection differ from governmental assent to pay later litigation costs. Thompson invokes the combined contracting setting, not merely a private statutory cause. Still, control and knowledge do not turn the command into the claimed indemnity. An obligation imposed solely because relief would be fair exceeds the Tucker Act's actual-contract boundary.

Sutton's actual-authority principle, appropriations limits and specific authorized indemnity mechanisms caution against readily inferring an open-ended commitment. They do not forbid every indemnity, establish that an officer lacked a proved delegation or establish exercise of special contracting authority here. Genuine authorized indemnities remain enforceable on their terms. The Court does not decide §707's full immunity boundary, including particular product-injury and accelerated-production situations, or create a statutory reimbursement action.

#### Limits and Questions Not Reached

Both petitioners' preserved specifications-warranty claims and Thompson's contextual implied-in-fact indemnity claim are decided. Hercules's compulsion allegations do not reopen its unpreserved separate indemnity claim. Knowledge, control and compulsion remain subsidiary arguments within preserved claims; no freestanding superior-knowledge, purchaser-use, good-faith or takings cause is newly adjudicated. No finding establishes defective specifications, relative knowledge, veterans' injuries, actual warranty breach, reasonableness or allocation of damages, or contract-specific authorization. Fees for prosecuting this contract action are not awarded, and absence of express indemnity creates no universal express-clause rule.

Allegations and advocacy in the complaints and the United States' August 31, 1984 opt-out-litigation submission do not establish breach. The printed Joint Appendix does not reproduce complete lodged contracts, original specifications, production orders, delegations or unprinted discovery. The decision rests on no newly found disclaimer, breach or particular authorization defect. The 1984 settlement shares were allocated by volume with a dioxin-level adjustment; claimed settlement and defense amounts are not adjudicated contract damages. Later statutory amendments supply no new retroactive conditions on the 1960s contracts.

#### Precedent Treatment and Current-Law Effect

- **United States v. Spearin, 248 U.S. 132:** The warranty applies to its actual performance undertaking; ordinary protection and breach damages survive, without establishing the additional third-party coverage asserted here.
- **Sutton v. United States, 256 U.S. 575:** Actual governmental authority is required for express and implied-in-fact undertakings alike; no new contract-specific appropriation or delegation finding follows.
- **Hatzlachh Supply Co. v. United States, 444 U.S. 460:** Genuine contract remedies remain independent of tort immunity and distinct from obligations implied in law.
- **Ryan Stevedoring Co. v. Pan-Atlantic Steamship Corp., 350 U.S. 124, and Weyerhaeuser Steamship Co. v. Nacirema Operating Co., 355 U.S. 563:** Their particular service undertakings are distinguished; foreseeable third-party consequences may still follow a proved duty without express indemnity.
- **Boyle v. United Technologies Corp., 487 U.S. 500:** The government-contractor tort defense is unchanged. The Court neither decides whether the Federal Circuit's causation equation independently supports the settlement disposition nor substitutes tort immunity for identifying a contract.
- **United States v. X-Citement Video, November 29, 1994, and Lebron v. National Railroad Passenger Corp., February 21, 1995:** Their distinct review-scope rules remain intact. The Government may defend its favorable judgment on an alternative ground, without new findings or independent unpresented causes.
- **Kania v. United States, 650 F.2d 264:** This Court of Claims decision concerned nonprosecution, authority and remote litigation expenses. Its language becomes no universal bar to third-party expenses from breach of an authorized procurement contract.

Effective March 4, 1996, these contractual-scope holdings leave actual authorized agreements, Spearin protection and applicable remedies governed by their terms. They create neither immunity from genuine contracts nor a prohibition on all third-party consequential damages. No earlier Supreme Court holding is overruled, no warranty/tort-defense causation equation is adopted, and no new rule governs veterans' tort claims or §707's full immunity boundary.

#### Separate Positions Relevant to the Decision

**Breyer, joined throughout by O'Connor, dissenting.** They would vacate in part and remand both petitioners' preserved warranty claims, including settlement and tort-defense expenses, and Thompson's contextual indemnity claim, leaving other disposed claims undisturbed.

Part I reads Spearin, Ryan, Weyerhaeuser and Hatzlachh to permit inquiry into whether an actual authorized undertaking included foreseeable third-party consequences. The dissent rejects the Federal Circuit's categorical equation of a successful warranty claim and certainly successful tort defense. The 1984 settlement cannot be assessed as though later defense rulings already assured victory; even successful defense can leave expenses. Kania's nonprosecution and remote-expense setting does not settle procurement damages. Actual duty, breach, causation, foreseeability, ex ante litigation risk, reasonableness and allocation require examination as necessary and procedurally available. Neither settlement approval nor government specifications prove those elements.

Part II agrees that §707 alone promises no reimbursement but would permit Thompson to attempt proof of an authorized implied-in-fact undertaking from the combined circumstances. Compulsion, specifications, alleged control and knowledge, and surrounding liability protection may inform the actual bargain. Sutton still requires authority; fairness alone is insufficient, and neither special contracting authority's existence nor a judicial label proves approval. The dissent infers no indemnity cap or insurer-equivalent amount.

The proposed remand and causation analysis are noncontrolling.

### SSC Corp. v. Town of Smithtown, petition No. 95-782 and cross-petition No. 95-1015

Merits decision, March 11, 1996

#### Chronology and Posture

On review under 28 U.S.C. §1254(1) of the Second Circuit's September 19, 1995 judgment, 66 F.3d 502. The petition and cross-petition numbers identify the underlying filings. The questions concern the destination requirement for residential waste collection and disposal purchased by the Town and the separate ordinance directing covered private waste transactions to the same privately owned incinerator. The district court invalidated both; the court of appeals sustained invalidation of the ordinance and reversed invalidation of the contract term. SSC challenges the contract disposition; the Town seeks restoration of the ordinance. All nine Justices participate.

Smithtown pays SSC for disposal as well as collection. The bid includes designated tipping fees; the hauler retains lower-volume savings and bears excess-volume costs. Ogden/CE Huntington Limited Partnership owns the incinerator on Huntington-owned land. Public financing, service-fee guarantees and purchased capacity do not establish public ownership. The Town alleges diversion of waste and withheld more than $750,000. Diversion, contract liability and the amount due remain disputed.

#### Judgment

| Judgment component | Disposition and vote | Supporting Justices | Remedy or remand |
|---|---|---|---|
| Destination term for Town-purchased residential collection and disposal | Affirmed, 9–0 | Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg, Breyer | The appellate reversal of the district court's constitutional invalidation remains in force. No breach or debt is established. |
| Separate compulsory-routing ordinance | Affirmed, 9–0 | Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg, Breyer | The appellate judgment sustaining invalidation remains in force; the ordinance is not restored. |

The Court decides no payment entitlement or damages amount and directs no substitute tax, fee, price or waste program.

#### Opinion Topology

Justice Kennedy delivers the Opinion of the Court, Parts I–III, joined throughout by Chief Justice Stone-Zsela and Justices Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer. Part I concerns purchased performance; Part II applies Carbone to the ordinance; Part III states the disposition and limits on further proceedings. All nine directly join each controlling proposition.

O'Connor and Souter join Part II as an application of controlling precedent while retaining their earlier objections to Carbone's original discrimination classification. The opinion neither reconsiders nor newly defends that classification. Their reservations withhold no assent from today's application or remedy, and no separate writing issues.

#### Controlling Propositions and Authority

##### The service the Town purchases

**Controlling proposition:** A municipality purchasing residential waste collection and disposal may specify the disposal facility as part of that performance. Payment through a hauler, separate price items and a privately owned facility do not make disposal an unrelated downstream transaction. The rule reaches the service actually bought; a contract label or participation in another market supplies no authority over unrelated business.

**Authority:** Part I of Kennedy's opinion, with all nine direct joins identified above, controls this application and its limits.

White permits conditions within the public performance a government buys and rejects an inflexible direct-privity boundary. Reeves distinguishes commercial participation from regulation of others. Smithtown pays for final disposal; its purchased service remains incomplete after collection alone. The disposal allowance, retained savings and excess-volume risk do not erase that component.

SSC argues that collection and disposal have separate suppliers and prices and that the destination term protects the same private processor as the ordinance. Those circumstances require attention to substance, but do not prevent purchase in both markets. South-Central Timber's four-Justice downstream discussion warns against using a completed sale to control later, separate business. It does not invalidate paid disposal yet to be performed. The Court neither converts that plurality into a prior majority holding nor endorses every contractual condition.

West Lynn Creamery requires examination of a linked program's actual operation. That examination reveals a genuine purchased component; the ordinance's invalidity does not erase it. Boston Harbor reinforces the purchasing distinction within labor preemption, but the Commerce Clause ruling rests on applicable participant authorities. Government need not own the incinerator or employ every performer. No restriction on unpaid, unrelated later transactions or materially different public waste system is decided.

##### The separate compulsory route

**Controlling proposition:** The challenged ordinance may not reserve independently arranged private waste-processing transactions to the designated local private operator. Carbone requires a legitimate local purpose that reasonable nondiscriminatory alternatives cannot adequately serve. Smithtown's financing commitments and sanitation interests establish no such justification on this record.

**Authority:** Part II of Kennedy's opinion has nine direct joins, including O'Connor and Souter's assent to this application while retaining their prior classification objections. No combination of separate rationales is necessary.

Carbone governs compulsory reservation of private processing, including when other local processors are excluded alongside interstate competitors. The ordinance reaches commercial and other covered waste beyond residential disposal the Town buys. Investment and capacity arrangements make neither independent customers' purchases the Town's own nor Ogden's facility publicly owned.

Criminal fines and imprisonment identify the sovereign mechanism compelling patronage. Penalties are not the whole test; lawful health regulation may also impose them. The defect is discriminatory control of private transactions outside the purchased service. Reported exclusions for recyclables and unacceptable waste do not remove that defect from covered demand.

Dependable flow may protect financing and sanitation, but those interests do not show why reasonable neutral health regulation, lawful purchasing and nondiscriminatory financing cannot adequately serve them. Carbone and Oregon Waste require that justification; ordinary Pike balancing cannot replace it once the discrimination rule applies. There is no perfect-certainty requirement or duty to defeat every imaginable alternative. The Court chooses no replacement program. State encouragement and contractual financial commitments supply no congressional authorization to suspend the rule. Carbone's existing classification controls without reconsideration of its original theoretical basis.

#### Limits and Questions Not Reached

Part III, joined by all nine, preserves only properly remaining ordinary proceedings. Validity of the destination term establishes no diversion, breach, payment obligation or entitlement to the withheld sum. Invalidation of the ordinance neither rescinds the whole contract nor establishes damages. No antitrust immunity, actual CERCLA liability, general subcontract rule or treatment of a materially different public system is decided.

The code and bid terms are reported in the lower opinion, rather than a complete code edition or executed contract; no omitted contract term or additional exception supports today's holding. The Second Circuit addressed the ordinance at 66 F.3d 502, 512–514, and the contract at 514–518. Its judgment affirmed in part and reversed in part; the availability of remaining proceedings does not add an express remand command to that judgment.

#### Precedent Treatment and Current-Law Effect

- **White v. Massachusetts Council of Construction Employers, 460 U.S. 204:** Purchased-performance and privity principles apply while limits on unrelated conduct survive. Its particular federal-grant authorization supplies no authorization for this ordinance.
- **Reeves v. Stake, 447 U.S. 429:** Commercial participation remains distinct from regulation. The decision authorizes neither every contractual restriction nor resolution of reserved natural-resource and broader downstream questions.
- **South-Central Timber v. Wunnicke, 467 U.S. 82:** The four-Justice analysis of control after a completed direct transaction is distinguished, with its substantive concern intact and its authority unexpanded. Its separate six-Justice congressional-authorization holding supplies no permission here and does not enlarge support for its downstream analysis.
- **C & A Carbone v. Town of Clarkstown, May 16, 1994:** Its compulsory private-processing and justification rules govern the ordinance, while its genuine-purchasing category receives this paid-disposal application. No broader public-facility rule is announced.
- **Oregon Waste Systems v. Department of Environmental Quality, April 4, 1994:** Discrimination and justification remain separate inquiries; a financing objective alone does not establish adequate justification.
- **West Lynn Creamery v. Healy, June 17, 1994:** The linked arrangement's actual operation controls. Neither formal separation nor an integrated label decides what the government buys; genuine purchasing is not identical to compulsory routing.
- **Building & Construction Trades Council v. Associated Builders & Contractors (Boston Harbor), March 8, 1993:** Its genuine-project-purchasing distinction remains within labor preemption and creates no blanket Commerce Clause exemption.
- **Pike v. Bruce Church, 397 U.S. 137:** Incidental-burden balancing remains available for genuinely evenhanded burdens, but cannot displace the discrimination rule here. No independent Pike application is decided.

Effective March 11, 1996, the Town-paid collection-and-disposal term permissibly specifies the service bought. Private contractors and facilities may perform that service, but contract form or financial stake does not exempt unrelated restrictions. Carbone continues to prohibit unjustified compulsory reservation of private processing business. Genuine purchasing and lawful nondiscriminatory health and financing measures remain available under their requirements. No precedent is overruled, new balancing framework adopted, public ownership inferred, compensatory tax approved or replacement program prescribed. Contract liability and monetary relief require their own proceedings and proof.

#### Mandate, Remedy, and Stage

Supreme Court merits review is complete. The paired constitutional judgment stands, and the case returns through ordinary mandate procedures for contract and payment proceedings only as properly pending and legally available. No predetermined outcome, damages or withheld-payment award, rescission, new hearing, automatic discovery, guaranteed trial, special mandate date or later Supreme Court event follows.

**End of entry: SSC Corp. v. Town of Smithtown, Merits decision, March 11, 1996.**

### Dalton v. Little Rock Family Planning Services, No. 95-1025

Summary review on certiorari petition, March 18, 1996

#### Chronology and Posture

On petition for certiorari to the Eighth Circuit's July 25, 1995 judgment, 60 F.3d 497, affirming the Eastern District of Arkansas, 860 F. Supp. 609. Rehearing below was denied September 22, 1995. No oral argument was held. The Arkansas appeal was heard with a Nebraska appeal below; only the Arkansas petition is decided here.

The first petition question asks whether a Medicaid-participating State must fund medically necessary abortions for eligible women whose pregnancies result from rape or incest despite its contrary constitution. The second challenges the scope of the injunction and declaration against Arkansas Amendment 68.

Amendment 68 §1 prohibits public abortion funding except to save the mother's life and operates both within and outside Medicaid. Section 2 declares a policy protecting unborn life from conception until birth to the extent the Federal Constitution permits. Section 3 excludes contraceptives from the amendment's operation and disclaims requiring an appropriation.

The published July 25, 1994 decree enjoined the whole amendment for as long as Arkansas accepts Medicaid funds and separately enjoined inconsistent abortion-funding provisions of the Medicaid plan. An unpublished July 27, 1994 stay-denial order declared the amendment null, void and of no effect. The Eighth Circuit sustained the broad relief on Arkansas inseverability grounds.

#### Public Action

Certiorari is granted limited to the second question and otherwise denied. The funding construction below is accepted for this review without deciding it.

#### Judgment

**Partial summary vacatur and remand on the scope of relief, 9–0.**

**Supporting:** Chief Justice Stone-Zsela and Justices Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer.

This published count concerns only the summary merits disposition. The Court vacates the judgment only insofar as it affirms invalidation or suspension of Amendment 68 beyond applications conflicting with the Medicaid obligation left unreviewed, and remands for corresponding declaratory and injunctive relief.

#### Opinion and Controlling Propositions

The Court issues a per curiam opinion. Chief Justice Stone-Zsela and Justices Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer join both Part I, addressing the limited question and the decree's excessive reach, and Part II, specifying retained protection, temporal limits and remand. All nine participate in the petition consideration and summary decision and directly join each controlling proposition. No separate writing is issued.

##### Relief must follow the established federal conflict

**Holding:** When an established Medicaid conflict concerns particular applications of a state funding restriction, federal declaratory and injunctive relief must protect those applications without invalidating or suspending severable applications for which no conflict has been established. Neither the July 25 injunction nor the July 27 declaration may reach Amendment 68 in its entirety; the underlying funding obligation is accepted without deciding the first petition question.

**Authority:** Part I of the per curiam opinion, directly joined by all nine Justices identified above, controls without combining separate rationales.

**Controlling explanation:** The Supremacy Clause makes federal law controlling where a state rule conflicts with it; it does not eliminate the rule's other operations. The unreviewed Medicaid construction supports protection for the specified eligible patients, but supplies no finding that every non-Medicaid public-funding application violates federal law. Harris v. McRae leaves the precise statutory funding issue open, and denial of review does not answer it.

The lower courts relied on Arkansas inseverability law and declined to write a new exception into the amendment. Limiting federal relief to conflicting applications leaves the enacted text in place: it neither creates a state appropriation nor inserts an exception. Because §1 retains lawful applications, the premise that §§2–3 must lose every function because §1 has disappeared cannot justify blanket invalidation. Any genuinely remaining state-law severability question remains for the proper court. Another section may not be used to evade the surviving Medicaid protection.

NTEU permits sufficiently broad relief when the proved defect and an indivisible prohibition require it, while excluding unrelated claims. Here, the Medicaid conflict establishes no defect in every application. Madsen's remedial holdings likewise preserve lawful independent operation; its speech-specific test does not govern this case. The Court adopts neither a general rule against broad relief nor a new facial-review formula. The crime-victim program illustrates §1's operation outside Medicaid; its funding source and independent constitutional validity are not decided.

##### Prospective protection follows the applicable obligation

**Holding:** The confined decree must preserve the adjudicated Medicaid protection while Arkansas participates and controlling federal law requires coverage. It may not suspend unrelated applications merely because participation continues despite a later change in governing conditions. An actual lawful change may support appropriate modification; the decision declares no current lapse or automatic dissolution.

**Authority:** Part II of the per curiam opinion, directly joined by the same nine Justices.

**Controlling explanation:** The decree's continued reach must correspond to the federal obligation it enforces. Pub. L. 104-91, Title I, 110 Stat. 10–14, continues the applicable FY1995 Medicaid authority and conditions. Section 101 retains the current-rate ceiling and applicable lower-rate qualifications when House and Senate amounts differ or only one House has acted. Sections 102–105 preserve the pertinent appropriation's extent and manner, prohibit initiation or resumption of projects unavailable in FY1995, exclude newly proposed cross-account conditions absent from the applicable FY1995 Act, and cover obligations or expenditures during the authorized period.

Unless Title I or the applicable appropriations Act otherwise provides, §106 ends authority at the earliest of an appropriation for the covered activity, enactment of the applicable appropriations Act without that activity, or September 30, 1996. Section 110 begins the covered period on December 16, 1995. The general continuing-resolution deadline is not this account's deadline. No Medicaid funding amount is adjudicated.

This continuation establishes no intervening repeal or expiration. Remand implements the decided scope correction while retaining the funding protection left unreviewed. A possible future change cannot be declared now. The receiving courts must express the actual obligation and its limits without adopting a replacement funding policy or predicting a later legislative choice.

#### Limits and Questions Not Reached

The Court decides neither the underlying Title XIX/Hyde construction nor a constitutional entitlement to public abortion funding. It does not adjudicate the constitutionality of a direct abortion prohibition or foreclose a properly preserved constitutional challenge to a remaining application supported by a developed record. Casey's complete standard and Fargo's application remain unchanged; their access protections do not become a funding entitlement. No independent standing, enforcement or jurisdictional issue from the separate Nebraska litigation, separate Nebraska judgment, or independent constitutional challenge to the crime-victim program is decided.

#### Precedent Treatment

- **Harris v. McRae, 448 U.S. 297:** Its actual limits remain intact; the precise statutory funding question left open there remains undecided here.
- **United States v. National Treasury Employees Union, February 22, 1995:** The Court applies its distinction between relief supported by the proved violation and relief for unrelated claims. Protection against that case's indivisible prohibition does not establish invalidity of every Amendment 68 application.
- **Madsen v. Women's Health Center, Inc., June 30, 1994:** Its bounded remedial principle preserves independent lawful operation; its content-neutral-injunction speech test is not extended to Medicaid funding.
- **Planned Parenthood of Southeastern Pennsylvania v. Casey, June 29, 1992, and Fargo Women's Health Organization v. Schafer, February 13, 1995:** Their access, proof and remedy rules remain intact. Neither supplies a statutory or constitutional funding entitlement in this limited review.

#### Current Law

The federal decree reaches the actual Medicaid conflict while preserving every severable nonconflicting application of Amendment 68. The policy and no-appropriation provisions are not invalidated wholesale but cannot defeat the retained federal protection. The decision supplies no authoritative new construction of every Arkansas severability question.

The lower funding ruling remains operative for the specified Medicaid-eligible patients; denial of the first question does not make it a Supreme Court holding. Current funding conditions continue under their actual statutory qualifications, and future relief depends on the applicable obligation rather than participation alone. No precedent is overruled, general facial-review rule adopted, or current injunction automatically dissolved.

For the presented questions and the separate July 27, 1994 order, see the [petition and appendices](https://archive.org/details/micro_IA40385013_0683), including Appendix D-1–D-2; see also the January 5, 1996 opposition addressing both questions. The published injunction and appellate judgment appear in the [District Court opinion, 860 F. Supp. 609](https://static.case.law/f-supp/860/html/0609-01.html), and the [Eighth Circuit opinion, 60 F.3d 497](https://static.case.law/f3d/60/html/0497-01.html). The July 25 injunction and July 27 declaration are distinct orders.

The statutory setting is supported by Pub. L. 103-112 §509, 107 Stat. 1113; Pub. L. 103-333 §509, 108 Stat. 2573, with the Medicaid advance at 2554–2555; and [Pub. L. 104-91 Title I, 110 Stat. 10–14](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/PLAW-104publ91/html/PLAW-104publ91.htm). The funding-construction dispute remains outside the granted question. The crime-victim example establishes neither the program's entirely state-funded character nor its constitutional validity.

#### Mandate, Remedy, and Stage

Supreme Court review is complete. Under ordinary mandate procedures, the case returns through the Eighth Circuit to the District Court to correct both the July 25 whole-amendment injunction and the July 27 blanket declaration. The merits correction is final; remand implements it without reopening the funding question by this Court's order.

The revised relief must preserve the prohibition against using Amendment 68 to refuse medically necessary abortions to Medicaid-eligible rape/incest patients while Arkansas participates in Medicaid and the applicable federal obligation requires coverage. The separate protection against inconsistent state-plan funding provisions remains operative to that extent, and no amendment section may evade it. Every severable nonconflicting application must remain outside the decree.

The Court awards no damages or past reimbursement, orders no payment for every abortion, and requires no continued state participation in Medicaid. It directs no new hearing or trial and prescribes no special mandate date, automatic hearing, guaranteed trial or later Supreme Court event.

**End of entry: Dalton v. Little Rock Family Planning Services, Summary review on certiorari petition, March 18, 1996.**
