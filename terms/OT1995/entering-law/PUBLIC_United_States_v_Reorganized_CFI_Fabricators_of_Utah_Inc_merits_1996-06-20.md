# Public reading copy

Derived verbatim from the Canonical Decision Record Public Projection. The Record remains authoritative.

## Event

United States v. Reorganized CF&I Fabricators of Utah, Inc., No. 95-325. Argued March 25, 1996; decided June 20, 1996. On writ of certiorari to the Tenth Circuit, 53 F.3d 1155. The questions are whether the allowed 1989 section 4971(a) assessment receives excise-tax priority under pre-1994 section 507(a)(7)(E), and whether section 510(c) permits its subordination on the grounds used below.

CF&I failed to make approximately $12.4 million in required pension contributions by September 15, 1990 and filed Chapter 11 on November 7, 1990. The approximately $1.24 million ten-percent assessment was allowed, but denied tax priority and subordinated. Basic liability and plan qualification are undisputed. Other disallowed assessments are outside review. The plan's zero-payment Class 13 has an alternative Class 12 general-unsecured classification. The lower courts expressly found no inequitable IRS conduct and did not decide the independent plan-law alternatives.

## Participation

Chief Justice Stone-Zsela and Justices Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer participated at argument and decision.

## Public Action

The Court vacates the Tenth Circuit's judgment and remands, preserving denial of excise-tax priority and setting aside the stated equitable subordination.

## Holdings

### I. This funding-default assessment does not receive excise-tax priority

**Controlling proposition:** The allowed 1989 section 4971(a) assessment is a noncompensatory penalty, not an excise tax entitled to priority under the governing pre-1994 section 507(a)(7)(E). When the Bankruptcy Code does not direct adoption of an external classification, the exaction's actual statutory operation controls its bankruptcy tax characterization; neither a tax label nor a deterrent purpose alone resolves that inquiry.

**Authority:** Part I of Souter's Opinion of the Court, joined by Chief Justice Stone-Zsela and Justices Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Ginsburg and Breyer; eight Justices adopt the rule and complete explanation.

**Controlling explanation:** Feiring requires federal bankruptcy characterization by statutory operation. United States v. New York applies that inquiry to federal exactions and recognizes that a revenue tax can encourage conduct without becoming a penalty. Sotelo looks beyond a penalty label where the assessment represents taxes already withheld and not paid. Those decisions make the governing legal operation decisive, rather than importing another statute's terminology or treating every regulatory purpose as disqualifying.

Section 507(a)(7)(E) contains no direction adopting the Internal Revenue Code's classification. This assessment arises from failure to perform an independent pension-funding obligation and measures the sanction by the accumulated deficiency. Payment neither satisfies that obligation nor replaces the independently due pension/PBGC liability. It therefore punishes the funding default rather than collecting an underlying tax or compensating that loss. Congress's use of the word tax and the provision's excise-tax setting do not overcome that operation. Congress remains free expressly to give a specified assessment priority.

The ten-percent assessment reviewed here is distinct from the additional hundred-percent assessment under section 4971(b). Minimum-funding waivers under section 412(d) and 29 U.S.C. section 1083(a) can remove the relevant deficiency and prevent either assessment; the distinction does not depend on treating only the additional assessment as avoidable. Section 507(a)(7)(G) separately requires a penalty related to a qualifying claim and compensation for actual pecuniary loss, which this assessment does not provide.

The Court adopts no general Lorber checklist, rule against regulatory taxation, or constitutional punishment classification. It leaves the allowed claim intact. Denial of priority does not itself permit subordination, decide dischargeability or proportionality, or restore any other disallowed assessment.

### II. The stated equitable ground cannot place this claim below ordinary unsecured claims

**Controlling proposition:** Section 510(c) does not authorize distributional subordination of this noncompensatory penalty merely because its payment reduces innocent creditors' recoveries. Its equitable authority remains available on a legally sufficient, supported claim-specific ground, but the stated class-based reasons are inadequate even if described as individual balancing; the challenged subordinate ranking must be set aside. Rejection of that equitable ground does not itself confer tax priority; the Part I majority separately resolves priority.

**Authority:** Part II of Souter's Opinion of the Court, joined by all eight other participating Justices, controls the equitable-subordination rule and the remand required by that rule. Thomas's join does not extend to Part I's denial of tax priority.

**Controlling explanation:** Noland preserves section 510(c)'s real statutory power while rejecting a judicial replacement of Congress's distribution choices. After notice and a hearing, the provision permits subordination for distribution of all or part of an allowed claim to another allowed claim, or an allowed interest to another allowed interest, under principles of equitable subordination. Priority status does not confer immunity from that power; lack of priority does not establish a sufficient ground for exercising it.

Here the lower courts relied on the assessment's noncompensatory character, the estate's inability to pay ordinary creditors fully, and the reduction in those creditors' recoveries. They expressly found no inequitable IRS conduct. The stated reasons recur whenever claims of this class compete with compensatory creditors in an insolvent estate. They supply a general preference for compensation, not a distinct equitable circumstance. Calling the process balancing does not change that deficiency.

This prepetition assessment differs from Noland's postpetition administrative penalties. That difference matters to statutory priority, but it does not permit the same inadequate equitable reason to operate within the general-unsecured class. Pepper preserves a misconduct-based equitable application; neither it nor this case establishes misconduct as indispensable to every possible section 510(c) application. The Court announces no frozen catalogue of equitable grounds and no entitlement to relief without misconduct.

The plan's ordinary-unsecured fallback remains available if no independently lawful basis supports different treatment. Properly presented arguments under sections 1122(a) and 1129(a)(7), including the claimed relevance of section 726(a)(4), remain open; the Court neither endorses them nor decides their merits. Any further equitable ground requires lawful presentation and support. No new misconduct hearing, lien transfer, final payment amount, or reopening of every factual issue is ordered.

## Precedent Treatment

- **City of New York v. Feiring, 313 U.S. 283 (1941):** Applies the federal bankruptcy inquiry into the exaction's legal operation rather than the creating statute's label.
- **United States v. New York, 315 U.S. 510 (1942):** Applies that inquiry to a federal exaction and preserves the distinction between a revenue tax that creates incentives and a sanction for violating an independent duty.
- **United States v. Sotelo, 436 U.S. 268 (1978):** Applies the rule that a penalty label does not change the character of liability for already-withheld taxes; distinguishes this independent funding-default sanction.
- **United States v. Noland, May 13, 1996:** Applies the limit on class-based equitable reprioritization, including recurring reasons described as balancing. Preserves statutory equitable susceptibility even for priority claims. Its distinct administrative classification of postpetition tax penalties is not extended to this prepetition assessment.
- **Pepper v. Litton, 308 U.S. 295 (1939):** Preserves its misconduct-based equitable application without converting misconduct into a necessary condition for every section 510(c) case. No precedent is overruled.

## Law After Decision

The allowed 1989 section 4971(a) assessment lacks excise-tax priority because of its funding-default sanction function. That characterization neither disallows it nor independently establishes subordinate rank. Noland's restriction on class-based equity applies within the general-unsecured setting while preserving lawful section 510(c) power. This decision establishes no constitutional punishment rule, universal misconduct prerequisite, automatic distribution, or ruling on the independent Chapter 11 classification and Chapter 7 comparison arguments.

## Separate Writings

Justice Thomas concurs in Part II and dissents from Part I. Congress enacted this federal assessment as a tax properly treated as an excise, and its regulatory or deterrent object does not authorize withdrawing the priority Congress gave excise taxes. He distinguishes the need to prevent a State's label from controlling federal bankruptcy law from the treatment owed Congress's own federal tax choice. The bankruptcy tax inquiry does not, in his view, license the Court to reclassify this federal excise on the majority's functional ground. He would reverse the penalty-based priority ruling and remand consistently with tax treatment, while retaining express Bankruptcy Code limitations; he does not claim that every IRS charge receives priority. He fully agrees that Noland forecloses the stated equitable ground, even for a claim he would classify as a priority tax. His disagreement therefore does not extend to rejection of the challenged subordination, preservation of legally supported statutory equity, or leaving independent plan issues open.

## Procedure After Action

The case returns through the Tenth Circuit for proceedings consistent with both holdings. The stated recurring class reasons cannot sustain subordination under a different label. The plan's Class 12 alternative remains available if no independently lawful basis supports different treatment. Properly presented sections 1122(a) and 1129(a)(7) arguments, the asserted relevance of section 726(a)(4), and any legally sufficient supported equitable ground remain undecided. The Court orders no new misconduct hearing, factual reopening, lien transfer or fixed distribution.

## Source Notes

The governing priority text is pre-1994 11 U.S.C. section 507(a)(7)(E), with its distinct actual-pecuniary-loss penalty provision in (G); the later subsection (a)(8) numbering does not govern. Section 510(c)'s notice-and-hearing, distribution and allowed-claim/interest terms remain operative. Section 4971(a) specifies ten percent, or five percent for a multiemployer plan; the actual ten-percent assessment and qualifying plan status are undisputed. The reviewed liability is separate from section 4971(b)'s additional assessment and the underlying pension obligations. The lower record is reported at 53 F.3d 1155. No final plan distribution or independently sufficient alternative ground was adjudicated below.
