## Event

United States v. Armstrong, 517 U.S. 456, No. 95-157. Argued February 26, 1996; decided May 13, 1996; October Term 1995. On certiorari to the en banc Ninth Circuit, 48 F.3d 1508 (March 2, 1995), which affirmed dismissal following the Government's refusal to comply with selective-prosecution discovery. The appeal below arose under 18 U.S.C. §3731 from dismissal, not directly from the discovery order.

Five Black defendants faced federal cocaine-base and firearm charges after a joint local-police/ATF investigation. State charges were never filed against them. They alleged racial selection for federal rather than state prosecution and sought charging information. No court found discrimination on the merits or adjudicated guilt.

Beller's declaration identified 24 Black defendants and no non-Black defendants in the federal public defender's cocaine-base matters closed during 1991, excluding out-of-district matters and cases in which retained counsel appeared before post-indictment arraignment. Reed described firsthand professional experience with other-race state crack-sale defendants, including repeat offenders. A separate declaration relayed a treatment professional's observations about users and dealers. These sources have different foundations and do not describe the entire population eligible for federal charging.

On reconsideration, Scheper offered sworn charging reasons involving drug quantities, repeated transactions, multiple participants, joint investigation, firearm allegations, recordings, threats and criminal histories, and denied racial selection. Investigators described the small subset referred federally and Black offenders prosecuted by the State. The Government identified non-Black federal defendants as well. Those proffers remain contested assertions, not judicial findings of neutrality, and do not logically disprove the precisely bounded closed-1991 sample.

The adjudicated order concerned cases involving both cocaine-base and federal-firearm charges, defendants' race, investigating agencies and federal-selection criteria. The September 8 hearing specified 1989 through 1992; a later crack-only exchange is ambiguous and does not establish an expanded order. The judge said she considered all parties' evidence; disputed reliance on supplemental declarations does not remove them from the record. The newspaper was discounted, and the Berk–Campbell study was outside the original district-court record.

## Participation

Chief Justice Alex-Lamar Stone-Zsela and Justices Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer participate at argument and decision. No Justice is recused or otherwise nonparticipating.

## Public Action

The Ninth Circuit's judgment is vacated, the discovery order and its dependent dismissal are set aside, and the case is remanded for a limited inquiry into appropriately bounded existing records, which this record justifies, under the staged framework stated here.

## Holdings

### I. Wayte's effect-and-purpose standard governs the selective-prosecution claim

**Controlling proposition:** A claim that federal prosecutors selected defendants for prosecution because of race is governed by Wayte's equal-protection standard: the defendant must ultimately prove that the charging decision had a discriminatory effect and was motivated by a discriminatory purpose. The Constitution does not require that prosecutions be proportional to offending or arrest rates among racial groups, and the showing that opens limited discovery is distinct from the proof that establishes a violation.

**Authority:** Part I of Breyer's Opinion of the Court, joined by Stone-Zsela, Stevens, Souter and Ginsburg; five. O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy and Thomas agree in their separate opinion that Wayte's effect-and-purpose standard governs, without joining the Court's opinion.

**Controlling explanation:** Wayte preserves broad prosecutorial discretion within constitutional limits: a charging decision may not deliberately rest on race, and a claimant must establish both discriminatory effect and discriminatory purpose. Yick Wo shows that discriminatory administration of a neutral law can be proved through a properly connected pattern. McCleskey requires that a statistical pattern be connected to the particular decision challenged; it does not exclude statistics categorically. The Court adds no proportionality requirement. A difference between the racial composition of those prosecuted and that of a general population, or of those who commit an offense, does not alone show that similarly situated persons were treated differently, and the Constitution does not command proportional charging.

Wayte reserved how a defendant may obtain evidence to support such a claim, and that question is distinct from the merits. Requiring completed proof of discrimination before any access to evidence would leave the constitutional limit unenforceable where the relevant information lies with the Government; allowing discovery on a bare allegation would expose ordinary charging decisions to inquiry without reason to believe the limit was breached. Parts II through IV resolve the reserved question. Whatever discovery follows, the claim remains Wayte's: the merits require effect and purpose, and no disclosure order supplies either. Whether direct evidence of discriminatory purpose dispenses with similarly situated comparison on the merits is not decided.

### II. Rule 16(a)(1)(C) reaches existing, material records for a properly presented constitutional defense capable of defeating the prosecution

**Controlling proposition:** Rule 16(a)(1)(C)'s provision for items material to the preparation of the defendant's defense includes a properly presented constitutional defense capable of defeating the prosecution. The selective-prosecution defense presented here is one such defense. The Rule authorizes inspection of existing, material records in the Government's possession, custody or control that subdivision (a)(2), subdivision (a)(3) or a privilege does not protect; it does not authorize an order compelling the Government to create statistics or compilations that do not exist, and subdivision (d)(1)'s protective-order provision is not a source of disclosure authority.

**Authority:** Part II of Breyer's Opinion of the Court, joined by Stone-Zsela, Stevens, Souter and Ginsburg; five. O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy and Thomas would hold that the provision does not authorize discovery to prepare this claim.

**Controlling explanation:** Paragraph (a)(1)(C) covers enumerated documents and objects through three disjunctive triggers: materiality to preparation of the defense, intended use in the Government's evidence in chief, or acquisition from or ownership by the defendant. The triggers perform different work. The second already reaches the Government's case in chief, so confining the first to rebutting that case would leave it little to do. A defense is an answer that, if established, defeats the prosecution; Rule 12(b)(1) treats defenses and objections based on defects in the institution of the prosecution as defenses to be raised before trial. A constitutional objection that would bar the prosecution is such a defense, and a proved selective-prosecution claim is one. Materiality still does real work: information is material only when it is significantly helpful to the defense as properly presented, which here depends on the threshold inquiry in Part III.

The Rule's structure confines what may be obtained. Paragraph (a)(2), except as provided in (a)(1)(A), (B), (D) and (E), excludes reports, memoranda and other internal Government documents made in connection with the investigation or prosecution of the case, and limits Government witness statements through §3500; paragraph (a)(3) preserves the grand-jury exceptions in Rules 6, 12(i), 26.2 and (a)(1)(A); privileges remain. Aggregate records, general selection criteria and internal case deliberations are therefore not automatically identical categories. The Rule permits inspection and copying of what exists; it imposes no duty to compile new data or to explain decisions in writing.

Protective and sanction powers regulate an established duty; they do not create it. Rule 16(d)(1) permits the court to deny, restrict or defer disclosure on a sufficient showing, including an ex parte written showing preserved under seal, and (d)(2) permits just responses to noncompliance. Respondents argued the Rule here, and the Government answered through materiality while denying that it sought a new categorical exemption; the Court resolves the presented interpretation without finding waiver. Because the Rule supplies the source for this claim, the Court does not decide whether inherent authority independently supports disclosure, and it decides no question about nonconstitutional defenses unrelated to guilt.

### III. Discovery proceeds through a threshold inquiry, focused disclosure of existing records and the merits

**Controlling proposition:** Selective-prosecution discovery proceeds in three stages. First, the court conducts a threshold inquiry: the defendant must present specific and reliable evidence that raises a substantial question of discriminatory effect and purpose in the relevant enforcement setting and identifies the information needed to resolve it; full comparator evidence is not indispensable when the defendant shows that it is reasonably unavailable. Second, if the threshold is met, the court may order focused disclosure of existing, material, unprotected records within a bounded offense class and period. Third, the claim is decided on the merits under Wayte; a disclosure order establishes neither discrimination nor dismissal.

**Authority:** Part III of Breyer's Opinion of the Court, joined by Stone-Zsela, Stevens, Souter and Ginsburg; five. O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy and Thomas dissent from this Part.

**Controlling explanation:** Wade's Part B requires a substantial threshold showing of improper motive before discovery or an evidentiary hearing about a refused substantial-assistance motion; useful assistance and generalized accusations do not suffice. The first stage retains that screen in the charging setting. Generalized allegations, demographic assumptions about who commits crimes and a selected count of those prosecuted do not meet it. The showing must concern the enforcement setting actually at issue: here, selection for federal rather than state prosecution of the offenses charged.

Similarly situated comparison remains the ordinary route to an inference of discriminatory effect. Klinger ties similarity to the challenged treatment without requiring identity, and Kimberlin permits statistical and circumstantial proof subject to specific support and lawful, tailored discovery limits. Where the information that would identify comparable persons passed over lies with the Government and cannot reasonably be obtained elsewhere, the defendant need not supply complete comparator evidence at the threshold, but must show that unavailability and offer other specific evidence connecting the asserted disparity to the selection. The threshold is less than a prima facie merits case; it asks whether a grounded inference exists, not whether discrimination has been proved.

At the second stage disclosure completes a grounded inference; it does not search for one. An order defines a bounded offense class and period, takes legitimate selection distinctions into account and protects subdivision (a)(2) and (a)(3) material and privileges. It begins with the least intrusive records adequate to the inquiry, and more intrusive discovery requires correspondingly stronger support. At the third stage the defendant must prove effect and purpose. The Government's explanations are tested there rather than accepted as findings at the threshold, and a disclosure order neither presumes discrimination nor authorizes dismissal.

### IV. A disclosure order must account for executive interests and legitimate selection distinctions

**Controlling proposition:** The staged inquiry is not routine supervision of charging policy. An order for disclosure must explain why the records are material to the claim, why less intrusive evidence is inadequate and how confidentiality and privileges are protected, and it must account for legitimate distinctions bearing on federal selection, including federal eligibility, drug quantities, strength of the evidence, criminal history, weapons and enforcement priorities. Direct evidence of a discriminatory directive can justify inquiry without comparators; statistics alone must connect to the mechanism by which defendants are selected for federal prosecution.

**Authority:** Part IV of Breyer's Opinion of the Court, joined by Stone-Zsela, Stevens, Souter and Ginsburg; five. O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy and Thomas dissent from this Part.

**Controlling explanation:** Charging decisions rest on considerations courts are ill-suited to second-guess: the strength of the case, deterrence, enforcement priorities and the relation of a case to the Government's overall enforcement plan. Wayte and Wade recognize the costs of exposing those judgments to inquiry. Those interests shape the inquiry without foreclosing it. The required explanation keeps disclosure tied to the constitutional claim rather than to general curiosity about prosecutorial practice, and it creates a record for review.

The listed distinctions are the ordinary reasons federal and state charging differ. Federal eligibility depends on the federal offense and its elements; quantity, firearm involvement, the strength and form of the evidence and a defendant's record bear on whether a case is referred and charged federally. A comparison that ignores them does not show that similarly situated persons were treated differently. They are not a mandatory checklist; their relevance depends on the claim and the enforcement setting.

Statistics can support an inference of discriminatory selection, but only when they speak to the selection mechanism—who was referred, who was eligible and who was charged—rather than to general offending, arrest or prosecution patterns that do not describe the challenged decision. Direct evidence of a directive to select defendants by race is different: it bears on the decision itself and can justify inquiry without comparators. Protective orders under subdivision (d)(1), redaction and ex parte review accommodate confidentiality within an authorized disclosure; they do not themselves authorize one.

### V. The order and dependent dismissal are set aside, and this record justifies a limited inquiry, though not the original order

**Controlling proposition:** The discovery order and the dismissal for refusing it are set aside, and the Ninth Circuit's judgment is vacated. The 24-case sample, the declarant Reed's state and federal experience, the severity of the federal charging consequences and the limits of the Government's initial explanations justify a limited inquiry, though not every component of the original order. On remand the district court must identify appropriately bounded existing records bearing on federal referral and selection criteria, including available anonymized information concerning relevantly comparable cases; if suitable records do not exist, it reassesses the alternatives, and the Government has no duty to produce what it lacks. If the Government refuses a valid order, the sanction is fitted to the violation, and dismissal is not automatic.

**Authority:** Part V of Breyer's Opinion of the Court, joined by Stone-Zsela, Stevens, Souter and Ginsburg; five. O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy and Thomas concur in setting aside the order and the dismissal on their separate ground, so that component is unanimous; they would reverse and remand for ordinary pretrial proceedings without a directed inquiry.

**Controlling explanation:** The order was entered before the inquiry these stages require and without the explanation Part IV demands. It called for information about covered cases from 1989 through 1992, the defendants' race, the investigating agencies and the Government's selection criteria, without determining which existing records were material and unprotected or why less intrusive evidence was inadequate. To the extent it required the Government to compile information or explain its decisions rather than permit inspection of existing records, Rule 16 supplied no authority for it. The order cannot stand, and neither can the dismissal for refusing it. This conclusion does not decide whether the same sanction would be appropriate after refusal of a valid order.

Neither court below applied the framework stated here, but the developed record permits its application. Beller's sample of 24 Black defendants in the defender's cocaine-base matters closed during 1991 supplies a numerator, not an eligible charging population, and by itself would not support the original order. The declarant Reed's firsthand state and federal experience supplies a specific contrast in the enforcement setting at issue; the federal charging consequences are severe; and the Government's initial explanations, contested assertions rather than findings of neutrality, leave unanswered how cases are referred and selected for federal prosecution. The information identifying comparable persons passed over lies with the Government and cannot reasonably be obtained elsewhere. Considered with the Government's response and the distinctions in Part IV, these submissions raise a substantial question and identify the needed information: they justify a limited inquiry, though not every component of the original order.

On remand the district court must identify appropriately bounded existing records bearing on federal referral and selection criteria, including available anonymized information concerning relevantly comparable cases. If suitable records do not exist, it reassesses the alternatives; the Government has no duty to produce what it does not have. The Court orders no new evidentiary submission, finds no discrimination and decides no remedy for proved selective prosecution. Williams's grand-jury duty and without-prejudice remedy do not assign a prejudice label here, and Carlisle confines supervision to the governing rules.

## Precedent Treatment

- Wayte v. United States, 470 U.S. 598: preserves its effect-and-purpose limits on broad charging discretion without adding a proportionality requirement, and resolves the discovery question it expressly reserved.
- McCleskey v. Kemp, 481 U.S. 279: preserves the need to connect a statistical pattern to the challenged decision, and distinguishes the showing that opens limited discovery from the proof that establishes a violation; it supplies no categorical statistical exclusion.
- Wade v. United States, May 18, 1992: applies its Part B requirement of a substantial threshold showing before discovery or an evidentiary hearing, retained as the first-stage screen in the charging setting; its sentencing rules are unchanged.
- Kimberlin v. Quinlan, June 12, 1995: applies the availability of statistical and circumstantial proof without a categorical direct-evidence requirement, together with specific support and lawful, tailored discovery limits.
- Klinger v. Department of Corrections, December 4, 1995: applies comparison tied to the challenged treatment, here federal selection, without demanding complete identity or treating disparity as sufficient proof of purpose.
- Yick Wo v. Hopkins, 118 U.S. 356: preserves discriminatory-administration inferences on a properly connected pattern; statistics must connect to the selection mechanism.
- United States v. Williams, May 4, 1992: its bounded grand-jury disclosure duty and separate supervisory-dismissal conditions remain intact; neither governs this disclosure duty, and a sanction for refusing a valid order is fitted to the violation.
- Carlisle v. United States, April 29, 1996: applies its requirement that supervision remain consistent with the governing rules; the Court construes Rule 16 itself and confines disclosure to the Rule's inspection authority, so neither protective-order provisions nor general supervisory power substitutes for it.

No precedent is overruled. No civil-discovery regime, jury-selection procedure, competence burden or universal supervisory rule is transferred to this case.

## Law After Decision

Effective May 13, 1996, Wayte's effect-and-purpose standard governs a selective-prosecution claim, with no proportionality requirement. Rule 16(a)(1)(C)'s provision for material preparation of the defense reaches existing, material, unprotected records for a properly presented constitutional defense capable of defeating the prosecution; subdivision (a)(2)'s protection of internal case documents and witness statements, subdivision (a)(3)'s grand-jury exceptions and privileges remain, and neither the Rule nor its protective-order provision authorizes compelled creation of nonexistent statistics. Discovery proceeds in three stages: a threshold inquiry requiring specific, reliable evidence that raises a substantial question in the relevant enforcement setting and identifies the needed information, without indispensable full comparator evidence where its reasonable unavailability is shown; focused disclosure of existing records; and the merits. These holdings have five direct votes.

A disclosure order must explain materiality, why less intrusive evidence is inadequate and how confidentiality is protected, and must account for federal eligibility, quantities, evidence, criminal history, weapons and enforcement priorities. Direct evidence of a discriminatory directive can justify inquiry without comparators; statistics alone must connect to the selection mechanism. A disclosure order establishes neither discrimination nor dismissal, and refusal of a valid order warrants a sanction fitted to the violation rather than automatic dismissal. On this record the submissions justify a limited inquiry into appropriately bounded existing records, though not the original order. Rule 16's application to nonconstitutional defenses unrelated to guilt, the source of any authority beyond the Rule, ultimate discrimination and the remedy for a proved violation remain unresolved. The opinion does not find the Government neutral, treat every racial imbalance as discriminatory or establish a universally accessible comparator database.

## Separate Writings

**Stevens, concurring.** He joins the Court's opinion in full. Rule 16 is the source of the defendant's entitlement to inspect existing material records, but in his view it does not displace the district judge's bounded discretion to call for a response to a disturbing evidentiary pattern; that discretion operates within the stages the Court describes and guides the district court in managing the limited inquiry on remand. The defined all-Black sample, Reed's firsthand state and federal experience, the substantial federal/state penalty difference and the judge's consideration of the developed response justify inquiry appreciably short of merits proof, as the Court holds. Relayed observations and firsthand experience should not be conflated, and the absence of a Government admissibility objection below does not establish a universal hearsay rule.

He joins the remand rather than affirming because the original order, which he acknowledges may have been broader than necessary, was entered before the inquiry and explanation the Court requires, and a proportionate order confined to existing records answers that breadth. The remand is not a new unconditional opportunity for the Government to refuse, obtain review and then comply after losing: a valid order binds when entered, and refusal to obey it warrants a sanction fitted to the violation, which may include dismissal where lesser measures would not cure it. Wayte's constitutional limits, Williams's bounded judicial protection and Wade's screening requirement support that structure. He supplies no representative denominator, merits discrimination finding or shifted ultimate burden.

**O'Connor, concurring in part and dissenting in part, joined by Scalia, Kennedy and Thomas.** They concur in the judgment insofar as it sets aside the discovery order and the dependent dismissal, and agree that Wayte's effect-and-purpose standard governs the claim, but would reverse the Ninth Circuit's judgment and remand for ordinary pretrial proceedings. Rule 16(a)(1)(C)'s provision for material preparation of the defense does not authorize discovery to prepare a selective-prosecution claim challenging the decision to prosecute. Preparation of the defense against the prosecution's criminal case differs from the independent constitutional challenge to the selection decision, and the structure of paragraphs (a)(2) and (a)(3), which protect the Government's internal case documents, witness statements and grand-jury material, reinforces that distinction. Protective and sanction powers regulate an established duty; they do not create one. The Rule's nonauthorization is not immunity from constitutionally warranted review, and it does not abolish supervision consistent with the governing rules.

Before discovery on this indirect racial-selection theory, they would require some credible evidence tending to show discriminatory effect and purpose, including a reliable basis to infer that persons of another race relevantly similarly situated for federal prosecution could have been prosecuted but were not. That showing is less than a prima facie merits case, and grounded statistics, circumstantial comparison and a specifically justified alternative to unavailable names may supply it. Beller's selected closed-1991 sample supplies a numerator, not an eligible charging population, and Reed's state-practice experience and the relayed treatment observations do not connect their subjects to the quantities, firearms, evidence and criminal histories relevant to federal selection. Considered with the Government's developed response, which identifies those gaps without proving neutrality, the showing fails on effect. Klinger ties comparison to the challenged treatment, and Wade's screening requires more than a disparity within a selected sample.

Because the developed record permits resolution of the challenged order, they would direct no renewed evidentiary inquiry, would not hold that this record justifies one and would not adopt a staged framework that the Rule's text does not establish; a later lawful request supported under the governing requirements would remain available through ordinary procedures. They decide no remedy for proved selective prosecution, find no neutrality and treat the dismissal as no acquittal.

## Procedure After Action

The case returns through the Ninth Circuit to the district court with the discovery order and refusal-based dismissal set aside. The district court conducts the limited inquiry this record justifies: it must identify appropriately bounded existing records bearing on federal referral and selection criteria, including available anonymized information concerning relevantly comparable cases, and may enter a focused order for their inspection with the required explanation; if suitable records do not exist, it reassesses the alternatives, and the Government has no duty to produce what it lacks. The Court orders no new evidentiary submission and leaves any further submission to ordinary procedure. Refusal of a valid order permits a sanction fitted to the violation, not automatic dismissal. O'Connor's proposed ordinary remand does not control the mandate. No dismissal-prejudice label, charging allocation, state prosecution, conviction, sentence or new trial after acquittal is ordered. Supreme Court review of this discovery dispute is complete, with ordinary mandate procedures and no prescribed subsequent Court event.
