## Event

**Whren v. United States, 517 U.S. 806, No. 95-5841 — merits decision, June 10, 1996.** Argued April 17, 1996, on writ of certiorari to the D.C. Circuit. Petitioners challenge the initial traffic restraint by plainclothes officers investigating drugs, contending that reasonable officers would not ordinarily have stopped them for the traffic purpose alone. The later search is not independently presented.

The district judge acknowledged discrepancies but credited Officer Soto's account. The vehicle waited unusually long at a stop sign, turned without signaling and traveled at unreasonable speed. Officers approached after it was already stopped at another intersection, directed it to park and then saw drugs in plain view. The Court of Appeals sustained traffic probable cause under the full-attention, signaling and speed provisions, 18 D.C.M.R. §§2213.4, 2204.3 and 2200.3.

The Court of Appeals rejected suppression on this ground but remanded by agreement to correct lesser-included §841 convictions and resentence under §860. That correction has occurred: the lesser convictions were vacated, the original prison terms were generally reimposed, Whren's fine was not, and the new sentencing appeals were held pending this review.

Petitioners' updated conflict discussion recognizes that Botero-Ospina, 71 F.3d 783, moved the Tenth Circuit to the objective-probable-cause approach; the cited Ninth and Eleventh Circuit decisions represented the remaining contrary line.

## Participation

Chief Justice Stone-Zsela and Justices Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer participate at argument and decision.

## Public Action

The judgment is vacated insofar as it rejects the initial-stop Fourth Amendment suppression challenge, and the case is remanded for application of the presumption-and-rebuttal framework. The completed vacatur of the lesser convictions and resentencing is undisturbed.

## Judgment & Remedy

**Vacated in part and remanded on the initial-stop issue, 5–4.**

**Supporting:** Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Ginsburg, Breyer. **Opposing:** Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, who would affirm.

The Court neither repeats the completed §841 vacatur and §860 resentencing nor decides the later sentencing appeals. If the Government establishes a lawful initial seizure, the remaining convictions stand against this challenge; otherwise the exclusionary-rule consequences are decided below. No acquittal, suppression, release or new trial is ordered by this Court. The holding supplies no independent determination of the later search or every ground of evidence admissibility.

## Opinion Topology

Justice Ginsburg delivers the opinion of the Court, joined in full by Chief Justice Stone-Zsela and Justices Stevens, O'Connor and Breyer. Justice Thomas dissents, joined by Justices Scalia, Kennedy and Souter.

## Holdings

**Authority:** Ginsburg's opinion, joined by the four other Justices identified in Opinion Topology, supplies five direct votes for each rule, application and qualification below. Scalia, Kennedy, Souter and Thomas dissent and would affirm.

### I. Probable cause of an observed violation makes the stop presumptively reasonable

**Operative rule:** An initial traffic stop supported by probable cause to believe that an observed traffic violation occurred is presumptively reasonable under the Fourth Amendment. The officer need not show the absence of an additional investigative motive, and an unrelated investigative aim does not by itself make the stop unreasonable.

**Controlling explanation:** Scott requires objective assessment of the action actually justified, and Robinson supports that distinction within its lawful-arrest and search setting. Prouse restricts suspicionless discretionary vehicle stops; known violations supply the individualized basis missing there. Probable cause therefore carries real constitutional weight, and officers need not prove a pure purpose. But the Fourth Amendment's command is reasonableness, and an individualized basis that pervasive minor offenses make available against nearly any motorist does not answer every claim that the authority it confers was used for something else. Bertine and Wells forbid using inventory authority as a ruse for general investigatory rummaging, and Burger confines administrative inspection to its qualifying purpose. Those decisions show that objectively available authority can be misused as an instrument for an unrelated investigation. Probable cause therefore supplies a presumption, not a conclusive answer to objective evidence of that misuse.

### II. Specific objective evidence of instrumental use can rebut the presumption

**Operative rule:** The presumption is rebutted only by specific, articulable, objective evidence supporting a reasonable inference that traffic authority was used as an instrument for an unrelated investigation lacking its own sufficient grounds. Relevant factors include material deviation from established enforcement practices; the nature and seriousness of the violation; departure from the officer's ordinary assignment or authority; comparable enforcement patterns; contemporaneous conduct and statements; and deliberate selection on an impermissible criterion. No factor is conclusive. Motive alone, the ordinary exercise of enforcement discretion, or a violation of internal policy alone does not suffice. The threshold showing precedes any further inquiry and creates no entitlement to unrestricted discovery.

**Controlling explanation:** Petitioners propose an objective would-have test, not merely an inquiry into personal intent, and their argument that pervasive minor violations permit arbitrary selection is substantial. A test turning on an officer's account of his own purpose would produce different results for identical conduct, and the Court does not adopt one. The rebuttal asks instead what objective circumstances show about how traffic authority was actually used. Kimberlin permits competent circumstantial proof where an improper aim is legally material, while requiring specific, nonconclusory support and lawful limits on discovery; the same structure governs here. Departmental rules and practices are evidence on this question, not its measure: a deviation from them can support the inference, and compliance with them cannot by itself defeat it. Requiring the threshold showing first protects ordinary enforcement from routine hearings and keeps the inquiry within the suppression record.

### III. After the threshold showing, the Government must justify the stop

**Operative rule:** Once the threshold showing is made, the Government must prove by a preponderance of the evidence either independent lawful investigative grounds for the stop or that an objectively reasonable traffic-enforcement decision would have produced materially the same stop without the unsupported investigative objective. That second inquiry is not controlled by departmental custom; courts independently examine whether the traffic purpose is genuine and sufficient. Safety, dangerous driving and consistently applied enforcement priorities can justify the stop, but a department cannot make arbitrary enforcement constitutional by adopting it as policy. Limits on the scope, duration and search authority of the stop apply under either branch.

**Controlling explanation:** The Government is best placed to explain the enforcement decisions it makes, and a stop that would have occurred for genuine traffic reasons in any event is not unreasonable because officers also hoped to investigate something else. Independent grounds, such as reasonable suspicion or probable cause of the unrelated offense, answer the showing directly. Variable local priorities, uniforms, assignments and vehicle markings do not themselves fix constitutional authority, which is why the second branch asks what an objectively reasonable traffic-enforcement decision would have produced rather than what a department's customs require. Courts decide that question themselves under Ornelas's allocation, with clear-error protection for historical findings and genuine due weight to grounded contextual inferences. Wilson's separation of authority from reasonable execution means that a justified stop still may not exceed what its traffic basis permits, and Garner, Welsh and Winston retain their distinct limits on deadly force, home entry and bodily intrusion.

### IV. Equal protection remains an independent prohibition

**Operative rule:** The Fourth Amendment rule requires no proof of racial discrimination, and purposeful racial selection remains independently forbidden by Equal Protection. Disparities alone do not prove discriminatory intent. Armstrong's Rule 16 reading and staged discovery inquiry govern federal prosecutorial discovery and are not transplanted into police-seizure challenges.

**Controlling explanation:** Deliberate selection on an impermissible criterion is a rebuttal factor because it bears on instrumental use of traffic authority, but the Fourth Amendment inquiry does not require proof of an equal-protection violation, and the equal-protection claim keeps its own elements of discriminatory effect and purpose. Soto's denial of racial motivation and the district judge's credit retain their procedural status; the Court enters no discrimination finding. Armstrong addresses discovery into a federal charging decision through Rule 16 and a staged inquiry; the threshold here concerns objective evidence presented on a suppression motion about a police seizure, and neither framework supplies the other's conditions. No discovery entitlement, immunity, damages remedy or suppression consequence for discriminatory selection is decided, and the supposed adequacy of another remedy is not a ground for this judgment.

### V. The credited record presents a substantial question for the lower courts

**Operative application:** The credited observations of a vehicle waiting unusually long at a stop sign, turning without signaling and traveling at unreasonable speed supply probable cause under the full-attention, signaling and speed provisions, 18 D.C.M.R. §§2213.4, 2204.3 and 2200.3, so the stop is presumptively reasonable. The stop was made by plainclothes officers investigating drugs. The departmental limits on plainclothes traffic enforcement and the surrounding circumstances raise a substantial issue under the rebuttal standard, and the driving may supply a genuine safety justification. The lower courts decide whether the threshold is met and, if so, whether the Government establishes either justification.

**Controlling explanation:** General Order 303.1, in its April 30, 1992 text, permits traffic enforcement by on-duty uniformed officers in marked vehicles; off-duty uniformed officers in marked vehicles participating in the take-home cruiser program; and on-duty uniformed Public Vehicle Enforcement Unit officers in unmarked vehicles. Other plainclothes or unmarked-vehicle enforcement requires a violation so grave as to pose an immediate threat to others' safety. A traffic stop ordinarily requires a Notice of Infraction or a Warning Notice of Infraction; an oral warning may be given in extreme circumstances, such as an immediate-response radio assignment or emergency transport of a sick or injured passenger to a hospital. The Court finds neither compliance nor violation and does not equate unreasonable speed with the immediate-threat exception. A departure from the order is evidence bearing on instrumental use, not proof of it, and a policy violation alone does not rebut the presumption.

The Court of Appeals sustained the stop on traffic probable cause alone and did not apply the standard adopted here. Ornelas protects the district judge's acceptance of Soto's account and other historical findings under the clear-error standard, with genuine due weight to grounded contextual inferences; the ultimate constitutional judgment under the new standard is independent. Independent judgment supplies neither another violation nor supposed antecedent drug probable cause, and officer experience does not establish the General Order's safety exception. Plainclothes status alone establishes no unusual execution, and missing duration or force details establish no affirmative finding. The subsequent search remains outside the independently presented issue.

## Precedent Treatment

- **Scott v. United States, 436 U.S. 128:** Applies objective assessment of the justified action; probable cause supplies a presumption without making motive alone a ground of invalidity or motives irrelevant to every constitutional claim.
- **United States v. Robinson, 414 U.S. 218, and Gustafson v. Florida, 414 U.S. 260:** Preserve their lawful-arrest/search settings; an additional investigative purpose does not itself remove objective authority, and no new arrest or search power is created here.
- **United States v. Villamonte-Marquez, 462 U.S. 579:** Its objectively authorized customs-inspection setting supports the same distinction without dispensing with that authority's statutory and constitutional predicates.
- **Delaware v. Prouse, 440 U.S. 648:** Distinguishes suspicionless discretionary selection from a stop founded on observed traffic violations; its concern with unconstrained discretion informs the rebuttal, and its constraints remain in force.
- **Colorado v. Bertine, 479 U.S. 367, and Florida v. Wells, 495 U.S. 1:** Preserve standardized or routine inventory constraints and purpose-related discretion; their prohibition on using inventory as an investigatory ruse is applied to the instrumental use of traffic authority.
- **New York v. Burger, 482 U.S. 691:** Preserves administrative-inspection requirements; its confinement of inspection authority to a qualifying purpose informs the rebuttal without importing its conditions.
- **United States v. Martinez-Fuerte, 428 U.S. 543, and United States v. Brignoni-Ponce, 422 U.S. 873:** Their distinct analyses of intrusions without probable cause do not require an additional ad hoc balance for this ordinary probable-cause stop.
- **Wilson v. Arkansas, May 22, 1995:** Applies the separation between authority and reasonable execution while preserving its actual home-entry qualifications and unresolved remedy.
- **Tennessee v. Garner, 471 U.S. 1; Welsh v. Wisconsin, 466 U.S. 740; and Winston v. Lee, 470 U.S. 753:** Preserve their distinct limits on unusually harmful execution and do not make plainclothes traffic intervention categorically immune from manner review.
- **Ornelas v. United States, May 28, 1996:** Applies independent constitutional judgment with protected historical findings and genuine contextual due weight; it governs review of the threshold and justification determinations and creates no new factual dispute here.
- **Kimberlin v. Quinlan, June 12, 1995:** Applied: competent circumstantial proof of a legally material improper aim, with specific nonconclusory support and lawful discovery limits, informs the rebuttal threshold without making motive alone an element of this stop rule.
- **United States v. Armstrong, May 13, 1996:** Its Rule 16 reading and staged charging-selection inquiry keep their federal prosecutorial-discovery scope, and its discrimination remedy remains unresolved; no police-stop discovery rule is adopted from it.
- **Abel v. United States, 362 U.S. 217, and Colorado v. Bannister, 449 U.S. 1:** Their regular-procedure and no-pretext observations neither establish petitioners' would-have rule nor bar the rebuttal adopted here. No precedent is overruled.

## Law After Decision

Effective June 10, 1996, an initial traffic stop supported by probable cause of an observed violation is presumptively reasonable, and an unrelated investigative motive alone does not make it unreasonable. The presumption yields only to specific, articulable, objective evidence that traffic authority was used as an instrument for an unrelated investigation lacking its own sufficient grounds, judged on the listed factors, none conclusive; motive alone, ordinary discretion or an internal policy violation alone is insufficient, and the threshold precedes any further inquiry without unrestricted discovery. After that showing, the Government must prove by a preponderance independent lawful grounds or that an objectively reasonable traffic-enforcement decision would have produced materially the same stop; departmental custom does not control that inquiry, and a department cannot constitutionalize arbitrary enforcement.

Inventory and administrative justifications retain their own purpose and procedural limits. Execution, duration, scope and search limits apply in either branch. Purposeful discriminatory selection remains independently forbidden by Equal Protection; disparities alone do not prove intent, and Armstrong's prosecutorial-discovery framework is not transferred to police-seizure challenges. No automatic suppression, acquittal or new exclusionary rule follows. Ornelas's allocation remains unchanged.

## Separate Writings

**Thomas, joined by Scalia, Kennedy and Souter, dissenting.** The dissent would affirm. An ordinary initial traffic stop supported by objective probable cause of a traffic violation does not become unreasonable because officers also seek to investigate an unrelated offense, and its validity should not additionally depend on whether reasonable officers ordinarily would have enforced the violation or on departmental custom. Scott requires objective assessment of the action actually justified; Prouse restricts suspicionless stops, and known violations supply the individualized basis missing there. Local priorities, uniforms, assignments and vehicle markings cannot change constitutional authority for the same known violation, and a test built on them must select the relevant custom and resolve its exceptions without explaining why custom controls the constitutional boundary. Bertine, Wells and Burger confine purpose conditions to the inventory and administrative exceptions whose authority depends on them; neither Bannister's no-pretext observation nor Abel's recital of regular procedure holds that deviation from practice invalidates an otherwise justified seizure.

The dissent regards the Court's rebuttal and justification inquiries as restoring that customary-enforcement condition through a burden placed on the Government, and the proposed balance of traffic-safety gains against plainclothes intervention as adding no condition for an ordinary probable-cause stop. It would preserve review of actual manner, scope and duration, Wilson's separation of authority from execution and the distinct limits of Garner, Welsh and Winston. It agrees that purposeful racial selection is independently forbidden by Equal Protection and that the adequacy of another remedy is no ground for decision. On the credited record and Ornelas's allocation, it would leave the rejection of suppression intact without a remand.

## Procedure After Action

The rejection of suppression on the initial-stop ground is vacated, and the case is remanded for the lower courts to determine whether petitioners make the threshold showing and, if so, whether the Government establishes independent lawful grounds or that an objectively reasonable traffic-enforcement decision would have produced materially the same stop. If the Government establishes a lawful initial seizure, the remaining convictions stand against this challenge; otherwise the exclusionary-rule consequences are for the lower courts. The Court orders no acquittal, suppression, release, new trial or sentencing proceeding. The completed vacatur of the lesser-included §841 convictions and resentencing concerning §860 remain undisturbed, including nonreimposition of Whren's fine. The later sentencing appeals remain subject to their own lawful disposition; no outcome or special timing is prescribed. The Court decides no independent challenge to the subsequent search. Supreme Court merits review is complete, subject to ordinary mandate procedures.
