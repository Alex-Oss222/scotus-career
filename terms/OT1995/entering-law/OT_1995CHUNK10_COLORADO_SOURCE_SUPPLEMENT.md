## Route, proceedings and scope established by the sources

The proceeding is review under 28 U.S.C. §1254(1) of the Tenth Circuit's June 23, 1995 judgment, 59 F.3d 1015; rehearing was denied September 6, 1995. The district court's opinion is 839 F. Supp. 1448. Both lower decisions are reproduced in the Joint Appendix, pp.17–49. The court of appeals did more than order an unspecified remand: it directed entry of judgment for the FEC and determination of the appropriate civil penalty under §437g(a)(6), 59 F.3d at 1024–1025. No penalty amount has thereby been fixed.

The petitioners' merits brief, p.i, presents (1) the First Amendment validity of §441a(d), (2) an express-advocacy construction to avoid vagueness, and (3) deference to the FEC's statutory interpretation. The respondent frames its questions as whether the payments fall under contribution limitations, including coordinated expenditures, and whether applying the Act to these payments violates the First Amendment. These are party formulations; they do not independently enlarge the supplied question granted. The breadth of the counterclaim and a possible remand for remaining claims must be distinguished from the narrow independent-expenditure question in the supplied packet.

## Supported record facts and their source status

The state committee was federally registered and unincorporated. In April 1986, before either party had selected its Senate nominee, it spent $15,000 for the radio advertisement called “Wirth Facts #1.” Wirth had registered as a candidate for the Democratic nomination. The advertisement identified Republican Party sponsorship, challenged Wirth's representations of his defense and balanced-budget record, and mentioned his Senate candidacy. It did not contain the narrow express words of advocacy used in Buckley and MCFL; both lower courts accepted that characterization. The appellate court instead treated it as an electioneering message identifying a candidate. See 59 F.3d 1017–1023 and n.10.

The state committee had assigned to the National Republican Senatorial Committee its authority to make all $103,248 of the committee's permitted §441a(d)(3) expenditures for the 1986 Senate contest. The lower court accordingly treated the additional $15,000 as exceeding that ceiling if the challenged advertising fell within the provision. That amount is the adjusted 1986 state-party allowance, not the statutory base amount or a general constitutional minimum. The committee reported the expense as voter-information advertising, rather than a §441a(d) disbursement. The FEC filed this civil enforcement action after administrative proceedings and unsuccessful settlement efforts. See 59 F.3d 1018–1019.

Actual candidate participation must remain distinct from the lower courts' legal presumption. The lower decisions call the spending coordinated because they accepted the FEC's categorical treatment of parties; that label is not an independent factual finding of consultation, request, suggestion or candidate control. The supplied neutral packet's independent-expenditure premise remains the premise to be evaluated. The original deposition is available: Joint Appendix pp.188–214. At pp.199–205, former chair Howard Callaway states that he approved the script, identifies possible internal party reviewers, describes the discussions leading to the ads, and notes substantial failures of recollection about authorship, production and particular meetings. At pp.192–196 he separately describes the party's general work with candidates and national committees. Those general descriptions do not themselves establish candidate involvement in this particular advertisement. At pp.207–208 he describes use of federally permissible funds. His views of Wirth's record are testimony explaining the advertising, not judicial findings that the accusations were true.

## Statutory terms verified against the official 1994 Code

Section 441a(d)(1): “Notwithstanding any other provision of law with respect to limitations on expenditures or limitations on contributions, the national committee of a political party and a State committee of a political party, including any subordinate committee of a State committee, may make expenditures in connection with the general election campaign of candidates for Federal office, subject to the limitations contained in paragraphs (2) and (3) of this subsection.”

Section 441a(d)(3): “The national committee of a political party, or a State committee of a political party, including any subordinate committee of a State committee, may not make any expenditure in connection with the general election campaign of a candidate for Federal office in a State who is affiliated with such party which exceeds—

“(A) in the case of a candidate for election to the office of Senator, or of Representative from a State which is entitled to only one Representative, the greater of—

“(i) 2 cents multiplied by the voting age population of the State (as certified under subsection (e) of this section); or

“(ii) $20,000; and

“(B) in the case of a candidate for election to the office of Representative, Delegate, or Resident Commissioner in any other State, $10,000.”

Section 441a(c) separately increases the subsection (b) and (d) limits by the certified percentage difference between the preceding calendar year's Consumer Price Index and the 1974 base period; the increased amount applies for that calendar year. Section 441a(e) supplies annual Commerce certification and defines voting-age population as resident population age eighteen or older. Section 441a(d)(2)'s presidential national-party allowance is distinct: two cents multiplied by United States voting-age population, in addition to expenditures by a national committee serving as a presidential candidate's principal campaign committee.

Section 441a(a)(7)(B)(i): “expenditures made by any person in cooperation, consultation, or concert, with, or at the request or suggestion of, a candidate, his authorized political committees, or their agents, shall be considered to be a contribution to such candidate”. Subparagraph (ii) separately addresses financing dissemination, distribution or republication of campaign materials prepared by the candidate, committees or authorized agents. Section 441a(a)(8) separately treats contributions earmarked or directed through an intermediary or conduit as contributions from the original person and requires identification of the original source and intended recipient. These are distinct statutory mechanisms, not proof that they occurred here.

The official Code is corroborated by Joint Appendix pp.216–223. Its amendment note shows the October 1986 change affected Internal Revenue Code references in other subsections, not the quoted §441a(d) rule. [Official §441a](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/USCODE-1994-title2/html/USCODE-1994-title2-chap14-subchapI-sec441a.htm).

## The lower court's grounds and the recorded opposing arguments

The district court construed “expenditures in connection with” narrowly, relying on Buckley and MCFL, and dismissed the counterclaim as moot once the FEC enforcement claim failed. The Tenth Circuit rejected that construction, distinguished party expenditures it deemed coordinated from independent corporate spending, deferred to FEC advisory opinions 1984-15 and 1985-14, and applied a clearly-identified-candidate plus electioneering-message standard. It relied on corruption and its appearance, the parties' candidate relationship, and Congress's electoral experience to sustain the restriction. The appellate opinion expressly acknowledges that Buckley's disposition of the party-expenditure provision answered a Fifth Amendment challenge rather than the First Amendment challenge here, 59 F.3d 1024 n.11.

The petitioners' brief argues that party expression and association are directly restricted, that the government must establish a compelling and narrowly served interest, that party speech differs from a transfer to a candidate, that equalization and reducing campaign costs are insufficient, and that express advocacy and constitutional avoidance limit the statute. It also challenges reliance on advisory opinions. The government's brief treats the amounts as contributions under §441a(a)(2), regards subsection (d) as supplementary spending permission, defends anti-circumvention and corruption interests, and opposes an express-advocacy limitation for party electioneering. Its position that parties inherently coordinate is a legal submission, not additional historical evidence of coordination in this transaction. Petitioners' reply pp.10–13 contests that legal classification and argues that party speech cannot simply be relabeled a contribution. These descriptions identify positions actually in the briefs; they make no present ruling on their merit.
