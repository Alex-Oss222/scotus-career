## Event

Hercules, Inc. v. United States, No. 94-818, 516 U.S. 417. Argued October 30, 1995; decided March 4, 1996. On writ of certiorari to the Federal Circuit, 24 F.3d 188, reviewing its May 4, 1994 judgment affirming summary judgments dismissing the separate government-contract suits of Hercules and Thompson.

The questions concern whether the government-contractor tort defense precludes the asserted specifications-warranty claims and whether the procurement circumstances, including Defense Production Act compulsion, establish contractual responsibility for third-party tort settlements and defense expenses. The manufacturers seek further proceedings on their contract claims; the United States seeks affirmance.

The manufacturers supplied Agent Orange under federal specifications, later settled veterans' tort litigation in 1984 and incurred defense expenses. No express governmental indemnity was included. Hercules's fifteen contracts and Thompson's two contracts had distinct production and knowledge histories; Thompson initially declined to supply, while the Government identifies initial competitive awards to Hercules. Alleged later compulsion, manufacturing control and superior knowledge remain distinct from established contractual terms. The Federal Circuit relied on the government-contractor defense to reject settlement causation and separately rejected defense expenses; coverage is the Government's alternative ground here.

## Participation

Chief Justice Stone-Zsela and Justices O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer participate. Justice Stevens takes no part in consideration or decision; no reason is stated.

## Public Action

The Federal Circuit's judgment is affirmed. The Court resolves the preserved contract claims on the absence of the asserted reimbursement undertaking and does not decide the veterans' underlying tort claims.

## Judgment & Remedy

**Affirmed, 6–2. Supporting:** Stone-Zsela, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas and Ginsburg. **Opposing:** O'Connor and Breyer, who would vacate in part and remand the preserved contract claims for further proceedings.

The same six-Justice coalition affirms denial of both petitioners' warranty-based settlement recovery, separately affirms denial of their associated tort-defense expenses, and affirms rejection of Thompson's contextual implied-indemnity claim. The underlying summary judgments remain in place. No payment, new equitable reimbursement action, damages determination or further merits remand is ordered. Other disposed claims are not reopened.

## Holdings

**Authority for all holdings:** Justice Souter's Opinion of the Court, Parts I–IV, joined throughout by Chief Justice Stone-Zsela and Justices Scalia, Kennedy, Thomas and Ginsburg. These six direct joins establish the following rules and their stated limits; no combination of separate opinions is required.

**An actual authorized contractual undertaking is necessary.** Under 28 U.S.C. §1491(a)(1), the asserted contract claim must rest on an express or implied-in-fact undertaking that the Government was authorized to make. The Tucker Act supplies jurisdiction, not the substantive reimbursement promise; fairness alone cannot supply an obligation implied only in law.

**Controlling explanation:** Sutton v. United States applies limits on governmental contracting authority to implied as well as express agreements. Hatzlachh Supply Co. v. United States preserves the separate point that the absence of a tort remedy does not itself defeat an actual contract claim. Both rules apply: the Government cannot escape a genuine contractual obligation merely by invoking tort immunity, and a claimant cannot create the necessary promise by calling equitable reimbursement a contract. An ordinary authorized warranty does not become invalid merely because its possible breach damages were uncertain when the parties contracted. The decision rests on the asserted undertaking's scope, not a finding that a particular appropriation, delegation or required approval was absent from these contracts.

**The asserted specifications warranty does not itself cover these third-party costs.** The implication arising from the Government's furnishing specifications assures satisfactory performance of the procurement undertaking; it does not, without more, promise reimbursement for these manufacturers' settlements of veterans' tort claims or their expenses of defending those claims. Both petitioners' warranty-based settlement and defense-expense claims therefore fail on this legal ground.

**Controlling explanation:** United States v. Spearin ties the specifications warranty and the consequences of its breach to the performance the purchaser prescribed. Its protection against defective specifications does not by itself make the purchaser an insurer of the supplier's liabilities to persons outside the contract. Ryan Stevedoring and Weyerhaeuser v. Nacirema recognize that a particular contractual service duty can carry foreseeable third-party consequences without an express indemnity clause. They require identification of that duty; they do not establish this additional promise merely because the Government specified and purchased the product. Hatzlachh preserves contract remedies but supplies no missing term.

The manufacturers correctly distinguish contractual loss from tort liability. That distinction does not establish the coverage they claim. Even taking the material allegations favorably for this legal inquiry, the asserted specifications implication does not undertake these losses. Defense expenses receive a separate disposition: they can exist despite a successful tort defense, but the same absent undertaking defeats their recovery here. The Court does not hold that every contractual damage ends at delivery, that third-party losses always require express indemnity, or that every such expense is categorically barred by Kania. A different demonstrated, authorized undertaking remains possible. The Federal Circuit's government-contractor-defense causation equation is unnecessary to the judgment and is not decided.

**Compulsion and statutory protection do not establish the asserted indemnity.** Thompson's preserved claim that the contracting circumstances imply a reimbursement undertaking fails: compulsory procurement, specifications, alleged control and superior knowledge, and the Defense Production Act's liability protection, considered together, do not establish the asserted authorized agreement to bear these third-party settlement and defense costs. Section 707 itself supplies no affirmative reimbursement promise.

**Controlling explanation:** Section 101 authorizes priorities and compelled performance. Section 707 protects against specified damages or penalties arising from compliance. A command to produce and protection against liability are different from governmental assent to pay a supplier's later litigation costs. Thompson relies on the combined contracting setting, not merely a private statutory cause of action. But the cumulative asserted inference still lacks the claimed undertaking: control and knowledge do not turn the procurement command into an indemnity promise. To impose the obligation solely because the suppliers should fairly be relieved of the loss would exceed the Tucker Act's actual-contract boundary.

The actual-authority principle in Sutton, the appropriations limits and the existence of specific authorized indemnity mechanisms reinforce caution against readily inferring an open-ended commitment. They do not establish that every indemnity is forbidden, that any particular officer lacked a proven delegation, or that special contracting authority was exercised here. Genuine authorized indemnities remain enforceable on their terms. The Court does not decide §707's full immunity boundary, including particular product-injury or accelerated-production situations, and creates no separate statutory reimbursement action.

**Scope and questions not reached:** Both petitioners' preserved specifications-warranty claims are decided. Thompson's separate contextual implied-in-fact indemnity is decided; Hercules's compulsion allegations do not reopen a separate indemnity claim not preserved below. Knowledge, control and compulsion may be considered as subsidiary arguments within the preserved claims, but no freestanding superior-knowledge, purchaser-use, good-faith or takings cause is newly adjudicated. No finding is made concerning defective specifications, relative knowledge, veterans' injuries, an actual warranty breach, the reasonableness or allocation of claimed damages, or contract-specific authorization. No claim for fees incurred prosecuting this contract action is awarded. The absence of an express indemnity does not establish a universal express-clause rule.

## Precedent Treatment

- United States v. Spearin, 248 U.S. 132: applies the specifications warranty to its actual performance undertaking; the warranty alone does not establish the additional third-party-loss coverage asserted here. Its ordinary protection and damages for breach remain intact.
- Sutton v. United States, 256 U.S. 575: applies the requirement of actual governmental authority equally to express and implied-in-fact undertakings; no new finding about an individual contract's appropriation or delegation follows.
- Hatzlachh Supply Co. v. United States, 444 U.S. 460: applies the independence of genuine contract remedies from tort immunity and the distinction between actual contracts and obligations implied in law.
- Ryan Stevedoring Co. v. Pan-Atlantic Steamship Corp., 350 U.S. 124, and Weyerhaeuser Steamship Co. v. Nacirema Operating Co., 355 U.S. 563: distinguish their particular service undertakings while preserving that foreseeable third-party consequences may follow a proved contract duty without an express indemnity clause.
- Boyle v. United Technologies Corp., 487 U.S. 500: leaves the government-contractor tort defense unchanged; the Court does not decide whether the Federal Circuit's causation equation independently supports the settlement disposition or use tort immunity as a substitute for identifying a contract.
- United States v. X-Citement Video, decision of November 29, 1994, and Lebron v. National Railroad Passenger Corp., decision of February 21, 1995: preserve their distinct review-scope rules. The Government's alternative may defend its existing favorable judgment, but no new factual findings or independent unpresented causes are added.

Kania v. United States, 650 F.2d 264, is a Court of Claims decision involving a nonprosecution agreement, authority and remote litigation expenses. Its language is not adopted as a universal bar to third-party expenses resulting from breach of an authorized procurement contract. No earlier Supreme Court holding is overruled.

## Law After Decision

The ordinary specifications warranty does not itself promise payment of these third-party tort settlements and defense expenses. Thompson's asserted combined procurement circumstances do not establish the additional implied indemnity, and §707 supplies no independent reimbursement promise. These are contractual-scope holdings effective March 4, 1996, not immunity from genuine contracts or a prohibition on all third-party consequential damages. Actual authorized agreements, Spearin's ordinary protection and applicable contract remedies remain governed by their own terms. The Court adds no causation rule equating a warranty claim with a successful tort defense, and adopts no new rule concerning the veterans' tort claims or the full reach of §707 immunity.

## Separate Writings

**Breyer, dissenting, joined throughout by O'Connor.** They would vacate the judgment in part and remand both petitioners' preserved specifications-warranty claims, including settlement and tort-defense expenses, and Thompson's contextual implied-in-fact indemnity claim. Other disposed claims would remain undisturbed.

In Part I, they read Spearin, Ryan, Weyerhaeuser and Hatzlachh as permitting inquiry into whether an actual authorized contractual undertaking included foreseeable third-party consequences. They reject the Federal Circuit's categorical equation between a successful warranty claim and a certainly successful government-contractor defense. The 1984 settlement cannot be assessed simply as though later defense rulings had already assured victory; defending even successfully can leave expenses. Kania's distinct nonprosecution and remote-expense setting does not settle procurement consequential damages. The actual duty, breach, causal connection, foreseeability, ex ante litigation risk, reasonableness and allocation must be examined to the extent necessary and procedurally available. Neither settlement approval nor government specifications prove those elements.

In Part II, they agree that §707 alone promises no reimbursement but would allow Thompson to attempt to prove an authorized implied-in-fact undertaking from the circumstances considered together. Compulsion, specifications, alleged control and knowledge, and the surrounding liability protection may inform the bargain actually made. Sutton still requires authority. An obligation imposed solely for fairness would fail, and neither the existence of special contracting authority nor a judicial label proves approval. They infer no indemnity cap or insurer-equivalent amount.

Part III limits the remedy to the preserved contract claims requiring further adjudication. The Government retains its available legal and factual defenses. Missing documents alone guarantee neither discovery nor trial; no promise, breach, payment entitlement or damages amount is found. The dissent does not revive Hercules's separate indemnity claim, adjudicate an independent takings or superior-knowledge cause, or award fees for this contract litigation. Its proposed remand and causation analysis are noncontrolling.

## Procedure After Action

The Federal Circuit's affirmance of the summary judgments stands. The contract suits receive no further merits remand, damages proceeding or new filing direction from this Court. No special mandate date or further Supreme Court event is prescribed. The decision does not adjudicate the veterans' tort claims or reopen independent theories outside the preserved review.
