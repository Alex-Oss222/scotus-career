## Public Projection

## Event

Action for Children's Television v. FCC, with Pacifica Foundation v. FCC; D.C. Circuit Nos. 93-1092 and 93-1100. Merits decision, March 4, 1996. On review under 28 U.S.C. §1254(1) of the June 30, 1995 en banc D.C. Circuit judgment, 58 F.3d 654. No separate Supreme Court docket or argument date is supplied.

The questions concern the safe-harbor structure, unequal treatment of broadcasters and administrative support for restrictions on protected indecent broadcasting. Section 16(a) of the Public Telecommunications Act of 1992 required prohibition from 6 a.m. to 10 p.m. on any day for public radio or television stations signing off at or before midnight, and from 6 a.m. to midnight on any day for every other radio or television broadcasting station. It required APA §553 procedures and final regulations within 180 days of enactment. Section 16(b) repealed the earlier all-day provision; §16 took effect August 26, 1992.

The court of appeals rejected the distinction and ordered a uniform 10 p.m.–6 a.m. harbor, leaving a 6 a.m.–10 p.m. restriction. The Government did not petition or cross-petition to restore midnight. Both petitioners challenge the surviving sixteen-hour rule. ACT also presented an independent vagueness argument; this decision addresses the schedule and its contextual burden without independently invalidating the generic definition or deciding any particular broadcast sanction.

## Participation

All nine Justices participate: Chief Justice Stone-Zsela and Justices Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer.

## Public Action

The Court sets aside approval of the mandatory 6 a.m.–10 p.m. restriction and remands with directions to remove the corresponding rule. Five Justices support that final constitutional determination and mandatory relief: Stone-Zsela, O'Connor, Kennedy, Thomas and Ginsburg. Stone-Zsela describes the action on the appellate approval as vacatur; the other four describe it as reversal. Their operative direction is the same. Souter separately would vacate for renewed review while leaving validity open; Stevens, Scalia and Breyer would affirm. The uniform 10 p.m.–6 a.m. protection remains undisturbed.

## Holdings

**Protected broadcasting and the applicable review.** Nonobscene indecent expression is protected. A restriction selecting that content must be carefully tailored to a compelling interest, with independent review of its necessity and means in light of broadcasting's actual characteristics. Protecting children and assisting parental supervision are compelling interests; their importance does not establish that this schedule is necessary. Ginsburg's Part I, joined by Stone-Zsela, O'Connor, Kennedy, Souter and Thomas, supplies six votes.

**Controlling explanation:** Pacifica permits the particular contextual regulation it sustained; it does not validate every general daily restriction. Sable requires protection of adult access and careful tailoring while preserving material differences between broadcasting and telephone services. Legislative predictive judgments receive substantial consideration, but asserted harm and the selected response remain subject to independent review. Neither scientific certainty nor a particular legislative report is indispensable. Turner preserves that judicial responsibility; its intermediate test for content-neutral cable carriage is not the test for this content-selected rule.

**The mandatory sixteen-hour schedule fails on this record.** The prescribed 6 a.m.–10 p.m. exclusion is insufficiently tailored to justify its broad burden on protected adult broadcasting. Ginsburg's Part II, joined by Stone-Zsela, O'Connor, Kennedy and Thomas, supplies five direct votes for final invalidity.

**Controlling explanation:** The record establishes substantial child access, including late audiences, and difficulties of supervision and rapid tuning. It does not adequately connect those circumstances to excluding this broad category of protected expression across sixteen hours, media and contexts. A child somewhere in the audience cannot by itself settle the necessary degree of adult exclusion. Late-night audiences, taping and purchased alternatives do not adequately answer practical access for adults relying on free broadcasting or the burden on parental choice. The FCC considered alternatives; the defect is their insufficient treatment and the sweep of the resulting rule, not the absence of any evidence or analysis.

The agency's contextual definition, attention to reasonable licensee judgment and clinical or bona fide news applications limit the burden but create no blanket news or serious-merit exemption. Its tentative defense permits market-wide evidence of no appreciable child audience at the relevant time; ordinary station/program ratings remain inadequate until convincing evidence establishes relevance despite grazing. Those qualifications do not cure the schedule's general fit, establish an automatic low-rating exemption or decide any individual defense.

Audience measures remain distinct. Receiver ownership shows opportunity; aggregate viewing does not establish actual indecent-program exposure. Radio quarter-hour audiences are not cumulative reach, and limited under-twelve radio data cannot be supplied by inference. Adult presence is not effective supervision; public radio's small teen listening share and weekly reach are not zero child audience. The holding requires neither proof of harm from every program nor flawless alternatives, and does not declare all broadcasting interchangeable with other media.

**The relief removes this rule while preserving lawful authority.** The appellate approval must be set aside and the D.C. Circuit must remove the corresponding rule and prevent §16(a)'s dependent command from requiring the invalid schedule. Ginsburg's Part III, joined by Stone-Zsela, O'Connor, Kennedy and Thomas, supplies five votes for that operative direction. The existing 10 p.m.–6 a.m. protection remains intact with all nine Justices' agreement.

**Controlling explanation:** Extending the 10 p.m. opening removed the unjustified classification but did not answer petitioners' independent breadth challenge. The Government's absence of a petition forecloses restoring the harsher decree through an alternative defense. The constitutional defect calls for removing the challenged mandate, not judicial selection of a new national opening hour. Section 16(b)'s repeal remains effective. Section 1464, Pacifica and ordinary obscenity enforcement retain their lawful scope; more confined FCC action must satisfy those authorities and today's ruling. No unexamined severability provision or statutory exception creates a substitute schedule. A renewed-record remand leaving validity open does not command five votes, and no Marks aggregation makes Souter's separate position the governing remedy.

The Court does not independently resolve facial vagueness, grant an automatic serious-merit exemption, adjudicate any enforcement sanction, overrule Pacifica or Red Lion, require station-by-station permission hearings, or prescribe a new multifactor test. Section 551 creates no present device-based factual ground for the judgment and does not automatically repeal §16, moot the case or require remand.

## Precedent Treatment

- FCC v. Pacifica Foundation, 438 U.S. 726: preserves its narrow contextual broadcast holding and distinguishes it from this general mandatory schedule. Its three-Justice discussion of speech value is not made controlling; the Court does not overrule it or Red Lion's separate broadcast holding.
- Sable Communications of California, Inc. v. FCC, 492 U.S. 115: applies protection of adult nonobscene speech, careful tailoring and independent review; preserves the distinction between telephone services and broadcasting.
- Butler v. Michigan, 352 U.S. 380: applies its protection against reducing adults to material suitable for children without deriving a broadcast timetable from it.
- Turner Broadcasting System, Inc. v. FCC, 512 U.S. 622: applies content classification and the limited controlling principle of deferential but independent evidentiary review. Its content-neutral cable test and four-Justice record-insufficiency application do not determine this case.
- United States v. X-Citement Video, Inc., 513 U.S. 64: applies the boundary allowing alternative defenses of an existing favorable judgment without enlarging respondents' rights under it.
- United States v. Chesapeake & Potomac Telephone Co., with National Cable Television Assn. v. Bell Atlantic Corp., February 27, 1996: its repeal-based remand rests on actual statutory repeal and unresolved continuing injury; it supplies no automatic remand from §551's enactment here.

## Law After Decision

Effective March 4, 1996, the mandatory uniform 6 a.m.–10 p.m. indecency schedule may not be enforced, and the D.C. Circuit must implement its removal. The already favorable 10 p.m.–6 a.m. protection continues. The Court adds a controlling, record-based limit on the mandatory schedule; it does not withdraw all authority to protect children through lawful, more confined broadcast regulation. Adult speech remains protected, Pacifica retains its actual contextual force and ordinary obscenity enforcement survives. No new general definition or replacement safe-harbor hour is prescribed.

Section 551's parental-choice provisions remain subject to their enacted conditions. Its findings are legislative findings, not proof of a presently effective alternative. The rating amendment takes effect one year after February 8, 1996 only upon the FCC's determination, after consultation with appropriate public-interest groups and interested private-sector individuals, that acceptable voluntary ratings and voluntary transmission agreements have not been established. The rating paragraph does not authorize political or religious ratings. The advisory committee must comprise parents, television broadcasters, television programming producers, cable operators, appropriate public-interest groups and other interested private-sector individuals, fairly balanced in political affiliation, represented viewpoints and committee functions. The FCC must supply staff and resources necessary for efficient and prompt performance and require a final recommendations report within one year of initial appointments.

The manufacturing requirement applies on an FCC-selected date, after consultation with the television manufacturing industry, no earlier than two years after enactment to covered apparatus with screens at least thirteen inches diagonally manufactured in the United States or shipped in interstate commerce. Carriers merely transporting without trading are excepted. FCC rules must provide Commission oversight of industry's adoption of blocking-technology standards and require covered apparatus to receive line-21 rating signals conforming to industry specifications established under Commission supervision. An FCC determination that alternative technology enables parental blocking by identifying programs without ratings, is comparably priced and blocks broadly across a multichannel system as effectively and easily requires permitting either technology. No such determination, implemented nationwide ratings/blocking system, household penetration, cost/effectiveness finding or radio counterpart is established. These provisions neither repeal §16 nor independently resolve this challenge.

## Separate Writings

**Souter, concurring in part and dissenting in part.** Souter joins the content-based standard, independent review and Pacifica's continued limits. He would vacate approval of the actual schedule for renewed examination of its boundary, contextual reach, practical adult opportunities and alternatives. The lower majority did address 10 p.m. by reasoning from its midnight conclusion and removing unequal treatment; he finds that analysis insufficiently focused, not wholly absent. Turner supports a careful remand without turning present uncertainty into final constitutional invalidity. Actual evidence exists, and further analysis may show adequate support or require relief. He therefore withholds assent to final invalidity and mandatory removal, preserves nighttime protection, and prescribes neither a new agency hearing nor automatic interim relief. Section 551 alone requires no remand, and future equipment is no present factual premise.

**Stevens, dissenting, joined by Scalia and Breyer.** The dissent would affirm the actual uniform decree under demanding review. Pacifica permits contextual broadcast channeling; Sable's rejection of wholesale adult exclusion does not establish an effective telephone-like means of segregating this audience. Substantial child access, rapid tuning, multiple receivers, the meaningful 10 p.m. harbor and evidence-sensitive contextual defenses together justify this schedule. The dissent accepts protected adult speech and independent review, but finds the majority gives insufficient weight to the remaining opportunities and the practical difficulty of protecting children without timed channeling. It requires neither a formal legislative report nor scientific certainty, makes no finding that every program harms children, and adopts no general lower-value hierarchy. No foolproof-alternative condition, operational V-chip or automatic station-rating exemption supplies its conclusion. Its proposed affirmance preserves the favorable nighttime protection, actual defenses and properly supported future challenges.

## Procedure After Action

The cases return to the D.C. Circuit for implementation of the five-Justice mandatory-set-aside direction. The FCC rule corresponding to the invalid 6 a.m.–10 p.m. schedule must be set aside and the dependent statutory command may not be enforced to require that schedule. Uniform nighttime protection remains. Lawful further FCC action must rest on remaining authority and comply with the constitutional ruling; it may not restore the invalid schedule under another label. The Court prescribes no replacement hour, individual sanction disposition, damages, new agency hearing or later Supreme Court date.
