## Jurisdiction, posture and record

Nos.95-124 and 95-227 seek certiorari under 28 U.S.C. §1254(1) from the D.C. Circuit's June6,1995 en banc judgment, 56 F.3d105. The underlying consolidated proceedings, Nos.93-1169,93-1171,93-1270,93-1276, were petitions for review of two FCC orders under 47 U.S.C. §402(a) and 28 U.S.C. §§2342(1),2344. They concern facial statutory/regulatory challenges by organizations of programmers and viewers, and an individual access programmer; they are not damages adjudications concerning a completed removal of one program. The earlier panel's contrary disposition, 10 F.3d812, was vacated, 15 F.3d186. The en banc judgment sustained §10 and the implementing rules. Sources: [full lower opinion](https://static.case.law/f3d/56/html/0105-01.html); [No.95-227 petition pp.i–ii,2,9 and appendix](https://archive.org/details/micro_IA40385013_0640).

The No.95-227 petition separately presents government action; preemption and encouragement; public-forum status; least-restrictive-means scrutiny; and adopts No.95-124's vagueness question concerning contemporary community standards, operators' reasonable belief and programmer self-certification. Broad descriptions of the case must preserve these alternatives. The petition's mention of a lower-panel government concession is a report of advocacy at that stage, not a substitute for the current statutory or constitutional inquiry.

The 1984 Act required designated commercial leased capacity in larger systems and authorized local franchising authorities to require public, educational and governmental (PEG) capacity. Leased access and PEG are distinct arrangements. “Commercial use” includes qualifying video programming regardless of profit status. The original limitations on operators' access-channel editorial control differed from operators' control of their other channels, and contained separate obscenity/unprotected-speech exceptions. No general conclusion that every access channel is a constitutional public forum follows from its statutory name. Specific franchise dedication, ownership, control and actual uses would matter to a channel-specific theory.

## Challenged enactment in terms

The official text is [Pub. L.102-385, §10, 106 Stat.1486](https://www.congress.gov/102/statute/STATUTE-106/STATUTE-106-Pg1460.pdf), checked against the printed page image. All extracted text of the downloaded Act was read; §10's visual page is saved as `CHUNK10_RESEARCH_A_SOURCES/DENVER_1992_SECTION10.png`.

**Section10(a), leased-access permission**, amends Communications Act §612(h) by inserting “or the cable operator” after “franchising authority” and adding:

> This subsection shall permit a cable operator to enforce prospectively a written and published policy of prohibiting programming that the cable operator reasonably believes describes or depicts sexual or excretory activities or organs in a patently offensive manner as measured by contemporary community standards.

Thus the new permission has a prospective, written-and-published-policy condition, a reasonable-belief condition and the stated substantive definition. It does not command every operator to prohibit indecent programming. The amendment acts on preexisting subsection(h); this supplement quotes the complete new operative sentence and the precise insertion, rather than substituting a reconstructed composite Code quotation.

**Section10(b), leased-access segregation and blocking**, adds §612(j):

> (j)(1) Within 120 days following the date of the enactment of this subsection, the Commission shall promulgate regulations designed to limit the access of children to indecent programming, as defined by Commission regulations, and which cable operators have not voluntarily prohibited under subsection (h) by—
>
> (A) requiring cable operators to place on a single channel all indecent programs, as identified by program providers, intended for carriage on channels designated for commercial use under this section;
>
> (B) requiring cable operators to block such single channel unless the subscriber requests access to such channel in writing; and
>
> (C) requiring programmers to inform cable operators if the program would be indecent as defined by Commission regulations.
>
> (2) Cable operators shall comply with the regulations promulgated pursuant to paragraph (1).

The voluntary prohibition clause limits the programming to which the mandatory segregation/blocking scheme applies. It does not make segregation and blocking optional when the operator carries covered programming. Programmer identification and notification are independent requirements.

**Section10(c), PEG permission**, provides:

> Within 180 days following the date of the enactment of this Act, the Federal Communications Commission shall promulgate such regulations as may be necessary to enable a cable operator of a cable system to prohibit the use, on such system, of any channel capacity of any public, educational, or governmental access facility for any programming which contains obscene material, sexually explicit conduct, or material soliciting or promoting unlawful conduct.

These are the enacted categories. Congress used “sexually explicit conduct” here, whereas (a) spells out sexual/excretory content and offensiveness. The FCC's construction linking those categories must be identified as agency interpretation. Section10(c) contains no mandatory segregation/blocking rule and no statutory written-request mechanism. Its governmental direction to issue regulations is distinct from the permission given to operators.

**Section10(d)** separately amends the access-programming civil/criminal liability immunity in §638 by inserting “unless the program involves obscene material.” The exception concerns obscenity, not all nonobscene indecency. The lower court notes the FCC's interpretation limiting loss of immunity to constitutionally unprotected material. Section10(d)'s existence can inform a claimed incentive theory; it cannot be restated as general operator liability for protected indecent material. Section28 makes the 1992 Act effective sixty days after enactment except where expressly otherwise provided; the specific (b)/(c) rulemaking deadlines remain distinct.

## FCC implementation and material qualifications

The first order, 8 FCC Rcd998 (1993), 58 Fed.Reg.7990, implemented (a)/(b), formerly 47 C.F.R. §76.701. The definition adds the medium-specific phrase: material describing or depicting sexual or excretory activities or organs in a patently offensive manner measured by contemporary community standards **for the cable medium**. This is not Miller obscenity: it does not incorporate Miller's whole-work prurient-interest and lack-of-serious-value elements. A work's literary, artistic, political or scientific value does not categorically exempt every depiction under the indecency definition. Context remains relevant; neither the lower dissent's examples nor this supplement finds every named program indecent.

The rules require provider identification, segregation and blocking of covered material not prohibited, subscriber notice, and written requests stating the requester is at least eighteen. The operator must satisfy a request **within thirty days**; this is an outside compliance period, not a mandatory thirty-day waiting period. The FCC permitted another blocked channel if the first lacked capacity, and an alternative involving lockboxes with operator-retained keys for qualifying small systems. Those implementation exceptions should not be converted into the statute's general rule. The complete text and exact eligibility of any exception should be obtained from the order before that exception is made outcome-determinative. Lower opinion pp.111–112,126–129, notes2,14,26; first order ¶¶66–69 and n.46.

The second order, 8 FCC Rcd2638 (1993), 58 Fed.Reg.19623, formerly §76.702, implements (c). It interprets “sexually explicit conduct” using the same indecency definition, permits operators to demand certification of absence of prohibited material, and construes material soliciting/promoting unlawful conduct as material otherwise proscribed by law. Although operators may adopt blocking for PEG, (b)'s obligation does not apply there. Lower opinion pp.112,128 and notes3,24. Certification concerns cannot be analyzed as if a government licensing board itself preapproves each transmission.

Before §10, §544(d)(2) already required operators on subscriber request to supply, by sale or lease, a device enabling the subscriber to prohibit viewing during selected periods. The existence of that obligation is distinct from findings concerning actual uptake, cost, effectiveness and household practices. The premium-channel free-preview rule, §544(d)(3), separately requires advance notice and blocking on request; it is not the general leased-access default. Lower opinion notes21–22 and Wald dissent n.16.

## Lower judgment and competing paths, provision by provision

**(a), operator ban discretion.** The en banc majority treats Congress as restoring private editorial choice: permission, regulation, a franchise monopoly and a legislative preference do not themselves make each operator decision governmental under Blum, Jackson, Flagg Brothers and Lugar. It distinguishes Reitman's constitutional entrenchment of racial discrimination from ordinary statutory revision. It rejects an established coercive-cost premise because allocation of blocking costs remained for another FCC proceeding and the industry comments did not demonstrate inevitable censoring incentives. This is a record conclusion, not a holding that coercive incentives can never supply state action.

Wald treats (a) and (b) as an integrated governmental ban-or-block choice; both possible branches impose a speech burden, and retaining a nominal private choice does not end scrutiny. Alternatively she reads technical, personnel, administrative and financial burdens as encouragement of bans. Edwards shares the integrated-scheme objection, but would not find (a) standing alone necessarily governmental. Rogers would sever the invalid blocking scheme while preserving the operator permission. These are distinct alternatives: state action in the statute, attribution of the operator's choice, practical inducement, and independent private editorial rights. Avoid assuming one resolves all others.

**(b), compulsory segregation/blocking.** The majority applies Sable's least-restrictive-means formulation, relies on protection of children and the attributes of home television, and regards the mixed and unpredictable character of leased programming as impairing subscriber-controlled lockboxes. It reasons that a written opt-in leaves adult access available, that some children watch late, and that different treatment of premium, ordinary and PEG channels is justified by different editorial and access arrangements. Its privacy discussion cites §551 protections and distinguishes Lamont's request-to-government deterrence; it does not find an actual database disclosure here.

Wald, joined on these parts by Rogers, stresses the combined effect on adult speakers and viewers: initial denial, written request, up-to-thirty-day activation, concentrated adult content, inability to choose one program without opening the whole channel, and potential suppression of mixed serious programming. She challenges the agency's evidence for rejecting lockboxes, separation by time/channel plus subscriber control, notice and cost-spreading. She distinguishes telephone systems' technological limitations and alternative credit-card/access-code options. Those criticisms are not adjudicated universal facts about every system. Edwards agrees the combined scheme fails, while discussing a potentially different parent-facilitation rationale and structure; do not convert that conditional possibility into a holding sustaining the enacted system.

**(c), PEG operator power.** The majority's private-discretion rationale survives independently because no (b) compulsion attaches to PEG. Wald sees content-selective governmental withdrawal of access protection and a statutory source for discriminatory choices. She does not decide public-forum status; Rogers's rejection of the forum claim is expressly tied to what this facial record establishes. Edwards and Rogers do not join Wald's invalidation of (c). Distinguish any particular city's management of a PEG facility from a universal status for all PEG systems; distinguish operators' interests on their own programming from power over separately administered access programming.

**Vagueness, restraint and severability.** The lower majority sustains the generic standard based on existing indecency decisions and agency resolution; rejects government prior-licensing treatment of notification/certification and treats operator bans under its private-action conclusion. The opposing claim is that reasonable-belief bans, uncertain community standards and certification consequences deter protected speech beyond settled obscenity. These issues remain analytically distinct from whether the particular restriction is necessary. If a component fails, Alaska Airlines supplies the severability inquiry: whether the surviving provision remains operable consistently with congressional intent. The lower writings disagree specifically whether (a) survives invalid (b); (c) has no necessary blocking link. No invalidation of the three provisions as a single undifferentiated unit is compelled merely by finding one defective.

## Entering simulated law: ACT and Turner must actually change the inquiry

**ACT / Pacifica, March4,1996**, as supplied in the complete sanitized public projection, protects nonobscene indecency and requires independent necessity and means review of content restrictions. Child protection and parental supervision are compelling interests but do not alone establish the constitutionality of the chosen restriction. The Court rejected the actual mandatory broadcast daytime schedule on its record, preserving the 10p.m.–6a.m. safe harbor without prescribing a new daytime cutoff. It did not invalidate the generic definition, establish an across-the-board news/merit exemption, overrule Pacifica/Red Lion, or decide cable regulation. Thus the present inquiry must genuinely assess the evidence and adult-access alternatives; it cannot sustain (b) by treating broadcast pervasiveness and child protection as a sufficient formula. Nor does ACT itself dispose of state action, operator editorial autonomy or a cable-specific evidentiary balance. The dissenting and narrower earlier positions remain Justice-specific evidence only within their actual writings.

**Turner, June27,1994**, in the supplied simulated law, protects cable editorial judgment without a broadcast-scarcity rationale. Content classification looks to selection and purpose. Its full-power must-carry holding applies intermediate scrutiny to those content-neutral obligations, does not require the least restrictive alternative, and does not establish that an expressly indecency-defined rule is content neutral. The substantial-but-reviewable predictive-judgment proposition has controlling support at its stated level; the particular evidentiary-insufficiency conclusion and a comprehensive methodological framework do not automatically share that support. Preserve Stevens's narrower joins and the contrary content-classification positions of O'Connor, Scalia, Thomas and Ginsburg. An individual Justice may draw from the actual prior writing; neither historic Turner disposition nor a synthesized five-vote methodology replaces the supplied rule.

**Other date-eligible anchors.** Stevens's Pacifica writing supports attention to context, medium, children and the limited character of its sanction; Powell's concurrence, joined by Blackmun, rejects assigning lower value to protected speech merely for indecency. Sable supplies compelling-interest/means scrutiny and the distinction between adult access and shielding children. Perry and Cornelius supply forum classification rules; state creation/regulation of a channel is relevant but not self-executing classification. Blum/Jackson/Lugar and Reitman/Crawford frame the competing government-action inquiries. Existing simulated Hurley protects private expressive editorial decisions in its own setting; Rosenberger, Pinette and Chabad preserve their respective government-program, forum and attribution qualifications. None of those decisions classifies every leased or PEG channel. The model must use each Associate's actual date-eligible writings and joins, rather than treating these anchors as a predicted alignment.

## February1996 Act: independent text and remedy limits

The [official Telecommunications Act, Pub.L.104-104, §§504–506,561](https://www.congress.gov/104/plaws/publ104/PLAW-104publ104.htm), is saved as `DENVER_1996.html`. Sections504/505 matter to available technological/parental alternatives; §506 changes editorial authority; §561 governs the stated route for facial title-V challenges. These sections are separate.

**Section504** adds Communications Act §640, 47 U.S.C. §560: upon a subscriber's request, the operator must, without charge, fully scramble or fully block the audio and video of each channel carrying covered programming so that a nonsubscriber does not receive it. It defines scrambling in terms of inability to view/hear understandably. It is request-triggered, not automatic blocking in every household. Do not assume every implementation fact or its practical sufficiency from enactment alone.

**Section505** adds §641, 47 U.S.C. §561: on channels primarily dedicated to sexually oriented programming, operators must fully scramble/block sexually explicit adult programming or other indecent programming so nonsubscribers do not receive it; until that is done the statute imposes time restrictions during FCC-determined hours when significant numbers of children are likely to view. Its effective date is thirty days after enactment. This provision's channel trigger differs from §10(b). Its validity, implementation and any separate litigation stay have not been adjudicated or established in this packet; no such assumption supplies a missing evidentiary finding here.

**Section506(a)** amends §531(e) to add the exception:

> except a cable operator may refuse to transmit any public access program or portion of a public access program which contains obscenity, indecency, or nudity

**Section506(b)** similarly amends §532(c)(2) to provide:

> a cable operator may refuse to transmit any leased access program or portion of a leased access program which contains obscenity, indecency, or nudity

These words are distinct from §10(a)'s written-policy/reasonable-belief definition and §10(c)'s enacted categories. The enacted §506(a) says public access; do not silently substitute every PEG category for those words. A disposition invalidating §10(c) does not erase this independent amendment. Conversely, no petition seeking review of the 1995 FCC-review judgment automatically supplies a new constitutional challenge or grant concerning §506.

**Section561 of the 1996 Act** (not the different provision codified as 47 U.S.C. §561) provides:

> (a) THREE-JUDGE DISTRICT COURT HEARING.—Notwithstanding any other provision of law, any civil action challenging the constitutionality, on its face, of this title or any amendment made by this title, or any provision thereof, shall be heard by a district court of 3 judges convened pursuant to the provisions of section 2284 of title 28, United States Code.
>
> (b) APPELLATE REVIEW.—Notwithstanding any other provision of law, an interlocutory or final judgment, decree, or order of the court of 3 judges in an action under subsection (a) holding this title or an amendment made by this title, or any provision thereof, unconstitutional shall be reviewable as a matter of right by direct appeal to the Supreme Court. Any such appeal shall be filed not more than 20 days after entry of such judgment, decree, or order.

The direct-appeal trigger is a holding of unconstitutionality, not every conceivable district-court disposition. The current case retains review of the challenged 1992/FCC provisions; the lawful reach of relief under intervening law must be addressed rather than assuming automatic mootness or total repeal.

The government's **February1996 supplemental brief**, read completely, argues §§504/505 do not alter §10, though §505 may answer an underinclusion argument, and §506 clarifies or adds authority whose additional reach would be challenged under the three-judge route. Those are litigant positions, not adopted rulings. [IA file12, supplemental brief](https://archive.org/details/micro_IA40385013_0640). No conclusion concerning standing, mootness, waiver or preservation is inferred from the absence of another document in the excerpts read.
