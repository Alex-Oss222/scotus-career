## Exact operative text

The [official 1994 Code, 2 U.S.C. §441a](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/USCODE-1994-title2/html/USCODE-1994-title2-chap14-subchapI-sec441a.htm), provides:

> (2) No multicandidate political committee shall make contributions—
>
> (A) to any candidate and his authorized political committees with respect to any election for Federal office which, in the aggregate, exceed $5,000;
>
> (B) to the political committees established and maintained by a national political party, which are not the authorized political committees of any candidate, in any calendar year, which, in the aggregate, exceed $15,000; or
>
> (C) to any other political committee in any calendar year which, in the aggregate, exceed $5,000.

The immediately relevant qualifications in (a)(4) are:

> The limitations on contributions contained in paragraphs (1) and (2) do not apply to transfers between and among political committees which are national, State, district, or local committees (including any subordinate committee thereof) of the same political party. For purposes of paragraph (2), the term “multicandidate political committee” means a political committee which has been registered under section 433 of this title for a period of not less than 6 months, which has received contributions from more than 50 persons, and, except for any State political party organization, has made contributions to 5 or more candidates for Federal office.

Section (a)(6) applies the candidate limits separately to each election, except that presidential elections other than the general election within one calendar year count as one. Section (a)(5) aggregates commonly established, financed, maintained or controlled committees, but preserves its separately stated joint-fundraising transfers, separate national/state party committee treatment, and conditional transfer between a candidate's principal committees for different offices. Section (a)(7)(B)(i) expressly treats an expenditure made in cooperation, consultation or concert with, or at the request or suggestion of, the candidate, authorized committees or agents as a contribution. Section (a)(8)'s earmarking/conduit rule is distinct. These are contribution-channel rules; the competing effect of (d)(1)'s notwithstanding clause is the dispute below.

## Competing submissions

**Respondent merits brief pp.14–18 and nn.13–15:** The government reads (a)(2)(A), (a)(7)(B)(i), and the FEC's attribution/coordination regulations together. It argues that (d) authorizes an extra party spending allowance that otherwise would be restricted as contributions; an expenditure outside (d) is therefore not automatically unrestricted. Since the state party had assigned its full (d) authority, it says the advertisement exceeded the ordinary contribution limit. It identifies both the statutory expenditure definition and coordinated treatment as necessary inquiries and asserts petitioners did not contest either. The latter is the respondent's characterization, not an independent factual-coordination finding. Footnote 13 explains that the FEC takes no position on whether the ad contains express advocacy because it regards that test as inapplicable to coordinated party expenditures. Footnote 15 equates the agency's attribution standard with its “in connection with” standard and argues that a narrower construction of the latter would leave some attributed spending governed by the smaller contribution cap. [IA record, respondent brief](https://archive.org/details/micro_IA40385013_0654).

**Party reply pp.7–13 and n.8:** The Party argues that the (a)(2) theory is newly raised and inconsistent with the complaint, conciliation, counterclaim, lower decisions, statute and agency practice. It distinguishes direct contributions, expenditures in connection with a general election under (d), and other speech outside those categories. It reads (d)(1)'s notwithstanding clause to remove party expenditures from (a)(7)'s coordination inquiry; cites the regulation making (d) expenditures additional to ordinary contributions and advisory opinions differentiating them. Its counterclaim challenges party speech limits and alleges expenditures it would have made absent their chilling effect. The reply expressly limits its requested invalidation to Senate-election limits in (d)(3), rather than all of (d). Its acknowledgment that parties necessarily coordinate with candidates accompanies its legal theory that party speech remains expenditure speech; it does not describe who prepared this advertisement. [IA record, reply brief](https://archive.org/details/micro_IA40385013_0654).
