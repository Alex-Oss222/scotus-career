STATEMENT OF THE CASE 


This case involves three separate actions that were 
consolidated for appeal by the Court of Federal Claims.' 
This brief is filed on behalf of Glendale Federal Bank, 
FSB, respondent in one of the consolidated actions. 


1. In 1981 the government entered into a contract 
with Glendale Federal Bank pursuant to which Glendale 
agreed to assume, through a merger, the government’s 
financial liability for a failing Florida thrift, First Federal 
Savings and Loan Association of Broward County 
(“Broward”). At the time, Broward was insolvent, and 
its excess liabilities of $734 million represented a direct 
demand on the government’s deposit insurance fund. 
Glendale’s agreement to assume Broward’s liabilities al- 
lowed the government to avoid seizing Broward, saving 
the government, by its own estimate, more than $783 
million in liquidation costs. To induce Glendale to ac- 
quire Broward, the government promised Glendale that 
Broward’s excess liabilities would be offset—not by cash 
assistance, which the government was unable to provide, 
but with a comparable asset, “goodwill,” which Glendale | 
could amortize over 40 years. 


Glendale has fully performed its own contract obliga- 
tions. It assumed financial responsibility for Broward’s 
$734 million in excess liabilities, and thereby completely 
eliminated the government’s insurance exposure of more 
than $783 million arising from Broward’s previously im- 
minent failure. In 1989, however, the government repu- 
diated its part of the bargain and required Glendale im-

2. In arguing otherwise, the government’s brief com- 
pletely ignores the most significant federal role with re-

Under the National Housing Act of 1934 (“Act”), the 
government chartered and operated the Federal Savings 
and Loan Insurance Corporation (“Insurance Corpora- 
tion” or “FSLIC”), which insured deposit accounts at 
savings and loans (or “thrifts”), upon payment of pre- 
miums and other costs. 12 U.S.C. § 1724 (1988) (re- 
pealed 1989). In the Act, Congress provided depositors 
with an enforceable right to obtain deposit insurance pro- 
ceeds upon the default of an insured institution. 12 
U.S.C. § 1728 (1988) (repealed 1989). 


To secure depositors’ insurance rights, the Act gave 
the Insurance Corporation broad authority to protect its 
deposit insurance fund, including the authority, upon de- 
fault of an insured thrift, to take over and operate the 
institution, to provide it with financial assistance, to liqui- 
date it, to merge it with another insured institution, or 
to take other appropriate actions. 12 U.S.C. § 1729(b)- 
(f) (1988) (repealed 1989). At the time of the Glen- 
dale/Broward merger in 1981, the Act broadly authorized 
the Insurance Corporation “[t]o make contracts,” 12 
U.S.C. § 1725(c)(3) (1976) (repealed 1989), and also 
specifically authorized it, “in order to facilitate a merger” 
of a failing thrift with another insured institution, to 
“guarantee such other insured institution against loss by 
reason of its merging . . . with” the failing thrift. 12

3. Beginning in the late 1970's, rampant inflation and 
related high interest rates caused an unprecedented num- 
ber of thrift institutions to fail. In an effort to avoid 
liquidations accompanied by costly insurance payouts, the 
Insurance Corporation and its operating head, the Federal 
Home Loan Bank Board (the “Bank Board” or 
“FHLBB”), see 12 U.S.C. § 1725(a), began to encour- 
age healthy thrifts to merge with failing ones.* Because 
the government had no authority to force healthy thrifts 
to engage in such mergers, and because the failing thrifts 
were by definition insolvent, the Insurance Corporation 
had to provide sufficient consideration to induce healthy 
thrifts to merge with failing thrifts. The forms of con- 
sideration available to the Insurance Corporation included 
direct cash assistance, which it provided to facilitate 
many early mergers. See C.A. App. 1744; see also 
Pet. App. 5a. However, in light of the large number of 
failing thrifts, by 1981 the Insurance Corporation could 
no more afford cash-assisted mergers than it could afford 
the cost of paying off depositors in liquidations. C.A. 
App. 1751. Therefore, the Insurance Corporation used 


its statutory contracting powers to provide non-cash in- 
ducements. 


Chief among the available cash substitutes was the 
government’s mandated use of goodwill and the “purchase 
method” of accounting, which is a generally accepted ac- 
counting principle (“GAAP”) used to account for busi- 
ness combinations in all industries.* Under the purchase 
method of accounting, the surviving entity in a merger 
records | as “goodwill” any excess of the purchase price 
(including, as here, the liabilities assumed) over the fair

Through use of purchase method accounting, the Insur- 
ance Corporation was able to facilitate workable mergers 
of failing thrifts with healthy ones even while it was cash 
poor. A thrift’s net worth (or capital, see infra note 16) 
determined the amount of income-producing loans and 
other assets the thrift could hold. See 12 C.F.R. § 563.13 
(b) (1981). Therefore, recognition of goodwill as part 
of net worth permitted the acquiring thrift to hold, over 
the amortization period, a greater amount of income- 
producing assets and thereby to obtain greater earnings. 
See H.R. Rep. No. 54(1I), 101st Cong., Ist Sess. 533 
(1989). These increased future earnings, which the ac- 
quiring thrifts were able to obtain under the Insurance 
Corporation’s goodwill agreements, both (a) compen- 
sated the acquiring thrifts for assuming the government’s 
deposit insurance liabilities, and (b) reduced, over time, 
the Insurance Corporation’s insurance risk on the deposits 
of the surviving thrifts by replacing the gradually-amor- 
tizing goodwill with newly-generated retained earnings. 
See id. at 498. This process was particularly effective 
where, as here, the government’s contracting partner was 
a healthy, well-run institution with substantial pre-existing 
net worth. The process also allowed the Insurance Cor- 
poration to conserve its dwindling cash for use when 
liquidations were unavoidable.°

a. Unmistakability Addresses Intent, Not Formality. 
The unmistakability principle simply requires a showing 
of the government’s clear contractual intent. See, e.g., 
Binghamton Bridge, 70 U.S. (3 Wall.) at 74 (“All 
contracts are to be construed to accomplish the inten- 
tion of the parties.”). There are no specific words 
or forms that must be used. Rather, unmistakability 
“does not deny to public [contracts] a fair and reason-

able interpretation” and is satisfied whenever the govern- 
ment’s contractual intent “satisfactorily appears” from the 
terms of the parties’ agreement and the surrounding cir- 
cumstances. Russell v. Sebastian, 233 U.S. 195, 205 
(1914). This Court has scorned use of the unmistakabil- 
ity principle to create a “narrow and technical” construc- 
tion of a contract that “would defeat the very end the 
[government] had in view.” Binghamton Bridge, 70 US. 
(3 Wall.) at 78; see Restatement (Second) of Contracts 
§ 202(1) (1981) (“{wJords and other conduct are inter-

principal purpose of the parties is ascertainable it is given 
great weight”).

Mississippi ex rel. Robertson y. Miller, 276 U.S. 174.

In claiming the defense of “sovereign acts,” the govern- 
ment does not, and cannot, deny that FIRREA and the

The government nevertheless argues that the “sovereign

The “sovereign acts” defense cannot apply, however,

precedent.
