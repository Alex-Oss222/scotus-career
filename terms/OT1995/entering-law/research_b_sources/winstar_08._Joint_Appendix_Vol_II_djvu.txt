REGULATORY CAPITAL MAINTENANCE 
AGREEMENT 


THIS AGREEMENT, dated March —_, 1988, is 
entered into by among The Statesman Group, Inc., a 
Delaware corporation (STATESMAN”), American 
Life and Casualty Insurance Company, a Dakota 
corporation wholly owned by STATESMAN 
(“AMERICAN LIFE”), Statesman Savings Holding 
Corp., a savings and loan company incorporated under 
the laws of the State of Iowa (“ACQUIRER”), 
Statesman Bank for Savings, FSB, Des Moines, Iowa, 
federally-chartered stock savings bank (“ACQUIR- 
ING ASSOCIATION”), and the Federal Savings and 
Loan Insurance Corporation (“FSLIC”), in its capac- 
ity as a corporate instrumentality and agency of the 
United States (“CORPORATION”). 


RECITALS 


A. AMERICAN LIFE owns all of the outstanding 
voting parties of the ACQUIRER and AMERICAN 
LIFE is wholly owned by STATESMAN. Therefore, 
STATESMAN and AMERICAN LIFE “control” the 
ACQUIRER, as that term is used in Part 574 of the 
Regulations for the Federal Savings and Loan 
Insurance Corporation (“Insurance Regulations”), 12 
C.F.R. Part 574 (1987). 

B. By Resolution No. 88-_._. dated March —_, 
1988, Federal Home Loan Bank Board (“Bank 
Board”) appointed the receiver (“RECEIVER”) for 
First Federated Savings Bank, Palm Beach, Florida, 
a stock savings bank chartered under laws of the 
State of Florida (“CLOSED ASSOCIATION”). By 
Resolution No. 88-.___, dated March ___., 1988, the 


416 


Bank Board has authorized the ACQUIRING ASSO- 
CIATION to purchase and assume essentially all of 
the assets and liabilities of the CLOSED ASSOCIA- 
TION from the Receiver pursuant to the Acquisition 
Agreement of even date herewith between the 
RECEIVER and the ACQUIRING ASSOCIATION 
(“Acquisition Agreement”). 

C. By Resolution No. 88-___, dated March —_, 
1988, the Bank Board has authorized the supervisory 
conversion of First Federal Savings Bank, Waterloo, 
Iowa, a federally-chartered Federal savings bank 
(“First Federal”), and the merger of First Federal 
with and into the ACQUIRING ASSOCIATION 
(“First Federal Merger”), pursuant to the Merger 
Agreement and Plan of Merger of the date herewith 
between First Federal and the ACQUIRING ASSO- 
CIATION (“First Federal Merger Agreement”). 

D. By Resolution No. 88-___, dated March ——_., 
1988, the Bank Board has authorized the supervisory 
conversion of The Perpetual Savings and Loan 
Association, Waterloo, lowa, a mutual savings and 
loan association chartered under the laws of the State 
of Iowa (“Perpetual”), and the merger of Perpetual 
with and the ACQUIRING ASSOCIATION (“Per- 
petual Merger”), pursuant to the Merger Agreement 
and Plan of Merger of even date herewith between 
Perpetual and the ACQUIRING ASSOCIATION 
(“Perpetual Merger Agreement”). 

E. By Resolution No. 88-___, dated March —_, 
1988, the Bank Board has authorized the supervisory 
conversion of Peoples Savings and Loan Association, 
Waterloo, Iowa, a Federally-chartered mutual savings 
and loan association (“Peoples”), and the merger of 
Peoples with and into the ACQUIRING ASSOCIA- 
TION (“Peoples Merger”), pursuant to the Merger 
Agreement and Plan of Merger of even date herewith

between Peoples and the ACQUIRING ASSOCIA-

“MERGED ASSOCIATIONS”. The First Federal 
Merger, the Perpetual Merger and the Peoples 
Merger may be sometimes referred to herein individ- 
ually as a “Merger” and collectively as the “Mergers”, 
and the First Federal Merger Agreement, the 
Perpetual Merger Agreement and the Peoples Mer- 
ger Agreement sometimes referred to herein individ- 
ually as a “Merger Agreement” and collectively as 
the “Merger Agreements”. 

G. By Resolution No. 88-___, dated March ___, 
1988, and Bank Board has authorized the purchase of 
all of the ACQUIRING ASSOCIATION’s stock by 
the ACQUIRER, and the ACQUIRER has agreed to 
purchase all of the shares of common stock to be 
usually issued by the ACQUIRING ASSOCIATION. 

H. The ACQUIRING ASSOCIATION, pursuant to 
the Acquisition Agreement, will succeed to substan- 
tially all of the rights, litigations, duties and liabilities 
of the CLOSED ASSOCIATION, and will acquire 
substantially all of the assets and property of every 
kind and character belonging to the CLOSED 
ASSOCIATION (Requisition”).

the Merger agreements, will succeed to all of the

MERGED ASSOCIATIONS, and will require all of 
the assets and property of every kind and character 
belonging to the MERGED ASSOCIATIONS.

QUIRER and the ACQUIRING ASSOCIATION. So

thereafter in effect (“Regulatory Capital Regula-

§ 1729(f)(5) (1982), as in effect now or as reenacted or 
amended to the future, and the regulations of the 
FSLIC issued pursuant to 406(f)(5), or any successor 
regulation, as now or hereafter in effect. The form of 
any capital infusion required by this §1 must be 
acceptable to the Principal Supervisory Agent of the 
Bank Board, Des Moines, Iowa, or his designee 
(“P.S.A.”). 

§ 2. Calculation of Required Regulatory Capital. 
For purposes of this Agreement, any determination of 
the ACQUIRING ASSOCIATION’s Required Reg- 
ulatory Capital shall be as calculated in the FSLIC or 
the P.S.A., and shall include in the ACQUIRING 
ASSOCIATION’s Regulatory Capital amounts per- 
mitted by the FSLIC to the Assistance Agreement 
and in the forbearances issued in connection with the 
transactions discussed herein. 

§ 3. Waiver of Net Worth Assistance. The AC- 
QUIRER and the ACQUIRING ASSOCIATION 
hereby waive any right to any net worth assistance 
for which the ACQUIRING ASSOCIATION may 
qualify pursuant to § 406(f)(5) of the NHA, 12 U.S.C. 
§ 1729(f)(5) (1982), and the regulations of the FSLIC 
issued thereunder, or any successor institute or 
regulations, as now or hereafter in effect.

B. Other FSLIC Actions 


During 1987 FSLIC resolved 47 troubled institu- 
tions at an estimated cost of $3.7 billion. Thirty-one of 
these cases were resolved by assisted sales to other 
financial institutions or to corporate and private 
investors, saving FSLIC approximately $400 million 
over the cost of liquidating the 31 institutions. From 


the enactment of the CEBA until the end of 1987, the . 


Board completed sixteen assisted acquisitions and 
four liquidations, for an average of approximately one 
transaction per week. The Bank Board has set a goal 
for FSLIC to continue consummating transactions at 
the same pace this year. This goal is in addition to 
case resolutions under the Southwest Plan. 

Given the size of its caseload and the available 
resources, FSLIC prioritizes cases according to the 


485 


cost-effectiveness of resolution. In most cases, the 
preferred resolution is to sell a troubled institution 
because that is normally the least costly alternative 
for FSLIC. In addition, an assisted acquisition offers 
the potential of preserving the viable aspects of the 
troubled institution (such as its branch structure, its 
case deposit base, and its employees), while providing 
new capital to the industry in a manner that avoids 
the cost to FSLIC of operating a receivership. 

During late 1986 and early 1987, FSLIC’s market- 
ing efforts were severely curtailed because of the 
depleted condition of the insurance fund. However, 
FSLIC embarked on a stepped-up marketing program 
late in the second quarter of 1987 and by year-end 
had marketed 47 institutions with total assets 
exceeding $16.5 billion and liabilities of $18.5 billion. 

Through May 25, 1988, FSLIC was successful in 
resolving 23 troubled institutions (with consolidated 
liabilities of approximately 4.9 billion) for an esti- 
mated cost of 1.32 billion. Thirteen of these cases, 
with total liabilities of $3.5 billion, were resolved by 
assisted sales to other financial institutions or to 
corporate and private investors. These transactions 
saved the FSLIC approximately $252 million over the 
cost of liquidating the institutions. In addition, the 
Southwest Plan has resulted in savings to FSLIC of 
$532 million, for a total savings of $784 million this 
year. 

For the second quarter of 1988, FSLIC projects 19 
case resolutions. Twenty-nine institutions with assets 
totaling $5.7 billion are tentatively scheduled for 
marketing. FSLIC expects to market through both 
mail solicitations and telephone solicitations. The first 
and second quarter marketing efforts coupled with 
the institutions marketed during the second half of 
1987 totaled over 100 institutions with assets exceed- 
ing $48 billion dollars. 


486

by FSLIC apart from those made under the South- 
west Plan involved the acquisition last March of four 
troubled institutions by the Statesman Group, Inc. 
(“Statesman”). This transaction is noteworthy not 
only for the number of institutions involved, but also 
because it represented the Board’s first exercise of 
its reinstated authority under section 408(m) of the 
NHA to override restrictions on cross-marketing of 
products and services between subsidiaries of the 
holding company and the newly acquired thrifts. 

Section 408(m) of the NHA, first enacted by the 
Congress in the Garn-St Germain Depository Institu- 
tions Act of 1982 and reinstated by CEBA, authorized 
the Board, with certain limited exceptions, to arrange 
for acquisitions and mergers of troubled thrift 
institutions notwithstanding the provisions of any 
other state or federal law. The reinstatement of 
section 408(m) in last year’s CEBA legislation was a 
reaffirmation of Congress’ original intent that the 
Bank Board be provided with broad discretion to 
preempt those laws that would otherwise materially 
interfere with its ability to negotiate with potential 
acquirers and to resolve supervisory cases at the 
lowest possible cost to the FSLIC. 

In approving the Statesman acquisition of three 
insolvent thrift institutions in Iowa and a fourth 
troubled savings and loan association in Florida, the 
Board agreed to waive certain cross-marketing 
restrictions generally applicable to thrift holding 
companies and their affiliates that could have pro- 
hibited the acquired institutions from marketing their 
affiliates’ insurance product and service. A key factor 
in the Board’s decision to grant these waivers was 
that Statesman’s acquisition offer involved less finan- 
cial assistance from the FSLIC than any other 


487 


available alternative. In fact, the financial assistance 
provided by the FSLIC in connection with these 
acquisitions was $30 million less than the cost to the 
FSLIC of liquidating the four institutions. In making 
this determination, the Board also noted that: (1) but 
for the acquisitions, Statesman’s insurance affiliates 
would have been able to enter into joint-marketing 
arrangements with the acquired institutions and to 
offer their products and services at the branch offices 
of the thrifts, and (2) the imposition of any cross- 
marketing restrictions, under the circumstances, 
would have been a material impediment to the 
acquisitions. Accordingly, these waivers constituted a 
proper exercise by the Board of its emergency 
acquisition authority under section 408(m). 

The Statesman transaction also represented a 
significant step by the Bank Board in permitting an 
affiliation of a thrift and an investment banking firm. 
The moratorium on such affiliation contained in 
section 408(r) of the NHA expired on March 1, 1988, 
therefore, there is no longer any statutory prohibition 
on acquisitions of insured institutions by companies 
with subsidiaries engaged principally in certain 
securities activities. Even if this section had survived, 
however, the affiliation between the institutions and 
Stateman’s broker/dealer subsidiary would have been 
exempt pursuant to Section 408(r)(2)(C) because the 
institutions would have had total assets of more than 
$300 million at the time of the acquisition. In 
approving this acquisition the Board imposed a series 
of firewall restrictions on the activities of Statesman’s 
subsidiaries in order to insulate the deposits of the 
insured institutions from the activities of the broker- 
dealer affiliates. 

As you are undoubtedly aware, we are attempting 
to resolve the problems of the troubled American 


488 


Savings and Loan Association of Stockton, CA, 
(“American”) the thrift subsidiary of Financial Corpo- 
ration of America. The Bank Board continues to 
monitor the day to day activities of American while 
striving to find a solution that entails the least cost to 
FSLIC. On April 21, 1988, the Bank Board agreed to 
negotiate exclusively with the Robert H. Bass Group, 
Inc. for a period of 75 days concerning the possible 
sale of American. These negotiations are ongoing. 


C. Tax and Other FSLIC-Related Legislative 
Interests 


It is generally more cost effective for FSLIC to 
resolve the problems of troubled thrifts through 
assisted mergers and acquisitions rather than liquida- 
tions. In addition to the reduced costs, assisted 
acquisitions afford additional benefits, such as main- 
taining public confidence in the financial system by 
avoiding failures and liquidations of depository 
institutions. 

Since 1981, the tax laws have contained a number 
of provisions (“the FSLIC tax provisions”) that were 
enacted to help FSLIC fulfill its statutory mandate of 
protecting the safety and soundness of insured 
institutions. These provisions are currently set to 
expire at the end of 1988. We believe these provisions 
should be extended until the end of 1991 and we ask 
you to support such an extension. 

There are two areas in which the expiring tax 
provisions affect the FSLIC’s ability to arrange for 
assisted acquisitions of troubled thrift institutions. 
First, the Internal Revenue Code provides that 
assistance paid by the FSLIC in connection with an 
assisted acquisition will not be considered taxable 
income.”! The benefit of this provision is not limited 


489 


to the troubled institution or the acquirer, it also 
inures to the benefit of the FSLIC by reducing the 
amount of assistance otherwise needed. For example, 
if under current law the FSLIC must pay an acquirer 
$100 as an inducement to acquire a troubled thrift, 
the FSLIC may need to pay potential acquirors $150 
if that assistance payment is to be subject to tax. 

Second, special provisions in the Internal Revenue 
Code’s corporate reorganization rules clarify that a 
FSLIC-supervised merger or acquisition can qualify 
as a tax-free reorganization, such that the net 
operating and built-in tax losses of the troubled 
institution can be fully utilized by the acquiring 
institution. Here again, although the nominal benefit 
of this provision accrues to the acquiring institution, 
in a very real sense, the benefit inures to the FSLIC 
as a result of contractual agreements between it and 
the acquirer requiring that tax benefits enjoyed by 
the acquiring institution must be accounted for and 
rotated back to FSLIC. 

By making each dollar of FSLIC assistance go 
further, the extension of these provisions will not 
only allow FSLIC to resolve a greater number of 
cases, and to resolve them more quickly than it would 
if these previsions were allowed to expire, but will, 
we believe, allow for the most cost effective resolu- 
tion of these cases from the Federal government’s 
viewpoint. For this reason, we would urge the 
members of this Committee to encourage your 
colleagues on the Finance Committee to support an 
extension of these provisions for an additional three 
years.

Dear Senator Sanford: 


During the Committee’s hearing of August 1 on bank 
and thrift fraud, you requested our opinion concern- 
ing actions the government may take to undo or 
restructure agreements that the Federal Savings and 
Loan Insurance Corporation (FSLIC) made in 1988 in 
order to facilitate the acquisition of troubled thrifts. 
You asked about the government’s recourse with 
respect to the agreements should evidence of im- 
proper conduct by government offiicals be uncovered, 
and also about measures the government could take if 
it were to be determined that financial assistance 
commitments made by FSLIC were overly generous. 


As explained in the enclosed analysis, provisions of 
FSLIC’s assistance agreements permit termination of

ANALYSIS 


At the outset, there is nothing in the statutes 
underlying the assistance agreements made by 
FSLIC or the changes recently effected by FIRREA 
that would permit wholesale cancellation of the 
government’s obligations under the agreements. 
FSLIC had very broad authority to provide financial 
assistance to acquirers of troubled thrifts and to set 
the terms of such assistance under provisions of title 
IV of the National Housing Act, then codified at 12 
U.S.C. §§ 1725, 1728, and 1729 (1982). Under section 
402(d) of the Act, FSLIC was empowered to issue 
notes and other obligations “upon such terms and 
conditions as (the Bank Board) may determine.” 
Section 406(f) of the Act allowed FSLIC, in its sole 
discretion, to make contributions to troubled thrifts 
and provide them with other forms of assistance. 
Under section 406(f)(2), FSLIC was authorized, in its 
sole discretion and under the terms and conditions it 
prescribed, to guarantee an institution against losses 
arising from its acquisition of a troubled thrift. Based 


513 


on these statutory provisions, we held in B-233063, 
Oct. 11, 1988, that FSLIC had the requisite authority 
to issue promissory notes and guarantees as part of 
its assisted acquisitions. In that opinion, we also held 
that FSLIC notes and guarantees are obligations of 
the United States, backed by its full faith and credit. 


With the passage of FIRREA, the Federal Deposition 
Insurance Corporation became responsible for admin- 
istering FSLIC’s assistance agreements and satisfy- 
ing FSLIC’s debts and liabilities from the newly 
established FSLIC Resolution Fund. FIRREA sec- 
tion 215. The Act explicitly preserved the rights, 
duties, and obligations of FSLIC and other persons 
arising under “any section of title IV of the National 
Housing Act,” which, as discussed above, authorized 
FSLIC’s assistance agreements. FIRREA section 
401(f). While the Resolution Trust Corporation (RTC) 
was charged with reexamining FSLIC’s agreements 
for purposes of identifying cost saving measures and 
exercising options for reducing costs, the specified 
options do not include cancellation of the 


agreements.® 


The terms of assistance agreements we reviewed 
allow the government to terminate obligations under 
the agreements only in specified circumstances, 
including the acquirer’s insolvency or failure on the

Of course, apart from any rights afforded to the 
government under the terms of FSLIC’s assistance 
agreements, the government would be able to cancel 
any agreement and discontinue its financial commit- 
ments thereunder if evidence of fraud or misdealing 
with respect to the agreement was uncovered. The 
courts have held that any government contract 
tainted by fraud or misdealing may be canceled by 
the government and, depending on the facts of the 
case, the government may be able not only to 
disengage itself from future financial commitments 
but also to recover any payments previously made. 
See J.E.T.S. v. United States, 838 F.2d 1196 (Fed. Cir. 
1988); K & R Engineering Co. v. United States, 616 
F.2d 469 (Ct. Cl. 1980). The courts have also held that 
the government may cancel any contract in which a 
government official had a conflict of interest, even if 
the-party contracting with the government was not 
guilty of wrongdoing. United States v. Mississippi 
Valley Generating Company, 364 U.S. 520 (1961). See 
also K & R Engineering Co., cited above. Addi- 
tionally, other types of illegality in the making or 
performance of a government contract will provide a 
basis for cancellation by the government. See, e.g., 
Alabama Rural Fire Ins. Co. v. United States, 572 
F.2d 727 (Ct. Cl. 1978). 


515 


In the absence of fraud or misdealing, or cireum- 
stances justifying termination under the provisions of 
FSLIC’s agreements, the government could cancel 
the agreements only with exposure to potentially 
enormous financial liability. The Supreme Court has 
held that, when the government enters into a 
contract, it ordinarily incurs responsibilities similar to 
those of a private person who is a partly to the 
contract. Lynch v. United States, 292 U.S. 571 (1934); 
Perry v. United States, 294 U.S. 330 (1935). Thus, if 
the government terminates a contract without justifi- 
cation, such termination is a breach of contract and 
the government becomes liable for all damages 
resulting from the breach. United States v. Behan, 
110 U.S. 338 (1884); United States v. Spearin, 248 
U.S. 132 (1918). 


Furthermore, although Congress could enact legisla- 
tion cancelling the assistance agreements, the enact- 
ment of any such legislation could be subject to 
challenge on constitutional grounds. The courts have 
held that contractual rights against the government 
are Fifth Amendment property interests that cannot 
be taken without just compensation and, therefore, 
that congressional power to abrogate government 
contracts is narrowly circumscribed. Lynch v. United 
States, 292 U.S. 571 (1934); Perry v. United States, 
294 U.S. 330 (1935); Larionoff v. United States, 533 
F.2d 1167 (D.C. Cir. 1976). The courts have also 
stated that although Congress may constitutionally 
impair existing contract rights in the exercise of a 
paramount government power such as the “war 
power,” Congress does not have the power to 
repudiate the government’s contractual obligations 
solely in order to save money. Lynch, cited above, 292 
U.S. at 580; Larionoff, cited above, 533 F.2d at 
1179-80. 


516 


As we indicated above, there are measures the 
government can take short of cancelling FSLIC’s 
assistance agreements in their entirety in order to 
reduce the government’s future expenses. For exam- 
ple, provisions in many agreements allow the govern- 
ment to purchase or require the sale of assets for 
which assistance has been guaranteed, thus eliminat- 
ing the need for future yield maintenance payments 
and capital loss coverage. Also, the terms of FSLIC’s 
negative net worth notes may provide for early 
payment on the notes, saving the government future 
interest expenses. 


Whether the government’s use of options mentioned 
above makes sense financially depends upon the facts 
of each transaction. As discussed previously, FIR- 
REA requires the RTC to review FSLIC’s agree- 
ments in order to identify and exercise cost-saving 
options available under those agreements. We under- 
stand that the RTC is actively conducting the review 
called for by FIRREA, and that it expects to report 
the results of its review in the near future.

NATURE AND SUMMARY OF THIS ACTION 


1. This is an action for breach of contract, 
restitution, and deprivation of property without just 
compensation or due process of law. Plaintiff Glen- 
dale’s claims are based upon (1) its written contract 
dated November 19, 1981, with the Federal Savings 
and Loan Insurance Corporation (“FSLIC”), an 
agency of the United States; (2) the breach, frustra- 
tion and abrogation of that contract by the United 
States, acting through various agencies and officials; 
and (3) other government conduct resulting from or 
related to the Financial Institutions Reform, Recov- 
ery, and Enforcement Act of 1989 (“FIRREA”), 


544 


regulations promulgated under FIRREA, and acts 
and omissions of government agencies and officials 
relating to FIRREA and the FIRREA regulations. 
For relief, Glendale seeks (a) restitution of money it 
paid directly to FSLIC pursuant to and in accordance 
with Glendale’s contract with FSLIC; (b) restitution 
of other benefits conferred upon the United States 
pursuant to the contract, which constitute unjust 
enrichment of the United States at Glendale’s 
expense; (c) damages to compensate Glendale for the 
consequences of the government’s breach, frustration 
and abrogation of the contract, the unconstitutional 
taking of Glendale’s property, and the other unlawful 
government conduct described below; and (d) other 
appropriate contractual, declaratory, and injunctive 
relief. 

2. In brief summary, this case involves deliberate 
conduct by the United States government in reneging 
on a fundamental contractual commitment made to 
Glendale. The government’s conduct is especially 
shocking because the contract at issue facilitated the 
successful acquisition by Glendale, a strong and 
healthy thrift institution, of another thrift institution 
that was weak and failing, and thereby relieved the 
government of the burden of resolving a substantial 
“problem case” in the burgeoning thrift industry 
debacle. 

3. Glendale’s 1981 contract with FSLIC set forth 
the terms and conditions pursuant to which the 
United States would and did approve Glendale’s 
acquisition of the other, failing thrift. The contract 
expressly incorporated the government’s commitment 
that Glendale could count goodwill acquired in the 
transaction as an asset for regulatory capital purposes 
and amortize it over 40 years. Indeed, the United 
States expressly required that Glendale count good- 
will as capital, pursuant to generally accepted 


545 


accounting principles (“GAAP”) and regulatory stand- 
ards, and neither Glendale nor FSLIC would have or 
could have approved the 1981 contract or Glendale’s 
acquisition of the other thrift unless the goodwill 
Glendale acquired was included in regulatory capital. 

4. Pursuant to the 1981 contract, Glendale paid the 
United States approximately $18.4 million, and Glen- 
dale has fully and faithfully performed all its other 
obligations under the contract. 

5. The United States, however, has now reneged on 
its fundamental contractual commitments concerning 
goodwill. As a result of FIRREA, the FIRREA 
regulations, and government conduct following FIR- 
REA, the United States now requires—contrary to 
its contractual commitment to Glendale, and contrary 
to GAAP principles—that most of the goodwill 
Glendale acquired in the 1981 transaction not be 
counted as an asset for regulatory capital purposes. 

6. The effect of the government’s blatant reversal is 
that for regulatory purposes Glendale now must 
deduct from its certified financial statements—which 
are prepared in accordance with GAAP and the 1981 
contract—approximately $675 million in as-yet un- 
amortized goodwill, even through the government 
originally promised that Glendale could include the 
goodwill, amortized over 40 years, as an asset for 
regulatory capital purposes, and even though neither 
FSLIC nor Glendale would have (or could have) 
approved or consummated the 1981 acquisition with- 
out full regulatory recognition of the goodwill Glen- 
dale acquired.

capital, Glendale meets and will continue to meet all 
regulatory capital requirements imposed by FIR- 
REA. Glendale is large enough and strong enough 
that the $675 million deduction now required by the 


546 


government—solely for regulatory capital purposes, 
and not for GAAP or any other financial reporting 


purposes—does not prevent Glendale from complying

The Fundamental Terms of Glendale’s Contract with 
the Government 


27. Because of a variety of financial, regulatory and 
other considerations, as well as the legal requirement 
of FHLBB approval for the merger, Glendale under- 
stood from the outset that it would have to reach a 
mutually acceptable agreement with FHLBB and 
FSLIC concerning a number of issues. Under prevail- 
ing regulatory practices, such agreements were set 
forth in written contractual form. 

28. As Glendale’s negotiations with Broward pro- 
gressed, Glendale focused on two sets of issues, both 
of which involved FHLBB and FSLIC and required 
their concurrence, that had to be addressed and 
resolved to make the deal work. One was the proper 
method of accounting for the merger and certain 
related financial matters, and the other was a variety 
of miscellaneous waivers or agreements concerning 
regulatory requirements that Glendale otherwise 
could not meet after the merger in view of Broward’s 
condition. Glendale commenced discussions with 
FHLBB and FSLIC aimed at obtaining their ap- 
proval and agreement on these issues. 


29. The accounting issues were straightforward. 
Under GAAP, and in accordance with prevailing 
FHLBB requirements at the time.of the merger, as 
set forth in FHLBB Memorandum R-31b, the acquisi- 
tion of Broward by Glendale was to be accounted for 
under the “purchase method” of accounting. 

30. Under “purchase accounting (which, under 
GAAP, is applied to most corporate acquisitions in all 
industries), the book values of the tangible assets and 
liabilities of an acquired entity are adjusted to fair 
value (“marked to market”) on the date of the 
acquisition. Any excess in the cost of the acquisition 
over the market value of tangible assets acquired is 
recorded as “goodwill” on the books of the acquiring 
institution. The cost of the acquisition includes, of 
course, all liabilities assumed by the acquiror. 

31. As explained further below, this “goodwill” is 
an intangible but well-recognized and valuable asset, 
and as an accounting matter is to be amortized over a 
period of up to 40 years. In accounting terms, 
goodwill is classified as an “asset” because it 
represents something what will benefit future 
period—i.e., the customers, deposit base, new mar- 
kets and going-concern value of the acquired 
institution—and the period for amortization repre- 
sents an estimate of the period to be benefitted by 
the valuable goodwill. 

32. Concerning Broward, rising interest rates had 
caused a corresponding and significant decline in the 
market value of Broward’s fixed-rate loans, its major 
assets. At the same time, the market value of 
Broward’s liabilities (principally obligations to deposi- 
tors, whose accounts by that time were variable rate) 
was comparable to the book value of those deposit 
liabilities. Accordingly, while Broward’s net worth 
measured at book value pursuant to GAAP was 


554 


approximately $50 million, the fair value of its 
liabilities exceeded the fair value of its assets at the 
time of the merger by approximately $750 million. 

33. In acquiring Broward, Glendale necessarily 
acquired not only its assets but also its liabilities to 
depositors. It was also clear under GAAP and 
FHLBB requirements, which prescribed purchase 
accounting as noted above, that Broward’s assets and 
liabilities would be “marked to market” on the date of 
the merger, and thus Glendale, at closing, would place 
on its books $750 million more of liabilities than of 
tangible assets acquired from Broward. 

34. Purchase accounting thus posed two fundamen- 
tal issues for Glendale. First, Glendale had to decide 
whether it wanted in effect to pay a $750 million 
premium for Broward’s tangible assets, by assuming 
$750 million more in liabilities than in assets acquired. 
And second, Glendale had to be certain that the 
goodwill it would book and amortize over forty years 
under purchase accounting, in the amount of $750 
million, would be recognized and accepted as an asset 
under GAAP not only for accounting purposes but 
also for regulatory capital purposes. If the goodwill 
would not count towards meeting Glendale’s regula- 
tory capital requirements, as the tangible assets it 
acquired from Broward did at their book value, then 
Glendale faced a potential shortfall of up to $750 
million in regulatory capital on the date of the 
merger. Glendale simply would not and could not 
have exchanged the book value of Broward’s tangible 
assets, for the goodwill that GAAP and FHLBB 
policy provided in return, unless it was clear that 
both counted equally toward Glendale’s regulatory 
capital requirements. As explained further below, 
FHLBB and FSLIC also would not and could not 
have approved the transaction, or entered into the 


555 


Supervisory Action Agreement, without the require- 
ment that the goodwill acquired in the transaction 
would be recognized as part of Glendale’s regulatory 
capital. |

business decision to absorb the $750 million in excess 
liabilities only subject to another important condition. 
The condition was this: although Glendale was 
confident that it could weather the prevailing high 
interest rates and ultimately reverse Broward’s 
operating losses, Glendale was not willing to take the 
additional risk that interest rates would stay high 
indefinitely, or even increase, thus compounding 
Broward’s operating losses. Glendale therefore de- 
cided to negotiate for contractual protection, from 
FSLIC, against the possibility of continued and 
increasingly high interest rates.

SYNOPSIS: ACCOUNTING FOR GOODWILL 
IN ACCORDANCE WITH GAAP IS 
ACCEPTABLE 


(Editor’s Note: Memorandum #R31la, dated March 3, 1974 is rescinded) 


This memorandum sets forth guidelines for the staff of 
the Federal Home Loan Bank Board in reviewing the 
proposed accounting, under generally accepted account- 
ing principles, for goodwill arising in the acquisition of a 
savings and loan association. This intangible asset gen- 
erally results when the purchase price for an institution 
exceeds the fair value of the assets acquired, reduced by 
the fair value of any liabilities assumed. It only arises 
when the accounting for a business combination is in 
accordance with the purchase method. Accounting 
Principles Board Opinion (APBO) No. 16 contains guide- 
lines for the use of the purchase method of accounting in 
a business combination. 


Accounting for business combinations involving insured 
institutions should be in accordance with generally 
accepted accounting principles (GAAP). APBO No. 16 
indicates the conditions for using the purchase method 
of accounting and provides instruction in its application. 
Financial Accounting Standards Board (FASB) 
Interpretation No. 9 provides additional guidance when 


572 


applying the purchase method in a business combination 
involving a savings and loan association. 


FASB Interpretation No. 9 describes the intangible 
assets and related accounting that can occur in a busi- 
ness combination involving a savings and loan associa- 
tion. It states that “the purchase price paid for a savings 
and loan association may include an amount for one or 
more factors, such as the following: 


a) Capacity of existing savings accounts and loan 
accounts to generate future income, 


b) Capacity of existing savings accounts and loan 
accounts to generate additional business or new 
business, and 


c) Nature of territory served. 


If the amount paid for any such factor can be deter- 
mined, that amount shall not be included in goodwill. 
Rather, the amount paid for that separately identified 
intangible shall be recorded as the cost of the intangible 
and amortized over its estimated life.” 


The interpretation goes on to state that any portion of 
the purchase price which cannot be so assigned shall be 
assigned to goodwill and amortized in accordance with 
APBO No. 17, “Accounting for Intangible Assets.” The 
interpretation recognizes that in some situations the 
amount paid for factors such as those described cannot 
be separately determined and, as a result, their value is 
included in the amount designated as goodwill. 


Identifying the fair value of these or other intangible 
factors, whether separately identified or included in 
goodwill, is a subjective task. It can be based on market- 


573 


ing and other demographic studies, analysis of the 
resulting association’s abilities to cross sell services, or 
analysis of past and expected behavior and lives of the 
acquired association’s savings and loan accounts. In 
addition to determining the value of these intangible 
assets, such analyses should determine the asset’s life 
and an appropriate amortization method to provide 
proper matching with the resulting income derived from 
the accounts. Any assumptions used in determining 
these amounts must be realistic in light of the resulting 
association’s management philosophies, practices, and 
abilities. 


In discussing the estimated life of intangible assets, 
APBO No. 17 indicates that “analysis of all factors 
should result in a reasonable estimate of the useful life 
of most intangible assets. As reasonable estimate of the 
useful life may often be based on upper and lower limits, 
even though a fixed existence is not determinable. The 
period of amortization should not, however, exceed forty 
years.” 


An application from an association requesting approval 
for a business combination to be accounted for by the 
purchase method of accounting, from which intangible 
assets will result, should include a description of any 
resulting intangible assets and the plan for their amorti- 
zation. This description should discuss the nature and 
results of management’s analysis of the underlying 
intangible assets and the resulting amortization periods 
and methods. 


This memorandum does not require a change in the 
accounting for goodwill from business combinations 
which have previously been consummated. However, 
upon obtaining approval of the Principal Supervisory 


574 


Agent, an association may change the accounting for 
goodwill to comply with this memorandum.

sition using the “Purchase Method” of accounting in

No. 16. Assets and liabilities will be recorded at their 
respective fair values as of the effective date of acquisi-

value of assets will be recorded as goodwill. 


In accordance with Accounting Principles Board 
Opinion No. 17, a portion of the resulting goodwill has 
been associated with the savings deposit base and will 
be amortized on a straight line basis over 12 years, the

over 40 years as GFS believes that this remaining good- 
will has an indefinite life since it is related to expansion 
of operations into an entirely new market area. 


Discounts and/or premiums resulting from the valuation 
to fair value of interest bearing assets and liabilities will 
be accreted and/or amortized using a level yield interest 
method over the estimated lives of the respective assets 
or liabilities. 


Any payments to or from the Federal Savings and Loan 
Insurance Corporation as a result of the Supervisory 
Action Agreement will be recorded to expense or 
income in the period in which they accrue.

Note Accounting for the Acquisition 


The generally accepted accounting principles followed 
by Glendale Federal Savings and Loan Association 
(GFS) with respect to the acquisition of First Federal 
Savings and Loan Association of Broward County 
(Broward) by GFS are as follows: 


Pursuant to the provisions of the Agreement of 
Merger between GF'S and Broward dated November 
20, 1981 and the Supervisory Action Agreement 
between GFS and the Federal Savings and Loan 
Insurance Corporation (FSLIC) dated November 20, 
1981, upon the effective date of November 20, 1981, 
GFS accounted for the acquisition using the “Pur- 
chase Method” of accounting in accordance with 
Accounting Principles Board Opinion No. 16 (APBO 
16). Purchase accounting was appropriate inasmuch as 
Broward was not autonomous within the meaning of 
APBO 16 before the combination because Broward 
had incurred significant losses and its financial 
condition was such that remedial supervisory action 
was required. FSLIC acted on behalf of Broward and 
provided substantial assistance to GFS in the form of 
interest rate protection and other guarantees. In its 
business and economic analysis GF'S has justified its 
investment in Broward by the future growth poten- 
tial in the market areas within the state of Florida 


624 


and GFS’s ability to reach those markets through use 
of its marketing methods. 


. Assets and liabilities have been recorded at their 
respective fair values as of the effective date of the 
acquisition in accordance with Accounting Principles 
Board Opinion No. 16. The excess of fair value of 
liabilities over the fair value of assets has been 
recorded as goodwill. 


In accordance with Accounting Principles Board 
Opinion No. 17, $18,000,000 of the resultant goodwill 
is associated with the savings deposit base and will be 
amortized on a straight line basis over 12 years, the 
estimated life of the savings deposit base. The 
remaining goodwill of $716,666,000 will be amortized 
on a straight line basis over 40 years as GF believes 
that the remaining goodwill has an indefinite life since 
it is related to expansion of operations into an 
entirely new market area. 


The loan discount of $796,418,000 will be amortized 
using a level yield interest method over the remain- 
ing contractual life of the loan portfolio adjusted for 
estimated prepayments. Such method results in the 
recognition of interest income in future periods to 
approximate a constant rate of return in the loans. 


All other discounts and/or premiums resulting from 
the valuation to fair value of interest bearing assets 
and liabilities will be amortized using a level yield 
interest method over the estimated lives of the 
respective assets or liabilities.

Capital Adequacy: Guidance on the Status of Capital 
and Accounting Forbearances and Capital 
Instruments Held by a Deposit Insurance Fund 


Summary: This Bulletin informs all savings 
associations that have been operating under 
capital or accounting forbearances that Section 
5(t) of the Home Owners’ Loan Act (“HOLA”) 
eliminates such forbearances. Accordingly, these 
forbearances should be eliminated in determining 
whether or not an association complies with the 
new capital regulation. All associations that do 
not comply with the new capital regulation must 
submit a capital plan. This Bulletin also clarifies 
the treatment of capital instruments held by a 
deposit insurance fund. 


For further Information Contact: The District Office 
in which you are located or the Supervision Policy 
Division of OTS, Washington, D.C. 


Thrift Bulletin, 38-2 
Introduction 


Questions have been raised regarding the status of 
previously granted forbearances and waivers as well 
as the treatment of FSLIC-owned warrants and other 
capital instruments. This Bulletin providing guidance 
to savings associations on these matters. 


Capital Compliance 


The Office of Thrift Supervision is applying the new 
capital standards to all savings associations, including 
those associations that have been operating under 
previously granted capital and accounting for- 
bearances. Section 5(t) of HOLA as amended by the 
Financial Institutions Reform, Recovery and Enforce- 
ment Act of 1989 (FIRREA) eliminates these for- 
bearances. All savings associations presently operat- 
ing with these forbearances, therefore, should elimi- 
nate them in determining whether or not they comply 
with the new minimum regulatory capital standards. 
(Any FSLIC capital contribution that resulted in the 
creation of goodwill will be subject to the require- 
ments for goodwill established in the capital 
regulation.) 


If the association determines that it will fail its 
minimum regulatory capital requirements upon the 
elimination of capital and accounting forbearances, it 
must submit a capital plan to its District Director in 
accordance with the regulatory capital regulations 
and Thrift Bulletin 36. 


A capital plan will not be acceptable if it includes the 
continuation of previously granted capital and ac- 
counting forbearances. Capital plans already submit- 
ted that propose to continue previous capital and 


627 


accounting forbearances will be either disapproved, 
returned for revision and resubmittal or conditionally 
approved with the requested forbearance denied. 


Capital Instruments Held by a Deposit Insurance 
Fund 


Capital instruments owned by a party with a financial 
obligation to a savings association will be treated in 
accordance with Generally Accepted Accounting Prin- 
ciples (GAAP). For example, under 12 CFR 567, for 
the purpose of the inclusion in capital of instruments 
owned by the FSLIC or the FDIC, the Office will 
rely on the presentation standards established under 
GAAP. FSLIC or FDIC holdings of capital instru- 
ments (including, but not limited to, warrants pre- 
ferred stock and subordinated debentures) will be 
required to be offset by any receivables (on the asset 
side of the balance sheet) from FSLIC or the FDIC 
for the purpose of calculating the amount of such 
holdings to be included in capital. The amount of the 
offset is limited to the amount of the FSLIC or FDIC 
capital instrument. 


When there is no offsetting obligation, the value of 
warrants and other qualifying core capital instuments 
may be counted as core capital unless the holder of 
the warrants and other instruments is a federal 
deposit insurance fund. If the holder of the capital 
instruments is a federal deposit insurance fund, then 
they must be classified as supplementary capital.

Plaintiff Glendale Federal Bank, FSB (“Glendale”),

in Glendale’s Memorandum in Support of Motion for 
Summary Judgment as to Liability. Those pages, in 
turn, provide the Court with citations to specific 
supporting Exhibits and Affidavits. 

1. Glendale is a federally chartered savings bank 
with over $20 billion in assets. Until 1989, Glendale 
was a federally chartered savings and loan 
association. 

2. Effective November 19, 1981, Glendale entered 
into a transaction with the United States government 
relating to Glendale’s acquisition of First Federal 
Savings and Loan Association of Broward County 
(“Broward”). (F.g., pp. 25-29). The terms of Glendale’s 
transaction with the government are set forth in a 
“Supervisory Action Agreement” dated November 19, 
1981 (“SAA”), and in various contemporaneous docu- 
ments including Federal Home Loan Bank Board 


644 


Resolutions Nos. 81-710, 81-711 and 81-712, a “for- 
bearance letter” attached as Exhibit B to the SAA 
and an Agreement of Merger dated November 12, 
1981 between Glendale and Broward. (£.g., pp. 29-33, 
40-44). The SAA was executed by Glendale and the 
Federal Savings and Loan Insurance Corporation 
(“FSLIC”), an agency of the United States operated 
by the FHLBB, also an agency of the United States. 
(E.g., pp. 2-4, 25-29). 

3. At the time of Glendale’s transaction with the 
government, Broward had been losing money for 
many months at rates ranging from $4-7 million per 
month, and was rapidly heading towards failure. (pp. 
10-11). As of November 19, 1981, Broward had a net 
worth at market value of negative $734,660,000. (£.9., 
p. 10). If Broward had been allowed to fail and had 
been liquidated, the government, pursuant to 
FSLIC’s deposit insurance obligations, would have 
had to satisfy Broward’s massive excess liabilities and 
pay other associated liquidation costs. (E.g., pp. 
11-14). As of November 19, 1981, the government 
estimated the liquidation costs for Broward to be in 
excess of $783 million. (E.g., pp. 11-12). 

4. At the time of Glendale’s transaction with the 
government, FSLIC’s deposit insurance fund was 
running out of cash, and the government therefore 
was seeking non-cash (or reduced cash) solutions for 
failing savings and loans like Broward. (pp. 8-10). One 
non-cash (or reduced cash) technique actively pro- 
moted by the government at the time was the 
“purchase method” of accounting. (pp. 9, 12-15). 

5. In the course of negotiations with Glendale over 
terms of the SAA transaction, the government 
considered other alternatives for avoiding the failure 
and liquidation of Broward. (E.g., pp. 25-26). The 
government determined, however, that no other 


645 


merger partner or other solution was available that

ultimately agreed to with Glendale. (pp. 25-26).

pursuant to the Agreement of Merger between 
Glendale and Broward which was both conditioned

approved by the FHLBB. (pp. 32-34, 18).

purchase method to account for the transaction; that 
the $734,660,000 in resulting goodwill could be used as 
an asset for regulatory capital purposes in accordance 
with Generally Accepted Accounting Principles 
(“GAAP”); and that most of the goodwill would be 
amortized over forty years (with a small portion 
being amortized over 12 years). (pp. 14-33, passim). 

8. Both parties to the transaction specifically 
understood that the terms regarding the inclusion of 
goodwill in regulatory capital and the amortization 
period for goodwill were fundamental provisions of 
the deal. (pp. 14-33, passim). The FSLIC Director 
stated at the time that “the major elements of this 
proposal involve the use of purchase accounting and 
an FSLIC guarantee.” (p. 27). Had the government 
not agreed to allow Glendale to account for goodwill 
in this manner, Glendale would not and could not 
have entered into the transaction. (£.g., pp. 20-21). 
Moreover, without the terms as to goodwill, the 
government as regulator would not have approved it. 
(E.g., p. 21). If Glendale had assumed Broward’s 
excess liabilities without including offsetting goodwill

in capital, it would have rendered Glendale imme-
