## Event

Pinder v. Johnson. Decided May 21, 1996; October Term 1995. On certiorari under 28 U.S.C. §1254(1) to the Fourth Circuit in No. 93-2125, reviewing its May 30, 1995 en banc judgment, 54 F.3d 1169. The question is Officer Johnson's individual qualified immunity on the substantive-due-process claim arising from his alleged conduct and assurances before Pittman's attack.

On March 10, 1989, Johnson responded after Pittman broke into Pinder's home, assaulted her, threw objects and threatened her and her children. Johnson was told of prior threats and an earlier attempt to burn the house. After arresting Pittman, Johnson assured Pinder that Pittman would remain detained overnight. Pinder alleges that this induced her to return to work instead of staying with or arranging protection for her children. Johnson charged trespass and malicious destruction of property under $300. Commissioner Ames released Pittman that evening; Pittman returned, set the house on fire and killed Pinder's three children. No warning of release is alleged.

The district court denied dismissal and immunity. A divided panel affirmed, but its opinion was vacated on rehearing. The en banc court granted immunity, 9–4 in judgment. Its principal opinion classified this account as nonprotection and found inadequate notice while recognizing a different affirmative-danger paradigm; the supporting judges did not all adopt the same reasoning. The competing view treated the assurance-induced withdrawal of private protection as affirmative causation. The review concerns those legal characterizations on proper favorable assumptions, not a finding that Pinder proved the alleged causal chain.

## Participation

Chief Justice Alex-Lamar Stone-Zsela and Justices Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer participate in the decision. No Justice is recused or otherwise nonparticipating.

## Public Action

The Fourth Circuit's judgment granting Johnson individual qualified immunity on the reviewed substantive-due-process claim is affirmed.

## Judgment & Remedy

**Judgment:** Affirmed, 5–4. **Supporting:** Stone-Zsela, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy and Thomas. **Opposing:** Stevens, Souter, Ginsburg and Breyer would vacate the reviewed immunity disposition and remand on a bounded affirmative-danger theory.

Johnson receives judgment on the individual damages claim within this immunity issue. The Court does not decide that the assumed affirmative conduct was constitutional or that custody is indispensable to every possible affirmative-deprivation claim. Other defendants and distinct preserved claims retain their proper disposition. No new merits or damages proceeding on this reviewed claim, liability finding or compensation award is ordered.

## Holdings

### I. Review reaches the legal immunity issue on favorable assumptions

**Controlling proposition:** The §1254(1) review and underlying §1291 legal-immunity appeal permit determination of the separable legal question on the properly assumed favorable account. They do not permit deciding factual sufficiency, selecting disputed intent or causation, or adjudicating the separate excluded claims.

**Authority:** Part I of O'Connor's Opinion of the Court; all nine Justices join.

**Controlling explanation:** Mitchell permits appeal of the separable legal immunity issue. Johnson v. Jones and Kimberlin distinguish that question from whether the plaintiff can prove the facts. Doe and Behrens preserve the same boundary: an official may obtain the legal consequences of the favorable account, but cannot obtain appellate findings that the alleged warnings, contribution or causal effect did not occur.

The district court expressly used Rule 12(b)(6); the appellate opinions describe summary judgment. Neither description authorizes an adverse factual determination here. The asserted assurance-induced withdrawal of private protection can be assumed for legal review without deciding credibility, knowing falsity or whether that withdrawal actually caused the deaths. No second pretrial immunity appeal is established, and no ruling on successive-appeal conditions is necessary.

Official-capacity or municipal claims, equal protection, §1985 and state tort counts do not enter this appeal merely because they concern the same events. Behrens preserves their distinct posture. No new procedural-due-process claim is supplied. The lawful legal question can therefore be decided without resolving the record's factual disputes or extending review to every claim.

### II. Private nonprotection, affirmative deprivation and historical notice remain distinct

**Controlling proposition:** Knowledge of danger, promises of protection and custody of Pittman create no custodial special relationship with Pinder or her children; the alleged affirmative loss of otherwise available private protection must be considered separately from a failure to supply public protection. Individual immunity requires particularized objective notice from all relevant preconduct law, without an identical-case requirement, and neither judicial disagreement nor the official's subjective belief in legality independently decides that inquiry.

**Authority:** Part II of the same unanimous Opinion of the Court.

**Controlling explanation:** DeShaney generally rejects a due-process duty to protect against private violence. Its custodial rule arises from state restraint of the injured person's ability to act, not knowledge, assistance or a promise. It also emphasized that the State had neither created the danger nor increased Joshua's vulnerability; it did not announce a complete test for every affirmative-danger claim. Johnson's alleged alteration of available private protection therefore cannot be discarded simply by identifying Pittman as the assailant.

That distinction does not establish liability. Section 1983 supplies a remedy for an attributable federal deprivation, not a general constitutional duty to perform state protective functions. Daniels excludes merely negligent injury. Culpability must concern the deprivation, not merely the intentional utterance of an assurance; its reservation concerning recklessness and gross negligence cannot be converted into a holding that any nonnegligent reassurance is unconstitutional.

Anderson requires unlawfulness sufficiently apparent in pre-existing law at the appropriate level of specificity. The exact act need not already have been held unlawful. Elder requires the entire relevant preconduct landscape, including adverse decisions and their limits. A subjective element of the underlying claim differs from the official's belief about legality; Kimberlin permits competent circumstantial proof without inventing a substantive mental-state requirement.

March 10, 1989 fixes notice. Collins, Doe, Hodge, Love and Sheets inform present analysis but cannot supply notice retroactively. Love permits a dispositive notice inquiry without mandating either merits-first or immunity-first sequencing. The common principles here do not decide whether the affirmative-danger theory succeeds on its constitutional merits or whether its particular wrong was sufficiently established then.

### III. The contemporaneous law did not sufficiently establish this particular obligation

**Controlling proposition:** Even accepting the alleged assurance-induced withdrawal of private protection for legal review, the pre-March 10, 1989 authorities did not make sufficiently apparent that this noncoercive undertaking, ensuing release and uncorrected exposure constituted Johnson's own constitutional deprivation. He therefore has individual qualified immunity on the reviewed claim; the Court reserves the distinct affirmative-danger merits.

**Authority:** Part III of O'Connor's Opinion of the Court, joined by Stone-Zsela, Scalia, Kennedy and Thomas; five direct joins control.

**Controlling explanation:** The alleged loss of private protection is consequential and more specific than a general failure to prevent crime. The question is nevertheless whether existing law sufficiently identified that combination of assurances, charging decisions, third-party release and reliance as this officer's constitutional wrong. DeShaney had rejected knowledge-and-promise protective duties sixteen days earlier, while leaving affirmative increases in danger incompletely defined.

Bowers distinguished an active state tortfeasor but rejected liability for releasing a dangerous person. Archie discussed increased risk and interference with private aid, yet found neither culpable deprivation nor state creation of danger or hindrance of aid. Its discussion of intentional deprivation and extreme indifference to known mortal risk cannot be detached from those limits. Jensen's distinct danger discussion remains relevant, but its broader knowledge-and-promise relationship ground cannot survive DeShaney. Those cases' adverse outcomes do not erase their reasoning; neither does their general recognition of affirmative injury settle this noncoercive reliance mechanism.

The contrary argument identifies the qualifying wrong as culpable displacement of otherwise available protection against a known immediate mortal danger. Even on that favorable legal account, the extension from earlier affirmative-injury and private-aid principles to this undertaking was not sufficiently apparent. This is uncertainty over constitutional attribution, not a requirement that an earlier case reproduce every fact or award a plaintiff relief. Disagreement among judges is not itself the test.

As Hodge and Doe's Caplinger holding illustrate in their own settings, an individual notice judgment can leave constitutional sufficiency unresolved. Those later decisions provide no retrospective notice here. The conclusion rests neither on finding negligence nor on rejecting alleged reliance. Greater charges are not established to have ensured detention, and unlawful preventive confinement is not the prescribed solution. No freestanding warning entitlement, endorsement of affirmative injury, general custody prerequisite or new actual-reliance immunity defense is adopted.

## Precedent Treatment

- Mitchell v. Forsyth, 472 U.S. 511: applies the separable legal-immunity collateral-order channel; it does not bring factual sufficiency or all related claims into review.
- Johnson v. Jones and Kimberlin v. Quinlan, June 12, 1995: apply the legal/factual appellate boundary; Kimberlin preserves circumstantial proof subject to actual constitutional elements and ordinary burdens.
- DeShaney v. Winnebago County Department of Social Services, 489 U.S. 189: applies the limits on private-protection and custodial duties while preserving the distinction concerning state-created or increased vulnerability. Its foster-care, culpability, individual-immunity and municipal-responsibility reservations are not resolved.
- Daniels v. Williams, 474 U.S. 327: applies the exclusion of mere negligence; its reservation concerning recklessness and gross negligence remains open.
- Anderson v. Creighton, 483 U.S. 635: applies particularized apparent unlawfulness without requiring an identical prior case.
- Elder v. Holloway, February 23, 1994: applies independent review of all relevant preconduct authority on the preserved question; it supplies no retrospective notice or mandatory merits-first sequence.
- Collins v. Harker Heights, February 26, 1992: preserves the distinction between a federal deprivation and state duties or municipal attribution; its reservation of a different affirmative-danger claim remains.
- Doe v. Taylor Independent School District, October 16, 1995: applies its review boundary and preserves its distinct personal-causation and Caplinger notice holdings; official-authority abuse is not treated as the same as this private attack.
- Hodge v. Jones, October 23, 1995: applies the method of individualized notice adjudication with constitutional merits reserved; its confidential-record claim is not made a danger-creation rule.
- Love v. Pepersack, December 11, 1995: preserves lawful sequencing and the distinction between state illegality and a federal wrong; no mandatory order of decision or general merits test is inferred.
- Behrens v. Pelletier, February 21, 1996: applies favorable legal-review assumptions and separate-claim limits; no second-appeal premise is supplied.
- Sheets v. Salt Lake County, April 29, 1996: preserves its confined affirmative-disclosure rule; it neither defines this danger theory nor supplies March 1989 notice.

Bowers v. DeVito, Archie v. City of Racine and Jensen v. Conrad are considered as preconduct circuit authority with their adverse outcomes and limited affirmative-danger discussions intact. They are neither treated as Supreme Court holdings nor overruled. No Supreme Court precedent is overruled; Albright's separate positions are not converted into a general bodily-integrity bar or state-tort cure.

## Law After Decision

Effective May 21, 1996, Johnson is individually immune on the reviewed due-process claim because the particular constitutional attribution was insufficiently established on March 10, 1989. The ruling applies existing objective notice law; it creates no identical-facts prerequisite and does not adopt a defense requiring actual reliance on authoritative law.

The Court leaves the outer substantive boundary of affirmative state-created danger unresolved. A private assailant does not categorically exclude an attributable affirmative official deprivation, but neither promises, serious injury nor intentionally speaking establishes one. No minimum culpability standard for every lesser account, general warning duty, mandatory detention rule or expanded remedial entitlement is announced. Constitutional merits, personal causation, historical notice and appellate authority retain their separate functions.

## Separate Writings

Justice Ginsburg, joined by Justices Stevens, Souter and Breyer, joins Parts I–II but would vacate the reviewed immunity disposition and remand. Injured-party custody is not indispensable where an official's own sufficiently culpable conduct actually removes otherwise available protection and thereby exposes the victims to a known immediate mortal danger. The dissent concludes now that the qualifying form of this alleged deprivation was sufficiently identified by pre-March 10, 1989 law to defeat immunity disposing of the entire theory at this stage.

Bowers's active-tortfeasor distinction, Archie's private-aid reasoning and Jensen's distinct danger discussion, read with DeShaney's no-increased-vulnerability limit, supply that particular principle. Their adverse outcomes and culpability restrictions remain material. Purposefully giving reassurance alone is insufficient; culpability must attach to the deprivation, and actual causal displacement must be shown. The dissent does not restore a knowledge-and-promise special relationship or infer notice from the deaths' severity. It disagrees with the en banc application to this account, not with an invented universal custody rule.

The favorable pleading possibility includes intentional or conscience-shocking deprivation without establishing that mental state. The dissent selects no minimum standard for every lesser account, finds no knowing falsity or favoritism, and does not assume greater charges would have prevented release. A later factual finding cannot create historical legal notice. Materially different or less culpable conduct retains its own constitutional and immunity analysis.

The requested remand would proceed through the Court of Appeals under ordinary preservation, pleading and proof rules, addressing culpability, actual causal predicates and genuinely available defenses only as needed. It would not unconditionally restore the entire district order or vacated panel opinion, mandate discovery, a hearing or trial, award damages, or adjudicate excluded counts. This bounded merits-and-notice position is noncontrolling.

## Procedure After Action

The individual immunity judgment stands on the reviewed substantive-due-process claim. The receiving courts must give effect to Johnson's judgment within that issue; the decision orders no new merits or damages proceeding on it. Official-capacity or municipal liability, equal protection, §1985, Maryland wrongful-death and survival counts, and other legally distinct preserved claims remain outside this disposition. No unpresented procedural claim is added. Supreme Court merits review is complete, subject to ordinary mandate procedures; no formal mandate date or additional Supreme Court event is prescribed.
