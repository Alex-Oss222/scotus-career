I. THE ASSERTED GOVERNMENTAL INTERESTS 
DO NOT JUSTIFY TERMINATING BUSINESS 
WITH PETITIONER BECAUSE OF HIS POLITI- 
CAL ASSOCIATION. 


This case involves governmental abuse of cherished indi- 
vidual freedoms -- participation in the political process through 
the exercise of rights of association and speech. The City of 
Northlake’s policy of political retaliation, whether denomi- 
nated as “patronage” or not, destroys the individual's stand- 
ing as a citizen, insults personal dignity and diminishes self- 
worth. The policy requires City vendors to kneel before the 
sovereign, to make financial tribute to the Mayor, and to re- 
frain from speaking in opposition to the Mayor’s continued 
rule. It thus results in forced contribution, forced associa- 
tion, and the suppression of opposing views. Northlake pe- 
nalizes those who, like Petitioners, refuse to compromise their 
conscience, values and beliefs, by summarily taking away their 
livelihoods. 

Respondents offer two reasons to justify the retribution 
which the City has inflicted upon Petitioners for adhering to 
their beliefs. First, the threat of unwarranted litigation will 
impair the effectiveness and efficiency of government, includ- 
ing both executive and judicial functions. Resp. Br. 12, 14- 
15. Second, Respondents also assert that all contractors must 
be politically loyal to assure efficient government, Resp. Br. 
at 13, 18-21, a requirement more traditionally asserted as serv- 
ing an interest in representative government. See Elrod v. 


Burns, 427 U.S. 347, 367 (1976); Rutan v. Republican Party, 
497 U.S. 62, 70 (1990).' Neither claim justifies the whole- 
sale evisceration of the associational rights of independent 
contractors.

Il. RESPONDENTS’ RETALIATORY POLICY VIO- 
LATES RIGHTS OF POLITICAL AFFILIATION 
UNDER ANY STANDARD OF CONSTITUTIONAL 
REVIEW. 


Respondents argue that the proper standard to be applied 
in determining whether political affiliation can be required 
generally for municipal contractors is a standard of reason- 
ableness, as used in United Public Workers of America v. 
Mitchell, 330 U.S. 75 (1947). Resp. Br. at 15. They further 
argue, borrowing from public employee speech cases such as 
Pickering v. Bd. of Educ., 391 U.S. 562 (1968), and Connick 
v. Meyers, 461 U.S. 138 (1983), that “a wide degree of defer- 
ence” be given the City’s judgment. Resp. Br. at 15. 

All contractors, simply because they do business with 
government, are not the equivalent of government employ- 
ees. Thus, the starting point of any analysis should be the 
assumption that contractors are no different from the general 
citizenry, whose political speech and associational activities 
have the highest protection; therefore, government efforts to 
abridge these rights must be subject to strict scrutiny. Buckley 
v. Valeo, 424 U.S. 1, 16(1976). Consequently, Petitioners do

not seek to be treated as if they were public employees, look- 
ing only to Elrod, Branti and Rutan for protection. The rights 
to choose one’s own political affiliations, to be free from com- 
pelled association and support, and to be free from viewpoint 
suppression are rights that this Court consistently has reaf- 
firmed in cases arising outside of the employment context, 
both before and after the Elrod, Branti and Rutan decisions. 
Petitioners’ rights are rooted in that larger body of precedent. 
See Pet. Br. at 9-21. 

Even assuming, however, that this Court’s public em- 
ployee decisions do provide the appropriate framework to 
judge this restraint on the political affiliation of non-employ- 
ees, it does not automatically follow that a mere test of “rea- 
sonableness” under Mitchell is the touchstone. Commitment 
to, and association to advance, political belief takes various 
forms, from simply joining a political party, to making cam- 
paign contributions, to pure speech itself, like displaying signs 
on one’s private property. When these fundamental rights 
are at stake, this Court has applied heightened scrutiny. Elrod, 
427 US. at 362. 

Contrary to Respondents’ reliance on them, this Court's 
decisions upholding the Hatch Act do not support some less 
stringent test for contract patronage. The Hatch Act, with its 
implementing Civil Service Commission regulations, left fed- 
eral employees free to express opinions privately and pub- 
licly on political subjects and candidates, and to make finan- 
cial contributions to political parties and organizations. United 


States Civil Service Commission v. National Association of

that patronage practices are necessary for efficient control of 
public services. For 30 years, notwithstanding his political 
affiliation, John Gratzianna and O' Hare Truck Services per- 
formed competently in every respect. The City thus has com- 
pletely failed to assert any “necessary impact on the actual 
operation of government” caused by contractors’ affiliation 
that a Pickering balance requires. National Treasury, 115 
S.Ct. at 1014, quoting Pickering, 391 U.S. at 571. 

Just as this Court in National Treasury rejected 
government's interest in preventing actual and perceived cor- 
ruption because its ban on honoraria for non-work-related 
speech was unsupported by a showing of harm, Respondents’ 
interest here is too attenuated to support its restraint on Peti- 
tioners’ nongovernmental-worksite associational activities. 
Accordingly, Respondents’ interest in contact patronage can- 
not be said to be weighty. 

Against this insubstantial interest must be weighed (he 
“breadth and intrusiveness” of its punitive action. National 
Treasury, 115 S.Ct. at 1020 (O’Connor, J., concurring). This 
side of the balance weighs not just contractors’ own interests 
in being able to manifest political beliefs with financial con- 
tributions and candidate affiliation, but also those with whom 
contractors would associate for political purposes as well as 
the candidates who would receive and use those contribu- 


14 


tions. See National Treasury, 115 S.Ct. at 1015 (considering 
the “significant burden on the public's right to read and hear 
what [government] employees would otherwise have written 
and said”). Obviously, these First Amendment interests far 
outweigh Northlake’s assertions of interest and must prevail 
in this Pickering balance. 

Accordingly, if a lower-tier standard of review drawn from 
Mitchell and the public employee speech cases were applied 
to this political association case, the balance is decidedly in 
Petitioners’ favor. Northlake’s “bare assertions” of need for 
control to enhance efficiency are not sufficiently substantial 
to support wholesale contract patronage.
