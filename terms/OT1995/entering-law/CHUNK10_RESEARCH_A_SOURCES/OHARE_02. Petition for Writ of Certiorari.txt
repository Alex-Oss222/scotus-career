STATEMENT OF THE CASE 


Petitioner O’ Hare Truck Service, Inc. (““O’ Hare”) is a busi- 
ness located in Northlake, Illinois, that provides towing ser- 
vices at the request of municipalities throughout Cook and 
DuPage Counties in northern Illinois. O'Hare is owned and 
operated by Petitioner Gratzianna. App. 21a, para. 2. 

Since 1965, O'Hare has participated in a system through 
which it provided the City of Northlake with towing services 
when the City needs a vehicle towed, as a result of an accident, 
mechanical breakdown, abandonment or other reason. App. 
21a, para. 6; 22a, para. 9. 

The City distributes its towing work among O’ Hare and other 
tow companies by means of a towing rotation list with the names 
of the various tow companies to be contacted in turn when a 
need arises. App. 22a, para. 7. O'Hare has provided the City 
with towing services via the rotation list for almost thirty years. 


4 


App. 22a, para. 9. A substantial part of O’ Hare’s income came 
from fees paid by municipalities and others as a result of re- 
quests for towing services by Northlake and other municipali- 
ties. App. 21a-22a, para. 6. 

It has been Northlake’s policy and practice that companies 
on the rotation list will not be removed from the list without 
cause. Several mayors of Northlake, including Respondent 
Paxson and Paxson’s predecessor, confirmed this policy and 
stated that O’ Hare would remain on the rotation list so long as 
the quality of its services was maintained. App. 22a, para. 8, 
10. 

Nevertheless, following the mayoral election in the spring 
of 1993, O’Hare was removed from the rotation list by Police 
Chief Sapoznik, acting at the request of newly re-elected Mayor 
Paxson. App. 23a, para. 14. O’Hare’s owner, Mr. Gratzianna, 
had refused to contribute to Mayor Paxson’s re-election cam- 
paign, and had publicly supported Mayor Paxson’s opponent, 
placing campaign posters for the opponent in O’ Hare’s place 
of business. App. 23a, para. 12, 13. On September 24, 1993, 
Petitioners filed this action, alleging that Respondents Mayor 
Paxson, Police Chief Sapoznik, and the City of Northlake, had 
violated their First and Fourteenth Amendment rights of politi- 
cal association and due process by ceasing to do business with 
O’ Hare in retaliation for Mr. Gratzianna’s refusal to contribute 
financially to Mayor Paxson’s campaign and for his support for 
the Mayor’s opponent during the last election. 

Respondents moved to dismiss the Complaint under Fed- 
eral Rule of Civil Procedure 12(b)(6). The District Court indi- 
cated in a preliminary order that it was disposed to grant dis- 
missal based on the Seventh Circuit’s holding in Downtown 
Auto Parks, Inc. v. City of Milwaukee, 938 F.2d 705 (7th Cir. 
1991), which rejected any First Amendment protection (whether 
speech or association) for non-employees. /d. at 709 n. 5. Pur- 


suant to the preliminary order, the Petitioners simultaneously 
submitted a First Amended Complaint and a memorandum of 
law opposing dismissal arguing that Downtown Auto Parks 
conflicted with pertinent Supreme Court precedent. The Dis- 
trict Court subsequently dismissed the First Amended Com- 
plaint with prejudice. 

On appeal, Petitioners asked the Seventh Circuit to recon- 
sider its broad denial of First Amendment rights to indepen- 
dent contractors in light of the principles enunciated in Rutan v. 
Republican Party of Illinois, 497 U.S. 62 (1990), and others of 
this Court’s cases. The Seventh Circuit noted that it had al- 
ready considered the impact of Rutan in Downtown Auto Parks, 
however, and refused to revisit the issue. Although the court 
had acknowledged in Downtown Auto Parks that “the scope of 
Rutan, and rationale behind it, seem to be at odds with the hold- 
ing of” earlier Seventh Circuit decisions rejecting contractors’ 
political association rights as the basis for a claim, the court 
nevertheless clung to the reasoning of those earlier cases. 938 
F.2d at 709. The court concluded that “it should be up to the 
Supreme Court to extend Elrod.” O'Hare, App. Sa. Relying 
on Downtown Auto Parks and earlier cases, the Seventh Cir- 
cuit summarily rejected Petitioners’ First Amendment claim 
and affirmed the dismissal. 


REASONS FOR GRANTING THE WRIT 


The United States Court of Appeals for the Seventh Circuit 
has held that an independent contractor does not state a claim 
under the First Amendment, when he complains that the city 
terminated their business relationship because he declined to 
support or to contribute to the election campaign of the mayor 
of the city and instead supported the opposition candidate. Not 
only is the Seventh Circuit decision in conflict with this Court’s 


6 


well settled precedent forbidding government to deny benefits 
because of one’s political beliefs and associations, it also con- 
tributes to a deepening conflict among the Courts of Appeals 
over the degree of protection to be afforded to the core political 
expression and association of independent contractors and the 
standards under which such claims are to be considered. This 
case, along with Heiser v. Umbehr, No. 94-1654, provides the 
Court with a most timely opportunity to provide much needed 
guidance to the Courts of Appeals on several aspects of these 
important issues. 


I. THIS CASE RAISES AN IMPORTANT 
QUESTION CONCERNING POLITICAL 
ASSOCIATION WHICH SHOULD BE 
DECIDED BY THIS COURT 


1. Respondents eliminated O’ Hare Trucking from the City’s 
referral list to punish Mr. Gratzianna for supporting the politi- 
cal candidate of his choice and declining to support the incum- 
bent mayor. This conduct calls into question the scope of the 
First Amendment’s protection of political association and 
whether it is broad enough to encompass the political activities 
of independent contractors. 

The circumstances of O’ Hare Truck Service’s removal from 
the City of Northlake’s towing system confirm that this matter 
involves the most basic rights of “political belief and associa- 
tion [that] constitute the core of those activities protected by 
the First Amendment,” Rutan v. Republican Party of Illinois, 
497 U.S. 62, 69 (1990) (citing Elrod v. Burns, 427 U.S. 347, 
355-356 (1976) (plurality opinion)), including the rights of a 
person: to choose his political affiliations; to demonstrate his 
support for candidates of his choice; and, to decline to associ- 
ate and to provide financial support to other candidates. 


This Court consistently has reiterated that a major purpose 
of the First Amendment is to protect discussion of governmen- 
tal affairs, including discussions of candidates for public of- 
fice. Mills v. Alabama, 384 U.S. 214, 218 (1966). In Monitor 
Patriot Co. v. Roy, 401 U.S. 265, 272 (1971), the Court referred 
to campaigns for public office as the “fullest and most urgent 
application” of the First Amendment. It is equally settled that 
business entities, as well as individuals, enjoy the right to par- 
ticipate in the political process. First Nat'l Bank of Boston v. 
Bellotti, 435 U.S. 765 (1978). 

The Court’s decisions also have affirmed that the right of a 
citizen to make political contributions and expenditures is a 
fundamental First Amendment interest. In Buckley v. Valeo, 
424 U.S. 1, 16-17, 22-23 (1976), the Court explained the inter- 
related character of political expression, association, and po- 
litical spending. “Making a contribution, like joining a politi- 
cal party, serves to affiliate a person with a candidate. In addi- 
tion, it enables like-minded persons to pool their resources in 
furtherance of common political goals.” /d. at 22. Conversely, 
exaction of contributions from unwilling contributors was de- 
scribed by the Court as “tantamount to coerced belief.” Rutan 
v. Republican Party of Illinois, 497 U.S. 62, 69 (1990) (approv- 
ingly citing Buckley, 424 U.S. at 19). 

This Court should take this case to decide, hopefully in the 
affirmative, whether terminating valuable business benefits as 
a patronage practice violates the associational rights of inde- 
pendent contractors. Additionally, this case presents a particu- 
larly timely opportunity to rule on this issue because 
privatization of government services is so rapidly expanding 
today. See, e.g.,G. Washburn, RTA Chief Urges CTA to Privatize 
Some Bus Routes, Chi. Trib., June 9, 1995, Metro West section 
at | (cities nationwide are privatizing former municipal ser- 
vices; over 50 city services contracted out in Indianapolis); J. 


Rainey, Study Urges L.A. to Privatize More Services, L.A. 
Times, November 14, 1994 at Metro B! (Los Angeles’ 
privatization efforts compared to programs elsewhere); G. Jus- 
tice, Suburbs Looking to Privatize Services, Phila. Inquirer, 
March 17, 1995, Local section at Al (cities’ budget woes are 
driving trend toward privatization). 

2. The Seventh Circuit’s decision in this case excluded, as 
did the decisions of the Third and Eighth Circuit discussed be- 
low, an entire class of persons from the protection of the First 
Amendment “[b]ecause O’ Hare is not an employee of the city.” 
O'Hare, App. Sa. In holding that independent contractors wil! 
not be afforded even the restricted rights of political speech 
and association that are enjoyed by public employees, the court 
followed the reasoning of its own prior decisions which had 
established this disfavored category of independent contractors. 
See LaFalce v. Houston, 712 F.2d 292 (7th Cir. 1983); Down- 
town Auto Parks, Inc. v. City of Milwaukee, 938 F.2d 705 (7th 
Cir.), cert. denied, _ U.S. ___, 112 S. Ct. 640 (1991). 

However, the appeals court’s reasoning distorts the impact 
of Elrod, Branti, and Rutan and erroneously suggests that those 
cases gave special status to employees not conferred on others. 
The Seventh Circuit has taken what was a limited exception to 
the general protection of the First Amendment and converted it 
to a limitation upon the right of persons generally. Nothing in 
the decisions of this Court warrants a blanket denial of the nghts 
of political association based solely upon occupational status. 
Similar efforts by government bodies to impose complete bans 
on fundamental First Amendment rights because of a person's 
identity or occupation have been firmly rejected over the years. 
See McDaniel v. Paty, 435 U.S. 618 (1978) (clergy); Abood v. 
Detroit Board of Educ., 431 U.S. 209 (1977) (non-union work- 
ers); Pell v. Procunier, 417 U.S. 817 (1974) (prisoners), Tinker 
v. Des Moines Indep. Sch. Dist., 393 U.S. 503 (1969) (students); 


9 


Pickering v. Board of Educ., 39\ U.S. 563 (1968) (teachers). 

Moreover, this Court has ruled in the Fifth Amendment con- 
text that government contractors are no less compelled to waive 
their mght against self-incrimination upon threat of loss of con- 
tract than government employees faced with loss of a job. 
Lefkowitz v. Turley, 414 U.S. 70, 83 (1973). See also Rutan, 
497 U.S. at 75 (deprivations less harsh than dismissals never- 
theless force employees to conform their beliefs). 

The Seventh Circuit has never addressed why, in light of 
these precedents, independent contractors should be treated as 
a class undeserving of constitutional protection. This Court 
should decide in this case whether those who serve govern- 
ment as independent contractors are excluded from the protec- 
tive ambit of the First Amendment and ipso facto forfeit their 
associational nghts. 

3. In this case, as in others involving governmental interfer- 
ence with the fundamental nghts of political speech and asso- 
ciation, it is immaterial that O’ Hare does not have a right to be 
or remain on the city’s list of approved companies to whom it 
will refer business. See Perry v. Sindermann, 408 U.S. 593 
(1972). Protection of these rights of political speech and asso- 
ciation 1s not reserved for persons in government employment. 
However, even Elrod and Branti both involved at-will employ- 
ex . with no expectation of continuing employment. Rutan, 497 
U.S. at 72. And the same was true in Rutan for the applicants 
for jobs and employees and former employees seeking promo- 
tions, transfers and recalls after lay-offs, Jd. 497 U.S. at 72, 77 
n. 9. 

This case therefore raises the additional and important ques- 
tion of whether the interest in government work of an indepen- 
dent contractor constitutes a “valuable governmental benefit” 
protected under the Perry doctrine of unconstitutional condi- 
tions. 408 U.S. at 597. 


10 


4. The Seventh Circuit's sole focus upon O’ Hare's status as 
independent contractor precluded the court from properly ana- 
lyzing O’ Hare’s claim under the strict legal standards demanded 
by the First Amendment. Accordingly, it conducted no mean- 
ingful analysis of corny eting interests under any prescribed stan- 
dard of scrutiny, or other test, enunciated or even obliquely sug- 
gested by the prior precedent of this Court. Because the City of 
Northlake retaliated against O' Hare for its owner's most basic 
and fundamental rights of political association, petitioner sub- 
mits that the actions of the city and its mayor are subject to 
exacting scrutiny. Buckley v. Valeo, 424 U.S. at 44-45; Meyer 
v. Grant, 486 U.S. 414, 420 (1988): Mcintyre v. Ohio Elections 
Comm'n, ___ U.S. ___, 115 S. Ct. 1511 (1995). While these 
cases involved government restrictions on the speech and asso- 
ciation of private citizens, in Rutan the Court used the same 
exacting scrutiny to examine restrictions on the political asso- 
ciational rights of public employees, 497 U.S. at 74. 

In this case, there is no hint of any public governmental in- 
terest supporting the removal; the only interest in the record is 
the mayor's individual financial and political self-interest. Had 
O'Hare acquiesced in the demand for a political contribution 
to remain on the rotation list, there would have been a quid pro 
quo arrangement of the type condemned in Buckley: “To the 
extent that large contributions are given to secure a political 
quid pro quo from current and potential office holders, the in- 
tegrity of our system of representative democracy is under- 
mined.” Buckley, 424 U.S. at 26-27. Nothing in the record 
supports any legitimate governmental interest in O’ Hare's re- 
moval from the rotation list. 

Nor do the considerations advanced by the Seventh Circuit 
in O'Hare and its earlier decisions to distinguish the political 
association rights of independent contractors from those of pub- 
lic employees constitute the type of compelling state interest 


i ae te ees 


required by this Court's decisions. First, relying on its own 
decision in LaFalce v. Houston, 712 F.2d 292 (7th Cir. 1983), 
cert. denied, 464 U.S. 1044 (1984), the Seventh Circuit con- 
sidered the extent of the likely interference with an indepen- 
dent contractor's freedom to associate politically and found 
“little chance that a contractor's freedom would be impinged.” 
O'Hare, App. Sa. This conclusion was explained in LaFalce 
and in Downtown Auto Parks as resting on the court's “sur- 
mise” that “independent contractors generally tend to be less 
dependent on government jobs than are public employees.” 
Downtown Auto Parks, 938 F.2d at 709. 

The Seventh Circuit's suggestion that the quantum of public 
business and a contractor's presumed lack of total dependency 
on that business justify infringements on political association 
is without support in this Court's decisions. Even if a contrac- 
tor enjoys other public and private business, that fact does not 
supply a government interest in retaliating against political 
speech. One need not be totally dependent on government in 
order to assert First Amendment rights. For example, the tax 
exemption in Speiser v. Randall, 357 U.S. 513 (1958), may have 
been of negligible amount compared to the citizen's income or 
net worth. Denial of the benefit to punish political belief and 
association was the essence of the claim, as it is here. 

The Seventh Circuit also failed to identify any state interest 
implicated by its second ground in LaFailce, the potential deci- 
sion of some public contractors to act as “political hermaphro- 
dites” by contributing to both political parties to insulate them- 
selves from retaliation. Of course, any such decision by a pri- 
vate individual or entity to contribute or not contribute is pro- 
tected by the First Amendment. See Buckley, 424 U.S. at 16. 
That some business owners may choose to contribute broadly 
provides no justification for coercing political contributions 
from those who would refrain from supporting some or all can- 


12 


didates. Elrod, 427 U.S. at 355; Rutan, 497 U.S. at 69; Miami 
Herald Publishing Company v. Tornillo, 418 U.S. 241 (1974); 
Wooley v. Maynard, 430 U.S. 705 (1977). 

The appellate court apparently confused the interests of an 
incumbent with the interests of his municipality. The interests 
of party and officeholder and the legitimate interests of govern- 
ment often conflict; in this case the Seventh Circuit has upheld 
the interests of the official without appropriate consideration 
of the public interest. 

This Court should grant this petition so that it may define 
the level of scrutiny which attaches to the rights in issue here. 
The Court should then ascertain whether there could be any 
compelling, vital, or overriding governmental interest to jus- 
tify the restriction of core political association and whether such 
restrictions are narrowly tailored. 


Il. THE SEVENTH CIRCUIT’S DECISION 
CONTRIBUTES TO A DEEP CONFLICT 
AMONG THE FEDERAL COURTS OF 
APPEAL REGARDING THE PROTECTION OF 
INDEPENDENT CONTRACTORS’ RIGHTS OF 
POLITICAL ASSOCIATION 


A deep division currently exists among the federal courts of 
appeal concerning the ability of government to punish inde- 
pendent contractors on the basis of political association. Two 
radically different conceptions of First Amendment rights out- 
side of the employment context underlie this disagreement. The 
decisions also display a general confusion as to the constitu- 
tional principles that are applicable. Unfortunately, the oppor- 
tunities for conflict and confusion will only multply, as more 
and more local units of governments privatize services that once 
were performed by government employees. For all of these

reasons, the Court should address this area of law and resolve 
the confusion in the circuits. 

The Seventh Circuit's decision in the present case rests on a 
line of cases dating back to LaFalce v. Houston, 712 F.2d 292 
(7th Cir. 1983), in which the court refused to afford contractors 
the First Amendment freedom of political association which 
government employees possess under Elrod v. Burns, 427 U.S. 
347 (1976), and Branti v. Finkel, 445 U.S. 507 (1980). The 
court distinguished the situations of independent contractors 
and public employees, hypothesizing that contractors were bet- 
ter equipped economically than employees to deal with any 
coercive pressure from politically-based decision-making. Al- 
th »gh the court found in Downtown Auto Parks, 938 F.2d 705, 
that much of the support for this position had been eroded by 
Rutan, the court reasoned that the Supreme Court had not yet 
directly addressed the rights of independent contractors per se. 
Id. at 709-710. Accordingly, the Seventh Circuit was free to 
continue to hold that the government may retaliate against a 
contractor for that contractor's speech or political associations. 
The Seventh Circuit makes no distinction between free speech 
and association cases, affording no protection to independent 
contractors in either situation. /d. at 709 n.5. 

Other federal courts of appeal, including the Third and the 
Eighth Circuits, have taken the same position as the Seventh 
Circuit. In Horn v. Kean, 796 F.2d 668 (3d Cir. 1986) (en 
banc), the Third Circuit agreed with the rationale of LaFalce 
and refused to apply Elrod and Branti to recognize a cause of 
action for a contractor claiming retaliation because of his party 
affiliation. That court has not revisited the issue of indepen- 
dent contractors’ First Amendment rights since that decision. 
The Eighth Circuit case, Sweeney v. Bond, 669 F.2d 542 (8th 
Cir.), cert. denied sub nom. Schenberg v. Bond, 459 U.S. 878 
(1982), predated LaFalce and, like Horn, rejected a claim of 


14 


retaliation against an independent contractor based on party 
affiliation. While Sweeney is still the law of the Circuit for 
association or patronage cases involving independent contrac- 
tors, that court recently retreated somewhat from that position 
by allowing an independent contractor to bring a claim of re- 
taliation for the exercise of his right to free speech. See Smith 
v. Cleburne County Hosp., 870 F.2d 1375 (8th Cir.), cert. de- 
nied, 493 U.S. 847 (1989) (the free speech claims of an inde- 
pendent contractor who was analogous to an employee could 
be considered). 

In direct opposition to the Seventh Circuit’s approach, the 
Tenth Circuit in Abercrombie v. City of Catoosa, 896 F.2d 1228 
(10th Cir. 1990), recognized political association by an inde- 
pendent contractor (a towing company’s lack of support for a 
mayor during a re-election campaign) as activity protected by 
the First Amendment. The court relied on Perry and Elrod for 
the more general principle that the government may not deny a 
person a benefit on any basis that infringes his constitutionally 
protected interests. /d. at 1233. Also in contrast to the Seventh 
Circuit's position, the Fifth Circuit earlier this year ruled that 
the government may not penalize an independent contractor 
for exercising his First Amendment freedom of speech simply 
because it does not like the content of that speech. See Blackburn 
v. City of Marshall, 42 F.3d 925 (Sth Cir. 1995). 

While Blackburn is limited to free speech and not political 
association, it implicitly rejects the blanket ban on contractors’ 
expressive rights formulated by the Seventh Circuit. As the 
Court explained in Blackburn: 


At the outset, we reject the district court’s ap- 
parent assumption that only public employees 
enjoy the protections of the First Amendment. 
The district court’s reasoning is inverted. Ev- 
ery citizen enjoys the First Amendment’s pro- 


15 


tections against governmental interference with 
free speech, but the First Amendment rights of 
public employees are restricted by the nature of 
the employer-employee relationship. 


Id. at 931. 

The Blackburn court went on to identify a two-tier analysis. 
If the claimant is either a public employee or closely akin to 
one, the test announced in Pickering v. Board of Educ., 391 
U.S. 563 (1968), and Connick v. Myers, 461 U.S. 138 (1983), 
applies. For non-employees or ordinary citizens, “a court gen- 
erally examines a free speech claim under the more First Amend- 
ment friendly standard enunciated in Perry [v. Sindermann, 408 
U.S. 593 (1972)].” 42 F3d at 932. Because the tow truck op- 
erator bringing the claim in Blackburn was not similar to an 
employee, the court applied the Perry test. 

Recently, and in conformity with its earlier decision in 
Abercrombie, supra, the Tenth Circuit in Umbehr v. McClure, 
44 F.3d 876 (10th Cir. 1995) cert. granted sub nom. Heiser v. 
Umbehr, __ U.S. __ (certiorari granted June 29, 1995) (No. 94- 
1654), agreed with this analysis. Although Heiser involved a 
free speech claim only, the court noted in dictum that associa- 
tional ties also enjoy protection from governmental interfer. 
ence, citing Rutan, 497 U.S. at 76: 


The Court stated the overriding principle as fol- 
lows: The First Amendment prevents the govern- 
ment, except in the most compelling circum- 
stances, from wielding its power to interfere with 
its employees’ freedom to believe and associate, 
or to not believe and not associate. 


Umbehr, 44 F.3d at 882. (See also Perry, 408 U.S. at 597 (“For 
if the government could deny a benefit to a person because of 
his constitutionally protected speech or associations, his exer- 


16 


cise of those freedoms would in effect be penalized and inhib- 
ited”) (emphasis added)). The Tenth Circuit recognized that 
this holding placed it squarely in conflict with the position 
adopted by other circuits, including the Seventh Circuit in 
LaFalce (and O'Hare), and specifically requested Supreme 
Court guidance to clarify this area. /d. at 883. 

The Ninth Circuit has not decided a case on the First Amend- 
ment rights of independent contractors per se, but its decision 
in Hyland v. Wonder, 972 F.2d 1129, 1135 (9th Cir. 1992), cert. 
denied, __ U.S.___, 113 S. Ct. 2337 (1993), indicates its agree- 
ment with the position taken by the Fifth and Tenth Circuits. 
Other federal courts of appeals have given independent con- 
tractors the same First Amendment rights as public employees, 
judging their free speech claims under the Pickering/Connick 
standard. In White Plains Towing Corp. v. Patterson, 991 F.2d 
1049 (2d Cir.), cert. denied, __. U.S. ___, 114 S. Ct. 185 (1993), 
the Second Circuit applied the Pickering/Connick test on the 
assumption that a towing operator who did not have a formal 
contract with the state nevertheless had an interest “tantamount 
to employment.” /d. at 1059. Similarly, in Smith v. Cleburne 
County Hospital, 870 F.2d 1375 (8th Cir. 1989), the Eighth 
Circuit found that a doctor-hospital relationship was sufficiently 
similar to an employment relationship that Pickering governed 
the doctor’s free speech claim. 

Unfortunate’ . the courts adopting these two approaches 
rarely identify o: enunciate the underlying theories, and, as a 
result, confusion reigns in an ever-widening area of law. Po- 
litical association cases such as the present one, which involve 
a contractor's refusal to provide support, financial or otherwise, 
to an incumbent public official in an election, are a particular 
source of confusion because they involve both freedom of 
speech and freedom of association concerns. Courts often gloss 
over the dual nature of these cases.

On June 29, 1995, this Court granted certiorari in Heiser vy. 
Umbehr (decision below, Umbehr v. McClure, 44 F.3d 876 (10th 
Cir. 1995)). While that case, like this one, involves the issue of 


18 


whether First Amendment rights extend to those who provide 
services to the government, it involves only a free speech claim. 
As a result, a decision by this Court in Heiser likely will be 
restricted to the issue of a contractor’s free speech claims unre- 
lated to political association issues. Thus, Heiser is as far from 
resolving the issues presented by O'Hare and its kin as Pickering 
was from resolving the issues presented by Elrod and its prog- 
eny. 

Even after this Court reaches a decision in Heiser, courts are 
likely to continue to have difficulty distinguishing political as- 
sociation claims from other speech claims by contractors, and 
will apply various tests on an ad hoc basis. Courts will con- 
tinue to be divided (as they are now) over several points: 
whether the tests applicable to an employee's free speech claims 
should also be applied to an independent contractor's political 
association claims; whether political association cases should 
be governed by the Elrod/Branti/Rutan analysis, or whether 
courts should attempt to define for themselves some blended 
type of analysis in political association cases. None of these 
questions is likely to be resolved by the Court’s ultimate deci- 
sion in Heiser. Because the need for guidance is as pressing in 
the area of contractors’ political association rights as in the 
speech area, Petitioners urge this Court to resolve both issues 
by accepting this case for review as well.

I. First Amendment 


O'Hare claims that, by removing it from the rotation list in 
response to its support of Paxson’s mayoral opponent, Northlake 
infringed its First Amendment right to freedom of political as- 
sociation. In Elrod v. Burns, 427 U.S. 347, 96 S. Ct. 2673, 49 
L. Ed. 2d 547 (1976), and Branti v. Finkel, 445 U.S. 507, 100 
S. Ct. 1287, 63 L. Ed. 2d 574 (1980), the Supreme Court held 
that most government employees may not be discharged solely 
on the basis of their political affiliation. The Court extended 
its holding in Rutan v. Republican Party of Illinois, 497 U.S. 
62, 110 S. Ct. 2729, 111 L. Ed. 2d 52 (1990), to cover hiring, 
promotion, transfers and recalls as well. 

Most courts that have explicitly addressed the issue whether 
to extend Elrod’s protection beyond government employees, 
however, have refused to do so. See, e.g., Horn v. Kean, 796 
F.2d 668 (3rd Cir. 1986); LaFalce v. Houston, 712 F.2d 292 
(7th Cir. 1983), cert. denied, 464 U.S. 1044, 104 S. Ct. 712, 79 
L. Ed. 2d 175 (1984); Sweeney v. Bond, 669 F.2d 542, 545 (8th 
Cir.), cert. denied, 459 U.S. 878, 103 S. Ct. 174, 74 L. Ed. 2d 

143 (1982). Recently, however, in Blackburn v. City of Marshall, 
42 F.3d 925 (Sth Cir. 1995), the Fifth Circuit, in a case similar 
to the one now before us, implicitly took a contrary position. 
See also Abercrombie v. City of Catoosa, 896 F.2d 1228 (10th 
Cir. 1990). On the other hand, in Downtown Auto Parks, Inc. v. 
City of Milwaukee, 938 F.2d 705 (7th Cir. 1991), this circuit 
held (consistent with Horn, LaFalce, and Sweeney) that a city 
may refuse to renew a parking lot lease with an independent 
contractor based upon that contractor's political affiliation. The

plaintiff in Downtown Auto Parks asked that we reexamine, in 
light of Rutan, our holding in LaFalce, the first case in this 
circuit that declined to afford independent contractors Elrod’s

In LaFalce, we gave three reasons for declining to extend 
Elrod to independent contractors. First, we examined the ex- 
tent of the likely interference with an independent contractor’s 
freedom to associate politically. We found that, compared to 
the impact on governmental employees, there was little chance 
that a contractor's freedom would be impinged. Second, we 
noted that the practical, administrative consequences of extend-

the issue. Because O'Hare is not an employee of the city, we 
hold that the district court did not err in dismissing O’ Hare's

To provide this District Court with subject matter jurisdic-

by invoking 42 U.S.C. § 1983 (“Section 1983”), claiming the 
violation of their constitutional rights to freedom of speech and 
to due process of law. 

But Downtown Auto Parks, Inc. v. City of Milwaukee, 938 
F.2d 705 (7th Cir. 1991) sounds the death knell of both of those 
claims. Downtown Auto Parks teaches that a non-employee of 
a unit of state government, in the absence of a contractual rela- 
tionship with that governmental body, lacks any interest in prop- 
erty such as to render actionable under Section 1983 any in- 
fringement of the non-employee’s expectation of doing busi-

Amendment protection pronounced in Rutan v. Republican 
Party of Illinois, 497 U.S. 62, 110 S. Ct. 2729, 111 L. Ed. 2d 52 
(1990). And Downtown Auto Parks rejects any non-employee’s 
cause of action of the type advanced here under Section 1983 
— either in terms of a claimed violation of free speech (938 
F.2d at 708-10) or in terms of any claimed entitlement to due 
process of law (id. at 710-11). 

That set of principles clearly dooms O’Hare’s and 
Gratzianna’s present federal claims against Paxson and Sapoznik
