STATEMENT OF THE CASE 


For nearly three decades, beginning in 1965 and continu- 
ing until July of 1993, Petitioners O’ Hare Truck Service, Inc. 
(“O'Hare”) and John Gratzianna provided towing services to 
Respondent City of Northlake, Illinois (“City”). First 
Amended Complaint at $] 9, 14, P.A. 22a, 23a. O'Hare pro- 
vided the City with towing services under various circum- 
stances, including accidents, mechanical breakdowns, and the 
abandonment of vehicles. 44 5,7, PA. 21a, 22a. When the 


a Mn 


City needed a vehicle towed, it would request service from a 
company on a list which was maintained by the City police 
department. It was the practice of the City to distribute its 
towing work among these companies -- including O’ Hare -- 
by selecting them in turn. 4 7, P.A. 22a. During this entire 
period, O’ Hare performed these services in a professional and 
competent manner. { 21, P.A. 24a. 

In 1993, Respondent Reid Paxson ran for reelection as 
mayor. 4 11,P.A.22a. Mr. Gratzianna was asked by Paxson’s 
campaign committee to contribute to Paxson’s reelection cam- 
paign. ¥ 12, PA. 22-23a. Mr. Gratzianna refused to do so 
and instead openly supported Paxson’s opponent, including 
placing campaign posters at O’Hare’s place of business in 
Northlake. { 13, P.A. 23a. 

Because Mr. Gratzianna refused to contribute to Paxson’s 
campaign and instead supported the mayor’s opponent, in July 
1993, the City removed O’ Hare from its list of towing com- 
panies and stopped doing business with Petitioners. {J 14, 
16, 17, P.A. 23a. As a consequence, Petitioners lost a sub- 
stantial amount of income. ¢ 15, P.A. 23a. 

On September 24, 1993, Petitioners filed this action, al- 
leging that Respondents, Mayor Paxson, Police Chief 
Sapoznik, and the City of Northlake, had violated their First 
and Fourteenth Amendment rights of political association, 
speech and due process by ceasing to do business with O’ Hare 
because of Mr. Gratzianna’s refusal to contribute financially 
to Mayor Paxson’s campaign and his support for the mayor's 
opponent during the last election. 


Respondents moved to dismiss the Complaint under Fed- 
eral Rule of Civil Procedure 12(b)(6). The District Court 
indicated in a preliminary order that it was inclined to grant 
dismissal based on the Seventh Circuit’s holding in Down- 
town Auto Parks, Inc. v. City of Milwaukee, 938 F.2d 705 
(7th Cir.), cert. denied, 502 U.S. 1005 (1991), which rejected 
any First Amendment protection (whether for speech or po- 
litical association) for independent contractors. Pursuant to 
the preliminary order, the Petitioners simultaneously submit- 
ted a First Amended Complaint and a memorandum of law 
opposing dismissal, arguing that Downtown Auto Parks con- 
flicted with pertinent Supreme Court precedents. The Dis- 
trict Court subsequently dismissed the First Amended Com- 
plaint with prejudice. 

On appeal, Petitioners asked the Seventh Circuit to re- 
consider its broad denial of First Amendment rights to inde- 
pendent contractors in light of the principles enunciated in 
Rutan v. Republican Party of Illinois, 497 U.S. 62 (1990), 
and others of this Court’s cases. The Seventh Circuit noted 
that it already had considered the impact of Rutan in Down- 
town Auto Parks, however, and refused to revisit the issue. 
Although the court had acknowledged in Downtown Auto 
Parks that “the scope of Rutan, and rationale behind it, seem 
to be at odds with the holding of” earlier Seventh Circuit de- 
cisions rejecting contractors’ political association rights as 
the basis for a claim, the court nevertheless clung to the rea- 
soning of its seminal case in the area of contract patronage, 
LaFalce v. Houston, 712 F.2d 292 (7th Cir. 1983), cert. de- 


nied, 416 U.S. 1044 (1984). The court concluded that “it 
should be up to the Supreme Court to extend Elrod [v. Burns, 
427 U.S. 347 (1976)].” O'Hare, P.A. Sa. Relying on LaFalce 
and subsequent cases, the Seventh Circuit rejected Petition- 
ers’ First Amendment claim and affirmed the dismissal. This 
Court granted certiorari on December 8, 1995. 


SUMMARY OF ARGUMENT 


This Court consistently has given paramount protection 
to the core First Amendment rights of political speech and 
association. Buckley v. Valeo, 424 U.S. 1, 14 (1976) (per 
curiam). No reason of law or policy justifies the blanket ban 
that the Seventh Circuit has imposed on the exercise of these 
rights by independent contractors. An individual who agrees 
to perform services for or at the request of government does 
not forfeit these rights. Nor may the government condition 
the receipt or retention of government benefits on the waiver 
of rights of speech and association. See Perry v. Sinderman, 
408 U.S. 593, 597 (1972). 

Any abridgement of these fundamental First Amendment 
freedoms is subject to the most exacting scrutiny of constitu- 
tional review. The burden is on government to establish that 
compelling interests justify its actions and that it has chosen 
means least restrictive of rights of association and speech. 
Buckley v. Valeo, 424 U.S. at 25, 45-46. 

Government's interest in the conduct of its independent 
contractors is limited to ensuring that the services are prop- 
erly and efficiently performed. Government generally lacks 


a sufficiently compelling reason to restrict the political affili- 
ation of such persons. Interests generally asserted in support 
of patronage practices do not justify terminating municipal 
business with an independent contractor because of a refusal 
to affiliate with an incumbent mayor during reelection. These 
interests serve no governmental purpose, except in the lim- 
ited circumstances where the contractor performs services for 
which political loyalty to the mayor would be “an appropri- 
ate requirement for the effective performance” of the services. 
Branti v. Finkel, 445 U.S. 507, 518 (1980). Petitioner towing 
company does not perform services requiring such political 
affiliation. 

Similarly, the related political speech of a contractor 
should not be penalized in the absence of compelling justifi- 
cation, such as disruption of the operational efficiency of gov- 
ernment. If, as in this case, the contractor performs its duties 
outside the government workplace, is not supervised by the 
government employer, cannot be said to speak for the gov- 


" e@mmment, and does not perform the duties of a high-level of- 


fice, there is no justification for restraints on political speech. 
See Waters v. Churchill, U.S. __, 114 S.Ct. 1878 (1994). 

The decision of the Court of Appeals conformed to no 
established standard of constitutional review or analysis. 
Under that court's precedents banning all rights of political 
association and speech for government contractors, Respon- 
dents were not required to and did not offer any justification 
for their actions. Distinguishing public employees whose 
nghts of political association have been protected by this Court 


from political discharge, the court below determined that the 
Petitioners were not as dependent on government as are em- 
ployees, and were therefore less likely to be vulnerable to 
forced political affiliation. The court then gave deference to 
the perpetuation of the institution of patronage, without ex- 
amining government's interest in retaliating against contrac - 
tors for their political associations. For these reasons, the 
Court of Appeals concluded that Petitioners were without le- 
gai recourse though they actually had been penalized for their 
refusal to associate and to make campaign contributions con- 
trary to their beliefs. Cf Elrod v. Burns, 427 U.S. 347, 359 
(1976) (dismissal for failure to support party “penalizes” the 
exercise of associational rights). The court's analytical frame- 
work failed to measure any of the interests required by any 
test under this Court's First Amendment junsprudence. 

In summary, no constitutional basis exists for creating a 
categorical exception to freedom of political association and 
speech for independent contractors. This Court should re- 
verse the decision of the Court of Appeals and remand the 
case to the District Court.

A. Petitioners’ Political Association With 
Paxson’s Opponent and Refusal to Associate 
Politically With Paxson are Protected. 

This Court long has recognized that the ability of indi- 
viduals to come together for collective political action is at 
the core of the First Amendment. “Our form of government 
is built on the premise that every citizen shall have the right 
to engage in political expression and association.” Sweezy v. 
New Hampshire, 354 U.S. 234, 250 (1957) (plurality). 

Political associational behavior frequently is intertwined 
with the exercise of many other forms of expression, such as 
freedom of speech, the right to petition for redress, and vot- 
ing for the candidate of one’s choice. Without protection for 
associational rights, these other civil liberties would be im- 
periled. Citizens Against Rent Control/Coalition for Fair 
Housing v. City of Berkeley, Cal., 454 U.S. 290, 294 (1981): 
Shelton v. Tucker, 364 U.S. 479, 486 (1960); NAACP v. State 
of Alabama ex rel. Patterson, 357 U.S. 449, 460-61 (1958). 

This Court's frequent decisions protecting associational 
activities recognize the right of political association as “fun- 
damental.” Eg. Illinois State Bd. of Elections v. Socialist 
Workers Party, 440 U.S. 173, 184 (1979) (political beliefs 
and voting); Buckley v. Valeo, 424 US. 1, 25 (1976) (per 
curiam) (campaign contributions and expenditures). See also 
Cousins v. Wigoda, 419 U.S. 477, 482-83 (1975) (right of 
party to control delegate selection). The right to associate 
with a political party is an “integral part” of the freedom of 
association. Kusper v. Pontikes, 414 U.S. $1, 56-57 (1973). 


12 


The elevated level of constitutional protection provided 
to the right of political association was a key aspect of the 
Court's analysis in Elrod v. Burns, 427 U.S. 347 (1976) (plu- 
rality). That case was initiated by Republican employees of 
the Cook County Sheriff's Department shortly after the elec- 
tion of a Democratic sheriff who threatened to terminate em- 
ployees refusing to affiliate with the Democratic Party. This 
Court described the toll that patronage takes on First Amend- 
ment freedoms. Employees must work for party policies in 
which they do not believe, and against policies they support. 
In addition, they are forced to make financial contributions 
that are “tantamount to coerced belief.” Jd. at 355. To retain 
their jobs, the Elrod workers were required to “pledge their 
political allegiance to the Democratic Party.” /d. at 355. “The 
threat of dismissal for failure to provide ... support unques- 
tionably inhibits protected belief and association, and dis- 
missal for failure to support only penalizes its exercise.” /d. 
at 359. The pressure to affiliate with the party in power con- 
strained the ability of employees to associate with others of 
like political persuasion, thus directly abridging their free- 
dom of political association. Jd. at 355-57. The interests 
proffered by the Sheriff were not sufficiently compelling to 
justify this broad restraint. Jd. at 369-73. 

Since Elrod, this Court has continued to afford the stron- 
gest protection to the right of political association. For ex-

B. Petitioners’ Political Speech is Protected. 


1. Petitioners were sanctioned under a 
practice of viewpoint suppression. 

Mr. Gratzianna displayed campaign posters at his place 
of business as a way of openly demonstrating support for his 
preferred candidate. Such a display on one’s own private prop- 
erty is a time-honored form of political speech. Like a resi- 
dential sign, a campaign poster is an “unusually cheap and 
convenient form of communication.” City of Ladue v. Gilleo, 
_._ U.S. _., 114 S.Ct. 2038, 2046 (1994). Posters and yard 
signs promoting one’s candidate are routinely displayed on 
supporters’ private property during election campaigns. 

Petitioners were sanctioned because their sign endorsed 
Paxson’s opponent; thus, the penalty was based on viewpoint. 

The effect of Respondents’ actions is “to penalize [Petition- 
ers] for such speech. Its deterrent effect is the same as if the 
[government] were to fine them for this speech.” Speiser v. 
Randall, 357 U.S. 513, 518 (1958). 

This Court’s most recent free speech cases resoundingly 
denounce systems of viewpoint suppression. For example, 
while regulations imposing a burden on the basis of content 
are “presumptively unconstitutional,” Simon & Schuster, Inc. 
v. Members of New York State Victims Bd., 502 U.S. 105, 108 
(1991), “the violation of the First Amendment is all the more 
blatant” when speech is targeted because of the speaker’s 
views. Rosenberger v. Rector & Visitors, U.S. ___, 115 S. 
Ct. 2510, 2516 (1995) (citing R.A. V. v. City of St. Paul, Minn., 
505 U.S. 377, 391 (1992)). “The government must abstain 


16 


from regulating speech when the specific motivating ideol- 
ogy or the opinion or the perspective of the speaker is the 
rationale for the restriction.” Rosenberger, 115 S. Ct. at 2516 
(citing Perry Ed. Ass'n v. Perry Local Educators’ Ass'n, 460 
U.S. 37 (1983)). This Court’s condemnation of systems of 
viewpoint suppression could not be more forceful. 

The City of Northlake’s conduct embodies the essence of 
viewpoint suppression. Government benefits are bestowed 
on those who express support for the incumbent mayor, while 
those who express the contrary view have those benefits uni- 
laterally withdrawn. In penalizing O'Hare for its owner's 
political speech and association, the City of Northlake has 
done precisely what this Court consistently has held the First 
Amendment to forbid. Like the university in Rosenberger 
that facilitated student speech except if it was religious, and 
like the state in Speiser v. Randall that conditioned property 
tax exemptions on a loyalty oath, the City extracts conform- 
ance to its orthodoxy by conditioning Petitioners’ contract on 
expressed loyalty to Mayor Paxson. 

In Elrod, the plurality noted that a system of punishment 
for refusal to embrace government-approved positions can 
result in a government-enforced orthodoxy of views. 427 
U.S. at 356 (relying upon West Virginia State Bd. of Educ. v. 
- Barnette, 319 U.S. 624 (1943)). See also Keyishian v. Bd. of 
Regents, 385 U.S. 589 (1967) (termination of teaching con- 
tract for refusal to sign loyalty oath); Baggett v. Bullitt, 377 
U.S. 360 (1964) (same); Speiser v. Randall, 357 U.S. 513 
(denial of property tax exemption for refusal to sign loyalty 


17 


oath). Cf. Rosenberger, 115 S. Ct. at 2524 (cautioning that 
government review of student writings for religious content 
risks requirement that writings must “meet some baseline stan- 
dard of secular orthodoxy”). Such an enforced orthodoxy is 
the anticipated result of the City’s practice here; all individu- 
als and companies who wish a business relationship with the 
City must demonstrate political support of the mayor’s re- 
election bid. 


2. Petitioners were sanctioned under a practice 
of forced speech. 

One of the vices of the political patronage practice held z 
unconstitutional in Elrod was that it forced workers, on pen- 
alty of termination, to work for causes that they did not per- 
sonally embrace. A long line of this Court’s cases teaches 
that government cannot compel individuals to espouse causes 
in which they do not believe. Riley v. National Fed’n of the 
3lind of N.C., 487 U.S. 781, 790-791 (1988); Wooley v. 
Maynard, 430 U.S. 705 (1977); Miami Herald Publishing 
Co. v. Tornillo, 418 U.S. 241 (1974); West Virginia State Bd. 
of Educ. v. Barnette, 319 U.S. 624 (1943). In Wooley v. 
Maynard, the Court explained the complementary nature of 
the right to speak and the right to refrain from speaking. “A 
system which secures the right to proselytize religious, po- 
litical, and ideological causes must also guarantee the con- 
comitant right to decline to foster such concepts.” 430 U.S. at 
714. 


Just last term, in McIntyre v. Ohio Elections Comm’ n, 115 
S. Ct. 1511, this Court struck a state statute requiring that 
campaign literature contain the name and address of persons 
publishing the literature. Rejecting arguments that the law 
should be analyzed under the less stringent tests used in cases 
governing the “mechanics of the voting process,” id. at 1518, 
this Court analyzed the regulation under traditional First 
Amendment standards, applying strict scrutiny to this abridg- 
ment of “core political speech.” Jd. at 1519. Just as the state 
could not compel the newspaper in Tornillo to print replies 
by candidates it had criticized, the state could not compel the 
leaflet’s author to identify herself. See Id. at 1520. 

Like the leaflets aimed at defeat of a proposed tax levy in 
Mcintyre, Mr. Gratzianna’s posters and public candidate sup- 
port, as Campaign speech by an interested voter, “occup[y] 
the core of the protection afforded by the First Amendment.” 
McIntyre, 115 S. Ct. at 1518. “The First Amendment affords 
the broadest protection to such political expression in order 
‘to assure [the] unfettered interchange of ideas for the bring- 
ing about of political and social changes desired by the 
people.’” Jd. at 1518-19 (citing Roth v. United States, 354 
U.S. 476, 484 (1957) and Buckley v. Valeo, 424 U.S. 1, 14-15 
(1976)). Finally, as in McIntyre, Mr. Gratzianna’s advocacy 
took place in the course of the electoral process, thereby 
strengthening the First Amendment protection afforded it. Jd. 

While Mrs. McIntyre was subject to a civil penalty of $100 
for her anonymous leaflets, Respondents exacted a far greater 
penalty from Petitioners for displaying campaign posters: 


19 


withdrawal of city business. If anything, the system at work 
in this case is more offensive to constitutional values than the 
one at work in Mcintyre: unlike that forced speech required 
by statute, this one is viewpoint-based, unwritten, and im- 
posed at the will of the City’s executive. 


C. Petitioners’ Refusal To Make Campaign Contri- 
butions To A Candidate They Oppose Is Protected. 


Petitioners’ choice of whether to support a political can- 
didate with a financial contribution, and which candidate to 
support, also enjoys the highest level of constitutional pro- 
tection. Limitations on political contributions and expendi- 
tures directly implicate fundamental First Amendment inter- 
ests. Buckley v. Valeo, 424 U.S. at 23. In addition to serving 
as a general expression of support for a candidate or issue, 
“|mJaking a contribution, like joining a political party, serves 
to affiliate a person with a candidate. In addition, it enables 
like-minded persons to pool their resources in furtherance of 
common political goals.” Jd. at 22. Political spending is “at 
the heart of the First Amendment’s protection,” First Nat'l 
Bank of Boston v. Bellotti, 435 U.S. 765, 776 (1978). Thus, 
restrictions on such spending or retaliation for political con- 
tributions operate as “a prohibition directed at speech itself.” 
Id. at 786. 

The Elrod plurality also singled out forced contributions 
for special criticism. “The financial and campaign assistance 
that [an employee] is induced to provide to another party fur- 
thers the advancement of that party’s policies to the detri- 
ment of his party’s views and ultimately his own beliefs, and 


20 


any assessment of his salary is tantamount to coerced belief.” 
Elrod, 426 U.S. at 355 (citing Buckley v. Valeo, 424 U.S. at 
19). This Court has continued, since Elrod, to actively guard 
against government-imposed systems of forced financial con- 
tributions for political causes. 

Abood v. Detroit Bd. of Educ., 431 U.S. 209 (1977), in- 
volved a challenge to a requirement that public employees 
pay union dues that included union expenditures for political 
causes. The Court’s analysis was based on the same prin- 
ciples that formed its holding in Elrod. Because public em- 
ployment cannot be conditioned on a waiver of constitutional 
rights and because compelling financial support for ideology 
coerces belief, the inclusion of funds for political causes in 
the dues assessment contravened the First Amendment. “For 
at the heart of the First Amendment is the notion that an indi- 
vidual should be free to believe as he will, and that in a free 
society one’s beliefs should be shaped by his mind and his 
conscience rather than being coerced by the State.” Abood, 
431 U.S. at 234-35 (citing Elrod, 427 U.S. at 356-57). 

That coercive financing of political causes abridges First 
Amendment freedoms has continuing vitality in this Court's 
jurisprudence. In Keller v. State Bar of California, 496 U.S. 
1 (1990), Chief Justice Rehnquist, writing for a unanimous 
Court, relied on Abood to strike a system of forced payments 
to a bar association to finance political and ideological ac- 
tivities. Jd. at 9-11. See also Rosenberger v. Rector & Visi- 
tors, __ U.S. __, 115 S. Ct. 2510, 2527 (1995) (O'Connor, J., 
concurring) (noting that the student fee at issue in that case 


21 


would be susceptible to challenge under Keller and Abood if 
students were compelled to support speech with which they 
disagreed). 

The City invoked government power to require a contri- 
bution to Paxon's political campaign as an implicit, but es- 
sential, condition of remaining on the rotation list. The tacit 
conditioning of a benefit on the waiver of First Amendment 
rights is no different from the termination of employment for 
speech and associational act'vities condemned in Perry. When 
the Petitioners refused to contribute, the City penalized that 
refusal with its most potent sanction: termination of City 
business. Just as surely as the First Amendment was violated 
by the forced contribution systems in Elrod, Abood and Keller, 
it is violated here. 

D. A Blanket Ban On The First Amendment Rights 

Of Government Contractors Cannot Be Squared 
With This Court’s Precedents. 

The Seveiith Circuit's categorical denial of First Amend-

reconciled with the case law discussed above. This Court 
has consistently rejected the notion that certain classes of 
speakers are simply outside the First Amendment. As the 
Court has pointed out, “[t}he inherent worth of the speech in 
terms of its capacity for informing the public does not de- 
pend upon the identity of its source, whether corporation, as- 
sociation, union, or individual.” First Nat'l Bank of Boston v. 
Bellotti, 435 U.S. 765, 777 (1978). “The Government's power 
to impose content-based financial disincentives on speech

& Schuster, Inc. v. New York Crime Victims Bd., U.S._., 
112 S.Ct. 501, 509 (1991). Similarly, efforts by government 
bodies to burden fundamental First Amendment rights be- 
cause of a person's occupation have been firmly rejected. See 
Simon & Schuster, Inc., 112 S.Ct. 501 (convicted authors); 
McDaniel v. Paty, 435 U.S. 618 (1978)(clergy); Abood v. 
Detroit Bd. of Educ., 431 U.S. 209 (1977)(non-union work-

ployee. Lefkowitz v. Turley, 414 U.S. 70, 83 (1973). “A sig- 
nificant infringement of constitutional rights cannot be justi- 
fied by the speculative ability of those affected to cover the 
damage.” /d. at 84. 

The second LaFalce factor of creating litigation also is of 
no legal consequence. The mere fact that affording constitu- 
tional protection for the political affiliation decisions of pri- 
vate invividuals might cause litigation is not a reason to deny 
protection to those decisions. Acceptance of the Appellate 
Court’s reasoning would mean that any fundamental freedom 
could be abridged simply to advance an interest of inhibiting 
litigation. 

Finally, the Court’s deference here to the institution of 
patronage without evaluating the specific interest of the City 
in Carrying out its political retaliation against a contractor is 
simply inexplicable. As in LaFalce, the court assumed that 
all patronage practices not already declared unconstitutional 
by this Court were simply to be allowed to continue and not 
subject to examination by the lower federal courts. 

While petitioners submit that the City’s conduct was sub- 
ject to the highest level of constitutional scrutiny, under any 
standard of review, the court was required to examine the 
strength and necessity of the City’s interest in requiring po- 
litical affiliation with, and financial support for, the Mayor

by towing companies. It also was required to evaluate the 
City’s interest in limiting the overt political speech of these 
businesses outside the confines of government offices. Rather 
than evaluating any of these interests, the Court relied en- 
tirely upon surmise about the potential impact of contract 
patronage in the abstract and deferred to the characterization 
of the City’s political retaliation as being patronage. 

III. GOVERNMENT INTERESTS IN PATRONAGE 
DO NOT JUSTIFY THE DENIAL OF FIRST 
AMENDMENT RIGHTS TO ALL WHO DO BUSI- 
NESS WITH GOVERNMENT. 

In this case, the Seventh Circuit gave great deference to 
the institution of patronage without ever analyzing 
government's need for a contract patronage system unbounded 
by the First Amendment. P.A. 5a. Thus, Respondents be- 
came the beneficiaries of the Seventh Circuit's per se rule 
that independent contractors may not challenge the denial of 
their associational rights. As a consequence Respondents were 
never required to and did not offer any specific justification 
for terminating O’ Hare from city towing business because of 
Petitioners’ political association. Petitioners submit that Re- 
spondents should have been required to establish the com- 
pelling interests which justify patronage practices and that 
the courts below similarly should have evaluated the suffi- 
ciency of those interests. Below, Petitioners review the “gov- 
ernment interests generally asserted in support of patronage,” 
Rutan, 497 U.S. at 69, and submit that those interests have 
even less force when applied to independent contractors.

A. Effective And Efficient Government. 


Effective and efficient government is, of course, a sig- 
nificant interest. Rutan, 497 U.S. at 69. But Elrod and Rutan 
rejected the notion that employees who belong to a presently 
out-of-power party will subvert an incumbent’s administra- 
tion, thus fostering ineffective or inefficient government. 
Elrod, 427 U.S. at 365-68; Rutan, 497 U.S. at 70. Similarly, 
those cases rejected the argument that patronage serves effi- 
ciency because employees are motivated to make sure of the 
continuing incumbency of the party in power. Elrod, 427 
U.S. at 365-68; Rutan, 497 U.S. at 70. The ability to dis- 
charge ineffective or subversive employees, and the avail- 
ability of merit systems, adequately promote efficiency and 
effective government without infringing on associational 
rights. Elrod, 427 U.S. at id; Rutan, 497 U.S. at id. 

This conclusion is equally true for non-employees who 
provide services to government. In this case, the City ran a 
merit-based program for many years. If a towing company 
was not performing adequately, in contrast to the facts here, 
P.A. 22a, 4 9, 10; 24a, { 21, the City was free to terminate 
the company from the rotation list on that basis. For that 
matter, it could have terminated O’ Hare for any reason other 
than one that is unconstitutional. Perry v. Sindermann, 408 
U.S. 593 (1972) 

The City also was free, subject to conditions already im- 
posed by State law on towing companies, to provide, through 
contract, specific duties and obligations on towing compa- 
nies that it utilized for services. If the towing company failed

to comply with the terms of the contract, it would be subject 
to termination and the consequences of its breach as it would 
be in its dealings with private contractual partners. There is 
no bar to government enforcing its contracts.’

U.S.C. § 874, and the Civil False Claims Act, 31 U.S.C. § 
3730. Illinois has established similar penalties for defective 
performance of government contracts, providing for refunds

B. The Need For Politically Loyal Employees 


The second interest identified in Elrod and Rutan -- the 
need for politically loyal employees -- also is unavailing when 
generally applied to government contractors. In Elrod, this 
Court held that, by limiting patronage requirements to 
policymaking positions, a governmental entity could ad- 
equately protect its need for employee loyalty. Elrod, 427 
U.S. at 367; see also id. at 376-77 (Stewart, J. concurring) 
(limiting patronage to “policymaking and confidential” em- 
ployees). This Court subsequently refined this exception in 
Branti. The test is “not whether the label ‘policy maker’ or 
‘confidential’ fits a particular position; rather, the question is 
whether the hiring authority can demonstrate that party affili- 


36 


ation is an appropriate requirement for the effective perfor- 
mance of the public office involved.” Branti, 445 U.S. at 
518; see Rutan, 497 U.S. at 74. 

The same should be true for services provided by inde- 
pendent contractors and other non-employees. For example, 
the City of Northlake might contract with a public relations 
firm to write press releases or with an attorney for advice on 
legal matters. Such positions may well demand political loy- 
alty. See Brown v. Trench, 787 F.2d 167 (3d Cir. 1986) (As- 
sistant Director of Public Information, writing press releases 
and speeches and presenting county's views to press and pub- 
lic, could be terminated consistent with Branti), Ness v. 
Marshall, 660 F.2d 517 (3d Cir. 1981) (same for city solicitor 
who served as chief law officer of city, represented city in 
legal actions and rendered legal opinions to city and its offi- 
cials). 

A functional analysis, however, shows that Petitioners ar- 
not in a position requiring political loyalty to the elected mayor. 
They do not determine the circumstances under which a tow 
truck will be called, nor do they even determine whether a 
tow truck should be called in any given instance. A tow truck 
on the rotation list either is available to respond to the police 
request to tow a stranded automobile or it is not. It either 
accepts the job or it rejects it. Petitioners’ conduct is regu- 
lated to a significant extent by state law. See supra at n.9. 
They could not possibly be said to be in a position requiring 
political allegiance to the mayor. In any event, such a factual 
determination would more properly be made at trial after re- 


37 


mand. Thus, the interest in political loyalty cannot justify a 
blanket abrogation of the First Amendment rights of all inde- 
pendent contractors. 


C. Preserving The Democratic Process 


The final interest advanced in Elrod was that of preserv- 
ing the democratic process, an argument premised on the idea 
that partisan politics is central to that process. 427 U.S. at 
368. This Court questioned whether eliminating patronage 
would in fact hamper the democratic process since political 
parties existed prior to extensive patronage practices and con- 
tinue despite the decline of patronage. The Court noted that 
the process functions as well as or better without patronage 
practices, for these “clearly retard” the democratic process 
by entrenching incumbent parties and by impeding free speech 
and association. /d. at 369-70. Patronage thus is not the least 
restrictive alternative of achieving whatever such practices 
do contribute. Jd. at 368-69; see also Rutan, 497 U.S. at 74- 
75. 

The practice of contract patronage raises no different con- 
siderations. By being forced to affiliate politically as a con- 
dition of doing business, contractors, like government em- 
ployees, are given a choice: affiliate with a purty or candi- 
date whose position one may wish to reject, or abandon the 
possibility of doing business with government. As with em- 
ployees, such a practice skews the democratic process by pro- 
viding incentive to affiliate with a particular party -- the party 
perceived as the most likely to win and thus distribute pa- 


38 


tronage spoils -- regardless of one’s actual beliefs. 

Moreover, patronage contracting does not advance party 
discipline and party strength, whatever one may believe about 
the effects of patronage employment. See Rutan, 498 U.S. at 
103-04 (Scalia, J., dissenting). At the local level, where con- 
tractors may be the most vulnerable to contract patronage, 
the practice is more likely to compel “candidate” affiliation 
rather than “party” affiliation. Under Illinois law, the mayor 
of Northlake is chosen in a nonpartisan election'® and the Pe- 
titioners were asked to contribute to Respondent Paxson’s 
campaign, not to a particular political party. See Buckley v. 
Valeo, 424 U.S. at 22 (giving money affiliates one with a can- 
didate). Thus the interests of “party” are secondary, if present 
at all.

contrary provides resources for purchases of professional ser-

Finally, contract patronage advances only incumbent party 
strength, just as the incumbency of Respondent Mayor was 
served by requiring political affiliation as a condition of do- 
ing business with the City. See Rutan 497 U.S. at 87 n.4 
(Stevens, J., concurring) (patronage use to protect entrenched 
majority). While Justice Scalia connects party strength to an 
interest in the two-party system, Rutan, 497 U.S. at 106 
(Scalia, J. dissenting), even if patronage did advance more 
than incumbency, it is questionable whether government has 
a compelling interest in perpetuating a two-party system. In 
Anderson v. Celebrezze, 460 U.S. 780 (1983), this Court struck 
an early-filing deadline for candidates that was purportedly 
justified, in part, by “political stability.” This Court observed 
that the interest “amounts to a desire to protect existing po- 
litical parties from competition.” /d. at 801. 

As in Anderson, this Court should reject the theory that 
“a political party could invoke the powers of the State to as- 
sure monolithic control over its own members and support- 
ers.” Id. at 803. That is precisely what has happened here. 
Moreover, contract patronage further entrenches incumbents 
at the expense of new candidates by siphoning off funds that 
might otherwise be donated to newcomers. Cf. Norman. v. 
Reed, 502 U.S. 279 (1992); Illinois State Bd. v. Socialist Work- 
ers Party, 440 U.S. 173 (1979). Any interest that may be 
asserted in requiring the political affiliation and support of 
Mayor Paxson by independent contractors such as Petition- 
ers is nothing more than an interest in maintaining Mayor

Paxson’s control over the City. 

In sum, the interests traditionally asserted to justify pa- 
tronage are woefully inadequate to legitimize the City's prac- 
tice of political retaliation. It neither serves government's 
operational efficiency nor contributes meaningfully to the 
democratic process. 


CONCLUSION 


For these reasons, the judgment of the Court of Appeals 
for the Seventh Circuit should be reversed. The case should 
be remanded to the United States District Court for the North-
