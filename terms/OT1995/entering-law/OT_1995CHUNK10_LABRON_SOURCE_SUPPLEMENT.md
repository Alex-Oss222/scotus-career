## Labron facts and source dispute

Police observed street-level transactions on Auburn Street in Philadelphia, with drugs repeatedly retrieved from the trunk of a parked **1977 Lincoln**. They had conducted prior investigation and expected a delivery. They arrested the participants and searched the vehicle without a warrant. The trial court suppressed; the Superior Court reversed; the Pennsylvania Supreme Court reversed the Superior Court after concluding that an independent exigency had not been shown.

**The vehicle's condition is materially different from the packet's blanket assertion that both vehicles were operational.** In the suppression transcript reproduced at **Appendix 180**, the officer answers the judge's impoundment question: the Lincoln could not be started and was left there. The subsequent description says the visible exterior looked fairly decent, though not new. Labron's opposition p.6 expressly cites that testimony. This establishes failed starting when police tried to impound it; it does not by itself establish when police learned its condition, whether the defect was temporary, or what observations supported ready mobility at the moment of search. Do not silently convert either actual operability or apparent mobility into an uncontested fact. Do not infer that no additional source answers these points without examining the recovered record.

The opposition pp.2–12 says the lower court accepted evidence of six weeks of investigation and information about the particular Lincoln before the search, with multiple sources and prior observation; it disputes the Commonwealth's characterization of an uncorroborated tip awaiting corroboration. The Commonwealth's June reply pp.1–3 responds that the dispute principally concerns when probable cause arose and that prior opportunity to obtain a warrant is legally irrelevant under its automobile-exception position. These are opposed factual/legal characterizations. The trial court's actual findings remain the historical-fact source; neither party's assertion substitutes for them.

The Pennsylvania majority also discusses the Commonwealth's concession regarding the defendant's ability to challenge the search and its failure to present the separate privacy-interest objection in the appellate statement of issues. Justice Castille's state dissent disputes standing/privacy and the exigency result. Do not revive an alternative privacy objection as though the majority had decided it in the Commonwealth's favor; consult the preserved issue and proper scope before using it.

## Independent-state-ground sources

**Labron's opinion**, petition appendix 8–20, invokes both the Fourth Amendment and Pennsylvania Article I, §8, then discusses *Carroll*, *Chambers* and Pennsylvania automobile decisions. Its concluding formulation states that “this Commonwealth's jurisprudence” has required probable cause and exigent circumstances and discusses advance information/time for a warrant. It relies on *Commonwealth v. White* and earlier Pennsylvania opinions, some explaining federal law. The opinion's precise language and actual source relationships, rather than a generic state citation count, are necessary to the jurisdiction inquiry.

Labron's opposition pp.13–21 expressly invokes **adequate and independent Article I, §8 protection** under *Michigan v. Long* and *Herb v. Pitcairn*. It argues the opinion's state jurisprudence is independently sufficient and cites Pennsylvania's tradition of greater protection. The Commonwealth's petition pp.12–14 and June reply pp.4–6 argue the decision is federal or interwoven, lacks an independent plain statement, and relies on cases interpreting the Fourth Amendment; they also dispute whether a broader state rule was presented below. These remain litigant positions, not settled findings in this preparation.

**Kilgore's opinion**, petition appendix 1–8, rests its analysis on *Cockfield*, *Linde*, and *Labron*, repeatedly frames the issue in Fourth Amendment terms, and concludes that the truck search violated the Fourth Amendment. It finds ample support for probable cause but no separate exigency because officers had custody and could have guarded the truck while obtaining a warrant. The source words matter: the final express federal statement in Kilgore and the mixed discussion in Labron are not interchangeable.

The applicable surviving *Michigan v. Long*, 463 U.S.1032 (1983), asks whether a judgment fairly appears primarily to rest on federal law or be interwoven with it and whether an adequate independent state ground is clear. Actual simulated *Arizona v. Evans* preserves that inquiry and state authority to afford greater protection; actual simulated *Sochor* preserves express independent preservation grounds despite alternative federal merits discussion. These current rules are in the supplied law slice. Do not treat an adverse federal ruling as forbidding a properly available independent state disposition on remand, and do not manufacture a state disposition that the record does not establish.

## Kilgore location, access and scope facts

The state opinion at petition appendix 2–3 describes the defendant's former wife obtaining money for a controlled drug purchase, driving to her father's farm, and walking with the defendant toward a pickup truck in the driveway. The observing officer lost sight of them while turning his vehicle and saw them walking away when he returned. She then delivered cocaine. Officers searched her and did not recover the purchase money or the additional requested cocaine.

At the farm, an officer went to the back door, saw someone run from the kitchen, announced police presence and entered; he found and detained the defendant in the living room. **The father consented to a search of the premises**; the initial search did not find the additional cocaine or money. Police then searched **the defendant's truck at the rear of the residence without a warrant or the defendant's consent**, finding baggies in a McDonald's cup beneath napkins/wrappers on the passenger-side floor. The state opinion identifies later recovery of the purchase money after the defendant disclosed its location under the living-room carpet. The procedural character of custody before truck search and arrest after it should be preserved as described, not rewritten as a finding of lawful arrest sufficient to justify any search.

Kilgore's opposition pp.1–5 emphasizes private land under police control, officers available for a warrant, the lack of vehicle-owner consent, adjacent outbuildings, the observing officer's lost view, and the absence of visible contraband. It distinguishes sudden stops, plain view, consent and search incident to arrest. The state opinion found probable cause notwithstanding the imperfect observation; the opposition's factual qualifications do not erase that ruling. The record identifies a driveway/rear-farm location but this extraction does not establish the full layout, property interest, physical access route, enclosure, use, or the legal extent of any curtilage.

The father's consent to house/premises searching is not evidence of the defendant's consent to the truck. No later curtilage decision is date-eligible. The packet already distinguishes authority to search a vehicle from lawful access to where it sits; the fresh validator must decide what access claim is actually presented/preserved and what facts are material, using the recovered record. An unsupported affirmative access or curtilage finding is unavailable. No absence or forfeiture is inferred from a missing operator extract.
