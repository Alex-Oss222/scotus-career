QUESTION PRESENTED 


Whether the 5-4 decision of the court below misinterpreted 18 
U.S.C. § 924(c)(1) — which imposes a mandatory 5-year consecu- 
tive sentence on a person who “uses or carries” a firearm “during 
and in relation to” a drug-trafficking offense — by adopting a 
standard that looks solely to the “proximity and accessibility” of 
the firearm to the drugs, drug paraphernalia, or drug proceeds, 
rather than to active employment of the firearm or to other rel- 
evant factors such as the type of weapon involved, whether it was 
loaded and ready to fire, and the presence of expert testimony to 
support the government’s theory of “use.”

STATEMENT 

In separate trials growing out of unrelated incidents, petition- 
ers Roland J. Bailey and Candisha S. Robinson were each 
convicted of, among other things, using or carrying a firearm 
“during and in relation to” the crime of possession of cocaine with 
intent to distribute it, in violation of 18 U.S.C. § 924(c)(1). 
Separate panels of the D.C. Circuit affirmed Bailey’s conviction 
and reversed Robinson’s. The court granted rehearing en banc in 
both cases and, in a closely divided decision, affirmed both 
convictions. 


1. Bailey’s Arrest, Trial, and Panel Appeal. a. We describe 
the evidence in the light most favorable to the government. On the 
morning of May 2, 1988, two police officers patrolling in Wash- 
ington, D.C., stopped petitioner Bailey when they noticed that the 
car he was driving had no front license plate and no inspection 
sticker. J.A. 18. Officer Washington asked Bailey for his driver’s 
license. When Bailey handed him a District of Columbia identifi-

ington then searched the passenger compartment of the car and 
discovered one round of .380-caliber ammunition on the console 
and a satchel containing 30 grams of cocaine in 27 plastic bags. 
J.A. 18, 80; 2/27/90 Tr. 74-75, 93, 106, 125. 

After arresting Bailey, the officers unlocked the car trunk 
(which was inaccessible from the passenger compartment) and 
found a vest containing $2500 in cash. 2/27/90 Tr. 97-98, 117.' 
The police also found, beneath “a whole lot of items” including 
“clothing and bags and other things,” a bag containing a 9-mm. 
handgun that was “loaded with one clip” of ammunition containing 
six .380-caliber bullets. J.A. 6, 9; 2/27/90 Tr. 106. The police 
could not see the gun when they opened the trunk; indeed, the 
trunk was so full that it took roughly one minute to find the bag 
containing the gun. J.A. 9. Both officers testified that they did 
not see Bailey do anything other than drive the car. J.A. 5, 8.

SUMMARY OF ARGUMENT 


Section 924(c)(1) imposes mandatory, consecutive sentences 
(of at least five years and of up to life imprisonment ) on any 
person who “uses or carries” a firearm “during and in relation to” 
a crime of violence or a drug-trafficking offense. In these cases, 
the Court is called on to decide what category of conduct Congress 
singled out for this especially severe sanction and, conversely, 
what conduct with respect to firearms Congress intended to treat 
differently by punishing through the process of sentencing for the 
underlying drug-trafficking offense. 

I. “Use” under Section 924(c) means the active employment 
of a firearm to carry out the predicate offense. Such active (as 
opposed to passive or potential) employment includes firing, 
discharging, aiming, pointing, bludgeoning, tra‘ling, threatening 
with or brandishing, or displaying. It may also include referring 
to the gun if such action assists in the accomplishment of the 
predicate offense. “Use” normally requires that the defendant 
handle the firearm or be aware of use of the weapon by others

Two years ago, in Smith v. United States, 113 S. Ct. 2050, 
2058 (1993), the Court held that a defendant who attempted to ob- 
tain a large quantity of cocaine from a pawnshop owner by dis- 
playing and offering to trade a machine gun for the drugs was 
properly convicted of “using” that firearm “during and in relation 
to” the predicate offenses of attempt (and conspiracy) to possess 
cocaine with intent to distribute it. The Court rejected the nar- 
rower interpretation that the statute requires “use as a weapon” and 
determined that a firearm is “used” if a defendant “employ(s]” or 
“derive[s] service from” the firearm. Although the Court noted 
that the definition of “use” is “expansive[ ]” (id. at 2058), it 
nevertheless cautioned that the term is “not without limits.” Jd. at 
2054 (citing United States v. Long, 905 F.2d 1572, 1576-1577 
(D.C. Cir.) (Thomas, J.), cert. denied, 498 U.S. 948 (1990)). 
The present cases afford the Court an opportunity to give meaning 
to the limits that circumscribe Section 924(c)(1)’s scope and halt 
the lower courts’ continuing efforts to rewrite the statute by

I. A DEFENDANT “USES” A FIREARM “DURING AND 
IN RELATION TO” A DRUG-TRAFFICKING OFFENSE 
BY ACTIVELY EMPLOYING THE WEAPON TO 
CARRY OUT THE PREDICATE OFFENSE 
The ordinary meaning of Section 924(c)(1)’s operative lan- 

guage aud the language and structure of Section 924 as a whole 

show that Congress intended to impose mandatory sentences only 
on those who actively employ a firearm to carry out the predicate 
offense. Such active employment includes firing or discharging, 
aiming, pointing, bludgeoning, trading, threatening with, brandish- 
ing, or displaying. It can also include referring to the weapon. 

To the extent there is any ambiguity about the meaning of the 

Statute, it is completely dispelled by the legislative history of 

Section 924(c)(1). Because there is no evidence that Bailey or 

Robinson ever “used” a firearm within the meaning of Section 

924(c)\(1), their convictions should be reversed. Even if other 

constructions of the term “use” are possible, the rule of lenity calls 

for this reading.

C. The Origins And Legislative History Of Section 924(c)(1) 
Demonstrate Congress’s Intent To Penalize Only Active 
Employment Of A Firearm To Commit The Predicate 
Offense 


Because the language and structure of Section 924 demonstrate 
that “use” of a firearm “during and in relation to” a “crime of vio- 
lence or drug trafficking crime” covers only the active employment 
of the weapon to accomplish the underlying offense, there is no 
need to consult the legislative history of the provision. If the 
Court concludes, however, that Section 924(c)(1)’s meaning is am- 
biguous, then we submit that such ambiguity is dispelled by a care- 
ful examination of the statute’s origins and legislative history. 

Originally enacted as part of the Gun Control Act of 1968, 
Section 924(c) has been amended many times. Nevertheless, only 
three of those amendments (in 1984, 1986, and 1988) bear on the 
scope of liability for “us{ing] or carry{ing]” a firearm. As we 
next explain, Congress intended in passing the 1968 Act to limit 
“use” to active employment to carry out the predicate offense; and 
there is nothing in the legislative record to suggest (and much that 
refutes) any claim that the 1984, 1986, or 1988 amendments were 
intended to broaden the scope of “use” liability.

intended to include passive drug-trafficking crimes as predicate

4. The Anti-Drug Abuse Act of 1988. Congress broadened 
the definition of “drug trafficking crime” in 1988 to include “any 
felony punishable under the Controlled Substances Act (21 U.S.C. 
801 et seq.), the Controlled Substances Import and Export Act (21 
U.S.C. 951 et seq.), or the Maritime Drug Law Enforcement Act 
(46 U.S.C. App. 1901 et seq.).” Pub. L. No. 100-690, § 6212, 
102 Stat. 4181, 4360. Congress thus made clear for the first time 
that a “drug trafficking offense” includes not just the more active 
crimes of distribution, importation, and manufacture but also 
passive crimes such as possession with intent to distribute. We 
have been unable to find any evidence in the slight legislative 
history of the 1988 Act to suggest that Congress considered, even 
for a moment, how the “carry” or “use” provision would function 
when applied to a predicate possessory offense. 

In sum, Congress intended in passing the 1968 Act to limit 
“use” to active employment of a firearm to carry out the predicate 
offense; there is nothing to suggest that the 1984, 1986, or 1988 
amendments were intended to broaden the scope of “use” liability; 
and indeed there is voluminous evidence in the legislative record 
to support the conclusion that Congress continued to have in mind 
an active concept of “use.”

4. A Firearm Is Not Used In Relation To The Predicate

It is equally clear that a defendant may not be convicted of 
using a firearm on the theory that the firearm “emboldens” the 
defendant to commit the predicate offense. That theory is too 
broad. In many instances, it would criminalize the mere posses- 
sion of a firearm during the predicate offense, since such posses- 
sion frequently can be alleged to “embolden” the defendant. Simi- 
larly, the emboldening theory subsumes almost every case where 
the firearm is accessible to the defendant. As just explained, 
however, accessibility alone cannot properly be the basis for 
liability. And, insofar as a defendant who is carrying a firearm 
can always be described as “emboldened,” the theory also renders 
the carrying element superfluous. 

The emboldening theory is erroneous not only because it does 
not describe a “use” of the firearm, but also because the kind of 
relationship to the offense it describes is not one made criminal by 
Section 924(c). In Smith, this Court observed that the phrase “in 
relation to” is “expansive” and that, “at a minimum, [it] clari-

to the drug trafficking crime; its presence or involvement cannot 
be the result of accident or coincidence.” 113 S. Ct. at 2059.

B. The D.C. Circuit’s “Proximity And Accessibility” Test Is 
Inconsistent With The Foregoing Limitations And Is 
Otherwise Erroneous 
The majority below rejected the “open-ended test” previously 

used in the D.C. Circuit and “repiace[d] it with a test [for ‘use’] 

that looks to two factors only”: (1) the proximity of the gun “to 
the drugs, drug paraphernalia, or drug proceeds” involved in the 
underlying offense; and (2) “the accessibility of the gun to the 
defendant from the place where the drugs, drug paraphernalia, or 
drug proceeds are located.” J.A. 80, 101. One “uses” a gun 
within the meaning of Section 924(c)(1), the court held, by placing 
or keeping the gun in a location “from which one (or one’s agent) 
can gain access to it if and when needed to facilitate a drug 
crime.” J.A. 95 (emphasis added). The court held further that, 
when the predicate drug offense is a “continuing offense” such as 
possession with intent to distribute, the government need not show 
that the gun was in such a place at the time the defendant was ar-

This test suffers from numerous flaws and should be rejected. 
As a threshold matter, it is difficult to see how the D.C. Circuit’s 
“proximity” factor adds anything to the “accessibility” factor. As 
Judge Wald correctly noted in her dissent, “whenever the guns are 
located proximately to the drugs, they must also be potentially

stored.” J.A. 103. Thus, the majority’s test essentially collapses 
into a single factor: the firearm’s proximity — not to the defen- 
dant, but to the “drugs, drug paraphernalia, or drug proceeds.” 
J.A. 80, 101. Yet the statute plainly does not criminalize storing 
or keeping a firearm in the same place as money or ziplock bags 
or even drugs — it criminalizes contemporaneous use of the gun

102 (“Congress * * * created no statutory presumption that ‘use’

defendant, drugs, and drug proceeds. J.A. 95, 96. But that 
rationale is necessarily too broad, if in fact Section 924(c) does not 
reach mere possession, because it would apply “virtually whenever 
a gun is present near the scene of a drug crime.” McFadden, 13 
F.3d at 469 (Breyer, C.J., dissenting). Such a “possession for 
protection” theory sweeps all cases of mere possession within the 
ambit of “use” because all firearms — even those that are only 
possessed — have a potential utility. See ibid.; see also United 
States v. Sullivan, 919 F.2d 1403, 1432 & n.45 (10th Cir. 1990)

1. Accessibility Of Firearm To Defendant. A factor courts 
have used to distinguish between use and possession is “the 
accessibility of the gun to the defendant.” United States v. Morris, 
977 F.2d 617, 621 (D.C. Cir. 1992); Derr, 990 F.2d at 1338; 
United States v. McKinnell, 888 F.2d 669, 675 (10th Cir. 1989). 
If a gun is sufficiently accessible to a defendant, that factor lends 
some support to an inference that the gun provided protection for 
the drug offense or that the defendant derived some service from 
the gun. By itself, this factor is not sufficient to establish use, as 
explained above. See United States v. Conner, 972 F.2d 1172, 
1174 (10th Cir. 1992) (“[mJere access to a firearm is insufficient 
to sustain a conviction” under Section 924(c)). Accessibility 
coupled with other circumstances can support a conviction, but 
inaccessibility of a firearm strongly undermines a conviction. 

2. Proof Of A Drug Transaction. A second indicium of fire- 
arm “use” is proof of an earlier drug transaction at which the fire- 
arm was actively employed or available to protect the drug opera- 
tions. Morris, 977 F.2d at 622; Bruce, 939 F.2d at 1055; United 
States v. Fermin, 32 F.3d 674, 678 (2d Cir. 1994), cert. denied, 
115 S. Ct. 1145 (1995); United States v. Feliz-Cordero, 859 F.2d 
250, 254 (2d Cir. 1988); United States v. Theodoropoulos, 866 
F.2d 587, 597 (3d Cir. 1989). Evidence of such a transaction is 
particularly important when the predicate offense is possession 
with intent to distribute, because the transaction can bridge the gap 
between the active verb “use” and the “passive crime” of posses- 
sion. Bruce, 939 F.2d at 1055. Evidence of a previous transac- 
tion that the firearm facilitated would be circumstantial evidence 
that the firearm was “used” during the later offense as well as that 
the defendant had incorporated it into a drug-trafficking operation. 
United States v. Reyes, 930 F.2d 310, 313, 314 n.5 (3d Cir. 
1991). 


3. The Type, Condition, And Number Of Firearms Involved. 
Many courts of appeals have ruled that the number of firearms in- 
volved in the predicate drug-trafficking offense is relevant in deter- 


46 


mining whether the firearms were “used”; the more guns present, 
the stronger the inference of use. Compare, e.g., Bruce, 939 F.2d 
at 1056 (conviction reversed where only a single firearm was in- 
volved), and Feliz-Cordero, 859 F.2d at 254 (same), with Morris, 
977 F.2d at 622-623 (“arsenal” of weapons supported Section 
924(c) conviction), and United States v. Williams, 952 F.2d 418, 
421 (D.C. Cir.) (same), cert. denied 113 S. Ct. 148 (1992). Al- 
though the “use” of a single weapon violates the statute, a purely 
circumstantial showing of “use” is weakened by the fact that no 
other weapons were present. The type and condition of a gun may 
also be relevant to whether it was used by a defendant and in what 
fashion. For example, a very large weapon such as a rifle would 
be difficult to “use” in a fashion that requires concealment on 
one’s person. Similarly, if the weapon is not readily operable, its 
utility would be impaired. Thus, the specific features of the fire- 
arm in question are relevant to whether the gun was “used.” 


4. Whether The Firearm Was Loaded And Ready To Fire. 
Some courts of appeals have stated that an inference of “use” is 
strengthened when the firearm at issue is loaded. Morris, 977 
F.2d at 622; Derr, 990 F.2d at 1338. Others, by contrast, have 
ruled that whether a gun is loaded is irrelevant to whether it is 
used because even an unloaded gun increases the risk of violence, 
and because “it is very difficult for a person to determine whether 
a gun was loaded or not when the gun is pointed at that person.” 
United States v. Gutierrez-Silva, 983 F.2d 123, 124-125 (8th Cir. 
1993); see also, e.g., United States v. Willis, 6 F.3d 257, 265 (Sth 
Cir. 1993). Certainly, this factor is relevant in at least some 
cases, depending on the government’s theory of how the gun was 
“used” in a particular case. 

5. Expert Testimony. Finally, some courts have looked to 
whether expert testimony bolstered the government’s theory of 
“use” as advanced in a particular case. Morris, 977 F.2d at 622; 
Derr, 990 F.2d at 1338.% An expert’s testimony concerning a

No single criterion identified above has dispositive weight;

D. Once These Factors Are Considered, Both Petiticers’ 
Convictions Should Be Reversed 


The evidence presented at petitioners’ trials was insufficient 
to prove, beyond a reasonable doubt, that either Bailey or Robin- 
son “used” a firearm during the predicate offense of possession of 
cocaine with intent to distribute it. As an examination of the 
relevant factors makes clear, the evidence showed, at most, that 
each petitioner possessed, but did not use, a firearm. 

1. Bailey was stopped for a traffic offense. When the police 
searched the car’s interior, they found one round of .380-caliber 
ammunition on the console and a satchel containing 30 grams of

106, 125. After arresting Bailey, the officers unlocked the car 
trunk (which was inaccessible from the passenger compartment)

Tr. 106. The police officers could not see the gun when they 
opened the trunk; indeed, the trunk was so full that it took roughly 
one minute to find the bag containing the gun. J.A.9. Bailey also 
had approximately $700 on his person.
