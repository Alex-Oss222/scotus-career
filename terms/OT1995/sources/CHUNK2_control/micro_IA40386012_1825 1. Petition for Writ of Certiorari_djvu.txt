QUESTIONS PRESENTED 


1. Does the Second Amendment to the United States 
Constitution guarantee an individual right to keep and 
bear arms, or a collective right of states to maintain 
militias?

ized state militia in order to have standing to assert a

A. The Parties and The Underlying Action. 


Petitioner’s 42 U.S.C. Sec. 1983 complaint arises out 
of the unlawful interference by the Maryland State 
Police with her attempt to purchase a handgun. Respon- 
dents include three State Troopers and the Superinten- 
dent of State Police, all of whom are alleged to have 
acted in their official capacities under color of state law 
[App. 35a-36a]. They are being sued, however, only in 
their individual capacities [App. 36a]. 


Petitioner sought to purchase a handgun from a retailer 
in Prince George’s County, Maryland. She filled out an 
“Application To Purchase A Firearm” (Maryland State 
Police Form #77R) which was forwarded to the licensing 
division of the Maryland State Police! [App. 36a]. Peti- 
tioner properly completed her application and all infor- 
mation given by her was true and correct [App. 37a]. It 
is undisputed that petitioner was not legally disqualified 
from purchasing a handgun. However, in violation of 
state law, respondents disapproved her application solely

application to the Superintendent of State Police. By signing the 
application a prospective purchaser certifies that he or she is not 
disqualified from purchasing or possessing a handgun by any fac- 
tors set forth in art. 27, Sec. 442(f). The Superintendent is 
required to conduct an investigation into the application. Md. 
Ann. Code, art. 27, Sec. 442(g). For 7 days from the date the 
application is submitted a transfer of the handgun to the prospec- 
tive purchaser may not take place. If, after seven days, the dealer 
does not receive a notice of disapproval from the Superintendent 
a transfer may take place.

C. The Appeal. 


Petitioner noted an appeal to the United States Court 
of Appeals for the Fourth Circuit. The case was cap- 
tioned Love v. Pepersack, No. 94-1582. In a published 
opinion dated February 3, 1995, Love v. Pepersack, 47 
F.3d 120 (4th Cir. 1995), the Court of Appeals affirmed 
the dismissal of petitioner’s amended complaint by the 
District Court [App. 31a]. 


The Court of Appeals held that the Second Amend- 
ment “preserves a collective, rather than individual right” 
[App. 31a]. This holding runs contrary to several opinions 
of this Court. See Scott v. Sanford, 60 U.S. (19 How.) 
393, 15 L.Ed. 691 (1857); United States v. Cruikshank, 
92 U.S. 542, 23 L.Ed. 588 (1876); Presser v. Illinots, 116 
U.S. 252, 6 S.Ct. 580, 29 L.Ed. 615 (1886); Miller v. 
Texas, 153 U.S. 535, 14 S.Ct. 874, 38 L.Ed. 812 (1894); 
United States v. Miller, 307 U.S. 174, 59 S.Ct. 816, 83 
L.Ed. 1206 (1939). In each of these cases in which this 
Court discussed, or touched upon the Second Amend- 
ment, it never conditioned the right to keep and bear 


6 


arms upon affiliation with or service in an organized 
militia. Even today, this court still refers to the Second 
Amendment in the context of tndtvidual rights, such as 
the right of freedom of speech and freedom from unrea- 
sonable searches and seizures. See United States v. 
Verdugo-Urquidez, 494 U.S. 259, 265, 110 S.Ct. 1056, 
108 L.Ed.2d 222 (1990); Planned Parenthood v. Casey, 
—_— U.S. —_— , 112 S.Ct. 2791, 120 L.Ed.2d 674 (1992). 


The opinion of the Court of Appeals is also in conflict 
with the opinion of the United States Court of Appeals 
for the Ninth Circuit in Fresno Rifle And Pistol Club v. 
Van De Camp, 965 F.2d 723, 731 (9th Cir. 1992) affirm- 
ing the holding of a district court that the Second Amend- 
ment guarantees an individual right (although declining 
to hold it applicable to the States via the Fourteenth 
Amendment). 


The Court of Appeals also relied upon United States v. 
Crutkshank, 92 U.S. 542, 23 L.Ed. 588 (1876) and 
Presser v. Illinots, 116 U.S. 252, 6 S.Ct. 580, 29 L.Ed. 
615 (1886) as sole authority for the proposition that the 
Second Amendment is inapplicable to the states [App. 
30a]. In doing so, the Court of Appeals ignored almost a 
century of case law in which this Court has repudiated 
Crutkshank’s holding that none of the Bill of Rights are 
applicable to the states. 


The Court of Appeals stated as fact things that are not 
apparent from the record in order to excuse respondents’ 
unlawful disapproval of petitioner’s application to pur- 
chase a firearm. In particular, the Court stated that ‘‘[the 
licensing division] was strapped for time’’ [App. 26a]. 
The Court also stated as fact that ‘‘[respondents] were 
forced by extreme time constraints to streamline investi- 
gations” [App. 29a]. Neither of these facts upon which 
the Court so heavily relied are apparent from the record. 


7 


That the respondents were ‘‘strapped for time’’ was never 
even suggested by the respondents in their brief. 


The Court of Appeals also treated this case as if it 
were one alleging unintentional conduct, although the 
amended complaint clearly alleges that respondents all 
acted willfully and maliciously. The Court held that a 
violation of substantive due process occurs: 


only where the government’s actions in depriving a 
person of life, liberty, or property are so unjust that 
no amount of fair procedure can rectify them [App. 


29a]. 


This is an appropriate standard in a case involving 
unintentional conduct. Daniels v. Williams, 474 U.S. 327, 
331, 106 S.Ct. 662, 665, 88 L.Ed. 2d 662 (1986). How- 
ever, petitioner’s amended complaint clearly alleges that 
respondents’ conduct was deliberate and respondents 
acted with knowledge that there was no lawful basis for 
disapproval of petitioner’s application [App. 38a, 41a, 
45a]. 


The Court of Appeals ignored an established standard 
of review in evaluating a Rule 12(b)(6) motion. That is 
whether the complaint, accepting the allegations as true, 
allows a recovery. Waterford Citizens Ass’n. v. Reilly, 
970 F.2d 1287, 1290 (4th Cir. 1992).

REASONS FOR GRANTING THE PETITION 


IN HOLDING THAT THE SECOND AMENDMENT “PRE- 
SERVES A COLLECTIVE, RATHER THAN AN INDI- 
VIDUAL RIGHT” THE COURT OF APPEALS DECIDED 
A FEDERAL QUESTION IN A WAY THAT CONFLICTS 
WITH APPLICABLE DECISIONS OF THIS COURT. 


The Court of Appeals reaffirmed its earlier decision in 
United States v. Johnson, 497 F.2d 548 (4th Cir. 1974) 
that the “Second Amendment preserves a collective, 
rather than an individual right’”’ [App. 31a]. It also sug- 
gested that petitioner, in order to prevail on any Second 
Amendment claim, would have to identify “how her 
possession of a handgun will preserve or insure the effec- 
tiveness of the militia” [App. 3la]. In holding that the 
Second Amendment preserves only a collective right (and 
suggesting that affiliation with a militia is required to 
assert a Second Amendment claim) the Court of Appeals 
decided a federal question in a way that conflicts with 
applicable decisions of this Court. On those occasions 
where it has discussed the right to keep and bear arms, 
this Court has always treated it as an individual right. 
Furthermore, this Court has never conditioned the right 
to keep and bear arms upon affiliation with an organized 
state militia. 


In Scott v. Sanford, 60 U.S. (19 How.) 393, 15 L.Ed. 
691 (1857) this Court addressed the question of whether 
persons of African descent could be citizens. It held that 
they could not in an opinion written by Chief Justice 
Taney, a Southerner from Maryland (where slavery was 
both legal and widely practiced). In 1857, Southerners 
were not keen on the idea of freeing the slaves, much less 
bestowing upon them the rights of citizens. The concerns 
of his fellow Southerners were not lost upon Chief 


9 


Justice Taney, who wrote that if blacks were recognized 
as citizens in any State of the Union, they would have 
the right to travel freely, engage in free speech, hold 
public meetings on political issues, and ‘‘keep and carry 
arms wherever they went.” 60 U.S. at 416-17. If the 
Second Amendment guaranteed only the collective right 
of states to maintain militias, there would have been no 
reason for Taney to fear that black citizens could ‘“‘keep 
and carry arms”’ since citizenship does not automatically 
entail service in an organized state militia. What con- 
cerned Chief Justice Taney was the rights blacks would 
enjoy as citizens. This Court’s opinion in Scott v. Sanford 
also listed the right to keep and bear arms with other 
rights which the Court has held to be of an individual 
nature, such as freedom of religion, free speech, freedom 
of the press, peaceable assembly, trial by jury, and the 
right against self-incrimination. 60 U.S. at 450. 


In United States v. Cruikshank, 92 U.S. 542, 23 L.Ed. 
588 (1876) this Court effectively repealed the Due Pro- 
cess Clause of the Fourteenth Amendment by holding 
that the Second Amendment, like the rest of the Bill of 
Rights, only operated as a restraint upon the federal 
government. In discussing the right to ‘‘bear arms for a 
lawful purpose”’ this Court stated that ‘“‘the people” 
should look to localities (not the National Government) 
for protection against “their fellow citizens’ of rights 
recognized by the Second Amendment. 92 U.S. at 553. 
Crutkshank affirmed an order arresting judgments of con- 
viction for conspiracy to deprive freed blacks in Louisiana 
of their civil rights. The fact that the black citizens who 
were victimized were not affiliated with an organized 
state militia had no bearing on the case. This Court in 
Crutkshank also used the phrase “the people” in the same 
context as “their fellow citizens.” 92 U.S. at 553. It 


10 


obviously viewed “‘the people”’ referred to in the Second 
Amendment as meaning individuals. States, unlike indi- 
viduals, do not have ‘“‘fellow citizens.” 


In Presser v. Illinots, 116 U.S. 252, 6 S.Ct. 580, 29 
L.Ed. 615 (1886) this Court upheld the conviction of a 
man charged with parading a body of armed men through 
Chicago without a license. Illinois law prohibited bodies 
of men to associate together as military organizations or 
to drill or parade in cities and towns unless authorized 
by law. Presser challenged this law as violating the Second 
Amendment. The Court held that these provisions did 
not infringe the right of the people to keep and bear 
arms. Citing Crutkshank, Presser also held that the Sec- 
ond Amendment is a limitation only upon Congress and 
the federal government. Presser did, however, hold that 
all citizens capable of bearing arms constitute the reserve 
military force or reserve militia of the United States and 
of the States, and that even without the Second Amend- 
ment states may not prohibit people from keeping and 
bearing arms “so as to deprive the United States of their 
rightful resource for maintaining public security.” 116 
U.S. at 265, 6 S.Ct. at 584. Presser thus drew a clear 
distinction between “the people” and “States.” As in 
Scott v. Sanford and Crutkshank, this Court in Presser 
never conditioned the right to keep and bear arms upon 
affiliation with an organized state militia. If the Second 
Amendment guaranteed only a collective right, this 
Court could easily have disposed of Presser’s Second 
Amendment claim by holding that he lacked standing to 
assert it. Instead, this Court addressed Presser’s Second 
Amendment claim on tts merits. 


In Miller v. Texas, 135 U.S. 535, 14 S.Ct. 874, 38 
L.Ed. 812 (1894) this Court declined to rule on the 
appellant’s Second Amendment claim due to his failure 


11 


to raise it in a timely fashion, stating, ‘‘[i]f the Four- 
teenth Amendment limited the power of the States as 
to such rights, as pertaining to citizens of the United 
States, we think it was fatal to this claim that it was not 
set up in the trial court.” 153 U.S. at 538. Miller had

THE COURT: Court is prepared to rule. 

This is an action for relief under Article ___, Title 42, 
Section 1983 of the United States Code. It is brought 
by plaintiff, April Love, against various state employees 
connected with the Maryland State Police. 

In summary, it is a claim for money damages to repre- 
sent, to redress the deprivation of what plaintiff claims 
were rights guaranteed her under the federal Constitution 
consistent with the language of 42 U.S.C., Section 1983, 
and it arises out of the efforts of Ms. Love to obtain, in 
1990, the right to purchase a firearm from a dealer in 
Maryland. 

The individuals involved are Robert G. Pepersack, 
Merrill A. Messick, Ernest Pletcher, and Elmer Tippett, 
Jr. The state of Maryland is also made a defendant in 
this case. 

Mr. Pepersack, at the time of the events in question, 
was supervisor of the firearms license section of the 
Maryland State Police. Mr. Messick was commander of 
the licensing division of the Maryland State Police. Mr. 
Pletcher was a corporal in the Maryland State Police, 
assigned to the firearms section of the licensing division 
of the state police. And Mr. Tippett was superintendent 
of the Maryland State Police. 


12a 


[3] Each of these individuals are sued in their indi- 
vidual capacity although they are alleged, at the time 
that they were acting, to have been acting as officials. 

The — Certain preliminary issues need to be addressed 
with regard to this case to get beyond the, the, even to 
discuss even whether there have been constitutional 
violations or not. 

And the first would go to whether the state of Mary- 
land has any business in this suit at all; that is, the state 
of Maryland itself. And that matter, in the Court’s view, 
is disposed of summarily by the argument made by the 
state that the Eleventh Amendment to the U.S. Consti- 
tuion basically immunizes the state from suit. That goes 
back as far as (INAUDIBLE). It has been reaffirmed 
many times; in Pennhurst State School and Hospital v. 
Halderman, 465 U.S. 89, a 1984 Supreme Court case, 
and also Weller v. Department of Social Services, a 901 
F.2d 387 Fourth Circuit case from 1990. 

Accordingly, the Court grants the defendants’ motion 
to dismiss the state of Maryland as a defendant. 

The other individuals are sued and it is said by counsel 
for those individuals that really they are being sued in 
their official capacity, not in their individual capacity, 
and that, that therefore they should, this should be 
treated as a suit against the state and the suit should also 
be dismissed as to them. 

[4] The Court agrees with the plaintiff in this case 
that effectively what we have here is a claim against state 
officials but they are sued in their individual capacity 
and as such they are liable to suit. Hafer v. Mellow, 112 
Sup. Ct. 358, 1991, in the Court’s view, makes them 
subject to suit under the present circumstances. 

We turn, however, under all those circumstances to 
the issue of whether a cause of action is stated as to these 
four defendants now fairly in suit. 


13a 


42 U.S.C. 1983 reads as follows. Quote, every person 
who under color of any statute, ordinance, regulation, 
custom, or usage of any state or territory of the District 
of Columbia subjects or causes to be subjected any 
citizen of the United States or other person within the 
jurisdiction thereof to the deprivation of any rights, 
privileges, or immunities secured by the Constitution and 
laws shall be liable to the party injured in an action at 
law, suit in equity, or other proper proceeding for redress. 
And that’s the end of the section for present purposes. 
We need not read more. 

The first and foremost, as the defendants point out, 
it is required of the plaintiffs, plaintiff in this case, that 
she demonstrate that she was in fact, she had some con- 
stitution—, federal constitutional right that she was 
deprived of, that she be, that there be such a right that 
she speaks of. 

[5] There are, as the Court understands it, three sepa- 
rate constitutional rights that the plaintiff relies upon. 
She says, first, that she has been denied a property right 
under the Fourteenth Amendment with regard to the 
acquisition of this firearm. She says she has been denied 
a right to contract by reason of the disapproval of her 
application, and she says finally she has been denied of 
her constitutional right to bear arms under the Second 
Amendment to the U.S. Constitution. 

I will start with the, the issue of ability to contract, 
which the Court has not yet seen as a separately stated 
federal constitutional right and I am, without much 
more comment, able to conclude that there is no sepa- 
rately cognizable federal constitutional right to contract 
that could be asserted under Section 1983. And so, to 
the extent that the complaint is predicated on that 
theory, it is rejected. 


l4a 


The next matter that I will turn to as far as the federal 
constitution might concern is the right to bear arms 
under the Second Amendment. It is contended by the 
plaintiff that consistent with the concept of order, liberty 
if you will, that the Second Amendment really is not 
dissimilar from all other federal Bill of Rights amend- 
ments and that it ought to, to be applicable to the states. 

However persuasive that argument may be, it is clear 
to this judge that that proposition has not ever been 
accepted [6] by courts in this country. Indeed, the 
Supreme Court of the United States has made it clear 
that the Second Amendment to the U.S. Constitution 
does not apply to the states. That was held as early as 
U.S. v. Crookshank, which is 92 U.S. 542-553, an 1875 
case where the court stated, concerning the right of 
bearing arms for a lawful purpose, quote, this is not a 
right granted by the constitution; neither is it in any 
manner dependent upon that instrument for its exist- 
ence. The Second Amendment declares that it shall not 
be infringed but this, as has been seen, means no more 
than that it shall not be infringed by Congress. 

And then that review, that position had been reaf- 
firmed in Pressor v. Illinots, whci is a 198— Excuse 
me — an 1886 case which appears at 116 U.S. 252. And 
has been affirmed also by the Maryland Court of Special 
Appeals in Underdunk (PHONETIC) v. Handgund Permit 
Review Board, 44 Md.F.132, which is a 1979 case. 

The Court, therefore, feels it need not inquire beyond 
the matter of the inapplicability of the Second Amend- 
ment to the states because under 42 U.S.C. 1983 it has 
to be a federal constitutional right or other law that is 
the matter or the subject of deprivation, and not some 
other law. 

To the extent that the Maryland Constitution may 
incorporate by reference the Second Amendment, that 


l5a 


may constitute a state constitutional claim that could be 
[7] litigated in state court, but it is not one that is liti- 
gateable in federal court pursuant to 42 U.S.C., Section 
1983. 

That leaves the third theory of, of deprivation of right

entitlement and then, if there is, one looks at the issue of 
whether there has been arbitrary, capricious behavior, or 
another way of saying that is whether there is behavior 
that shocks the conscience. 

So we look first at whether there is a claim of entitle- 
ment in this case, because even if there — If there [8] is 
no claim of entitlement, then however arbitrary the

discretion to deny issuance of the permit or to withhold 
its approval. Any significant discretion conferred upon 
the local agency defeats the claim of a property interest.” 
Gardner v. Baltimore Mayor & City Council, 969 F.2d 
63, 68 (4th Cir. 1992).
