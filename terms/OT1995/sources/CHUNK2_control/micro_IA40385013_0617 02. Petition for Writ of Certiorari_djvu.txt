STATUTORY PROVISION INVOLVED 
Section 924(c)(1) of Title 18 provides, in pertinent part: 
"Whoever, during and in relation to any crime of violence or drug 
trafficking crime * * * uses or carries a firearm, shall, in 
addition to the punishment provided for such crime * * *, be 
sentenced to imprisonment for five years * * *, nor shall the term 
of imprisonment imposed under this subsection run concurrently with 


any other term of imprisonment * * *." 
STATEMENT

no front license plate and no inspection sticker. App., infra, 3a. 
Bailey was ordered out of the car when he produced a District of 
Columbia identification card in response to the officers’ request 
for his driver’s license. Id. at 3a, 25a. The officers searched 
the passenger compartment of the car and discovered a satchel 
containing 30 grams of cocaine. Id. at 3a.

3. Bailey was convicted on all three counts of the indict- 
ment and was sentenced to concurrent 51-month terms on Counts I and

The D.C. Circuit granted rehearing in banc and consolidated 
Bailey’s case with the unrelated prosecution of another defendant, 
Candisha Robinson. App., infra, 22a-23a. A sharply divided court 
then affirmed Bailey’s and Robinson's convictions under Section 
924(c) (1). With only five judges subscribing to the majority 
opinion, the court held that, when the predicate drug trafficking 
offense is possession with intent to distribute, the prosecution 
need only show (1) that a firearm was located near the drugs or 
drug proceeds, and (2) that the gun was sufficiently accessible at 
some point during the drug possession to have been available for 
use if the need arose. App., infra, 3a, 13a. 

The court held that one "uses" a gun within the meaning of 
Section 924(c) (1) by placing or keeping the gun in a location "from

While recognizing that Section 924(c) (1) does not criminalize "the 
mere possession of a firearm by someone who commits a drug offense" 
(id. at 4a), the court reasoned that "use" is an "expansive([)" term 
that does not require active employment of a gun. Id. at 9a-10a 
(citing Smith v. United States, 113 S. Ct. 2050, 2054, 2058 
(1993)). Thus, the court concluded that a jury can infer "use" 
whenever the potential for use inheres in the location of the gun. 
The court held further that, when the predicate drug offense is a 
"continuing offense" such as possession with intent to distribute, 
the government need only show that the gun was located near the 
drugs "at some point" in time during the defendant's possession of 
drugs. App., infra, 10a. 

The majority also held that a firearm is used "during and in 
relation to" a predicate drug offense if it is "positioned so as to 
be available for use during ongoing drug activity." App., infra, 
lla. The court stressed that the gun did not have to "further" the 
drug offense, provided that its location suggests that it had the 
potential to facilitate the drug offense. Ibid. 

Applying those standards, the court concluded that the gun in 
the locked trunk of Bailey’s car was "used" because it was 
"proximate to the drugs" in the passenger compartment. Id. at l2a. 
The court also ruled that the gun was used “during and in relation 
to" Bailey’s illegal possession of drugs because the gun "was 


easily accessible to Bailey at any time." Ibid, The majority 


6 
opinion made no attempt to define the "carry" component of Section 
924(c) (1) or to delimit the scope of "use" vis-a-vis "carry." 

Judge Wald dissented in part. App., infra, 13a-15a. She 
stated that the majority’s twofold standard for determining whether 
a firearm was "used" -- proximity of the gun to drugs or drug 
proceeds, and accessibility of the gun at some point during the 
possession offense -- "artificially limit([s]" the factors necessary 
to distinguish "use" from mere possession. Id. at 14a. Judge Wald 
identified several additional factors, such as the number and type 
of gun involved, whether the gun was loaded, and whether expert 
testimony supported an inference of "use," that are crucial for 
distinguishing "use" from mere possession. Ibid. Despite her 
staunch disagreement with the legal standard adopted by the 
majority, Judge Wald -- alone among the nine participating judges 
of the court of appeals -- concluded that Bailey’s conviction could 
be affirmed through the application of a traditional balancing of 
the appropriate factors. 

Judge Williams, joined by Judges Silberman and Buckley, 
dissented outright. App., infra, 15a-2la. Judge Williams faulted 
the majority’s reliance on inferences of potential use, arguing 
that, under the plain meaning of the statutory term, "use" 
"normally implies activity." Id. at 15a, 16a (citing United States 
v. McFadden, 13 F.3d 463, 467 (lst Cir. 1994) (Breyer, C.J., 
dissenting) ). The three-judge dissent expressly rejected the 
majority’s reliance on Smith v. United States to extend "use" to 


encompass situations of potential use (App., infra, 19a): 


7 

Nothing in Smith precludes limiting "use" to active 
employment. [Smith’s] statement that "§ 924(c) (1)’s language 
sweeps broadly", 113 S. Ct. at 2054, means only that the 
proscribed use of firearms is not limited to the obvious or 
typical ways in which they may assist in the commission of a 
crime. Rather, a gun may be "used" regardless of whether it 
acts as an instrument to shoot, to bludgeon, or for exchange. 
That is, whether a firearm is "used" does not depend on 
whether it is employed in the customary way or a more creative 
way. But it does depend on whether the gun is employed at 
all, and nothing [in Smith] expands "use" beyond active 


employment. 
Judge Williams also noted that Section 924 itself distinguish- 


es between the actual use of a firearm, on the one hand, and the 
intent or potential for use, on the other. Id. at 18a. Subsection 
(ad) cf Section 924 provides for forfeiture of firearms "used in any 
knowing violation" of certain offenses, and of firearms "intended 
to be used" in other crimes. "Thus Congress [was] clearly quite 
alert to the distinction between firearms ‘used’ in commission of 
a crime and merely ‘intended’ for such use * * *." Ibid. Quoting 
this Court’s observation in Smith, 113 S. Ct. at 2057, that “using 
a firearm" should not have a "different meaning in § 924(c) (1) than 
it does in § 924(d)," Judge Williams concluded that the majority 
opinion was inconsistent with Congress’s choice not to reac mere 
"intent to use" in subsection (c). Finally, Judge Williams 
observed that the majority’s definition of "use" was so broad "as 
to leave no apparent role" for the alternate element of "carrying" 
a firearm during and in relation to a drug offense. App., infra, 
18a. 

Judge Silberman, in addition to joining Judge Williams’

REASONS FOR GRANTING THE PETITION 

Two years ago, this Court noted divergences among the courts 
of appeals in their interpretations of Section 924(c) (1). Langston 
v. United States, 113 S. Ct. 361, 361-362 (1992) (White, J., joined 
by Thomas, J., dissenting from denial of certiorari). Those 
divergences have deepened with the passage of time. Currently, 
five courts of appeals unambiguously hold that mere possession of 
a firearm by someone who commits a drug offense is not sufficient 
to establish "use" under Section 924(c)(1).° Three courts of 
appeals, on the other hand, have definitively held that mere 
possession -- or even constructive possession -- of a firearm 


during a drug offense does suffice.* Three appear to have embraced

The majority opinion below did nothing to harmonize the 
discord among the circuits; on the contrary, it added yet another 
dissonant voice by formulating a new standard for Section 924(c) (1) 
analysis that -- while coming dangerously close to equating "use" 
with mere possession -- is formulated differently from the 
standards employed in all of the other courts of appeals. This 
Court should grant certiorari to bring order to those substantial 
conflicts. Moreover, the correct interpretation of Section 
924(c) (1) presents an important issue, particularly in light of the 
frequency with which the issue arises in the lower courts. 
Certiorari is also warranted because the decision below is 
erroneous. 


I. THE DECISION BELOW CONFLICTS WITH THE INTERPRETATION OF 18 
U.S.C. § 924(c) (1) BY SEVERAL OTHER COURTS OF APPEALS 


In Smith v. United States, this Court held that the plain 


language of Section 924(c)(1) requires two elements to be proved

Cir. 1988) ("section 924(c) requires more than mere possession of 
a firearm"), with United States v. Meggett, 875 F.2d 24, 28 (2d 
Cir.) ("mere possession of a gun can constitute ‘use’"), cert.

los, 866 F.2d 587, 597 (3d Cir. 1989) (mere availability of a

10 

beyond a reasonable doubt. First, the accused must have "use[d]" 
or "carrie[d)" the firearm; second, such use or carrying must have 
been done "during and in relation to" a drug trafficking offense. 
113 S. Ct. 2050, 2058 (1993). The majority opinion in the court 
below adopted a "proximity and accessibility" standard for 
evaluating those two elements. App., infra, 13a. The court held 
that, as a matter of law, a firearm is "used" if it is kept ina 
location that is proximate to the location of drugs or drug 
proceeds. App., infra, 10a. The court then held that any such 
"use" of a firearm was necessarily "during and in relation to" a 
drug offense if the firearm was accessible to the accused -- that 
is, "positioned so as to be available for use" if the need arose -- 
at any time during the drug offense. Id. at lia. 

Under the approach of the court below, the inquiry for both 
elements focuses on the location of the firearm; if the location is 
such that the accused could retrieve and employ the firearm at any 
time during the drug offense, the elements of Section 924(c) (1) are 
met. The court rejected the notion that Section 924(c) (1) requires 
active or actual use of a firearm (App., infra, 9a); rather, the 
potential for use suffices. Furthermore, when the predicate drug 
offense is possession with intent to distribute, Section 924(c) (1) 
is satisfied if the jury could infer that the firearm was proximate 
and accessible at any time during the drug possession. Id. at 10a. 

Decisions of the First and Second Circuits have squarely 
rejected the stark "proximity" test embraced by the court below. 


In United States v. Torres-Maldonado, 14 F.3d 95, 101-102 (ist 


11 

Cir.), cert. denied, 115 S. Ct. 193 (1994), the First Circuit 
reversed a Section 924(c) (1) conviction even though a gun was found 
in the same room as $4000 in cash, cocaine, and drug-dealing 
paraphernalia. That court expressly rejected the government’s 
argument that the mere "physical proximity” of the gun to the drugs 
sufficed to establish use. Instead, the court looked for evidence 
that the defendant "exercised" dominion over the gun or "partici- 
pated in armed drug deals." Finding no such evidence, the court 
reversed the conviction. 

In United States v. Feliz-Cordero, supra, the Second Circuit 
reversed a conviction under Section 924(c)(1) on the ground that 
the presence of a firearm "in the same room as" drugs, drug 
proceeds, and drug paraphernalia "is not sufficient evidence to 
sustain a conviction." 859 F.2d at 254. The court reasoned that 
the presence of a firearm at the apartment did not constitute "use" 
because Congress did not intend to penalize the mere possession of 
a firearm during a drug offense. Ibid.; see also United States v. 
Long, 905 F.2d 1572, 1578 n.10 (D.C. Cir.) (Thomas, J.) ("A person 
can possess a gun without * * * ‘using’ it."), cert. denied, 498 
U.S. 948 (1990). 

Decisions of the Second and Sixth Circuits are inconsistent 
with the D.C. Circuit majority’s position that, when the predicate 
drug offense is a "continuous offense" such as possession with 
intent to distribute, the presence of a firearm at the time of 
arrest supports an inference that the gun was used at some prior 


time during the possessory offense. See App., infra, 10a. In 


12 

United States v. Lasanta, 978 F.2d 1300, 1308-1309 (1992) (citing 
Feliz-Cordero, supra), the Second Circuit held that the possession 
of a firearm did not support an inference that the gun had provided 
protection at some prior drug transaction. In United States v. 
Clemis, 11 F.3d 597 (6th Cir. 1993), cert. denied, 114 S. Ct. 1858 
(1994), the Sixth Circuit held that the presence of firearms in a 
house containing marked money from a controlled drug sale did not, 
standing alone, constitute "use" of those weapons (id. at 602 
(emphasis added) ): 

The government would have us recognize that the presence 
of the "marked" money establishes that the weapons found were 
used to protect the proceeds of a drug transaction, thereby 
facilitating the drug transaction. We cannot make this jump. 
While we agree that the terms of [Section 924(c) (1)] are to be 
construed broadly, it is still the law that mere possession of 
a weapon during the course of criminal conduct is not enough. 
Numerous decisions have rejected the broad "accessibility" 

test approved below for establishing that a firearm was used or 
carried "during and in relation to" the predicate drug offense. In 
United States v. Theodoropoulos, 866 F.2d 587 (1989), for example, 
the Third Circuit reversed a Section 924(c)(1) conviction even 
though drugs were found inside an apartment and loaded guns were 
discovered outside on the porch. The court held that "the mere 
availability of a firearm nearby * * * [does not] equal * * * use 
‘in relation to’" a drug trafficking offense. Id. at 597. The 
court reasoned that the accessibility test urged by the government 
impermissibly extended Section 924(c) (1) to situations in which a 


firearm was merely possessed during a predicate offense: "Had 


[Congress] intended the provision to encompass possession of a 


13 
firearm during a drug trafficking offense it would have so 
provided." Id. at 597-598. 

Likewise, the Tenth Circuit has repeatedly rejected the 
argument that the government need only show that a firearm was 
accessible at some time during a drug offense to meet the "during 
and in relation to" test. In United States v. Connor, 972 F.2d 
1172, 1174 (1992), the court held that "(mjere access to a firearm 
is insufficient to sustain a conviction" because access alone 
cannot prove that the firearm was an “integral part of the drug 
trafficking offense." In United States v. Matthews, 942 F.2d 779, 
783 (10th Cir. 1991), the court reversed a Section 924(c) (1) 
conviction even though it found that a firearm was accessible to 
the defendant during the drug offense: "(S)ection 924/(c) (1) 
requires more than mere access to a firearm." See also United 
States v. Sullivan, 919 F.2d 1403, 1432 & m.45 (10th Cir. 1990) 
(access to firearm, standing alone, is insufficient to support a 
Section 924(c) (1) conviction). 

The decision below also conflicts with decisions from other 
courts of appeals in that it curtails the number of factors that 
courts may consider in determining whether a firearm was used or 
carried during and in relation to a drug offense. In the D.C. 
Circuit, it is now the law that only two factors are relevant in 
Section 924(c)(1) analysis: the proximity of the firearm to drugs 
or drug proceeds, and the accessibility of the firearm. See App., 
infra, 3a. The court has discarded several other factors on which 


prior panel opinions had relied, stating that such factors 


14 
"produced widely divergent and seemingly contradictory results." 
Id. at 7a. Other courts of appeals, however, employ those very 
factors in Section 924(c)(1) analysis.

rendered completely irrelevant by the decision below -- the absence 
of evidence of an drug actual transaction -- in reversing Section 
924(c) (1) convictions. See Feliz-Cordero, 859 F.2d at 254; Torres- 
Maldonado, 14 F.3d at 101-102. The First, Second, and Eighth 
Circuits have relied on the type and number of firearms involved in 
determining whether an inference of "use" was justified. See 
United States v. Hadfield, 918 F.2d 987 (list Cir. 1990), cert. 
denied, 500 U.S. 936 (1991); Feliz-Cordero, 859 F.2d at 254; United 
States v. Lyman, 892 F.2d 751, 753 (8th Cir. 1989), cert. denied, 
498 U.S. 810 (1990). That factor, too, has now been rendered 
irrelevant in the D.C. Circuit by the opinion below. 

The opinion below is inaccurate in stating that "[(e)very other 
circuit similarly focuses, in essence," solely on the proximity and 
accessibility factors. App., infra, 8a. To the contrary, while 
every circuit does indeed apply those two factors, only the D.C. 
Circuit excludes all others from consideration. See App., infra, 
13a (Wald, J., dissenting) ("the majority’s new ‘proximity and 
accessibility’ test offers only a straightjacket [(sic)" for judges 
faced with "a steady stream of new fact situations"). The majority 
opinion itself acknowledged that its truncated Section 924(c) (1) 
analysis conflicted with the approach of at least one other court

ment"), cert. denied, 114 S. Ct. 2720 (1994). 

Bailey’s conviction under Section 924(c)(1) would have been 
reversed if the D.C. Circuit had not crafted its new two-factor 
test for evaluating the sufficiency of the evidence against him, 
because the evidence showed (at most) that Bailey possessed a gun. 
First, there was no proof of any actual drug transaction at which 
the gun in the trunk played a role. The only activity Bailey 
engaged in was driving a car; the presence of the gun in the locked 
trunk of that moving car had no role, active or otherwise, in his 
possessory crime. See Feliz-Cordero, 859 F.2d at 254 (reversing 
Section 924(c)(1) conviction where there was no evidence of a 
specific drug transaction involving the firearm) ; Torres-Maldonado, 
14 F.3d at 101-102 (same). Second, the gun was sufficiently 
removed from Bailey to preclude an inference that he derived any 
service from it. Indeed, the government conceded that "the firearm

Section 924(c)(1) conviction where firearm was “nearby” but not

Gefendant in apartment), cert. denied, 112 S. Ct. 1771 (1992). 

Third, only a single firearm was found in the trunk of 
Bailey’s car. Although a single weapon can support an inference of 
"use" in appropriate cases, a purely circumstantial showing of 
"use" is greatly weakened by the fact that no other weapons were 
present. See, e.g., Hadfield, 918 F.2d at 991 (presence of nine 
firearms supported inference of "use"); United States v. Matra, 841

Bailey’s conviction was upheld on evidence that showed nothing more

II. THE CORRECT INTERPRETATION OF 
= SECTION 924(c) (1) IS A QUESTION 


The lower courts have been called on with increasing frequency 
to determine the proper scope of Section 924(c)(1). See Langston, 
113 S. Ct. at 362 (White, J., joined by Thomas, J., dissenting from 


17 
denial of certiorari) ("this issue arises with some frequency"). 
The courts of appeals have issued hundreds of rulings -- published 
and unpublished -- in the last few years in which the principal 
issue on appeal has been whether the evidence met the legal 
standard to support a conviction under Section 924(c) (1). The 
district courts must resolve that issue even more frequently. 

The conflicts among the courts of appeals that two Justices 
noted in 1992 still "show[{] no signs of abating." Langston, 113 
S. Ct. at 362 (dissent). To the contrary, the discussion above 
illustrates that the divisions have become deeper and more 
numerous. Resolution by this Court of the proper interpretation of 
Section 924(c) (1) is necessary to promote uniformity in the courts 
of appeals and to prevent applicability of this federal statute 
from depending on the fortuity of the defendant's locale. 

Section 924(c) (1) convictions entail serious consequences for 
defendants. If a defendant is convicted under Section 924(c) (1), 
the trial court is required to add a five-year consecutive sentence 
to the sentence for the defendant’s underlying crime. The trial 
court has no discretion in the matter, nor can the Sentencing 
Guidelines lessen the blow. In some cases, such as Bailey's, the 
Section 924(c) (1) violation more than doubles the punishment for 
the underlying offense. 

This case presents a singularly appropriate vehicle for this 
Court to address the meaning of “use” of a firearm “during and in 
relation to" a drug trafficking offense. Although the issue arises 


in varying fact patterns, Bailey's case -- involving a gun that che 


18 

government has conceded was "relatively inaccessible" in the locked 
trunk of a moving vehicle -- represents one typical scenario, and 
one clearly at the outer fringes of the government's plausible 
candidates for a "use" prosecution. The issue was explored with 
unusual thoroughness in this case, and the D.C. Circuit majority's 
attempt to craft a bright-line rule, while in conflict with other 
circuits and (we submit) ultimately misguided, sharpens the issues 
for this Court’s consideration -- a feature lacking in most Section 
924(c) cases. The thoughtful dissenting opinions below likewise 
will aid this Court’s consideration of the issue presented. If 
‘this Court wishes ever to address the important and recurring -- 
but elusive -- issue of the scope of Section 924(c) (1), this case 
is an unusually compelling candidate for review. 

III. THE DECISION OF THE COURT OF APPEALS IS ERRONEOUS 

Unanimously declining to go as far as the Fourth, Ninth, and 
Eleventh Circuits (and panels of the Second, Third, and Tenth), see 
notes 4 & 5, supra, the D.C. Circuit correctly conceded that 
Congress did not intend in Section 924(c) (1) to reach mere posses- 
sion of a firearm. App., infra, 4a. The majority then attempted 
to draw a line between possession and use, but the line it drew is 
invisible. 

A. By focusing on the proximity of a firearm, and by relying 
on inferences of potential use rather than evidence of actual use, 
the court affirmed Bailey’s conviction based on the "theoretical 
possibility” of use rather than on use itself. See McFadden, 13 
F.3d at 468 (Breyer, C.J., dissenting). The “ordinary meaning" of 


19 
the word "use" "carries a more active meaning"; it "excludes simple 
(drug-crime-related) possession." Id. at 466-467. The "proximity" 
standard adopted by the opinion below violates that ordinary, 
active meaning of "use" by expanding it to include the possibility 
of future use "when needed to facilitate a drug crime." App., 
infra, 10a. 

The opinion below states that a firearm kept in an accessible 
location is "used" because it can serve to protect the defendant, 
drugs, and drug proceeds. App., infra, 9a, 10a, 12a. But that 
rationale is necessarily too broad, if in fact Section 924(c) (1) 
does not reach mere possession, because it would apply "virtually 
whenever a gun is present near the scene of a drug crime." 
McFadden, 13 F.3d at 469 (Breyer, C.J., dissenting). Such a 
"possession for protection" theory sweeps all cases of mere 
possession within the ambit of "use" because all firearms -- even 
those that are only possessed -- have a potential utility. See 
ibid. (under the "possession for protection" theory, "possession of 
a gun (when related to a drug crime) automatically means a ‘use’ ") 
(emphasis in original); see also Sullivan, 919 F.2d a 1432 & n.45 
(rejecting the “possession for protection" rationale). 

Furthermore, the "continuing nature" of the predicate drug 
offense of possession with intent to distribute cannot justify 
inferring past use of a firearm from present possession. Clemis, 
11 F.3d at 602. Such a principle subsumes all cases of mere 
possession and permits conviction based on mere speculation of past 


use. The present case illustrates that problem. The prosecution 


20 
presented no evidence whatever that Bailey used the firearm in his 
trunk at any time in the past. Accordingly, the court relied on 
generalizations about the prevalence of gun usage in the drug world 
(App., infra, 12a) to conclude that past use could be inferred from 
Bailey’s possession of a firearm at the time of arrest. Permitting 
one person to be convicted on the basis of assumptions about other 
persons’ conduct demonstrates the limitless reach of the majority 
opinion’s interpretation of "use." 

The "emboldening”" rationale approved by the majority (App., 
infra, 4a-5a) -- which holds that a firearm is "used" if the 
possessor was "emboldened" by the gun’s existence -- suffers the 
same defect: it fails to separate use from possession. A jury 
could infer in every case that the possession of a gun somehow 
"enhance[d) the defendant’s confidence." App., infra, 19a 
(Williams, J., joined by Silberman and Buckley, JJ., dissenting). 
Moreover, the "emboldening" theory invites juries and courts to 
speculate about whether a defendant was psychologically comforted 
by the existence of a gun, an inquiry that is incapable of 
consistent application and is impossible to review. Thus, the 
"“emboldening" rationale promises either "a carte blanche for 
conviction" or a "continuous and vacillating review of jury 
verdicts." Id. at 20a. 

The mercuric rationales advanced in the majority opinion point 
out the need for tangible guideposts for juries and courts to 
follow in separating the actual use of a gun from the "theoretical 


possibility" of use. The "active use" approach favored by Judges 


21 

Williams, Silberman, and Buckley -- and the alternate approach that 
looks to concrete indicia of use to support inference of "use" 
(such as proof of an actual drug transaction at which the gun 
played a role) -- both provide objective ways to distinguish use 
from mere possession. Such an approach is crucial for separating 
cases where it was "likely [that the defendant] put the gun to 
active use" from cases where use was merely a "theoretical 
possibility." McFadden, 13 F.3d at 468 (Breyer, C.J., dissenting) 
(emphasis in original). 

B. The majority’s "accessibility" test for determining 
whether a gun was used or carried "during and in relation to" a 
drug offense is also deeply flawed. The premise of the majority 
opinion is that any gun accessible to a drug offender “becomes a 
part of the drug activity and thus is used by the defendant in 
relation to the drug activity." App., infra, 10a. That rationale 
swallows every case in which a drug offender possesses a firearm, 
because the existence of the firearm would necessarily make it "a 
part of the drug activity." Thus, if a person who possessed drugs 
with the intent to sell them used a gun at any time for any 
purpose, that use could be imputed to the drug offense. Although 
this Court held in Smith that the phrase "in relation to" is to be 
read broadly (113 S. Ct. at 2058), the consequences of the 
"accessibility" test are too broad. See Long, 905 F.2d at 1578 
n.10 (Thomas, J.) ("A person can possess a gun without either 
‘using’ it pe using it ‘during and in relation to’ a given 


crime."). 


22 

C. It is a cardinal principle of statutory interpretation 
that a statute must be read as a whole because "(t]he meaning of 
statutory language, plain or not, depends on context." Brown v. 
Gardner, 63 U.S.L.W. 4035, 4036 (U.S. Dec. 12 1994) (No. 93-1128) 
(quoting King v. St. Vincent’s Hospital, 502 U.S. 215, 221 (1991)). 
Each term in a statute should be read in light of the surrounding 
words and provisions of the law as a whole, because that context 
illuminates the scope and meaning of each word within the statutory 
framework. John Hancock Mutual Life Insurance Co. v. Harris Trust 
& Savings Bank, 114 S. Ct. 517, 523 (1993). The broad interpre- 
tation of "use" by the court below violates those principles in two 
important respects. 

3. The majority opinion’s broad reading of "use" brings 
subsection (c) (1) of Section 924 into conflict with subsection (d). 
Subsection (c)(1) proscribes "use" of a firearm. By contrast, 
subsection (d) provides for forfeiture of firearms "used" in 
certain offenses and of firearms "intended to be used." Thus, 
Congress was "clearly quite alert to the distinction between 
firearms ‘used’ in commission of a crime and [firearms] merely 
‘intended’ for such use." App., infra, 18a (Williams, J., joined 
by Silberman and Buckley, JJ., dissenting). 

"It is generally presumed that Congress acts intentionally and 
purposely when it includes particular language in one section of a 
statute but omits it in another." City of Chicago v. Environmental 
Defense Fund, 114 S. Ct. 1588, 1593 (1994) (internal quotation 


marks omitted); Brown, 63 U.S.L.W. at 4037. Congress chose to 


23 

include an "intent to use" element in subsection (d) but not to 
include such an element in subsection (c)(1). The "proximity" 
test," however, reads a "floating intent to use" (App., infra, léa 
(dissent)) into subsection (c) (1) by permitting a finding of "use" 
if a gun has merely been kept in an accessible place for potential 
use. Id. at 10a. Including within "use" the possibility of future 
retrieval and employment of a gun "effect[ively] dilute[s] ‘use’ to 
mean simple possession" and rewrites subsection (c)(1). Id. at léa 
(dissent). 

This Court has ruled that "using a firearm" should not have a 
"different meaning in § 924(c)(1) than it does in § 924(d)." 
Smith, 113 S. Ct. at 2057. Because subsection (d) distinguishes 
between "use" and the "intent to use," subsection (c) (1) should be 
construed to do so as well. 

The court below offered no answer to this point made by Judge 
Williams’ dissent. It is not clear what answer the court (or the 
government) could offer. The decision below, which ignores this 
critical point, is simply wrong.

also eclipses Section 924(c)(1)’s alternate element of "carrying" 
a firearm, and thus violates the tenet that each word in a statute 
must be given effect. United States Department of Treasury v. 
Fabe, 113 S. Ct. 2202, 2210 (1993). Although the court declined to 
address the scope of "use" vis-a-vis "carry," its "proximity" test 
leaves no room for the separate application of the “carrying”

covered by "use." The plain meaning of the term "carry" is "[t]o

In an effort to distinguish between mere

v. Morris, 977 F.2d 617, 621 (D.C.Cir.1992).

(W]e have enumerated a nonexclusive set

Defendants Bailey and Robinson do not

approach were properly applied, he or she

evidence presented at trial. In response, the 
government argues that it needs to present 
evidence only that the defendant possessed a 
firearm and that the firearm in some way 
facilitated the drug crime; thus implicitly the 
Government asks us to abandon the Bruce-— 
Morris—Derr approach in favor of a simpler 
test. 

In form the dispute between the parties is 
little more than a disagreement over the

substance, however, this case is about what

formulate an appropriate test for assessing

B. Section 92}(c) Henceforth 


As the Supreme Court has made clear, the 
government must make two distinct show- 
ings in order to obtain a conviction under 
§ 924(c)(1): “that the defendant ‘use{d) or 
carrie[d) a firearm’” and “that the use or 
carrying was ‘during and in relation to’” a 
predicate offense. Smith v. United States, 
— US. —-—, 113 S.Ct. 2050, 124 L.Ed.2d 
138 (1993). Therefore, in determining what 
the proper standard should be, we must de- 
termine both what constitutes the use or 
carrying of a gun and in what circumstances 
such a use or carrying is in relation to a drug 
trafficking offense. 


1. “Use” of a Firearm 


In the context of § 924(c)(1), “use” could 
be defined either narrowly, so as to encom- 


pass only the paradigmatic uses of a gun, ie., 
firing, brandishing, or displaying the gun 


during the commission of the predicate of- 


fense, or more broadly, so as to include the 
other ways in which a gun can be used to 
facilitate drug trafficking. The narrow defi- 
nition has the virtue of simplicity; it is, after 
all, relatively easy to determine whether the 
defendant’s firing, brandishing, or displaying 
a gun was related to the defendant’s contem- 
poraneous (recall the “during” requirement 
of § 924(c)(1)) drug trafficking offense. The 
narrow definition also has the vice of simplic- 
ity, however; it is too narrow to capture all 
of the various uses of a firearm that the 
Congress apparently intended to reach via 
§ 924(c)(1). 


Certainly nothing in the statute indicates 
that the Congress intended to exclude any 
use from the scope of its prohibition. In- 
deed, the Supreme Court has said that in 
§ 924(c)(1), the “Congress employed the 
term ‘use’ expansively.” See Smith, — U.S. 
at ——, 113 S.Ct. at 2058. Wherefore, it 
held that the statute prohibits the “use” of a 
firearm even as an object that can be bar- 
tered for drugs. See Smith, — US. at 
—, —, 113 S.Ct. at 2055, 2057. 

Like firing, brandishing, or displaying a 
gun, barter involv.s handling the gun. A 
gun can surely be used even when it is not 
being handled, however. For example, a gun 
placed in a drawer beside one’s bed for fear 
of an intruder would, in common parlance, be 
a gun “used” for domestic protection.

shoot, to bludgeon, or for exchange. That is, 
whether a firearm is “used” does not depend 
on whether it is employed in the customary

depend on whether the gun is employed at
