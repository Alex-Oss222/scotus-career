## Questions, preservation and remedy

Petitioners' merits question is whether appellate courts should use de novo review of reasonable suspicion to stop and probable cause to search in nonwarrant cases. The United States expressly accepts historical facts unless clearly erroneous and requests independent review of the ultimate conclusions. The opposing Isakoff amicus requests clear-error review of the ultimate applications. All these positions preserve deference to historical facts and credibility; disagreement about the ultimate classification remains real despite the parties' shared position.

The authorized simulated scope is this review-standard question. The separate petition claim concerning access to the raw NADDIS data is outside that scope. The merits legality of this particular stop/search is not requested for initial Supreme Court resolution: petitioners' merits brief p42 asks reversal and remand to the court of appeals to determine the suppression motions under de novo review; their reply p18 also requests reversal and remand. The United States pp39–40 seeks vacatur and remand, says it believes the stop and search valid even under independent review, and states that the court of appeals should apply the selected standard first. Isakoff p33 and AELE/NDAA p8 seek affirmance. Agreement on the standard does not eliminate the underlying dispute over suppression or dictate which judgment to enter.

Conditional guilty pleas under Criminal Rule11(a)(2) and reservation of the suppression issue are recited in the original lower orders and JA selected district entries. The final April18,1995 appellate order affirms the district judgment using clear-error review. Petition pp10–31 and PB pp9–10 say the independent-review contention was made in both appeals, quoting or identifying the appellate briefs; those complete appellate briefs and plea agreements are not reproduced in the JA. The record supports the stated preserved review question without inventing an additional reservation or default.

The government's concession of petitioners' ability to challenge the car search eliminates no other threshold by implication. It also did not claim that either the stop or the consent justified dismantling the car. The first appeal expressly held inevitable discovery waived after the government failed to defend it. The remand magistrate and US merits brief p10 n7 acknowledge that holding. It is not a live alternative affirmance ground merely because the dog was present.

## Supported competing paths

* Independent ultimate review: PB pp11–42, US pp13–40 and petitioners' full reply distinguish historical facts from objective constitutional application. They invoke uniform national standards, incremental law development, guidance, error correction and appellate institutional advantages. They distinguish issuing-magistrate substantial-basis review as serving the warrant preference. Their requested appellate remand need not decide ultimate suppression.
* Clear-error ultimate review: Isakoff pp9–33 and AELE/NDAA pp3–8 emphasize practical probabilities, the trial court's ability to weigh evidence, fact-specific applications and limited gains from repeated appellate reassessment. They retain correction of legal errors and clear mistakes. Deference is to the district court, **not** directly to the police; the amicus says the same standard would govern government appeals and that warrant incentives remain through existing warrant doctrines. Isakoff specifically answers the magistrate-referral objection in p24 n14: institutional expertise and the district judge's opportunity to supplement the record support one appellate standard whether or not the judge personally heard witnesses.
* A formulation separating ultimate review from treatment of subsidiary inferences must identify the precise object and legal basis of deference. No exact intermediate formula is attributed to a party merely from the fact that officers' training matters to the substantive reasonable-officer inquiry. No source supports silently deciding the search itself under such a formulation.

The parties dispute whether earlier independent-examination cases settled the appellate allocation, whether Gates's observation about the limited precedential utility of individual probable-cause determinations transfers to this setting, and whether habeas, First Amendment, civil litigation-management and criminal-sufficiency analogies fit. The invitation amicus's claim that Jackson sufficiency review is virtually indistinguishable from clear error is contested by petitioners, not an established equivalence.

## Statutory boundaries

Section636(b)(1)'s district-court de novo determination of objected-to portions of a magistrate's report concerns a different relationship from court-of-appeals review of the district judgment. JA pp68–71 quote the pertinent language and preserve the Raddatz distinction between making a determination on the record and rejecting credibility without rehearing. In this record the district judge accepted the remand credibility finding. This intake did not recover an official dated complete §636 text; no additional statutory qualification is invented.

The full official 1994 §3731 is recovered and read in `ornelas_3731_1994.txt` (GovInfo provenance in the paired HTML). A prosecution appeal of suppression is subject to the specified jeopardy-stage exclusion and US attorney certification that it is not for delay and the evidence substantially proves a material fact; the section requires a thirty-day appeal and diligent prosecution. The 1971 amendment savings clause excludes district cases begun before January2,1971. Thus the symmetry argument refers to authorized government appeals, not unrestricted review of every acquittal or suppression order. Section3731 is an analogy here, not the jurisdictional vehicle for these defendants' appeal. No material current transition is claimed.

Civil Rule52(a) is not itself the direct governing criminal rule. The original briefs acknowledge criminal clear-error practice notwithstanding the absence of an identical criminal rule. Section2254(d) is invoked by analogy only: this is direct federal criminal review, and a historical habeas statutory presumption cannot be imported as controlling text. Historical brief descriptions of post-divergence Supreme Court decisions must yield to the actual simulated law.
