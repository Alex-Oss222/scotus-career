#### Record and posture

On the evening of March 10, 1989, Officer Johnson of the Cambridge, Maryland police responded to a domestic-disturbance call at Pinder's home, where her former boyfriend, Don Pittman, had broken in. Pinder told Johnson that Pittman had pushed and punched her, thrown objects at her, and threatened to kill her and her children; a neighbor, Darnell Taylor, had restrained him until police arrived. Johnson arrested Pittman, who kept making threats during the arrest. Pinder told Johnson that Pittman had threatened her before and had been convicted of trying to burn her house about ten months earlier. She asked whether it was safe to return to work that evening. Johnson assured her that Pittman would be held overnight and indicated that a county commissioner would not be available until morning. Based on those assurances, Pinder returned to work, leaving her three children at home. That same evening Johnson brought Pittman before Dorchester County Commissioner George Ames, Jr. for an initial appearance, charging only trespassing and malicious destruction of property valued under $300, both misdemeanors. Pinder alleges that assault and breaking and entering a dwelling could have been charged. Ames released Pittman on his own recognizance and warned him to stay away from Pinder's home; the district court's account puts his time in custody at little more than an hour. Pittman walked about ten minutes from the police station to the house, broke in and set it on fire. Pinder was at work; her three children, asleep inside, died of smoke inhalation. Pinder alleges that no attempt was made to warn her of the release. She also alleges that Johnson knew Pittman from high school, and suggests that this acquaintance may have influenced the charging decision.

No findings have been made. The district court's May 10, 1993 memorandum decided a Rule 12(b)(6) motion to dismiss and accepted the complaint's allegations as true; the panel and en banc opinions describe Johnson's motion as one for summary judgment. No court has determined what charges the facts would have supported, whether different charges would have produced overnight detention, why Johnson chose the charges he did, or whether his statement about the commissioner's availability was knowingly inaccurate. The en banc dissent calls that statement a false representation; the en banc majority does not characterize it.

Pinder sued Johnson, individually and in his official capacity, and the Commissioners of Cambridge under 42 U.S.C. §§1983 and 1985 (equal-protection and due-process counts) and under Maryland law (wrongful death and survival). The district court, Pinder v. Commissioners of Cambridge, 821 F. Supp. 376 (D. Md. 1993), refused to dismiss the federal counts or the state counts against Johnson, dismissed the state counts against the City of Cambridge, and rejected Johnson's qualified-immunity defense on both federal counts. On due process, it reasoned that DeShaney left open whether, and how far, state action that creates or enhances a danger can give rise to a duty outside custody. Undercharging alone would not meet DeShaney's requirements. But Johnson's alleged assurances, relied on by Pinder, increased the danger, and the allegations also satisfied the special-relationship factors of Jensen v. Conrad, 747 F.2d 185 (4th Cir. 1984), which the court treated after DeShaney as a starting point to be combined with state creation or enhancement of the danger. It held that, under both Jensen and the danger-creation decisions, the right was clearly established in March 1989, and that an exact prior holding was not required. A divided panel affirmed, Pinder v. Johnson, 33 F.3d 368 (4th Cir. 1994). Judge Russell, joined by Judge Michael, held that DeShaney permits a duty outside custody where the State's affirmative conduct creates or enhances the danger, and that such a right was clearly established in March 1989. Senior Judge Roney, sitting by designation, dissented on the ground that it was not clearly established. On October 14, 1994, the Fourth Circuit granted rehearing en banc and vacated the panel opinion.

The judgment under review is the en banc Fourth Circuit's May 30, 1995 judgment reversing the district court's denial of qualified immunity to Johnson, 9–4 in the judgment. Judge Wilkinson's opinion was joined in full by Judges Hall, Wilkins, Niemeyer and Williams. Judge Widener joined all but three sentences, which describe state-created-danger cases as a different paradigm in which the absence of custody may not be dispositive. Judge Motz joined Parts I–IV. The opinion holds that no due-process right to protection was clearly established in March 1989. It reasons that DeShaney locates the affirmative duty in custody and rejected a special relationship based on an official's awareness of a risk or promises of aid; that neither Pinder nor her children were in custody; and that treating the assurances and charging decision as creating the danger would make every police representation and every failure to incarcerate actionable, so the claim is one of omission. It adds that pre-1989 decisions finding a duty outside custody involved a larger and more direct state role in creating the danger, and none rested on assurances alone; that several decisions tied the duty to custody (citing among others Wideman v. Shallowford Community Hospital, Harpole v. Arkansas Department of Human Services and Estate of Gilmore v. Buckley); that DeShaney singled out Jensen and similar cases for their broad view of special relationships; and that later decisions could not supply notice. A footnote states that "creation" of a danger is not a second DeShaney exception but an alternate framework of direct causation, which should not be stretched beyond immediate interactions between officer and plaintiff. Part V gives policy reasons why a broad right to affirmative protection was not clearly established. Judge Motz concurred to state that the sole holding is qualified immunity, because after DeShaney a reasonable officer in 1989 could not have known that Johnson's promise created a special relationship, and that Part V's suggestion that there is not and should not be a broad right to affirmative protection is dicta she considers erroneous. Judge Hamilton, joined by Judge Luttig, concurred only in the judgment: given DeShaney and decisions confining the duty to custody, a reasonable officer would not have known that the assurances and later actions would violate due process. Judge Russell, joined by Chief Judge Ervin and Judges Murnaghan and Michael, dissented. In his view, pre-1989 decisions including Bowers v. DeVito, Archie v. City of Racine, Wells v. Walker, Ketchum v. Alameda County, Escamilla v. City of Santa Ana and Jensen clearly established a duty where the State creates a danger or renders a person more vulnerable to it, and DeShaney held only that no duty arises where the State played no part in creating the danger. Anderson does not require a precise prior holding. Johnson's assurances, his statement about the commissioner and his charging decision placed the family in danger and required at least a warning of Pittman's release. The dissent accepts that DeShaney rejected a special relationship based on knowledge and expressed willingness to protect, and relies on Jensen only for its danger-creation statement.

**Positions reflected below.** Pinder argues first that Johnson's express assurances, given with knowledge of Pittman's threats and history and relied on by her, created a special relationship giving rise to a due-process duty of protection. Separately, she argues that his own acts, the assurances and the decision to charge only minor offenses, created or enhanced the danger, so that her claim concerns affirmative conduct rather than an omission and falls outside DeShaney's rule. She relies heavily on Jensen and on other decisions recognizing affirmative duties outside custody, and also cites circuit decisions issued after March 1989. Johnson argues that DeShaney confines the affirmative duty to situations in which the State has restrained the victim's own freedom to act, and that neither Pinder nor her children were in custody. He argues that neither his awareness of the danger nor his arrest of Pittman created it, and that his assurances do not trigger a duty because DeShaney rejects one based on knowledge and expressions of intent to help. At minimum, he argues, given DeShaney's limits, any such right was not clearly established in March 1989.

Assigned §1254(1) review of the en banc interlocutory qualified-immunity judgment. Section 1983 supplies the action. Johnson v. Jones permits a legal immunity question on assumed facts but not appellate resolution of disputed factual sufficiency. The critical notice date is March 10, 1989. Only the substantive-due-process claim against Johnson individually is presented. The panel dissent noted that the official-capacity claim was not before the court of appeals. Neither appellate opinion addresses the equal-protection count, the §1985 claims, the state-law counts or the claims against the Commissioners, and none is presented. Lawful dispositions include affirmance because any right was not clearly established on that date, with or without deciding the constitutional question; affirmance because no due-process violation is alleged; and reversal or vacatur, returning the claim for further proceedings, if the alleged conduct violated clearly established law. The two theories may be resolved differently. No liability, damages or factual finding can be entered in this review.

DeShaney, decided in February 1989 shortly before the conduct, is surviving pre-divergence authority. It holds that, as a general matter, a State's failure to protect an individual against private violence does not violate the Due Process Clause. It locates the affirmative duty in custodial restraint: the duty "arises not from the State's knowledge of the individual's predicament or from its expressions of intent to help him, but from the limitation which it has imposed on his freedom to act on his own behalf." It rejected the argument that a special relationship arose because the State knew the child faced a special danger and proclaimed its intention to protect him. It also observed that, although the State may have been aware of the dangers, it "played no part in their creation, nor did it do anything to render him any more vulnerable to them." Simulated Collins v. City of Harker Heights (February 26, 1992) reads DeShaney as "leaving distinct custody and affirmative-deprivation questions" and holds that voluntary public employment is not the custody that supports affirmative duties of care. Under the Standards and Tests provision "Voluntary public employment and substantive workplace safety duties" (present operation effective February 26, 1992), a properly alleged affirmative-danger claim remains unresolved; Collins creates neither a general affirmative-danger test nor a categorical rejection of one. Collins postdates the conduct and supplies no notice for March 1989. Open in this review: whether assurances and reliance of the kind alleged can support a duty after DeShaney's special-relationship ruling; whether state conduct outside custody that creates or increases a danger gives rise to a due-process duty, and whether the alleged conduct is such conduct or an omission; and whether any such duty was clearly established on March 10, 1989.

Simulated Elder requires independent review using all relevant precedent, including authority not cited below, and bars later decisions from supplying notice retroactively; it preserves Anderson v. Creighton's particularized objective inquiry. Anderson (1987) predates the conduct. Under it, the right's contours must be "sufficiently clear that a reasonable official would understand that what he is doing violates that right"; the very action need not previously have been held unlawful, but in light of pre-existing law its unlawfulness must be apparent. Albright's simulated judgment supplies no controlling general bar to a bodily-security claim. No mandatory merits-first immunity sequence is inherited, and Love v. Pepersack (below) adds that there is no immunity-first duty either.

**Qualified-immunity legal appeals and factual sufficiency.** Immediate review may address the separable legal question whether conduct on the relevant assumed facts violates clearly established law, including the legality of a categorical direct-evidence condition. It may not determine only whether pretrial evidence creates a genuine dispute about the official’s participation, or choose between disputed motive, causation or factual accounts. A legal appeal uses the district court’s assumed facts; if not stated, the appellate court may identify likely assumptions favorable to the nonmovant without deciding truth or reweighing sufficiency. An abstract legal contention does not bring the factual dispute within immediate review.

#### Effective-date and record checks

Current-term rulings entered before this event:

- **Doe v. Taylor Independent School District (October 16, 1995).** Part I (nine Justices) limits immediate immunity review to separable legal questions on properly assumed facts. Part II (five Justices) holds that a school supervisor's knowing, causally effective acquiescence in a teacher's sexual abuse violates the pupil's bodily-integrity right, and that preconduct law gave notice of that wrong. Part III (eight Justices) holds the superintendent immune because conduct-time law did not clearly establish that his particular conduct was unlawful; a broad duty to protect children does not answer the particularized notice inquiry. Use Doe within its state-actor abuse and individual-responsibility scope; it is not a ruling on protection against a private actor, and it supplies no notice for March 1989 conduct.
- **Hodge v. Jones (October 23, 1995).** O'Connor's Part I (eight Justices) holds officials immune because the specific duties asserted were not clearly established at the 1989–1990 conduct date, applying Elder to particularized historical notice and reserving the constitutional merits; a general interest in family autonomy or privacy does not itself supply that notice.
- **Love v. Pepersack (December 11, 1995).** Part I (eight Justices) states that Doe and Hodge permit notice-based immunity on properly presented claims without first deciding the constitutional merits, and that the Court creates neither a merits-first nor an immunity-first duty.
- **Behrens v. Pelletier (February 21, 1996).** Part II (nine Justices) holds that an authorized immunity appeal decides legal questions on properly assumed or sufficiently supported facts, without choosing between factual accounts.

The Records reviewed, through March 18, 1996, show no other entered ruling on these issues; any later decision effective before May 21, 1996 is checked at the pre-event refresh.

Record discrepancies:

- **Procedural vehicle.** The district court applied the Rule 12(b)(6) standard to a motion to dismiss; the panel and en banc opinions describe a summary-judgment motion.
- **Pittman's earlier offense.** The en banc majority says he had just been released from prison after an attempted-arson conviction, and the dissent that he had just finished serving his sentence. The district court says he was on probation after an arson conviction for trying to burn the house (eighteen months, twelve suspended, followed by three years' supervision).
- **Statement about the commissioner.** The en banc majority says Johnson told Pinder she had to wait until the next day to swear out a warrant because no commissioner would be available before morning; the dissent says he indicated that Pittman could not be released that night for that reason. The district court and panel opinions do not mention the statement.
- **Threat wording.** The en banc majority reports a threat to murder Pinder and the children; the district court and the dissent quote, in differing words, a statement that if he went to jail this time it would be for murder.
