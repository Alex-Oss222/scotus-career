## OT1995-065 — Doctor’s Associates, Inc. v. Casarotto

**Event:** 1996-05-20 | **Category:** MERITS | **Action:** Argued merits decision

**Citation:** 517 U.S. 681

**Docket(s):** No. 95-559

### SECTION I — NEUTRAL MODELING PACKET

- **Case Name:** Doctor’s Associates, Inc. v. Casarotto
- **Citation / Docket:** 517 U.S. 681; No. 95-559
- **October Term:** 1995
- **Entry Stage / Event Type:** Granted merits review; decision pending in this simulation.
- **Question Presented:** Whether the Federal Arbitration Act preempts a Montana law making arbitration clauses unenforceable unless specially noticed on the first page of the contract. As framed in the petition: whether Section 2 of the Act, 9 U.S.C. §2—which makes written agreements to arbitrate “valid, irrevocable, and enforceable, save upon such grounds as exist at law or in equity for the revocation of any contract”—“preempts a state law that makes agreements to arbitrate, but not other agreements, unenforceable unless a specific notice of the arbitration agreement is placed on the first page of the contract.”
- **Established review stage:** The inventory supplies argued merits review. The questions stated above supply the described review scope; no additional question is inferred.
- **Legal Area:** Arbitration — FAA §2 preemption of a state arbitration-notice requirement
- **Court and Judgment Under Review:** Supreme Court of Montana, *Casarotto v. Lombardi*, 274 Mont. 3, 901 P.2d 596 (decided August 31, 1995), reaffirming and reinstating its December 15, 1994 decision, 268 Mont. 369, 886 P.2d 931, and remanding to the district court for further proceedings. The 1994 lower-state decision, by a 4–3 vote, reversed the district court’s order staying the claims against Doctor’s Associates, Inc. (DAI) and Nick Lombardi pending arbitration. On the first issue, it held under Montana conflict-of-laws principles (Restatement (Second) of Conflict of Laws §§187(2), 188) that Montana law governs despite the agreement’s Connecticut choice-of-law clause: absent an effective choice, Montana law would apply because Montana has a materially greater interest in the issue, and applying Connecticut law would be contrary to a fundamental Montana public policy established by the notice requirement of Mont. Code Ann. §27-5-114. The court derived that policy from legislative history reflecting concern that Montanans not unknowingly waive their right of access to Montana courts or be compelled to arbitrate at distant locations, and from what it described as the legislature’s determination that citizens are entitled to notice before agreeing to a procedure “as potentially inconvenient, expensive, and devoid of procedural safeguards” as arbitration under American Arbitration Association (AAA) rules, together with the terms of this contract (a hearing in Connecticut, with costs shared equally). On the second issue, it held that, under *Volt*, the inquiry is whether §27-5-114(4) would “undermine the goals and policies of the FAA,” and that it does not, because the FAA does not require parties to arbitrate when they have not agreed to do so and the requirement does not preclude parties from knowingly agreeing to arbitrate; it did not treat as controlling that *Volt* applied state rules the parties had chosen, whereas Montana law applies here under conflict-of-laws principles. It therefore held §27-5-114(4) not preempted by 9 U.S.C. §2 and concluded that, because the agreement did not comply with the notice requirement, it is not subject to arbitration under Montana law. Justice Weber and Justice Gray, each joined by Chief Justice Turnage, dissented in 1994 on both issues. Each concluded that §27-5-114(4), which by its terms addresses notice that a contract is subject to arbitration “pursuant to this chapter,” does not reach this agreement, which selects Connecticut law, and therefore cannot supply a fundamental policy overriding the parties’ choice of Connecticut law; each also concluded that the requirement is in any event preempted. Justice Gray distinguished *Volt* because the parties there had chosen California arbitration law and the California stay provision did not render their agreement unenforceable, and she would have affirmed the stay. The four-Justice majority reasoned that, under *Volt*, state law is preempted only to the extent it stands as an obstacle to the FAA’s purposes; that a rule requiring only that parties be informed before they agree to arbitrate does not undermine those purposes; and that *Allied-Bruce* concerned a statute invalidating predispute arbitration agreements and the Act’s commerce coverage, not such a notice requirement. It stated that its 1994 decision had assumed that the transaction involved interstate commerce and that any state law frustrating the FAA’s purposes would be preempted. Justice Leaphart, specially concurring, added that the notice rule is part of Montana’s Uniform Arbitration Act, which specifically recognizes arbitration agreements, and that it furthers consensual arbitration by helping ensure that a consumer who signs a nonnegotiated form contract knowingly agrees to arbitrate. Justice Gray, joined by Chief Justice Turnage and Justice Weber, dissented. She objected that the majority decided the remand without acting on defendants’ request to brief and argue it; she read *Allied-Bruce*’s reaffirmation of *Southland* as bearing on the case and rejected the majority’s reading of *Volt* as a retrenchment from *Southland*; and, reinstating her earlier dissent, she concluded that the notice statute is preempted because its application renders the parties’ agreement to arbitrate unenforceable.
- **Requested Supreme Court Disposition / Relief:** Petitioners Doctor’s Associates, Inc. and Nick Lombardi seek reversal of the Montana Supreme Court’s judgment refusing enforcement of the arbitration clause; respondents Paul and Pamela Casarotto seek preservation of the judgment. Petitioners’ position (petition): §2’s savings clause permits only grounds that exist “for the revocation of any contract,” so a State may not condition enforcement of a covered arbitration agreement on a requirement that applies to arbitration agreements but not to other contracts. They rely on *Southland*, *Perry* (482 U.S. at 493 n.9: a state-law principle that “takes its meaning precisely from the fact that a contract to arbitrate is at issue” does not comport with §2), *Moses H. Cone Memorial Hospital v. Mercury Construction Corp.*, 460 U.S. 1, and their reading of *Allied-Bruce* as reaffirming that rule. They argue that *Volt* does not support the judgment, because there the parties chose California’s arbitration rules and the stay provision addressed a procedural matter without rendering the agreement unenforceable, whereas here the court applied Montana law over the parties’ choice of Connecticut law and refused enforcement; that Montana’s notice requirement departs from the 1955 Uniform Arbitration Act, which contains none; and that varying state notice and placement rules would subject multistate businesses to inconsistent conditions on enforcement. They acknowledge that States may apply general contract principles such as those against unconscionable agreements, but contend that a State may not single out arbitration agreements out of concern that those agreements in particular reflect unequal bargaining power. No respondent filing in this Court is among the packet sources. In the Montana courts the Casarottos argued that Montana law applies notwithstanding the choice-of-law clause, that the arbitration provision is unenforceable under §27-5-114(4) for lack of first-page notice, that *Southland* and *Perry* must be considered in light of *Volt*, and that earlier Montana decisions enforcing arbitration agreements did not involve agreements violating Montana statutory law; they relied on Texas and Indiana appellate decisions holding state notice provisions not preempted. The Montana majority and Justice Leaphart’s concurrence, described above, state the grounds on which the judgment rests: under *Volt*, preemption reaches state law only insofar as it obstructs the FAA’s purposes; a requirement that parties be informed before agreeing to arbitrate, contained in an act that otherwise declares arbitration agreements valid and enforceable, does not obstruct and instead furthers consensual arbitration; and *Allied-Bruce* addressed categorical invalidation and commerce coverage rather than a notice rule. Any decision must stay within the presented issues and source-authorized remedy.
- **Material Facts and Record:** DAI, the national franchisor of Subway sandwich shops, entered into a standard franchise agreement dated April 25, 1988, for a Subway shop in Great Falls, Montana; Lombardi was DAI’s development agent in Montana. The Montana Supreme Court’s opinions describe Paul and Pamela Casarotto as parties to the agreement; the district court’s order identifies Paul Casarotto as the franchisee, and the petition states that only he executed the agreement and asserts the claims against DAI and Lombardi, while noting that the opinions below treated both plaintiffs alike. Paragraph 10(c), on page nine, provides that “[a]ny controversy or claim arising out of or relating to this contract or the breach thereof shall be settled by Arbitration in accordance with the Commercial Arbitration Rules of the American Arbitration Association at a hearing to be held in Bridgeport, Connecticut”; it makes commencement of arbitration “a condition precedent to the commencement of legal action by either party” and provides that the cost of the proceeding “will be borne equally by the parties.” Paragraph 12 provides that the agreement “shall be governed by and construed in accordance with the laws of the State of Connecticut.” Mont. Code Ann. §27-5-114(4), part of Montana’s Uniform Arbitration Act, provides: “Notice that a contract is subject to arbitration pursuant to this chapter shall be typed in underlined capital letters on the first page of the contract; and unless such notice is displayed thereon, the contract may not be subject to arbitration.” Subsections (1) and (2) of the same section make written agreements to arbitrate existing and future controversies valid and enforceable except upon such grounds as exist at law or in equity for the revocation of a contract, subject to listed exclusions from subsection (2). The first page of the agreement carried no notice that the contract was subject to arbitration. As alleged in the amended complaint (which the Montana Supreme Court presumed true for its review), the Casarottos agreed to open at a less desirable location after Lombardi told them their preferred location was unavailable and orally promised them the exclusive right to open there when it became available; the location was instead awarded to another franchisee, and they lost their business. They sued DAI, Lombardi and other defendants in the Montana Eighth Judicial District Court, Cascade County, asserting breach of contract, fraud, breach of the covenant of good faith and fair dealing, and other tort and statutory claims. DAI and Lombardi moved to dismiss or to stay proceedings pending arbitration under 9 U.S.C. §3, contending that Montana law could not bar enforcement because the agreement selected Connecticut law and because the FAA preempts Montana law; DAI also filed a demand for arbitration. The Casarottos opposed on the grounds that Montana law applied notwithstanding the choice-of-law clause and that the arbitration provision was unenforceable under §27-5-114(4). On June 2, 1993, the district court found that the agreement concerned interstate commerce, that the claims against DAI and Lombardi arise out of and relate to the agreement and are referable to arbitration, and that DAI had demanded arbitration, and it stayed those claims under 9 U.S.C. §3 until arbitration was had; the stay did not reach other defendants who were not parties to the agreement. On July 8, 1993, it certified that order as final under Montana Rule of Civil Procedure 54(b). Paul Casarotto filed an affidavit in the district court stating that he read the agreement before signing it but that no one told or explained to him that it contained an arbitration clause or a Connecticut choice-of-law provision; petitioners state that DAI’s franchise offering circular disclosed that the agreement contained a binding arbitration agreement. The Montana Supreme Court’s ruling rests on the absence of the statutory first-page notice, and the sources show no finding on the Casarottos’ actual knowledge of the clause.
- **Preservation / Threshold / Vehicle Issues:** The FAA preemption question was raised in the district court and decided against petitioners by the Montana Supreme Court in 1994 and again on remand in 1995. The Montana Supreme Court’s rulings that Montana law governs notwithstanding the Connecticut choice-of-law clause, and that §27-5-114(4) applies to this agreement, rest on Montana law (the 1994 dissenters disputed both); the petition states that, because it contends the statute is preempted, the Court need not decide whether the conflict-of-laws analysis was independently flawed. The district court’s findings that the agreement involves interstate commerce, that the claims against DAI and Lombardi fall within the clause, and that DAI demanded arbitration were not disturbed; the Montana Supreme Court stated that it assumed the transaction involved interstate commerce. The judgment under review reverses a stay pending arbitration that the district court certified under Montana Rule 54(b) and remands for further proceedings; petitioners invoke 28 U.S.C. §1257(a). Claims against defendants who are not parties to the agreement (Daniel L. and Deb Hudson and D&D Subway Corporation) are outside the stay. Otherwise none identified beyond ordinary preservation, finality, and remedy questions.
- **Relevant Dates:** Franchise agreement April 25, 1988; district-court stay order June 2, 1993 (Rule 54(b) certification July 8, 1993); Montana Supreme Court decisions December 15, 1994 and August 31, 1995; certiorari petition dated September 29, 1995; argument April 16, 1996; decision May 20, 1996.

- **Conflict / Percolation:** Petitioners assert a direct conflict with decisions holding state arbitration-notice or disclosure requirements preempted: *David L. Threlkeld & Co. v. Metallgesellschaft Ltd. (London)*, 923 F.2d 245 (2d Cir. 1991) (Vermont acknowledgment statute; petitioners note that the governing FAA provision there was 9 U.S.C. §202 because the agreement involved international commerce); *Securities Industry Ass’n v. Connolly*, 883 F.2d 1114 (1st Cir. 1989) (Massachusetts disclosure regulations); *Webb v. R. Rowland & Co.*, 800 F.2d 803 (8th Cir. 1986) (Missouri notice statute); *Collins Radio Co. v. Perryville Feed & Produce, Inc.*, 685 S.W.2d 837 (Mo. 1985) (en banc) (Missouri notice statute). They also cite *Snap-On Tools Corp. v. Vetter*, 838 F. Supp. 468 (D. Mont. 1993), as holding the Montana statute preempted, and *Saturn Distribution Corp. v. Williams*, 905 F.2d 719 (4th Cir.), which addressed a different Virginia restriction, as rejecting a similar consent-based argument. Below, DAI relied on *Threlkeld*, *Connolly*, *Webb* and *Bunge*, while the Casarottos relied on *American Physicians Service Group, Inc. v. Port Lavaca Clinic Associates*, 843 S.W.2d 675 (Tex. Ct. App. 1992), and *Albright v. Edward D. Jones & Co.*, 571 N.E.2d 1329 (Ind. Ct. App. 1991), as post-*Volt* decisions holding state notice provisions not preempted. The Montana Supreme Court found none of the decisions cited on either side persuasive because they preceded *Volt* or contained little or no reference to it. Petitioners also identify arbitration-specific notice, placement or acknowledgment requirements in other States, including Missouri, South Carolina and Vermont.

**Operative Standards and Tests provision (S2):** Arbitration — *Actual commerce and state-court enforcement of arbitration agreements*. Governing question: Coverage and enforceability under FAA §2. Current rule: Section 2 governs covered written arbitration agreements in state as well as federal court and makes them valid, irrevocable and enforceable except on grounds existing at law or equity for revocation of any contract. A State may not single out predispute arbitration for categorical disability. “Involving commerce” reaches transactions within Congress’s full constitutional commerce power, determined by actual interstate commercial operation rather than the parties’ contemplation of substantial interstate activity. Present operation effective: 1995-01-18; component authorities: Allied-Bruce, “Section 2 continues to govern covered agreements in state court” and “Coverage turns on actual commerce within Congress's power” (Opinion of the Court, seven Justices). Exceptions and limits: Remote interstate history alone is not declared conclusive and the outer constitutional ceiling is reserved. Formation, consent, fraud, duress, unconscionability, severability and other ordinary contract questions retain their actual requirements and preservation limits; coverage alone orders no universal party-by-party arbitration.

**Allied-Bruce**

**Holding:** FAA §2 governs covered written arbitration agreements in state as well as federal court, making them valid, irrevocable and enforceable save upon grounds existing at law or equity for revocation of any contract. The Court retains Southland's state-court holding; Alabama may apply generally applicable contract defenses but may not deny enforcement solely because its law singles out predispute arbitration agreements for a categorical disability.

**Proposition-level authority:** Opinion of the Court (Breyer), joined by Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Kennedy, Souter and Ginsburg: seven Justices.

**Treatment of earlier authority:**

- Southland Corp. v. Keating, 465 U.S. 1: retains the substantive state-court application of §2 against the express request to overrule it; its commerce and general-contract-defense conditions remain.
- Perry v. Thomas, 482 U.S. 483: applies its rejection of a state judicial-forum exception directed at arbitration while preserving generally applicable contract defenses.
- Volt Information Sciences, Inc. v. Board of Trustees of Leland Stanford Junior University, 489 U.S. 468: preserves enforcement of an actual contractual choice of state arbitration rules; a forum-wide hostility to arbitration is not itself that choice.

**Holding:** Section 2's phrase involving commerce reaches contracts evidencing transactions within the full commerce authority Congress constitutionally possesses, and coverage turns on the transaction's actual interstate commercial operation rather than whether the parties contemplated substantial interstate activity when contracting. The multistate service operations and interstate procurement of treatment and repair materials establish the necessary commerce here, requiring reversal of Alabama's coverage ruling; this statutory construction does not define the outer constitutional boundary or make remote interstate history alone conclusive.

**Proposition-level authority:** Opinion of the Court (Breyer), joined by Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Kennedy, Souter and Ginsburg: seven Justices.

**Treatment of earlier authority:**

- Bernhardt v. Polygraphic Co. of America, Inc., 350 U.S. 198: distinguishes a record failing to establish the requisite commerce; its transaction predicate survives, without a newly attributed contemplation requirement.
- Southland, 465 U.S. 1: applies its substantive enforceability rule after identifying the actual covered transaction; general hostility to arbitration does not substitute for the statutory commerce inquiry.

**Limits and questions not reached:** The Alabama Supreme Court judgment is reversed and the cause remanded under the actual-commerce rule. Formation, consent, fraud, duress, unconscionability, severability and other generally applicable contract defenses remain for ordinary adjudication to the extent properly presented and preserved below, together with party assent, clause scope and appropriate implementing procedure. Coverage is not a final order compelling all parties to arbitrate every claim. Section 1's separate employment exclusion is not presented by this service bond. The Court does not decide the tort merits, damages, a particular fraud-in-the-inducement defense, a contractual choice of state rules not established here, or wholesale state-court incorporation of §§3–4.

**Mastrobuono**

**Holding:** A general New York governing-law clause in this agreement does not withdraw punitive authority from its broad arbitration submission: it supplies substantive rights and duties without incorporating Garrity’s special allocation of punitive power exclusively to courts. The punitive award therefore cannot be vacated for that asserted excess of arbitral power, while genuine contractual remedy restrictions and governing substantive limits remain enforceable.

**Proposition-level authority:** Opinion of the Court (Breyer), eight Justices; Thomas does not join.

**Treatment of earlier authority:**

- Volt Information Sciences, Inc. v. Board of Trustees, 489 U.S. 468 — actual selection of state arbitration rules remains enforceable; its accepted contract construction does not dictate this agreement’s meaning.
- Allied-Bruce Terminix Cos. v. Dobson (January 18, 1995) — the FAA’s enforcement command continues to respect assent, scope, and genuine contract restrictions.
- Mitsubishi Motors Corp. v. Soler Chrysler-Plymouth, Inc., 473 U.S. 614 — selection of arbitration does not itself surrender substantive rights.
- Garrity v. Lyle Stuart, Inc., 40 N.Y.2d 354 — its special punitive-forum rule is not incorporated here; its general displacement by federal law is not decided.

**Limits and questions not reached:** FAA §2 makes covered written arbitration agreements valid, irrevocable, and enforceable, subject to generally applicable grounds for revoking any contract. Section 4 requires arbitration in the manner the agreement provides. Section 10(a)(4) permits vacatur when arbitrators exceed their powers or so imperfectly execute them that no mutual, final, and definite award on the submitted subject is made; this case concerns the former ground. The agreement selects New York law and submits controversies arising from the accounts, transactions, agreement, or its breach to arbitration unless federal or state law makes arbitration unenforceable. It selects the then-effective NASD, NYSE, and/or AMEX rules at the customer’s election; if the customer fails to elect by registered mail within five days after demand, Shearson may elect. Future federal-securities disputes are excluded only to the extent the law makes their compelled arbitration impermissible. Award entry requires a court with jurisdiction. No term expressly names a punitive-damages exclusion. NASD Code §41(e) specifies award contents; it does not itself create every remedy. The Arbitrator’s Manual is explanatory context, not automatically incorporated superior law. Rule 21(f)(5) limits (f)(1)–(4) to new agreements signed by existing or new customers after September 7, 1989. No qualifying later agreement is established; Rule 21(f)(4) has no operative application to this 1985 agreement.
