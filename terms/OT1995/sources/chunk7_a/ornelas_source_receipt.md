## Verified facts, preservation and remedial constraints

The first appeal confirms:

- The Government did not challenge either defendant's right to object to the vehicle search and did not ask the court to distinguish driver from passenger.
- The Government conceded that a reasonable person in their position would not have felt free to leave. It did not argue that either the stop or the consent authorized dismantling the car. The panel therefore treated the intrusive search as dependent on probable cause.
- The record contained no evidence establishing NADDIS reliability; the panel treated its entries as no more reliable than an informant not known to be reliable. The two entries and their purported mutual corroboration were part of the lower court's analysis, not a finding that the named persons actually were petitioners. The opinion explicitly says the two suspected dealers were neither defendant and, as far as the panel knew, were unrelated to them.
- The first appeal applied clear-error review to reasonable suspicion based on Spears, which had adopted that standard for probable cause. It expressly called the stop question close.
- The magistrate judge disbelieved the rusty-screw testimony. The district judge relied on loose-panel testimony without resolving its credibility. The first appeal held that the credibility question could not be ignored, while leaving open the general question whether a district judge may reject a magistrate's credibility finding without hearing the witness.
- The Government waived inevitable discovery by failing to defend it against the opening brief's challenge. The remand was to resolve loose-panel credibility and, if credited, decide whether the panel, NADDIS information and other circumstances supplied probable cause. That first remand did not decide the eventual reasonable-suspicion/probable-cause standard at the Supreme Court.

The distinct questions are historical factfinding, the ultimate application of reasonable suspicion and probable cause, inference based on officer experience, and the warrant preference. - Independent review can distinguish historical facts and supported credibility findings from the ultimate constitutional application, relying on law development, uniformity, appellate guidance and the objective nature of the tests.
