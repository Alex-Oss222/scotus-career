## OT1995-064 — Henderson v. United States

**Event:** 1996-05-20 | **Category:** MERITS | **Action:** Argued merits decision

**Citation:** 517 U.S. 654

**Docket(s):** No. 95-232

### SECTION I — NEUTRAL MODELING PACKET

- **Case Name:** Henderson v. United States
- **Citation / Docket:** 517 U.S. 654; No. 95-232
- **October Term:** 1995
- **Entry Stage / Event Type:** Granted merits review; decision pending in this simulation.
- **Question Presented:** Whether Federal Rule of Civil Procedure 4’s period for service (120 days after filing, subject to a showing of good cause, Fed. R. Civ. P. 4(j) (1988)) supersedes the Suits in Admiralty Act’s direction that the libelant “shall forthwith serve” the United States, 46 U.S.C. App. §742, or whether compliance with that “forthwith” requirement is a condition of the Act’s waiver of sovereign immunity and therefore a jurisdictional prerequisite the Federal Rules cannot alter. The petition also asks, if Rule 4 does not supplant the requirement, whether district courts may extend the time for service the Act provides in appropriate circumstances; the United States’ merits brief adds whether the district court correctly dismissed the complaint for failure to serve “forthwith.”
- **Established review stage:** The inventory supplies argued merits review. The questions stated above supply the described review scope; no additional question is inferred.
- **Legal Area:** Federal procedure — service of process and suits against the United States
- **Court and Judgment Under Review:** United States Court of Appeals for the Fifth Circuit, *Henderson v. United States*, 51 F.3d 574 (5th Cir. 1995) (per curiam; No. 94-40697; judgment May 10, 1995), affirming the Eastern District of Texas’s June 16, 1994 order dismissing Henderson’s complaint without prejudice for lack of subject-matter jurisdiction under Fed. R. Civ. P. 12(b)(1). Following *United States v. Holmberg*, 19 F.3d 1062 (5th Cir. 1994), the court held that §742’s forthwith requirement is a condition of the Government’s waiver of sovereign immunity and therefore a jurisdictional prerequisite that Rule 4(j) does not supersede, so the district court’s fifteen-day extension did not affect jurisdiction or Henderson’s §742 obligations; that, even read for reasonableness and even assuming good cause for the delay, completing service in 148 days is not forthwith; and that “forthwith” applies both to service on the United States Attorney and to the mailing to the Attorney General, so service on the Attorney General alone could not satisfy §742. It did not decide whether the 47-day service on the Attorney General was forthwith, and it did not reach Henderson’s due-process argument because the dismissal was without prejudice and he did not argue that a new suit would be time-barred.
- **Requested Supreme Court Disposition / Relief:** Henderson asks the Court to reverse the Fifth Circuit’s judgment and remand for trial on the merits, holding that Rule 4’s 120-day period supplants §742’s forthwith requirement or, alternatively, that district courts may extend the time for §742 service in appropriate circumstances; the United States asks that the judgment be affirmed. Any decision must stay within the presented issues and source-authorized remedy.
- **Material Facts and Record:** Lloyd Henderson, a merchant seaman, alleges he was injured on or about August 27, 1991 (the Government’s district-court memorandum says August 29), aboard the M/V DELMONTE, a vessel owned by the United States through the Maritime Administration. After his administrative claim under the Clarification Act, 50 U.S.C. App. §1291(a), was disallowed, he sued the United States on April 8, 1993, in the Southern District of Texas, Galveston Division, invoking the Suits in Admiralty Act and the Public Vessels Act. The Government maintains that the claim lies only under the Suits in Admiralty Act and that the forthwith requirement applies to Public Vessels Act suits in any event through 46 U.S.C. App. §782. Henderson’s counsel attributes the delay in service to summons forms the clerk’s office was slow to supply, a summons issued without the court’s seal, and replacement forms lost in the mail. The Attorney General received the summons and complaint by certified mail on May 25, 1993, 47 days after filing. Rule 4(j)’s 120 days ran on August 6, 1993. Henderson received properly sealed summonses on August 25, 1993, and places the end of §745’s two-year period on August 27, 1993. On August 30 (day 144) he moved to postpone the scheduling conference because the United States had not been served. By order dated August 31 and filed September 1, 1993, the court granted the motion and ordered him to serve the United States within fifteen days; the order made no express good-cause finding, and Henderson has treated it as an implied finding of good cause and diligence. The United States Attorney was personally served on September 3, 1993, 148 days after filing. The case was then transferred, on the Government’s motion, to the Eastern District of Texas, Beaumont Division. The United States moved to dismiss under Rule 12(b)(1) on the ground that service was not “forthwith” under §742; its motions and briefs rest on §742 alone and do not contend that service failed Rule 4, and its district-court memoranda state that Henderson “properly effected service” on September 3, 1993, which they, like the district court’s dismissal order, count as 150 days after filing. The district court denied the motion on January 21, 1994. After *Holmberg*, it granted the Government’s renewed motion and dismissed without prejudice on June 16, 1994.
- **Parties’ Positions:** Henderson argues that §742’s forthwith requirement is a procedural provision adopted in 1920, before uniform federal rules existed; that the Civil Rules were extended to admiralty cases in 1966; that §2072(b) makes laws in conflict with the Rules “of no further force or effect,” and Congress itself enacted Rule 4’s 120-day period and good-cause exception (Federal Rules of Civil Procedure Amendments Act of 1982, Pub. L. 97-462, 96 Stat. 2527); that service is a procedural matter under *Hanna v. Plumer*, 380 U.S. 460 (1965); that §743 directs that suits proceed under the rules of practice obtaining between private parties; and that a jurisdictional reading defeats the Act’s waiver and yields inconsistent rulings on what “forthwith” means. In reply, he argues that the provisions conflict because Rule 4(j) allows more time than courts have allowed under “forthwith” and lets a court extend the time for good cause, while under §742 a court must dismiss once it finds that service was not forthwith; and that, under *Mississippi Publishing Corp. v. Murphree*, 326 U.S. 438 (1946), a rule governing the means of bringing a defendant before a court that already has subject-matter jurisdiction neither extends that jurisdiction contrary to Rule 82 nor abridges substantive rights contrary to §2072(b). Alternatively, he argues that a district court may extend the time for §742 service in appropriate circumstances, relying by analogy on *American Pipe & Construction Co. v. Utah*, 414 U.S. 538 (1974), and on the Fifth Circuit’s holding in *McCormick v. United States*, 680 F.2d 345 (5th Cir. 1982), that §745’s two-year limitation can be tolled in appropriate circumstances, and that, given the clerk’s-office and mail problems he encountered, the extension granted here should be honored; his reply adds, as a further alternative, that Rule 4 should be read with §742 so that the district court judges whether service was “forthwith” by Rule 4’s standard. The United States argues that §742 and Rule 4(j) can both be given effect, because Rule 4(j) sets only an outer limit and confers no affirmative right to take 120 days; that *Hanna* is distinguishable because it addressed whether a state procedural rule applies in diversity litigation, not whether a federal statute specifically addressed to Suits in Admiralty Act litigation should be enforced, and appears to have read the Rule there as conferring an affirmative right to serve process in the specified ways; that §743’s general direction yields to §742’s specific service command, which the legislative history ties to a need for prompt admiralty litigation; that, in any event, forthwith service is a term of the United States’ consent to be sued and so a jurisdictional condition that §2072(b) (the Rules “shall not abridge, enlarge or modify any substantive right”) and Rule 82 forbid the Rules to relax (citing *United States v. Sherwood*, 312 U.S. 584 (1941), and *United States v. Mitchell*, 445 U.S. 535 (1980)); that “forthwith” means immediate, prompt action with reasonable dispatch; that, even accepting Henderson’s declarations, his counsel made no effort to serve immediately, because counsel did not obtain or complete summons forms before filing and apparently did not file by hand, and the clerk’s-office mistakes that worsened the delay arose only after the forthwith obligation had already been breached, so the dismissal was correct; and that limitations-tolling principles are of little relevance to the requirement. Because Congress itself enacted Rule 4(j), the United States calls §2072(b)’s reference to “such rules” technically inapplicable to it, but agrees with Henderson that §2072(b) provides the best evidence of congressional intent on how the Rule interacts with other laws. Henderson replies that nothing in the rules forbids requesting summons forms by mail and that the clerk’s delay in forwarding the forms caused the delay in service.
- **Preservation / Threshold / Vehicle Issues:** Whether §742’s requirement is jurisdictional and whether Rule 4(j) supersedes it were raised in both district-court motions and decided below. Henderson’s separate due-process claim was not reached by the Fifth Circuit and is not presented as a question here; his merits brief invokes due-process and equal-protection concerns only in support of applying Rule 4’s uniform 120-day period. No court has decided whether the 47-day service on the Attorney General was forthwith or whether the August 31, 1993 order rested on good cause. Filing came within §745’s two years; personal service on the United States Attorney came after the end of that period as Henderson places it. Henderson’s merits brief states that, although the dismissal was without prejudice, the limitations period had already run when it was entered.
- **Relevant Dates:** Injury on or about August 27, 1991; complaint filed April 8, 1993; service on the Attorney General May 25, 1993 and on the United States Attorney September 3, 1993; dismissal June 16, 1994; Fifth Circuit judgment May 10, 1995; petition filed August 8, 1995; argument March 19, 1996; decision May 20, 1996.
- **Companion or Consolidated Matters:** None.
- **Known Participation Issue:** Stone, Stevens, O’Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg, and Breyer form the event-date roster; no case-specific nonparticipation is established.

#### Additional public case information

- **Conflict / Percolation:** The Fifth Circuit followed *Holmberg* and cited the Second, Ninth and Eleventh Circuits (*Battaglia v. United States*, 303 F.2d 683 (2d Cir.), cert. dismissed, 371 U.S. 907 (1962); *Amella v. United States*, 732 F.2d 711 (9th Cir. 1984); *Libby v. United States*, 840 F.2d 818 (11th Cir. 1988)) as treating the forthwith requirement as jurisdictional, and the Third Circuit’s *Jones & Laughlin Steel, Inc. v. Mon River Towing, Inc.*, 772 F.2d 62, 66 (3d Cir. 1985), as treating it as procedural and superseded by Rule 4(j). Henderson relies on *Jones & Laughlin* as a square conflict; the United States calls its statement dictum because that court found jurisdiction lacking under the Suits in Admiralty Act. Henderson replies that supersession was the question the Third Circuit actually addressed, the Federal Tort Claims Act point not having been raised there, and that it held Rule 4 governs service under both statutes.

**United States v. Nordic**

**Current rule:** Original §106(c) does not unequivocally waive immunity from an affirmative personal money judgment recovering an unauthorized transfer under §550. A plausible reading under which its application-and-binding language governs nonmonetary or claim determinations defeats clear consent to that remedy; legislative history cannot supply missing enacted clarity. Sections 106(a) and (b) retain their own claim-linked and offset operations. General estate-property jurisdiction under §1334(d), an asserted trust interest, and the money's origin in estate assets do not supply the missing consent. An order requiring payment of a sum, rather than adjudicating identified property under the court's control, cannot be preserved by calling it a property proceeding or defensive equitable recoupment.

**Authority by component:**

- Original §106(c) monetary-waiver construction: [United States v. Nordic Village — Original §106(c) does not clearly authorize this monetary recovery](https://github.com/Alex-Oss222/scotus-career/blob/87e62b7612c15409039d524fd66f743d0ad75e2a/terms/OT1991/records/United_States_v_Nordic_Village_merits_1992-02-25.md), February 25, 1992.
- Distinction between jurisdiction and consent; inadequacy of property, trust, and recoupment labels: [Nordic Village — Jurisdictional and property labels do not sustain this money judgment](https://github.com/Alex-Oss222/scotus-career/blob/87e62b7612c15409039d524fd66f743d0ad75e2a/terms/OT1991/records/United_States_v_Nordic_Village_merits_1992-02-25.md), February 25, 1992. **Keene**

**Current rule:** Section 1500 bars a Claims Court action when, at filing, a suit for or in respect to the same claim is pending elsewhere against the United States or a covered federal actor. Later dismissal of the other action does not cure the original defect. Claims arising from substantially the same operative facts and seeking overlapping relief are not separated merely by different legal theories or the inability to plead them in one forum. The Court leaves open the reverse filing order and suits seeking wholly different relief; no general tolling rule follows.

**Authority by component:**

**National Private Truck**

**Holding:** Because the invoked §1983 equitable action affords no relief on this record, §1988 supplies no fee entitlement through that action. Success on state law does not categorically defeat fees where an independently viable fee-eligible federal claim exists.

**Proposition-level authority:** Thomas’s Opinion of the Court; all nine Justices join this proposition.

**Limits and questions not reached:** Maher continues to permit fees through an available fee-eligible action without an express federal merits decision. No independently viable alternative fee-eligible federal claim is established here; reasonable amount, an unpresented damages action and the independent state fee ground remain outside review.

**Holding:** Section 1983 does not authorize injunctive or declaratory interference with state tax administration where an adequate legal remedy actually protects the claimant. The limitation arises from federal equitable principles informing §1983, not direct application of 28 U.S.C. §1341 to state courts, and does not restrict otherwise lawful state-law equitable relief.

**Proposition-level authority:** Thomas’s Opinion of the Court, joined by Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O’Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Ginsburg and Breyer; nine Justices adopt this remedial rule.

**Limits and questions not reached:** Extraordinary relief remains possible for irreparable injury, cloud on title or a genuine risk of numerous suits between the same parties involving the same law or facts. Mere parallel claims by many taxpayers do not suffice. An inadequate remedy, including continued unconstitutional taxation after a refund judgment, may change the equitable inquiry. No general exhaustion rule or withdrawal of a promised refund is authorized; the general state-court duty to entertain section 1983 is assumed, not decided.
