I. RESPONDENT ERRED IN CLAIMING THAT THE RECORD IN THIS CASE 
PROVIDED AN AMPLE FACTUAL BASIS FOR THE PETITIONER'S 


FORFEITURE 


At pages 13-14, Respondent's Brief For The United States In 


Opposition, + Respondent so stated, 


"The record in this case provided an ample factual basis 
for petitioner's forfeiture. Petitioner's plea 
agreement expressly provided for forfeiture and 
specified the assets to be forfeited. Plea agreement 2- 
3. Count 6 of the indictment also specified the 
property to be forfeited and explained why the property 
was forfeitable under 21 U.S.C. 853. Superseding 
Indictment 31-32. At the change of plea hearing, the 
court specifically advised petitioner that ‘all the 
property that's described in Count 6 could be forfeited 
to the United States,' and read aloud the allegations in 
Count 6 specifying the property to be forfeited." 


Respondent then asserts, id. at 14-15, that based on this record, 
petitioner would not be entitled to relief under any standard, 
and therefore, further review is not warranted. 

Because Respondent's claims are based on an erroneous 
factual basis concerning the assets listed in Count 6 of the 
indictment and the assets included in *he Order of Forfeiture, 
Respondent's conclusion that the record in this case provided an 


ample basis for petitioner's forfeiture is erroneous. 


1 Hereinafter RB or "Respondent's Brief." 


A. Because a majority of the property included in the Order of 
Forfeiture was not specified or identified in Count 6 of the 
Indictment as property to be forfeited, Respondent's reliance 
on the Indictment as establishing the factual basis for the 
forfeiture of all of the property in the Order of Forfeiture 


is erroneous. 


Respondent perpetuates the misconception throughout 
Respondent's Brief that all of the property included in the Order 
of Forfeiture was "specifically" listed in the indictment, under 
Count 6, as Rule 7(c)(2) Fed.R.Crim.P. mandates. For example, 
Respondent claims at page 2, id., that Count 6 specified the 
property to be forfeited, and then at footnote 1, id., Respondent 
goes on to claim that all the property was identified by 
description. Respondent also states, id. at 13, "Count 6 of the 
indictment also specified the property to be forfeited and 
explained why the property was forfeitable under the standards of 
21 U.S.C. 853." 

Despite Respondent's assertions to the contrary, a vast 
majority of the property included in the Order of Forfeiture was 
not “specified” in Count 6 of the indictment, as property to be 
forfeited, as Rule 7(c)(2) Fed.R.Crim.P. requires, and no 
explanation as to "why [this unspecified property] was 
forfeitable under the standards of 21 U.S.C. 853" can be found in 
Count 6 of the indictment. The majority of the property included 
in the Order of Forfeiture was not property subject to forfeiture 
"under the standards of 21 U.S.C. 853," and nothing in the record 


supports any claim to the contrary. 


First, two items of real property, identified as two 
condominiums in items (B) and (C) of the Order of Forfeiture, 
were forfeited. These condominiums were neither identified in 
the indictment, nor were these properties subject to forfeiture 
under §853. Respondent misstates this fact at page 2, and at 
page 2 n. 1, when Respondent assumes this real property was 
"specified" in Count 6 of the indictment. 

Second, two childhood bank accounts, which Petitioner was 
unaware of the existence of when he pled guilty, items (U) and 
(V), were also neither “specified” in Count 6 of the indictment, 
nor property subject to forfeiture. Aguin, Respondent misstates 
facts in assuming, and then implying throughout Respondent's 
Brief, that all of the property forfeited was "specified" in the 
indictment. 

Third, Petitioner's employee payroll savings plan, item (NN) 
in the Order of Forfeiture, was neither “specified” in the 
indictment, nor property subject to forfeiture as described in 
§853. This employee payroll savings plan consisted entirely of 
employer deductions from Petitioner's paychecks he earned as an 
accounting supervisor at General Chemical Corporation. It is 
worth noting that this asset was included in the Order of 
Forfeiture as substitute assets under §853(p), and not, as 
Respondent has stated at page 2 RB, “derived from proceeds 
[petitioner] obtained” or were “used and intended for use * * * 
to commit or to facilitate the commission" of the CCE offense. 

Fourth, Petitioner's legal firearms business inventory, and 


personal firearms collection, (items I-P and {2 Order of 


Forfeiture), were neither “specified” in the indictment, nor 
subject to forfeiture under § 853. Respondent assumes throughout 
Respondent's Brief that such property was alleged in the 
indictment as property subject to forfeiture, in accordance with 
Rule 7(c)(2) Fed.R.Crim.P., but such is not so. 

Because Petitioner's condominiums, employee payroll savings 
plan, childhood bank accounts, and legitimately owned and 
acquired firearms business inventory, and personal firearms 
collection, were not specified as property subject to forfeiture 


in Count 6 of the indictment, or listed anywhere in the 


indictment, Respondent's claims that the indictment “specified 
the property to be forfeited and explained why the property was 
forfeitable under the standards of 21 U.S.C. 853" are erroneous, 
and must fail. A fortiori, Respondent's assertion that “the 
record in this case provided an ample factual basis for 
petitioner's forfeiture” must also fail, because this assertion 
is premised on the erroneous assumption that all of the property 
included in the Order of Forfeiture was specified in the 
indictment. 

For the reasons stated above, Respondent's reliance on the 
indictment, and the District Court's Rule 11 advisement, as 
providing “an ample factual basis" for petitioner's forfeiture is 


error. 


B. Because the District Court's advisement regarding the 
property subject to forfeiture did not include all of the 
property included in the Order of Forfeiture, Respondent's 

reliance on the District Court's advisement, as establishing 


the factual basis for Petitioner's forfeiture, is erroneous. 


As stated above, a vast majority of the property ultimately 
included in the Order of Forfeiture was neither “specified” in 
the indictment as assets to be forfeited, nor as property 
described as subject to forfeiture under § 853. Throughout 
Respondent's Brief Respondent repeatedly asserts, incorrectly, 
that the District Court's Rule 11 advisement advised petitioner

was advised, and understood, that only property “that's described 
in Count 6 could be forfeited," 10/5/92 Change of Plea Tr. 7, 18- 
19, and RB., 13-14, and then only if that property was owned by 
petitioner “by reason of any drug transaction,” Change of Plea 
Tr. at 21. 

When the District Court read aloud the allegations in Count 
6 specifying the property alleged to be subject to forfeiture, 
Change of Plea Tr. at 18-19, the District Court recited the list 
of property in Count 6, and informed Petitioner that no other 
property could be forfeited, by saying “and that's it.” Because 
the District Court's advisement as to what property could be 
forfeited did not include petitioner's condominiums, childhood 


bank accounts, employee payroll savings plan, or firearms 
business inventory or personal firearms collection, Respondent's 
reliance on the District Court's advisement as providing “an 


ample factual basis” for petitioner's forfeiture is error. 


c. Neither the indictment, nor the record, provided a factual 


basis for the forfeiture of substitute assets under §853(p) 


Item (NN) in the Order of Forfeiture is Petitioner's 
employee payroll savings plan. The Order of Forfeiture states 
that this asset was “to be forfeited as substitute assets for 
assets dissipated and otherwise expended by Libretti." Order of 
Forfeiture at page 7. Substitute assets forfeited under §853(p) 
are, by definition, not property subject to forfeiture under 
$853(a). 

As stated above, petitioner's employee payroll savings plan 
was not “specified” in Count 6 of the indictment, nor did Count 6 
explain “why the property was forfeitable under the standards of 
21 U.S.C. § 853." RB at 13. Furthermore, Count 6 did not 
specify that this asset was two be furfeited because it was 
"either derived from proceeds [petitioner] obtained" from his 
CCE, or were “used and intended for use * * * to commit or 
facilitate the commission” of the CCE offense. By virtue of the 
employee payroll savings plan's status as substitute assets, it 
is conceded that this asset was not derived from, or used or 
intended to be used, to facilitate the offense. 


Nothing in either the indictment or the record provides a 
factual basis for the forfeiture of substitute assets under 
853(p). No assets were ever identified as tainted property as 
described in § 853(a), yet unavailable for forfeiture “as a 
result of any act or omission of the defendant.” The District 
Court made a similar factual finding in its April 2, 1993 ORDER 
AMENDING DECEMBER 23, 1992 FORFEITURE ORDER, pages 6-7, n. 1, 


after analyzing the issue of substitute assets in this case. 


"However, the government has not met its burden of proof 
that property subject to forfeiture pursuant to §853(p) 
falls into one of the five categories listed above, and 
that substitute assets should be forfeited in lieu of 
these assets.” 


In the Tenth Circuit's ruling, the Tenth Circuit stated, 


"Defendant also alleges that Fed.R.Crim.P. 32(c) (3) (D) 
requires factual findings when a defendant alleges an 
inaccuracy in the presentence report. Because the

11(f).* 


United States v. Libretti, 38 F. 3d 523, 527 n. 5(10th Cir.

It is difficult to see how the District Court could not have 
relied on any alleged inaccuracies in the presentence report, but 
at the same time make the requisite findings necessary to forfeit 
property as substitute assets under §853(p). This is a 
contradiction. Defendant stated in the presentence report that 
he derived no more than $70,000.00 from the offense of 
conviction. The Order of Forfeiture already exceeded 
$400,000.00, when, in addition, Petitioner's employee payroll 


savings plan was forfeited as substitute assets. To forfeit the 


employee payroll savings plan as substitute assets the District 
Court had to rely on an alleged inaccuracy, such inaccuracy being 
the amount of monetary gain Defendant derived from the offense of 
conviction. That is, the Court had to rely on the government's 
disputed claims that Defendant had derived monetary gain in an 
amount exceeding that which Defendant claimed. In addition, the 
District Court would also have had to find that this alleged, but 
unavailable, monetary gain, was not available for forfeiture as a 
result of any act or omission of Defendant, and not unavailable 
for any other reason. The Tenth Circuit was unable to identify 
any facts upon which the District Court could have relied on in 
order to forfeit Petitioner's legitimately earned, nonforfeitable 
employee payroll savings plan, as substitute assets. 

Thus, Respondert's erroneous claims, at pages 2 and 13, RB, 
and permeated throu shout Respondent's Brief, that the forfeiture 
of all of the property included in the Order of Forfeiture was 
supported by facts in either the indictment or the record must 
fail, in light of truth and reason. In light of the fact that 
the above described real and personal property was neither 
"specified" in the indictment, nor property subject to forfeiture 
as described in §853, the Respondent's claims to the contrary 


defy both logic and common sense. 


Il. A FINDING OF A FACTUAL BASIS FOR THE FORFEITURE OF PROPERTY 
UNDER § 853 IS REQUIRED TO PREVENT THE VIOLATION OF THE 
CONSTITUTIONAL PRINCIPLE OF SEPARATION OF POWERS, AND TO 
GUARANTEE THAT THE DEFENDANT'S PUNISHMENT DOES NOT EXCEED 

THE PUNISHMENT AUTHORIZED BY CONGRESS 


As the Supreme Court held in Whalen v. United States, 445 


U.S. 684, 63 L.Ed.2d 715, 100 S.Ct. 1432 (1980), a defendant has 


a right to be deprived of liberty as punis*ment for criminal 


conduct only to the extent, authorized by Congress. Petitioner in 
this case contends that a defendant also has a right to be 


deprived of property as punishment for criminal conduct only to 


the extent authorized by Congress. 

The criminal forfeiture of Petitioner's property was a 
penalty, inflicted as punishment for criminal conduct. RB. at 9. 
The power to prescribe the punishments to be imposed upon those 
found guilty of criminal offenses resides wholly with Congress. 
Whalen, 445 U.S. at 689, 63 L.Ed.2d at 722, 100 S.Ct. 1432. Ifa 
federal court exceeds its own authority by forfeiting property 
under § 853 that is not described as forfeitable under § 853, the 
federal court violates the constitutional] principle of separation 
of powers in a manner that trenches particularly harshly on 
individual liberty and constitutional rights. Federal courts may 
constitutionally impose only such punishments as Congress has 
seen fit to authorize. 

Congress has not authorized the forfeiture of property, 
under § 853, that is not described as forfeitable under § 853. 


In order to prevent the forfeiture of nonforfeitable property, a 
factual basis for a forfeiture must be found to exist. For 
should a court ever forfeit property under § 853 that was not 
subject to forfeiture under § 853, then the defendant's 
constitutional guarantee to be punished only to the extent 
authorized by Congress has been violated. In this case at bar, 
Petitioner's right to be punished only to the extent authorized 
by Congress has been violated. Property not subject to 
forfeiture under § 853 has been forfeited under § 853. The Tenth 
Circuit explained this dichotomy by finding that Petitioner 


"agreed" to the forfeiture, so therefore, the District Court was 


not required to find any factual basis as to whether or not any —- 


of the property, such as Petitioner's employee payroll savings 
plan, was subject to forfeiture under § £53. The Tenth Circuit's 
holding was erroneous. In so ruling, the Tenth Circuit has 
upheld the propriety of any sentencing scheme that exceeds the 
punishment authorized by Congress, so long as the sentence is 
"agreed" to by the defendant, or the result of a “bargained for 
exchange." In allowing the District Court to hand down a 
sentence that exceeds the punishment authorized by Congress, the 
Tenth Circuit ignored the constitutional principle of separation 
of powers. The Constitution does not allow a District Court to 
hand down a sentence that exceeds the punishment authorized by 
Congress, regardless of whether the defendant "“agreed" to the 
excessive punishment, or whether the excessive punishment was the 
resuit of a “bargained for exchange.“ When the District Court 
entered the Order of Forfeiture that resulted in the forfeiture 


10 


of Petitioner's nonforfeitable property, the District Court 
violated both the Constitutional principle of separation of 
powers, and violated Petitioner's constitutional right to be 


punished only to the extent authorized by Congress. 


CONCLUSION 


In cases wher: the forfeiture of property is inflicted as a 
punishment for criminal conduct, a defendant's constitutional 
right to be punished only to the extent authorized by Congress 
can only be guaranteed when a factual basis for the deprivation 
of property is found to exist. If a factual basis for the 
criminal forfeiture of a piece of property is not required to be 


found, then what guarantee does a defendant, being sentenced to a 


forfeiture of property under § 853, have that the forfeiture will 
not include nonforfeitable property, and what remedy would a 
defendant have if a sentencing court erroneously forfeited 
nonforfeitable property? 

For the above stated reasons, neither “he record nor the 
indictment could have provided an “ample factual basis" for the 
forfeiture of over $350,000.00 worth of nonforfeitable property 
which was neither “specified” in the indictment, nor shown in the 
record to be subject to forfeiture. No degree of analysis of the 
record provides “an ample factual basis" for the forfeiture of 
Petitioner's employee payroll savings plan as substitute assets 
under §853(p), or for the forfeiture of any of the other property 


11 


included in the Order of Forfeiture, but not specified in the 
indictment as alleged to be forfeitable, as Rule /7(c) (2) 
Fed.R.Crim.P. requires. Based on the facts and the record, 


further review is warranted.
