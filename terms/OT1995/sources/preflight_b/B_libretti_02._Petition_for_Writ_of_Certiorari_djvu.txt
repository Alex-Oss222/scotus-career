JURISDICTION 
On October 19, 1994, the United States Court of Appeals for 


the Tenth Circuit entered its order and judgment affirming the 
judgment of the United States District Court for the District of 
Wyoming. Jurisdiction in this Court is conferred by 28 U.S.C. § 


1254(1). 


RULES AND STATUTES INVOLVED

Any person convicted of a violation of this 
subchapter . . . punishable by imprisonment 


for more than one year shal] forfeit in the

(1) any property constituting, or 
derived from, any proceeds the person 
obtained, directly or indirectly, as the

(2) any of the person’s property used, 
or intended to be used, in any manner or part,

(3) in the case of a person convicted of

Fed. R. Crim. P. Rule 11(f) states that "(n)jotwithstanding the

STATEMENT OF THE CASE 

In this case, the Tenth Circuit decided two issues of first 
impression that have resulted in splits among the circuits, and 
that have not been addressed by this Court. 

Mr. Libretti pled guilty to one count of continuing criminal 
enterprise (CCE) in violation of 21 U.S.C. § 848. He was sentenced 
to 20 years imprisonment and a fine. In addition, the district 
court entered a criminal forfeiture order pursuant to 21 U.S.C. § 
853(a). 

Petitioner’s plea agreement provided that he would agree to 
criminal forfeiture of his assets under § 853. It stated: 

10. The Defendant agrees to transfer his 
right, title, and interest in all of his 
assets to the Division of Criminal 
Investigation of the Wyoming Attorney General 
including, but not limited to: all real 
estate; all personal property, including guns, 
the ter, and every other item now in the 
possession of the United States; all bank 
accounts, investments, retirement accounts, 


cash, cashier’s checks, travelers checks and 
funds of any kind. 


At the change of plea hearing, the judge explained to the 
petitioner that the forfeiture applied to "the property that is 


owned by you by reason of any drug transaction." The judge then 
advised: 


And then under the topic Forfeiture, [the 
indictment] alleges that upon conviction of 
the defendant for engaging in a continuing 
criminal enterprise, the United States is 
entitled to forfeiture of all property of any 
kind constituting or derived from proceeds 
Libretti obtained directly and indirectly by 
engaging in said continuing criminal 
enterprise; all property of any kind which was 
used or intended for use in any manner or part 
to commit or to facilitate the commission of 
this criminal -- continuing criminal 
enterprise, including but not limited to the 
following, and then there is listed real 
property, your lot in Star Valley [Wyoming], 
conveyances of two vehicles, cash proceeds 
over a hundred thousand dollars and then 
another $12,000 and a five -- 5,100 and 7,600 
amount in currency and jewelry and property 
consisting of a mobile home and a computer 
system, bank accounts that are listed in the 
Indictment, investments that are listed in the 
Indictment, and additional cashier’s checks 
and the contents of safe deposit boxes, and 
that’s it. 


The petitioner was not advised of, and did not waive, his right to 
a jury trial on the issue of which assets were actually subject to 
forfeiture. Likewise, the judge made no findings of any factual 
basis for the forfeiture plea. 

At sentencing, the judge granted the forfeiture order. 
Petitioner’s counsel did not object. The petitioner did, saying "I 
would just like to object to what I see as a failure to find any 


factual basis for the whole forfeiture, just for the record." The 
judge overruled his objection. 

Petitioner filed a notice of appeal from the forfeiture order. 
He filed a pro se Opening Brief and undersigned counsel was 
appointed to file a Supplemental Opening Brief. The key issues 
raised on appeal were (1) whether the district court erred by 
ordering forfeiture of nonforfeitable property and failing to make 
findings of a factual basis for forfeiture; and (2) whether 
petitioner was entitled to be advised, as part of entering his 
guilty plea, that he had the right to a jury trial on the 
forfeiture issue (apart from any trial on the drug offense itself). 

In its decision, the Tenth Circuit noted: “Whether Rule 11(f) 
applies to a stipulated forfeiture in a plea agreement is an issue 
of first impression in this circuit." United States v. Libretti, 
38 F.3d 523, 537 (10th Cir. 1994). The court also noted the 
"divergent conclusions" reached by four other Circuits. Id. at 
528. 

The Seventh Circuit has held that Rule 11(f) applies to 
forfeitures that are included as part of the plea agreement. 
United States v. Roberts, 749 F.2d 404 (7th Cir. 1984), cert. 
denied, 470 U.S. 1058 (1985). 

The Fourth Circuit has held that a defendant’s agreement to 
forfeit an item in the plea agreement does not, without more, make 
the item forfeitable. United States v. Reckmeyer, 786 F.2d 1216, 
1222 (4th Cir.), cert. denied, 479 U.S. 850 (1986) (Rule 11(f) 
requires the district court to inquire beyond the plea agreement to 


determine if there is a factual basis for forfeiture of the 
assets). 

The Fifth Circuit has held that the Rule 11(f) requirement of 
a factual basis for the plea does not apply to a forfeiture order. 
United States v. Bachynsky, 949 F.2d 722, 730 (Sth Cir. 1991), 
cert. denied, 113 S. Ct. 150 (1952) (defendant has the burden to 
establish an inadequate factual basis for a forfeiture order, in 
the absence of a timely objection). 

The Eleventh Circuit has squarely held to the contrary. That 
Circuit has held that because an agreement to plead to forfeiture 
is not an agreement to “plea to a substantive charge," Rule 11(f) 
does not apply. United States v. Boatner, 966 F.2d 1575, 1581 
(11th Cir. 1992). 

In Libretti, the Tenth Circuit adopted the Eleventh Circuit’s 
Boatner reasoning. 38 F.3d at 528. Applying that analysis, the 
court held that the district court properly ordered forfeiture of 
all defendant’s assets, regardless of whether they had any 
connection with his drug dealings in general or the CCE conviction 
in particular, “based on defendant’s written agreement and knowing 
and voluntary ratification of that agreement at the change of plea 
hearing." Libretti, 38 F.3d at 530. 

The second issue on appeal arose from petitioner’s right to a 
jury trial on the forfeiture issues. The Court again faced another 
“apparent({) split [in the circuits) on the issue of a 
constitutional right to a jury trial on forfeiture." Libretti, 38 


F.3d at 530. Compare United States v. Garrett, 727 F.2d 1003, 1012 


& n.6 (llth Cir. 1984) (defendant has a constitutional right to a 


jury trial in a 21 U.S.C. § 848 proceeding to determine if an asset 


is forfeitable), aff’d on other grounds, 471 U.S. 773 (1985) with 


United States _v. Robinson, 8 F.3d 418, 420 (7th Cir. 1993) 
(defendant may not have a constitutional right to jury trial on 


forfeiture issues, but does have the statutory right to a jury 
determination under Fed. R. Crim. P. 31(e)). Rather than address 
the constitutional issue, the Circuit agreed that the petitioner 
had a statutory right to a jury trial, but concluded that he had 
knowingly and voluntarily waived it. Libretti, 38 F.3d at 530. 
See Robinson, 8 F.3d at 421 (constitutional waiver principles 
applied to analyze whether defendant waived his statutory right to 
a jury trial). 

Neither the in-court advisement nor the “ agreement in this 
case informed the petitioner that he had a statutory or 
constitutional right to a jury trial. Libretti, 38 F.3d at 531. 
Thus, the petitioner was never aware of any right to a jury trial 
on the forfeiture. "The right to a jury trial is personal to a 
defendant and one which he alone may waive." Robinson, 8 F.3d at 
423 (internal quotations omitted). Despite this, the Circuit held 
that "specific reference to a jury trial on forfeiture issues was 
not necessary in light of the unambiguous plea agreement and 
defendant’s knowing and voluntary plea." Libretti, 38 F.3d at 531. 
This conclusion is wrong, because a defendant cannot knowingly and 


voluntarily waive a right of which he has not been advised. 


REASONS FOR GRANTING THE WRIT 
Supreme Court Rule 10.1.(a, c) states that certiorari review 
may be appropriate when “a United States court of appeals has 
rendered a decision in conflict with the decision of another United 
States court of appeals on the same matter,” and when a United 
States court of appeals has decided an important question of 


federal law that has not been, but should be, settled by the 


Supreme Court. 

This case fits within both parameters. The forfeiture/Rule 
11(f) question had already created a split in the federal circuits. 
After the Tenth Circuit decision adopting the minority view, the 
split is 3-2. In addition, this is an important issue not yet 
addressed by the Supreme Court. 

The same analysis applies to the second issue (right to jury 


trial), where the split in the federal circuits is now 2-1. 


CONCLUSION 
For these reasons, the petitioner respectfully requests that 
this Court issue a petition for writ of certiorari.

(1991). A guilty plea must be knowing and 
voluntary. Santodello v. New York, 404 U.S. 
257, 261, 92 S.Ct. 496, 498, 30 L.Ed2d 427 
(1971); United States v. Elias, 937 F 2d 
1514, 1617 (10th Cir.1991). A defendant's 
decision to plead guilty must be deliberate 
and intelligent and chosen from available al- 
ternatives. Rhodes, 913 F.2d at 843. “[T)o 
determine whether a plea is voluntary, a 
court must assess whether the defendant ful- 
ly understood the consequences of the plea” 
United States vu Williams, 919 F.2d 1451, 
1456 (10th Cir.1990), cert denied, 499 U.S. 
968, 111 S.Ct 1604, 113 L.Ed 2d 667 (1991). 
The defendant's attorney “and the court have 
a duty to apprise the defendant of the conse- 


quences of the plea and ensure that it is 
voluntary.” Jd Federal Rule of Criminal 
Procedure 11(cX1) requires that a district 
court must, among other things, ensure the 
defendant understands the nature of the 
charge and the mandatory minimum and 
maximum penalties. Elias, 937 F.2d at 1517. 


{13} In this case, the district court thor- 
oughly assessed whether defendant under- 
stood the consequences of the guilty plea 
The court informed him that all property 
described in the CCE count of the indictment 
could be forfeited. II R. 7. Defendant only 
questioned the extent to which the $1,500,000 
limit on ferfeiture applied to future legiti- 
mate earnings. His attorney and the district 
court agreed that the $1,500,000 limit applied 
only to products of drug transactions and not 
to legitimate future earnings. /d at 3-9. 
Thereafter, defendant stated that he had 
signed, read, and understood the plea agree- 
ment, and that he had no questions about it 
Id at 19-20. He further stated that he 
understood the consequences of his plea, in- 
cluding that all of his property owned by 
reason of any drug transaction could be for- 
feited. Jd at 21. Defendant conceded that 
he was pleading guilty because of the trial 
testimony received thus far. /d at 22. 


Nothing in the record indicates defendant's 
plea would have been different if he had been 
questioned further about forfeitability of his 
assets. He intended to forfeit all of his 
property without requiring the government 
to prove the assets were forfeitable. The 
plea agreement requires forfeiture pursuant 
to § 853, which includes forfeitable assets 
under § 853(a) and substitute assets under 
§ 853(p). See also 21 U.S.C. § 853(0) (§ 853 
is to be liberally construed).

other counts in the indictment. One of those 
counts charged him with use of firearms with 
silencers in drug trafficking which mandates, 
upon conviction, a thirty-year sentence con-
