STATUTORY PROVISIONS INVOLVED 


This case involves consideration of five sections of the 
United States Bankruptcy Code, 11 U.S.C. §§101, et 
seq. (1988).! They are 11 U.S.C. § § 362, 363, 506(a), 
542(b) and 553. The pertinent text of each of these 
sections is set forth in the Appendix found at the end of 
this Brief. Because this case was commenced prior to the 
Bankruptcy Reform Act of 1994, enacted on October 
22, 1994, the provisions of the Code referred to in this 
Brief are those in effect prior to the 1994 amendments. 
None of these provisions, however, has been amended 
by the Bankruptcy Reform Act of 1994 in a manner 
material to the outcome of this case.

STATEMENT OF THE CASE 


On November 28, 1989, the Petitioner, Citizens Bank 
of Maryland (“Citizens”),” loaned the Respondent, David 
Strumpf (“Strumpf” or the Debtor“), $5,068.75. The 
loan was evidenced by an unsecured consumer loan note 
(“the Note”), (R. 1) (the Note), in which Strumpf 
agreed to repay the loan (including interest) in twenty- 
four monthly installments. The Note provided for the 
immediate acceleration of the amount due upon 
default, the payment of attorney’s fees, and expressly 
granted Citizens the right of setoff upon default. Id. 


On January 25, 1991, Strumpf filed a petition for 
relief under Chapter 13 of the Bankruptcy Code in the 
United States Bankruptcy Court for the District of 
Maryland. (J.A. 3a); (R. 1) (Chapter 13 plan, statement,

On or about February 26, 1991, the bankruptcy court 
issued to Strumpf’s creditors a copy of an Order for 
Meeting of Creditors, Combined with Notice Thereof 
and of Automatic Stays, informing them of Strumpf's

Included among these dates was June 13, 1991, the date

to have a claim allowed and to share in distributions from

Citizens timely filed its proof of claim on June 13, 1991, 
asserting a secured claim in the amount of $2,555.50. On 
April 8, 1991, the bankruptcy court entered an order 
confirming Strumpf’s Chapter 13 plan. (J.A. 10a). The 
plan made no mention of Citizens or its particular claim. 


On October 7, 1991, Citizens filed in the bankruptcy 
court a Motion for Relief from Automatic Stay and for 
Setoff, asking the bankruptcy court for permission to 
exercise its right to set off Strumpf’s debt to Citizens 
under the Note against funds held on deposit in Strumpf’s 
checking account at Citizens. (J.A. 15a). As stated in 
Citizens’ motion, the amount then due on the Note, 
including attorney’s fees, was $3,259.68. Contemporane- 
ously with the filing of the motion, Citizens placed an 
administrative “hold’’ on $3,500.00 in Strumpf's check- 
ing account, the balance of which was then $7,206.59. 
(J.A. 26a). The administrative hold allowed Strumpf to 
withdraw from his account all but $3,500.00, thus pre- 
venting Strumpf from withdrawing funds to which Citi- 
zens claimed a setoff right. 


5 


On October 9, 1991, Strumpf filed a motion to hold 
Citizens in contempt, claiming that the administrative 
hold on his bank account violated the automatic stay of 
11 U.S.C. §362(a). J. A. 23a). The Debtor also filed on 
October 31, 1991, a second motion against Citizens, 
requesting that Citizens be ordered to release the adminis- 
trative hold and turn over all funds in the account to 
Strumpf. (R. 1) (Strumpf's motion seeking release of 
administrative hold and turnover of funds). On November 
4, 1991, the bankruptcy court (the Honorable Paul 
Mannes, Chief Judge, presiding), held a hearing on Citi- 
zens’ motion for relief from automatic stay and for 
setoff, at the conclusion of which the court requested 
Strumpf's counsel to submit a legal memorandum and 
reserved ruling on the merits. (R. 1) (transcript of Nov. 4, 
1991 hearing). On November 13, 1991, Judge Mannes 
held a hearing on Strumpf’s motion to hold Citizens in 
contempt, and granted the motion, holding Citizens in 
contempt for violating the automatic stay and awarding 
Strumpf attorney’s fees of $500.00, punitive damages of 
$375.00, and nominal damages of $25.00. (J.A. 27a). 


In reaching this result, the court clearly felt bound by 
the decision of the Fourth Circuit in United States v. 
Reynolds,* stating that under Reynolds, I have to find 
that Citizens Bank violated the stay of 11 U.S.C. §362(a) 
(7) through the administrative freeze process used here.“ 
(R. 1) (transcript of Nov. 13, 1991 hearing). Judge 
Mannes indicated that his own view of the correct result 
may have been different: Were the decision on my part 
one written on a clean slate, I might look at the case 
somewhat differently.“ Id. 


1764 F.2d 1001 (4th Cir. 1985). See infra at 36-38 for a dis-

On December 2, 1991, the bankruptcy court granted 
Citizens’ motion for relief from the automatic stay, auth- 
orizing Citizens to exercise its right of setoff against the 
funds in Strumpf’s account. (J. A. 29a). By that time, 
however, Citizens was unable to effectuate a setoff 
because Strumpf had withdrawn all of the funds from 
his account after Citizens lifted the administrative hold 
as required by the bankruptcy court’s earlier contempt 
order. 


Citizens appealed the bankruptcy court’s decision on 
Strumpf’s contempt motion to the United States District 
Court for the District of Maryland (the Honorable John 
R. Hargrove, presiding). (R. 1) (notice of appeal). Strumpf 
did not appeal from the bankruptcy court’s order grant- 
ing Citizens relief from the automatic stay. The district 
court reversed the decision of the bankruptcy court, 
summing up the proceedings below: 


The ironic result for Citizens Bank was that it was 
held in contempt, was later granted its Motion for 
Relief From Automatic Stay and for Setoff, but 
could not exercise its right to setoff because (as 
predicted by Citizens Bank) Strumpf had removed 
the money from the checking account. This series 
of events rendered the Bankruptcy Court’s ultimate 
granting of Citizens Bank’s Motion meaningless. 


(J. A. 3la, 37a); Citizens Bank of Maryland v. Strumpf, 
138 B. R. 792, 794 (D. Md. 1992), rev’d, 37 F.3d 155 
(4th Cir. 1994). The district court held that the place- 
ment of an administrative hold on funds in an account 
pending the bankruptcy court’s ruling on a motion for 
relief from stay did not equate to a setoff and did not 
violate the automatic stay. Jd. As stated by the district 
court, “permitting the bank to freeze the funds until 
such time as the Court rules on the Bank’s Motion for 


7 


Relief from Automatic Stay and for Setoff is the only 


sensible procedural approach under these circumstances.“ 
Id. 


Strumpf appealed the district court’s judgment to the 
United States Court of Appeals for the Fourth Circuit, 
which reversed the district court’s ruling and remanded 
the case for reinstatement of the bankruptcy court’s 
order. (J.A. 39a); Citizens Bank of Maryland v. Strumpf, 
37 F.3d 155, 159 (4th Cir. 1994). The opinion of the 
Fourth Circuit followed the earlier decision of a panel 
of the same court in United States v. Reynolds, 764 F.2d 
1004 (4th Cir. 1985), a case involving a “freeze” of a 
tax refund by the Internal Revenue Service, and con- 
cluded that the administrative hold was “tantamount” 
to a setoff and violated 11 U.S.C. §362(a)(7). Strumpf, 
37 F.3d at 158. The court further reasoned that while 
the “banker’s dilemma” created by mutual obligations 
between a bank and its depositor may allow a debtor to 
defeat a creditor’s right of setoff, the language of § 362(a) 
produces this result. Id. at 149. On October 27, 1994, 
Citizens filed a petition for rehearing or, in the alterna- 
tive, suggestion of rehearing in banc with the Fourth

SUMMARY OF THE ARGUMENT 


The right to setoff mutual debts has long been recog- 
nized in American common law, and in bankruptcy law. 
The doctrine of setoff is premised upon the “absurdity 
of making A pay B when B owes A,” Studley v. Boylston 
Nat Bank, 229 U.S. 523, 528 (1913), and recognizes 
the inherent fairness and efficiency in canceling mutual 
obligations to arrive at one true debt. 


The application of this setoff doctrine in cases under 
the Bankruptcy Code involving a nonbank creditor is 
straightforward and has generated little controversy, with 
the exception of cases involving the Internal Revenue 
Service (“IRS”). The Bankruptcy Code, in §553, expressly 
recognizes and preserves a creditor’s right of setoff. The 
automatic stay under §362(a)(7) prevents the creditor 
from exercising its setoff right without first obtaining 
the bankruptcy court’s permission. However, under 
§542(b) of the Bankruptcy Code, the creditor is not 
required to pay its debt to the debtor, to the extent of 
the creditor’s claim against the debtor. The mere refusal 
by the creditor to pay its debt to the debtor is not con- 
sidered to constitute a setoff and, therefore, is not con- 
sidered to be a violation of the automatic stay. 


The confusion surrounding setoffs under the Bank- 
ruptcy Code primarily has arisen if the creditor is a bank 
where the debtor maintains a bank account and the bank 
takes some action after the commencement of the deposi- 
tor’s bankruptcy case to restrict the debtor’s ability to 
use the funds on deposit. A bank creditor generally has a 
right under nonbankruptcy law to offset a depositor’s 
bank account, that is, deduct from the account and 
apply the amount of deduction to reduce the balance due 
on the depositor’s defaulted debt to the bank. Upon 
commencement of a bankruptcy case, the bank will 


9 


want to preserve its ability to exercise its setoff right 
against the debtor’s bank account, after being authorized 
to do so by the bankruptcy court, by ensuring that the 
account funds are not dissipated. The confusion in these 
cases arises in part from the lower courts’ misapprehen- 
sion of the nature of the relationship between a bank 
and its depositor. 


This Court has long recognized that a bank account 
creates the relation of debtor and creditor between the 
bank and its depositor. The money deposited in legal 
contemplation is a loan to the bank and becomes part 
of the general funds of the bank, and the depositor has 
the right to draw on the account to the extent of the 
deposit. When the depositor files a bankruptcy petition, 
§542(b) entitles the bank to refuse to pay its obligation 
to the debtor from the bank’s assets to the extent the 
bank has a right of setoff. The method used by a bank 
to refuse to pay its obligation to a debtor with respect 
to the debtor’s bank account is referred to as an adminis 
trative “‘hold” or “‘freeze.” 


The issue presented in this case is whether the bank 
can place an administrative hold on the debtor’s bank 
account to preserve the bank’s right of setoff against the 
account prior to obtaining relief from the automatic stay 
to exercise its setoff right. During the course of Strumpf's 
bankruptcy case below, Citizens placed an administrative 
hold on a portion of Strumpf’s account at Citizens and 
contemporaneously filed a motion requesting the bank- 
ruptcy court to modify the automatic stay to permit 
Citizens to accomplish a setoff. 


Consistent with §542(b), the imposition of an admin- 
istrative hold on Strumpf’s bank account was not itself 
a setoff. To the contrary, by filing a motion requesting 
the bankruptcy court’s authority to exercise its setoff 


10 


right contemporaneously with its imposition of the 
administrative hold, Citizens made clear its intention to 
delay the actual exercise of its setoff right pending bank- 
ruptcy court approval. This limited hold on the account 
did not accomplish the setoff; the balance of the account 
was not reduced by the amount of the setoff and Citizens 
in all respects acted in a manner evidencing that the 
setoff had not been accomplished. 


The Fourth Circuit panel below focused only on 
§ §362(a)(7) and 553 of the Bankruptcy Code, and 
ignored the impact of § §363, 506(a) and 542(b). That 
error, coupled with the Fourth Circuit’s apparent failure 
to recognize the nature of a bank account as the bank’s 
debt to the debtor rather than funds owned by the 
debtor, predictably led the Fourth Circuit to the wrong 
conclusion. 


If a financial institution, or for that matter nonbank 
creditors, were to be denied the ability to use a tempor- 
ary hold to preserve the status quo pending determina- 
tion of the parties’ respective rights, the creditor’s valid 
setoff right would be nullified by the debtor’s ability to 
withdraw or collect the funds faster than the bankruptcy 
court could rule on the matter. A ruling by the bank- 
ruptcy court recognizing the bank’s setoff right after all 
funds are withdrawn is the proverbial attempt to lock the 
barn door after the horse has been stolen. That is pre- 
cisely what happened in this case. By the time the bank- 
ruptcy court granted Citizens relief from the automatic 


stay to exercise its right of setoff, Strumpf had with- 


drawn all of the funds on deposit at Citizens, leaving 
Citizens with a hollow right and no remedy. 


Consequently, the absurdity long eschewed by the 
doctrine of setoff became a reality in this case. The 
Bankruptcy Code neither requires nor supports a nullifi- 


11 


cation of a creditor's right of setoff. Accordingly, Citi- 
zens respectfully asks this Court to reverse the decision 
below and to reinstate the decision of the district court.

B. Citizens’ Setoff Right is Recognized and Pre- 
served by the Bankruptcy Code. 


1. Section 553(a) Expressly Provides that a 
Creditor’s Right to Offset a Mutual Debt is 
Not Affected by the Bankruptcy Code, 
Except as Otherwise Provided in Sections 
362 and 363. 


A resolution of the question presented by this case 
requires the interpretation of several sections of the 
Bankruptcy Code. The starting point in construing any 
statute is the plain language of the statute. Pennsylvania 
Public Welfare Dept. v. Davenport, 495 U.S. 552, 557 
(1990); Kelly v. Robinson, 479 U.S. 36, 43 (1986); Con- 
sumer Product Safety Commission v. GTE Sylvania, Inc., 
447 U.S. 102, 108 (1980). Where the statute’s language 
is unambiguous, “the sole function of the court is to 
enforce it according to its terms.” Caminetti v. United 
States, 242 U.S. 470, 485 (1917). Accord Connecticut 
Nat ] Bank v. Germain, 503 U.S. 249, 112 S. Ct. 1146, 
1149 (1992). Separate provisions of the same act are to 
be given a harmonious and comprehensive meaning. Clark 
v. Uebersee Finanz-Korporation, A.G., 332 U.S. 480, 
488 (1947). Where the statutory scheme is coherent and 
consistent, there is no need to inquire beyond the plain 
language. United States v. Ron Pair Enterprises, Inc. , 489 
U.S. 235, 240-41 (1989). Adherence to these maxims 
leads to a result contrary to the one reached by the 
Fourth Circuit below. 


Section 553 of the Bankruptcy Code expressly pre- 
serves the common law right of setoff in bankruptcy 
cases. It provides, in pertinent part: 


(a) Except as otherwise provided in this section and 
in sections 362 and 363 of this title, this title does 


16 


not affect any right of a creditor to offset a mutual 
debt owing by such creditor to the debtor that arose 
before the commencement of the case under this 
title against a claim of such creditor against the 
debtor that arose before the commencement of the 
case... 


11 U.S.C. §553(a) (1988). 


The preservation of the common law setoff right in 
bankruptcy proceedings has a long history in American 
bankruptcy law. See 4 James W. Moore & Lawrence P. 
King, Collier on Bankrupcy, 4553.01, at 553-1 to 553-9 
(15th ed. 1994) (tracing the history of the preservation 
of the setoff right in bankruptcy); Carmelita J. Hammon, 
Setoff in Bankruptcy: Is the Creditor Preferred or 
Secured?, 50 U. Colo. L. Rev. 511 (1979). The Sixth 
Circuit Court of Appeals succinctly articulated the 
rationale for the stringent preservation of this favored 
right: “The principle upon which the rule [of setoff] 
proceeds is that in case of mutual debts, it is only the 
balance which is the real and just sum owing by or to the 
bankrupt.” Prudential Ins. Co. of America v. Nelson, 101 
F.2d 441, 443 (6th Cir.), cert. denied, 308 U.S. 583 
(1939). 


Section 553 continues this tradition under the Bank- 
ruptcy Code. It was intended to carry forward the 
principles governing setoff that had been found in section 
68 of the Bankruptcy Act of 1898, as later amended by 
the Bankruptcy Act of 1938. See H.R. Rep. No. 95-595, 
95th Cong., Ist Sess. at 183-86, 377 (1977), reprinted in, 
1978 U.S.C.C.A.N. 5787, 6143-47, 6333. By maintaining 
a creditor’s common law right to set off the obligations 
of its debtor against its own debt to the debtor, §553 
recognizes the injustice in permitting a creditor to obtain 
repayment of only a portion of the debt owed by the 


17 


debtor, while at the same time compelling the creditor to 
pay its full indebtedness to the debtor. 


Absent a setoff, a creditor . .. is in the worst of 
both worlds: it must pay its debt to the debtor in 
full, but is only entitled to receive a tiny fraction 
of the money the debtor owes it. It was to avoid this 
unfairness to creditors that setoffs were allowed in 
bankruptcy in the first place. 


In re De Laurentiis Entertainment Group, Inc., 963 F.2d 
1269, 1277 (9th Cir.), cert. denied, — U.S. 113 
S. Ct. 330 (1992). See also 4 Collier on Bankruptcy, 
supra, J 553.01, at 553.11. 


The only significant changes made in the Bankruptcy 
Code relating to the right to set off mutual prebank- 
ruptcy obligations are procedural; they do not alter the 
long-standing goal of preserving the right of setoff 
throughout the bankruptcy case. These changes are (i) to 
limit the exercise of the right of setoff by subjecting the 
right to the broad scope of the automatic stay of §362; 
and (ii) to permit the trustee or debtor in possession 
to use property subject to the right of setoff, under 
the circumstances described in §363. See H.R. Rep. No. 
95-595, 95th Cong., Ist Sess. at 183-86, 377 (1977), 
reprinted in, 1978 U.S.C.C.A.N. 5787, 6143-47, 6333. 
See also 4 Collier on Bankruptcy, supra, 9553.01, at 
553-6 to 553-9 (discussing changes made by the Code).’ 


Section 553 also alters the treatment of claims against the 
debtor acquired from a third party, a situation not presented by 
the facts here.

3. A Bank’s Right to Refuse to Turn Over to a 
Debtor Funds on Deposit and Subject to Set- 
off is Expressly Recognized by Section 542(b). 


A creditor’s setoff right against a debtor and his bank- 
ruptcy estate is further protected by §542(b) of the 
Bankruptcy Code. Section 542(b) generally covers the 
subject of “turnover” of property of the estate; that is, 
when third parties holding possession of property of a 
bankruptcy estate must deliver the property to the 
trustee or debtor in possession. Section 542(b) plainly 
authorizes a creditor not to pay a debt to the debtor to 
the extent that such debt is subject to setoff: 

(b) Except as provided in subsection (c) or (d) of 
this section, an entity that owes a debt that is prop- 
erty of the estate and that is matured, payable on 
demand, or payable on order, shall pay such debt to, 
or on the order of, the trustee, except to the extent 
that such debt may be offset under section 553 of 
this title against a claim against the debtor. 


11 U.S.C. §542(b) (1988) (emphasis added). 


The operation of §542(b) is best illustrated outside 
the bank-depositor context. In the case of Jn re Whitman, 
38 B.R. 395 (Bankr. D.N.D. 1984), a commissioned 
salesman was both a customer and employee of a corpo- 


23 


ration. As an employment benefit, the salesman obtained 
a discount on products he purchased from the corpora- 
tion for personal use. Over time, the salesman purchased 
$4,215.01 worth of product, and in that same period 
earned $2,265.92 in unpaid sales commissions. After the 
salesman filed for bankruptcy relief, he instituted a turn- 
over action under §542 of the Bankruptcy Code demand-

court correctly held that, by operation of §542(b), the 
corporation was entitled to refuse to pay the commis-

and the debtor’s turnover request was denied. 


Application of §542(b) in a bank-depositor context 
is no different. Because the funds in a deposit account 
constitute a debt owed by the bank to its customer, the 
bank may refuse to pay that debt, i. e., release the funds 
in the account, to the extent that it has a right of offset 
against the funds. 


The leading bankruptcy treatise agrees with this 
view: “If the deposit account may be set off against a 
debt owed by the debtor to the bank, then turnover of 
the deposit account must be made except to the extent 
the account may be set off against the debt under section 
333.“ 4 Collier on Bankruptcy, supra, 9542.03, at 
542-15 (emphasis added), Accord In re Edgins, 36 B.R. 
480, 483 (Bankr. 9th Cir. 1984); In re Carpenter, 14 B. R. 
405, 407-08 (Bankr. M.D. Tenn. 1981). The commenta- 
tors who have analyzed this issue also generally agree 
with this conclusion. See Russell J. Passamano, Setoff in 
Bankruptcy: An Overview of the Mechanics, 105 Banking

Alan N. Resnick, Freezing the Debtor’s Account: A 
Banker’s Dilemma Under the Bankruptcy Code, 100 
Banking L. J. 316 (1983). Applied to the facts of the

refuse to turn over funds from the Debtor’s account to

C. The Interplay of the Five Relevant Bankruptcy 
Code Sections—§ §362, 363, 506, 542 and 
553—Gives Rise to a Purposeful Statutory 
Equilibrium Between the Competing Rights of 
the Debtor and the Bank Creditor to Funds in 
a Bank Account. 


The restrictions on the exercise of the right of setoff 
by a creditor on the one hand, amd on a debtor’s ability 
to use and obtain a turnover of funds on the other, give 
rise to a purposeful statutory equilibrium appropriately 
described by one court as a “stalemate.’’ Edgins, 36 B. R. 
at 483. By virtue of §362(a)(7), a creditor may not exer- 
cise its right of setoff absent relief from the automatic 
stay. From the debtor’s perspective, §363(c)(2) restricts 
the debtor’s use of funds that are subject to setoff and 
deemed cash collateral. The debtor also lacks free access 
to the funds because §542(b) expressly allows a creditor 
to refuse to pay a debt that is subject to setoff. 


The Bankruptcy Appellate Panel of the Ninth Circuit 
in Edgins described the possible resolutions to this 
statutory equilibrium in the bank-depositor context as 
follows: 


This stalemate may be alleviated by either party 
initiating a number of alternative proceedings in the

frozen account is security by virtue of Section

Section 553. The creditor might bring an action for

Edgins, 36 B. R. at 483. See also Weintraub & Resnick, 
supra, at 3-4. 


In the present case, the statutory stalemate took effect 
upon the Debtor’s bankruptcy filing. The parties’ respec- 
tive obligations relating to the Debtor’s bank account and 
the Note were created prebankruptcy and were unques-

common law and contractual right to set off against the 
Debtor’s bank account an amount equivalent to what the 
Debtor owed Citizens under the Note was preserved 
under §553. While Citizens was stayed from exercising 
its right of setoff without bankruptcy court approval, 
Citizens was entitled under §542(b) to decline to pay the

outstanding on the Note, absent a court order to the

II. 
THE IMPOSITION OF AN ADMINISTRATIVE HOLD 
DOES NOT VIOLATE THE AUTOMATIC STAY. 


A. An Administrative Hold is Not a Setoff and 
Does Not Violate Section 362(a)(7). 


The Fourth Circuit’s analysis below focused on the 
propriety and effect of the administrative hold placed on 
the account by Citizens. As explained below, the mere 
imposition of an administrative hold or freeze“ such as 
the one employed by Citizens is not an act of setoff as 
contemplated by §362(a)(7), because it is not the result 
of an intent to set off money in a debtor’s account and 
does not represent affirmative action to effectuate that 
intent. 


While many creditors with a right of setoff may exer- 
cise their rights under §542(b) by simply informing a 
debtor that they choose not to pay their debt subject 
to a setoff, a financial institution does not have the 
luxury of simplicity. Financial institutions face signifi- 
cant communicative, technological and geographical 
difficulties in manifesting an intent and implementing a 
decision not to pay. Funds in a demand deposit or check- 
ing account in most financial institutions are easily 
accessible in various ways. The account holder may write 
a check, which comes to the bank through collection 
channels from another institution or is personally pre- 
sented by the individual or entity to whom the check is 
drawn, The account holder may appear in person and 
make a withdrawal over the counter at any number of 
bank branches. The depositor may also access funds from 
automated teller machines all over the country, if not the 
world. The funds in a bank account can be disbursed to a 
third party pursuant to an agreement with the bank, for 
example, in a monthly bill pay” arrangement. Bank 


27 


funds may also be transmitted by electronic transfer in 
response to a request from the depositor or a third party. 


A financial institution must have a mechanism to 
communicate its decision to exercise its right under 
§542(b) not to permit withdrawal of funds subject to 
setoff to multiple branches, automated teller machines, 
and others with responsibility for administering bank 
accounts, The solution is the administrative hold or 
“freeze.” The hold is accomplished by electronically 
coding a financial institution’s computers to restrict 
access to specific funds, and is the most efficient proce- 
dure—indeed, the only procedure—by which a bank may 
ensure institution-wide compliance with its internal 
account-related directives. Ultimately, it is nothing more 
than a bank’s means of communicating to various bank 
personnel and computers its intent not to pay its debt to 
its depositor. 


Because the timing of a setoff may affect priorities 
among competing creditors, some states have developed 
precise tests to determine just when a setoff has occurred. 
No reported Maryland case has directly addressed this 
question, However, the predominant and better view 
among jurisdictions that have formulated such tests 
requires evidence of an intent to set off coupled with 
action effectuating that intent. In the leading case of 
per v. Nat ! City Bank of Cleveland, the Sixth Circuit 
stated: 


[T]he act of setoff is not complete until three steps 
have been taken: (1) the decision to exercise the 
right, (2) some action which accomplishes the set- 
off and (3) some record which evidences that the 
right of setoff has been exercised. 


511 F.2d 1016, 1018 (6th Cir, 1975). The test for setoff 
articulated in Baker has been cited repeatedly with

Cir. 1988); In re Saugus Gen. Hosp., Inc., 698 F.2d 42, 
47-48 (Ist Cir. 1983). See also 4 Collier on Bankruptcy, 
supra, 4553.02, at 553-12.!° 


The language of §542(b) supports the distinction 
between declining to pay a debt that is subject to setoff 
and the actual exercise of the setoff right. Section 542(b) 
states that an entity that owes a debt to the debtor must

The quoted language contemplates that the right of set-

courts should not needlessly confront constitutional 
issues and recognizes that a court will assume that Con- 
gress did not intend to infringe upon constitutionally 
protected liberties. Id. 


The Fifth Amendment to the United States Constitu- 
tion provides, in part: “No person shall be . . . deprived 
of life, liberty, or property, without due process of law; 
nor shall private property be taken for public use, with- 
out just compensation.“ U.S. Const. amend. V. As dis- 
cussed below, the Fourth Circuit’s interpretation of 
§362(a)(7) may be read to effect an unconstitutional 
taking of a bank’s property—the right of setoff—without 
adequate compensation. However, this Court need not 
wrestle with the constitutionality of the Bankruptcy 
Code as interpreted by the Strumpf decision, because the 
correct and harmonious construction of all the relevant 
Code sections raises no such constitutional issue. 


The right of setoff is similar to a secured creditor’s 
interest in its collateral. Like a mortgage or other security 
interest, the right of setoff enables a creditor to secure 
the payment of a debt and to satisfy the debtor’s obliga- 
tion by permitting the creditor to retain funds that he 
otherwise would be obligated to pay to the debtor. See 


40 


Barrier v. Marine Midland Trust Co., 263 Md. 596, 607, 
284 A.2d 418, 424 (1971) (the right of setoff is an 
exercisable right that is an incident to the remedy to 
secure payment of a debt). This similarity between the 
right of setoff and other types of security interests has 
been recognized by §506(a) of the Bankruptcy Code, 
which treats a claim of a creditor that is subject to set- 
off as a secured claim“ to the extent of the amount 
subject to setoff. See supra at 19. 


This Court has recognized the rights of secured credi- 
tors to be property interests entitled to Fifth Amend- 
ment protection. See United States v. Security Indus. 
Bank, 459 U.S. 70, 74-76 (1982); Armstrong v. United 
States, 364 U.S. 40, 44 (1960) (recognizing protected 
property interest in materialman’s lien under state law); 
Wright v. Union Central Life Ins. Co., 311 U.S. 273, 
279-81 (1940); Louisville Joint Stock Land Bank v. 
Radford, 295 U.S. 555, 594 (1935) (mortgagee’s rights 
were property interests protected by the Fifth Amend- 
ment). The right of setoff likewise should be recognized 
as a protected property right. 


Congress’ bankruptcy power under Art. I, §8, clause 
4 of the Constitution is subject to the Fifth Amend- 
ment’s prohibition against the taking of private property 
without compensation, As stated in by this Court in 
Security Industrial: 


[H]owever “rational” the exercise of the bank- 
ruptcy power may be, that inquiry is quite separate 
from the question whether the enactment takes 
property within the prohibition of the Fifth Amend- 
ment. 


459 U.S. at 75. See also Radford, 295 U.S. at 589 (The 


bankruptcy power, like the other great substantive 
powers of Congress, is subject to the Fifth Amend- 


41 


ment.“). Under the Fourth Circuit’s interpretation in 
Strumpf, §362(a)(7) may be read to deprive banks of 
their constitutionally protected property interest in the 
right of setoff without adequate compensation. 


The governmental action in this case, the enactment of 
§362(a)(7), as interpreted by the Fourth Circuit, would 
result in the virtual elimination of banks’ reasonable 
expectations relating to the right of setoff.'® Without 
the ability to place an administrative freeze on the 
debtor’s account, banks essentially would be compelled 
to permit the debtor to withdraw funds on deposit (i.e., 
pay over the debt subject to the bank’s right of setoff to 
the estate), even though those funds secure the debtor’s 
obligations to the bank and represent a debt that the 
bank may expressly decline to pay under §542(b). Such 
an interpretation of §362(a)(7) would raise a serious 
question as to whether the Code operates as an uncon- 
stitutional taking without adequate compensation or pro- 
tection. See generally Security Industrial, 459 U.S. at 
75-78 (finding governmental action of bankruptcy code 
destroying property rights of secured party to be an 
unconstitutional taking). This constitutional issue can 
and should be avoided by this Court’s adherence to the 
plain language of the Code and the harmonious interpre- 
tation of §362(a)(7) with § §553 and 542(b).

IV. 
ALLOWING A BANK CREDITOR TO IMPOSE AN ADMIN. 
ISTRATIVE HOLD PENDING DETERMINATION BY THE 
BANKRUPTCY COURT OF THE BANK’S SETOFF RIGHT 
IS CONSISTENT WITH THE POLICY GOALS OF THE 
BANKRUPTCY CODE, 


A. Under Strumpf. the Extraordinary Measure of 
Ex Parte Relief Will Become Routine. 


The Strumpf decision leaves banks with only one 
method of attempting to prevent the dissipation of the 
funds to which they claim a right of setoff: initiating 
immediate ex parte litigation in the bankruptcy court 
upon the first notice that a customer has filed for bank- 
ruptcy relief. If banks are required to go immediately 
into court upon the suspicion that the debtor might 
attempt to defeat the bank’s setoff right, the bankruptcy 
court system will suffer from a significant increase in 
frantic litigation and financial institutions and debtors 
alike will be forced to incur greater expense in legal repre- 
sentation. Even courts that advocate this approach recog- 
nize its inherent drawbacks. See Jn re Homan, 116 B.R. 
595, 605 (Bankr. S.D. Ohio 1990) (“This court has no 
desire to add additional proceedings to its existing 
docket, nor to increase the litigation expenses of parties 
in Chapter 13 cases”). Under the Fourth Circuit’s hold- 
ing in Strumpf, requests for emergency relief will become 
routine in federal bankruptcy courts. 


Ex parte litigation, however, will not provide financial 
institutions with complete protection and is not a mean- 
ingful substitute for an administrative hold. If banks are 
denied the ability to freeze funds in which they claim a 
right of setoff, they must move for ex parte protection 
immediately upon becoming aware of the commence- 
ment of the bankruptcy case. Where a depositor sends 


43 


notice of his bankruptcy filing, or the bank learns of the 
filing through a notice to creditors, prior to the deposi- 
tor’s withdrawal of his funds, ex parte relief may be 
effective. However, if the bank learns of the filing con- 
temporaneously with a request for turnover of the funds, 
ex parte action would not be effective. See In re Moreira, 
173 B.R. 965, 968 (Bankr. D. Mass. 1994). If the request 
for turnover is in the form of an over-the-counter with- 
drawal, the bank cannot stall its depositor while its 
attorneys run into court.“ The same is true for auto- 
mated teller withdrawals, electronic transfer requests, 
automatic bill pay arrangements, and other instantaneous 
means by which funds are withdrawn from a bank 
account. If the request for turnover comes in the form of 
a check, the bank must have its attorneys and the judicial 
system act within the statutory midnight payment dead- 
line for such instruments, See Jn re Patterson, 967 F.2d 
505, 511 (11th Cir. 1992) (discussing the speed with 
which a bank must act to obtain ex parte relief under the 
midnight payment deadline), If ex parte relief is the only 
means available to a bank to protect its rights, the bank 
thus faces a substantial possibility of having no protec- 
tion whatsoever. 


Furthermore, banks deprived of the ability to protect 
their security in the event of a customer’s bankruptcy (or 
faced with the prospect of expensive ex parte litigation) 


19 ding on the account type, for example, a certificate 
of deposit, a bank may have a contractual or other right to delay 
payment to its depositor. However, if the account is a standard 
checking account, like Strumpf's, or a demand deposit account, in 
the usual course of business a bank must pay its depositor immedi- 
ately,

will have a strong incentive to accomplish setoff before 
the prospective debtor files a bankruptcy petition. The 
adoption of an interpretation of the Bankruptcy Code 
which encourages banks to set off debts prior to bank- 
ruptcy filings can only lead to a worsening of the finan- 
cial positions of their customers, thereby possibly necessi- 
tating, or at least hastening, a bankruptcy filing. 


B. Strumpf May be Read as Requiring All Creditors 
to Turn Over Property Subject to a Setoff Right 
Upon Demand. 


By denying a bank the right to impose an administra- 
tive hold, the Fourth Circuit in Strumpf effectively held 
that a bank cannot independently exercise its choice 
under §542(b) not to turn over property in which it 
claims a right of setoff. A bank must immediately turn 
over the subject funds (by, for example, permitting a 
withdrawal or honoring a check) or violate §362(a). The 
logic of Strumpf, if adopted by this Court, necessarily 
would apply outside of the bank-depositor context. 


Under the intepretation adopted by the Fourth Cir- 
cuit, any debtor could demand immediate payment of 
a debt owed to him by a creditor, despite the creditor’s 
assertion of a right of setoff. For example, an impecuni- 
ous debtor, “B”, has an outstanding, mature and unse- 
cured debt of $10,000.00 owing to a creditor, “A”, and 
A owes B $7,500.00. Both obligations arose prior to B’s 
bankruptcy filing and are unquestionably mutual. Under 
Strumpf, B may approach A and demand immediate 
payment of the $7,500.00 debt (the equivalent of a 
withdrawal demand). A would not be able to decline to 
pay and seek relief from the automatic stay from the 
bankruptcy court to set off the $7,500.00 against B’s 
$10,000.00 debt. Instead, A would be required to turn 


45 


over the $7,500.00 to B, and be relegated to receive from 
B’s bankruptcy estate a prorated distribution of funds 
available for general creditors, which often is little or 
nothing. The injustice is obvious: A has been forced to 
pay B, but B, who is bankrupt, may end up paying little 
or nothing of his $10,000.00 debt to A. Thus, B, under 
the decision below, would be able to use the Bankruptcy 
Code to divest the creditor, A, of A’s setoff right for 
B’s own enrichment. 


In a perfect world, the statutory stalemate between a 
bank creditor and a debtor which is created upon the 
debtor’s bankruptcy filing, see supra at 24-25, should 
ensure that cash collateral is not dissipated. See in re 
Wildcat Constr. Co., Inc., 57 B.R. 981, 985-86 (Bankr. D. 
Vt. 1986) (Since, if the bank is right about the status of 
the funds it is holding in the debtor’s accounts, the debtor 
may not use this cash collateral in any event, freezing the 
accounts hardly seems necessary.. . . We refuse to pre- 
sume either that most debtors are profligate or that most 
banks are avaricious.“). However, this is not a perfect 
world and harsh reality requires, and the Code permits, 
a bank to be allowed to impose an administrative hold on 
a debtor’s account. As one commentator noted: 


If this were Camelot, these provisions of the Bank- 
ruptcy Code [§§ 362(a), 363, 506(a), 542(b), and 
553] would, by themselves, impose the freeze. 
Bankers would not put administrative holds on 
debtors’ bank accounts and debtors would not 
attempt to withdraw funds from the account with- 
out the bank’s permission or a court order obtained 
upon a showing of adequate protection. 


Paul S. Groschadl, “Freezing” ihe Debtor’s Bank 
Account: A Violation of the Automatic Stay?, 57 Am. 
Bankr. L. J. 75, 77 (1983). 


46 


If denied the ability to use a temporary hold, a finan- 
cial institution would have no means of preventing its 
depositor from drawing on his account. As noted in the 
Edgins case: 

Creditors with a valid right of setoff under 11 U.S.C. 

Section 553 would be required to turnover to the 

debtor funds subject to setoff and thereafter attempt 

to obtain an order from the court to preclude the 
debtor from improvidently dissipating the funds. 

This will, all too often, be an attempt to lock the 

barn door after the horse has been stolen. 


Edgins, 36 B.R. at 484 (emphasis added). A setoff 
against an account with no funds is a meaningless act, and 
no effective remedy exists by which a creditor can 
retrieve the improperly removed funds. See Williams, 
61 B. R. at 575.” In view of the extreme liquidity of 
funds on deposit, “permitting the temporary freeze serves 
to give banks needed protection against the immediate 
dissipation of its cash collateral and resulting destruction 
of its right of setoff before it has an opportunity to be 
heard in a bankruptcy court.” Weintraub & Resnick, 
supra, at 324. 


20. Once cash collateral has been dissipated and spent, 
court-fashioned sanctions such as retroactive adequate protec- 
tion, appointment of a Chapter 11 trustee or prohibitions against 
the further use of cash collateral can be hollow victories for a 
secured creditor and do not rise to the level of a ‘remedy.’ This 
is especially true where all remaining property of the estate is 
encumbered and the cash which has been spent without permis- 
sion cannot be replaced.” Jd. As a legal matter, a bank’s release 
of funds may be viewed as a waiver and extinguish the bank’s 
right of setoff. See Uniform Commercial Code §9-306(2) (1978). 
See also, In re Archer, 34 B.R. 28, 30 (Bankr. N.D. Tex. 1983) 
(release of funds could extinguish setoff rights although monies 
constitute cash collateral, but holding that no waiver occurred).

The facts of this case vividly demonstrate the conse- 
quences of depriving a financial institution of its right to 
freeze a bank account pursuant to §542(b). Citizens 
initiated the process of determining the parties’ compet-

the account was not frozen Citizens later would have

JA. 27a, 29a). As Citizens predicted, by the time the

Citizens’ cash colateral was gone. The relief from the

setoff without anything to set off against is worth little 
more than the book in which it is found.” Jn re Davis, 
889 F.2d 658, 662 (5th Cir. 1989), cert. denied, 495 U.S. 
933 (1990).
