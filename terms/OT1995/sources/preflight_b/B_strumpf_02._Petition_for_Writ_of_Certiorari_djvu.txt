STATEMENT OF THE CASE 

On January 25, 1991, David Strumpf 
("Strumpf" or the "Debtor") filed a 
bankruptcy petition in the United States 
Bankruptcy Court for the District of 
Maryland seeking relief under Chapter 13 
of the Bankruptcy Code. The bankruptcy 
court had jurisdiction under 28 U.S.C. 
§ 1334(a). 

At the time the bankruptcy petition 
was filed, Strumpf had a demand deposit 


account with a balance of $11,279.86 at 


Citizens Bank of Maryland ("Citizens"),’ 
and owed Citizens the sum of $3,250.48 in 
connection with a consumer loan which was 
in default. 

On October 7, 1991, Citizens filed 
in the bankruptcy court a motion for 
relief from the automatic stay, pursuant 
to 11 U.S.C. § 362(d), to permit Citizens 
to exercise its right to set off 
Strumpf’s debt to Citizens against funds 
held on deposit and due to Strumpf. 
Citizens simultaneously placed an 
"administrative hold" on $3,500 in the 
Strumpf account. On October 9, 1991, 
Strumpf filed a motion to hold Citizens 
in contempt for violation of the 


automatic stay. 


2 Pursuant to Supreme Court Rule 29.1,

5 


On November 7, 1991, the bankruptcy 
court held a hearing on Citizens’ motion 
for relief from the automatic stay, at 
the conclusion of which the bankruptcy 
court reserved its ruling on the merits. 
On November 13, 1991, the bankruptcy 
court held a hearing on Strumpf’s motion 
to hold Citizens in contempt, and granted 
the motion. The bankruptcy court held 
Citizens in contempt for violation of the 
automatic stay and awarded Strumpf 
attorneys’ fees of $500.00, punitive 
damages of $375.00 and nominal damages of 
$25.00. 

Ironically, the bankruptcy court 
subsequently granted Citizens’ motion for 
relief from the automatic stay on 
December 2, 1991. By that time, however, 
Citizens was unable to effectuate a 


setoff, because Strumpf had withdrawn all 


of the funds from his account after 
Citizens lifted the administrative hold 
in response to the bankruptcy court’s 
November 13, 1991 ruling. 

Citizens appealed the decision of 
the bankruptcy court to the United States 
District Court of Maryland (the Honorable 
John R. Hargrove). The district court 
reversed the bankruptcy court, holding 
that the imposition of an administrative 
hold on Strumpf’s account did not violate 
the automatic stay, where Citizens moved 
immediately for an order seeking 
authority to exercise its setoff rights. 
See Citizens Bank of Maryland v. Strumpf, 
138 B.R. 792, 794 (D. Md. 1992). 

Strumpf then appealed to the United 
States Court of Appeals for the Fourth 
Circuit, which reversed the district 


court’s ruling and remanded the case for 


reinstatement of the bankruptcy court’s 
Order. See Citizens Bank of Maryland v. 
Strumpf, 37 F.3d 155, 159 (4th Cir. 
1994). The opinion of tie court of 
appeals construed the administrative hold 
as “tantamount” to a setoff. The court 
further reasoned that the brief length of 
time between a creditor’s imposition of 
an administr*tive hold and such 
creditor’s request for permission to 
exercise its right of setoff had no 
bearing on whether the creditor violates 


the automatic stay. Id. at 158-59. 


REASONS FOR GRANTING THE PETITION 

I. THIS CASE PROVIDES THE COURT WITH 
THE OPPORTUNITY TO RESOLVE 
WIDESPREAD CONFLICT AMONG LOWER 
COURTS CONCERNING EXERCISE OF THE 
RIGHT OF SETOFF IN BANKRUPTCY. 
Whether a creditor with a right of 

setoff may refuse a bankrupt’s request to 


pay money due to the debtor otherwise 


subject to setoff, pending the bankruptcy 
court’s determination of the creditor’s 
setoff rights, is one of the most 
fundamental unresolved issues of 
insolvency law. During the sixteen years 
since the enactment of the Bankruptcy 
Reform Act of 1978, confusion and 
division on this issue have developed 
unabated. This case presents the Court 
with an opportunity to return the law 
governing setoffs in bankruptcy cases to 
the carefully balanced statutory 
framework established by Congress in the 
Bankruptcy Code. By reviewing the 
decision below, this Court will prevent 
further confusion, among creditors and 
debtors alike, with regard to the proper 
disposition of funds held in a bank 
account which are subject to a creditor’s 


right of setoff and which are treated as 


the bank’s collateral under § 506(a) of 
the Bankruptcy Code. 


A. The Circuit Courts Of Appeals 
Are Divided On The Effect Of A 
Creditor’s Exercise Of Its 
Rights Under § 542 Of 
The Bankruptcy Code. 


In this case, Citizens sought 
permission from the bankruptcy court tu 
exercise its right of setoff against 
Strumpf’s deposit account, to the extent 
of Strumpf’s debt to Citizens. Citizens’ 
right of setoff is recognized expressly 
in § 553 of the Bankruptcy Code. To 
preserve that right of setoff, Citizens 
refused to honor Strumpf’s demand to 
withdraw funds from his deposit account. 
Citizens’ declination to pay money to the 
Debtor to the extent of the Debtor’s 
defaulted loan to Citizens is authorized 
by § 542(b) of the Bankruptcy Code. To 


maintain the status quo and prevent 


» £6 « 


withdrawal of the funds while it sought 
relief from the automatic stay to 
exercise its setoff rights, Citizens 
placed an "administrative hold" on 
Strumpf’s account. 

The Fourth Circuit couched the issue 
as one of "first impression" among the 
circuit courts of appeals. In the narrow 
sense of the issue of the appropriateness 
of the use of an "administrative hold" by 
a commercial bank, that characterization 
is correct. Viewed in a more appropriate 
broader context, however, the 
administrative hold at issue in this case 
is nothing more than a refusal by a 
creditor (here, a bank), which both owes 
money (a deposit account) to a debtor and 
also is owed money by the debtor, to 
honor a debtor’s payment request to the 


extent of the debtor’s obligation. 


ll 


The circuit courts of appeals are 
divided on this broader question of 
whether such a refusal is permitted 
before relief from the automatic stay is 
granted. The Third, Fourth and Eighth 


Circuits have held that it is not. See 


Small Business Admin. v. Reinhardt, 887 
F.2d 165, 169 (8th Cir. 1989) (SBA not | 
permitted to place administrative hold on 
payments due to the debcor under farm 
program); United States v. Reynolds, 764 
F.2d 1004, 1007 (4th Cir. 1985) (IRS not 
permitted to place administrative hold on 
debtor’s tax refund); United States v. 
Norton, 717 F.2d 767, 774 (3rd Cir. 1983) 
(same). These courts hold that an 
administrative hold is equivalent to the 
exercise of setoff rights and, therefore, 
violates the automatic stay of 


§ 362(a)(7) of the Bankruptcy Code. 


12 


Alternatively, the administrative hold is 
viewed as an attempt to exercise control 
over property of the debtor, violative of 
§ 362(a)(3). 

The Eleventh Circuit, by contrast, 
does permit a "hold" of an account, at 
least for a brief period of time, to 
allow a creditor sufficient time to seek 
bankruptcy court approval to exercise its 
setoff rights. In In re Patterson, 967 
F.2d 505 (llth Cir. 1992), a credit union 
"froze" a debtor’s account upon receipt 
of notice that the debtor had commenced a 
case under Chapter 13 of the Bankruptcy 
Code. The credit union filed a proof of 
claim in the debtor’s bankruptcy case, 


noting its security interest in the 


13 


debtor’s account, but otherwise took no 
action to obtain authorization to 
exercise its setoff rights until the 
debtor commenced a turnover proceeding 
and sought an injunction against the 


credit union. Patterson, 967 F.2d at 


508. 


The Patterson court held that the 


credit union, while validly concerned 
that the debtor might dissipate the 
collateral against which it sought to 
exercise its right of setoff, was 
required to act promptly to preserve the 
funds: 
The Credit Union’s 

underlying concern is not 

frivolous, however. The Credit 

Union froze the Pattersons’ 


accounts to avoid paying the 
Pattersons’ checks, which would

into the registry of the

Patterson, 967 F.2d at 510-11. In short, 


the Fleventh Circuit does allow a

The Fifth Circuit also has held that

right of setoff, without incurring

The resolution of the conflict 
between the Third, Fourth and Eighth. 
Circuits, on the one hand, and the
