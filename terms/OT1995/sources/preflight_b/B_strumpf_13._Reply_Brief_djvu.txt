STRUMPF’S ARGUMENT THAT CITIZENS WAIVED

A. Strumpf’s “Waiver” Argument, Not Having Been

This Court granted the petition for a writ of certiorari 
filed by Petitioner, Citizens Bank of Maryland (“Citi- 
zens”’),' to resolve the question presented: “Whether a 
bank creditor may refuse a bankrupt depositor’s request 
to withdraw funds pending a determination by the bank- 
ruptcy court of the bank’s setoff right, without violating 
the bankruptcy stay under 11 U.S.C. §362(a).” See 
Citizens’ Petition for a Writ of Certiorari, filed on Feb- 
ruary 6, 1995 and granted on March 27, 1995. The 
Respondent and debtor in bankruptcy, David Strumpf 
(“Strumpf”’), attempts to avoid this important issue by 
asserting as his lead argument, for the first time at any 
stage of this case, that Citizens waived its setoff right by 
failing to object to the confirmation of Strumpf’s Chap-

Brief”) at 7-12. 


This argument was never raised below, in the bank- 
ruptcy court, the district court, or the court of appeals. 
Having never been asserted below, Strumpf’s waiver argu- 
ment was never mentioned by either the bankruptcy

(J.A. 27a-38a). The Fourth Circuit likewise did not 
address the issue in its opinion. (J.A. 39a-46a).

Without cross-petitioning for certiorari, a prevailing

ground properly raised below, whether or not that

provided that an affirmance on the alternative 
ground would neither expand nor contract the rights 
of either party established by the judgment below.

sider issues not raised in the petitioner’s question pre-

498 U.S. 517, 111 S. Ct. 913, 917 (1991) (Court rejected

B. Section 553(a) Of The Bankruptcy Code Ex- 
pressly Preserves Citizens’ Right Of Setoff. 


Even if this Court were to depart from its general 
practice and consider Strumpf’s “waiver” argument, the 
argument should be rejected as without merit. Strumpf 
cites no case or other authority in support of his argu- 
ment, other than an unexplained citation to 11 U.S.C. 
§1327(a). Section 1327(a) simply provides that the 
terms of a confirmed Chapter 13 plan bind the debtor 
and each creditor, “whether or not the claim of such 
creditor is provided for by the plan, and whether or not 


5 


such creditor has objected to . . . the plan.” 11 U.S.C. 
§ 1327(a) (1988). 


In this case, Strumpf’s Chapter 13 plan provided that 
unsecured creditors would be paid “‘pro-rata” from his 
monthly payments of $100 to the Chapter 13 trustee. 
Citizens’ right to receive payments under the plan as an 
unsecured creditor is not at issue in this case. The plan 
makes no mention of Citizens or Citizens’ right of setoff, 
which is the issue before this court. (J.A. 3a-4a). It 
certainly does not provide that Citizens would forfeit its 
setoff right against Strumpf’s bank deposits at Citizens. 
Id. Accordingly, §1327(a) in no way supports Strumpf’s 
attempt to challenge Citizens’ setoff right. 


Moreover, Strumpf’s argument ignores 11 U.S.C. 
§553(a), which provides in relevant part: 


Except as otherwise provided in this section and 
in sections 362 and 363 of this title, this title does 
not affect any right of a creditor to offset a mutual 
debt owing by such creditor to the debtor that 
arose before the commencement of the case... . 


11 U.S.C. §553(a) (1988).2 Section 553(a), which spe- 
ceifically governs the treatment of setoff rights in the 
Bankruptcy Code (“the Code”), thus precludes an 
argument that §1327(a), or any other Code provision 
(other than the expressly excepted § §362 and 363),° 
deprived Citizens of its setoff right. See In re Tillery, 
179 B.R. 576, 578-79 (Bankr. W.D. Ark. 1995) (holding

CITIZENS’ REFUSAL TO PERMIT STRUMPF TO WITH- 
DRAW BANK FUNDS PENDING THE BANKRUPTCY 
COURT’S DETERMINATION OF CITIZENS’ SETOFF 
RIGHT WAS NOT ITSELF A SETOFF AND DID NOT 
VIOLATE THE AUTOMATIC STAY. 


In addressing the question presented, Strumpf argues 
that Citizens’ imposition of an administrative freeze on 
his bank account, while simultaneously seeking relief 
from the automatic stay, violated 11 U.S.C. §362(a)(7), 
(a)(3), and (a)(6). See Respondent’s Brief at 12-16. 
These arguments, which for the most part are addressed 
in the Brief for the Petitioner (‘‘Petitioner’s Brief’’) at 
26-31, are unsound. Stated succinctly, “[t]he freeze 
should not be considered a violation of the stay.” 4 
James W. Moore & Lawrence P. King, Collier on Bank- 
ruptcy, 9553.15, at 553-87 (15th ed. 1995). 


A. An Administrative Hold Is Not A Setoff And 
Does Not Violate Section 362(a)(7). 


In the Petitioner’s Brief, Citizens explains that a 
bank’s imposition of an administrative hold on a bank 
account pending determination of the bank’s motion for 
relief from the automatic stay is not an act of setoff as 


— ——¢-——— >» 


7 


contemplated by 11 U.S.C. §362(a)(7). See Petitioner’s 
Brief at 26-29, 35-36. The starting point for this analysis 
is a determination of when a setoff occurs. The pre- 
dominant view, articulated in the oft-cited case of Baker 
v. Nat'l City Bank of Cleveland, 511 F.2d 1016 (6th 
Cir. 1975), is that an act of setoff is not complete until 
three steps are taken: 


(1) the decision to exercise the right; 


(2) some action which accomplishes the setoff; 
and 


(3) some record which evidences that the right of 
setoff has been exercised. 


Id. at 1018. An administrative hold like the one imple- 
mented by Citizens lacks all three of the elements of a 
setoff. By seeking a bankruptcy court order modifying 
the stay to permit the setoff, Citizens clearly evidenced 
an intent to defer exercise of its right of setoff until the 
bankruptcy court authorized Citizens to act upon the 
right. The administrative hold alone does not accomplish 
a setoff, but rather preserves the status quo pending 
bankruptcy court consideration of the matter. See 
Petitioner’s Brief at 26-29. 


The Respondent’s Brief does not address Citizens’ 
explanation that an administrative hold is not a setoff. 
Instead, Strumpf does nothing other than restate the 
Fourth Circuit’s conclusion and cite to two opinions 
relied on by the Fourth Circuit, United States v. Reyn- 
olds, 764 F.2d 1004 (4th Cir. 1985), and United States 
v. Norton, 717 F.2d 767 (3rd Cir. 1983), and one opin- 
ion which expressly declined to address the issue of 
whether a freeze constitutes a setoff, In re Patterson, 
967 F.2d 505, 513 (11th Cir. 1992) (“This argument 
raises the issue whether a freeze constitutes a setoff, per 
se. However, we need not address this issue because the 


bankruptcy court based its holding on more than the 
freeze alone.”’”). See Respondent’s Brief at 12. Strumpf’s 
argument thus suffers from the same five fundamental 
mistakes made by the Fourth Circuit below, and is not 
persuasive. See Petitioner’s Brief at 31-38. 


B. An Administrative Hold Is Not An Act To 
Exercise Control Over Property Of The Estate 
And Does Not Violate Section 362(a)(3). 


Strumpf readily concedes that the funds he deposited 
at Citizens were the property of Citizens and not the 
estate. See Respondent’s Brief at 13. Nevertheless, 
Strumpf argues that his right to withdraw money from 
the account was “at least an equitable right” which 
became property of the estate upon his bankruptcy 
filing, and that Citizens’ refusal to permit Strumpf to 
withdraw all of his bank deposits was an act to control 
property of the estate prohibited by §362(a)(3). Jd. 
Strumpf’s argument, made without support from any 
authority, is premised on a false assumption, namely 
that Strumpf had an absolute right to withdraw the 
funds on deposit at Citizens at the time Citizens imposed 
the freeze. 


The relationship between a bank and its depositor is 
contractual. Carman v. Franklin Bank, 61 Md. 467, 
469 (1884) (“The deposit of money in bank, by one in 
his own name and in his own right, creates, no doubt, the 
relation of debtor and creditor—the contract on the part 
of the bank being to pay the checks of the depositor, so 
long as it has funds in hand sufficient to do so.’’). The 
contractual rights that become the property of the bank- 
ruptcy estate are exactly those the depositor had prior 
to bankruptcy. A bankruptcy filing does not expand the 
debtor’s contractual rights: “Though this paragraph 


n) 


NE 


9 


[§541(a)] will include choses in action and claims by the 
debtor against others, it is not intended to expand the 
debtor’s rights against others more than they exist at the 
commencement of the case.” H.R. Rep. No. 95-595, 
95th Cong., lst Sess. at 367 (1977), reprinted in, 1978 
U.S.C.C.A.N. 5787, 6323; S. Rep. No. 95-989, 95th 
Cong., 2nd Sess. at 82 (1978), reprinted in, 1978 
U.S.C.C.A.N. 5787, 5868. Accord 4 James W. Moore & 
Lawrence P. King, Collier on Bankruptcy, 9541.01, at 
541-7 (15th ed. 1995). A depositor’s contractual right to 
withdraw funds from a bank account is not absolute. 


In the instant case, it is undisputed that the parties’ 
contractual relationship granted Citizens a right to set off 
funds in Strumpf’s account to the extent of his debt to 
the bank. It is further conceded that Strumpf was in 
default and, prior to the bankruptcy filing, Citizens 
could have exercised its right of setoff. Strumpf’s right 
to withdraw funds subject to Citizens’ setoff right was, 
therefore, at Citizens’ discretion; Strumpf did not have 
the absolute right to withdraw the funds. Upon Strumpf’s 
bankruptcy filing, this underlying relationship did not 
change. Citizens’ contractual rights were not diminished, 
and Strumpf’s contractual rights did not expand to pro- 
vide him with an absolute right of withdrawal that did 
not exist prior to his bankruptcy filing. 

As Citizens set forth in the Petitioner’s Brief, 11 U.S.C. 
§542(b) expressly entitles a creditor to retain funds 
otherwise payable to the debtor to the extent the credi- 
tor holds a right of setoff. See Petitioner’s Brief at 22-24. 
Strumpf’s §362(a)(3) argument has been rejected by 
several courts which recognized that the language of 
§542(b) prevails over §362(a)(3), including In re 
Williams, 61 B.R. 567, 573 (Bankr. N.D. Tex. 1986): 


10 


Because Section 542(b) provides that a creditor who 
has a right of offset does not have to turnover the 
funds, it unmistakably follows that the person can 
continue to hold the funds (i.e., an “administrative 
freeze” or “hold”) until an order is entered by a 
bankruptcy court providing for disposition of the 
funds in question. 


Citizens’ exercise of this right thus is not an act to 
exercise control over Strumpf’s right of withdrawal, 
because Strumpf’s right of withdrawal was limited by 
Citizens’ right to retain the funds subject to setoif. A 
freeze of an account, unlike a setoff, simply maintains 
the status guo and does not violate the automatic stay. 
In re Tillery, 179 B.R. 576, 580 (Bankr. W.D. Ark. 


1995). 


C. An Administrative Hold Is Not An Act Seeking 
To Collect Or Recover A Claim And Does Not 
Violate Section 362(a)(6). 


Strumpf also argues that Citizens’ administrative hold 
violated §362(a)(6), which provides that the automatic 
stay acts as a stay of “‘any act to collect, assess, or recover 
a claim against the debtor ....” 11 U.S.C. §362(a)(6) 
(1988). In making this argument, Strumpf relies on two 
cases, In re Patterson, 967 F.2d 505 (11th Cir. 1992), 
and In re Figgers, 121 B.R. 772 (Bankr. S.D. Ohio 


1990).* 


*Strumpf also cites United States v. Norton, 717 F.2d 767 
(3rd Cir. 1983), but that case does not even discuss § 362(a)(6). 
As explained in the Petitioner’s Brief, Norton found that a freeze 
constituted an act of setoff under the “‘automatic setoff” doctrine 
peculiar to Pennsylvania law, and therefore found a violation of 


§ 362(a)(7). 


11 


Patterson does hold that a credit union’s freeze of the 
debtor’s deposit account was a violation of §362(a)(6), 
but in so holding the Eleventh Circuit mistakenly assumed 
that the funds in the account were property of the 
bankruptcy estate, an assumption which even Strumpf 
acknowledges is wrong. See Patterson, 967 F.2d at 511. 
The Eleventh Circuit also premised its conclusion on the 
fact that the credit union coupled the administrative 
freeze with an attempt to coerce the debtors to pay their 
debt to the credit union. Jd. at 512. 


The court in Jn re Figgers also concluded that the 
credit union’s conduct in that case violated § 362(a)(6), 
but the facts in that case were quite different from the 
typical bank freeze case like the present one. In Figgers, 
as of the filing of the debtors’ bankruptcy petition there 
was only $39.91 in the debtors’ credit union account. 
The debtors made postbankruptcy deposits in the account, 
and by the time the credit union imposed a freeze based 
upon a prebankruptcy loan, it appears that there was 
$496.49 in the account. The freeze imposed by the credit 
union covered the entire account. See Figgers, 121 B.R. at 
774, Consequently, the credit union clearly did violate the 
automatic stay, because it had no right of setoff against 
most of the funds it held. Moreover, the court’s discus- 
sion of the law is not illuminating, as the court does not 


consider or even mention §542(b), the focal point of the 
issue before this Court. 


Recognition of a depository institution’s right under 
§542(b) to refuse to pay its debt to a bankrupt deposi- 
tor to the extent of its setoff right, coupled with an 
understanding that the funds on deposit are the institu- 
tion’s property and not the estate’s, should dispel any 
confusion suffered by the lower courts regarding the 


12 


propriety of an administrative hold. As pointed out in 
the Petitioner’s Brief, the Bankruptcy Code’s restric- 
tions on a creditor’s exercise of a setoff right on the 
one hand, and on a debtor’s ability to obtain a turnover 
of funds from the creditor on the other hand, creates a 
purposeful statutory equilibrium which entitles the 
parties to protect their respective rights by maintaining 
the status quo as of the bankruptcy filing, absent a 
bankruptcy court order altering this balance. See Peti- 
tioner’s Brief at 24-25. By imposing a fréeze on Strumpf’s 
account, Citizens merely exercised its right under §542(b) 
to decline to pay a debt subject to setoff, to preserve the

STRUMPF’S RECOMMENDATION FOR COURTS TO 
ADOPT SPECIAL RULES FOR TAXING AUTHORITIES 
TO AVOID “A PLETHORA OF UNNECESSARY LITIGA- 
TION” (1) WILL NOT HELP MOST CREDITORS, AND 
(2) OFFERS AN INADEQUATE SOLUTION TO A PROB- 
LEM THAT DOES NOT EXIST UNDER A CORRECT 
READING OF THE BANKRUPTCY CODE. 


In Part IV of the Respondent’s Brief, Strumpf attempts 
to address the concerns of the United States with respect 
to tax refunds and to debtors who owe income taxes to 
the Government. Under Citizens’ view, the right of the 
United States to refuse to pay income tax refunds to a 
debtor to the extent of the Government’s right of setoff 
is fully protected under §542(b). According to Strumpf's 
position, which was adopted by the Fourth Circuit 
below, the United States would be required to file ex 
parte motions for relief from the automatic stay in 
countless cases to avoid having to pay money to debtors 
who owe money to the Government. 


Strumpf acknowledges that the United States’ con- 
cerns are “legitimate”, and that the prospect of numerous

creditor’s claim is disputed or the creditor is not a taxing 
authority. Third and most importantly, the problems

considers a creditor’s right under §542(b) without read- 
ing the language of §362(a) in isolation. See Tillery, 
179 B.R. at 580 (“In Strumpf, the Fourth Circuit looked 
solely to the language of section 362, [and] ignored the 
provisions of sections 506, 542, 553... .”). 


The concerns of the United States which Strumpf 
recognizes as legitimate are concerns of bank and non- 
bank creditors alike. The Bankruptcy Code properly 
addresses these concerns through a statutory equilib- 
rium which balances a debtor’s interests in obtaining a
