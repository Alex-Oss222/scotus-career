506(a), 362(d) and 363(e)?

Summary of Argument 


In holding that an administrative freeze violates the 
automatic stay, the Fourth Circuit misconstrued the 
Bankruptcy Code. 

First, the holding below nullifies the protection that the 
Bankruptcy Code explicitly and comprehensively accords a 
creditor with a right of setoff. (Point I) Although the exercise 
of the right of setoff is automatically stayed by a bankruptcy 
filing (11 U.S.C. § 362(a)(7)), the Code authorizes such a 
creditor to withhold payment of a debt owed the estate to the 
extent the debt is subject to setoff (11 U.S.C. § 542(b)), 
preserves the creditor’s right of setoff under nonbankruptcy 
law (11 U.S.C. § 553(a)), provides such a creditor with a 
secured claim (11 U.S.C. §506(a)), authorizes the creditor to 
seek relief from the automatic stay (11 U.S.C. § 362(d)), and 
stipulates that estate property subject to a right of setoff may 
not be used unless the creditor’s right of setoff is adequately 
protected (11 U.S.C. § 363(e)). The decision below sweeps 
away these interlocking statutory provisions that carefully 
accommodate the competing interests of the estate and of a 
creditor having a right of setoff, by interpreting the automatic 
stay as prohibiting a depository institution from even placing 
a hold on a debtor’s account to the extent necessary to 
maintain the status quo with respect to the institution’s right 
of setoff. If a debtor can require a depository institution that 
is also a creditor to pay an account balance that would 
otherwise be subject to the institution’s right of setoff, the 
debtor can unilaterally abrogate and nullify the rights afforded 
by the Code to such a creditor. 


4 


Second, the court of appeals erroneously held that an 
administrative freeze “is tantamount to the exercise of a right 
of setoff and thus violates the automatic stay of § 362(a)(7).” 
JA 43a; 37 F.3d at 158. (Point IT) In so holding, the court of 
appeals disregarded widely-accepted prerequisites of a setoff, 
which a freeze does not satisfy. The court of appeals also 
failed to reconcile its view concerning § 362(a)(7) with the 
Code provisions that preserve a creditor’s right of setoff, as 
discussed above. Instead of construing the stay to preserve 
the status quo, the court of appeals interpreted the stay to 
enable a debtor to alter the status quo by defeating the setoff 
rights of a creditor. 


Construing the automatic stay as preventing a creditor 
from maintaining an existing setoff right results in a 
deprivation of property without due process of law, in viola- 
tion of the Fifth Amendment to the Constitution of the United 
States. (Point III) Finally, the result below contradicts the 
policy objectives as well as the express provisions of the 
Code. It penalizes a creditor for prepetition forbearance; 
forces increased bankruptcy filings by encouraging depository 
institutions to act quickly to exercise setoff rights when 
borrowers default outside of bankruptcy; and necessitates 
expensive and unnecessary emergency litigation at the 
beginning of a bankruptcy case. It will cause increased bank 
losses and more stringent credit terms. (Point IV)

I, 


The Bankruptcy Code Authorizes 
an Administrative Freeze. 


The commencement of a bankruptcy case creates an 
estate. 11 U.S.C. § 541(a). The estate consists of “all legal 
or equitable interests of the debtor in property as of the

or a specific fund or other identifiable property of the 
institution. Barnhill v. Johnson, 503 U.S. 393, 398 (1992). 
Through a carefully constructed statutory arrangement, in 
Section 553 and in several additional sections, the Bankruptcy

The turnover obligation of an entity (such as a 
depository institution) that owes a debt that is property of a 
bankruptcy estate (such as a prepetition deposit account) is

“(b) Except as provided in subsection (c) or (d) 
of this section, an entity that owes a debt that is 
property of the estate and that is matured, payable 
on demand, or payable on order, shall pay such 
debt to, or on the order of, the trustee, except to 
the extent that such debt may be offset under 
Section 553 of this title against a claim against the 
debtor.”

action which does not change the substantive rights of the 
parties. It prevents nullification of the creditor's setoff right

creditor may move for relief from the automatic stay, and if 
such relief is granted execute the setoff. Assuming a setoff

creditor may continue to withhold payment of the amount 
subject to a right of setoff. Indeed, the Code specifically 
provides a creditor that has a right of setoff with a secured 
claim to the extent of such right. 11 U.S.C. § 506(a). In

Section 542(b) and simply maintains the status quo. While it 
is true that a hold prevents a debtor from obtaining payment 
of an account balance, a debtor is not entitled under 
Section 363 to use such balance without obtaining court 
approval, which at the creditor’s request must be conditioned 
on providing adequate protection. 

The court of appeals gave short shrift to the Code provi- 
sions that protect a creditor’s right of setoff. Section 542(b) 
is mentioned in a footnote (JA 41a; 37 F.3d at 157 n.*) but 
is otherwise ignored, even though it should have been dis- 
positive of the issue before the court of appeals. The court of 
appeals devoted more attention to Section 553(a), but 
construed Section 362(a)(7) in a way that nullifies the setoff 
rights that Section 553 explicitly preserves. Similarly, the 
court of appeals acknowledged that a creditor is entitled to 
request relief from the automatic stay under Section 362, but 
nonetheless deemed a refusal to pay an account balance while 
the request is pending as a violation of the stay. As a result, 
the creditor would be powerless to prevent a debtor from 
rendering moot the request for relief from the automatic stay. 
The court of appeals made no reference to Section 363 or any 
of its limitations on a trustee’s powers or its protection of 
rights of creditors. 


The justification offered by the court of appeals for an 
interpretation of Section 362(a)(7) that effectively repeals 
numerous creditor protections explicitly set forth elsewhere 
in the Code was not based on the words of the statute. Rather 
it was based on a policy argument, contained in the 
conclusory assertion that “If a bank could freeze the debtor’s 
accounts upon the filing of a petition in bankruptcy, the 
debtor’s chances for successful rehabilitation would be 
substantially diminished.” JA 45a; 37 F.3d at 159 (quoting 


10 


United States v. Norton, 717 F.2d 767, 773 (3d Cir. 1983)). 
Even assuming this to be so, the Code does not provide for 
rehabilitation of a debtor at all costs; rather that goal must be 
pursued in comity with the rights of creditors. Rights 
accorded creditors by the Code may not be written out of the 
Code by a court, even though such rights may hinder or 
defeat a particular debtor's rehabilitation. Norwest Bank 
Worthington v. Ahlers, 485 U.S. 197, 206-07 (1988); Air 
Atlanta, Inc. v. National Bank of Georgia, 81 Bankr. 724 
(N.D. Ga. 1987). 


Moreover, it is not at all clear why a freeze that protects 
a right of setoff should prevent successful implementation of 
a chapter 13 plan, the objective of which is to enable a debtor 
to pay creditor claims from disposable income during the 
term of the plan. Such a plan must in any event provide that 
a secured creditor retains its lien (i.e., in this co itext, its 
right of setoff). 11 U.S.C. § 1325(a)(5). The debtor in the 
meantime can seek relief from the court to obtain access to its 
deposit account, provided that the depository institution is 
afforded adequate protection. 


In sum, the court of appeals erred by failing to give 
effect to the limitations on a depository institution’s turnover 
obligation under Section 542(b), the preservation of the 
institution’s right of setoff under Section 553, the institution’s 
right to seek relief from the automatic stay under 
Section 362(d) (which would be nullified should the 
institution be required to pay the account balance subject to 
setoff without being provided with adequate protection), and 
limitations upon the power of a debtor to use cash collateral 
under Section 363 (c) and (e) (which require court approval 
and adequate protection). The Fourth Circuit swept away all 
the foregoing rights of the institution, and limitations on the 
powers of the debtor, based on the mistaken conclusion that 
a freeze in these circumstances is “tantamount to setoff” and 
is thus stayed by Section 362.

Il. 


A Freeze Is Not a Setoff and Thus 
Does Not Violate Section 362(a). 


The filing of a bankruptcy petition “operates as a stay, 
applicable to all entities, of . . . the setoff of any debt owing 
to the debtor that arose before the commencement of the case 
under this title against any claim against the debtor.” 
11 U.S.C. § 362(a)(7). 

By its terms, Section 362 (a) provides for a “stay.” That 
is, it operates to maintain the status quo, protecting the estate 
from dismemberment while the case is being administered. 
The fundamental error of the court of appeals was to treat the 
automatic stay as a substantive rather than a procedural 
provision, to abrogate and defeat a creditor’s substantive right 
of setoff. The court of appeals’ decision alters the status quo 
which Section 362 is designed to preserve by prohibiting the 
creditor from maintaining it. 


A freeze is not a setoff because a freeze does not change 
the debtor’s account balance or loan balance or the parties’ 
obligations. A  setoff, by contrast, changes all three. 
Moreover, a setoff has not occurred unless there has been 
(1) a decision to effectuate a setoff, (2) an overt act accom- 
plishing the setoff, and (3) a record of the setoff. See, e.g., 
Horton Dairy, Inc. v. United States, 986 F.2d 286, 291 
(8th Cir. 1993). The status quo is altered when a setoff is 
effected (because the loan and account balance are reduced to 
the extent they are offsetting) or when the account balance 
subject to a right of setoff is withdrawn by a debtor; a freeze 
maintains the status quo (i.e., the existing account balance

The court of appeals sought to justify its equation of a 
freeze with a setoff by observing that “both effectively 
deprive the debtor of use of the funds.” JA 43a; 37 F.3d at 
158. In fact, Section 363(c) prohibits the use of cash 
collateral (such as a deposit account subject to a mght of 
setoff) without court approval or consent of the creditor. An 
administrative freeze may prevent a debtor from ignoring the 
requirements of Section 363(c), but a creditor does not violate 
the automatic stay merely by requiring the debtor to follow 
procedures that the Code independently requires. Moreover, 
as set forth above, the creditor’s withholding of payment is 
expressly authorized by Section 542(b). In expanding the 
meaning of “setoff” to include the mere withholding of 
payment, the court of appeals erroneously disregarded the 
established elements of a completed setoff (which the mere 
withholding of payment does not satisfy), and thereby brought 
Section 362(a)(7) into conflict with the Code provisions that 
preserve setoff rights. 


The court of appeals did not analyze the relationship 
between Section 362(a) and the other Code provisions that are 
relevant to the issue presented, nor did the court of appeals 
attempt to reconcile its position that a freeze is “tantamount 
to a setoff” with the material substantive differences between 
the two. Instead, the court of appeals relied upon three other 
appellate decisions, Small Business Admin. v. Rinehart, 887 
F.2d 165 (8th Cir. 1989) and United States v. Reynolds, 764 
F.2d 1004 (4th Cir. 1985), both of which in turn rely upon 
United States v. Norton, 717 F.2d 767 (3d Cir. 1983). None 
of those earlier court of appeals decisions reconciles its 
holding with the Code provisions protecting rights of setoff

or the generally-recognized prerequisites of setoff, and 
bankruptcy courts in other judicial circuits, recognizing these 
defects, have refused to follow Norton and its progeny. See, 
e.g., In re Tillery, 1995 WL 139231 (Bankr. W.D. Ark. 
1995). 


In fact, Norton, the foundation of these court of appeals 
decisions, itself rests upon a peculiarity of the law of 
Pennsylvania and is therefore irrelevant to this and most other 
cases. See United States v. Norton, supra, 717 F.2d at 772.° 
Even when that peculiar rule is applicable, Section 362(a)(7) 
prevents occurrence of a setoff by operation of law, so that 
the creditor may maintain the status quo by withholding 
payment of an account balance to the extent the creditor has 
a right of setoff, as provided in Section 542(b). None of the 
courts of appeals that has addressed this issue has given 
proper consideration to Section 542(b) or to the peculiarity of 
Pennsylvania law upon which Norton was based. 


* Norton looked to Pennsylvania law to determine whether a setoff had 
occurred and concluded that under Pennsylvania law a setoff is 
accomplished when a creditor gives sufficient evidence of intent to 
make a setoff, and that the retention of a debtor's funds by a creditor 
is sufficient evidence of that intent. United States v. Norton, 717 F.2d 
at 772. This statement of Pennsylvania law in Norton was at variance 
with the Third Circuit's earlier decision in Pittsburgh National Bank 
v. United States, 657 F.2d 36 (3d Cir. 1981), which found that under 
Pennsylvania law a setoff occurs by operation of law the moment a 
creditor’s claim matures. Had the Third Circuit in Norton adhered to 
Pittsburgh National Bank's reading of Pennsylvania law, it would have 
concluded that either a setoff occurred by operation of law prior to the 
bankruptcy filing (in which case the creditor would no longer owe the 
debt that had been offset) or that the automatic stay prevented a setoff 
by operation of law post-petition. In neither case was Norton justified 
in equating a withholding of funds with a setoff. Reinhart, Reynolds

The only additional statutory authority cited by any of 
these decisions is Section 362(a)(3), which provides for a stay 
of any act “to exercise control over property of the estate.” 
See Small Business Admin. v. Rinehart, supra, 887 F.2d at

broadly as to cover a refusal to pay a debt where such refusal 
is authorized by the Code, and an administrative freeze is 
nothing more than that. The language of Section 362(a)(3) 
must be interpreted in harmony with other provisions of the 
Code, and in particular should not be read to stay conduct 
that is otherwise authorized by the Code.’ Jn re Lough, 163

In the case of a deposit account, the estate’s property 
right is a claim against the depository: institution; the estate 
has no property interest in the institution’s cash, which is the 
institution’s property. The institution has no control over 
the estate’s claim against the institution, and the refusal to 
pay the balance (unlike a setoff) does not extinguish or 
otherwise alter the claim. Nonpayment of a debt that is 
property of the estate should not be deemed a violation of the 
automatic stay when nonpayment simply preserves a right of 
setoff and is thus authorized explicitly by Section 542(b). 
Indeed, under Section 542(b), a right of setoff is a defense to 
a demand for turnover. Stephenson v. Salisbury, supra, 
967 F.2d at 1077. The assertion of the defense provided by 
the Code cannot be a violation of the automatic stay.

leads to results that plainly were not intended. For example, because 
Section 362(a)(3) applies to all entities, if read in isolation it would 
prohibit a debtor or trustee, as well as a creditor or other entity, from 
exercising any control over property of the estate.

The court of appeals erred by applying Section 362(a) 
not as a stay, but to defeat substantive rights, including rights 
specifically provided in Sections 553(a) and 542(b). The 
automatic stay, like the exception under Section 542(b) to the 
obligation to make payment of a debt owing to the estate, is 
intended to preserve the status quo. A depository institution’s 
nonpayment of an account balance subject to a right of setoff 
preserves the status quo until the court authorizes the creditor 
to effect a setoff, or the debtor to use the account balance 
after providing adequate protection, or the matter is resolved 
through a plan or liquidation, in which case the creditor’s 
rights as the holder of a secured claim would be recognized. 
The result below, by contrast, defeats the status quo and the 
creditor’s right of setoff by permitting a debtor to obtain 
payment in full of the debtor’s account balance. 


Il. 


Construing the Automatic Stay to 
Prohibit an Administrative Freeze 


Results in a Deprivation of Property 
Without Due Process of Law. 


A depository institution’s right of setoff is not only a 
contractual right, but also a property interest in the debtor’s 
account balance. The property interest arises from setoff 
rights that are widely recognized by statute and by common 
law, and from Section 506(a) of the Bankruptcy Code, 
providing that a creditor with a right of setoff holds a secured 
claim to the extent of the setoff right. See In re Lawndale 
Steel Co., 155 B.R. 990, 992 (Bankr. N.D. Ill. 1993). A 
secured creditor’s interest in a debtor’s property is a property 
interest of which a creditor may not be deprived without due 
process of law. United States v. Security Industrial Bank, 459 
U.S. 70 (1982). See U.S. CONST., amend. V. 


16 


The Bankruptcy Code, as any other statute, should be 
construed so as not to conflict with the Constitution. Edward 
J. DeBartolo Corp. v. Florida Gulf Coast Bldg. & Constr. 
Trades Council, 485 U.S. 568 (1988). Interpretation of the 
Bankruptcy Code in a manner that nullifies a creditor’s right 
of setoff creates a wholly unnecessary conflict with the 
Constitution. The constitutional conflict is avoided by giving 
effect to the explicit protection of creditor interests set forth 
in Section 542(b) and in the other Code provisions discussed 
above. 


IV. 


The Rule Applied by the Court of Appeals 
Will Have Adverse Consequences for 
Debtor-Creditor Relations and for the 
Administration of Bankruptcy Cases. 


The rule applied by the court of appeals encourages 
prepetition setoffs and emergency litigation at the commence- 
ment of a bankruptcy case, neither of which is desirable. 

A financial institution that has an opportunity to exercise 
a right of setoff (typically, because a borrower has defaulted 
under a loan agreement) faces a prepetition choice between 
action (implementing the setoff) and forbearance. Forbearance 
provides the best opportunity for rehabilitation. Prepetition 
setoff encourages bankruptcy. The Code recognizes the 
desirability of forbearance by preserving the creditor’s right 
of setoff as described above and thereby protecting the 
creditor after the petition is filed. 


The Fourth Circuit’s policy choice, that a freeze would 
diminish a debtor’s chances for rehabilitation, 37 F.3d at 
159, is at odds with the policy of Section 553 and the other 
provisions described above and penalizes the creditor for for- 
bearing. Instead of encouraging financial institutions to give 


17 


borrowers leeway to straighten out their affairs without 
having to file under the Code, the decision below discourages 
forbearance and will result in more bankruptcy filings. As 
such, the decision is utterly at odds with established 
bankruptcy policy. 


Section 542(b) clearly contemplates that a depository 
institution with a right of setoff may withhold payment of an 
account balance to the extent it is subject to setoff. The 
creditor may then move for relief from the automatic stay if 
it chooses to do so. The debtor may apply for permission to 
use cash collateral if it chooses to do so. Or the parties may 
negotiate a compromise, thus minimizing the need for judicial 
involvement. 


The decision of the court of appeals, by contrast, makes 
it virtually impossible for a depository institution to preserve 
a right of setoff. A depository institution could conceivably 
obtain ex parte an order authorizing a freeze pending a 
hearing on a request for relief from the automatic stay, as 
suggested by the Fourth Circuit, see JA 41a-42a; 37 F.3d at 
157-58, but the timing and result are uncertain, and there is 
nothing in the Code that mandates or encourages ex parte 
litigation to preserve rights the Code already affords. If the 
debtor demands the account balance before a court order 
authorizes a freeze, in the court of appeals’ view the 
institution is powerless to preserve its right of setoff. By 
writing the Section 542(b) exception out of the Code, this 
result also makes it incumbent upon the depository institution 
to commence emergency litigation, with attendant expense to 
the creditor and the debtor and burden upon the bankruptcy 
court, if the creditor is to have any hope of preserving its 
setoff right. Such litigation is obviated, without compromising 
the substantive rights of the debtor or the creditor, if 


18 


Section 542(b) and Section 363, along with Section 362, are 
recognized as controlling." 

In sum, the Bankruptcy Code already provides the rules 
and procedures applicable in these circumstances, and there 
are good reasons not to substitute the court of appeals’ 
suggested policies and procedures for what the Code requires.
