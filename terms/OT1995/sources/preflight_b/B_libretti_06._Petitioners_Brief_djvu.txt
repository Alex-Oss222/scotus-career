STATUTORY AND CONSTITUTIONAL 
PROVISIONS INVOLVED 


Rule 11(f) of the Federal Rules of Criminal Procedure 
provides: 

(f). Determining the Accuracy of the Plea. Not- 

withstanding the acceptance of a plea of guilty, the 

court should not enter a judgment upon such plea 

without niaking such inquiry as shall satisfy it that 

there is a factual basis for the plea. 

The Sixth Amendment of the United States Constitution 
provides, in pertinent part: 

In all criminal prosecutions, the accused shall enjoy 

the right to a speedy and public trial, by an impar- 

tial jury of the State and district wherein the crime 

shall have been committed, which district shall have 

previously been ascertained by law * * * . 


The pertinent portions of Rules 7, 11, 23, 31, 32, and 54 
of the Federal Rules of Criminal Procedure, and 21 U.S.C. 
§ 853(a), (nm) and (p) are reprinted infra at App. 1-6. 
STATEMENT 


The district court accepted petitioner’s guilty plea to the 
continuing criminal enterprise (CCE) count of a multicount 


2 


indictment. The plea was based upon a plea agreement that 
included a forfeiture provision. At sentencing the court 
rejected petitioner’s objection that the government had not 
shown a factual basis for the forfeiture, Petitioner appealed 
from the forfeiture order, and also reiterated his objections in 
the district court. The district court amended its forfeiture 
order, holding that a fact-finding hearing would be necessary 
to determine whether all of the assets covered in the order 
were subject to forfeiture. This order was stayed pending the 
appeal. The court of appeals concluded that the district court 
lost jurisdiction once the notice of appeal was filed, and the 
appeals court affirmed the original forfeiture order. 


The Indictment 


On May 22, 1992, the grand jury in the district of 
Wyoming returned an eleven-count superseding indictment 
charging petitioner with drug, weapons, and money-launder- 
ing offenses. J.A. 38-71. Count 6 of the indictment,' to which 
petitioner ultimately pleaded guilty, charged that from 1984 to 
1992 petitioner engaged in a continuing criminal enterprise. 
J.A. 56-63; see 21 U.S.C. § 848. The indictment charged that 
petitioner operated a cocaine and marijuana distribution orga- 
nization in Wyoming and Colorado, and that he used his 
federal firearms license to launder drug proceeds by purchas- 
ing firearms.

SUMMARY OF ARGUMENT 


1. When a defendant pleads guilty, Rule 11(f) of the 
Federal Rules of Criminal Procedure requires the court to 
“satisfy itself that there is a factual basis for the plea” before 
the entry of judgment. If the case involves criminal forfeiture, 
Rule 11(f) should require a factual basis for both components 
of the plea: (1) the defendant's admission that he committed 
an offense charged in the indictment, and (2) the defendant's 
admission that some or all of the property named in the 
indictment is subject to forfeiture. 


Requiring a factual basis for the forfeiture component of 
the plea is necessary to carry out the core function of Rule 11: 
assuring that the plea is knowing, intelligent, and voluntary. if 
the court finds that there is no factual basis for the forfeiture 
to which a defendant has agreed, this is a strong indicatior 


that his plea is not knowing and intelligent. In such a case, the 
defendant may not have understood that neither what he has 
admitted nor what the government can prove would establish 
a basis for forfeiture, and he may have agreed to the forfeiture 
because of this fundamental misunderstanding. 

The factual basis inquiry should also bring to light any 
misunderstanding by the defendant regarding the scope of the 
forfeiture to which he has agreed. A plea is not knowing and 
intelligent unless a defendant understands the direct conse- 
quences of his piea, and criminal forfeiture is one of the most 
significant direct consequences of the plea. That is certainly 
the case here, where the plea agreement has been interpreted 
as allowing forfeiture of all of the defendant's property, 
including substantial legitimate income and other admittedly 
untainted property. 

A factual basis inquiry under Rule 11(f) is also a neces- 
sary correlative to Rules 7, 31, and 32 of the Federal Rules of 
Criminal Procedure, which establish the procedural frame- 
work for criminal forfeiture. These rules require that criminal 
forfeiture be charged in the indictment, found by the jury in a 
special verdict, and recorded in a judgment of forfeiture. 
These rules reflect a decision to treat forfeiture as a substan- 
tive charge or element of criminal liability, not merely as a 
matter of sentencing. Indeed the Criminal Rules Advisory 
Committee rejected a draft that would have treated forfeiture 
as a matter to be dealt with by the judge at sentencing, 
adopting instead the common law practice of providing the 
defendant with notice in the indictment and a special jury 
verdict. Given the heightened procedural safeguards for crim- 
inal forfeiture, including the requirement of a special jury 
verdict, Rule 11(f) should be interpreted to require the court 
to assure itself that there is at least a factual basis for a plea to 
a forfeiture charge. That logic applies with special force 
where, a3 here, the government seeks to forfeit all of the 
defendant's assets, including substantial assets that were 
legitimately acquired. 

Finally, requiring a showing of a factual basis under Rule 
11(f) is necessary to ensure that criminal forfeiture does not 
exceed the limitations established by Congress. Federal law 


12 


does not authorize forfeiture of estate. Forfeiture is statutorily 
authorized only where the prosecution can prove a connection 
between the assets sought to be forfeited and the underlying 
offense. This is true even in the case of substitute assets, 
which may be forfeited only upon a showing that there were 
tainted assets and a further showing that those tainted assets 
cannot be reached by the forfeiture order. Unless the govern- 
ment can establish that it is a substitute asset, property that 
has no connection with the offense is not subject to forfeiture 
under federal law. The factual basis inquiry will provide some 
assurance that this limitation is observed in guilty plea cases. 

This assurance is sorely needed. History teaches that the 
government may abuse forfeiture authority. The severe sen- 
tences under the federal drug laws give the prosecution enor- 
mous leverage, and there is a real danger that federal 
prosecutors will employ their charging and bargaining author- 
ity to pressure defendants to give up their right to property 
that is not forfeitable under federal law. Interpreting Rule 
11(f) to require a factual basis inquiry will deter prosecutorial 
efforts to forfeit property for which there is no statutory 
mandate. The factual basis inquiry will also provide a degree 
of protection for third parties whose rights may be adversely 
affected by entry of a forfeiture order pursuant to a plea 
agreement. 

2. Rule 3i(e) of the Federal Rules of Criminal Pro- 
cedure requires a special jury verdict on “the extent of the 
interest subject to forfeiture, if any.” This rule stands on both 
a common law and a constitutional foundation, and any 
waiver of this right must be the intentional relinquishment of 
a known right. 

The historical record demonstrates that at common law in 
England and the American colonies the question of the nature 
and extent of property subject to forfeiture was submitted to 
the petit jury. The legislative history of Rule 31(e) reveals 
that the Rules Advisory Committee intended the special jury 
verdict provision to adopt the common law procedure. 

Rule 31(e) also stands on a constitutional foundation. 
This Court’s decisions teach that the Sixth Amendment guar- 
antees a jury trial in cases where it would have been available 


13 


at common law, and explain that the purpose of the right is to 
prevent government oppression and to provide a safeguard 
against a corrupt or overzealous prosecutor. Given the com- 
mon law tradition of submitting the issue of forfeiture to the 
petit jury and the potential for governmental abuse of forfei- 
ture, the right to a jury trial on forfeiture must be guaranteed 
under the Sixth Amendment. 

A waiver of the constitutional and statutory right to a 
jury trial on criminal forfeiture must be knowing and intel- 
ligent, and should be explicit as well. This Court's prior 
decisions require that the waiver of the Sixth Amendment 
right to trial by jury be knowing and intelligent. Rule 23(a) of 
the Federal Rules of Criminal Procedure implements the con- 
stitutional standard for waiver, requiring that the defendant 
personally waive the right to a jury trial in writing. 

Whether judged by constitutional standards or by Rule 
23(a), the waiver of the right to a jury trial and special verdict 
on forfeiture under Rule 31(e) must be knowing and intel- 
ligent. This standard is not met merely because a defendant 
has tendered a guilty plea. A plea of guilty does not neces- 
sarily waive the right to have a jury determine the issue of 
forfeiture. To the contrary, the lower courts have recognized 
that a defendant may enter a plea of guilty but insist on a trial 
of the forfeiture issue to the jury, just as he might plead guilty 
on one count but go to trial on another. 

Nor is it sufficient for the defendant to make a general 
waiver of the right to a jury trial if he was not aware of his 
right to have a jury determine what property, if any, would be 
subject to forfeiture. Absent some evidence that the defendant 
was advised of his rights under Rule 31(e) and the Sixth 
Amendment, the defendant’s statement that he waives his 
“right to a jury trial” does not establish a knowing waiver of 
the right to have a jury determine the issue of forfeiture. This 
is particularly true where, as here, the court specifically 
advises the defendant that pleading guilty means that the jury 
will not decide the issue of guilt or innocence. 

Absent evidence of the defendant’s awareness of the right 
to a jury determination of the extent of any property subject 
to forfeiture, any purported waiver could not be knowing or 


14 


intelligent. In the absence of such a valid waiver, a judgment 
of forfeiture is invalid.

A. Section 853 Authorizes Forfeiture for Only a 
Limited Class of Tainted Assets. 


Although the statutory provisions authorizing criminal 
forfeiture are extremely broad, they are not unlimited. They 
require a showing of a substantial nexus between the property 
in question and the criminal violations punishable by forfei- 
ture. Section 853 authorizes forfeiture for the offense to 
which petitioner pleaded guilty. Section 853(a) provides for 
the forfeiture of property constituting or derived from “pro- 
ceeds” of the offense, property used or intended to be used to 
“facilitate” the offense, and property which afforded control 
over the enterprise involved in a CCE violation.'? Section 
853(p) allows forfeiture of substitute property only after the 
government has first identified property that would be for- 
feited as either proceeds or property used to facilitate an 
offense, and then has established one of the specified justi- 
fications for forfeiting substitute property in lieu of the 
tainted property.'® Property that does not fall within section 
853(a) or section 853(p) is not subject to forfeiture under 
section 853. The plain language of section 853 authorizes 
forfeiture only of tainted assets, not forfeiture of a defen- 
dant’s entire estate. Thus the necessary concomitant to inclu- 
sion of an item of property in an order of forfeiture is a 
threshold determination that the property is actually subject to 
forfeiture under section 853.

A. At Common Law the Issue of Criminal Forfeiture 
Was Submitted to the Jury. 


Common law juries in both England and the American 
colonies made findings on criminal forfeiture. Reference 
works used by English judges and court personnel during the 
seventeenth and eighteenth centuries record the standard 
charge to the jury on the issue of forfeiture. For example, in 
1799 The Crown Circuit Companion instructed that once the 
jury had finished deliberating, the clerk should advise them as 
follows: 

Look upon the prisoner; you that are sworn, what 

say you, is he guilty of the felony whereof he stands 

indicted, or not guilty? If they say Guilty, then the 

clerk asks them, What lands or tenements, goods or 
chattels, he (the prisoner) had at the time of the 
felony committed, or any time since? 


Thomas Dogherty, The Crown Circuit Companion 21-22 
(1799) (emphasis in original). This charge was little changed 
from the charge recommended more than a century earlier in 
The Office of the Clerk of Assize and The Office of the Clerk

B. The Sixth Amendment Incorporates the Common 
Law Right to a Jury Determination of the Prop- 
erty Subject to Criminal Forfeiture. 


The purpose of the right to trial by jury is “to prevent 
oppression by the Government” and to provide a “safeguard 
against the corrupt or overzealous prosecutor and against the 
compliant, biased, or eccentric judge.” Duncan v. Louisiana, 
391 U.S. 145, 155-56 (1968) (footnote omitted). As devel- 
oped more fully at pp. 27-29, supra, the potential for raising 
enormous revenues by forfeiture naturally gives rise to a 
danger of governmental overreaching. Historically the jury 
has served as a safeguard against such governmental oppres- 
sion. 


The standard for determining when a jury trial is required 
is the common law. As Justice Powell observed, “[t}he rea- 
soning that runs throughout this Court’s Sixth Amendment 
precedents is that, in amending the Constitution to guarantee 
the right to jury trial, the framers desired to preserve the jury 
safeguard as it was known to them at common law.” Johnson 
v. Louisiana, 406 U.S. 366, 370-71 (1972) (Powell, J., dis- 
senting in Nos. 69-5035 and 69-5046) (footnote omitted). 
While some of this Court’s opinions have departed from the 
common law precedents in defining the characteristics of trial 


45 


si this Court has not retreated from the principle that 
re ixth Amendment guarantees a jury trial in cases where 
safeguard would have been available at common law. 


The historical record discussed above makes it clear that 
the determination of the property subject to criminal forfei- 
ture was submitted to the jury in England and the American 
colonies, and that jury verdicts finding no property placed an 
important check on government authority. Accordingly, the 
determination of the property, if any, that is subject to crimi- 
nal forfeiture should be recognized to be a part of the criminal 
prosecution for purposes of the Sixth Amendment, which 
guarantees the right to a jury trial “in all criminal prosecu- 
tions.” U.S. Const., amend. VI. 


C. Rule 31(e) Supplements the Sixth Amendment By 
Requiring a Special Jury Verdict on the Nature 


and Extent of Property Subject to Criminal For- 
feiture. 


The Sixth Amendment right to a jury determination of 
criminal forfeiture is supplemented by Fed. R. Crim. P. 31(e), 
which requires a special jury verdict on “the extent of the 
interest or property subject to forfeiture, if any.” Special 
verdict provisions are rare in criminal cases. 18 U.S.C. App.., 
Notes of the Advisory Committee on Rules — 1972 Amend- 
ment. Indeed, forfeiture is the only matter on which the 
Federal Rules of Criminal Procedure require a special verdict. 
As described more fully above, see supra pp. 15-21, Rule 
31(¢) and companion amendments to Rules 7 and 32 reflect 
the common law tradition that a defendant had the right to 
notice, trial, and a special jury finding on criminal forfeiture, 
which the Rules treat as an element o/ criminal liability.

evidence that framers meant to “equate the constitutional and common-law charac.

Rule 31(e) reflects the heightened procedural safeguards 
that are required when the government seeks criminal forfei- 
ture. It stands firmly on both a common law and a constitu- 
tional foundation, carrying forward the tradition of requiring 
any government claims to criminal forfeiture to be submitted 
to the jury, the historic safeguard against governmental over- 
reaching. 


VI. THE RIGHT TO A JURY DETERMINATION OF 
CRIMINAL FORFEITURE CAN BE EXTIN- 
GUISHED ONLY BY A KNOWING AND INTEL- 
LIGENT WAIVER. 


A. The Waiver of the Sixth Amendment Right to 
Trial By Jury Must Constitute the Intentional 
and Intelligent Relinquishment of a Known 
Right. 

This Court's prior decisions make it clear that an inten- 
tional relinquishment — in other words, a knowing and intel- 
ligent waiver — is required to extinguish the Sixth Amendment 
right to trial by jury. The Sixth Amendment cases follow the 
general rule that the waiver of a constitutional right must be 
the “intentional relinquishment or abandonment of a known 
right or privilege.” See Johnson v. Zerbst, 304 U.S. 458, 464 
(1938). 


The Court applied the standard of a knowing and intel- 
ligent waiver to the right to jury trial in Adams v. United 
States ex rel. McCann, 317 U.S. 269, 278 (1942) (citation and 
footnote omitted, emphasis added), where it stated: 

[O}ne charged with a serious federal crime may 
dispense with his Constitutional right to jury trial, 
where this action is taken with his express, intel- 
ligent consent, where the Government also con- 
sents, and where such action is approved by the 
responsible judgment of the trial court. And 
whether or not there is an intelligent, competent, 
self-protecting waiver of jury trial by an accused 
must depend upon the unique circumstances of each 
case. 


47 


The knowing and intelligent waiver standard was articulated 
to ensure “the validity of a defendant's decision to forego a 
right constitutionally guaranteed to protect a fair trial and the 
reliability of the truth-determining process.” Schneckloth v. 
Bustamonte, 412 U.S. 218, 236 (1973); see id. at 237 (know- 
ing and intelligent waiver standard applies to waiver of right 
to trial by jury). 

Fed. R. Crim. P. 23(a) restates the constitutional right to 
trial by jury and requires a written waiver of that right. The 
requirement of a written waiver embodied the existing prac- 
tice in 1945, when the Rules of Criminal Procedure were 
adopted. 18 U.S.C. App., Notes of Advisory Committee on 
Rules. The purpose of this requirement is “to ensure that a 
criminal defendant is aware of his jury right before waiving it 
and that any waiver is personal and unequivocal.” United 
States v. Garrett, 727 F.2d 1003, 1012 (11th Cir. 1984), aff'd 
on other grounds, 471 U.S. 773 (1985). The rule requiring a 
written waiver implements the constitutional requirement that 
any waiver of the right to trial by jury be made knowingly and 
intelligently by the defendant himself. 


B. The Waiver of the Right to a Special Verdict 
Under Rule 31(e) Must Be the Express and Intel- 


ligent Relinquishment of a Known Right. 


The appropriate standard for a waiver of the right to a 
special verdict under Rule 31(e) should be considered in light 
of the history and function of the rule. Rule 31(e) confers an 
independent statutory right, but it also carries forward a 
common law tradition stretching back more than 300 years, a 
tradition that evolved as a significant protection to the 
accused when the government sought to strip him of his 
property in the course of a criminal proceeding. Even if this 
Court chooses not to reach the constitutional issue, it is plain 
that Rule 31(e) expresses a fundamental judgment about the 
safeguards that are necessary when the government seeks to 
impose forfeiture as well as imprisonment on an accused. 


Given the fundamental importance of the procedural pro- 
tection afforded by Rule 31(e), nothing less than a knowing 


48 


and intelligent waiver should be sufficient to extinguish the 
right to a jury determination under this rule. Whether assessed 
as a common law, statutory, or constitutional right, the func- 
tion of the special jury verdict under Rule 31(e) is the same. 
The requirement that all criminal forfeiture claims be submit- 
ted to a lay jury provides an important protection against an 
overzealous or corrupt prosecution. As the Seventh Circuit 
correctly recognized, the character of the interest protected by 
Rule 31(e) is so fundamental that a knowing and intelligent 
waiver standard is appropriate. United States v. Robinson, 8 
F.3d 418, 421 (7th Cir. 1993). 

Two other provisions in the Federal Rules, Rule 1 1(c)(4) 
and Rule 23(a), provide additional support for the view that 
the waiver of the right to a jury determination of forfeiture 
under Rule 31(e) must be the intentional relinquishment of a 
known right. Rule 11(c)(4) directs the court to advise a 
defendant that if it accepts a plea of guilty “there will not be a 
further trial of any kind.” (Emphasis added). In light of the 
right to a jury determination of forfeiture, Rule 11(c)(4) 
requires that the defendant whose property may be subject to 
forfeiture be advised of his rights under Rule 31(e), as well as 
his right to a jury verdict on his guilt or innocence, before he 
enters his plea. Rule 23(a) also supports the view that a 
waiver of Rule 31(e) must be knowing and intelligent. The 
requirement in Rule 23(a) of an explicit personal waiver of 
the right to jury trial carried forward the existing federal 
practice, and that practice should apply to the right to a 
special jury verdict under Rule 31(e). To be sure, Rule 23(a) 
does not explicitly refer to criminal forfeiture, but it was 
adopted in 1945, long before the 1970 legislation reviving 
criminal forfeiture. 


C. Neither the Plea Agreement Nor the Court’s 
Comments Informed Petitioner of His Right to a 
Jury Trial and Special Verdict on Forfeiture, and 
Petitioner Made No Knowing and Intelligent 
Waiver of this Right. 


The plea agreement acknowledged petitioner's waiver of 
“various constitutional rights, including the right to a jury 


49 


trial,” J.A. 80, and the court advised petitioner that his plea 
would mean the end of the trial, which had commenced 
several days earlier. J.A. 87. In most cases, that would have 
been sufficient to dispose of any jury trial issue. In the instant 
case, however, petitioner had a right not only to have a jury 
determination of the issue of guilt or innocence, but also a 
right to have a jury trial and special verdict on the forfeiture 
issues. His Sixth Amendment right to a jury determination of 
forfeiture was supplemented by the statutory right to a special 
verdict under Rule 31(e). 

The record contains no indication that petitioner was 
made aware of his righ to a jury trial and special verdict on 
the forfeiture issues, and equally no indication that he know- 
ingly waived that right. The plea agreement made no mention 
of the right to a jury determination of criminal forfeiture or a 
waiver of that right. (To the contrary, as discussed above, 
supra pp. 22-23, the agreement seems to contemplate further 
proceedings to determine which of petitioner's assets were 
tainted, though there was no indication of the mechanism for 
that determination.) The waiver of a jury determination of 
forfeiture was neither implicitly nor explicitly part of peti- 
tioner’s guilty plea. A defendant may plead guilty but none- 
theless avail himself of the right to contest the forfeiture 
issues before the jury. See, <.g., United States v. McKeithen, 
822 F.2d 310 (2d Cir. 1987). In pleading guilty petitioner had 
two choices: to concede both his guilt and the forfeitability of 
his assets, or to concede his guilt but to contest the for- 
feitability of some or all of his assets before the jury. While 
the first choice would require a waiver of his rights under 
Rule 3i(e), the second would not. 

The court’s comments at the plea hearing directed peti- 
tioner’s attention solely to the fact that his plea would waive 
his right to a jury determination of guilt or innocence. The 
court noted that a guilty plea would “be the end of this trial,” 
with the result that “the jury is not going to decide whether 
you're guilty or not.” J.A. 87. The court also noted that it 
would dismiss the jurors, and “that will be that.” /d If 
anything, these comments erroneously suggested that the 


50 


jury's function was exclusively to determine guilt or inno- 
cence, and that this determination was the only right peti- 
tioner was waiving. 

Since the record provides no indication that petitioner 
knowingly relinquished his right to a jury determination of 
forfeiture under Rule 31(e), he made no valid waiver and his 
rights under Rule 31(e) were not extinguished by his plea. 
Accordingly, the district court erred in entering the order of 
forfeiture, and the court of appeals erred in affirming that 
order. 


CONCLUSION 


The judgment of the court of appeals for the Tenth 
Circuit should be reversed. The case should be remanded for 
further proceedings in which the district court inquires into 
the factual basis for the forfeiture component of petitioner's 
plea and determines whether petitioner knowingly waives his 
right to a special jury verdict on forfeiture.
