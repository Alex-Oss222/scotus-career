## The property order and what was actually stipulated

The December 23 order, JA155–164, ordered forfeiture of the defendant's right, title, and interest in listed property, subject to the third-party process. The list includes the Star Valley lot; two jointly owned condominiums; two vehicles; cash; Porter's ring; a mobile home; firearms; an IRA savings account; other bank accounts including the two childhood accounts; securities; cashier's and travelers' checks; and the General Chemical distribution check. The statutory basis is not allocated item by item throughout that list. That observation describes this order's text, not the existence or absence of evidence elsewhere.

**The pension-related item is concrete.** Paragraph NN, JA162, identifies an $8,416.23 check payable to Libretti representing distribution of his General Chemical 401(k) account, issued April 2, 1992, and directs reissuance to the United States as substitute property for assets dissipated or otherwise expended by him. This is a defendant-owned distribution/check claim in the order, not the $1,333.45 claim made by Claudia Porter and not a finding that a subsisting pension interest is inalienable. The separate Columbia IRA account appears in paragraph Q at JA158 (pinpoint corrected after the clean validator's direct check). Neither can be silently equated with the other.

Agreement paragraph 2 describes forfeiture of all known assets as prescribed by §853 and later-discovered assets up to $1.5 million (JA79). Paragraph 9 requires financial disclosure and identification of facilitating assets; paragraph 10 transfers his right, title, and interest in all assets, with an inclusive list containing retirement accounts (JA80–81). Paragraph 3 contains the general jury waiver. Those are the written terms. They do not list the five substitute-property conditions as individual factual admissions. The government's merits position reads paragraph 10 as a sentencing stipulation that the property met §853; the petitioner's reply, 1–2, contests that characterization, treating it as an ultimate concession rather than an admission of subsidiary sentencing facts. This is a disputed interpretation, not a source-author resolution.

The colloquy's drug-related descriptions and discussion excluding later legitimate salary/inheritance from the future-assets clause remain important (JA88–89, 100). The personal sentencing objection to the factual basis for the whole forfeiture is recorded at JA154. Counsel's earlier acknowledgment that legitimate and illegitimate money had become difficult to separate, and that substitution could take legitimate property (JA149), is counsel's statement. It is not interchangeable with an express personal admission of each predicate or with a statutory finding.

The superseding indictment's forfeiture allegations, JA64–67, identify proceeds and facilitating property and a list; the earlier restraining order identifies $102,360 alleged drug proceeds expended or disposed of (JA28–29). The September bill of particulars and amended restraint add the condominiums on the government's assertion of purchase with drug proceeds (JA72–78). These are allegations and restraint orders, not trial findings proving every item forfeitable. The original order's broader list and the plea's scope therefore must be described accurately.

## Affirmative evidence and disputes, rather than presumed absence

The prosecutor's November statement (JA122–135) asserts extensive proceeds, purchases, transfers, and concealment. Its paragraph 38, JA134, describes allegedly unlocated cash and transfers. This is an advocate's statement, not a stipulation by both parties. In the later hearing Agent Tony Young testified to profits exceeding $1 million and an asset total substantially below that; he described transactions, concealed funds, and commingled earnings (JA204–251, especially 205, 218–238). He acknowledged incomplete individual-check information and legitimate earnings (JA230, 245). His gross-firearms-receipts/profit terminology was corrected on cross-examination (JA247–251). The value and tracing questions were contested.

Libretti testified under oath to substantial employment income and conceded some tainted deposits in the Wyochem account, while disputing their amount, the tracing of specific purchases, and the government's total-proceeds estimate (JA262–283). His illustrative account calculations at JA268–273 are hypothetical examples of accounting approaches, not judicial findings or actual transaction findings. His specific later account example at JA274–275 is his assertion. The W-2 at JA172 corroborates that an employment wage record was submitted; it does not itself trace every asset or establish substitute-property predicates.

The petitioner's merits brief at 25 identifies his claims concerning a Bicentennial coin collection acquired in seventh grade, high-school property, childhood accounts, a complimentary airline ticket, and payroll savings. It cites his pro se appellate brief, pages 33–36. These are positively identified party claims. They are not an adjudication that every such item appears in the operative order, remains defendant-owned, or is beyond §853(p). The brief at 32–33 and reply at 8, 11–12 invoke the later district proceedings to dispute substitution. No conclusion that an unsupported own-asset forfeiture has already been judicially established follows merely from that argument.

## March hearing and April 2 order: exact findings and reserved questions

At JA288–290 the judge declined to rule on substitution during the oral exchange, questioned proof of concealment, and deferred the commingling argument for fuller facts. The formal April 2 order gives a more precise statement. **JA303 footnote 1 expressly says the government had not met its burden to establish that §853(a) property fell into one of §853(p)'s five categories and that substitute assets should be forfeited instead.** The footnote responds to the government's theory that allegedly more than $1 million in proceeds and a smaller forfeited estate justified substitution. It appears within discussion of Porter's $1,333.45 claim and the Wyochem account. It is not a final item-by-item adjudication invalidating every defendant-owned asset, and it did not amend the 401(k) item by an express separate ruling.

JA304 states that the government had not argued that the Wyochem funds were directly used to facilitate illegal activity. The order distinguishes Harris on that basis and addresses tracing under §853(a)(1) and §881(a)(6). That is an express description of the argument in this proceeding, not a universal finding that no asset facilitated the enterprise. JA305–306 finds the tainted proportion unclear and directs a magistrate hearing on Wyochem's sources. The $1,333.45 belongs to Porter's asserted third-party claim; the court reserves its disposition. The fund originated in a transfer from Libretti's Wyochem account and Porter claimed compensation/services or ownership. Her claim and Libretti's own challenge are distinct.

JA306 affirmatively finds the diamond ring purchased with legitimate funds and separately finds Porter an innocent owner. JA307 reserves the Star Valley property's disposition pending source findings; the father's asserted interest followed Libretti's transfer after arrest. JA308 finds the childhood-account funds not traceable to drug transactions and recognizes the father's standing as joint tenant, ordering return of passbooks and funds to the parents with interest. These are positive findings and orders, not merely unsatisfied evidence requests. The district court's reliance on the civil innocent-owner provision must be reported as its reasoning; §853(n)(6)'s superior/preexisting-interest and bona-fide-purchaser-for-value branches must not be replaced with a general §881 innocent-owner defense. Petitioner's brief 36–38 and footnote 31 itself identifies that distinction.

The April order then separately addresses **Libretti's own interests**, JA309–310. It expresses concern about enforcing the plea's forfeiture terms and directs a fact-finding hearing at which he could attempt to establish nonforfeitability. It does not finally resolve the tracing, amount, or pension status of his property. The order also preserves the property's status quo pending those proceedings and third-party rulings (JA311–312). The oral hearing similarly reserved the securities/cashier's-check source question (JA294), whereas the written order denied the father's particular securities claim (JA311). These different requests should not be collapsed.

## Appellate jurisdiction and the pension qualification

The controlling judgment below is the October 19, 1994 appellate judgment. JA318–319 expressly distinguishes continuing district-court jurisdiction over third-party §853(n) claims from lack of jurisdiction over **the defendant's claims** after his appeal. JA318 footnote 4 also holds that his request did not satisfy the then seven-day Rule35(c) limit and sought relief beyond its scope. The government's motion to dismiss the appeal as moot was rejected because Libretti continued to challenge his forfeiture (JA317–318 n3). Thus the later district proceedings do not themselves displace the December defendant forfeiture or supply effective relief to him; equally, they cannot be erased as nonexistent findings, and third-party jurisdiction was expressly retained. The docket records that the magistrate proceedings were stayed pending appeal (JA11); the appellate account confirms that stay (JA317).

JA326 footnote 8 is conditional: a defendant could agree to forfeit only property over which he had power of disposition; an employer profit-sharing/pension interest **if inalienable by law**, or an interest as beneficiary of a valid spendthrift trust created by another person, would be excluded as third-party-owned property would be. The footnote does not identify the actual General Chemical distribution check or the Columbia IRA as such an interest. The source-supported propositions are the existence of the qualification, the actual check/item, and the challenge to legitimate earnings. A definitive pension exclusion would additionally need the actual plan/distribution terms and applicable alienability law applied to this item; this addendum makes no claim that those materials do not exist elsewhere.

The petition and merits briefing frame the presented issues as Rule11(f) and informed jury/special-verdict waiver. Reply20 asks first for a remand to determine whether Libretti will now waive; it leaves the relief if no waiver occurs unresolved because agreement may be reached. Government34, with footnote12, distinguishes accepting guilt and determining forfeitable assets from the Rule11(e)(1)(C) situation in which a contingent sentencing bargain is rejected and withdrawal must be allowed. Neither party passage supports automatically retaining every other plea benefit while vacating the agreed forfeiture without considering bargain structure.

## Limits for the clean validator

This fuller record supplies positive support that was previously only summarized or reserved. It does not independently decide the Rule11(f) or jury questions, establish an unlawful forfeiture of a particular still-defendant-owned asset, or alter the jurisdictional limit. A bounded procedural ruling need not settle every trace or plan term. A proposed substantive ruling that any identified own asset must be returned, that §853(p) has been satisfied for all property, or that the 401(k) check falls within the appellate footnote would require the specific predicate findings and procedural route for that additional ruling.
