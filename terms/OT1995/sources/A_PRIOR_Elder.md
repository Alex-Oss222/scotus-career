### Elder v. Holloway, No. 92-8579

Merits decision, February 23, 1994

510 U.S. 510. Argued January 10, 1994. On certiorari to the Ninth Circuit, 975 F.2d 1388, affirming the officers' qualified-immunity summary judgment. The question is whether appellate review of that preserved immunity issue may exclude relevant preexisting decisions because the plaintiff did not cite them in the district court.

On April 27, 1987, Idaho officers sought Elder on Florida warrants but obtained no Idaho arrest warrant. They planned an arrest at his workplace; finding he had left, they surrounded his residence and ordered him out. Elder suffered seizures during the episode; an officer instructed him to crawl out to avoid falling. He walked through the doorway, suffered another seizure and fell, sustaining serious brain injury and partial paralysis. His §1983 action alleged an unreasonable seizure under the Fourth Amendment. The district court recognized the warrant requirement for a nonconsensual arrest inside a home absent exigency, but found the law insufficiently clear about an officer-commanded exit. It granted qualified-immunity summary judgment. The Ninth Circuit identified United States v. Al-Azzawy, 784 F.2d 890, which Elder cited on appeal but had not supplied below; it refused to use the decision because it treated the plaintiff's cited precedents as a closed body of legal facts.

All nine Justices participated at argument and decision: Chief Justice Stone-Zsela and Justices Blackmun, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas and Ginsburg.

The Ninth Circuit judgment is reversed and the case remanded, 9–0.

All nine Justices (Stone-Zsela, Blackmun, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas and Ginsburg) support reversal and remand of the qualified-immunity judgment that applied a citation restriction. The Court directs independent review of immunity using all relevant law and the proper record; ultimate immunity remains open.

Ginsburg writes for the Court, joined in full by Stone-Zsela, Blackmun, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter and Thomas. All nine join the legal-authority rule, its application and its reservations.

Whether the asserted federal right was clearly established when the officers acted is a legal question reviewed independently on appeal using all relevant precedent, including decisions neither cited to nor discovered by the district court. That duty does not admit a new claim or factual record, make every considered precedent controlling, or permit later decisions to supply notice retroactively; here the notice inquiry remains fixed at April 27, 1987.

The holding rests on Ginsburg's opinion of the Court, joined by Stone-Zsela, Blackmun, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter and Thomas. All nine adopt the legal-authority rule and the limited reversal and remand.

The Court holds that counsel's incomplete research cannot change what law governed the officers' conduct. Harlow makes immunity depend on objective legal notice; Mitchell recognizes the legal character of that inquiry, and Pierce supplies independent appellate review of legal questions. Davis v. Scherer requires the federal right supporting relief to be clearly established, rather than an unrelated legal duty. It does not convert precedent into evidence that must be introduced below. The Ninth Circuit's rule could release defendants solely because counsel or a court missed a governing decision, a circumstance officers could not predict when acting. Requiring consideration of all relevant law leaves the substantive defense intact. Hunter preserves the court's responsibility to apply the legal standard to a proper summary-judgment record; it neither makes unsupported allegations findings nor assigns legal immunity to a jury. Al-Azzawy must be considered for its actual force, including its exigency analysis. The Court remands for that inquiry without deciding whether the arrest violated the Fourth Amendment, exigent circumstances existed, or these officers ultimately receive immunity.

The Court applies the objective clearly-established-law protection of Harlow v. Fitzgerald, 457 U.S. 800; the defense is preserved, not replaced by a new reliance-only rule. Mitchell v. Forsyth, 472 U.S. 511, supplies the legal character of the immunity inquiry. The Court applies the independent appellate review of legal questions supplied by Pierce v. Underwood, 487 U.S. 552; this later decision does not itself supply officers with notice in April 1987. Davis v. Scherer, 468 U.S. 183, distinguishes a requirement that the relevant federal right be clearly established from a rule excluding uncited authority; unrelated duties do not defeat immunity. Hunter v. Bryant, December 16, 1991, preserves judicial determination under ordinary summary-judgment constraints using the supported nonmovant version of the complete contemporaneous record; its later decision date supplies no retroactive notice. Anderson v. Creighton, 483 U.S. 635, preserves the particularized objective inquiry used in current review; because it was decided after April 27, 1987, it is not itself pre-arrest notice authority. Payton v. New York, 445 U.S. 573, retains its warrant protection against a nonconsensual home arrest absent exigent circumstances for the lower court's application; this decision does not resolve that application.

The Court neither grants nor denies ultimate immunity, decides the factual or legal sufficiency of exigency, awards damages, orders new factfinding or an automatic trial, nor imposes a mandatory merits-first sequence. United States v. Al-Azzawy, 784 F.2d 890, is relevant circuit law predating the arrest; its rule concerning a compelled exit and its ultimate exigency disposition must both be evaluated. Depositions about access to guns and five days of advance arrest planning bear in different directions and are not resolved here. Florida warrants and the absence of an Idaho warrant are distinct facts; the Court does not decide that either alone disposes of the home-arrest question. The lower courts must distinguish a coerced exit from a genuinely voluntary appearance under the governing home-arrest doctrine. Ordinary preservation of claims and evidence remains separate from consideration of legal authority on this preserved claim.

The proposition-specific treatments above preserve the existing immunity defense and correct the use of Davis v. Scherer as a citation-forfeiture rule. No immunity precedent is overruled. Al-Azzawy remains relevant circuit authority whose application and exigency analysis require consideration on remand.

Effective February 23, 1994, appellate qualified-immunity review may not restrict relevant precedent to the cases presented below. This adds the specific legal-source rule while retaining Harlow's objective immunity, Anderson's particularity and Hunter's proper-record requirement. Legal notice remains tied to the officers' conduct, April 27, 1987 here. The decision creates no liability presumption from an omitted citation, no general exception to claim forfeiture and no new constitutional remedy or merits-first sequence.

The officers may prevail or further proceedings may be necessary under that inquiry; neither result is directed.
