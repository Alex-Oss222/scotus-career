JURISDICTION 


On March 15, 1995, the United States Court of 
Appeals for the Sixth Circuit issued a judgment. That 
judgment reversed the judgment of acquittal of Petitioner 
Charles Carlisle by the United States District Court for 
the Western District of Michigan. This case was remanded 
to the District Court for reinstatement of the jury’s ver- 
dict and for sentencing. The United States Supreme Court 
has jurisdiction to review the judgment entered by the 
United States Court of Appeals for the Sixth Circuit 
under 28 U.S.C. § 1254(1). The United States District 
Court for the Western District of Michigan had jurisdic- 
tion over this criminal case pursuant to 28 U.S.C. § 1331.

On July 13, 1993, a jury convicted Petitioner Charles 
Carlisle of conspiracy to possess marijuana with intent to 
distribute in violation of 21 U.S.C. §§ 841 and 846. On 
July 23, 1993 the Petitioner, through his court appointed 
trial counsel, filed a motion for judgment of acquittal 
under Federal Rule of Criminal Procedure 29(C). J.A. 6. 
On August 19, 1993, the United States District Court for 
the Western District of Michigan denied the motion for 
judgment of acquittal finding that a rational trier of fact 
could find the Petitioner guilty. J.A. 15. The district court 
did not deny the Petitioner’s motion for judgment of 
acquittal on the basis that the motion was untimely filed. 
The district court did not discuss in its written opinion 
the fact that the motion was filed eight business days 
after the jury’s verdict. 


Sen‘encing was scheduled for October 14, 1993. On 
October 14, 1993, the district court reversed its earlier 
opinion and granted Petitioner Charles Carlisle’s motion 
for judgment of acquittal. J.A. 24. The district court found 
that the Petitioner was innocent after reviewing all the 
evidence. 


The district court subsequently issued an eight page 
written opinion. The district court held as follows: 


The facts of this case require the same result as 
those in Burgess and Pearce. The government 
produced no evidence that Charles Carlisle 
entered into an agreement with any member of 
the conspiracy. Like the Burgess defendant, he 
was clearly present during the conspiracy, and 
the jury certainly could have found that he 
knew about it. However, in the course of hours 
of tape recorded conversations, no conspirators 
suggest that Carlisle is involved in this conspir- 
acy. There was no evidence that he was involved 
in arranging the transport of the marijuana to 
Michigan. In fact, Crawford testified that prior 
to coming to Michigan he had never heard of 
Carlisle. Carlisle never handled the drugs or the 
truck which contained them, and there was no 
evidence that Carlisle was to benefit from the 
conspiracy. I find that Carlisle’s hearsay state- 
ment concerning scanners, and the fact that he 
drove a conspirator (his common law mother-in- 
law) to her home, and then drove two conspira- 
tors to a hotel and dropped them off, is insuffi- 
cient to establish that he knowingly and 
voluntarily joined the conspiracy. 


Viewing the evidence in the light most favorable 
to the government, I find that the government's 
case against Charles Carlisle is insufficient to 
prove beyond a reasonable doubt that Carlisle 
knowingly and voluntarily joined the conspir- 
acy. As a result, the defendant’s motion will be 
granted. J.A. 33-34. 


The government filed an appeal with the United 
States Court of Appeals for the Sixth Circuit. The govern- 
ment contended that Petitioner’s motion for judgment of 
acquittal should have been filed by July 22, 1993. It was 
filed on July 23, 1993. The government argued that the 
motion was filed one day late and, as a result, the Peti- 
tioner lost his right to have the district court decide his 
motion for judgment of acquittal. On February 21, 1995, 
the United States Court of Appeals for the Sixth Circuit 
issued a published Opinion, and reversed the judgment 
of acquittal because “under Rule 29, it is beyond the 
court’s jurisdiction to grant an untimely motion for 
acquittal.” J.A. 48. The Sixth Circuit recognized that the 
motion for judgment of acquittal was filed on July 23, 
1994. The jury’s verdict was July 13, 1994. The seven day 
period under Federal Rule of Criminal Procedure 29 does 
not include weekends. The seven day period lapsed on 
July 22,-1994, one day before the motion was filed. The 
Sixth Circuit relied on the case of United States v Davis, 
992 F.2d 635 (1993), where the Sixth Circuit found that a 
district court does not have inherent supervisory power. 
The Sixth Circuit held that “if Carlisle had moved for 
acquittal within seven days of the discharge of the jury 
(or later, if the court so allowed during that seven day 
period), it would have been within the Court's jurisdic- 
tion to grant Carlisle’s motion for acquittal.” J.A. 48. 


On May 12, 1995, Petitioner Charles Carlisle’s Peti- 
tion for Writ of Certiorari was docketed with the United 
States Supreme Court. The United States Supreme Court 
granted the Petition for Writ of Certiorari on September 
27, 1995. 


SUMMARY OF THE ARGUMENT 


The issue which the United States Supreme Court 
must decide is whether a district court can deviate from 
the strict seven day time limitation for filing a motion for 
judgment of acquittal under Federal Rule of Criminal 
Procedure 29(C) in order to guarantee that an innocent 
defendant receives a fair trial. District courts should be 
given discretion to go beyond the strict time requirements 
of Federal Rule of Criminal Procedure 29(C) to ensure 
that innocent defendants receive fair trials. The power of 
the district courts to go beyond the strict time require- 
ments of Federal Rule of Criminal Procedure 29(C) 
derives from two sources. First, the district courts have 
inherent supervisory power, in limited circumstances, to 
act outside the Federal Rules of Criminal Procedure. Sec- 
ond, the district courts have the power to grant an 
untimely motion for judgment of acquittal under 28 
U.S.C. § 1651. The district courts should be given the 
power to go outside the strict time limits of Federal Rule 
of Criminal Procedure 29(C) when (1) there is a claim that 
the defendant was legally innocent, (2) the motion is filed 
prior to sentencing and (3) the motion was not timely 
filed because of a mistake made by the defendant's attor- 
ney. 


The rationale behind the time limit established in 
Federal Rule of Criminal Procedure 29(C) is to prevent a 
district court from deciding a motion for judgment of 
acquittal after a considerable period of time has passed 
and the trial judge may not remember the particular facts 
of the trial. That rationale does not apply to the facts of 
this case where the motion for judgment of acquittal was 
filed eight (8) days after the trial. The strict seven day 


time limit in Federal Rule of Criminal Procedure 29(C), as 
applied to the facts of this case, has resulted in the 
Petitioner being deprived of his liberty without due pro- 
cess of law as guaranteed by the Fifth Amendment to the 
United States Constitution.

ARGUMENT 


I. THE DISTRICT COURTS HAVE INHERENT 
SUPERVISORY POWER TO ACT OUTSIDE THE 
FEDERAL RULES OF CRIMINAL PROCEDURE TO 
MAKE SURE THAT INNOCENT DEFENDANTS 
RECEIVE FAIR TRIALS. 


The general supervisory power of the federal courts 
should include the ability to relieve an innocent defen- 
dant from the strict operation of the time limits under 
Federal Rule of Criminal Procedure 29(C). This Court has 
recognized that the federal courts “may, within limits, 
formulate procedural rules not specifically required by 
the Constitution or the Congress.” United States v Hast- 
ings, 461 U.S. 499 (1983). The purpose underlying the 
supervisory power of the federal courts is to provide a 
remedy for a violation of a defendant’s rights and to 
preserve judicial integrity by ensuring that a conviction is 
valid. It is appropriate to give the district courts the 
authority to go outside the strict time requirements set by 
Federal Rule of Criminal Procedure 29(C) to ensure that 
an innocent man is not found guilty. In this case the 
district court found that the Petitioner was an innocent 
man. The evidence was insufficient to convict him. 
Allowing the district-court to go outside the strict time

requirements of Federal Rule of Criminal Procedure 29(C) 
will preserve the integrity of the criminal justice system. 


The Federal Rules of Criminal Procedure should be 
flexibly applied. The motion for judgment of acquittal 
was filed only one day late. The district court correctly 
ruled it had the right to consider the motion as timely 
filed. Federal Rule of Criminal Procedure 2 states: 


These rules are intended to provide for the just 
determination of every criminal proceeding. 
They shall be construed to secure simplicity in 
procedure, fairness in administration and the 
elimination of unjustifiable expense and delay. 


The proposition that Federal Rule of Criminal Pro- 
cedure 2 gives a district court the inherent authority to 
enter a judgment of acquittal was set forth in United 
States v Broadus, 664 F. Supp. 592, 596-597 (D.D.C. 1987): 


Most important, Rule 2 of the Federal Rules of 
Criminal Procedure sets out the purposes of the 
criminal rules. Rule 2 mandates that a court 
ensure “the just determination of every criminal 
proceeding” if it is to conform to the Federal 
Rules. Fed.R.Crim.P.2. Rule 2 demands that a 
Court attend both to the needs of the individual 
litigants and to the public at large and approach 
procedural problems flexibly so that justice, fair- 
ness in administration, and efficiency are 
accommodated. See, e.g., Fallen v. United States, 
378 U.S. 139, 142, 84 S.Ct. 1689, 1691-92, 12 
L.Ed.2d 760 (1964); Warren v United States, 358

Rule 2 is the criminal procedure analogue to 
Fed.R.Civ.P.1, see Advisory Committee ‘Note to 
Rule 2, which requires a court confronted with a 
civil case to place justice ahead of an unyielding 
fealty to the words of the rules. If the words of 
the civil rules cannot be used to defeat justice, 
Cook v Boorstin, 763 F.2d 1462, 1472 
(D.C.Cir.1983); Hormel v Helvering, 312 U.S. 552, 
557, 61 S.Ct. 719, 721-22, 85 L.Ed. 1037 (1941), a 
seemingly plausible inference from a criminal 
rule cannot command blind adherence if it 
would deprive an accused person, for whom the 
stakes are so very much higher than a civil 
litigant, of a just determination of his or her 
cause. Thus, Fed.R.Crim.P.2 implies that a trial 
court is obligated to ensure that every guilty 
verdict is supported by at least some evidence 
on every element of the crime charged. 


The Rules of Criminal Procedure cannot elevate 
form over substance to the point where consid- 
erations of efficiency are irrelevant and a trial 
court is deemed powerless to review a convic- 
tion that would fall upon collateral attack. A 
rule of law such as this, which would so wrong- 
fully waste judicial resources, which would 
result in a great loss of time and money to a 
possibly wronged defendant, and which would 
fail to serve the public’s interest in speedy, fair, 
and resource-efficient criminal justice, would be 
utterly at odds with the commands of Rule 2. 
See, e.g., IV American Bar Association Standards for 
Criminal Justice 22-2.1 (1980).

The proposition that the federal courts have super- 
visory powers to protect substantial rights is supported 
by Gaca v United States, 414 U.S. 618 (1973). In Gaca, the 
defendant's appeal was dismissed because of his failure

attack upon the conviction.” Id. An innocent defendant, 
like the Petitioner in this case, could collaterally attack 
his or her conviction under 28 U.S.C. § 2255. It would be 
wasteful for all the parties (the federal courts, the govern- 
ment and the Petitioner) to require that a legally innocent 
person be imprisoned and forced to pursue collateral 
relief. The motion under 28 U.S.C. § 2255 could only be 
filed after sentencing because the moving party under 28 
U.S.C. § 2255 must be a “prisoner in custody under

motion under 28 U.S.C. § 2255 is filed. The motion could 
be granted or denied. If the motion under 28 U.S.C.

the intolerable prospect of the continued jailing of a

court is denied the power to grant relief to an innocent 
person eight days after trial but the district court, after 
sentencing, after a hearing on a 28 U.S.C. § 2255 motion 
and after the innocent person is imprisoned, may then 
grant the appropriate relief. Under either result, it is far 
better for a district court to decide the motion initially 
under Federal Rule of Criminal Procedure 29. 


It will be difficult for the Petitioner to prevail on a 
collateral attack of his conviction. This Court has estab- 
lished rules that make it more difficult for a defendant to 
upset a conviction by collateral, as opposed to direct, 
attack. In United States v Frady, 456 U.S. 152, 165 (1982), 
this Court held “an error that may justify reversal on 
direct appeal will not necessarily support a collateral 
attack on a final judgment.” In Murray v Carrier, 477 US. 


14 


478, 487-488 (1986), this Court ruled that a habeas peti- 
tioner cannot avoid procedural default by asserting 
merely that his counsel made a significant error. This 
Court concluded that as “long as a defendant is repre- 
sented by counsel whose performance is not constitu- 
tionally ineffective under the standard established in 
Strickland v Washington, we discern no inequity in requir- 
ing him to bear the risk of attorney error that results in a 
procedural default.” In this case there is a clear inequity. 
The Petitioner could lose if he collaterally attacks his 
conviction because he has to “bear the risk of attorney 
error.” The Petitioner, who has been found innocent by 
the district court, would be denied relief. 


In Brock v Pierce County, 476 U.S. 253 (1986), this 
Court held that, despite statutory language that the Sec- 
retary of Labor shall issue a final determination of misuse 
of funds within 120 days after audit or receipt of a 
complaint of misuse, the Secretary of Labor does not lose 
power to recover misused funds after 120 days. This 
Court held: 


We would be most reluctant to conclude that 
every failure of an agency to observe a pro- 
cedural requirement voids subsequent agency 
action, especially when important public rights 
are at stake. When, as here, there are less drastic 
remedies available for failure to meet a statutory 
deadline, courts should not assume that Con- 
gress intended the agency to lose its power to 
act. Id. at 261. 


In this case, there is much more involved than merely 
money. The Petitioner is a decorated war veteran with no 
prior record. Fundamental rights are at stake. It should

not be assumed that the district court lost its power to act 
eight days after the jury’s verdict. 


In Arizona v Manypenny, 672 F.2d 761 (9th Cir. 1982), 
the defendant was a United States border patrolman. He 
was charged with a criminal assault on an illegal alien 
under Arizona law. The defendant removed the prosecu- 
tion to the district court under 28 U.S.C. § 1442. The 
defendant's attorney made a decision not to raise the 
federal immunity defense at trial. The defendant was 
convicted. The defendant timely filed a motion for judg- 
ment of acquittal based on insufficiency of the evidence 
under Federal Rule of Criminal Procedure 29. The district 
court denied the motion. The defendant subsequently 
filed a motion for new trial under Federal Rule of Crimi- 
nal Procedure 33 and under Federal Rule of Criminal 
Procedure 34 alleging that the court lacked jurisdiction 
because state law could not govern the acts of a federal 
official under the circumstances of the case. The district 
court treated the Federal Rule of Criminal Procedure 34 
motion as a Federal Rule of Criminal Procedure 29(C) 
motion and granted the motion. The district court found 
that it had committed “fundamental error” by not grant- 
ing the original motion for a new trial under Federal Rule 
of Criminal Procedure 29(C).

Court of Appeals for the Ninth Circuit held: 


We conclude that a federal district court, which

manifest error. There are limits on this authority,

Il. THE RATIONALE BEHIND THE TIME LIMIT 
ESTABLISHED IN FEDERAL RULE OF CRIMINAL 
PROCEDURE 29C), TO PREVENT HAVING A 
TRIAL JUDGE DECIDE A MOTION FOR JUDG- 
MENT OF ACQUITTAL AFTER A CONSIDER- 
ABLE PERIOD OF TIME HAS PASSED AND THE 
TRIAL JUDGE MAY NOT REMEMBER THE PAR- 
TICULAR FACTS OF THE TRIAL, DOES NOT 
APPLY TO THE FACTS OF THIS CASE WHERE 
THE MOTION FOR JUDGMENT OF ACQUITTAL 
WAS FILED EIGHT (8) DAYS AFTER THE TRIAL. 


There are two questions which this Court should 
address in deciding whether to strictly enforce Federal 
Rule of Criminal Procedure 29(C). First, what prejudice is 
there to the government? On this issue, it certainly cannot 
be argued that there was any prejudice to the govern- 
ment. The motion was filed only one day late. This extra 
day created no prejudice to the government. 


The second question this Court should address is 
what is the purpose behind Federal Rule of Criminal 
Procedure 29(C)? The purpose behind the rule, as identi- 
fied in United States v Smith, 331 U.S. 469 (1947), is to 
avoid having a trial judge decide a motion after a consid- 
erable period of time has passed. After a considerable 
period of time has passed the trial judge may not remem- 
ber the particular facts of the trial. When there is a 
considerable period of time (not eight days) there is a 
legitimate reason to not allow a district court to decide 
the motion and grant a new trial.

Rule 29(C) allows defendants seven days after 
the jury is discharged, “or within such further 
time as the court may fix during the 7-day 
period,” to bring a motion for judgment of 
acquittal. The government correctly points out 
that this deadline occurred on July 22 in this 
case, but defendant’s motion was filed on July 
23. However, I can conceive of no prejudice to 
the United States which will result from consid-

case. Because I believe that refusal to hear this 
motion would result in grave injustice, and 
because the Rule permits the Court to extend 
the deadline, I will consider this motion as if it 
were filed in a timely manner. (Page 1 of the 
District Court’s October 28, 1993 Opinion found 
at J.A. 37.) 


The purposes behind Federal Rule of Criminal Pro- 
cedure 29(C) would not be served by applying the strict 
requirements of the rule to the facts of this case. The 
Petitioner's trial attorney mistakenly filed the motion for 
judgment of acquittal one day late. The Petitioner should 
not be completely prejudiced by his court appointed 
attorney's mistake. The district courts have available to 
them less drastic sanctions to enforce Federal Rule of 
Criminal Procedure 29, as opposed to completely denying 
a defendant his or her right to have a motion for judg- 
ment of acquittal decided. If the delay is the fault of 
defense counsel, as in this case, the district courts have 
the authority to appropriately sanction defense counsel. 
The severe penalty of refusing a defendant his or her 
right to have a motion for judgment of acquittal decided

should not be invoked where the motion is filed one day 
late. 


In United States v Smith, the defendant was convicted 
of tax evasion. The defendant timely filed a motion for 
new trial which was denied by the trial judge. The defen- 
dant appealed to the United States Court of Appeals for 
the Third Circuit. The Third Circuit affirmed the convic- 
tion and held there was no error in the proceedings. A 
petition for certiorari was denied by the United States 
Supreme Court in Memolo v United States, 327 U.S. 800 
(1946). The United States Court of Appeals for the Third 
Circuit then issued its mandate of affirmance and the 
defendant was taken into custody. On the same day that 
the defendant was taken into custody the trial judge 
entered an order vacating the judgment and granting a 
new trial to the defendant. The trial judge did not give 
any reason for granting the motion for a new trial other 
than that it was in the interest of justice. This Court 
reversed the decision of the trial judge and reinstated the 
conviction. This Court reasoned as follows: 


Support for the interpretation urged by respon- 
dent rises from fear of miscarriage of justice. 
New trials, however, may be granted for error 
occurring at the trial or for reasons which were 
not part of the court’s knowledge at the time of 
judgment. For the latter, the Rules make ade- 
quate provision. Newly-discovered evidence 
may be made ground for motion for new trial 
within two years after judgment. Rule 33. For 
the former, habeas corpus provides a remedy for 
jurisdictional and constitutional errors at the 
trial without limit of time. Johnson v Zerbst, 304 
U.S. 458, 58 S.Ct. 1019, 82 L.Ed. 1461, 146 A.L.R. 


22 


357; Walker v Johnston, 312 U.S. 275, 61 S.Ct. 
574, 85 L.Ed. 830; Waley v Johnston, 316 U.S. 
101, 62 S.Ct. 964, 86 L.Ed. 1302; Adams v United 
States ex rel. McCann, 317 U.S. 269, 63 S.Ct. 236, 
87 L.Ed. 268, 143 A.L.R. 435. Possibility of unre- 
dressed injustice therefore remains only in prej- 
udicial happenings during trial. The trial judge 
is given power by the Rules to entertain motions 
for new trial within five days after verdict and 
may extend that time for so long as he thinks 
necessary for proper consideration of the course 
of the trial. But extension of that time indefi- 
nitely is no insurance of justice. On the contrary, 
as time passes, the peculiar ability which the 
trial judge has to pass on the fairness of the trial 
is dissipated as the incidents and nuances of the 
trial leave his mind to give way to immediate 
business. It is in the interest of justice that a 
decision on the propriety of a trial be reached as 
soon after it has ended as is possible, and that

This case is easily distinguished from United States v

acquittal was granted before sentencing, not after the 
appeal was decided. This Court should distinguish United

sentencing, (2) the motion was not timely filed because of

In United States v Smith, this Court implied that Fed-

Criminal Procedure 33 would not protect the Petitioner’s

Under 28 U.S.C. § 1651, the district courts have the 
power to grant writs of coram nobis. In United States v 


Smith, at footnote 4, the United States Supreme Court 
stated as follows: 


Although this Court has reserved decision on 
whether the federal district courts are 
empowered to entertain proceedings in the 
nature of coram nobis “to bring before the court 
that pronounced the judgment errors in matters 
of fact which had not been put in issue or 
passed upon, and were material to the validity 
and regularity of the legal proceeding 
itself. * * * ” United States v Mayer, 235 U.S. 55, 
68, 35 S.Ct. 16, 19, 59 L.Ed. 129, it is difficult to 
conceive of a situation in a federal criminal case

power to investigate whether a judgment was

66 S.Ct. 1176, 90 L.Ed. 1447. When the draftsmen

The facts of this case justify a holding by the United 
States Supreme Court that the district courts should have 
the power to entertain proceedings in the nature of coram 
nobis. The writ of coram nobis should be used by the 
district courts in extraordinary situations to protect inno- 
cent defendants. This case represents such an extraordin- 
ary situation. The Petitioner has been found innocent by 
the district court. The only reason the Petitioner comes to 
this Court as a guilty defendant is because his trial coun-

acquittal by one day. There is a judgment error in this 
case that is material to the validity and regularity of the 
legal proceeding itself. 


The Federal Rules of Criminal Procedure should not 
be narrowly construed so as to eliminate any discretion 
which the district courts may have. This Court should 
hold that the district courts have the power through a 
writ of coram nobis to correct fundamental errors in a 
criminal trial, such as occurred in this case. That power, if 
conferred upon the district courts, is certainly not with- 
out limits. If a district court uses the power in a manner 
or form that is not designed to correct a fundamental 
error in the proceedings, such as occurred in this case, the 
abuse of that power by the district court can be cured on 
appeal. The power could be limited to situations where 
(1) the motion is filed prior to sentencing, (2) the defen- 
dant’s claim is time barred because of counsel's mistake, 
and (3) a claim is made that the defendant is actually 
innocent.

CONCLUSION 


This Court should hold that an untimely motion for 
judgment of acquittal brought by an innocent defendant 
under Federal Rule of Criminal Procedure 29(C) that is 
filed prior to sentencing can, in the discretion of the 
district court, be heard and decided to reach a fair and 
just result. In the alternative, this Court should hold that 
an untimely motion for judgment of acquittal brought 
under Federal Rule of Criminal Procedure 29(C) may be 
heard by the district court to correct a fundamental error 


30 


in the proceeding. This Court should rule that the federal 
trial courts have the inherent supervisory power to go 
outside the strict time limits established in Federal Rule 
of Criminal Procedure 29(C) only when (1) there is a 
claim that the defendant was legally innocent, (2) the 
motion is filed prior to sentencing and (3) the time limit 
was not met because of a mistake made by the defen- 
dant’s attorney. The district court’s power to entertain the 
untimely motion comes from the inherent supervisory 
power of the district courts. The district court's power to 
decide the untimely motion also comes from the district 
court’s ability to hear a writ of coram nobis. If this Court 
holds that the district court did not have the authority to 
decide the motion that was filed one day late, this Court 
should hold that Federal Rule of Criminal Procedure 
29(C), as applied to the facts of this case, violates the Fifth 
Amendment to the United States Constitution. For the 
reasons set forth above, the Supreme Court of the United 
States should reverse the Judgment and Opinion of the 
Sixth Circuit Court of Appeals dated February 21, 1995, 
and reinstate the Opinion and Judgment of the United 
States District Court for the Western District of Michigan 
dated October 28, 1993.
