JURISDICTION 


On March 15, 1995 the United States Court of Appeals for the Sixth Circuit issued a 
Judgment ordering that the District Court's Judgment of Acquittal of Defendant Charles Carlisle 
be reversed. The case was remanded to the District Court to reinstate the jury's verdict and for 
sentencing. The United States Supreme Court has jurisdiction to review the Judgment entered 
by the United States Court of Appeals for the Sixth Circuit under 28 U.S.C. § 1254(1). 


CONSTITUTIONAL AND STATUTORY 
PROVISIONS INVOLVED 


Federal Rule of Criminal Procedure 29(C) provides: 


Motion after discharge of jury. If the jury returns a verdict of 
guilty or is discharged without having returned a verdict, a motion 
for judgment of acquittal may be made or renewed within 7 days 
after the jury is discharged or within such further time as the court 
may fix during the 7-day period. If a verdict of guilty is returned 
the court may on such motion set aside the verdict and enter 
judgment of acquittal. If no verdict is returned the court may 
enter judgment of acquittal. It shall not be necessary to the 
making of such a motion that a similar motion has been made prior 
to the submission of the case to the jury. 


STATEMENT OF THE CASE 


The United States District Court of the Western District of Michigan had jurisdiction over 
this criminal case pursuant to 28 U.S.C. § 1331. 


On April 12, 1993 Defendant Charles Carlisle was named in one count of a seven 
defendant criminal complaint charging conspiracy to possess marijuana with intent to distribute, 
in violation of 21 U.S.C. §§ 841 and 846. On July 13, 1993, a jury convicted Defendant 
Charles Carlisle of conspiracy to possess marijuana » th intent to distribute, in violation of 21 
U.S.C. §841 and §846. On July 23, 1993, Defendant Charles Carlisle, through his trial counsel, 
filed a Motion for a Judgment of Acquittal. On August 24, 1993, the United States District 
Court for the Western District of Michigan denied the Motion for a Judgment of Acquittal, 
finding that a rational trier of fact could find the Defendant guilty. 


The District Court did not originally deny the Motion for a Judgment of Acquittal on the 
basis that the Motion was not timely filed. The District Court, in its written opinion, did not 
discuss the fact that the Motion was filed eight business days after the jury's verdict. 


Sentencing was scheduled for October 14, 1993. On October 14, 1993, the District 
Court reversed its earlier opinion, and granted Defendant Charles Carlisle's Motion for a 


Judgment of Acquittal. The District Court subsequently signed an eight page written opinion. 
The District Court specifically found as follows: 


The facts of this case require the same result as those in Burgess 
and Pearce. The government produced no evidence that Charles 
Carlisle entered into an agreement with any member of the 
conspiracy. Like the Burgess defendant, he was clearly present 
during the conspiracy, and the jury certainly could have found that 
he knew about it. However, in the course of hours of tape 
recorded conversations, no conspirators suggest that Carlisle is 
involved in this conspiracy. There was no evidence that he was 
involved in arranging the transport of the marijuana to Michigan. 
In fact, Crawford testified that prior to coming to Michigan he had 
never heard of Carlisle. Carlisle never handled the drugs or the 
truck which containec them, and there was no evidence that 
Carlisle was to benefit from the conspiracy. I find that Carlisle's 
hearsay statement concerning scanners, and the fact that he drove 
a conspirator (his common law mother-in-law) to her home, and 
then drove two conspirators to a hotel and dropped them off, is 
insufficient to establish that he knowingly and voluntarily joined 
the conspiracy. 


Viewing the evidence in the light most favorable to the 
government, I find that the government's case against Charles 
Carlisle is insufficient to prove beyond a reasonable doubt that 
Carlisle knowingly and voluntarily joined the conspiracy. As a 
result, the defendant's motion will be granted. (Pages 4-5 of the 
District Court's October 28, 1993 Opinion.) 


The United States of America filed an Appeal with the United States Court of Appeal for 
the Sixth Circuit. On February 21, 1995 the United States Court of Appeals for the Sixth 
Circuit issued a published Opinion, and reversed the Judgment of Acquittal because “under Rule 
29, it is beyond the court's jurisdiction to grant an untimely motion for acquittal."

In this case the United States of America has argued that the Motion for a Judgment of 
Acquittal should have been filed by July 22, 1993. It was filed on July 23, 1993. Therefore, 
it is the United States of America’s argument that the Motion was filed one day too late, and as 
a result, the Defendant has completely lost his right to file a Motion for a Judgment of Acquittal. 
The district court ruled as follows: 


Rule 29(c) allows defendant seven days after the jury is 
discharged, “or within such further time as the court may fix 
during the 7-day period,” to bring a motion for judgment of 
acquittal. The government correctly points out that this deadline 
occurred on July 22 in this case, but the defendant's motion was 
filed on July 23. However, I can conceive of no prejudice to the 
United States which will result from consideration of a motion that 
is one day late in this case. Because I believe that refusal to hear 
this motion would result in grave injustice, and because the Rule 
permits the Court to extend the deadline, I will consider this 
motion as if it were filed in a timely manner. (Page | of the 
District Court's October 28, 1993 Opinion.) 


There are two questions which the United States Supreme Court should address in 
deciding whether or not to strictly enforce Federal Rule of Criminal Procedure 29(a). First, 
what prejudice is there to the United States of America? On that issue, it vertainly cannot be 
argued that there was any prejudice. The motion was only filed one day late. 


The second question the United States Supreme Court should address is what is the 
purpose behind Federal Rule of Criminal Procedure 29(c)? The purpose behind the rule, as

identified in Unites States v Smith, 331 U.S. 469, 67 S.Ct. 1330, 91 L.Ed.2d a is 
to avoid a trial judge deciding a motion after a considerable period of time has passed.

of the trial. Therefore, when there is a considerable period of time (not eight days) there is a 
legitimate reason to not allow the district court to decide the case and grant a new trial. 


The purpose behind Federal Rule of Criminal Procedure 29(c) would not be served by 
applying the rule to the facts of this case. The Motion for a Judgment of Acquittal was filed 
only one day late. The purpose for the rule would not be served by refusing to allow a District 
Court to decide a Motion for a Judgment of Acquittal that is filed one day late. The District 
Court has available to it less drastic sanctions to enforce Federal Rule to Criminal Procedure 29, 
as opposed to completely denying the Defendant his right to have his Motion for a Judgment of 
Acquittal decided. If the delay was due to defense counsel, the District Court has the authority 
to appropriately sanction defense counsel. But, the severe penalty and sanction of refusing the 
Defendant his right to have the Motion for a Judgment of Acquittal decided, should not be 
invoked where the motion is filed one day late. 


Other circuits have recognized that a defendant should not be completely prejudiced

inherent power to enter a judgment of acquittal does not extend to 
judgments granting a new trial because such judgment, entered sua 
sponte by the court, might place the defendant twice in jeopardy.

The rules of criminal procedure, of course, do not demarcate the 
outer limits of a trial court's authority to vacate a judgment of 
conviction. Under its inherent supervisory power, a district court 
may consider an untimely post-trial motion for judgment of 
acquittal or may grant such a motion sua sponte to correct manifest 
error when it retains jurisdiction over the case. See United States 
v. Coleman, 811 F.2d 804, 806-07 (3d Cir.1987); Arizona v 
Manypenny, 672 F.2d 761, 764-65 (9th Cir.) cert. denied, 459 
U.S. 850, 103 S.Ct. 111, 74 L.Ed.2d 98 (1982); United States v 
Doherty, 675 F. Supp. 726, 729 (D.Mass. 1987); United States

regard to the technicalities of pleadings. See Ansley v. United 
States, 135 F.2d 207, 208 (Sth Cir. 1943). 


The rules should be applied flexibly. The Motion for a Judgment of Acquittal was filed 
only one day late. The district court correctly ruled it had the right to consider the Motion.

for the reasons given therein, this Court’s Opinion and Order of August 19, 1993, is withdrawn.

The matter before the Court is defendant Carlisle’s motion for a judgment of acquittal 
pursuant to Fed. R. Crim. P. 29(c). Defendant was charged in a single count Indictment with 
conspiracy to possess marijuana with intent to distribute. On July 13, 1993, a jury found

further time as the court may fix during the 7-day period,” to bring a motion for judgment of 
acquittal. The government correctly points out that this deadline occurred on July 22 in this 
case, but defendant’s motion was filed on July 23. However, I can conceive of no prejudice to 
the United States which will result from consideration of a motion that is one day late in this 
case. Because I believe that refusal to hear this motion would result in grave injustice, and 
because the Rule permits the Court to extend the deadline, I will consider this motion as if it 
were filed in a timely manner.

The standard of review for claims of insufficient evidence is "whether after viewing the 
evidence in the light most favorable to the prosecution, any rational trier of fact could have 
found the essential elements of the crime beyond a reasonable doubt." Jackson v. Virginia, 443 
U.S. 307, 319 (1979) (emphasis in original); United States v. Boling, 869 F.2d 965 (6th Cir. 
1989). Reviewing the evidence in the light most favorable to the government means that I must 
make all credibility choices and inferences in a manner that supports the jury’s verdict. Further, 
circumstantial evidence alone is sufficient to sustain a conviction and such evidence need not 
remove every reasonable hypothesis except that of guilt. United States v. Stone, 748 F.2d 361, 
363 (6th Cir. 1984). 


The evidence presented against Carlisle at trial was as follows. On the evening of April 
10, 1993, defendant Rupert, Carlisle's common-law wife, defendant Ramon, Ms. Rupert's 
mother, and Thomas Crawford arrived at the residence on Yale Street that Carlisle and Rupert 
shared. Ramon travelled in a car driven by Rupert, and they were followed by a truck full of

wearing a tape-recording device at the time.

Upon review of the evidence in this case under the foregoing standards, I find that it is 
not sufficient to sustain defendant’s conviction. Viewed in the light most favorable to the

government, I believe that a rational trier of fact could find beyond a reasonable doubt that 
Carlisle knew about the conspiracy. However, I do not believe that a rational trier of fact could 
find beyond a reasonable doubt, as the jury was instructed in Instruction 10B it must, that 
Carlisle knowingly and voluntarily joined the conspiracy. Instruction 10C explained that in 
order to establish knowing and voluntary joinder, 


the government must prove that [the defendant] knew the 
conspiracy’s main purpose, and that he voluntarily joined it 
intending to help advance or achieve his goals. . .. But proof that 
a defendant simply knew about a conspiracy, or was present at the 
time, or associated with members of the group, is not enough, 
even if he approved of what was happening or did not object to it. 
Similarly, just because a defendant may have done something that 
happened to help a conspiracy does not necessarily make him a 
conspirator. These are all things that you may consider in 
deciding whether the government has proved that a defendant 
joined a conspiracy. But without more they are not enough. 


Sixth Circuit Pattern Criminal Instruction No. 3.03 (1991) (emphasis added). The 
Committee Notes cite many cases in support of this instruction. E.g., United States v. 
Richardson, 596 F.2d 157, 162 (6th Cir. 1979) (there must be proof of an intention an 
agreement to cooperate in the crime); United States v. Bostic, 480 F.2d 965, 968 (6th Cir. 1973) 
(there must be intentional participation in the transaction with a view to the furtherance of the 
common design and purpose). 


In the unpublished decision of United States v. Burgess, 1992 U.S. App. LEXIS 34740 
(6th Cir.), the court acknowledged that "[cJonspiracy cases often present difficult questions about 
the extent to which seemingly passive participants are nonetheless culpable members of the 
conspiracy." Id. at *21. The appellate panel went on to hold that there was insufficient 
evidence to support defendant Burgess’ conviction for conspiracy to possess marijuana with 
intent to distribute. In Burgess, a defendant named Muncy planned to buy marijuana from DEA 
agent and informant. Muncy drove to the scheduled rendezvous point with Burgess and a third 
defendant, Rose. When they arrived, Muncy walked to the agents’ car and discussed the 
purchase. Muncy then went back to his own car and brought Rose to the agents’ car with him. 
After the transaction began, all three were arrested. A search of the car revealed a loaded 
revolver and $15,000 in cash in the front seat, and scales in the trunk. 


Upon review of these facts, the Sixth Circuit concluded that the jury could have inferred 
Burgess’ knowledge of the purpose of the trip, White's possession of money to purchase 
marijuana, and even the presence of a weapon in the car. However, because Burgess did not 
participate in the discussions with the agents, he was not mentioned in those conversations, he 
did not drive the car, and he never left the middle of the front seat, the court held that a rational 
trier of fact could not find that Burgess knowingly and voluntarily joined the conspiracy. 


United States v, Pearce, 912 F.2d 159 (6th Cir. 1990), cert, denied sub nom Thorpe v. 
United States, 111 S.Ct. 978 (1991), provides another example of evidence which the Sixth 
Circuit found to be sufficient to sustain a conspiracy conviction. Defendants Thorpe and Pearce 
were convicted of conspiring to possess and distribute crack in violation of 21 U.S.C. § 846 on 
the following evidence. A SWAT team entered what they suspected was a crack house after an 
informant purchased crack at the house with registered bills. When they entered, Thorpe ran 
into the basement yelling “it’s a bust.” The SWAT team found Thorpe nearby two doses of 
crack and a loaded revolver. Thorpe had a live round in his pocket that matched those found 
in the gun. The police found Pearce sitting on a couch in the living room. Next to him was a 
bag of crack and the registered bills the informant used in his purchase. 


The Sixth Circuit reversed both conspiracy convictions. It found that the record was 
devoid of any evidence that Thorpe and Pearce had entered into an agreement. Although the 
defendants’ presence at the house was unexplained, the court concluded that the no rational trier 
of fact could have found that even tacit coordination between Thorpe, Pearce, or any third party, 
to maintain a crack house or to distribute crack. 


The facts of this case require the same result as those in Burgess and Pearce. The 
government produced no evidence that Charles Carlisle entered into an agreement with any 
member of the conspiracy. Like the Burgess defendant, he was clearly present during the 
conspiracy, and the jury could have found that he knew about it. However, in the course of 
hours of tape recorded conversations, no conspirators suggest that Carlisle is involved in the 
conspiracy. There was no evidence that he was involved in arranging the transport of the 
marijuana to Michigan. In fact, Crawford testified that prior to coming to Michigan, he had not 
heard of Carlisle. Carlisle never handled the drugs or the truck which contained them, and there 
was no evidence that Carlisle was to benefit from the conspiracy. I find that Carlisle’s hearsay 
statement concerning the scanners, and the fact that he drove a conspirator (his common law 
mother-in-law) to her home, and then drove two conspirators to a hotel and dropped them off,

Viewing the evidence in the light most favorable to the government, I find that the 
government's case against Charles Carlisle is insufficient to prove beyond a reasonable doubt 
that Carlisle knowingly and voluntarily joined the conspiracy. As a result, defendant's motion 
will be granted.
