## Operative provisions reproduced in the primary filings

Petition App. 18–19, 48–50; petitioners' merits statutory addendum App. 1–2:

- **R.I. Gen. Laws §3-8-7:** No manufacturer, wholesaler, or shipper from outside Rhode Island, and no holder of a license under the title and chapter, may cause or permit advertising in any manner of the price of malt beverages, cordials, wine or distilled liquor offered for sale in Rhode Island. The express exception is for price signs or tags attached to or placed on merchandise for sale **within licensed premises**, in accordance with department rules.
- **§3-8-8.1:** A newspaper, periodical, radio or television broadcaster or broadcasting company, or other person, firm or corporation with a principal place of business in Rhode Island engaged in advertising or selling advertising time or space may not accept, publish or broadcast in Rhode Island an advertisement stating or referring to alcoholic-beverage prices. Violation is a misdemeanor: first offense $50; each additional offense a fine not exceeding $100. Publication/broadcast also may be enjoined on complaint of a retail licensee or association of retail licensees. The distinct exception covers a trade journal duly recognized and authorized to be exempt by the Liquor Control Administrator.
- **§3-8-8:** Authorizes rules enforcing §3-8-7 and license suspension for violations.
- **Regulation 32:** No placard or sign visible from outside Class A retail premises may refer to the price of alcoholic beverages or mixtures of them. It limits the premises exception; the exception does not authorize exterior-visible price advertising.
- **§3-1-5:** Declared purpose is promotion of temperance and reasonable control of traffic in alcoholic beverages. Quoted in CA1 ¶1 and State merits pp.18–19.
- Petition App.48 reproduces First Amendment text and Twenty-first Amendment §2: transportation or importation into a State, Territory or possession for delivery or use of intoxicating liquor in violation of its laws is prohibited.

## Lower proceedings, findings, and boundaries

1. **District court:** *44 Liquor Mart, Inc. v. Racine*, 829 F. Supp. 543 (D.R.I. Aug. 10, 1993), Pettine, after trial. Jurisdiction identified as 28 U.S.C. §1331 and 42 U.S.C. §1983. Petition App.18–19. It declared §§3-8-7, 3-8-8.1 and Regulation 32 unconstitutional, finding failure both to advance reduced consumption and to meet the extent/fit requirement. App.47. It did not decide a general ban on alcohol advertising; App.31 n.6 expressly disclaims findings about total or partial general-advertising bans.
2. **Controlling appellate disposition:** 39 F.3d 5, **October 24, 1994**, Aldrich with Cyr and Stahl. Petition App.1–15. The original July 8 opinion was withdrawn and judgment vacated on defendants' rehearing petitions. The October judgment reverses the district court and directs judgment for defendants, with costs to defendants. This is the operative judgment reviewed.
3. **Breyer:** Petition App.16 records that Chief Judge Stephen Breyer did **not** participate in the July 29, 1994 lower rehearing/en-banc denial. The merits panel was Aldrich, Cyr and Stahl. These are lower-court participation facts only.
4. **Threshold facts:** CA1 ¶5 expressly recognizes both plaintiffs' standing to challenge the statutes in every particular. CA1 ¶13 treats lawful/nonmisleading speech as undisputed and notes plaintiffs' concession that temperance is substantial. The stipulation itself says proposed speech does not concern illegal activity and **presumably** would not be false or misleading, and that Rhode Island has a substantial interest in regulating alcohol sales. JA46–48. Preserve the stipulation's precise phrasing separately from the appellate concession.
5. **Commerce Clause:** CA1 ¶32 holds Peoples' separately pleaded Commerce Clause contention waived because it was dropped on appeal, declines to decide it without briefing/argument, and reverses Peoples' judgment on its remaining speech claim. Petitioners' merits pp.24–26 argue a distinct *Bigelow* First Amendment ground for lawful Massachusetts sales and explicitly say their argument is First Amendment based. Association merits pp.28–30 dispute that ground and characterize §3-8-8.1 as regulation of in-state advertising. The waived commerce claim is not converted into a preserved claim by the speech argument.

## Underlying facts and trial evidence

- 44 Liquormart operated in Johnston, Rhode Island; Peoples operated in New Bedford/Fairhaven, Massachusetts. JA46. A December 17, 1991 administrative hearing found an advertising violation; 44 paid a **$400 fine** and did not pursue the statutory administrative appeal. JA46–47; district App.20–21. The ad displayed named liquor bottles without numerical liquor prices, beside price advertising for other products and highlighted “WOW” bursts. The administrator required that ad to cease. These facts describe the challenged enforcement; no refund judgment is supplied by the sources read.
- Peoples used lawful price advertising in Massachusetts and unsuccessfully attempted to place such ads in Rhode Island media. Rhode Island media reach citizens of both States. The Administrator vigorously enforced the ban; other retailers and newspapers had been warned/fined. Each petitioner **believed** lifting the ban would produce over $100,000 annual economic benefit. Those are stipulated expectations, not findings of increased statewide consumption. JA46–48.
- The district court found no significant impact of this off-premises price-advertising ban on Rhode Island consumption. App.31. It credited Smart and Pittman over Saffer on the specific issue and distinguished general advertising studies from price-only restrictions. This is a trial finding; the First Circuit rejected the district court's legal treatment of the government's burden and regarded the economic inference as reasonable. CA1 ¶¶14–20 and n.2. Do not describe either disputed causal proposition as a neutral stipulated fact.
- **State witness Henry Saffer:** JA69–74 gives the search-cost and money-price mechanism: price information makes cheaper goods easier to find; the ban increases search costs and dispersion, and higher overall price tends to reduce consumption. He predicted lower average prices and higher consumption if the ban were removed. His own international work addressed general advertising bans in 17 countries over 14 years, not price bans. He cited Ornstein–Hanssens as theoretical and empirical support for price-advertising effects.
- **Limits found below:** App.29–31 says Saffer did not meaningfully control several country differences, lacked Rhode Island consumption-trend and projected-price-drop information, could not identify an optimal Rhode Island price, and conceded advertising could shift market share/profits without increasing overall consumption. These are the district judge's account of the deposition and credibility assessment.
- **Smart's concessions and limits:** JA79–81: real price relative to disposable income affects consumption; he acknowledged search-cost theory and called the prediction reasonable, but expressly said the authors lacked search-cost data and were speculating. He acknowledged disagreement among researchers and the difficulty of measuring cumulative advertising effects. These concessions coexist with, and do not erase, his view credited by the trial court that the ban had no significant Rhode Island effect.
- **Pittman's concessions and limits:** JA83: he knew no empirical evidence refuting the proposition that banning price ads promotes temperance. JA84: removing the ban should theoretically lower prices. Reply pp.15–16 cites the underlying Pittman transcript p.12 for his qualification that the effect on consumption would be “small and insignificant”; this latter qualification is verified as a **quotation in the filed reply**, not by an independently read full deposition.
- **State's trial interest:** JA67–68 reproduces counsel's argument stressing irresponsible purchases by people with limited income, including young people/college students, and preventing price wars. Reply pp.6–9 argues that later defense of reducing all consumption changes the asserted interest; the State merits pp.18–19 insists on the statutory temperance purpose and opposes speculation about concealed protectionism. These remain litigants' legal positions, not an established waiver of all broader temperance arguments.
- **Intervenor's stated concern:** CA1 ¶9 quotes the Association's desire to protect small licensees against advertising costs and multi-license competitors. JA43–45 reproduces its intervention memorandum. CA1 ¶25 does not find protectionism established as the State's purpose; it says plaintiffs made no attempt to rebut the declared legislative purpose.

## Dated submissions concerning the disputed legal grounds

These are descriptions of actual briefs and lower reasoning, not independent proposed outcomes.

- Petitioners merits pp.9–24 and reply pp.13–17 contend the State has not established material advancement and that the appellate “reasonableness” approach improperly relaxes its burden. Their reply expressly disclaims demands for perfect performance, unanimity of experts, exhaustive empirical data, or exhaustion of every imaginable alternative. State merits pp.21–28 relies on ordinary economics, Saffer, concessions by Smart/Pittman, and the Ornstein–Hanssens article. Association merits pp.17–25 stresses legislative prediction and disagreement in research.
- **Non-speech alternatives actually supported in the record:** District App.30 quotes Saffer's concession that minimum prices and/or higher sales taxes could achieve reduced consumption; App.45–47 relies on those comparisons. Petitioners merits pp.22–24 repeats that argument. Association merits p.27 responds that higher taxes risk interstate flight and that minimum-price arrangements can create antitrust/constitutional difficulties, citing *Midcal* and *324 Liquor*. State merits pp.29–37 says *Fox* does not require the least restrictive choice or proof that every other method is inferior. The cited cases do not themselves constitute a finding that every state-set minimum price is unlawful.
- **Twenty-first Amendment / vice theory:** First Circuit ¶¶15,20 invokes *LaRue*'s added presumption; both respondents defend it, with State pp.18–37 also arguing it prevails without that presumption. Petitioners merits pp.26–32 distinguish licensed-premises conduct in *LaRue* and *Bellanca* from off-premises factual information and rely on other Bill of Rights limits. The briefs cite historical post-divergence Supreme Court decisions; their descriptions must be reconciled to actual simulated authority rather than imported as current law.
- **Queensgate:** State merits pp.19–21 invokes *Queensgate Investment Co.*, 459 U.S.807 (1982), a pre-divergence summary dismissal, as binding. CA1 ¶¶21–25 considered the Ohio discounted-drink case factually comparable but upheld the Rhode Island law “with Queensgate or without,” without making that an exclusive ground. District App.40–42 distinguished the factual predicate and limited summary precedential effect under *Mandel*. Reply p.5 n.4 disputes respondent's characterization and cites Beer Institute amicus p.18 n.9; that amicus was not read in this work.
- **Other source-status disputes:** Association merits pp.22–23 says the Moreland Commission report was excluded at trial and Waterson first appeared in Supreme Court filings. State merits p.28 cites a June 1995 Hacker–Stuart study; reply pp.10–11 disputes its evidentiary status and significance. Do not silently treat these as admitted trial findings. The three empirical articles reproduced at JA86 onward were not fully read here; opposing characterizations and the court's findings are documented instead.
- **Relief stated:** Petitioners merits p.32 and reply p.20 seek reversal of the First Circuit; State p.44 and Association p.34 seek affirmance. The district constitutional declaration concerns the two statutes and Regulation 32. JA32 complaint prayer seeks a declaration and other appropriate relief; allegations of monetary injury do not establish a damages award, automatic fine refund or preserved separate antitrust judgment.
