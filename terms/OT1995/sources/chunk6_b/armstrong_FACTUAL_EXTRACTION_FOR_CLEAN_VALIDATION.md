## Procedural and record facts

1. Five Black defendants—Christopher Lee Armstrong, Aaron Hampton, Freddie Mack, Shelton Auntwan Martin, Robert Rozelle—were indicted April21,1992 following a joint Inglewood police/ATF investigation. All faced a cocaine-base distribution conspiracy under 21 USC846; the lower courts describe substantive 841(a)(1) and 18 USC924(c) charges as well. Panel App.69a–71a describes three informants, seven purchases February13–April6 totaling124.3g, an April8 hotel/residence search yielding9.29g more and a loaded gun, and later arrests of some defendants. These are allegations/record recitations before trial, not adjudications of guilt. The comparison in App.4a,70a–71a is between severe federal and lower California penalties; it does not establish an individual defendant's final sentence.

2. **No preceding state prosecution established:** at JA157 Cho expressly stated, “state charges were never filed in these defendants.” Defense counsel had clarified the same point for their clients. Their complaint concerned selection of federal rather than state prosecution, not an established transfer after dismissal of state charges. JA156–158.

3. Martin moved for discovery and alternatively dismissal in July1992, others joined, and Judge Consuelo B. Marshall ordered discovery September8. The four demands, as consistently summarized in both lower opinions, were: (i) cases during the preceding three years in which the government charged both cocaine-base and firearm offenses; (ii) defendants' race; (iii) whether investigation was state, federal, or joint; (iv) criteria used to choose federal cocaine-base prosecutions. App.5a,35a,72a. JA162 supplies the actual oral definition: “those persons who have been indicted by the Government on cocaine base and a Federal firearms violation”; timeframe “1989 through 1992”; racial identity and agency involvement; and whether criteria exist for state-versus-federal selection and what they are. Cho asked whether both counts were required; the court answered “Yes.” **Transcript qualification:** at JA163 O'Connor then sought cocaine-base-only cases too, and the court said, “Yes, you—the record reflects your statement. Yes?” The lower opinions nevertheless describe the order as both cocaine base and firearms. Preserve the transcript rather than silently resolve its ambiguity.

4. Initial showing: paralegal Marla Beller's July17 declaration says three FPD paralegals reviewed all office cases **closed during1991** alleging 841 and/or846, then selected cocaine-base matters, excluding out-of-district cases and cases where retained counsel appeared before post-indictment arraignment. Drug type came from indictment/counsel, race from presentence report/counsel. Her exact result is “Of 24 defendants charged with cocaine base offenses, all—or100%—were black” (JA69, spelling normalized). Some lower discussion calls these24cases. Do not convert this to all district prosecutions, all1991 indictments, or a representative census of eligible offenders. The sample does not itself supply the race/number of uncharged offenders or matched state defendants. App.16a–20a,36a–37a,79a–83a; JA68–70.

5. At the initial hearing Cho could not explain the FPD numbers and disclaimed known racial motivation; the court viewed the numbers, duration, and common charges as sufficient for information, expressly reserving the merits motion. JA149–151. On reconsideration the government supplied substantially more: five declarations, a federal narcotics prosecution printout, and other material. Thus “government offered no reasons” is true only of the initial response in its limited sense, not the developed record.

6. Government's specific selection explanation: AUSA David Scheper, who supervised charging, listed federal-offense guidelines, evidence strength, deterrence/federal interest, criminal histories and other race-neutral criteria. For these respondents he identified over100g, multiple sales/multiple defendants, jointATF investigation, multiple intertwined federal firearm violations, strong audio/video evidence, threats to arresting officers, and several narcotics/firearm histories; he denied race played any role. JA80–81; App.34a,57a–58a. Attribute these to his declaration, rather than treat every allegation as found true. Investigator Campbell said only four of his several hundred crack cases had gone federal, jointly investigated with ATF, three with recovered firearms/federal firearm counts; Cochran said three of40–50arrests met federal referral criteria and many Black offenders went to state authorities. JA75–79. Their assertions about absence of racial selection and demographic patterns were contested; they are not neutral population findings.

7. Cho's declaration identified seven nonblack federal cocaine-base defendants (four represented currently/previously byFPD) in cases with1989/1992 numbers; lower discussion reports four more names added later. JA82–83; App.6a,20a–21a,36a–37a. Majority and dissent differ about comparability to the closed1991sample. The majority notes none were non-Hispanic white and that the sample's defined result was not refuted; dissent emphasizes nonblack prosecutions during relevant filing periods. The existence of those examples does not establish a complete racial census. Cho's computer printout counts were approximate, with duplicate defendant entries; it supplied no racial breakdown. JA82.

8. Defense supplemental declarations: O'Connor relayed intake coordinator Chris Fernandez's treatment experience of equal numbers of Caucasian and minority cocaine-base users/dealers (JA138). David Reed described extensive federal indigent practice and six named Black federal crack clients; he recalled no nonblack federal crack case and stated many state crack-sale cases involved other racial groups, particularly Latinos, including repeat narcotics offenders still on probation (JA140–141). Neither declaration supplied the complete quantity/firearm/evidence histories necessary to establish individually matched armed-crack comparators. Reed's direct experience and relayed observations must remain distinguished. Majority App.23a n.8 states the government did not object to admissibility below; US later criticized vague hearsay and insufficient comparability. That procedural history is not a general ruling that all hearsay must be accepted.

9. District reconsideration reasoning: at JA217 the judge said statistical data “raises a question about the motivation” that disclosure of criteria could satisfy and she could not attribute it to social phenomena without expert testimony. At JA218 she expressly said she had reviewed **all** evidence from all parties and found the newspaper article unpersuasive. US opening p.28n.2 says she did not rely on the defense affidavits; Martin pp.57–58 disputes this using JA218 and the appellate treatment. Treat reliance as disputed, not resolve it by declaring declarations absent from the record.

10. **Remedy:** government refused compliance to seek appellate review; after procedural clarification, defendants moved to dismiss as sanction. JA225–226: the court granted present defendants' motions because it saw no other sanction for refusal to produce the ordered information; Armstrong's ruling was deferred for appearance/waiver. JA212–213 contains poor-OCR minute orders from January5 and January19. Neither intelligible oral grant nor the read minute OCR verifies an explicit “with prejudice” label. En banc App.2a–4a treats dismissal as the appealable order under18USC3731 and says a government discovery appeal cannot be created with a later option to comply after losing. Wallace App.28a–29a separately insists dismissal need not be with prejudice without warning. **Do not state that the indictment was dismissed for a proven constitutional violation, or certify dismissal with prejudice.** The government challenged the discovery basis rather than the appropriateness of the chosen sanction. Stays/bail discussion was collateral to that issue.

11. **Berk–Campbell study outside original record:** panel dissent App.101a expressly identifies its 1993 crack-charging statistics as not before the district court and not part of the record reviewed. It discusses potential judicial notice/further proceedings. US reply p.16n.5 says the government objected to notice of a contested study and the en banc majority did not rely on it. Martin pp.12–13 referred to it. It cannot silently substitute for Beller's actual district-court showing.

## What the lower judges decided or argued

- **En banc Reinhardt majority, App.7a–27a:** discovery reviewed for abuse of discretion; “colorable basis” requires specific facts, beyond allegations, tending to show both discriminatory effect and intent, with substantially less proof than a prima facie merits case. Consider all evidence, including government's response. It removes Bourgeois's “high threshold” formulation and allows statistical/circumstantial proof rather than necessarily named comparators. It regarded FPD results and supplemental declarations as sufficient, without deciding actual discrimination. Its assumption that people of all races commit crimes absent compelling contrary evidence, and its statistical inference, are judicial reasoning—not independently established population facts. App.15a n.5 reserves the extent of a judge's discovery power based on personal observations, while saying the same colorable standard would apply.
- **Wallace concurrence, App.28a–31a:** concurs in result under deferential review; would retain Bourgeois's higher threshold below prima facie proof; personally might deny discovery but cannot find clear error of judgment. Does not join broad merits-statistics propositions/overruling Gutierrez, and raises the separate with-prejudice concern.
- **Rymer dissent joined by Leavy/T.G.Nelson/Kleinfeld, App.32a–67a:** discovery also requires less than prima facie proof but credible facts about effect and purpose. Says FPD sample gives no relevant denominator, omits firearms/comparable severity and those passed over, and excludes categories of counsel/closing dates. Emphasizes government's actual case-specific explanation. Recognizes circumstantial intent; does not demand direct admissions in every case. Invokes Feeney/Wayte's “because of,” not merely “in spite of,” adverse racial effects. Burden/rough file-count assertions are the dissent's account, not independently authenticated cost findings.
- **Superseded panel, App.68a–84a:** Wood majority applies Bourgeois and finds the showing insufficient, particularly no relevant similarly situated group and inadequacies of affidavits/sample. **Reinhardt dissent, App.85a–104a:** stresses discovery/merits distinction, district-court experience/deference, practical access and circumstantial inference. Its discussion of extra-record study and possible judge-initiated inquiry should not be treated as an en banc holding.

## Verified rule language and parties' source-reported positions

**Rule16 provisions (contemporaneous filings quote only relevant portions):** Martin brief p.3 and US reply p.2 quote16(a)(1)(C):

> Upon request of the defendant the government shall permit the defendant to inspect and copy or photograph books, papers, documents, photographs, tangible objects, buildings or places, or copies or portions thereof, which are within the possession, custody, or control of the government, and which are material to the preparation of the defendant's defense * * *.

US reply p.4 supplies the other alternatives: materials “intended for use by the government as evidence in chief at the trial” or “obtained from or belong to the defendant.” The asterisks are the filing's omissions; this is not represented as the complete rule.

Martin p.27 quotes16(a)(2)'s exclusion of “reports, memoranda, or other internal government documents made by the attorney for the government or other government agents in connection with the investigation or prosecution of the case,” and describes its government-witness statement limitation/Jencks Act interaction. This excerpt does not reproduce all introductory exceptions.

Martin p.31n.53 quotes16(d)(1):

> Upon a sufficient showing the court may at any time order that the discovery . . . be denied, restricted, or deferred, or make such other order as is appropriate. Upon motion by a party, the court may permit the party to make such showing, in whole or in part, in the form of a written statement to be inspected by the judge alone.

Independent official full-rule retrieval was attempted but unsuccessful: the downloaded1994Title18PDF contains principal code only, not the rules appendix; failed guessed URLs are marked `FAILED_URL`. **No complete16(a)(2) exceptions or16(d)(2) text is independently authenticated in this handoff.** Do not mistake failed HTML responses or mainTitle18PDF for proof of the full rule. The case's sanction is directly supported by lower orders/opinions, without assuming that its exact basis was16(d)(2).

**US opening pp.12–37; reply pp.1–19:** proposes a substantial, concrete threshold on discriminatory effect and purpose, explicitly **less than a prima facie case** but beyond nonfrivolous assertion (opening25). Absent direct discriminatory evidence, requires credible evidence similarly situated different-race offenders were not federally prosecuted. Emphasizes Executive discretion/presumption of regularity, delay and intrusion; invokes Wayte, Wade, McCleskey, YickWo/AhSin and comparison-pool reasoning. Reply14–15 acknowledges direct-intent evidence or comparably situated persons committing a different offense can matter in a suitable case. Reply18–19 identifies comparable drug quantities/firearm use as minimum here. These are party proposals, not neutral holdings.

**Rule16 posture:** US reply1 says Martin first raised the rule-based objection in this litigation; reply2–8 responds that materiality demands threshold support for the proposed defense and that the threshold does not invent a fourth categorical exemption. Do not replace that argument with an attributed categorical holding that selective-prosecution discovery necessarily falls outsideRule16. Martin pp.19–32 argues the rule covers documents material to a genuine defense, supports an objective reasonable basis to inquire, rejects fishing expeditions, and points to ordinary materiality/protective mechanisms. He also invokes Rule17(c), Brady, compulsory process, and inherent judicial authority. Those alternative claims are briefs' arguments; their acceptance below is not established.

**Access and bounded discovery:** Martin pp.23–25,31–32 proposes redaction, protective orders, in-camera review, ordering least burdensome discovery first, and requiring reasonable use of other accessible sources. He argues federal/state joint work and government's computer material make government access easier; US argues public state records/counsel contacts/localFPD studies could supply preliminary comparators, with relative convenience relevant only after threshold. Martin18n.47 characterizes earlier government remarks as acknowledging state subpoenas; **US reply17n.6 expressly denies unconditional enforceability**, invoking Rule17/Nixon and the same threshold. Do not state a concession without this qualification.

**Respondent arguments differ:** Rozelle pp.7–23 principally defends colorable-basis/reasonable-inference discovery and reserves merits proof; Martin pp.38–50 additionally argues named matching offenders are one means of showing racial motive, not indispensable in every possible discriminatory enforcement scenario. Martin51–53 says the ultimate “exceptionally clear proof” versus ordinary equal-protection burden is premature, distinguishes McCleskey and relies on Wayte/Batson. US disputes those analogies in reply12–14. Martin54–59 contests government's characterization of record reliance, ease of access and moving comparison criteria. No party position is converted here into a finding.
