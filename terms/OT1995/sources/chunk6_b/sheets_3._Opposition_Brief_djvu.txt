STATEMENT OF THE CASE 
A. STATEMENT OF FACTS 


On October 15, 1985, two bombs exploded in Salt 
Lake City, killing Steve Christensen, Respondent Gary 
Sheets’ former business associate, and Kathy Sheets, Mr. 
Sheets’ wife. The bombings generated tremendous media 
interest and public curiosity. App. 2a. 


A few weeks after Mrs. Sheets’ death, Salt Lake City 
detectives Jim Bell and Kenneth Farnsworth asked Mr. 
Sheets for Mrs. Sheets’ diary as part of the investigation 
of her murder. The diary contained personal and intimate 
information respecting Mr. Sheets’ marriage and family 


, 


relationships. Believing that the diary would remain con- 
fidential, Mr. Sheets voluntarily, but reluctantly, turned 
the diary over to Detective Bell. Detective Farnsworth 
gave Mr. Sheets assurances of confidentiality and relayed 
this message to other investigators. App. 2a. 


Copies of the diary were distributed to investigators 
and detectives assigned to the case, including Petitioner 
Michael George. Mr. George reviewed the diary, photo- 
copied certain excerpts, and made verbatim notes of its 
entries for his personal files. The original diary was 
returned to Mr. Sheets. App. 3a. 


In January 1987, when Mark Hofmann pleaded guilty 
to the murders, the investigation was closed. Salt Lake 
County’s investigative file, including copies of the diary, 
was then archived. Mr. George retained his personal file. 
App. 3a. 


At the time the murder investigation was concluded, 
the office of the Salt Lake County Attorney had an 
unwritten policy that allowed its employees, after an 
investigation was closed, to disclose to the media any 
information gathered during the course of an investiga- 
tion, so long as all media representatives were given 
equal access. Pursuant to the policy, Mr. George met with 
three authors writing books about the bombings. One of 
the authors was Robert Lindsey, a good friend of Mr. 
George. Mr. George allowed Mr. Lindsey to examine his 
personal files. Mr. George did not allow the other authors 
access to his personal files. App. 3a. 


There is no evidence in the record that the disclosure 
of the contents of Mrs. Sheets’ diary served any govern- 
mental interest. App. 8a. 


All three authors published books about the bomb- 
ings. Mr. Lindsey’s book, A Gathering of Saints, was the 
only one to contain verbatim quotations from Mrs. 
Sheets’ diary. App. 3a. 


B. COURSE OF THE PROCEEDINGS 


On July 31, 1989, Mr. Sheets filed suit against Lindsey 
and his publisher, claiming an invasion of his privacy 
arising from publication of Mr. Lindsey’s book. On 
December 5, 1990, Mr. Sheets filed an Amended Com- 
plaint to add a claim under 42 U.S.C. § 1983 against Salt 
Lake County, Mr. George and Salt Lake County Attorney 
Theodore Cannon for violation of his constitutional right 
to privacy. App. 16a. 


Salt Lake County moved to dismiss Mr. Sheets’ § 1983 
claims as untimely pursuant to Utah Code Ann. 
§ 78-12-28(3), a two-year statute of limitations specifically 
targeting § 1983 claims. The District Court denied the 
motion, rejecting the specific statute of limitations and 
applying Utah’s four-year residual personal injury stat- 
ute, Utah Code Ann. § 78-12-25(3), in accordance with 
Wilson v. Garcia, 471 U.S. 261 (1985), and Owens v. Okure, 
488 U.S. 235 (1989). App. 20a. 


At the close of Plaintiff’s case, Defendants moved to 
dismiss (in reality a motion for judgment as a matter of 
law pursuant to Fed. R. Civ. P. 50[a]), which the court 
denied. At the close of all the evidence, Defendants 
moved for jucgment as a matter of law, which motion 
was denied as to Petitioners Salt Lake County and 
Michael George, but granted as to Mr. Cannon. App. 4a. 


The District Court then instructed the jury on the 
elements of Mr. Sheets’ § 1983 claim, giving the following 
privacy interest instructions, to which Petitioners did not 
object: 


Jury Instruction 20: You are instructed that the 
United States Constitution protects the interests 
of individuals in their personal privacy and in 
avoiding the public disclosure of intimate or 
personal matters involving home, marriage and 
family. This right of privacy is a “right 
... secured by the Constitution” for purposes of 
Section 1983. To prove the second element of his 
claim, Plaintiff must prove by a preponderance 
of the evidence that his right to privacy, to avoid 
public disclosure of intimate or personal mat- 
ters, was violated. 


Jury Instruction No. 21: You must decide 
whether the information contained in the Kathy 
Sheets diary is of an intimate or personal nature, 
and whether a reasonable person in Plaintiff’s 
position would expect that the information con- 
tained in the Kathy Sheets diary would not be 
disclosed to the public while that information 
was in the possession of the Defendants. 


Petitioners sought no instruction concerning any govern- 
mental interest to be served by disclosure of the diary 
information. 


The jury returned a verdict of $650,000.00 against Salt 
Lake County and Mr. George. The jury specially found 
that the information contained in the diary was of an 
intimate or personal nature; that a reasonable person in 
Mr. Sheets’ position would expect that the information 


would not be disclosed to the public while the informa- 
tion was in Petitioners’ possession; and that Michael 
George intentionally disclosed the information in the 
diary to Robert Lindsey. App. 4a, 6a-10a. 


Petitioners filed motions for judgment as a matter of 
law, for a new trial and for remittitur. The District Court 
denied the post-trial motions. On Respondent’s motion, 
the District Court awarded him $77,896.93 in attorney’s 
fees. App. 4a. 


The Tenth Circuit affirmed and upheld the District 
Court's application of Utah’s four-year residual statute of 
limitations, Utah Code Ann. § 78-12-25(3). The Tenth 
Circuit further held that Mr. Sheets had a constitutionally 
protected privacy interest in avoiding disclosure of per- 
sonal matters; that the diary information was subject to a 
reasonable expectation of privacy; that Mr. Sheets legit- 
imately expected the diary to remain confidential; and 
that there was no compelling state interest in disclosure 
of the diary’s contents. App. 5a-8a.

REASONS FOR DENYING THE PETITION 


I. THE SPECIFIC PRIVACY ISSUES PRESENTED BY 
THIS CASE DO NOT MERIT REVIEW BY THIS 
COURT. 


The Due Process Clause of the Fourteenth Amend- | 
ment protects individuals from state intrusion on funda- 
mental aspects of personal privacy. See Roe v. Wade, 410 
U.S. 113, 152-53 (1973). This Court has held that the right 
to privacy safeguards individuals from government dis- 
closure of personal information. See Nixon v. Administrator 


of Gen’l Servs., 433 U.S. 425, 457 (1977); Whalen v. Roe, 429 
U.S. 589, 598-99 & n.24 (1977). While the circuits have 
wrestled with what information is sufficiently “personal” 
to warrant constitutional protection, no indication exists 
that any court in the land would refuse to protect Mr. 
Sheets’ interest in avoiding disclosure of the intimate 
marital and family information contained in his wife’s 
diary. 


A. Petitioners Have Failed to Set Forth Accurately 
the Decision of the Tenth Circuit Court of 
Appeals. 


Petitioners’ effort to portray a conflict between the 
Tenth Circuit’s holding in this case and the decisions of 
other circuits on the scope of the constitutional right to 
privacy in the disclosure context hinges largely upon a 
series of mischaracterizations of the Tenth Circuit’s hold- 
ing in this case. 


For example, Petitioners assert: 


The Tenth Circuit . . . found a constitutionally- 
rooted claim for privacy arises whenever an 
individual “claims an interest in avoiding dis- 
closure of things personal to him.” Petition at 7. 


Petitioners then declare: 


As shown by Sheets (App. 1a-14a), the Tenth 
Circuit permits a jury to find a constitutional 
right based almost entirely on subjective testi- 
mony from a plaintiff; if a court is willing to let 
a jury define a constitutional right with little to 
guide it but a plaintiff’s personal expectation, 
the contours will surely remain murky and 
unpredictable. Petition at 11-12. 


Building on that inaccurate premise, Petitioners state: 


In the Tenth Circuit, jurors are free to award 
damages to anyone claiming to have a personal 
expectation that some information is confiden- 
tial. Petition at 13. 


Petitioners also argue: 


At the extreme, the Tenth Circuit has recog- 
nized, for example, a constitutional right of free- 
dom from embarrassment arising from another 
person’s seemingly innocuous writings. Petition 
at 12. 


Petitioners then fashion the following conclusion 
based on their flawed construction of the opinion: 


Because the issue remains open, the Tenth Cir- 
cuit has stretched the scope of privacy, virtually 
obliterating any objectivity in determining what 
kinds of information should be given constitu- 
tional protection against disclosure. The Tenth 
Circuit transformed Mr. Sheets’ expectation into 
a constitutionally protected right not to be 
embarrassed. Petition at 18. 


A simple reading of the Tenth Circuit’s straightfor- 
ward conclusions and holding readily demonstrates that 
the opinion does not begin to approach the “murky and 
unpredictable” contours which Petitioners imagine. The 
Tenth Circuit ruled: 


Defendants have mischaracterized the issue. 
Plaintiff does not claim an interest in avoiding 
disclosure of his deceased wife’s general feel- 
ings and thoughts, rather he claims an interest 
in avoiding disclosure of things personal to him, 
such as his wife’s written perceptions of their 
marriage. 


ean _ 


Arguably, the information is not extremely sen- 
sitive in nature, in other words, the facts 
revealed in the diary about Mr. Sheets are not 
particularly controversial or embarrassing. 
However, information need not be embarrass- 
ing to be personal and whether it is sufficiently 
personal to be protected is, in this case, a legiti- 
mate question for the jury. We find that informa- 
tion conveyed to one’s spouse or that one’s 
spouse has observed about one’s character, mar- 
riage, finances and business to be personal in 
nature and subject to a reasonable expectation of 
privacy. Mrs. Sheets’ diary entries about her 
husband and their marital relationship are cer- 
tainly as personal as medical and certain finan- 
cial records, which courts have placed within 
the ambit of constitutional protection. 


The fact that Detective Bell could not recall 
whether he had initially assured Mr. Sheets of 
the diary’s confidentiality does not negate both 
Mr. Sheets and Detective Farnsworth’s testi- 
mony that an understanding of confidentiality 
did exist. In addition, the very nature of the 
item — a diary, a place traditionally reserved for 
the recording of private thoughts - which was 
given to the police for the specific purpose of 
aiding their investigation, bolsters Mr. Sheets’ 
claim that he expected the item to remain confi- 
dential. 


App. at 6a-8a (emphasis supplied).1 


? The Tenth Circuit’s holding affirms the jury instructions, 
which are consistent with the decisions of this Court in Paul v. 
Davis, 424 U.S. 693 (1976), Roe v. Wade, Whalen, and Nixon. Any

Far from Petitioners’ characterization of the Tenth 
Circuit’s holding as a carte blanche permitting plaintiffs to 
avenge hurt feelings, the Tenth Circuit’s opinion provides 
objective standards within constitutional parameters, and 
consistent with other circuits, for the redress of unwar- 
ranted disclosures of legitimately private information. 
Petitioners’ overreaching analysis of the Tenth Circuit's 
holding simply demonstrates the lack of merit in their 
argument that the Tenth Circuit’s decision is some sort of 
aberration. 


The crux of Petitioners’ argument is essentially that 
the jury found facts with which Petitioners disagree. The 
Tenth Circuit concluded that there was ample evidence 
for the jury to reach its verdict. This Court “[does] not 
grant a certiorari to review evidence and discuss specific 
facts.” United States v. Johnston, 268 U.S. 220, 227 (1925). 
Review of the jury’s verdict in this case is not warranted 
as an exercise of the Court’s certiorari jurisdiction. 


B. No Conflict Exists Among the Circuits Con- 
cerning Constitutional Protection of Intimate 
Information Respecting Marriage and Family 
Relationships. 


Contrary to Petitioners’ assertions, no circuit court of 
appeals “refuse[s] to recognize” the constitutionally pro- 
tected interest in nondisclosure of personal matters. Anal- 
ysis of the cases cited by Petitioners reveals merely that 


objection which Petitioners might have had to the instructions 
was waived by their failure to object to the instructions at trial 
and by their failure to seek an instruction concerning govern- 
mental interest. Fed. R. Civ. P. 51. 


11 


two circuits — the Sixth and Eighth — have denied consti- 
tutional protection to specific information alleged by the 
plaintiffs in those cases to be private. However, the cir- 
cumstances of each of those cases differ sharply from 
those of the present case. 


In the leading Sixth Circuit case, J.P. v. DeSanti, 653 
F.2d 1080 (6th Cir. 1981), the court found that interagency 
disclosure of certain juvenile court records was “indis- 
tinguishable” from the disclosure permitted in Paul. No- 
ting that “the Constitution does not encompass a general 
right to nondisclosure of private information,” the court 
concluded that the right of privacy should be restricted to 
the boundaries established by Paul and Roe v. Wade, i.e., 
those personal rights that can be deemed “fundamental” 
or “implicit in the concept of ordered liberty.” 653 F.2d at 
1090. 


The Sixth Circuit did not, however, refuse to recog- 
nize the existence of a constitutional right of non-dis- 
closure. To the contrary, it cautioned: “Our opinion does 
not mean that we attach little significance to the right of 
privacy, or that there is no constitutional right to non- 
disclosure of private information.” 653 F.2d at 1090 
(emphasis in original). See also Doe v. Wigginton, 21 F.3d 
733 (6th Cir. 1994) (rejecting a prisoner’s argument that 
disclosure of his HIV infection to a corrections officer 
during initial penitentiary processing violated the pris- 
oner’s constitutional right to privacy). 


The factual bases of DeSanti and Wigginton are polar 
opposites of those of the instant case. Both Sixth Circuit 
cases involve limited dissemination of criminal justice 


12 


system records. Neither case involves general public dis- 
closure of a crime victim’s diary containing intimate mar- 
ital and family information. 


Petitioners’ Eighth Circuit case is Alexander v. Peffer, 
993 F.2d 1348 (8th Cir. 1993), in which the court denied 
constitutional protection from disclosure of that plain- 
tiff’s unsuccessful attempt to become a police officer. The 
court found that “[t]he disclosures neither involved mat- 
ters deemed to be fundamental rights nor addressed 
highly personal medical or financial information.” Id. at 
1350-51. As with the Sixth Circuit cases, Alexander's facts 
differ to the -ore from those of this case, which involves 
both a fundamental right, marital privacy, and other 
highly personal information. 


DeSanti, Wigginton, and Alexander have a common 
denominator: The specific information disclosed in those 
cases did not implicate any fundamental right. However, 
both the Sixth and the Eighth Circuits have recognized 
that marriage and family relationships are “fundamental” 
in accordance with this Court’s decisions.? Accordingly,

Petitioners resort to speculating that Mr. Sheets 
might have no claim in the Fifth Circuit. However, the 
Fifth Circuit has consistently recognized a right to free- 
dom from disclosure of intimate matters, either under a 
pledge of confidentiality, Fadjo v. Coon, 633 F.2d 1172, 
1176 (Sth Cir. 1981), or when a “legitimate expectation of 
privacy” exists, Plante v. Gonzalez, 575 F.2d 1119, 1135 (5th

privacy interest . . . includes the right to be free from the

The decisions of the Fifth, Sixth and Eighth Circuits 
are in complete harmony with decisions of the Tenth 
Circuit concerning the scope of the constitutional right of 
Privacy. For example, the Tenth Circuit has declined to 
extend constitutional protection to police investigative 
files relating to police officers’ work as officers, Denver 
Policemen’s Protective Ass’n v. Lichtenstein, 660 F.2d 432 
(10th Cir. 1981); to an internal police report indicating 
that firefighters had used contraband drugs, Mangels v. 
Pena, 789 F.2d 836 (10th Cir. 1986); and to an expunged 
record of a plaintiff’s conviction for forcible sexual abuse, 
Nilson v. Layton City, 45 F.3d 369 (10th Cir. 1995).

Mr. Sheets has never claimed a “general right to 
nondisclosure.” Mr. Sheets claims a specific protected 
interest in the confidentiality of information respecting 
his marriage and family relationships. This interest is in 
perfect accord with Paul, which recognized that “matters 
relating to marriage, procreation, contraception, family 
relationships, and child rearing and education” are con- 
stitutionally protected areas of privacy in the § 1983 
context. 424 U.S. at 713. The Tenth Circuit’s recognition of 
a constitutionally protected privacy interest in intimate 
information concerning Mr. Sheets’ marriage and family 
relationships is likewise consistent with the interpreta- 
tions of this Court and of all circuits, including the Fifth,

Sixth and Eighth Circuits. Petitioners’ perceived conflicts 
simply do not exist.4 


C. Petitioners Have Failed to Demonstrate Any 
Governmental Interest to Justify Disclosure of 
the Diary Information. 


This Court has allowed intrusion into an individual’s 
zone of privacy only after finding that society’s interest in 
disclosure outweighs the privacy interest.

PRESENTED BY THIS CASE DO NOT MERIT

A. The Only Circuit Court Decision Which Argua- 
bly Conflicts with the Decision of the Tenth 
Circuit Has No Continuing Vitality. 


Nored v. Blehm, 743 F.2d 1386 (9th Cir. 1984), is the 
only circuit court case cited by Petitioners which argua- 
bly conflicts with the Tenth Circuit’s decision not to bor- 
row a state statute of limitations purporting to apply to 
§ 1983 claims.” In Nored, the Ninth Circuit found that 
federal courts could appropriately apply an Oregon stat- 
ute of limitations targeting § 1983. 


Nored has no continuing vitality for two distinct rea- 
sons. First, Nored was decided before this Court’s deci- 
sions in Wilson v. Garcia, which characterized § 1983 
actions as involving claims for personal injuries, and 
Owens v. Okure, which held that where a state has one or 
more statutes of limitations for enumerated intentional 
torts and a residual statute for all other personal injury 
actions, the residual statute applies.* Wilson and Owens 
clearly set forth criteria for selection of appropriate state

Hughes v. Sheriff of Fall River County Jail, 814 F.2d 532, 535 (8th 
Cir. 1987); Johnson v. Davis, 582 F.2d 1316, 1318-19 (4th Cir. 1978).

Second, since the Nored decision, the language in the 
Oregon statute referring specifically to § 1983 claims has 
been deleted. See Or. Rev. Stat. § 30.265(1). The Ninth

Petitioners’ own argument demonstrates that the law 
is settled, and consistent, in other jurisdictions. Following 
Wilson and Owens, courts have routinely adopted state 
general and residual personal injury statutes as the “most 
analogous” state statutes applicable to § 1983 claims -

The Utah statute of limitations rejected by the Tenth

specifically targets § 1983 claims. See Arnold v. Duchesne 
County, 26 F.3d 982, 985 (10th Cir. 1994), cert. denied, __

has addressed the issue, and because the law is settled 
among the circuits, it is unlikely that the issue of a state’s 
power to adopt statutes of limitation targeting only 
§ 1983 claims will recur. Certiorari is therefore inap-

B. The Tenth Circuit Correctly Held That Utah 
Code Ann. § 78-12-25(3) is the Most Appropri- 
ate Utah Statute of Limitations with Respect to 
§ 1983 Claims. 


In Mismash v. Murray City, 730 F.2d 1366, 1367 (10th 
Cir. 1984), the Tenth Circuit held that all § 1983 claims 
brought in federal court in Utah were subject to the four- 
year limitations period provided in Utah Code Ann. 
§ 78-12-25(3). 


Apparently in response to that decision, the Utah 
legislature adopted Utah Code Ann. § 78-12-28(3), which 
prescribes a two-year limitation for § 1983 claims. During 
the Utah Senate debate, Sen. Lyle Hillyard commented 
that a two-year, rather than four-year statute should be 
adopted because of the “proliferation” of § 1983 lawsuits, 
“especially in our prisons.” Floor Debate, 47th Utah Leg., 
Gen. Sess. (Jan 16, 1987) (statements of Sen. Lyle Hillyard) 
(Senate Recording No. 1, Side 1). It is reasonable to con- 
clude, as the Tenth Circuit did in Arnold, that the purpose 
of the statute was simply to reduce the number of such 
lawsuits, a purpose contradictory to section 1983’s broad 
remedial objectives. Arnold, 26 F.3d at 988.9 


Petitioners argue that, since other circuits have bor- 
rowed one- and two-year residual or general personal 
injury statutes of limitations, the Tenth Circuit’s adoption 
of Utah’s longer four-year statute creates “anomalous
