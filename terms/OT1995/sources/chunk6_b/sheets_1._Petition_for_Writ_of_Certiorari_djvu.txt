QUESTIONS PRESENTED 


1. In the face of conflicting circuit court standards, 
what is the appropriate way to determine whether infor- 
mation collected during a criminal investigation (here, 
brief excerpts from a homicide victim’s diary) should be 
given constitutional protection under the confidentiality 
branch of the right to privacy? 


2. If a constitutionally protected right to privacy in 
given information has been deduced, which of the several 
court standards determines when government may dis- 
close that information? 


3. May the Court of Appeals invalidate Utah’s two- 
year statute of limitations solely because the statute 
specifically refers to Section 1983 claims when other 
states may apply two-year (or even shorter) limitation 
periods to Section 1983 claims because their statutes do 
not refer to Section 1983?

STATEMENT OF THE CASE 


1. Early on the morning of October 15, 1985, a bomb 
exploded in Salt Lake City, killing Steve Christensen, 
a former executive of Coordinated Financial Services 
(“CFS”). Later that morning, a second bomb killed 
Kathy Sheets, wife of Appellee J. Gary Sheets, chairman 
of the board of CFS. App. 2a. 


The Salt Lake City Police and the Salt Lake County 
Sheriff immediately began investigating the bomb scenes 
and the lives of the victims, seeking leads to the bomber. 
Other local and federal agencies joined the investigation. 
Several days after the initial explosions, a third bomb 
exploded in a car owned by Mark’ Hoffman, seriously 
injuring Mr. Hoffman, a dealer in rare documents and 
a business acquaintance of Mr. Christensen and Mr. 
Sheets. The car had been parked on a downtown street 
a few blocks from Mr. Christensen’s office. The bomb- 
ings generated broad media attention and public concern, 
even some fear. 


During the investigation of the deaths, Salt Lake City 
Detectives Jim Bell and Kenneth Farnsworth made a 
routine request that Mr. Sheets let them take his late 
wife’s diary. After reading the diary, Mr. Sheets com- 
plied with the request, having been told by Detective 
Farnsworth it would remain confidential. App. 2a. The 
Salt Lake City Police never marked the diary confidential. 
Detective Bell provided the diary to the Salt Lake County 
Attorney’s office but never said to keep it confidential. 
A County employee made three copies (file copy, working 
copy and defense copy), then returned the original to 


4 


Detective Bell who made a copy before returning the 
diary to Mr. Sheets. 


Appellant Michael George, one of Salt Lake County’s 
investigators on the homicides, never was told the diary 
was promised to be confidential. App. 40a. He read the 
working copy and may have photocopied excerpts or 
made handwritten excerpts. 


In January 1987, Mr. Hoffman pleaded guilty to the 
two homicides. He also admitted forging several docu- 
ments that had generated widespread controversy in Utah. 
Salt Lake County thus closed its investigation. 


Salt Lake County keeps all investigative materials con- 
fidential during an investigation. After an investigation 
is closed, County files are archived, making them avail- 
able for public inspection. App. 3a. Copies of the diary 
were archived among voluminous other investigative ma- 
terials. Salt Lake County also permits its investigators 
to speak to the press after an investigation is closed, so 
long as all media representatives are accorded equal 
access. App. 3a. 


After the Hoffman plea, Mr. George met with three 
authors writing books about the bombings. Mr. George 
did not intentionally make a copy of the diary available to 
the authors. At most, he allowed one of them, Robert 
Lindsey, access to a file box that may (although he could 
not remember) have contained photocopies and his own 
notes about the diary. App. 3a.

3. On July 31, 1989, Mr. Sheets filed suit against 
Mr. Lindsey and his publisher because of the publication 
of the excerpts. App. 16a. On December 4, 1990, Mr. 
Sheets amended his complaint to add a claim under 42 
U.S.C. § 1983 against Salt Lake County. Mr. George 
and the then County Attorney. App. 3a-4a, 16a. Mr. 
Sheets claimed he had a reasonable expectation his wife’s 
writings would remain confidential and that publication 


6 


of the excerpts embarrassed him, violating his constitu- 
tional right of privacy and taking his property. 


The County defendants moved to dismiss on statute of 
limitations grounds because Mr. Sheets had failed to 
assert his purported Section 1983 claim within the two- 
year period allowed by Utah law. Utah Code Ann. § 78- 
12-28(3). The district court denied the motion, finding 
Utah’s specific statute of limitations governing Section 
1983 claims was “neither consistent with, nor analogous 
to, federal law and policy.” App. 22a. The district court 
opined Utah’s residual four-year statute of limitations, 
Utah Code Ann. § 78-12-25(3), was “the most analo- 
gous statute of limitations with respect to § 1983.” App. 
23a; see 42 U.S.C. § 1988. Accordingly, the district 
court ruled the claim timely and allowed the Section 
1983 claim to be tried to the jury. 


After the close of Mr. Sheets’ case and again after 
trial, the County and Mr. George moved for judgment 
as a matter of law under Fed. R. Civ. P. 50(1), arguing 
that publication of the excerpts did not rise to a violation 
of constitutional privacy. The district court dismissed the 
claim against the County Attorney but denied the rest 
of the motions, and concluded that under applicable Tenth 
Circuit law, Mr. Sheets had a constitutional privacy right 
if he had any reasonable expectation of confidentiality in 
the material. App. 39a-40a; see also App. 27a-40a. 


Although Mr. Sheets could testify only to his personal 
expectations of privacy, the jury returned a verdict of 
$650,000 against Salt Lake County and Mr. George. 
App. 4a. The District Court denied post-trial motions 
for judgment as a matter of law, for a new trial and for 
remittitur, then awarded an attorney’s fee of $77,896.93 
against Salt Lake County and Mr. George under 42 
U.S.C. § 1988(b). App. 25a-26a. 


The Tenth Circuit affirmed, following its earlier hold- 
ing that, in enacting U.C.A. § 78-12-28(3), “the Utah 
legislature exceeded its authority,” thus finding Utah’s 


7 


specific two-year limitation on Section 1983 claims “in- 
valid.” App. 4a, see Arnold v. Duchesne County, 26 
F.3d 982 (10th Cir. 1994), cert. denied, US. 
, 115 S.Ct. 721 (1995). Like the district court, 
the Tenth Circuit determined that Utah’s residual four- 
year statute of limitations, Utah Code Ann. § 78-12-25 
(3), applied. App. 5a. 


The Tenth Circuit next found a constitutionally-rooted 
claim for privacy arises whenever an individual “claims 
an interest in avoiding disclosure of things personal to 
him,” App. 6a, explaining that “Mrs. Sheets’ diary en- 
tries about her husband and their marital relationship 
are certainly as personal as medical and certain financial 
records, which courts have placed within the ambit of con- 
stitutional protection.” App. 7a-8a, citations omitted. 
Unless the County could show a “compelling state inter- 
est” in disclosing the information, a compensable Section 
1983 claim exists. App. 6a, 8a. The court found no 
such County interest. App. 8a.

REASONS FOR GRANTING THE PETITION 


I. THE DECISIONS OF THE COURTS OF APPEALS 
ARE IN DIRECT CONFLICT AS TO THE APPLIC- 
ABLE STANDARDS IN THE RECOGNITION AND 
ANALYSIS OF FEDERAL CONSTITUTIONAL PRI- 
VACY RIGHTS. 


Although apparently recognizing that some expectation 
of confidentiality might be so weighty as to invoke con- 
stitutional protection, this Court has never given con- 
stitutional protection to any claim of privacy under Sec- 
tion 1983 for disclosure of allegedly confidential informa- 
tion. 


Eighteen years ago, in a trilogy of cases decided in 
the 1975 and 1976 Terms, the Court provided scant 
analysis to determine when, if ever, an individual’s claim 
to a general expectation of confidentiality may rise to a 
level of constitutional protection under a right of privacy. 
Nixon v. Administrator of General Services, 433 US. 
425, 465 (1977); Whalen v. Roe, 429 U.S. 589 at 602- 
03 (1977); Paul v. Davis, 424 U.S. 693, 712-14 
(1976).' In Whalen, this Court stated: 


The cases sometimes characterized as protecting “pri- 
vacy” have in fact involved at least two different 
kinds of interests. One is the individual interest in 
avoiding disclosure of personal matters, and another 
is the interest in independence in making certain 
kinds of important decisions. 429 U.S. at 599-600, 
footnotes omitted.

tory because they discussed a branch of the privacy right

B. Even if a Constitutional Confidentiality Right Has 
Been Recognized, the Circuits Are in Deep Conflict 
About the Way to Treat the Right in the Face of 
Competing Governmental Interests. 


In Nixon, supra, 433 U.S. 425, 456-57, the majority 
agreed with the lower court’s finding of a reasonable 
balance between the government’s overriding interest in 
preserving Presidential materials and segregating purely 
personal documents. However, this Court did not explain 
how to treat future claims of privacy interests and gov- 
ernment interests. 


The First Circuit has commented that although “bal- 
ancing of competing interests” might be required when 
interests in personal privacy and governmental need are 
at issue, the cases “provide little guidance as to how such 
interests would be balanced.” Borucki v. Ryan, 827 F.2d 
836, 849 (Ist Cir. 1987). See Pesce v. J. Sterling Mor- 
ton High Sch. Dist. 201, 830 F.2d 789, 797 (7th Cir. 
1987) (constitutional right to confidentiality does not 
protect against any and every compelled disclosure; stand- 
ard of review governing claim of abridgment of right to 
confidentiality may not be settled). 


Because the Sixth Circuit focuses primarily on the 
limited extent of a right of privacy, it avoids balanc- 
ing government action against claimed rights of privacy. 
J.P. v. DeSanti, 653 F.2d 1080, 1091 (6th Cir. 1981). 
The Sixth Circuit may stand alone in its perception of 
privacy rights and governmental! disclosure.

In the absence of standards from this Court, some Cir- 
cuits have ventured upon the “delicate task of weighing 
competing interests,” United States v. Westinghouse Elec. 
Corp., 638 F.2d 570, 578 (3d Cir. 1980), adopting 
sharply different, conflicting standards to apply once a 
particular claim to a right of privacy has been acknowl- 
edged. With the many conflicts between Circuits and 
even uncertainties within a Circuit, orderly classification 
of all Circuit tests is virtually impossible. Nonetheless, 
cases can be partially grouped into what may be useful 
and illustrative categories. 


BURDEN ON PLAINTIFF. The Eighth Circuit clearly 
stands alone in its balancing test. It recently put the 
burden on plaintiff to show that a disclosure would be 
“shocking” or “egregious” enough to defeat a specific 
State interest: “to violate [Plaintiff]’s constitutional right 
of privacy the information disclosed must be either a 
shocking degradation or an egregious humiliation of her 
to further some specific state interest, or a flagrant 
bre[a]ch of a pledge of confidentiality which was instru- 
mental in obtaining the personal information.” Alexander 
v. Peffer, 993 F.2d 1348, 1350 (8th Cir. 1993), em- 
phasis added. The Eighth Circuit’s objective test is 
diametrically opposite to the Tenth Circuit in Sheets. 


“COMPELLING” STATE INTEREST. The Tenth Circuit 
imposes a burden on government to demonstrate a “com- 
pelling” interest in disclosing information. Denver Police-

435 (10th Cir. 1981): see also Nilson v. Layton City, 
45 F.3d 369, 371 (10th Cir. 1995). 


Only the Ninth Circuit might agree with the result in 
this case because the Ninth Circuit has also applied a 
“compelling state interest” test to determine what dis- 
closures of confidential information can be permitted. 
Grummett v. Rushen, 779 F.2d 491, 494 (9th Cir. 
1985) (compelling state interest in prison security de-

obliterating any objectivity in determining what kinds of 
information should be given constitutional protection 
against disclosure. The Tenth Circuit transformed Mr. 
Sheets’ expectation into a constitutionally protected right 
not to be embarrassed. 


The Sheets case sharply focuses the open issues from 
the Whalen trilogy; now is the ideal and compelling time 
to return to the confidentiality branch of the right of 
privacy to resolve almost two decades of uncharted and 
unguided conflicts among the courts of appeals over the 
scope of confidentiality and how to evaluate competing 
private and public interests in the disclosure of material 
that has been found to be confidential.
