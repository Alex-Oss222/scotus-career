### Rubin v. Coors Brewing Co., No. 93-1631

Merits decision, April 19, 1995

#### Chronology and Posture

Argued November 30, 1994; decided April 19, 1995. On writ of certiorari to the United States Court of Appeals for the Tenth Circuit, reviewing 2 F.3d 355 (August 23, 1993). The question is whether the federal ban on truthful numerical alcohol-content statements on malt-beverage labels satisfies the First Amendment. The separately upheld, unappealed advertising restriction is outside the judgment under review.

Section 205(e)(2) prohibits malt-beverage alcohol-content statements unless state law requires them. Its wine clause requires alcohol-content statements only above fourteen percent by volume; it does not prohibit lower-strength wine disclosure. Section 205(f)(2), addressing advertising, contains an alcohol-information exception for malt beverages and wines, without the labeling clause’s state-required-disclosure exception. A later interstate malt-beverage paragraph expressly addresses both labeling and advertising and conditions their application on similar state requirements for comparable intrastate transactions. The agency administers the label ban except where state law affirmatively requires disclosure, but administers the advertising ban where a corresponding state advertising prohibition exists. This is the operating construction, not a holding that the later paragraph mentions only advertising or a new statutory challenge to that construction.

Under the challenged §7.26 regime, state-required statements follow the manner specified by the State; absent such a specification, they use percentage by weight or volume rather than proof, maximums or minimums. Low or reduced alcohol requires less than 2.5 percent alcohol by volume. Non-alcoholic requires the directly conjoined, readily legible, contrasting-background statement that the product contains less than 0.5 percent alcohol by volume. Alcohol-free requires no alcohol. These distinct permissions do not provide all useful numerical comparison information. Sections 7.29(f) and (g), concerning strength descriptions and numerical or design suggestions of strength, are outside this adjudication. Separate outdoor-advertising and publisher/radio provisions are not conflated with the state-law clauses or independently adjudicated. The April 1993 interim suspension of regulatory enforcement complied with the injunction; it was not statutory repeal or a concession of invalidity.

The Government invokes strength competition, possible shifts in consumer preferences, and assistance to state alcohol policy. Coors contests the effectiveness and breadth of suppressing accurate percentages while other channels and beverages receive different treatment. No independently verified fifty-state tally or future strength war is found. The Court assumes the state-assistance rationale may be considered and rejects its unsupported justification on the merits; it does not decide forfeiture.

Chief Justice Alex-Lamar Stone-Zsela and Justices Stevens, O’Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg, and Breyer participated.

#### Judgment

The Court unanimously affirms the judgment sustaining the injunction against the challenged prohibition of truthful numerical alcohol-content statements on malt-beverage labels. Eight Justices join the Court’s commercial-speech grounds; Justice Stevens concurs in the judgment.

| Judgment component | Disposition and vote | Supporting Justices | Opposing Justices | Remedy or remand |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Challenged numerical label ban | Affirm, 9–0 | Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O’Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg, Breyer | No opposing Justice | Preserve the injunction against the challenged label restriction. |

The unappealed advertising judgment remains undisturbed. The Court orders no national label format, alcohol ceiling, tax, or replacement regulation and invalidates no separate state statute or descriptive-strength provision.

| Concurrence in the judgment | Stevens | No other Justice | Affirm | Distinct objection to suppressing accurate information because consumers may use it; agrees the scheme fails to advance its stated aim, but does not join the Court’s framework or independent Fox holding. |

#### Controlling Propositions and Authority

##### The challenged scheme does not materially advance its asserted health interest

**Controlling proposition:** The Government may not suppress truthful, nonmisleading numerical alcohol-content statements on lawful malt-beverage labels unless the restriction directly and materially advances a substantial interest. Preventing strength competition and its health harms is substantial, but the challenged scheme fails that requirement on this record; predictive judgment is permissible, while abstract concern and unexplained inconsistencies do not establish the necessary connection.

**Authority:** Justice Kennedy’s opinion of the Court, joined by Stone-Zsela, O’Connor, Scalia, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer: eight direct votes. Stevens agrees that the scheme fails in practice but supplies a distinct judgment ground rather than a framework join.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that this numerical-label ban has not been shown materially to reduce the strength competition invoked to defend it. Central Hudson requires a real connection between the restriction and the substantial interest; Edenfield requires justification for the actual restraint. The operating scheme permits alcohol information for other beverages, permits malt-beverage advertising where no corresponding state prohibition applies, and yields to state-required label disclosure. Those features leave comparable incentives operating while suppressing useful factual labels. The Court does not demand identical rules for all beverages, controlled studies in every case, or regulation of all sources at once. Edge permits a demonstrated incremental accommodation, while Discovery Network and Lanphere require the chosen distinction to address the asserted harm. The Government’s predictive argument and concern that existing preferences reflect the ban deserve consideration. They do not explain how this scheme materially works despite its inconsistencies. The holding concerns accurate, nonmisleading percentages, not deceptive claims or direct control of alcohol strength.

**Precedent treatment:**

- Central Hudson Gas & Electric Corp. v. Public Service Commission, 447 U.S. 557: applies its substantial-interest and direct-advancement requirements.
- Edenfield v. Fane, April 26, 1993: applies the requirement of responsive justification for the actual commercial-speech restriction.
- Cincinnati v. Discovery Network, March 24, 1993, and Lanphere & Urbaniak v. Colorado, April 18, 1995: apply the requirement that the selected speech restriction address the demonstrated harm, without an all-sources-at-once rule.
- United States v. Edge Broadcasting Co., June 25, 1993: distinguishes its supported state-based scheme; its holding creates no categorical vice-speech exemption.

##### The ban independently lacks reasonable proportional fit

**Controlling proposition:** Independently of the failure of material advancement, the categorical suppression of accurate numerical beer-label information lacks a reasonable fit to the asserted strength-competition harm in light of its unexplained breadth and workable measures directed more closely at that harm. Reasonable fit requires proportionality, not the least restrictive imaginable means, and the Court prescribes no particular substitute regulation.

**Authority:** Kennedy’s Court opinion and the same eight direct joiners adopt this independently sufficient ground. Stevens’s distinct concurrence is not counted as a Fox-framework vote.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that the label prohibition is disproportionate to the harm offered to justify it. Fox requires a reasonable fit, not perfection or the least restrictive conceivable choice. Here direct regulation of alcohol strength, focused controls on promotion emphasizing high strength, and a restriction directed to the allegedly affected market segment provide concrete comparisons with a ban on every accurate percentage. The Government answers that national strength limits could displace state choices and that a malt-liquor limitation might miss competition elsewhere. Those objections do not explain suppressing useful comparison information across the challenged category, including information that may assist selection of weaker products. Discovery Network and Lanphere require an account of that actual burden; Ibanez likewise requires a justification responsive to the selected restriction. The existing record and litigated district-court ground permit this alternative holding although the court of appeals stopped at advancement. The Court orders no tax, strength ceiling, national label design or replacement program, and leaves properly justified deceptive-claim regulation available.

**Precedent treatment:**

- Board of Trustees v. Fox, 492 U.S. 469: applies reasonable proportional fit and preserves rejection of a least-restrictive-means requirement.
- Discovery Network and Lanphere: apply their distinct fit inquiry to the burden and supported alternatives here.
- Ibanez v. Florida Department of Business and Professional Regulation, June 13, 1994: applies the need for justification responsive to the actual truthful-information burden.

##### The asserted need to assist state policy supplies no alternative justification on this record

**Controlling proposition:** Assuming the Government’s state-assistance rationale is properly considered, its unsubstantiated assertion that federal suppression of accurate label information is needed to facilitate state alcohol policy does not justify this restriction. Legitimate accommodation of differing state policies remains possible when its need and operation are supported; federalism and the Twenty-first Amendment do not alone replace First Amendment scrutiny.

**Authority:** Kennedy’s Court opinion and the same eight Justices directly adopt this rejection of the asserted alternative justification. The Court decides no independent forfeiture issue.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that the Government has not established the particular state-policy justification advanced for federal label suppression. Edge recognizes that an effective accommodation of differing state policies may satisfy commercial-speech review. It does not treat an invocation of state authority as proof of the need for a federal information ban. Here the Government has not demonstrated why States cannot operate their own alcohol policies without this suppression of truthful percentages. The statutory state-law provisions and the agency’s differing administration of labeling and advertising describe the scheme; they do not themselves prove its necessity or effect. The Court assumes the rationale may be considered and therefore need not decide Coors’s preservation objection. Nor does it deny the States’ authority to address alcohol harms or hold that federal assistance can never be legitimate. On this record the asserted assistance supplies neither a sufficient independent justification nor an answer to the demonstrated defects in advancement and proportional fit.

**Precedent treatment:**

- Edge Broadcasting: preserves the legitimacy of supported state-policy accommodation while distinguishing the missing demonstrated mechanism here.
- Central Hudson: requires the asserted justification to support the actual speech restriction rather than stand as an unexamined label.

#### Precedent Treatment and Current-Law Effect

Central Hudson and Fox remain the commercial-speech framework. Discovery Network, Edenfield, Ibanez and Lanphere supply the actual requirements of a harm-related classification, responsive evidence, and reasonable fit. Edge preserves supported incremental and state-based regulation at its controlling scope. No precedent is overruled; no categorical vice exception, strict-scrutiny rule for all commercial speech, or least-restrictive-means requirement is adopted.

The challenged truthful numerical malt-beverage label ban fails both material advancement and, independently, reasonable proportional fit. The unsupported state-assistance rationale does not cure either defect. The advertising judgment, distinct strength-description and design rules, state enactments and other applications of §205 remain outside the relief. Lawful direct regulation and properly justified measures against deception remain available without a judicially prescribed replacement program.

#### Separate Positions Relevant to the Decision

Justice Stevens concurs in the judgment. Accurate, unadorned information about a lawful product cannot be suppressed here simply because consumers may use it to choose a stronger beverage. His Edge dissent identifies the objection to keeping consumers uninformed, while Discovery Network requires the selected speech category to relate to an actual harm. He agrees that this scheme fails to advance the stated objective, but does not suggest that greater success at withholding truthful knowledge would cure the underlying objection. His Lanphere joinder in a bounded Central Hudson application did not abandon that distinct ground. He does not deny the Government’s authority to regulate alcohol’s harms directly, forbid deceptive claims, or require properly justified useful disclosures. He declines to join the Court’s commercial-speech framework, its acceptance of the information-suppression interest as framed, or its Fox alternative holding. His truthful-information rationale is noncontrolling; the eight-Justice Court opinion controls without combining separate grounds.

[27 U.S.C. §205](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/USCODE-1994-title27/pdf/USCODE-1994-title27.pdf) supplies the distinct labeling, advertising and interstate state-law provisions. The agency’s operating construction is distinguished from the statute’s text; no fifty-state survey, unverified state enactment, or unappealed advertising issue is independently adjudicated.

#### Mandate, Remedy, and Stage

The injunction against the challenged truthful numerical label restriction remains in force. No further merits remand is required. The Government may pursue lawful, appropriately justified alternatives, but the Court commands no particular replacement. The unappealed advertising judgment and separate descriptive-strength provisions are undisturbed.

### Florida Bar v. Went For It, Inc., No. 94-226

#### Controlling Propositions and Authority

##### Truthful targeted mail and the demonstrated fit of the waiting period

**Controlling proposition:** On this record, Florida may not forbid truthful, nonmisleading targeted direct-mail solicitation about lawful legal services until an accident or disaster is more than thirty days old. The asserted privacy and professional-confidence interests are substantial, but Florida has not established that this categorical silence materially and proportionately addresses concrete harm; the referral-service rule is likewise invalid insofar as it incorporates that restriction.

**Authority:** Kennedy’s Opinion of the Court, joined by Stone-Zsela, Stevens, Souter and Ginsburg; five Justices adopt the same advancement-and-fit ground.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that protecting privacy does not dispense with the State’s burden to connect the restriction it chose to actual harm. Central Hudson requires material advancement and reasonable fit; Board of Trustees v. Fox does not demand the least restrictive conceivable means. Shapero distinguishes a mailed communication that the recipient can discard from the coercive pressure of an in-person encounter. Florida’s study summary and complaints show disapproval of immediate solicitation and concern for professional reputation. They do not establish that truthful, identified mail deprives recipients of choice or that suppressing all such mail during the fixed period protects privacy in a way proportionate conduct rules cannot. Existing restrictions can address deception, coercion, harassment, unwilling recipients and misuse of confidential information. Lanphere and Rubin require the actual causal connection while permitting supported predictive judgments. The Court requires no numerical study or least-restrictive-means showing. A materially different record establishing concrete injury and fit remains open.

**Precedent treatment:**

- Central Hudson Gas & Electric Corp. v. Public Service Commission: applies material advancement and reasonable fit to protected lawful, truthful commercial speech.
- Board of Trustees v. Fox: preserves reasonable fit without a least-restrictive-means requirement.
- Shapero v. Kentucky Bar Association: applies the difference between discardable mail and in-person pressure.
- Bates v. State Bar of Arizona: preserves protection for truthful lawyer advertising.
- Lanphere & Urbaniak v. Colorado; Rubin v. Coors Brewing Co.: apply the current actual-harm, material-advancement and reasonable-fit requirements; supported prediction is permitted, professional distaste alone is insufficient.

The Court does not invalidate supported rules against in-person coercion, false or misleading content, repeated contact after an opt-out, known inability to exercise reasonable hiring judgment, represented recipients, confidential-record misuse or narrowly fitted disclosure requirements. Those separate restrictions are not exceptions authorizing premature mail under the challenged waiting-period text. The Bar’s abandoned undue-influence rationale supplies no independent holding.

#### Precedent Treatment and Current-Law Effect

No professional-advertising exception replaces Central Hudson, and Bates and Shapero are not overruled.

The specific waiting-period restriction and its referral incorporation cannot be enforced on this record. Florida remains free to regulate proven deception, coercion, harassment and confidential-record misuse and to adopt proportionate privacy protections. The evidentiary holding does not forbid every supported temporal restriction or require numerical studies.

#### Separate Positions Relevant to the Decision

O’Connor, joined by Scalia, Thomas and Breyer, dissents. The dissent regards the unrequested intrusion on people recently injured or bereaved as a concrete privacy harm supported by the study summary and public submissions. A finite waiting period, with public advertisements and later direct mail available, is reasonably fitted under Central Hudson and Fox. The dissent distinguishes Shapero’s broad mailing ban and rejects any need to exhaust every conceivable alternative. It does not rely on professional distaste alone, revive the abandoned undue-influence theory or overrule protection for truthful advertising.

### United States v. Edge Broadcasting Co., No. 92-486

Merits disposition, 1993-06-25

#### Chronology and Posture

Argued 1993-04-21; decided 1993-06-25. On writ of certiorari to Fourth Circuit, 956 F.2d 263 (1992).

**Question:** Whether the federal restriction on lottery advertisements by a station licensed in a nonlottery State violates the First Amendment as applied to a border station whose audience is predominantly in a lottery State.

**Material record:** Edge’s station was licensed to a North Carolina community and broadcast from North Carolina near Virginia. About 92.2 percent of its audience lived in Virginia, which operated a lottery, and 7.8 percent in nine North Carolina counties. North Carolina prohibited the relevant lottery activity. The station accounted for an estimated eleven percent of listening in that North Carolina area. Sections 1304 and 1307 prohibited the advertisements on stations licensed in nonlottery States while permitting specified state-lottery advertising in lottery States. The unopposed construction below excluded noncommercial lottery information from the restriction.

**Relief sought:** The United States seeks reversal of the as-applied invalidation. Edge seeks preservation of declaratory and injunctive relief allowing Virginia lottery advertisements.

All nine Justices—Stone-Zsela, White, Blackmun, Stevens, O’Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter and Thomas—participated at argument and decision.

#### Judgment

Reversed and remanded, 7–2; the as-applied First Amendment basis for the injunction is removed.

#### Controlling Propositions and Authority

##### Meaningful reduction and reasonable fit in a border broadcaster’s application

**Controlling proposition:** Assuming truthful advertising of lawful activity, the licensing-State lottery restriction satisfies Central Hudson as applied to Edge because it meaningfully reduces lottery promotion reaching North Carolina residents and reasonably accommodates the different policies of lottery and nonlottery States. Complete exclusion of cross-border advertising is unnecessary, but the actual advancement and reasonable-fit requirements remain; no categorical exception for vice advertising or bar on as-applied challenges follows.

**Authority:** White’s Opinion of the Court, this proposition; Stone-Zsela, White, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas (5 Justices). Direct majority support at the stated level of generality; no Marks aggregation.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that the local effect of this restriction is substantial enough to justify its application. Edge obtains most listeners and advertising revenue from Virginia, but it also accounts for eleven percent of radio listening in the affected North Carolina counties. Excluding lottery promotion from that listening time materially serves the federal accommodation of North Carolina’s policy. Central Hudson requires advancement of the asserted interest; it does not require complete eradication of advertising from every other source. Fox requires reasonable fit and therefore permits consideration of an administrable licensing line connected to the community served. Discovery Network involved a distinction unrelated to the physical harms asserted there, while this distinction tracks differing state lottery policies. Edenfield’s requirement of real support remains satisfied by this audience record. The Virginia audience’s lawful interest is weighed rather than ignored. The Court does not decide whether merely marginal advancement would suffice, whether gambling advertisements lack protection categorically, or any unresolved alternative equality claim.

**Precedent treatment:**

- Central Hudson Gas & Electric Corp. v. Public Service Commission,447 U.S.557: four-part scrutiny applied, with the first inquiry assumed in Edge’s favor.
- Board of Trustees v. Fox,492 U.S.469: reasonable fit applied; no least-restrictive-means requirement.
- Discovery Network, March 24, and Edenfield, April 26, 1993: unrelated classifications and unsupported harms distinguished, their scrutiny retained.
- Posadas,478 U.S.328: no overruling and no adoption of the greater-power/lesser-power argument.

#### Current-Law Effect

Effective June 25, Edge supplies a five-Justice record-specific Central Hudson application. Actual meaningful reduction and reasonable fit control; no majority establishes a no-as-applied-review rule, categorical vice exception or automatic permission to suppress speech for federalism alone.

The Court neither adopts their broader interstate-information rule nor forecloses the preserved alternative claim.

### Edenfield v. Fane, No. 91-1594

Merits decision, 1993-04-26

Edenfield v. Fane; No. 91-1594; October Term 1992. Argued: 1992-12-07; decided 1993-04-26. Source locator: 507 U.S. 761 (1993). Eleventh Circuit, 945 F.2d 1514 (1991). On writ of certiorari to that court.

Question: Whether Florida may categorically prohibit direct personal solicitation of business clients by certified public accountants on the justifications and evidence presented.

Fane proposed contacting business executives to offer CPA services. The Board invoked prevention of fraud, protection of privacy and professional independence. It supplied neither studies nor anecdotal evidence showing the alleged harms from CPA solicitation; the Dooner affidavit made generalized assertions. Contrary professional evidence and the controlled business setting distinguished lawyer solicitation of vulnerable accident victims. The injunction reaches the challenged business-solicitation prohibition, not fraud or coercion.

Chief Justice Stone-Zsela and Justices White, Blackmun, Stevens, O’Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter and Thomas participate at argument (1992-12-07) and decision (1993-04-26). No recusal or other nonparticipation is established.

The Court affirms, 8–1. The prohibition on direct, uninvited business solicitation by CPAs cannot stand on this record. Stone-Zsela, White, Blackmun, Stevens, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter and Thomas support the judgment; O’Connor dissents. Justice Kennedy delivers the Court’s commercial-speech opinion, joined by Stone-Zsela, White, Blackmun, Stevens, Scalia, Souter and Thomas. Blackmun also concurs separately; O’Connor files a dissent. Neither separate writing has another joiner.

When truthful, nonmisleading solicitation concerns lawful CPA services, the State must demonstrate that the harms supporting its restriction are real and that the restriction alleviates them to a material degree. Florida’s unsupported predictions about direct solicitation of business clients do not satisfy that burden; the ruling neither requires empirical studies in every case nor immunizes deceptive or coercive solicitation.

The controlling authority is Kennedy’s Opinion of the Court; Stone-Zsela, White, Blackmun, Stevens, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas (8 Justices). This proposition commands a majority of the Court.

The Court holds that the State must connect its legitimate concerns to the speech it suppresses. Central Hudson requires more than a substantial interest stated in the abstract; Fox preserves a reasonable fit rather than demanding the least restrictive imaginable rule. Florida’s interests in preventing fraud, protecting privacy and maintaining independence are substantial, but the record does not establish the asserted threat from CPA business solicitation. The Board’s generalized affidavit does not show that such contacts undermine professional judgment, and the professional evidence points the other way. Ohralik permitted prophylaxis against personal solicitation by lawyers in circumstances involving vulnerable clients and pressure for immediate commitment. Business executives considering accounting services can control the time and setting and need not decide immediately. That difference defeats the proposed analogy, not every professional prophylactic rule. Nor can labeling the prohibition a time, place and manner restriction cure its failure materially to advance the asserted interests, even assuming that alternative framework applies. Fraud, coercion and materially different settings remain open.

Central Hudson Gas & Electric Corp. v. Public Service Commission, 447 U.S. 557: applies the real-advancement burden.

Board of Trustees v. Fox, 492 U.S. 469: retains reasonable fit without least restrictive means.

Ohralik v. Ohio State Bar Assn., 436 U.S. 447: distinguished by professional setting, vulnerability and pressure.

Ward v. Rock Against Racism, 491 U.S. 781: alternative time/place/manner characterization cannot cure deficient advancement.

Cincinnati v. Discovery Network, March 24, 1993: consistent application; no expansion to strict scrutiny.

Before: Discovery Network, Central Hudson and Fox require real advancement and reasonable fit. After: those requirements invalidate this unsupported CPA business-solicitation prohibition; no strict scrutiny, universal studies requirement or least-restrictive-means rule is adopted. Fraud, coercion and materially different professional settings remain regulable.

Blackmun joins the opinion and judgment but maintains that truthful, nonmisleading commercial speech deserves more protection than the intermediate framework affords; that broader view is not adopted. O’Connor dissents. Her Shapero and Peel positions support attention to the culture and responsibilities of a profession, not simply the sophistication of a particular prospective client. She would allow Florida to protect independence and prevent pressure through a prophylactic restriction without waiting for proven abuse. In her view the Court understates experience-based risks and permits an injunction extending to business solicitation generally on a record centered on Fane’s proposed conduct. She would reverse; no Justice joins her writing.

On writ of certiorari to the Sixth Circuit's October 11, 1991 judgment, 946 F.2d 464, affirming declaratory and injunctive relief against the City's selective commercial-handbill newsrack prohibition. #### Controlling Propositions and Authority

##### 1. The selected commercial category lacks the required fit

**Controlling proposition:** When truthful, nonmisleading commercial publications concerning lawful activity and newspapers cause the same asserted physical harms through their newsracks, the City must justify why its selected commercial category reasonably fits its safety and aesthetic interests. Cincinnati has not done so: removing respondents' sixty-two racks while retaining approximately 1,500–2,000 physically similar newspaper racks violates the First Amendment; a small reduction is not invalid merely for being small, and no least-restrictive-means or all-sources-at-once requirement follows.

**Authority:** Stevens's opinion for the Court, joined by Stone-Zsela, Blackmun, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy and Souter: seven, throughout.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that the content distinction must do constitutional work connected to the City's asserted objectives. Central Hudson requires a substantial interest, direct advancement and an appropriately limited restriction of protected commercial speech. Board of Trustees v. Fox makes that fit reasonable and proportional, not necessarily perfect or the least restrictive available. Here safety and appearance are substantial concerns, but respondents' racks do not create different physical harms from the many newspaper racks the City leaves untouched. The lower value assigned to commercial speech does not explain the choice of this category to address those harms. Removing sixty-two racks produces some reduction; its size alone is not decisive. The defect is the unexplained selection among equivalent sources of the stated problem. Neutral size, location, number and maintenance rules illustrate alternatives relevant to fit without becoming mandatory alternatives. The Court does not abolish commercial-speech distinctions or adopt the view that all truthful commercial speech receives full strict scrutiny.

**Precedent treatment:**

- Central Hudson Gas & Electric Corp. v. Public Service Commission, 447 U.S. 557 (1980): applies the existing commercial-speech test to the accepted classification.
- Board of Trustees v. Fox, 492 U.S. 469 (1989): applies reasonable proportional fit and government burden; preserves rejection of least restrictive means.
- Metromedia, Inc. v. San Diego, 453 U.S. 490 (1981): distinguishes the billboard treatment; does not permit an unrelated content distinction without the required fit.

##### 2. The selective prohibition is not content neutral

**Controlling proposition:** A newsrack prohibition determined by whether its publication is a commercial handbill is content based when the asserted safety and aesthetic harms do not justify that distinction without reference to the publication's content. Cincinnati cannot sustain this selective ban as a content-neutral time, place and manner restriction merely because its objective is benign; otherwise lawful neutral regulation of newsracks remains available.

**Authority:** Stevens's Court opinion, joined by Stone-Zsela, Blackmun, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy and Souter: seven. This rejects the City's independent time, place and manner defense.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that the City cannot convert this selective prohibition into a neutral regulation by describing its objective as safety or appearance. Ward v. Rock Against Racism and Clark v. Community for Creative Non-Violence permit appropriate regulation of the time, place and manner of speech when it is justified without reference to content. The operative distinction here requires looking at the publication and excludes commercial handbills while leaving newspapers in the same physical setting. The City's interests explain concern about racks generally but do not explain that selection. Renton v. Playtime Theatres permits attention to distinct secondary effects; the record identifies no comparable difference between the physical effects of these categories of racks. Benign motive therefore does not establish content neutrality. The Court does not hold that every regulation touching commercial expression is invalid or that the City lacks authority over sidewalks. A properly drawn neutral regulation remains subject to the established standards and may address the demonstrated physical problems.

**Precedent treatment:**

- Ward v. Rock Against Racism, 491 U.S. 781 (1989): applies justification without reference to content; a benign objective alone does not validate the selective line.
- Clark v. Community for Creative Non-Violence, 468 U.S. 288 (1984): preserves valid content-neutral time, place and manner regulation.
- Renton v. Playtime Theatres, Inc., 475 U.S. 41 (1986): distinguishes different secondary effects; no such difference justifies this rack classification.

#### Precedent Treatment and Current-Law Effect

Central Hudson and Fox remain the governing commercial-speech framework; Metromedia is distinguished, not overruled. Ward, Clark and Renton retain their existing operation. The decision creates no general rule equating commercial and noncommercial speech. Casey's bounded medical-information holdings and the other speech decisions remain in their distinct settings.

The particular selective ban fails reasonable fit and cannot be upheld as content-neutral time, place and manner regulation. Incremental regulation remains possible, and alternatives matter without imposing least restrictive means. A city must explain a content distinction's relation to the harms it invokes; physical rack rules remain available if otherwise lawful. No claim about all newsrack restrictions, misleading advertising, unlawful transactions or every commercial classification is resolved.

#### Separate Positions Relevant to the Decision

Blackmun joins the Court but would afford truthful, noncoercive commercial speech about lawful activity the full protection ordinarily afforded speech. Virginia Pharmacy protects the public's interest in receiving useful commercial information; commercial motivation alone does not justify reduced protection. Deceptive or coercive practices, offers of unlawful transactions and appropriate time, place and manner regulation remain regulable. His broader rule is not needed to invalidate this selective rack prohibition and does not replace the Court's Central Hudson/Fox analysis.

White, joined by Thomas, would uphold the ordinance's application. The dissent sees each removed rack as a real reduction of obstruction and visual clutter and allows the City to proceed incrementally. Metromedia permits differing treatment of commercial advertising, and Fox rejects a demand that government use the least restrictive means. In the dissent's view, the accepted distinction between commercial publications and newspapers permits the City to preserve the latter while addressing a portion of the physical problem. The dissent warns that the Court's use of the small affected fraction and alternative regulations undervalues local discretion. The Court answers that neither small scale nor incremental action alone is the defect; the unexplained content selection is.

#### Mandate, Remedy, and Stage

Supreme Court review is complete and the Sixth Circuit's judgment is affirmed. Declaratory and injunctive relief against this selective prohibition remains in place. The City may adopt otherwise lawful content-neutral location, size, number, safety and maintenance requirements. No particular new ordinance, enforcement action or later litigation is prescribed, and no mandate-issuance date is supplied.
