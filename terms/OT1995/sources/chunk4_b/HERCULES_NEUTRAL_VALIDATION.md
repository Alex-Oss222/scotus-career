## Corrected neutral posture and questions

The petition's printed p.i presents (1) whether the Boyle government-contractor tort defense precludes otherwise permissible Spearin contract claims, and (2) whether DPA-compelled production permits the Government to avoid contractual liability for damages incurred in third-party tort litigation. It introduces these questions through Judge Plager's dissent below. The merits brief restates a broader combined question; the United States states the question as whether these military supply contracts included an implied-in-fact reimbursement obligation. Preserve the actual source distinctions rather than calling any paraphrase the verbatim granted question.

The Federal Circuit's May 4, 1994 judgment, 24 F.3d 188, consolidated appeals from summary judgments dismissing separate Claims Court suits. The parties seek **affirmance**, or **reversal and remand for further proceedings and development of the contract claims**. Petitioners' merits conclusion p.42 and reply pp.20–21 request remand. The present record does not establish entitlement to a final money award, final causation findings, or the amount of recoverable damages.

The runtime's statement that the Federal Circuit simply rejected extension of the performance warranty to third-party costs omits its selected ground. The appellate court expressly recognized that a specifications warranty can reach problems arising after performance (App.17a), left several warranty/breach/damages questions unresolved, and selected **lack of causation because the government-contractor defense would have defeated tort liability** as its ground for rejecting settlement recovery (18a–23a). The Government's narrower account of the warranty's existence and scope is an alternative argument for affirmance, not a substitute description of the appellate holding.

## Material record, with statuses preserved

- App.3a–4a identifies the undisputed factual account used below: Hercules supplied under fifteen contracts between May 1964 and May 1968; Thompson, after declining to bid, was required under the DPA to supply under two contracts dated April 19, 1967 and May 24, 1968. Government specifications and conformity are described there; the two companies' knowledge and production histories differ. Hercules learned of dioxin risks in 1965 and changed its processing so its product had no measurable dioxin from 1966–1970. Thompson's lack of knowledge of hazards to users is separately described.
- Do not flatten these facts into all contracts being involuntary from inception. The petition says all supplied product was under rated orders; petitioners' merits p.4 says **largely** under rated orders. The Government accepts Thompson's initial unwillingness for present purposes, but says Hercules initially won competitive bids and disputes a later production order becoming a complete governmental takeover (U.S. merits p.20 n.14). There is a distinct alleged March 1967 order compelling increased/full-capacity production.
- The contracts contained no express governmental indemnity undertaking; the pleaded obligations are implied. The JA TOC calls the best available Thompson May 2, 1967 and Hercules March 1, 1968 contract copies **Lodged**. They are not reproduced in the printed JA. U.S. merits p.14 n.8 identifies contract copies in the Federal Circuit appendix at 464–481 and 573–618, and notes that the separately lodged discovery transcripts were petitioners' submission and had not been presented below. Do not claim the full contracts or transcripts were inspected here.
- The military prescribed a 50/50 blend of herbicidal components, and dioxin was an unwanted manufacturing byproduct, not a stated desired ingredient. Petitioners allege specifications forbade warnings and instructions, government control and superior knowledge, and in Thompson's case compelled higher-temperature processing. The Government contests that its specifications required dioxin or controlled proprietary manufacturing processes. The reply distinguishes eliminating measurable dioxin from the delivered product through extraction from avoiding its creation in manufacture. Do not resolve those scientific and factual disagreements by adopting either brief's assertion.
- Printed JA 5–20 is Hercules' complaint; JA 21–32 is Thompson's amended complaint. Their allegations, including detailed government misuse and knowledge allegations, are not adjudicated findings merely because printed in the record. JA 33 is the June 14, 1991 order staying discovery in Hercules pending decision on the dispositive motion. The United States says existing documents/testimony from extensive tort litigation were submitted despite no new Claims Court discovery (merits p.8 n.6). The claim that the lower courts had only bare pleadings is therefore inaccurate.
- JA 34–71 is the United States' August 31, 1984 opposition to summary judgment in the **opt-out** litigation. It disputes contractor compulsion, specification authorship, compliance, manufacturing control and relative knowledge. This supports the argument about uncertainty and the Government's positions, but is advocacy in a different procedural setting, not a judicial finding or an admission that all current warranty elements are satisfied. It postdates the class settlement. Both merits briefs distinguish the Government's nonparticipation in the settlement from its later opposition to opt-out summary judgment.
- The class settlement was reached May 7, 1984, on the eve of trial; total fund $180 million. Shares were approximately $18.773 million for Hercules and $3.097 million for Thompson, allocated by volume with adjustment for dioxin level (App.7a), not merely market share. Claims additionally include over $6 million and approximately $3 million respectively in defense expenses. Actual complaint prayers are broader: Hercules seeks settlements, defense costs, other expenses and consequential damages (JA19–20); Thompson also pleads business losses and litigation expenses (JA31–32). None is an adjudicated damages figure for this contract suit.
- The tort settlement's judicial approval as reasonable does not itself establish breach of an implied government contract, causation under it, or foreseeable contract damages. Conversely, the absence of a final tort judgment is not conceded to bar settlement recovery; that is the disputed rule. Courts in the tort litigation identified serious deficiencies in proof of injury causation; petitioners distinguish proof of an unsafe specification causing economic litigation loss from proof that a particular veteran was injured by exposure.

## Lower-court grounds and scope

**Specifications warranty and settlement.** App.17a–23a sets out warranty, breach, and causation as distinct elements. The court discussed doubts about a warranty encompassing settlement losses and scientific proof of injury but expressly rested on the contractor defense/causation ground. It treated the 1983, 1985 and 1987 lower-court defense decisions as demonstrating that litigation to conclusion would have defeated liability and viewed settlement as the manufacturers' voluntary choice. Petitioners and Plager challenge both certainty at the 1984 settlement date and the claimed logical equivalence of the two doctrines.

**Defense fees are separate.** App.23a–25a relied on Kania, construed this warranty as not including defense against meritless tort claims, and found no allegations that such a fee undertaking was directly contemplated at formation. Petitioners distinguish Kania's prosecutorial/jurisdictional setting and invoke third-party-litigation consequential-damages principles (merits p.23 n.8; reply pp.7,16 n.7). Even a successful defense can leave fees; settlement causation alone does not dispose of that fork.

**Superior knowledge.** App.15a–16a rejected extension beyond withheld information affecting costs or duration of contract performance, even assuming foreseeability and superior government knowledge. Plager (41a–43a) treats this as unwarranted temporal restriction of a common contract principle. The Claims Court expressly declined to decide relative knowledge as fact-intensive (56a). Do not report that issue as already found against petitioners.

**Reverse warranty/good faith.** Hercules pleaded purchaser-use obligations and good faith. App.25a–27a separately discusses reverse warranty; it doubts an actual agreement but again rests on causation and separately rejects fees. Claims Court 58a–59a rejected good-faith cost claims as performance-related. The Government says these separate claims were not renewed in the petition; current merits briefs chiefly present warranty, superior knowledge and DPA indemnity. Do not revive the broader original complaints' theories without a reachability determination.

**DPA indemnity.** App.28a–33a states that **Thompson, not Hercules**, pressed this claim below. It construes §§101/707 together as protecting against third-party contract breaches caused by required priority and rejects reimbursement for subsequent product tort liability. App.29a n.15 expressly leaves open the district court's suggestion of protection for increased risks caused by accelerated production; do not turn the lower holding into a universal denial of all DPA tort protection. The United States' merits pp.38–40 separately urges that even broad statutory immunity does not promise reimbursement.

**Authority/appropriations.** Claims Court 78a–80a and 100a–103a supplies an additional ground: actual authorized contracting power is required for express and implied-in-fact promises; no sufficient appropriation or statutory exemption was shown for the posited indemnity. It cites Merrill, Sutton, the Anti-Deficiency Act and California-Pacific Utilities. The Federal Circuit did not select this as its warranty disposition. It declined the trial court's separately asserted wholesale contractor-defense policy ground (15a), while using that defense within particular contract claims.

**Dissent.** Plager would remand for trial on unresolved facts/law (40a), objects to hindsight application of Boyle, and questions importing a state-law displacement doctrine into the tort litigation's federal/national-consensus setting. He connects superior knowledge and specifications warranties, construes the DPA's words broadly, and treats compulsion and control as strengthening contractual responsibility. His Takings Clause analogy (45a–46a) is not a preserved independent Supreme Court takings question or a takings holding.

## Preservation and entering law

The separate superior-knowledge theory presents a genuine scope dispute: petitioners' merits p.31 n.12 and reply p.10 n.4 say it is fairly included in the broad question and specification-warranty claim; U.S. merits p.17 n.11 says a genuinely distinct theory was not presented in the petition and is waived. The DPA theory for Hercules is likewise disputed: Government merits pp.8 n.7,34 relies on nonpresentation below; reply p.16 n.9 relies on factual DPA allegations in Hercules' complaint. Allegations of compulsion and a preserved legal claim of DPA indemnity are different propositions.

Use the actual entering-law Standard **Presented questions, subsidiary arguments and exceptional Supreme Court review**, whose present-operation components include Lebron (February 21, 1995) and X-Citement Video (November 29, 1994), with the distinct earlier Izumi, Caspari and Madsen components retained. It supplies a strong presumption against deciding independent unpresented questions, distinguishes subsidiary arguments, and allows a respondent to defend the same favorable judgment on a record-supported alternative without cross-appeal. This permits evaluating the Government's alternatives; it does not make contested facts established or compel their selection. A reference in the petition's body alone does not establish fair inclusion.

The Tucker Act's express/implied-in-fact versus implied-in-law boundary is undisputed in the core briefing; U.S. merits p.14 n.9 cites Hatzlachh. Petitioners' reply pp.11–12 invokes **Hatzlachh Supply Co. v. United States, 444 U.S. 460, 465–466 (1980)** against treating tort immunity itself as a bar to a genuine implied-in-fact contract. That counterauthority is material and absent from the runtime's brief list; a neutral packet should not suggest that Feres/Boyle automatically eliminate contractual liability. The dispute remains whether this obligation was agreed and authorized, what it covers, and whether breach caused recoverable loss.

## Competing supported paths for independent modeling

1. **Affirm for absent promised coverage.** Read Spearin as assuring the ability to perform supplied specifications and produce the required deliverable, rather than a promise to bear third-party tort-defense/settlement losses. Actual contractual intention and authority, Hatzlachh's tort/contract distinction, and the asserted third-party-damages analogies still require examination; tort immunity alone is not the entire reason. Separately reject a DPA indemnity because its shield language does not establish a reimbursement promise, without deciding the full breadth of statutory immunity.
2. **Affirm on the appellate causation and fees grounds.** Accept the claimed equivalence between proof of the warranty theory and a complete government-contractor defense, and distinguish voluntarily assumed settlement and unpromised defense costs. This must answer the actual 1984 uncertainty, later opt-out posture, potential-versus-actual liability principles, and the independent fee claim; simply citing Boyle is incomplete.
3. **Reverse/vacate and remand for the supported contract theory.** Treat warranty scope and foreseeable consequential damages under Spearin/contract principles as capable of encompassing the pleaded losses; reject categorical hindsight reliance on a tort defense as sufficient to negate causation; require factual development of agreement, specifications, breach, knowledge, foreseeability, potential liability, reasonableness, and authorized commitment as material to the selected theory. Petitioners seek this opportunity, not an immediate judicial finding that all those elements are met. This path can be limited to the warranty theory and to properly presented petitioners/components.
4. **Resolve DPA contract inference separately.** The strongest petitioners' formulation in reply pp.16–20 is an implied-in-fact promise inferred from the whole contractual setting, including statutory protection, coercion, control, superior knowledge and alleged contractor innocence. They expressly disclaim a private statutory cause of action under Cort v. Ash and disclaim treating §707's words alone as affirmative indemnity. The strongest government answer is that an immunity defense is not an authorized promise to pay, while a court-created obligation resting only on fairness is implied in law. A conclusion about §707's scope should not silently decide actual contracting authority, and vice versa.
5. **Alternative no-breach/authority affirmance only on an adequate record.** The Government expressly presses no proof of defective specifications/injury causation (merits pp.31–33) and lack of authorized open-ended indemnity (pp.39–40 n.23). Petitioners contest both, distinguish contract economic causation, invoke the judgment obligation under a valid Spearin warranty, and claim further facts are needed. A record-supported legal alternative can preserve affirmance, but disputed scientific or contract facts cannot be turned into new appellate findings. No general proposition that every implied warranty violates the Anti-Deficiency Act is supported.

These are supported choices for modeling, not commitments or a demand for a particular split. Narrow component dispositions remain possible subject to the preserved claim and complete remedy.
