pletion of performance under those contracts, peti- 
tioners were sued by third parties who claimed to 
have been injured by the government’s military use 
of Agent Orange. In that litigation, petitioners prop- 
erly raised the government contractor defense. The 
first district judge to consider the matter granted 
summary judgment for petitioners on the basis of 
that defense. That decision was withdrawn by a sec-

the defense in affirming a partial settlement of the 
litigation, in granting petitioners summary judgment

and in rejecting petitioners’ third-party claims 
against the United States for indemnity and con- 
tribution. This Court denied further review of two 
of those decisions.* Petitioners have now reformulated 
their claims for reimbursement in the language of 
implied warranty and contractual indemnity.

Spearin involved a contractor who agreed to build 
a drydock according to detailed plans provided by the 
Navy. The plans called for the contractor to relocate 
a sewer in a specific manner, which he did. A defect 
in the Navy’s plan of the existing sewer caused the 
relocation to be unsatisfactory, leading to additional 
costs and a dispute over completion of the contract. 
248 U.S. at 133-135. The contractor sued, and this 
Court upheld a judgment in his favor for costs and 
lost profits. Jd. at 188-139. Noting that the contrac- 
tor had no knowledge of the plan defect and that his 
independent investigation of the site was adequate 
under the circumstances (id. at 134-135, 187), the 
Court held that the government’s “insertion [into the 
contract specifications] of the articles prescribing the 
character, dimensions and location of the sewer im- 
ported a warranty that, if the specifications were 
complied with, the sewer would be adequate.” 

The facts of this case are remote from those of 
Spearin. Petitioners do not claim direct contract 
damages based on a contention that some defect in 
the government’s specifications rendered the manu- 
facture of Agent Orange more difficult or expensive 
than they expected, or caused the product, although 
properly manufactured, to be unfit for its intended 
use. Rather, they claim remote and consequential 
damages arising from the settlement of unproven 
third-party tort claims related to the government’s 
use of the Agent Orange produced. The Claims Court 
recognized that distinction when it explained (Pet. 
App. 65a-66a, 98a-99a; emphasis added) that even if 
the facts might present a triable issue of fact con- 
cerning the existence of an implied warranty under
