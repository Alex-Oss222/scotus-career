INTRODUCTION 


This petition arises out of Matsushita Electrical 
Co. Ltd. and its subsidiaries’ ("Matsushita") $6.1 billion 
dollar acquisition of the common stock of MCA, Inc. 
("MCA"). The class complaint charged that Matsushita's 
public tender offer violated federal securities laws because 
of (a) a special stock swap arrangement that was provided 
to Lew Wasserman, MCA’s chairman and chief executive 
officer, and (b), a $21,000,000 bonus paid to Sidney 
Sheinberg, its chief operating officer and owner of 
1,170,000 shares (ER 100:3).' These arrangements were 
alleged to have violated two SEC rules that enforce section 
14(d)(7) of the Exchange Act: rule 14d-10, the so-called 
“all-holders, best-price" requirement, because “during [a] 
tender offer" Matsushita provided higher consideration to 
Wasserman and Sheinberg, and "in a tender offer" also 
offered consideration not open to all stockholders; and rule 
10b-13, because they resulted in Matsushita’s illegal 
purchase of shares during the tender offer period (ER 
100:2, 17-18). Expressly designed to reject Delaware 
corporation law, rule 144-10 prohibits premium payments 
("App.") 36a n. 31.

REASONS FOR DENYING THE WRIT 


A. WASSERMAN TRANSACTION 


Petitioners nowhere deny that rule 14d-10, the 
"all-holders, best-price rule," requires shareholder equality 
"during [a] tender offer." The court below held that, on 
the undisputed facts, the Wasserman stock purchase 
transaction occurred during such an offer. Petitioners 
assert that this holding conflicts with the Fourth Circuit’s 
decision in Kahn*, which the court below had carefully 
considered and harmonized. App. 19a. The alleged 
"conflict" is non-existent, and the claim is designed to 
mask what the petition really invites: this court’s review 
of the application of settled legal principles to a fact- 
intensive, complex, and unique set of events. Rare indeed 


* Kahn v. Virginia Retirement System, 13 F.3d 110 (4th Cir. 
1993), cert. denied, 114 S.Ct. 1834 (1994). Petitioner’s appendix 
includes documents related to the Kahn transaction. These documents 
were not before any court below. We will, however, not now clutter 
the record with a motion to strike. Suffice it to say that those 
documents are not properly part of the record here. Stern, supra, 
§12.4. 


Even as amplified by these materials entirely outside the 
record, however, there is no conflict: the “private” purchase in Kahn 
did not tie the price to changes in the tender offer price or give the 
“private seller” the right to prohibit payment to others until the private 
purchaser was paid. Finally, even if the court below misunderstood 
the facts of the Kahn transaction (rather than the legal rule) that would 
raise no issue of general importance. Certiorari surely is not 
appropriate to review not the legal rule but two facts-specific takeover 
arrangements. 


7 


are the occasions in which certiorari has issued for such 
purposes. Stern, supra, at §4.14. Since no issue of 
general public importance -- none whatever -- is raised, 
certiorari is inappropriate. Layne & Bowler Corp. v. 
Western Wells Works, 261 U.S. 387, 393 (1923); Stern, 
§4.11. 


Petitioners’ attempt to inflate the court’s ruling on 
what petitioners themselves acknowledge to be a unique 
transaction into a fundamental threat to the integrity of the 
tender offer mechanism is preposterous. Contrary to the 
claim that the court’s decision created uncertainty, it 
removed it. The opinion makes clear what is included 
"during [a] tender offer" and what is not. App. 18a-19a. 
In so doing, the court did no more than apply the doctrine 
set out in Judge Winter’s careful opinion in Field v. 
Trump, 850 F.2d 938, 944 (2d Cir. 1988), cert. denied, 
489 U.S. 1012 (1989) (App. 17a-18a), a decision not even 
mentioned in the petition. Petitioners cite not a single 
tender offer that would have been or will be threatened by 
this judgment. 


The court’s decision was plainly correct on the 
merits. The structure of the Matsushita~MCA transaction 
was a transparent violation of rule 14d-10(c). The rule 
requires that where a tender offerer employs more than 
one kind of consideration, for example, cash and 
securities, each shareholder must be allowed the same right 
to choose among the options offered. That right, in turn, 
protects the right to receive equal value for one’s shares, 


8 


and it avoids complex valuation arguments as to whether a 
particular amount of cash is worth more or less than some 
new security. Thus, Matsushita could not legally offer 
Wasserman preferred shares’ and other shareholders cash. 
To avoid the rule’s prohibition, Matsushita executed an 
agreement with Wasserman a few minutes before the 
signing of the public tender offer documents, agreeing to 
provide the preferred stock in exchange for MCA shares, 
the side deal to close a few moments after the public had 
surrendered its shares of MCA, and only if the tender 
offer attracted sufficient shares. The deal was wholly 
conditional on the tender offer and its documents: Any 
delay in the tender offer automatically extended the time 
for closing the Wasserman deal. Any breach of condition 
cancelling the tender offer, cancelled the Wasserman offer. 
If there were any increases in price -- if the hoped-for (by 
Wasserman) bidding war broke out for MCA (as had 
happened with Time Warner), and Matsushita raised its 
price, Wasserman got the benefit. Indeed, Matsushita 
promised Wasserman it would not pay cash for the shares 
it had “accepted for payment" from the shareholders, 
unless and until the Wasserman deal was carried out. And
