PRELIMINARY STATEMENT 


Respondents’ brief is an extended detour around the ques- 
tion before the Court: “whether a federal court can withhold 
full faith and credit from a state court final judgment approv- 
ing a class action settlement simply because the settlement 
released federal claims.” Pet. for Cert. at i; see also Order 
granting certiorari. Unable to adduce any authority or policy 
supporting the Ninth Circuit’s analysis, respondents advance 
“issues” regarding the severability of releases from judg- 
ments, the Supremacy Clause, the adequacy of class represen- 
tation and due process, all of which respondents concede were 
not addressed by the Ninth Circuit and none of which is en- 


compassed bv the question accepted by the Court. 


2 


Respondents ask this Court to approve an arbitrary limita- 
tion on the established authority of a state court to enter a 
judgment approving a settlement of matters properly before 
the court, asserting that state courts are not competent to enter 
a judgment approving a class action settlement when the set- 
tlement includes a release of exclusively federal claims. Re- 
spondents concede, however, that a state court may approve 
the release of exclusively federal claims in an individual or 
derivative settlement. See Resp. Br. at 24 n.26. There is no 
principled basis for a different rule in the class action context, 
and any such distinction would undermine the policy of pro- 
moting the finality of class action settlements. 


The linchpin of respondents’ newly fashioned argument is 
that the Delaware judgment approving the class action settle- 
ment did not incorporate a release of claims. If the judgment 
did not incorporate a release, respondents argue, then the 
Ninth Circuit did not offend the Full Faith and Credit Act 
when it disregarded the state judgment. But respondents’ as- 
sertion is demonstrably untrue. The judgment expressly re- 
leased all claims arising out of Matsushita’s tender offer for 
MCA, “including without limitation the claims asserted in the 
California Federal Actions.” See J.A. 206. 


The Full Faith and Credit Act presumptively applies to all 
judgments, and implied repeals of § 1738 -- which are disfa- 
vored and rare -- are not i absent a “manifest 
incompatibility” between § 1738 and the federal provision at 
issue. Kremer v. Chemical Constr. Corp., 456 U.S. 461, 470 
(1982). The argument that exclusive federal jurisdiction to 
adjudicate certain securities law issues is incompatible with 
granting full faith and credit to a state judgment entered on a 
consensual settlement controverts every decision of this Court 
construing the interaction of the Full Faith and Credit Act and 
subsequently enacted federal iegislation. See, e.g., Marrese v. 
American Academy of Orthopaedic Surgeons, 470 U.S. 373, 
381 (1985); Migra v. Warren City Sch. Dist. Bd. of Educ., 465 
U.S. 75, 84 (1984); Allen v. McCurry, 449 U.S. 90, 105 
(1980); see also Murphy v. Gallagher, 761 F.2d 878, 885 (2d 


3 


Cir. 1985) (Exchange Act's “grant of exclusive jurisdiction 
. .. does not constitute a repeal [of §1738] by implication”). 


Respondents’ assertion that the Supremacy Clause some- 
how “preempts” state courts from approving the release of 
“exclusively federal” claims in class action settlements is il- 
logical. No coherent Supremacy Clause theory could preclude 
recognition of a judgment releasing securities law claims in 
class actions but allow such a release in individual or deriva- 
tive actions. Thus, respondents’ Supremacy Clause argument 
collapses of its own weight because respondents acknowledge 
(as did the Ninth Circuit) that state courts have the power to 
approve the settlement of exclusively federal claims in an in- 
dividual or derivative action. See Resp. Br. at 20; Pet App. 


 37a-38a & n.32. 


Finally, respondents challenge the Delaware judgment on 
the ground that inadequate representation of the class in the 
Delaware proceedings purportedly deprived the class of due 
process. But respondents concede the Ninth Circuit decision 
is not predicated on any due process issue. See Resp. Br. at 8. 
Moreover, adequacy of representation was an issue actually 
litigated and resolved in the Delaware courts, which found the 
representation of the class to be adequate. The Ninth Circuit 
properly did not revisit that finding. The class members here 
received notice and an opportunity to object or opt out. All 
but 19 of them in effect “opted in” by not objecting and by 
accepting the consideration paid in the settlement. 

Respondents should not be permitted to sit out state court 
proceedings affecting the rights of the class and then sponsor 
in federal court an argument that could have been (and actu- 
ally was) made in state court. Indeed, respondents’ collateral 
attack on the competency and integrity of the Delaware courts 
only underscores the importance of § 1738 to the preservation 
of state-federal comity.

C. Adequacy of Representation Is a Matter of 
Judicial Determination, Reinforced by the 


Right to Opt Out 


Class actions are a mechanism for a single, binding adjudi- 
cation of multiple claimants’ rights, while assuring due proc- 
ess to absent class members and repose to defendants. See, 
e.g., Califano v. Yamasaki, 442 U.S. 682, 701 (1979) (“the 
class-action device saves .. . resources . . . by permitting an 
issue potentially affecting every [class member] to be litigated 
in an economical fashion under Rule 23”); American Pipe & 
Constr. Co. v. Utah, 414 U.S. 538, 559-51 (1974) (“the prin- 
cipal function of a class suit” is to avoid multiple suits by 
binding absentees). In a class action settlement, a court 
authorizes class representatives to enter into a settlement on 
behalf of the class, and the representatives thereafter submit 
the settlement to the court for approval at a fairness hearing. 
See, e.g., Fed. R. Civ. P. 23(e); In re Beef Indus. Antitrust 
Litig., 607 F.2d 167, 173-80 (Sth Cir. 1979), cert. denied, 452 
U.S. 905 (1981). Due process mandates that class members 
receive notice describing the effect of the settlement, be af- 
forded an opportunity to object (e.g., to the adequacy of the 
settlement and representation) and, in appropriate circum- 
stances, be afforded the opportunity to opt out. If the court 
finds that requisite proceduial protections have been met and 
the settlement is fair, the class members who do not opt out 
are bound. See, e.g., Shutts, 472 U.S. at 808; General Tel., 
457 U.S. at 160-61; American Pipe & Constr., 414 U.S. at 
550.

the claims to be released and the court authorized the repre- 
sentatives to represent the class in connection with that pro- 
posed settlement. J.A. 187-88, 198. The class members were 
informed that the settlement released all claims arising out of 
the Matsushita tender offer, specifically including the claims
