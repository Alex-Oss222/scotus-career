COMPLAINT 


Plaintiff Hercules Incorporated, for its complaint against 
defendant the United States of America, alleges upon 
knowledge with respect to its own acts and upon infor- 
mation and belief as to all other matters as follows: 


NATURE OF THE CLAIMS 


1. Hercules Incorporated (“Hercules”) seeks contrac- 
tual indemnification against defendant United States of 
America (“USA”) for damages arising from contracts be- 
tween defendant and Hercules. Hercules seeks to recover 
from the USA increased expenses of performance and 
damages Hercules has sustained and costs, including at- 
torneys fees, it has incurred in defending and settling cases 
(the “Agent Orange cases”) brought by certain plaintiffs 
in connection with claimed exposure to phenoxy herbi- 
cides, including Agent Orange ( collectively referred to 
hereinafter as “Agent Orange”), in Southeast Asia or in 
connection with the conflict in Southeast Asia ( collectively 
referred to hereinafter as “Southeast Asia”). 


JURISDICTION 


2. Jurisdiction of the Court is invoked pursuant to the 
Tucker Act, 28 U.S.C. § 1491 (1970), as amended. 


6 


PARTIES 


3. Hercules is a corporation organized under the laws 
of the State of Delaware and has its principal place of 
business in Delaware. Hercules is the sole owner of the 
claims asserted herein and is entitled to recover the 
amounts claimed from the defendant. 


4. The USA is a sovereign state which has consented 
to be used under the Tucker Act. 


GENERAL FACTUAL ALLEGATIONS 


A. The USA’s Formulation of Phenoxy Herbicides and 
Development of An Agent Orange Specification 


5. As early as World War II, the USA undertook 
defoliant research with phenoxy herbicides, including 
2,4,5-trichlorophenoxyacetic acid (“2,4,5-T”’) and 2,4- 
dichlorophenoxyacetic acid (“2,4-D”). 


6. After World War II, both 2,4-D and 2,4,5-T were 
introduced for commercial use as herbicides. Hercules 
began to produce such products commercially in 1961 
after it purchased Reasor-Hill, a herbicide manufacturer. 


7. During the early 1960's, the USA also conducted 
experiments with many herbicides, including 2,4,5-T and 
2,4-D, to formulate herbicides specifically for military 
use in Southeast Asia. Hercules did not participate in 
this or the earlier experimentation or formulation. 


8. Based on its experimentation, the USA developed 
the Agent Orange formulation for militarily use in 
Southeast Asia. Agent Orange, as formulated by the 
USA, consisted of a mixture of fifty percent 2,4-D and 
ffity percent 2,4,5-T butyl esters.

B. Hercules’ Commercial Sale of Phenoxy Herbicides 
With Federally Sanctioned Warnings and _Instruc- 
tions for Use 


9. The 2,4-D and 2,4,5-T products sold commercially 
by Hercules in the 1960’s carried an identification of 
their active ingredients as well as instructions for use 
which called for heavy dilution of the product. For 
example, the instructions for use of Hercules’ Butyl Ester 
B-2D-2T herbicide, which contained approximately equal 
amounts of 2,4-D and 2,4,5-T, called for application 
using one gallon of the herbicide mixed with 99 gallons 
of water. 


10. Hercules’ herbicides also contained extensive 
warnings, which alerted the user, among other things, 
as follows: 


(a) “Minute quantities may cause severe injury by 
drift or vaporization. Avoid drift of spray and use a 
coarse spray which is less likely to drift.” 


(b) “DO NOT contaminate any body of water by 
direct application, cleaning of equipment, or disposal of 
wastes and containers. Toxic to fish.” 


(c) “DO NOT graze dairy animals on treated areas 
within six weeks after application.” 


(d) “DO NOT use around the home, recreation areas, 
or similar sites.” 


(e) “DO NOT contaminate feed or foodstuffs.” 


(f) “DO NOT use spray equipment contaminated 
with this product for any other purpose.” 


(g) “DO NOT reuse empty containers. Destroy by 
burying in a safe place away from water supplies.” 


(h) “CAUTION—This product may cause skin irrita- 
tion. Avoid inhaling spray mist. Harmful if swallowed. 
Avoid contact with the eyes, skin, or clothing. Flush 
eyes with clear water and get prompt medical attention.” 


8 


11. The warnings and instructions for use provided 
on Hercules’ herbicide products were supplied in com- 
pliance with federal regulation, including the version of 
the Federal Insecticide, Fungicide and Rodenticide Act 
(“FIFRA”) in effect at that time. Pursuant to FIFRA, 
Hercules’ phenoxy herbicide products had been registered 
with the U.S. Department of Agriculture (“USDA”) and 
the warnings and instructions for use provided on such 
products had been cleared through the USDA. 


12. Hercules sold 2,4,-D products commercially for 
the removal of broad leaf weeds, such as dandelions. 
Hercules sold 2,4,5-T products to remove woody plants, 
such as chickweed and poison ivy. Hercules also sold 
herbicidal products which contained a mixture of 2,4-D 
and 2,4,5-T. 

13. Hercules sold most of its 2,4-D and 2,4,5-T prod- 
ucts at virtually pure concentrations to commercial ap- 
plicators who, in accordance with Hercules’ instructions 
for use, substantially diluted the herbicides. Hercules 
sold a smaller amount of 2,4-D and 2,4,5-T products di- 
rectly to the public with warnings and instructions sub- 
stantially to dilute the concentrations for proper use. 


14. Hercules also sold versions of the same commer- 
cial 2,4-D and 2,4,5-T products to the U.S.A. for peace- 
time use. These commercial herbicide products, like 
those sold to the private sector, carried identification of 
active ingredients, warnings and instructions for use. 


15. The intended and actual commercial use of the 
2,4-D and 2,4,5-T products made and commercially sold 
by Hercules was thus at very low concentrations and 
rates of application and for standard selective weed re- 


moval purposes.

D. The USA’s Unprecedented Military Use of Agent 
Orange in Southeast Asia and the Potential Health 
Risks Attendant With Such Use 


21. The Agent Orange provided by Hercules to the 
USA was used by the USA and those acting in concert 
with it in the conflict in Southeast Asia that was going 
on at the time. 


22. At the time the USA developed its specifications 
for Agent Orange and purchased Agent Orange from 
Hercules made pursuant to those specifications, the USA 
knew or had reason to know that the Agent Orange 
posed possible health hazards if utilized in the unprece- 
dented ways it was to be used, and was used, in South- 
east Asia. 


23. The USA was aware, or had reason to be aware, 
that its intended and actual use of Agent Orange in 
Southeast Asia involved an unprecedented use in unpre- 
cedented quantities, concentrations and rates of applica- 
tion. This wartime use, which was entirely different from 
the previous and contemporaneous peacetime commercial 
use of 2,4-D and 2,4,5-T, included use in battlefield sit- 
uations in undiluted form and at overkill rates, wholesale 
dumping to escape enemy fire and application of Agent

Orange to areas well beyond those intended to be sprayed. 
Even the containers in which Agent Orange was pack- 
aged were misused; empty containers were used as 
shower stalls, barbecues and water storage receptacles. 


24. In connection with the above unprecedented mili- 
tary use of Agent Orange by the USA in Southeast Asia, 
the USA failed to provide identification of active ingre- 
dients as well as warnings and instructions adequate for 
use. 


25. Even before the USA purchased Agent Orange 
from Hercules, the USA was aware that the unprece- 
dented use of Agent Orange in Southeast Asia posed 
possible health hazards. For example, a report prepared 
in 1963 by the Institute for Defense Analysis for the 
Department of Defense concluded that phenoxy herbi- 
cides were safe when used commercially, but could be 
hazardous in military use because higher concentrations 
would be applied by less experienced personnel under 
the pressures that are inherent in battle field use. 


26. The unprecedented intended and actual use of 
Agent Orange by the USA in Southeast Asia as a mili- 
tary agent, rather than as the agricultural agent commer- 
cially marketed by Hercules, and the potential safety 
hazards attendant with such unprecedented military use, 
were not known or reasonably foreseeable to Hercules. 


E. Hercules’ Voluntary Removal of Dioxin From Its 
Agent Orange 


27. In February 1965, Hercules’ representatives were 
invited to attend a session with other herbicide manu- 
facturers. At that session, held in March 1965, Hercules’ 
representatives were advised for the first time that dioxin 
was a by-product of 2,4,5-T production and that dioxin 
could be highly toxic. 


28. Hercules immediately thereafter embarked upon 
a program to reduce the level of dioxin contained in its 


12 


Agent Orange and 2,4,5-T products. Toward that end, 
Hercules implemented a toluene extraction process which 
enabled Hercules virtually to eliminate the dioxin in its 
products. Beginning in or about January 1966, the level 
of dioxin contained in Agent Orange sold by Hercules to 
the USA was reduced to the point of being undetectable. 


29. At the time the USA developed its specification 
for Agent Orange and during the time the USA pur- 
chased Agent Orange from Hercules, the USA knew that 
dioxin was a by-product of 2,4,5-T production. 


30. In using Agent Orange in Southeast Asia, the 
USA mixed the Agent Orange supplied by Hercules with 
that supplied by other manufacturers. 


F. The USA’s Cessation of Use of Agent Orange 


31. In 1969 the USA ended its use of Agent Orange 
in Southeast Asia and its purchase of Agent Orange from 
Hercules. This cessation of use followed public disclosure 
of the findings in the Bionetics Report sponsored by the 
National Institute of Health concerning possible terato- 
genic effects related to 2,4,5-T. 


G. The Agent Orange Cases 


32. Beginning in 1979, lawsuits were commenced 
against Hercules and other manufacturers of Agent 
Orange. These lawsuits were consolidated in the United 
States District Court for the Eastern District of New York 
(the “Agent Orange Litigation” ). 


33. The individual plaintiffs in the Agent Orange 
Litigation were (1) individuals who were or had been 
members of the U.S. military and allied military, or em- 
ployees of civilian agencies, who had been exposed, by 
reason of the USA’s actions, to Agent Orange in South- 
east Asia, or (2) the spouses or children, born and un- 
born, of individuals who had been exposed to Agent 
Orange. The plaintiffs alleged, among other things, that 


13 


the defendants negligently had manufactured and sold 
Agent Orange and that the USA negligently had used 
Agent Orange. They also asserted claims based on 
theories or strict liability, breach of warranty and inten- 
tional tort and nuisance. 


H. The USA’s Extensive Involvement In the Agent 
Orange Litigation 


34. The USA initially was named as a defendant in 
the Agent Orange Litigation. Thereafter, the USA was 
named as a third party defendant by Hercules and by 
other defendants in the Agent Orange Litigation. 


35. The USA participated extensively and fully in the 
Agent Orange Litigation, including in the discovery stage 
of the Litigation. 


I. The Settlement of the Agent Orange Litigation and 
Consequent Damage to Hercules 


36. Notwithstanding Hercules’ denial of responsibility 
for personal injuries allegedly caused by exposure to 
Agent Orange, and because of the expensive, uncertain 
and time-consuming nature of litigation and the potential 
liability alleged, Hercules settled the Agent Orange Liti- 
gation. The USA failed and refused to participate in or 
contribute in any way toward the settlement. In October 
1984, pursuant to a settlement agreement, Hercules 
caused $18,772,568 to be paid into a special settlement 
fund for distribution to various plaintiffs in the Agent 
Orange litigation. 

37. The cost to Hercules of the Agent Orange Litiga- 
tion was not limited to the settlement amount paid to the 
plaintiffs. In addition, Hercules incurred substantial costs, 
including legal expenses, in connection with and as a 
result of the Agent Orange Litigation. To date, these 
expenses exceed $6 million. 


14 


J. Other Related I awsuits 


38. Some plaintiffs in the Agent Orange Litigation did 
not agree to the settlement and instead proceeded against 
Hercules and others. Other Agent Orange cases have also 
been brought against Hercules. By reason of these ac- 
tions, some of which have been concluded, Hercules has 
incurred, and can be expected to incur, additional dam- 
ages, costs and expenses, including attorneys fees. 


39. Hercules may incur additional damages as the re- 
sult of pending and anticipated Agent Orange cases and 
Hercules will supplement this complaint, if necessary or 
appropriate, to assert these additional damages and 
grounds for relief. 


40. No action is now pending on Hercules’ claim in 
Congress, in any department of the defendant or in any 
judicial proceeding other than as set forth herein. 


FIRST CLAIM FOR RELIEF 


(Breach of Warranty of Adequate 
Specifications and Requirements ) 


41. Hercules repeats and realleges each and every 
allegation set forth in paragraphs | through 40 as if set 
forth at length herein. 


42. The USA promulgated and enforced mandatory 
contract specifications and requirements for Agent Orange, 
including packaging and labelling specifications and re- 
quirements which failed to provide for, and precluded the 
provision of, identification of active ingredients, warnings 
and instructions for safe use on the Agent Orange sold to 
the USA. Defendant warranted the adequacy and suit- 
ability of said specifications and requirements for defend- 
ant’s intended and actual military use, including that 
Agent Orange could be sold by Hercules for safe use in 
Southeast Asia without the need for appropriate identifi- 
cation of active ingredients, warnings and instructions for 
safe use. 


15 


43. Defendant breached this contractual warranty to 
Hercules by failing to establish specifications and require- 
ments for Agent Orange sufficient to make Agent Orange 
adequate and suitable for the unprecedented intended and 
actual military use to which it was put in Southeast Asia, 
including by failing to establish packaging and labelling 
specifications and requirements which would have per- 
mitted Hercules to set forth in the packaging and/or 
labelling of its Agent Orange sold to the USA an identifi- 
cation of active ingredients, warnings and instructions 
sufficient to make Agent Orange adequate, suitable and 
safe for its unprecedented military use in Southeast Asia. 


44. By breaching its warranty to Hercules, defendant 
caused Hercules to sustain increased expenses of perform- 
ance and incur substantial monetary damages in connec- 
tion with the Agent Orange Litigation and other Agent 
Orange cases in which claimants have alleged personal 
injury from exposure to Agent Orange made, packaged 
and sold by Hercules and others pursuant to defendant's 
specifications and requirements. Hercules has expended 
considerable sums in defending and settling such cases 
and is entitled to contractual indemnification from defend- 
ant therefor. 


SECOND CLAIM FOR RELIEF 


(Breach of Duty to Disclose Knowledge of 
Unprecedented Military Use and Potential 
Health Risks Attendant With Such Use) 


45. Hercules repeats and realleges each and every 
allegation set forth in paragraphs | through 40 as if set 
forth at length herein. 


46. Defendant established mandatory contract specifi- 
cations and requirements for Agent Orange manufactured 
for and sold to defendant, including packaging and label- 
ling specifications and requirements which precluded 
Hercules from placing identification of active ingredients, 


16 


warnings and instructions for safe use on the Agent 
Orange manufactured for and sold to defendant. 


47. At and prior to the time of contracting with 
Hercules for the manufacture, purchase and sale of Agent 
Orange, defendant had actual knowledge that Agent 
Orange, when used in Southeast Asia in the unprecedented 
military manner intended and actually used by the USA, 
and absent adequate identification of active ingredients, 
warnings and instructions for use, might endanger the 
health and safety of those exposed to Agent Orange. 


48. At all relevant times, defendant had greater 
knowledge than Hercules concerning defendant's intended 
and actual unprecedented military use of Agent Orange 
in Southeast Asia and the possible health risks attendant 
with such use. 


49. Defendant’s actual and intended unprecedented 
military use of Agent Orange in Southeast Asia, and the 
possible health risks attendant therewith, were not known 
or reasonably foreseeable to Hercules. The USA knew 
or should have known that fact and that Hercules had no 
reason to obtain such knowledge. 


50. Defendant had a contractual duty to reveal to 
Hercules its superior knowledge of the unprecedented mili- 
tary use of Agent Orange by the USA in Southeast Asia 
and the possible dangers attendant with such use. The 
USA's specification for Agent Orange misled or failed to 
put Hercules on notice concerning such unprecedented 
military use and possible dangers. 


51. Defendant breached its contractual duty by with- 
holding from Hercules defendant’s superior knowledge 
concerning the unprecedented military use of Agent 
Orange by the USA in Southeast Asia, and the possible 
health risks attendant with such use, while at the same 
time inducing and mandating Hercules to enter into con- 
tracts for the manufacture and sale of Agent Orange. 


17 


52. By breaching its contractual duty to disclose its 
superior knowledge to Hercules, defendant caused Her- 
cules to sustain increased expenses of performance and 
incur substantial monetary damages in connection with 
the Agent Orange cases, and Hercules was and is entitled 
to contractual indemnification therefor. 


THIRD CLAIM FOR RELIEF 


(Breach of Duty to Exercise Due 
Care in Using Hercules’ Agent Orange ) 


53. Hercules repeats and realleges each and every 
allegation set forth in paragraphs | through 40 as if set 
forth at length herein. 


54. The USA was aware that Agent Orange posed 
possible health risks if used militarily in the unprecedented 
manner it was used in Southeast Asia, including use with- 
out adequate precautions, safeguards, identification of ac- 
tive ingredients, warnings and instructions for safe use. 
Despite such knowledge, defendant did not use the Agent 
Orange it purchased from Hercules in Southeast Asia in 
a manner reasonably calculated to avoid such potential 
health risks and failed to take adequate precautions and 
safeguards or provide identification of active ingredients 
and adequate warnings and instructions concerning the 
proper use of and exposure to Agent Orange. 


55. By reason of the foregoing, and by reason of 
defendant's preclusion in its Agent Orange specifications 
of identification of active ingredients, warnings or instruc- 
tions for safe use, defendant had a contractual duty to 
Hercules to use Agent Orange in Southeast Asia in a 
manner designed to avoid potential health risks, including 
by taking adequate precautions and safeguards and pro- 
viding identification of active ingredients and adequate

UNITED STATES’ MEMORANDUM IN OPPOSITION TO 
DEFENDANTS’ MOTION FOR SUMMARY JUDGMENT 
BASED ON THE GOVERNMENT CONTRACT DEFENSE 


I. INTRODUCTION 


For over five years, the defendants have unabashedly 
promoted a compelling account of herbicide manufac- 
turers “acting under compulsion of federal law in pa- 


35 


triotic furtherance of the Vietnam War effort,” supply- 
ing a necessary, effective and unique military weapon 
that met, in every respect, the Government’s detailed 
specifications and expectations. 506 F. Supp. 737, 795 
(E.D. N.Y. 1980). Drawing upon such themes as “com- 
pulsion to manufacture”, “a product invented by the gov- 
ernment”, “absence of defendants’ negligence”, absence 
of any defect in their product”, “defendants’ compliance 
with the terms of the contracts and the governments’ 
specifications”, and “parity of knowledge apropos any 
health hazards associated with the use of their product,” 
defendants now declare that they are entitled to sum- 
mary judgment because they have satisfied all elements 
of the government contract defense. 


Judge Pratt’s rulings provided a powerful incentive for 
defendants to identify only that evidence which might 
support their version of the relevant events—a complete 
defense to all claims asserted against them. 534 F. Supp. 
1046, 1055 (E.D. N.Y. 1982). They have provided 
this one-sided story up until now without meeting sig- 
nificant opposition. Indeed, defendants have done all 
they could to prevent such opposition. 


Only days before defendants’ answers to the United 
States’ first discovery requests in the history of this 
litigation were due, the defendants moved for and se- 
cured, ex parte, a stay of all discovery. As defendants 
were well aware, the information sought by this discovery 
directly related to the factual issues which lie at the 
heart of the government contract defense, and which 
have not yet been presented to this Court. They also 
knew that, had defendants honored their discovery obli- 
gations, they ran the risk of revealing facts which would 
significantly undercut their motion for summary judg- 
ment. 

Thus believing themselves secure from the threat of a 


factual assault upon their singular version of the defolia- 
tion program, defendants wasted no time in moving this 


36 


Court for a declaration that there are no genuine issues 
of material fact relating to the government contract 
defense, and that they were entitled to summary judg- 
ment as a matter of law. 


The United States contends that there exists substan- 
tial and convincing evidence that establishes not only 
that there are indeed genuine issues of material fact 
directly bearing upon the validity of each and every 
contention relating to the government contract defense, 
but also that the defense does not further the policies it 
purports to serve, particularly in light of the facts pre- 
sented here. 


Accordingly, defendants’ motion for summary judg- 
ment should be denied, insofar as it is premised on the 
government contract defense. 


Il. THE POLICY CONSIDERATIONS UNDER- 
LYING THE GOVERNMENT CONTRACT 
DEFENSE ARE INAPPLICABLE TO THE 
FACTS AT BAR 


A. By Virtue Of Pre-Trial Order 91, The Ra- 
tionale For The Application Of The Gov- 
ernment Contract Defense No Longer Exists. 


In 1980, the defendants moved for summary judgment 
based upon what has been termed the “government con- 
tract defense.” They argued that liability may not be 
imposed upon them for injuries arising out of the United 
States’ use of Agent Orange, because (1) they merely 
manufactured and supplied Agent Orange to the gov- 
ernment pursuant to contracts; (2) Agent Orange was 
not manufactured before and has not been manufactured 
since; (3) they completed their compelled manufacture 
of Agent Orange in strict compliance with the specifica- 
tions supplied by the government, specifications that con- 
tained no obvious defects which would have alerted them 
to any danger in following them; and (4) they manu- 
factured Agent Orange without any negligence cn their 
part. 506 F. Supp. 762, 795 (1980). 


37 


By Order and Memorandum of the Court dated De- 
cember 29, 1980 the Court granted the United States’ 
Motion to Dismiss the third-party complaints in accord- 
ance with the rules of Feres v. United States, 340 U.S. 
135 (1950), and Stencel Aero Engineering Corp. v. 
United States, 431 U.S. 666 (1977). The Court also 
recognized that “a government contract defense exists 
and has possible application to the facts at bar.” 506 
F. Supp. at 796. 


The Court observed that considerations of fairness and 
public policy underlie the government contract defense. 
First, the Court found that tort liability principles seek 
to impose liability on the wrongdoer who caused the 
injury, not “the otherwise innocent contractor whose only 
role * * * was the proper performance of a plan supplied

CONTRACTORS FOR DEFECTIVE OR NEG-

SERVE THE IMPORTANT POLICIES UN-

A. Liability For Government Manufacturers 
Promotes Safe Products, Pools The Risk Of 
Hazardous Products, And Relieves The In- 
nocent Victims Of Heavy Unanticipated Bur- 
dens 


Judge Pratt correctly observed that tort liability seeks 
to impose the burdens of the injury on the responsible 
party. 506 F. Supp. at 793. He also correctly noted 
that deterrence and safety are important underlying poli- 
cies of tort law, and that these policies would not be 
served by imposing liability upon a government con- 
tractor who has no control whatsoever over the develop- 
ment, design, manufacture, and use of a product. /d. 
However, where a government contractor dominates the 
development of the product and the design of its specifi- 
cations or has significant discretion in manufacturing the 
product—all which apply here—the policies in support 
of imposing tort liability, including strict liability, remain 
in full force. 


When a manufacturer designs and develops a com- 
mercial product, it usually has the expertise to make the 
product either more or less safe. Greenman v. Yuba 
Power Co., 27 Cal. Rptr. 697 (1962); Wachtel v. Rosol 
Construction Co., 34 Conn. Supp. 204, 207 (1978). The 
herbicides sold to the government were marketed com- 
mercially before and during sale to the government. In 
producing a product that allegedly met government speci- 
fications, defendants enjoyed absolute discretion over the 


Se eee ee. eC ee Pare Ie 


55 


manufacturing process. Defendants concede that it was 
the manufacturing process alone that caused the presence 
of dioxin in the product furnished to the government. 
Negligence and strict liability provide incentives to choose 
practices or policies which enhance safety. Applying the 
government contract defense, under the circumstances pre- 
sented here, eliminates important deterrence to unsafe, 
indifferent, or even reckless conduct on the part of gov- 
ernment contractors. 


Another justification for imposing liability on manu- 
facturers is that they can best pool the risk of harm 
resulting from their products. The social costs of the 
product will be spread among the producers and con- 
sumers of the product. See, e.g., Greenman v. Yuba 
Power Products, 27 Cal, Rptr. at 697. The manufactur- 
ers are in the best position to purchase liability insurance 
and they are free to adjust the price and profits of their 
product to reflect either the insurance or damage judg- 
ments resulting from the hazards of their product. With- 
out liability, the costs of the hazards are borne solely by 
the victims of their conduct. 


B. Imposing Liability On Government Contractors 
Will Not Interfere With Military Decision- 
Making Or The Government Procurement 
Process 


Judge Pratt explained that the government contract 
defense is necessary to ensure that the government can 
easily obtain supplies and materials, and that national 
security might be comprised if a manufacturer or a 
court second-guessed military decision-making. 534 F. 
Supp. at 1054. However, government procurement and 
military decisionmaking would remain intact and unob- 
structed when the inquiry is focused upon the negligence 
of the supplier of commercial products, particularly when 
any inquiry into governmental liability should be fore- 
closed by sovereign immunity. 


56 


Imposing a duty to exercise reasonable care in the 
manufacture of a product or to warn of latent defects 
in products supplied to the government, will neither 
hinder the flow of supplies to the government nor in- 
terfere with the military’s ability “to wage war in what- 
ever manner the Government deems advisable.” Jd. This 
is hardly a situation in which a government specification 
commanded a particular manufacturing process or where 
the alleged injury-causing aspect of the product was man- 
dated by contract. Contract compliance, quality assur- 
ance and inspections were the responsibility of the man- 
ufacturer under the express provisions of the herbicide 
contracts. The manufacturers’ elimination of the dioxin 
in the product would not have involved “second-guessing” 
military decision-making, for there was no military de- 
cision for Agent Orange to contain dioxin. 


As discussed above, the argument for a government 
contract defense is also weakened when a product is not 
manufactured exclusively for the military, but is, instead, 
commercially available and the government is just one 
of several consumers. Chapin v. Johns-Manville Sales 
Corp., supra; Plas v. Raymark Industries, Inc., supra. 


C. The Government Contract Defense Cannot Be 
Justified As A Cost-Saving Measure 


Some have argued that the government contract de- 
fense will increase the Government’s cost of obtaining 
needed materials, but that is far from clear. The cost 
of liability insurance is often built into the bid submitted 
for a government contract. In fact government contracts 
often require the contractor to carry liability insurance. 
See Defense Acquisition Regulations (formerly Armed 
Services Procurement Regulations), 32 C.F.R. Section 
X, Parts 3-5. If insurance has already been acquired, 
then the government contract defense results in a wind- 
fall to the insurer at the expense of the plaintiffs. 


The Supreme Court has recognized that government 
contractors set their bid prices to reflect the risk of pos- 


57 


sible liability. Stencel Aero Engineering Corp. v. United 
States, 431 U.S. at 666. In Stencel Aero, the Court 
noted that there was no unfairness to government con- 
tractors in prohibiting an indemnity suit against the 
United States because the contractor “no doubt had suf- 
ficient notice as to take his risk [liability] into account 
in negotiating its contract... .” Id. at 674. 


There is no reason to assume that the costs of insur- 
ing against liability are uniform for all military equip- 
ment suppliers. Those with proven safety records may 
be able to secure liability insurance at much lower rates 
than less careful suppliers. Presumably, such cost savings 
enabled these manufacturers to make lower bids and be 
more competitive. It is by no means clear, therefore, 
that the government contract defense will make govern- 
ment procurements more cost-efficient or even more 
efficient generally. 


IV. THERE ARE GENUINE ISSUES OF MATE- 
RIAL FACT BEARING ON THE VALIDITY 
OF EACH AND EVERY CONTENTION RE- 
LATING TO THE GOVERNMENT CON- 
TRACT DEFENSE 


Defendants declare that they are entitled to summary 
judgment because all of the elements of the government 
contract defense have been established. The principles of 
summary judgment practice in the Second Circuit are 
very well established. Cereali v. Continental Grain Co., 
563 F.2d 563, 565 (2d Cir. 1977); Heyman v. Com- 
merce and Industry Insurance Co., 524 F.2d 1317, 1319 
(2d Cir. 1975): 


[T]he “fundamental maxim” remains that on a mo- 
tion for summary judgment the court cannot try 
issues of fact; it can only determine whether there 
are issues to be tried. Moreover, when the Court 
considers a motion for summary judgment, it must 
resolve all ambiguities and draw all reasonable in- 
ferences in favor of the party against whom sum- 


58 


mary judgment is sought, with the burden on the 
moving party to demonstrate the absence of any ma- 
terial factual issue genuinely in dispute. This rule 
is clearly appropriate, given the nature of summary 
judgment. This procedural weapon is a drastic de- 
vice since its prophylatic function, when exercised, 
cuts off a party’s right to present his case to the 
jury. 
524 F.2d at 1319-1320 (citations omitted. ) 


A prerequisite to summary judgment is the absence 
of any genuine issues as to any material fact. Bridge- 
port Brass Co. v. Bostwick Laboratories, Inc., 181 F.2d 
315, 316 (2d Cir. 1950). Thus, “if liability is dependent 
upon any disputed questions of fact, the party opposing 
the motion has the right to have those questions deter- 
mined upon a trial.” Jd. The Second Circuit has con- 
sistently held that summary judgment is a harsh remedy 
which should be exercised with care taken to give the 
parties an opportunity to develop the facts. Shoenbaum 
v. Firstbrook, 405 F.2d 215, 218 (2d Cir. 1968); Judge 
v. City of Buffalo, 524 F.2d 1321 (2d Cir. 1975). Trial 
by affidavits has consistently been condemned. Poller v. 
Columbia Boradcasting Systems, Inc., 368 U.S. 465, 473 
(1962). This is especially so where proof depends upon 
credibility. Colby v. Klune, 178 F.2d 872, 873-875 (2d 
Cir. 1949): 


This case illustrates the danger of founding a 
judgment in favor of one party upon his own ver- 
sion of facts within his sole knowledge as set forth 
in affidavits prepared ex parte. Cross-examination 
of the party and a reasonable examination of his 
records by the other party frequently bring forth 
further facts which place a very different light upon 
the picture. 


178 F.2d at 872 (citations omitted). 


Virtually all the discovery conducted in this litigation 
concerning the government contract defense occurred 


59 


prior to this Court’s February 16, 1984 ruling restoring 
the United States to party status. The United States has 
never had an opportunity to question any witness whose 
deposition defendants now offer in support of their sum- 
mary judgment. Indeed, only days before moving for 
summary judgment, defendants moved ex parte for an 
order staying all discovery indefinitely. The Order issued, 
and the United States’ first opportunity to look behind 
defendants’ allegations was lost. Nevertheless, it is doubt- 
ful that there has ever been a case less amenable to 
summary judgment on the government contract defense 
than the one presently before this Court. This aspect 
of the case abounds in genuine issues of material fact 
bearing directly on the validity of each and every con- 
tention relating to the government contract defense. For 
this reason, summary judgment on the government con- 
tract defense must be denied.

B. Whether The Agent Orange Manufactured By 
The Defendants Met Government Specifica- 
tions Involve Genuine Issues Of Fact 


At the time the Court set forth this element of the 
government contract defense, it seems unlikely that the 
Court appreciated the complexity of this issue.’ There 
are over sixty known contracts for the manufacture of 
herbicides containing 2,4,5-T for use in Vietnam, in- 
volving at least seven manufacturers, spanning a decade. 
Despite of defendants’ assertions of strict compliance 
with the specifications, there is evidence that government 
in fact rejected individual batches of Agent Orange. For 
example, 8,200 gallons of Dow’s product failed to meet 
the required blend ratio. Exhibit 31. Hercules sold de- 
fective Agent Orange to the government in July and 
August of 1967. In September, 1967 Hercules shut down 
its Jacksonville, Arkansas plant due to its inability to 
produce “on-spec” Agent Orange. Exhibit 32. More- 
over, by the express terms of the contracts, compliance 
with the specifications, quality assurance, and inspections 
were the sole responsibility of the defendants. 


By a procedural maneuver, the defendants have at- 
tempted to obtain summary judgment, while insuring that 
the United States would not discover evidence on these 
very issues. Nevertheless, it is clear that there are indeed 
genuine issues of material fact concerning defendants’ 
compliance with terms of various government contracts.

Once again, in formulating this element of the govern-

knowledge”, in the hope of creating an overall impression 
of satisfying this element of the defense. In doing so, 
they failed to grasp the full, factual implications of the 
so-called “third” element of the defense and, consequently, 
have failed to establish the facts necessary to support the 
government contract defense.

V. CONCLUSION 


The government contractor defense should be rejected 
in this case because it would not further the policies it 
purports to serve. In addition, there are genuine issues 
of material fact exists which bears upon the validity of 
each contention relating to the defense.

on the government contractor defense should be denied.
