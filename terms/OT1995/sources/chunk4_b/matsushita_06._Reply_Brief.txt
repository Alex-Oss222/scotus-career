PETITIONERS’ REPLY BRIEF 


The Ninth Circuit's opinion announced new legal principles 
of broad applicability concerning (1) the balance of authority 
between state and federal courts, (2) the implication of private 
rights of action under administrative rules, and (3) the nature of 
the event that triggers federal tender offer regulation. Each of 
these new legal principles puts the Ninth Circuit in irreconcilable 
conflict with the established law of other circuit courts: 


* In conflict with the law of the First and Third Circuits, the 
Ninth Circuit refused to give Full Faith and Credit to a state 
court judgment entered upon a class action settlement, utilizing 
an analysis that jeopardizes all comprehensive settlements of 
State court class actions which involve the compromise of both 
state and exclusively federal claims. Petition for a Writ of 
Certiorari (“Pet.”) at 10-17. 


2 


* In conflict with the law of the District of Columbia, Third, and 
Sixth Circuits, the Ninth Circuit held that an agency rule 
promulgated pursuant to an enabling statute that has been held 
to be privately enforceable automatically will have a private 
right of action. Pet. at 17-21. 


* In conflict with the law of the Fourth Circuit (and uniform 
SEC authority), the Ninth Circuit transformed a precise and 
well understood tender offer regulation into an amorphous rule, 
thereby eliminating the predictability necessary to structure 
tender offers. Pet. at 21-24. 


Far from merely resolving a “unique” factual dispute, the Ninth 
Circuit's enunciation of these new legal principles has generated 
extraordinary uncertainty in a number of legal arenas. 


Respondents suggest that the Court look past this case on 
the strength of their representation that the Delaware settlement 
was the product of a corrupt state bar and judiciary. Epstein 
Brief in Opposition (“Epstein Opp.”) at 15-26. Yet Respondents 
concede that such was not the basis of the Ninth Circuit's 
decision. Jd. at 5, 22. The Ninth Circuit's opinion urgently 
requires review because it provides circuit court authority to 
upset state court approved class action settlements releasing both 
state and federal claims regardless of the adequacy of class 
representation or level of judicial review to which the settlement 
was subjected.! 


This petition is not premature. Contrary to Respondents’ 
suggestion that further district court proceedings may “moot or 
reshape” the Ninth Circuit's holdings, Epstein Opp. at 3, the 
Ninth Circuit's decision has established novel and far reaching 
legal principles, none of which can be altered by proceedings on

Il. TENDER OFFERS REQUIRE CERTAINTY 
REGARDING THE PARAMETERS OF RULE 14d-10 


The scope of federal tender offer regulation is likewise a 
matter of national importance. The Ninth Circuit's holding con- 
cerning the scope of Rule 14d-10, contrary to Respondents’ 
assertion, is not limited to the supposedly “unique” and “com- 
plex” Wasserman transaction. While the Wasserman transaction 
involved a complex structure, the decision of the Ninth Circuit 
should have turned on the simple and Straightforward question 
of whether the transaction occurred during the tender offer. As 
written (and as previously construed by courts and universally 
understood by securities law practitioners), Rule 14d-10 operates

{fl. THE NINTH CIRCUIT HAS CREATED A NEW AND 
IMPROPER STANDARD FOR IMPLYING PRIVATE 
RIGHTS OF ACTION 


Respondents do not challenge the core of Matsushita's 
implied rights analysis.? As Matsushita previously demonstrated, 
the Ninth Circuit’s implied rights analysis leads to the untenable 
result that every rule promulgated pursuant to a privately 
enforceable statute has a private right of action. Pet. at 19. The 
cases cited by Respondents finding an implied right of action 
under Sections 14(d)(7) and 14(d)(6) are irrelevant. See, e.g., 
Field, 850 F.2d at 945 (Section 14(d)(7)); Pryor v. United States 
Steel Corp., 794 F.2d 52, 57-58 (2d Cir.) (Section 14(d)(6)), 
cert. denied, 479 U.S. 954 (1986). In their complaints and now, 
Respondents make their claims under Rule 14d-10, not Section 
14(d)(7). Rule 14d-10 should not be read to afford a private right 
of action because even if a private right is recognized under 
Section 14(d)(7), “the breadth of the right once recognized 
should not, as a general matter, grow beyond the scope congres- 
sionally intended.” Virginia Bankshares, Inc. v. Sandberg, 501 
U.S. 1083, 1102 (1991). The Epstein Respondents state that 
“[{t]he rule [14d-10(c)] requires that where a tender offerer 
employs more than one kind of consideration ...each share- 
holder must be allowed the same right to choose among the 
options offered.” Epstein Opp. at 7. This erroneous interpretation 
of Rule 14d-10(c), which was endorsed by the Ninth Circuit, 
goes far beyond Section 14(d)(7), which requires that higher 
consideration be paid to certain shareholders before a claim is 
actionable.
