STATUTORY PROVISIONS INVOLVED 


Several provisions of the Defense Production Act 
(“DPA”), 50 U.S.C. §§ 2061, et seg. (1988), are re- 
lated to the disposition of petitioners’ contract claims. 


Section 101 of the DPA, 50 U.S.C. § 2071(a), pro- 
vides: 


The President is authorized (1) to require that per- 
formance under contracts or orders (other than con- 
tracts of employment) which he deems necessary or 
appropriate to promote the national defense shall 
take priority over performance of any other contract 
or order, and, for the purpose of assuring such pri- 
ority, to require acceptance and performance of such 
contracts or orders in preference to other contracts 
or orders by any person he finds to be capable of 
their performance, and (2) to allocate materials and 
facilities in such manner, upon such conditions, and 
to such extent as he shall deem necessary or appro- 
priate to promote the national defense. 


Section 707 of the DPA, 50 U.S.C. § 2157, provides: 


No person shall be held liable for damages or penal- 
ties for any act or failure to act resulting directly or 
indirectly from compliance with a rule, regulation, 
or order issued pursuant to this Act, notwithstanding 


3 


that any such rule, regulation, or order shall there- 
after be declared by judicial or other competent au- 
thority to be invalid. No person shall discriminate 
against orders or contracts to which priority is as- 
signed or for which materials or facilities are allo- 
cated under title I of this Act or under any rule, 
regulation, or order issued thereunder, by charging 
higher prices or by imposing different terms and con- 
ditions for such orders or contracts than for other 
generally comparable orders or contracts, or in any 
other manner. 


STATEMENT OF THE CASE 
A. The Compelled Production of Agent Orange 


This case involves the most recent chapter in the long 
history of the government’s use during the Vietnam War 
of the defoliant known as Agent Orange.’ Petitioners 
are two chemical companies. During the 1960s, acting 
under the authority of the DPA, 50 U.S.C. §§ 2061, 
et seq.. the government ordered petitioners to produce 
and sell Agent Orange to the Department of Defense. 
(J.A. 8-10; Pet. App. 41a, 43a). Although Hercules and 
Thompson had some experience in producing herbicides 
for commercial use, neither company had ever produced 
defoliants and neither company understood the potential 
hazards that might accompany the military use of such 
defoliants. (C.A. App. 0544, 0546-0548, 0448, 0431, 
0432).° 


In contrast, the government had engaged in years of 
study concerning the use of defoliants for military appli- 
cations and possessed a wealth of information pertaining 
to the hazards associated with the military use of such 
compounds. (C.A. App. 0430, 0502-0503). It is undis-

The government periodically inspected Hercules’ and

The government knew as early as 1959 that the manu- 
facture of Agent Orange according to these specifications 
could produce a dangerous by-product known as “dioxin.” 
Hercules and Thompson, however, were ignorant of this 
possible result when they began manufacturing Agent 
Orange pursuant to the government’s specifications. (J.A.

The Agent Orange produced by Hercules and Thomp- 
son was provided to the government largely under “rated 
orders” pursuant to the DPA. See In Re Agent Orange 
Prod. Liab. Litigation, 597 F. Supp. 740, 844 (E.D.N.Y. 
1984), aff'd. 818 F.2d 145 (2d Cir. 1987), cert. denied, 
484 U.S. 1004 (1988). Under these rated orders. 
Hercules and Thompson were subject to civil and crimi- 
nal penalties, as well as mandatory injunctions, if they 
failed to comply with the government’s commands. The 
government's demand for Agent Orange eventually re- 
sulted in the virtual takeover of Hercules’ and Thomp- 
son’s production facilities, despite the protestations of 
both companies. (J.A. 24; C.A. App. 0619-0622, 0629, 
0641-0422). Indeed, in March of 1967, the United 
States Department of Defense issued an order that re- 
quired Hercules to divert its entire herbicide production 
capacity to the manufacture of Agent Orange. Hercules 


5 


requested relief from the government's rated orders in 
order to enable Hercules to satisfy its preexisting com- 
mercial commitments, but the government rejected that 
request. 


For its part, Thompson went to extraordinary lengths 
to attempt to avoid altogether the compelled production 
of Agent Orange. Among other things, the President of 
Thompson personally flew to Washington, D.C., and 
petitioned the Department of Defense to release Thomp- 
son from the rated orders so that it would not have to 
manufacture Agent Orange. These efforts failed, and 
Thompson was ordered under the DPA to continue pro- 
ducing Agent Orange. (J.A. 24: Pet. App. 4a). 


B. The Agent Orange Litigation 


In the late 1970s, Hercules and Thompson, along with 
other Agent Orange manufacturers, were sued in a class 
action by thousands of U.S. veterans of the Vietnam 
War, and others, seeking damages for health problems 
allegedly resulting from exposure to Agent Orange 
(“Agent Orange litigation”). The plaintiffs alleged that 
Agent Orange produced in accordance with government 
specifications contained dioxin as a by-product, which 
they alleged was an extremely toxic substance that caused 
them to suffer health problems. The plaintiffs claimed up 
to $40 billion in damages. (J.A. 12-13, 25; Pet. App. 
4a-Sa, 50a; C.A. App. 0510). 


The defendant manufacturers impleaded the govern- 
ment into the case as a third-party defendant. (Schuck 
at 60: J.A. 13). The trial court in the Agent Orange 
litigation granted summary judgment to the government 
with regard to the third-party complaint. Jn Re Agent 
Orange Prod. Liab. Litigation, 611 F. Supp. 1221, 1223 
(E.D.N.Y. 1985), aff'd, 818 F.2d 204, 210 (2d Cir. 
1987). The trial court, however, did not enter an order 
dismissing the government entirely from the action, but 
instead retained the government as a party for discovery 
purposes. (J.A. 13). 


6 


Hercules and Thompson moved for summary judgment 
in 1982 against the plaintiff class. They argued that the 
government had ordered them to produce Agent Orange 
in accordance with precise specifications and that, al- 
though the government knew of the product’s alleged 
defects, Hercules and Thompson were unaware of any 
risk. Accordingly, Hercules and Thompson argued that 
they could not be held liable for any injuries that were 
caused by the government’s use of Agent Orange. (Pet. 
App. 5a-6a, 50a). This defense, now known as the “gov- 
ernment contractor defense,” subsequently was recognized 
by a sharply divided Court in Boyle v. United Technol- 
ogies Corp., 487 U.S. 500 (1988). 


Judge Pratt, the first district judge assigned to the 
Agent Orange litigation, wrote a decision that granted 
petitioners’ summary judgment motion. Jn Re Agent 
Orange Prod. Liab. Litigation, 565 F. Supp. 1263 
(E.D.N.Y. 1983). Judge Weinstein, however, took over 
the Agent Orange litigation when Judge Pratt was ap- 
pointed to the Second Circuit, and withdrew Judge 
Pratt’s decision before it was entered as a formal judg- 
ment.* Thereafter, Judge Weinstein ruled that Hercules 
and Thompson should remain as defendants in the litiga- 
tion and that the viability of the government contractor 
defense could not be determined on summary judgment, 
but would have to be determined by the jury during the 
trial on liability. In Re Agent Orange Prod. Liab. Litiga- 
tion, 100 F.R.D. 718, 723 (E.D.N.Y.), certification of 
appeal denied, 100 F.R.D. 735 (E.D.N.Y. 1983). 


Neither Judge Pratt’s nor Judge Weinstein’s initial 
analysis of the government contractor defense was in- 
formed by the views of the government. The government 
refused to support the assertion by Hercules and Thomp- 
son of the government contractor defense. Instead, the 
government watched from the sidelines in the class action

as the manufacturers struggled to deal with the myriad 
claims of United States veterans for injuries that allegedly 
were inflicted on them by the government. See In Re 
Agent Orange Prod. Liab. Litigation, 597 F. Supp. at 
879. (Pet. App. 7a, 8a). Further, the government ac- 
tively opposed and frustrated the contractors’ efforts to 
obtain discovery from the government pertaining to the 
government contractor defense. (See Transcripts of dis- 
covery-related proceedings in the Agent Orange litigation 
lodged with the Clerk of the Court simultaneously with 
the filing of this brief). 


Rather than risk a catastrophic judgment and continue 
to incur enormous attorneys’ fees and litigation costs, 
Hercules and Thompson joined with the other defendant 
companies and settled the Agent Orange litigation. On 
May 7, 1984, on the eve of trial, Hercules, Thompson, 
and all of the other defendant companies settled with the 
Agent Orange class for a total of $180 million to be paid 
by the defendants on a market-share basis. (J.A. 13, 26; 
Pet. App. 50a, 7a). 


Hercules’ share of the settlement was $18,772,568. 
(J.A. 13; Pet. App. 5la, 7a). Thompson’s share of the 
settlement was $3,096,597. (J.A. 26-27; Pet. App. 7a). 
Also, Hercules incurred defense costs in excess of $6 
million during the litigation. (J.A. 13; Pet. App. 5la). 
Thompson incurred defense fees in excess of $3 million. 
(J.A. 27). 


The government had refused to participate in the class 
action settlement negotiations. In Re Agent Orange Prod. 
Liab. Litigation, 597 F. Supp. at 879. On April 24, 
1984, the government expressly advised the district court 
that “[t]he United States declines to attend or participate 
in settlement negotiations or court settlement conferences 
because any settlement of this case that calls for contri- 
bution by the United States is not warranted. This is the 
United States’ firm position and we anticipate no change 
whatsoever in any aspect of it.” Jd. 


Subsequently, the district court affirmed the Agent 
Orange settlement as reasonable and fair to the plaintiff 
class. The district court held that, if Hercules and Thomp- 
son had not settled, “there was a possibility of an ultimate 
liability with claims totalling billions of dollars.” Id. at 
749 (emphasis added). 


Some of the Agent Orange plaintiffs opted out of the 
class action settlement and continued to press their in- 
dividual claims. (J.A. 14). Petitioners later moved for 
summary judgment against the opt-outs on various 
grounds, again including the government contractor de- 
fense. (Pet. App. 8a, 51a). Although the government 
had remained neutral during the earlier proceedings, now 
the government, a nominal party solely for discovery pur- 
poses, volunteered a vehement opposition to the assertion 
of the government contractor defense. (J.A. 34-71). The 
government argued in a brief in opposition to the peti- 
tioners’ motion for summary judgment that “there exists 
substantial and convincing evidence that establishes not 
only that there are indeed genuine issues of material fact 
directly bearing on the validity of each and every conten- 
tion relating to the government contract defense, but also 
that the defense does not further the policies it purports 
to serve.” (J.A. 36). 


Notwithstanding the government’s position, Judge Wein- 
stein granted summary judgment on the basis of the 
government contractor defense against all plaintiffs who 
had opted out of the class. In Re Agent Orange Prod. 
Liab. Litigation, 611 F. Supp. 1223, 1263-64 (E.D.N.Y. 
1985), aff'd, 818 F.2d 187 (2d Cir. 1987), cert. denied, 
487 U.S. 1234 (1988). In doing so, Judge Weinstein 
took a position exactly opposite to the position he had 
taken in the class action summary judgment motion filed 
by Hercules and Thompson. 


The cost of the Agent Orange settlements and the legal 
fees had serious and adverse effects on the financial status 
of both Hercules and Thompson. In fact, Thompson ulti-

mately was forced to cease operations entirely. (J.A. 

27-28). Moreover, both petitioners continue to defend 

numerous Agent Orange tort claims at considerable 
‘4 


expense. 
C. The Government’s Escape from Accountability 


In 1990, Hercules and Thompson independently com- 
menced actions in the United States Claims Court seeking 
damages from the government for the settlement and de- 
fense costs they had incurred in connection with the Agent 
Orange litigation. (J.A. 5-29, 21-32). Petitioners’ prin- 
cipal claim was based on United States v. Spearin, 248 
U.S. 132 (1918) and its progeny, including Helene 
Curtis Industries v. United States, 312 F.2d 774 (Ct. 
Cl. 1963). Hercules and Thompson each alleged that 
the government breached a contractuai werranty of 
specifications by requiring thein to manufacture Agent 
Orange, which the government knew to present significant 
hazards. (J.A. 14-15, 29-30; C.A. App. 0546-0549, 
0633-0634, 0655-0661). Hercules and Thompson alleged 
that the government specifications were defective and that 
these specifications ultimately gave rise to the veteran 
plaintiffs’ claims that they were harmed by Agent Orange. 
These claims subjected Hercules and Thompson to poten- 
tially enormous third-party liability. (C.A. App. 0502- 
0507, 0541-0542, 0549-0550). 


Petitioners’ other claims for relief arose from the gov- 
ernment’s breach of its obligation to disclose its superior

(Pet. App. 23a). The Federal Circuit concluded that the

SUMMARY OF ARGUMENT 


The government has engaged for years in unfair and 
harmful conduct towards Hercules and Thompson. Three 
decades ago, the government exercised control over the 
production facilities of Hercules and Thompson under the 
authority of the DPA and ordered them to manufacture 
Agent Orange. (J.A. 9, 23). The government provided 
Hercules and Thompson with exact specifications that the 
government, but not Hercules and Thompson, knew would 
result in a potentially dangerous product when used in 
the unprecedented manner intended by the government. 
(J.A. 9-10, 23-25). Years later, when Hercules and 
Thompson were sued by veterans alleging injuries from 
Agent Orange, the government refused to support their 
assertion of the government contractor defense and even 
opposed and frustrated their efforts to obtain discovery 
on the issue. (J.A. 34-71). Now, when Hercules and 
Thompson seek to recover in contract from the govern- 
ment for the significant, out-of-pocket financial losses they 
incurred in defending and settling the Agent Orange liti- 
gation, the government disclaims all liability on the 
grounds that petitioners simply volunteered millions of 
dollars to the Agent Orange class. 


13 


The government’s approach reflects its assumption that 
it is completely immune from any accountability for order- 
ing the production and use of Agent Orange. Under exist- 
ing law, the government is not accountable in tort for its 
conduct. This Court has established that the government 
is immune from tort claims by members of the armed 
forces. Feres v. United States, 340 U.S. 135 (1950). 
This Court also has established that the government is 
immune from contribution claims by government con- 
tractors arising out of tort claims against contractors by 
members of the armed forces. Stencel Aero Eng’g Corp. 
v. United States, 431 U.S. 666 (1977). 


This Court, however, never has held the government 
immune from breach of contract actions in circumstances 
such as those presented here. Indeed, three separate legal 
theories establish, both independently and in unison, a 
right to recover in contract from the government: (a) 
breach of implied warranty, based on this Court’s decision 
in Spearin; (b) breach of implied warranty, based on the 
government’s superior knowledge of the hazards involved 
in the manufacture of Agent Orange, as articulated in 
Helene Curtis; and (c) an implied-in-fact indemnification 
obligation that derives from the compelled production of 
Agent Orange pursuant to the DPA and under circum- 
stances that warrant finding such an indemnity obligation. 
Although the facts support all three of these grounds, any 
one of them, taken alone, is sufficient to entitle Hercules 


and Thompson to recover from the United States for the 
losses at issue. 


The government is accountable to Hercules and Thomp- 
son for breach of implied warranty because the govern- 
ment dictated the specifications to Hercules and Thomp- 
son for the production of Agent Orange. (J.A. 14-15, 
29). These specifications were defective because they 
resulted in a product that contained dioxin, the substance 
that the Agent Orange plaintiffs claimed caused their 
injuries. The government also is accountable to Hercules 


14 


and Thompson under the related superior knowledge doc- 
trine because the government possessed knowledge about 
the dioxin hazard that petitioners did not have, but the 
government failed to provide this knowledge to petition- 
ers. (J.A. 12, 30). Finally, the government is account- 
able to Hercules and Thompson because the government 
ordered Hercules and Thompson pursuant to the DPA to 
produce Agent Orange and because, when a contract is 
imposed on a contractor under the DPA in circumstances 
such as those present here, the contract contains an 
implied-in-fact indemnification provision. 


The Federal Circuit held that the government was not 
liable on the Spearin claims solely because Hercules and 
Thompson settled the Agent Orange action. The Federal 
Circuit reasoned that “[t]here can be no serious doubt” 
that Hercules and Thompson would have avoided all lia- 
bility had they proceeded to trial, and therefore that Her- 
cules and Thompseii had “volunteered” to suffer the losses 
represented by the settlement amount. (Pet. App. 23a). 
This holding cannot be sustained because absolute vindi- 
cation by a jury never is a certainty, and could not have 
been predicted in the Agent Orange class action at the 
time petitioners entered into a settlement agreement. This 
holding also undermines the well-established policy of 
encouraging reasonable settlements of litigation. 


The Federal Circuit rejected the superior knowledge 
argument on the additional ground that recovery is avail- 
able only when the government withholds knowledge that 
bears upon the immediate costs incurred during the gov- 
ernment contractor’s performance under the contract at 
issue. (Pet. App. 16a). This limitation is contrary to 
the principles underlying the Helene Curtis doctrine, 
which are that the government cannot betray a con- 
tractor into a ruinous course of action by withholding 
knowledge pertinent to a contract. See Helene Curtis, 


15 


312 F.2d at 778. Although in Helene Curtis the govern- 
ment withheld knowledge pertinent to the actual perform- 
ance of a contract, the principle underlying that decision 
logically extends to the government’s withholding of 
knowledge pertinent to the safety of a product produced 
under a government contract. 


Finally, the Federal Circuit rejected the argument that 
the orders exercised against petitioners under the DPA 
created an implied-in-fact agreement to indemnify peti- 
tioners against damages that might result. The Federal 
Circuit did recognize that the DPA established a quid 
pro quo, protecting government contractors to the extent 
that the power of the DPA is exercised. (Pet. App. at 
29a-31a). The Federal Circuit concluded, however, that 
the DPA protected contractors only against damages re- 
sulting from the requirements of priority and that its 
protections did not reach third-party tort damages. (Pet. 
App. 32a-33a). This conclusion was erroneous because, 
as Judge Plager noted in dissent, it is not supported by 
the language of the DPA, is illogical, and is completely 
unjust to contractors that are required to perform govern- 
ment contracts because of the power of the government 
to inflict criminal and civil penalties against them. (Pet. 
App. 44a-45a).

ARGUMENT 


It simply cannot be the law that the government can 
exert control over the operations of a government con- 
tractor, insist that the contractor manufacture a product 
according to precise specifications that carry a risk of 
harm to others, insist that a product be produced that the 
government—but not the contractor—knows could be 
potentially hazardous, do nothing to support the con- 
tractor who is sued by those who allegedly are harmed by 
the product due to defective specifications, and then avoid 
any accountability for the millions of dollars the private 
party loses. When the government exposes its contractors 
to such injuries, the government should be liable. 


Here, petitioners seek an obviously right result by rely- 
ing upon several related legal theories. The effort, how- 
ever, to tease out the proper application of these various 
theories should not conceal the underlying and funda- 
mental point: the government is the proper party to bear 
the costs that the compulsory production of Agent Orange 
imposed upon petitioners. 


In searching for the proper legal theory upon which to 
allocate these costs to the government, petitioners first 
turn to the elements of the Spearin claims and show how 
the Federal Circuit’s causation argument simply cannot 
be reconciled with established principles of contract law 
governing breach of warranty. Petitioners then argue 
that the closely related superior knowledge doctrine pro- 
vides an alternative and reinforcing basis for recovery 
under the contracts at issue here. Petitioners finally show 
that the DPA clearly provides a basis for an implied-in- 
fact indemnification obligation when a government con- 
tractor suffers injury as a result of claims made by third 
parties stemming from the allegedly dangerous products 
the contractor is ordered to produce in accordance with 
government specifications. 


At the end of the day, the point becomes unassailable 
that the Federal Circuit’s decision to insulate the govern- 


17 


ment from all liability is profoundly unfair and unsup- 
portable and, therefore, should be reversed. 


I. THE GOVERNMENT'S BREACH OF AN IMPLIED 
WARRANTY OF SPECIFICATIONS ENTITLES 
HERCULES AND THOMPSON TO DAMAGES 
UNDER UNITED STATES v. SPEARIN 


A. The Federal Circuit Erroneously Held That 
Hercules and Thompson Cannot Establish Legal 
Causation Between Their Damages and the Gov- 
ernment’s Breach of Implied Warranty 


Almost 80 years ago, this Court recognized a contrac- 
tual right of action against the government for breach of 
implied-in-fact warranties. The Court in United States v. 
Spearin, 248 U.S. 132 (1918), held that the government 
impliedly warrants that, if the contractor follows detailed 
specifications included as part of a government contract, 
the contractor “will not be responsible for the conse- 
quences of defects in the plans and specifications.” 
Spearin, 248 U.S. at 136. Instead, the government is 
liable to its contractors if the specifications are defective, 
and must reimburse its contractors for the damages in- 
curred by complying with the federal government’s direc- 
tions, including lost profits. Spearin, 248 U.S. at 136; 
Poorvu v. United States, 420 F.2d 993, 999-1000 (Ct. 
Cl. 1970); Luria Bros. & Co. v. United States, 369 F.2d 
701, 707-08 (Ct. Cl. 1966); Laburnum Constr. Corp. v. 
United States, 325 F.2d 451, 457-58 (Ct. Cl. 1963). 


In applying the Spearin doctrine, lower courts have 
held that the warranty implied when the government 
issues specifications include a warranty of safe design. As 
one court pointedly observed: 


It is not necessary to spell out that specifications 
having major safety defects are fully as much in 
breach of the implied warranty as defects in the 
feasibility, practicability, or commercial possibility 
of performance as specified.

Ordnance Research, Inc. v. United States, 609 F.2d 462, 
479 (Ct. Cl. 1979); see also Greenbrier Indus., Inc., 
ASBCA No. 22121, 81-1 BCA € 14,982, recons. denied, 
81-BCA 4 15057 (1991). 


A breach of warranty claim generally involves the fol- 
lowing elements: (1) a valid warranty existed; (2) the 
warranty was breached; and (3) plaintiff's damages were 
caused by the breach. (Pet. App. 17a). The Federal 
Circuit held here that Hercules and Thompson could not 
prove the third element: causation betwen their damages 
and the breach of implied warranty.” 


The Federal Circuit held that causation was lacking on 
the sole premise that Hercules and Thompson voluntarily 
had entered into a settlement with the underlying plain- 
tiffs. According to the Federal Circuit, had Hercules and 
Thompson pursued the Agent Orange litigation to judg- 
ment, the jury would have determined that the govern- 
ment contractor defense barred the imposition of tort 
liability against them. Thus, the Federal Circuit held that 
the settlement was a voluntary assumption of liability by 
Hercules and Thompson, for which the government could 
not be held responsible. (Pet. App. 23a). 


The Federal Circuit was wrong because it is impossible 
to predict a jury verdict or the overall course of litiga- 
tion under any circumstances. As Judge Plager stated in 
dissent: “I do not know what the ultimate decision in

issue, nor does anyone.” (Pet. App. 40a). Notably, 
Hercules and Thompson had exhausted other methods of

B. The Federal Circuit’s Approach Penalizes Hercules 
and Thompson for Their Unquestionably Reason- 
able Decision To Settle and Violates the Well- 
Established Principle That the Law Favors 
Settlements 


The Federal Circuit held that Hercules and Thompson 
lost their right to assert a Spearin claim solely because 
they settled the Agent Orange litigation. By that reason- 
ing, Hercules and Thompson would have been entitled 
to pursue a Spearin claim had they proceeded to trial and 
lost. In that event, presumably there would have been 
“causation” between the alleged damages (i.e., the ver- 
dict) and the implied warranty. Thus, the Federal Cir- 
cuit’s decision penalizes Hercules and Thompson solely 
because they decided to settle. (Pet. App. 18a-20a). 


Penalizing litigants who reasonably settle underlying 
actions does violence to the well-established principle 
that the law favors settlement. See Janneh v. GAF 
Corp., 887 F.2d 432 (2d Cir. 1989); cert. denied, 
498 U.S. 865 (1990) (“[L]itigants, courts, and Congress 
view settlement as a positive force, indispensable to judi- 
cial administration”); Ahern v. Central Pac. Freight 
Lines, 846 F.2d 47, 48 (9th Cir. 1988) (quoting from 
United States v. McInnes, 556 F.2d 436, 441 (9th Cir. 
1977)) (“[t]here is an overriding public interest in set- 
tling and quieting litigation”). 


Basic contract principles call for the government to 
pay to Hercules and Thompson the damages that they 
incurred to third parties, whether these damages resulted

from a jury verdict or from a settlement with the third 
parties. Where a breach by one party to a contract re- 
sults in claims by third persons against the other party 
to the contract, the party sued is not required to litigate 
the third-party claims to conclusion and lose in order to 
recover damages associated with the third-party claims. 
Restatement (Second) of Contracts § 351, cmt. c (1981). 
Rather, damages in a contract action often include both 
the cost of a settlement of an underlying third-party ac- 
tion and defense costs incurred in the underlying action. 
“In furtherance of the policy favoring private settlement 
of disputes, the injured party is also allowed to recover 
the reasonable amount of any settlement to avoid litiga-

(Second) of Contracts § 351, cmt. c (1981).*

D. Recognition of a Spearin Claim Against the Gov- 
ernment Will Establish Government Accountability 
for Ordering the Production of an Allegedly Haz- 
ardous Product 


Recognition of a contract remedy against the govern- 
ment for losses incurred by government contractors as a 
result of third-party claims is necessary to compel govern- 
ment accountability for ordering the production and use 
of a potentially hazardous product when the government, 
and not the contractors, has knowledge of the risks. 
Liability in this case also will ensure symmetry among the 
government’s existing immunities and protections. 


Congress has adopted two Acts that allow suits against 
the government: the Tucker Act, 28 U.S.C. § 1346(a)(2) 
(1988), which permits the assertion of contract claims, 
and the Federal Tort Claims Act (“FTCA”), 28 U.S.C. 
$§ 2671-2680 (1988), which permits the assertion of tort 
claims. 


29 


The jurisprudence surrounding the assertion of contract 
claims against the government is fairly simple. Cases 
arise under the Tucker Act between parties in privity; the 
government waives its immunity against private parties 
when it enters into contracts with such parties. The court 
in a contract matter simply enforces the agreement be- 
tween the parties pursuant to established principles of 
contract law. 


The government is answerable for its contract breaches 
even when the contract involves purchases and designs 
that are protected from tort claims by third parties: 


The policy considerations that motivate the sovereign 
to adopt the overall program do not create an um- 
brella that shields the Government from liability for 
breach of valid contracts customarily entered. A 
multitude of defense contractors would be surprised 
to be told that contracts for ship construction, air- 
craft production and munitions testing were un- 
enforceable because they were related to defense and 
foreign policy goals. 


Peter v. United States, 6 Cl. Ct. 768, 778 (1984). This 
Court in Boyle noted the contract basis of such actions, 
Stating that the “liability may be styled one in tort, but 
it arises out of performance of the contract.” 487 U.S. at 
505. See, e.g., Bird & Sons, Inc. v. United States, 420 
F.2d 1051 (1970). Indeed, the Federal Acquisition 
Regulations (FAR) applicable to cost reimbursement con- 
tracts do not distinguish between the recoverability of a 
tort-related cost and any other cost incurred in the course 
of performing the contract. See 48 C.F.R. §§ 28.311-2 
and 52.228-7(c) (1994). 


In contrast, a complex jurisprudence has developed 
around the government’s waiver of sovereign immunity for 
tort claims. This waiver is complicated by a series of 
both statutory and judicial exceptions and by procedural 
and substantive limitations on tort recovery. For example, 


30 


the FTCA excepts: (a) tort claims that arise from the 
exercise of the government’s discretionary functions or 
duties, 28 U.S.C. § 2680(a); and (b) claims arising in 
particular areas of governmental activity, such as com- 
batant activities of the armed forces. See 28 U.S.C. 
§ 2680(j). These limitations all are designed to restrict 
the government’s tort exposure for injuries to individuals 
employed by the government who suffer from the govern- 
ment’s discretionary activities. 


The judiciary also has established certain limitations on 
the government’s liability in tort actions. Specifically, this 
Court established in Feres that the government is not 
liable under the FTCA to a member of the armed forces 
who sustains an injury while in the course and scope of 
his duties. See Feres, 340 U.S. at 135. This exception 
was broadened in Stencel to preclude third-party claims 
against the government for contribution or indemnification 
in tort for damages paid by the third party to a member 
of the armed forces injured during military service. See 
Stencel, 431 U.S. at 666. 


In Stencel, this Court reasoned that permitting a party 
to sue in tort under the Federal Tort Claims Act for 
indemnification against a tort claim brought against it by 
a member of the armed forces would circumvent Feres 
because Feres would bar a direct tort suit against the 
government by that member of the armed forces. Stencel, 
431 U.S. at 673. That reasoning, however, does not pre- 
clude a contract action against the government by a 
government contractor. For example, permitting a con- 
tract action does not offend the principal underpinning of 
the Feres doctrine, which has been stated as the need to 
avoid litigation about military discipline, supervision and 
control. See United States v. Shearer, 473 U.S. 52, 58 
(1985). 


The Feres doctrine has been heavily criticized. See 
United States v. Johnson, 481 U.S. 681, 700 (1987) 


31 


(Scalia, J. dissenting) (“Feres was wrongly decided and 
heartily deserves the ‘widespread, almost universal criti- 
cism’ it has received”) (citations omitted). This Court 
has shifted away from the Feres doctrine, most recently 
by resting the government contractor defense in Boyle 
upon federal common law, rather than the Feres doctrine. 
Boyle, 487 U.S. at 504-07. Accordingly, this Court 
should hold that the Feres doctrine does not prevent 
government contractors from suing the government for 
breach of contract under Spearin. In this way the govern- 
ment is protected from tort liability, but remains account- 
able to contractors which have a direct contractual rela- 
tionship with the government. Such contractors have no 
means other than contract of protecting themselves from 
potentially wide-ranging damage claims that ultimately 
may result from their compliance with government speci- 
fications.

C. The Anti-Deficiency Act Does Not Constitute a Bar 
to the Government’s Indemnification of Plaintiff 


In opposition to certiorari, the government argued that 
the Anti-Deficiency Act, 31 U.S.C. § 1341 (1988), sup- 
posedly bars the implied-in-fact indemnity relied upon by 
Hercules and Thompson by characterizing petitioners’ 
claim as seeking to enforce an unauthorized contractual 
provision. The Anti-Deficiency Act provides in relevant 
part: 


41 


(a)(1) An officer or employee of the United 
States Government or of the District of Columbia 
government may not— 


(A) make or authorize an expenditure or obliga- 
tion exceeding an amount available in an appropri- 
ation or fund for the expenditure or obligation; or 


(B) involve either government in a contract or 
obligation for the payment of money before an ap- 
propriation is made unless authorized by law. 


An implied-in-fact indemnity, however, falls outside the 
scope of this statute. Such an indemnity is judicially 
fashioned and is not an express contractual provision. 
An implied-in-fact indemnity arises from the powers of 
the court, not from the powers of a government officer 
or agent. The recognition and enforcement of an implied- 
in-fact indemnity therefore does not violate the Anti- 
Deficiency Act.* 


The Anti-Deficiency Act also is inapplicable here be- 
cause the contracts at issue arise from the compulsory 
powers of the DPA and also from the DPA’s provisions 
that protect contractors from third-party claims resulting 
from the exercise of those compulsory powers. What 
Congress has created by statute is enforceable without 
regard to the Anti-Deficiency Act.
