Pursuant to Rule 37 of the Rules of this Court, this brief is 
respectfully submitted, in support of the Petition, by amicus 
curiae The Business Roundtable. The consent of the parties to 
the filing of this brief has been requested. Petitioners have 
given consent; respondents have not. 


INTEREST OF THE AMICUS CURIAE 


The Business Roundtable is an association of chief execu- 
tive officers of approximately 220 major corporations repre- 
senting virtually every major business sector in the United 
States, which examines public policy issues that affect the 
economy and develops positions which seek to reflect sound 
economic and social principles. Because members of The 
Business Roundtable have substantial familiarity with class 
action setthkements—whose integrity and finality have in many 
cases been called into question by the Ninth Circuit's deci- 
sion—The Business Roundtable and its members have a sub- 
stantial interest in the Court’s resolution of the questions 
presented by the Petition. 


SUMMARY OF ARGUMENT 


Settlement, efficiency and finality are three bedrock goals 
of our civil justice system. They assume special importance 
in complex class action litigation, in which an array of claims 
may be brought on behalf of literally thousands of plaintiffs 
located throughout the country. In this context, our preference 
for settlement, the dictates of judicial economy and the need 
for finality have given rise to the widely accepted principle 
that the settlement of a class action—federal or state—binds 
all class members with respect to all claims released, so long 
as the settlement has received judicial scrutiny and comports 
with due process. 


The Ninth Circuit's decision below severely undermines 
this fundamental principle. It places an inapposite “jurisdic- 


tional” constraint on the scope of class action settlements with 
the admonition that “a court’s jurisdiction to extinguish 
claims by class settlement should not exceed its jurisdiction 
to extinguish claims by adjudication.” Epstein v. MCA, Inc., 
50 F.3d 644, 664 (9th Cir. 1995). This new constraint, how- 
ever, has no basis in the jurisprudence of this Court. To the 
contrary, parties have long been able to effectuate class action 
settlements without regard to whether the court before which 
those parties appeared would otherwise have jurisdiction to 
adjudicate the settled claims. The question is one of con- 
tract—not jurisdiction. 


The Ninth Circuit recognized that contractual principles 
govern the scope of releases with respect to “the settlement of 
traditional litigation by individual parties,” id. at 666, but 
refused to apply these principles to class actions because of 
its view that “the settlement of a class action is fundamentally 
different from the settlement of traditional litigation.” /d. The 
Ninth Circuit, however, cited no case law, statutory or rule 
authority for its determination not to apply contractual prin- 
ciples to class action settlements; nor did it argue that apply- 
ing such principles would violate due process. Instead, the 
court below simply fashioned its own law in refusing to 
enforce the release of exclusively federal claims in the state 
class action settlement before it. Moreover, in doing so, the 
Ninth Circuit ignored the fact that the principal reason offered 
for this new limitation on class action settlements—that such 
settlements are subject to judicial approval while private set- 
tlements are not—militates toward greater solicitude for the 
finality and enforcement of class action settlements, rather 
than new, unfounded constraints on their contractual scope. 


Uncorrected, the Ninth Circuit’s decision will yield serious 
and untoward consequences for both the state and federal civil 
justice system. First, the decision retroactively calls into 
question the finality and repose of any class action settlement 
that released claims over which the settling court did not have 
jurisdiction and which are not yet time-barred. It thus may 


spur a wave of new litigation over claims that were, until the 
Ninth Circuit's decision, unequivocally resolved in class 
action settlements that had received the final approval of the 
courts. Second, the decision will make the settlement of 
future class actions—both state and federal—considerably 
more difficult to achieve. Because it will often be the case 
that no court has jurisdiction over all claims and parties, 
global class action settlements will become increasingly rare. 
Further, the litigation in different forums of multiple class 
actions involving the same parties will proliferate—an 
extremely disturbing result from the standpoint of judicial 
administration and the already substantial burdens of class 
action litigation. 


This Court should grant certiorari to resolve the disabling 
uncertainty created by the Ninth Circuit's departure from 
accepted principles of settlement and class action law. If not 
reversed, the decision below will have destructive effects on 
the ability to settle not only class actions involving the fed- 
eral securities laws, but also class action settlements involv- 
ing antitrust, environmental, and numerous other federal and 
state statutes in which there is exclusive jurisdiction in only 
one group of courts. 


ARGUMENT 


I. THE NINTH CIRCUIT’S DECISION CONFLICTS 
WITH THE LONG-STANDING RULE THAT ANY 
CLAIM CAN BE CONTRACTUALLY RELEASED 
IN A LITIGATION SETTLEMENT 


In its decision below, the Ninth Circuit ruled, contrary to 
the decisions of other Circuits, that class action settlements 
cannot release claims which are not subject to the jurisdiction 
of the court approving the settlement. Epstein v. MCA, Inc., 
50 F.3d 644 (9th Cir. 1995). As a result, the Ninth Circuit 
refused to give effect to the contractual release of federal 


securities Claims in a Delaware class action settlement agree- 
ment because those claims could not have been adjudicated by 
the Delaware state court. The Ninth Circuit made this ruling 
despite the fact that of the 10,000 or more class members who 
were permitted to opt out of the settlement, only 19 chose to 
do so. The decision will thus allow those thousands of class 
members to enjoy the benefits of the Delaware class action 
settlement, but still repudiate the release of federal securities 
claims that was an unequivocal part of the quid-pro-quo for 
that settlement. Such a result, which is not based upon any 
authority, severely undermines the law of both settlements 
and class actions. '

Where Those Claims Could Not Be Adjudicated 
In That Jurisdiction

and settle any claims they choose regardless of where those 
claims are pending, or whether those claims could be adju- 
dicated in the court presiding over the settlement.’ Until the

B. The Ninth Circuit’s Announcement Of A New 
Jurisdictional Constraint On Class Action Set- 
tlements Is An Unwarranted Departure From 
Established Class Action Law 


The Court of Appeals below acknowledged the applicability 
of the above-stated principles to individual settlements and 
releases, but then went on to hold—without citing any sup- 
porting authority—that class action settlements could not be 
similarly enforced. 50 F.3d at 666-67. Specifically, the Court 
of Appeals stated that because class members are not all pre- 
sent before the court and judicial approval is required of a 
class action settlement, such settlements could not include 
claims that were beyond the jurisdiction of the approving 
court to adjudicate. /d. at 667. The Roundtable believes that 
this reasoning—which is not based on any case, statutory or 
rule authority, or principle of due process—is flawed. 


Throughout its history, the principal purposes of the class 
action mechanism have remained the same: to promote “the 
efficiency and economy of litigation.” General Tel. Co. of 
Southwest v. Falcon, 457 U.S. 147, 159 (1982); American 
Pipe & Constr. Co. v. Utah, 414 U.S. 538, 553, reh'g denied, 
415 U.S. 952 (1974); 1 Herbert Newberg & Alba Conte, New- 
berg on Class Actions § 1.01 at 1-3 (3d ed. 1992), By com- 
bining similarly situated litigants into one class, and 
permitting settlement, a class action can “achieve economies 
of time, effort, and expense, and promote uniformity of deci- 
sion as to persons similarly situated. . . .” Fed, R, Civ, P. 23 
Advisory Committee Notes to 1966 Amendment, United 
States Parole Com. v. Geraghty, 445 U.S. 388, 403 (1980).’ 
Because class actions are designed to facilitate settlements on 
a large scale and conserve judicial resources, the importance 
of the release as a final and binding contract is even more

important in the class action context than it is for individual 
lawsuits. See, e.g. Air Lines Stewards & Stewardesses Ass'n

It is true, as the Ninth Circuit points out, that class action 
settlements are different from individual settlements in that 
additional procedural protections, including the requirement 
of court approval, are provided to satisfy due process con- 
cerns. 50 F.3d at 667. However, that difference makes it more, 
not less, appropriate to enforce the release of al/ claims dis- 
charged as part of a judicially approved class action settle- 
ment, Indeed, to comport with due process, members of a 
class are provided with a host of procedural protections to 
ensure that any class action settlement is reasonable and 
fair—protections not afforded in non-class actions. See gen- 
erally Phillips Petroleum, 472 U.S. at 808-14. In the class at 
issue here, these protections included the right to opt out of 
the class action settlement—a right which only 19 class mem- 
bers out of thousands decided to utilize. It can hardly be said 
that it is unfair to require the remaining class members who 
chose to accept the benefits of the settlement to be bound by 
the release of securities law claims which was an express and 
integral part of that settlement. 


The federal and state rules governing class actions, plus the 
requirements of due process set forth by this Court in Phillips 
Petroleum, establish the proper limits of class action settle- 
ments. The Ninth Circuit improperly imposed its own addi- 
tional limitation without any authority for doing so.

If not corrected, the Ninth Circuit's decision may well lead 
to a deluge of litigants seeking to re-open class action claims 
that were finally and conclusively settled long ago. For, as 
long as the statute of limitations has not run on any class 
claims which are beyond the approving court's jurisdiction, 
class members would be free to repudiate any release of those 
claims included in a class action settlement. This would be 
true not only as to securities law class actions, but also as to 


numerous other class action settlements in which the approv- 
ing state court did not have jurisdiction to adjudicate the 
exclusively federal claims being released.’ 


In all previous class action settlements, the parties have 
properly relied upon the fact that once the settlement received 
final judicial approval, all settled claims were released— 
whether or not those claims were within the jurisdiction of the 
approving court to adjudicate. This substantial reliance inter- 
est will be destroyed if the decision below stands and class 
members are permitted to accept the benefits of a class action 
settlement without being bound to the release of claims that 
was the quid-pro-quo for that settlement. 


Further, the decision below will have the additional harm- 
ful effect of making it more difficult to settle class actions in 
the future. Under the jurisdictional constraint announced by 
the Ninth Circuit, class action representatives often will be 
unable to release a// of their claims in a single settlement. To 
the contrary, whenever there is any chance that an exclusively 
federal claim could be brought against the defendants by the 


_ Class, it will be impossible for the class action representatives 


to settle that claim in state court as part of an overall settle- 
ment. Similarly, a class action brought in federal court will 
not be able to release all claims of the class unless a deter- 
mination is made that the federal court has subject matter 
jurisdiction over all of the claims being released. For exam- 
ple, a class action settlement in federal court would not be 
able to release any state law claim that does not meet the 
requirements of federal diversity or supplemental jurisdic- 
tion.” Indeed, in some cases, it is possible that no one court in
