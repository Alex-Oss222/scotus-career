STATEMENT OF INTEREST OF AMICUS CURIAE 


The Chamber of Commerce of the United States of 
America (“the Chamber”) is the largest association of 
businesses, companies and associations in the world. 
With substantial membership in each of the fifty states, 
the Chamber represents more than 220,000 companies, 
trade and professional associations, and state and local 
Chambers of Commerce. It is the principal voice of the 
American business community. An important function 
of the Chamber is to represent the interests of its mem- 
bers in critical issues before the Supreme Court. The 


2 


Chamber seeks to advance those interests by filing briefs 
in cases of importance to the business community. 
Among the Chamber’s constituencies is a core of busi- 
nesses which contract with the United States. 


The decision of the United States Court of Appeals 
for the Federal Circuit below has serious adverse impli- 
cations for large and small corporations alike, because 
it would allow the government to avoid the consequences 
of its actions when it mandates production of potentially 
hazardous materials and products in support of the na- 
tional defense and other government-sponsored objectives. 
The Federal Circuit’s view of governmental responsibility 
for government actions, if upheld, would thrust upon pri- 
vate parties liability for doing the government’s will. The 
Chamber believes it is important for this Court to recog- 
nize the significance of this issue to the business com- 
munity. 


Permitting tort liability which is the direct and proxi- 
mate result of government requirements ultimately to be 
imposed on government contractors raises serious legal 
and constitutional concerns, since this liability is neither 
bargained for nor avoidable. In the Chamber's view, 
such liability is wholly inconsistent with the overarching 
legal principle that control over a contract equals re- 
sponsibility for its consequences. It is also inconsistent 
with existing doctrines protecting government contractors 
from contractual and third-party tort liability for actions 
dictated by the government through mandatory contracts, 
and specifically inconsistent with the expressed will of 
Congress embodied in Section 707 of the Defense Pro- 
duction Act of 1950. 50 U.S.C. app. §§ 2061-2171 
(1988 & Supp. V 1993) (the “DPA”). The Federal 
Circuit’s crabbed reading of contract law and the govern- 
ment contractor defense is inconsistent with applicable 
decisions of this Court, and should therefore be reversed.’

STATEMENT OF THE CASE 


The facts in this action are set forth in greater detail 
in petitioners’ brief and in the record below. However, 
several facts are important to understand the nature of 
the Chamber’s concerns regarding the Federal Circuit’s 
view of the government contractor defense and the DPA. 


During the Vietnam War, the government ordered the 
petitioners to produce Agent Orange. While the peti- 
tioners had produced herbicides, they had not produced 
defoliants and were not fully informed of the potential 
dangers of Agent Orange, which they produced according 
to governmental fiat and government specifications. The 
government did not disclose the risks of Agent Orange 
to the petitioners. 


In the late 1970s, petitioners (and other Agent Orange 
manufacturers) were sued by a class of Vietnam veterans. 
The class plaintiffs sought damages for health problems 
allegedly caused by exposure to Agent Orange.

Faced with what the district court later termed “a 
possibility of an ultimate finding of liability with claims 
totalling billions of dollars,” In Re Agent Orange Product 
Liability Litigation, 597 F. Supp. 740, 749 (FE.D.N.Y. 
1984), aff'd, 818 F.2d 145 (2d Cir. 1987). cert. denied, 
484 U.S. 1004 (1988), the petitioners (and other Agent 


4 


Orange manufacturers) settled the class action at a cost 
to them of approximately $22 million (of a total settle- 
ment of $180 million). 


Petitioners sought recovery of the settlhement amount 
from the government in the Claims Court under the 
doctrine of United States v. Spearin, 248 U.S. 132 
(1918), alleging unsuccessfully that the government had 
breached its warranty of specifications by ordering the 
manufacture of Agent Orange. The Claims Court also 
rejected petitioners’ argument that they were entitled to 
relief under Section 707 of the DPA, which protects gov- 
ernment contractors from liability for “damages . . . for 
any act or failure to act” resulting from DPA compli- 
ance. 50 U.S.C. app. § 2157 (1988). 


The Federal Circuit, over the vigorous dissent of 
Judge Plager, affirmed the dismissal of petitioners’ Spearin 
claims, holding that the government contractor defense 
would have provided petitioners an absolute defense to 
liability, and that therefore no damages were “caused” by 
the government. Hercules, Inc. v. United States, 24 F.3d 
188, 200 (Fed. Cir. 1994). 


The Federal Circuit also denied recovery under the 
DPA, holding that relief available under the DPA is 
limited to costs caused by giving priority to DPA con- 
tracts over private contracts. /d. at 202-04. 


In dissent, Judge Plager argued that no one could have 
determined what the outcome of the application of the 
government contractor defense would have been. /d. at 
207. Likewise, he attacked the majority’s interpretation 
of the DPA as a “non sequitur” that ignored the lan- 
guage of the statute. /d. at 209-10.

SUMMARY OF ARGUMENT 


1. The history of the DPA demonstrates that Con- 
gress, recognizing the government’s responsibility for the 
consequences of producing material for defense of the 
nation, intended to indemnify contractors. Section 707 
of the DPA indemnifies the petitioners for the conse- 
quences of contracts entered into pursuant to the DPA, 
including damages in tort. 


2. The Federal Circuit’s misapplication of Section 707 
is wholly inconsistent with existing protections for gov- 
ernment contractors. The government contractor defense 
enunciated by this Court in Boyle shields government 
contractors from third-party tort liability for work per- 
formed pursuant to government contract specifications. 
The Spearin doctrine, which protects government con- 
tractors from the effects of faulty or harmful government 
contract specifications, provides a separate basis for the 
protection of government contractors performing the gov- 
ernment’s will. The Federal Circuit’s analysis of Section 
707 clashes with the reasoning of these important legal 
doctrines. 


3. A review of closely-related, analogous settings 
within the law illustrates a fundamental principle: when 
a dominant party controls the terms, conditions, and 
method of performance in a contract of adhesion, it as- 
sumes responsibility for such control. In the law of 
adhesion contracts, control over a contract carries greater 
imposition of responsibility for its consequences. In the 
developing law of environmental liability, overweening 
government control over activities causing pollution 
also creates governmental liability. 


4. The government compelled petitioners to produce 
Agent Orange and had exclusive power over all aspects 
of the contracts. As a matter of policy and economics, 
the government should not be allowed to escape its duty 
to indemnify the contractors. 


6 


ARGUMENT 


This case concerns whether and to what extent the 
federal government may avoid liability for contracts 
which it has imposed upon private entities. Affirming the 
decision below would allow the government to escape 
the consequences of compelling petitioners to produce 
Agent Orange, which the government knew was poten- 
tially hazardous, dictating its content and labeling, and 
knowingly exposing service personnel to that substance. 


Concomitantly, this case concerns the protections af- 
forded to government contractors performing important 
and sometimes hazardous public policy goals. The protec- 
tions afforded to government contractors are broader than 
the limited and internally inconsistent view of govern- 
ment contract indemnity adopted by the Federal Circuit. 


While it is well-settled after this Court’s determination 
in Spearin that the government warrants specifications 
it imposes in contracts, the Federal Circuit found that 
Spearin did not extend to indemnify the petitioners (which 
manufactured Agent Orange to government specifications ) 
for the tort claims against them. Likewise, the court of 
appeals found that DPA Section 707 does not protect a 
contractor from tort liability caused by use of dangerous 
government specifications. The Federal Circuit then com- 
pounded its error by asserting that petitioners “voluntar- 
ily” failed to invoke the government contractor defense. 
None of these holdings survives scrutiny. 


In sum, this case involves the misapplication of three 
doctrines that should work integrally to shield from liabil- 
ity government contractors doing the government's bid- 
ding: Boyle, which shields government contractors from 
third-party tort claims thorough a “shared immunity” 
with the government; Spearin, which provides an affirma- 
tive remedy to protect contractors from defective govern- 
__ment specifications; and Section 707 of the DPA, which 
mandates that a-contractor-shal-not—be—liable_for “dam- 
ages .. . for any act or failure to act resulting directly 
or indirectly from compliance with a rule, regulation, or 


7 


order issued pursuant to this Act.” These protections all 
have application in a setting in which the government 
maintains near-complete control over a contract of ad- 
hesion. Consistent with general contract principles, and 
legal principles in analogous cases involving the govern- 
ment, these protections place responsibility on the con- 
trolling party—the government. 


The Federal Circuit failed to address the integral na- 
ture of these defenses, and held that the DPA provides 
no protection to the petitioners. As a result, government 
contractors are left defenseless when the government ef- 
fectively seizes control of a private entity, then disclaims 
responsibility for the consequences of its seizure. 


I. THE DEFENSE PRODUCTION ACT INDEMNIFIES 
THE PETITIONERS 


As a concomitant of the broad powers it gives the gov- 
ernment, the DPA provides contractors express protec- 
tion from the consequences of compelled government 
contracts. This is clear from the history of the DPA and 
the language of the Act itself. Nevertheless, the Federal 
Circuit claimed to harmonize DPA Sections 101 and 707 
when it stated that Section 707 provides no basis for 
indemnifying contractors for tort liability. The court of 
appeals cited no direct authority for this view, which con- 
flicts with the text and the history of the DPA. To the 
contrary, the history of the DPA itself, as well as its in- 
terrelationship with the law concerning the government 
contractor defense and the contract protection against 
defective specifications, provide persuasive authority that 
the government must be responsible for the consequences 
of its compelled contracts. 


A. History of the Defense Production Act 
1. Historical Development 


Grants of immunity and indemnity against third-party 
liability by the United States to its contractors are per- 
mitted by the authority conferred in the Constitution to 


8 


enter into contracts* and to exercise “War Powers.” * 
When these two powers have fused, those who contract 
with the government for defense-related goods are pro- 
tected from the consequences of the government's exer- 
cise of its political responsibilities,* particularly when the 
government has compelled the production. 


Since before the First World War, Congress found it 
necessary to authorize the executive branch to compel 
production of goods. Congress recognized that contrac- 
tors must be immunized or indemnified from liabilty 
which might grow out of less-than-voluntary contract per- 
formance.’ Indeed, Congress has on other occasions also 
recognized the need to indemnify contractors facing per- 
formance risks. See, e.g., 10 U.S.C. $2354 (1994) 
(providing indemnification for contracts involving haz- 
ardous activities). 


During the World War I era, Congress granted extra- 
ordinary powers to the executive branch to facilitate mili- 
tary mobilization. The National Defense Act of 1916 
empowered the President in time of war or imminent war 
to place orders for products and materials required by 
the Army.“ A similar act supported Navy procurement.’ | 
Compliance with these Acts took precedence over any 
other contract, and it was plain that these laws were in- 
tended to transform the normal process of commercial 
contracting into compelled undertakings. Neither act di- 
rectly protected contractors from third-party liability:

2. World War Il 


The contracting powers granted to the President during 
World War I expired at the cessation of hostilities. Thus, 
in 1940, when the situation in Europe required that the 
United States again mobilize for war, the President's 
special procurement powers were limited. 


The National Defense Act of 1940 empowered the 
President, for two years, to require that contractors give 
priority to Army and Navy contracts or orders,’ and was 
later amended to give the executive authority to require 
contractors to give priority to orders for materials deemed 
vital to the national defense." Congress also expressly 
immunized contractors from liability for breach of con- 
tract damages resulting from the performance of priority 
orders." The Selective Training and Service Act of 
1940 * gave the President authority to order necessary

A. Contracts of Adhesion Are Construed Against the 
Dominant Drafting Party 
It is fundamental to contract theory and interpretation 
that the parties to an agreement must have the freedom to 
initiate and negotiate. Such freedom is necessary to bind 
the parties to the terms of a contract. In this way, the law 
gives binding effect to the inalienable right of individual 
liberty. At its core, a binding contract is a private, mutual 
matter between parties who are free to negotiate terms 
and conditions. 


Contracts of adhesion are generally not private affairs, 
but reflect a social institution where standard forms are 
used among parties of unequal bargaining power on a 
take-it-or-leave-it basis. These contracts are not the result 
of free bargaining. Accordingly, contracts of adhesion are 
not viewed through the lens of the traditional common- 
law rules of contract. See generally Todd Rakoff, Con- 
tracts of Adhesion: An Essay in Reconstruction, 96 Harv. 
L. Rev. 1173 (1983). Instead, their terms are subject 
to judicial scrutiny for reasonableness and “fundamental 
fairness.” Carnival Cruise Lines v. Shute, 499 U.S. 585, 
595 (1991).

Where contracting parties are freely allowed to nego-

reasonable expectations. Parties may allocate the risk

In the instant case, however, as with most adhesion 
contracts, the contractors did not have the option of 
adjusting the price for greater security. In fact, the 
contract prohibited the petitioners from protecting them-

the contractors were not allowed to place warning labels

counterpart drafts a contract of adhesion exculpating him-

a party with the control over a contract to draft the

III. THE GOVERNMENT HAS ATTEMPTED TO AVOID 
LIABILITY FOR THE ENVIRONMENTAL CONSE- 
QUENCES OF ITS WARTIME CONTRACTS 


The instant case is not the only one in which the gov- 
ernment has sought to avoid responsibility for the con- 
sequences of contracts forced upon contractors. Courts 
have found responsibility, and therefore liability, based 
upon control over the contracting process in the context 
of environmental law. In several cases, the government 
has sought to avoid responsibility for its actions under 
the Comprehensive Environmental Response, Compensa- 
tion, and Liability Act (“CERCLA”), 42 U.S.C. § 9607 
(1988), just as it has under the DPA; see Elf Atochem 
N. Am., Inc. v. United States, 868 F. Supp. 707, 712- 
13 (E.D. Pa. 1994) (rejecting government’s contention 
that World War II era pollution at chemical plant could 
not implicate the government under CERCLA; cleanup 
liability imposed ). 


CERCLA imposes liability upon those who have the 
actual responsibility for, or in some cases, the mere au- 
thority to control, disposal of hazardous waste. See gen- 
erally 42 U.S.C. § 9607; compare Sidney S. Arst Co. v. 
Pipefitters Welfare Educ. Fund, 25 F.3d 417, 421 (7th 
Cir. 1994) (plaintiff must allege defendant “directly and 
personally” engaged in the acts causing environmental 
damage) with Nurad, Inc. v. William E. Hooper & Sons, 
966 F.2d 837, 842 (4th Cir.) (individuals “need not 
have exercised actual control in order to qualify as op- 
erators under § 9607(a)(2), so long as the authority to

control the facility was present”), cert. denied, 113 S. Ct. 
377 (1992).” 


The government’s attempts to avoid owner-opera’ © 
liability under CERCLA are highlighted in FMC Corp. 
v. United States Department of Commerce, 29 F.3d 833 
(3d Cir. 1994). FMC involved World War II contracts 
for the production of synthetic rubber to aid the United 
States war effort. The production took place at plants 
over which the government exerted considerable control. 
As in the instant case, the government used its wartime 
powers to compel production. FMC Corp. v. United 
States Dep't of Commerce, 786 F. Supp. 471, 474-75 
(E.D. Pa. 1992). 


Years later, the Environmental Protection Agency 
sought to recover cleanup costs at the plant from a sub- 
sequent owner, FMC Corporation. FMC sought contri- 
bution from the government under CERCLA, claiming 
the hazardous waste was due to wartime government 
contracts. 


In a divided en banc opinion, the Third Circuit held 
that the government had waived sovereign immunity to 
liability under CERCLA, and that given the extent of 
government control over the facility, it shared cleanup 
liability. The court concluded “the leading indicia of 
control were present, as the government determined what 
product the facility would produce, the level of produc- 
tion, the price of the product, and to whom the product 
would be sold.” FMC, 29 F.3d at 843. 


In responding to the government’s claim that a finding 
of governmental liability would result in costs far in ex- 
cess of anything Congress could have intended under 
CERCLA, the FMC court noted, “we point out that at 
bottom our result simply places a cost of the war on the 
United States, and thus on society as a whole, a result 
which is neither untoward nor inconsistent with the policy

IV. PUBLIC POLICY REQUIRES THE GOVERNMENT 
TO BEAR THE CONSEQUENCES OF ITS OWN 
DECISIONS 


Under the War Powers and Defense Production Acts, 
the government has extraordinary power to compel con- 
tractor compliance with public demands. Because of these 
powers, the freedom to negotiate is missing. As the Fed- 
eral Circuit itself recognizes, “the Government routinely 
grants itself privileges—like the right to terminate a con- 
tract for the Government’s convenience without penalty— 
that are not available to other contracting parties.” S.J. 
Amoroso Constr. Co. v. United States, 12 F.3d 1072, 
1079 (Fed. Cir. 1993) (Plager, J., concurring). There 
can be no pretense that the contracts at issue were bar- 
gained for and executed with equal bargaining power on 
both sides—rather, the law and the facts surrounding 
them suggest they were in fact contracts of adhesion. 


Fundamental fairness requires that the government not 
be permitted to escape liability for damages suffered by 
contractors through third-party tort actions arising directly 
out of obligations imposed because of such contracts.” 
The rulings below wrongly insulate the government from 
the consequences of its conduct. The contractors reason- 
ably expected to be protected from liability that arose 
through the contracts—by the Spearin doctrine for con- 
tract claims, by the Boyle government contractor defense 
for third-party tort claims, and by DPA Section 707 for 
other liabilities incurred in contract performance. These 
reasonable expectations are being frustrated by the gov- 
ernment’s attempt to avoid responsibility for the conse- 
quences of actions it required the petitioners to take. 
Petitioners had to produce what the government wanted, 
according to the government’s terms. The petitioners 
effectively lost the right to their physical plant, the choice

In sum, that the government may not force private 
entities to bear the costs of public benefits is basic to

To view Section 707 as permitting the government to 
force liability, as well as production, upon a contractor is 
inconsistent with its historical meaning and with other 
doctrines protecting contractors, and offends basic notions 
of the sanctity of property. For constitutional as well as 
legal reasons, the Federal Circuit's decision that the DPA 
protects a contractor only against third-party breach of 
contract claims is unsound.
