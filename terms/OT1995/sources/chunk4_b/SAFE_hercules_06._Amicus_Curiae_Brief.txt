QUESTIONS PRESENTED 


1. Whether Section 707 of the Defense Production Act 
of 1950 (the “DPA”), 50 U.S.C. app. § 2157, which 
mandates that a contractor shall not be liable for “dam- 
ages . . . for any act or failure to act” resulting from 
compliance with orders under that Act, shields govern- 
ment contractors from damages incurred as a result of 
mandatory work ordered to be performed by the govern- 
ment. 


2. Whether the Federal Circuit’s limited view of the 
indemnification provided by Section 707 is fundamentally 
inconsistent with the government contractor defense cre- 
ated by this Court in Boyle v. United Technologies Corp., 
487 U.S. 500 (1988), which protects government con- 
tractors from third-party tort claims, and the protections 
afforded in United States v. Spearin, 248 U.S. 132 
(1918), which also protecis contractors from the results 
of faulty government specifications.

STATEMENT OF INTEREST OF AMICUS CURIAE 


The Chamber of Commerce of the United States of 
America (“the Chamber”) is the largest federal associa- 
tion of businesses, companies and associations in the 
world. With substantial membership in each of the fifty 
states, the Chamber represents more than 215,000 busi- 
nesses and organizations. It is the principal voice of the 
American business community. An important function of 
the Chamber is to represent the interests of its members 
in critical issues before the Supreme Court of the United 
States. The Chamber seeks to advance those interests by 
filing briefs in cases of importance to the business com- 
munity. Among the Chamber’s constituencies is a core 
of businesses which contract with the United States. 


The decision of the United States Court of Appeals for 
the Federal Circuit in the instant case has serious adverse

implications for large and small corporations alike. The 
decision below is of great concern to the Chamber’s mem- 
bers in that it allows the government to escape liability 
for the consequences of its actions in entering into manda- 
tory contracts for hazardous materials which are critical 
to the implementation of important public policy goals, 
and thrusts upon private parties the costs of its actions. 
The Chamber believes it is important for this Court to 
recognize the significance of this issue to the business 
community.

intended to indemnify contractors for the consequences of 
producing material for use in defense of the nation. Sec- 
tion 707 of the DPA indemnifies the Petitioners for the

DPA, including damages in tort. 


2. The Federal Circuit’s misapplication of Section 707 
is wholly inconsistent with the government contractor de- 
fense enunciated by this Court in Boyle v. United Tech-

REASONS FOR GRANTING THE WRIT 


The petition at bar concerns the protections afforded to 
government contractors performing important though 
sometimes hazardous public policy goals. Concomitantly, 
the petition concerns the extent to which the federal gov- 
ernment can avoid liability for contracts which it has 
imposed upon private entities. The protections afforded 
to government contractors are broader than the limited 
and internally inconsistent view of government contract 
indemnity adopted by the Federal Circuit. 


The decision below would allow the government to 
escape the consequences of having compelled petitioners 
to produce a material, Agent Orange, that the government 
knew was potentially hazardous, and then knowingly ex- 
posing service personnel to that material. 


While it is well-settled after this Court’s determination 
in Spearin that the government warrants the specifications 
which it imposes in contracts, the Federal Circuit found 
that Spearin did not extend to indemnify the petitioners 
for the tort claims against it, and that Section 707 pro- 
tected the contractor only from additional costs or delays 
imposed by government contract, not from the risk of 
injury to third parties caused by use of dangerous govern- 
ment specifications. The Federal Circuit then compounded 
this error by asserting that tiie petitioners “voluntarily” 
failed to invoke the government contract defense. None 
of these decisions will survive scrutiny. 


In sum, this case involves the misapplication of three 
protections that should work integrally to shield from 
liability government contractors doing the government's 
work. Boyle, which shields government contractors from 
third party tort claims, Spearin, which protects contractors 


4 


from defective government specifications, and the DPA, 
which mandates that a contractor shall not be liable for 
“damages . . . for any act or failure to act” resulting from 
compliance with orders under that Act, all have similar 
tests and similar justifications. The Federal Circuit failed 
to address the integral nature of these defenses, limited 
protection under the DPA, and, at the urging of the gov- 
ernment, held that the DPA provides no protection to the 
petitioners. 


I. THE DEFENSE PRODUCTION ACT INDEMNIFIES 
THE PETITIONERS 


Claiming to read the plain language of the DPA and 
to harmonize Sections 101 and 707 of the DPA, the 
Federal Circuit stated that Section 707 did not indemnify 
contractors for tort liability. The lower court cited no 
direct authority for its view that Section 707 does not 
extend such protection to contractors, and its view is in 
conflict with the text and the history of the DPA. 


A. History of the Defense Production Act 


1. Historical Development 


Grants of immunity and indemnity against third party 
liability by the United States to its contractors are per- 
mitted by the authority conferred in the Constitution to 
enter into contracts* end to exercise “war powers.” * 
When these two powers have fused, it has been necessary 
to protect those who contract with the government for 
defense-related goods from the consequences of the gov- 
ernment’s exercise of its political policy responsibilities,‘ 
particularly when the government has compelled produc- 
tion to meet war efforts. 


Since before the First World War, Congress found it 
necessary to authorize the executive branch to compel 
production of goods. Congress recognized that contrac-

C. Section 707 Immunizes Contractors From Liability 
for Damages in Tort or Contract 


By its plain language, Section 707 shields a contractor 
from liability for “damages . . . for any act or failure 
to act” resulting from compliance with the DPA. 


In statutory construction cases, “when a statute speaks 
with clarity to an issue judicial inquiry into the statute’s 
meaning, in all but the most extraordinary circumstance, 
is finished.” Estate of Cowart v. Nicklos Drilling Co., 
112 S. Ct. 2589, 2594 (1992). The lower court claimed 
to be reading this plain language when it denied recovery 
to the petitioners. Not so; it construed Section 707 in a 
manner that denies its plain meaning. 


By its language, Section 707 immunizes a contractor 
from all liability resulting from compelled production, 
whether arising in contract, tort, or otherwise. 


There is no indication that Section 707 was intended 
to excuse only liability under contractual defaults. Indeed, 
Congress rejected the explicit “liable . . . for any default 
under any contract” language of the predecessor in favor 
of the broader “liable . . . for any act or failure to act”

language of Section 707, providing powerful evidence to 
the contrary. Had Congress intended such a limitation it 
could very easily and simply have adopted the prior lan-

Second, the references to Section 707 in the DPA’s 
legislative history are wholly consistent with the proposi- 
tion that the immunity language applies to all damages or 
claims, whatever the legal basis. The House report stated: 
“This section [707] provides an exemption from damages 
or penalties which might result from compliance with 
rules, regulations, or orders issued under title I.” Simi- 
larly, the relevant Senate report stated: “Section 707 con- 
tains an exculpatory clause protecting persons who have 
complied with the act against liability for damages there- 
after.” ** When Congress re-examined Section 707 in 
1952, it stated that Section 707 “protects a person from 
liability, under contract or otherwise, as the result of com- 
pliance with the act or regulations issued under the act.” ™ 


In sum, the plain meaning of the provision, its legisla- 
tive history, and the historical context in which it was 
enacted all demonstrate that Congress indeed intended 
Section 707 to immunize contractors from all damages 
resulting from compelled production.

As stated previously, the other shields involved in this 
case are the government contractor defense set forth in 
Boyle and the warranty of adequate specifications eluci- 
dated in Spearin. Those defenses, which involve nearly 
identical elements, protect the government contractor from 
third-party tort claims and from liabilities incurred due to 
reliance on government contract specifications. Section 
707, which also is geared to protecting government con- 
tractors performing mandated services for the government, 
should be construed similarly. When properly integrated, 
these three defenses provide freedom from third party tort 
liability, indemnification for reliance on government spe- 
cifications, and, under the DPA, indemnification from all 
liabilities incurred as a result of the contract performance. 
The opinion of the Federal Circuit, however, diverted 
from the logic of this trio of government contractor pro- 
tections to hold that the DPA will not indemnify contrac- 
tors for incurred liabilities. See Hercules, Inc. v. United 
States, 24 F.3d 188, 204 (Fed. Cir. 1994). This decision 
is not only inconsistent with the DPA’s text and history, 
but with Boyle and Spearin. 


In Boyle, this Court held that a contractor manufactur- 
ing products for the government is not liable in tort to 
third parties if: 

(1) the United States approved reasonably precise 
specifications; (2) the equipment conformed to those 
specifications; and (3) the supplier warned the 
United States about the dangers in the use of the 
equipment that were known to the supplier but not 
to the United States. 


12 


487 U.S. at 512." These three elements are simply ap- 
proval, conformance, and relative knowledge. 


The first two elements of the test tie the production of 
the product to the government’s demands. “[A] product 
conforms to reasonably precise specifications if it satisfies 
an intended configuration even if it may produce unin- 
tended and unwanted results.” Kleeman v. McDonnell 
Douglas Corp., 890 F.2d 698, 703 (4th Cir. 1989) 
(quotations omitted), cert. denied, 495 U.S. 953 (1990). 
The contractor satisfies the third element by proving the 
government knew of (or at least the contractor did not 
conceal) a danger. Boyle, 487 U.S. at 512; Ramey v. 
Martin-Baker Aircraft Co., 874 F.2d 946, 951 n.10 (4th 
Cir. 1989). 


The Spearin test is similar. In Spearin, this Court stated 
that, “if the contractor is bound to build according to 
plans and specifications prepared by the owner, the con- 
tractor will not be responsible for the consequences of 
defects in the plans and specifications.” 248 U.S. at 136. 
To succeed on an implied warranty claim, the contractor 
must show: (1) government specifications, (2) with 
which the contractor complied, and (3) unsatisfactory 
result. R.C. Hedreen Co., ASBCA No. 20,599, 77-1 
BCA 4 12,328, at 59,555. The contractor must show also 
that the government had superior knowledge of the defec- 
tive specification. Johns-Manville Corp. v. United States, 
13 Cl. Ct. 72, 118-20 (1987), vacated on other grounds, 
855 F.2d 1571 (Fed. Cir. 1988).

ment specifications. In Yearsley v. Ross, 309 U.S. 18,

Ill. THE FEDERAL CIRCUITS INTERPRETATION OF 
SECTION 707 WOULD PERMIT THE GOVERN- 
MENT TO IMPOSE ONEROUS CONTRACTS FOR 
HAZARDOUS PRODUCTS ON CONTRACTORS, 
WHILE FORCING THE CONTRACTORS TO BEAR 
THE CONSEQUENCES OF THESE DECISIONS 


While this appeal does not question the constitutional 
appropriateness of the War Powers or Defense Production 
Acts, it must be noted that these acts give the government 
extraordinary power to compel contractor compliance with 
executive demands. Because of these powers, the freedom 
to negotiate, a crucial aspect of traditional contract theory 
and interpretation, is missing. As the Federal Circuit it- 
self recognizes, “the Government routinely grants itself 
privileges—like the right to terminate a contract for the 
Government’s convenience without penalty—that are not 
available to other contracting parties.” S.J. Amoroso 
Constr. Co. v. United States, 12 F.3d 1072, 1079 (Fed. 
Cir. 1993) (Plager, J., concurring). There can be no 
pretense that contracts with the government are bargained 
for and executed with equal bargaining power on both 
sides, and it is well accepted that they are in fact contracts 
of adhesion. 


15 


It is clear that the government has the power to impose 
contracts entirely against the will of a private party, or, at 
best, on a take-it-or-leave-it basis. Such contracts cannot 
be viewed through the lens of traditional common-law 
rules, since “such a contract is ‘sold not bought.’ The one 
party dictates its provisions; the other has no more choice 
in fixing those terms than he has about the weather.” 
Schwartz v. §.S. Nassau, 345 F.2d 465, 471 (2d Cir.) 
(quotation omitted), cert. denied, 382 U.S. 919 (1965); 
see also Todd Rakoff, Contracts of Adhesion: An Essay 
in Reconstruction, 96 Harv. L. Rev. 1173 (1983). 


The Agent Orange contracts were compulsory, not vol- 
untary. The government drafted the contracts pursuant to 
the DPA and supplied all specifications. The contractors 
had no bargaining power at all. Indeed, the contracts at 
issue cannot even be said to have been let merely on a 
take-it-or-leave-it basis, but on a “take-it-or-go-to-jail” 
basis. The contractor could not refuse to produce Agent 
Orange, demand to produce a safer defoliant, or make one 
that it felt was more effective. Petitioners had to produce 
what the government demanded not through negotiation, 
but submission.” 


This enormous disparity in contracting power illustrates 
the rationale for the indemnity provision of Section 707. 
Where contracting parties negotiate terms and reach a 
meeting of the minds, they may allocate the risk (and the 
price) of consequential damages. 


In the instant case, however, the contractors had no 
such option. They were not able to negotiate liability is- 
sues because they were ordered to produce what the gov- 
ernment demanded. See Hercules, 24 F.3d at 190. More- 
over, the potential danger of Agent Orange was known 
solely to the government. The contractors were making 
a product, under government order, with unknown dan-

IV. THE GOVERNMENT'S ACTIONS ARE PART OF A 
PATTERN TO AVOID LIABILITY FOR THE CON- 
SEQUENCES OF ITS WARTIME CONTRACTS 


In the instant case, the government has sought to avoid 
indemnifying the petitioners under contracts it imposed 
pursuant to the DPA. While this may seem inconsistent 
with the government’s current position that contractors are 
immune from government contract liability under Boyle, 
it is consistent with the government’s attempts, in this 
forum and in many others, to avoid the costs of its past 
decisions, costs which in many cases are only now be- 
coming clear.” Now that the government is realizing the 
cost implications of its compulsory contracts for hazard- 
ous materials critical to various defense efforts, it has 
sought to shift these costs of its policies to the private 
entities which were compelled to place these policies into 
effect.

policy on the government. The government faces the same 
threat, and has reacted similarly, in other contexts. For 
example, in the environmental context, the government
