STATUTORY PROVISION INVOLVED 


Section 1738 of Title 28 provides with respect to the authen- 
ticated acts, records and judicial proceedings of any State: 

Such Acts, records and judicial proceedings, or copies 
thereof, so authenticated, shall have the same full faith 
and credit in every court within the United States and 

its Territories and Possessions as they have by law or 
usage in the courts of such State, Territory or Posses- 
sion from which they are taken. 


STATEMENT OF THE CASE 


The Delaware Chancery Court approved an “opt out” set- 
tlement of a class action arising out of Matsushita's 1990 ac- 
quisition of MCA. The Chancery Court entered a judgment on 
the settlement that incorporated a release of all claims relating 
to the acquisition, including the exclusively federal securities 
law claims asserted here by ts. The Delaware Su- 
preme Court affirmed the judgment. The Ninth Circuit re- 
fused to give full faith and credit to the judgment on the 

that “a court’s jurisdiction to extinguish claims by 
class settlement should not exceed its jurisdiction to extin- 
guish claims by adjudication.” Epstein v. MCA INC., 50 F.3d 
644, 664 (9th Cir. 1995) (reprinted at Pet. App. la) (emphasis 
added). 

The Ninth Circuit decision nullified the state court judg- 
ment and freed the class members from their obligations un- 
der the court-approved contract of settlement. The Ninth Circuit 
thus countenanced a collateral attack on the state judgment by 
respondents who neither objected to nor opted out of the state 
court settlement. The Ninth Circuit decision undermines fun- 
damental interests of federalism and the fostering of settle- 
ments, encourages the type of inefficient litigation strategies 
adopted by respondents, and forces complex class action liti- 
gations into the federal courts.

29, 1990. The acquisition involved a tender offer to the public

SUMMARY OF THE ARGUMENT 


The Delaware court approved the consensual settlement of 
a class action properly before it. In so doing, it exercised the 
established power of a court to approve a settlement of all 
claims arising from the controversy before it, including ex- 
clusively federal claims. Contrary to the Ninth Circuit's 
analysis, a class action settlement, like any other settlement, is 
a private contractual agreement between the parties upon 
which a judgment can be entered. When a state court enters a 
judgment upon a settlement, no legitimate federal interest is 
implicated that can justify intrusion by a federal court on state 
judicial processes. This is particularly so where, as here, the 
state settlement is subjected to a state court approval process 
that provides an opportunity for all class members who wish 
to pursue their claims -- state or federal -- to opt out of the 
settlement and do so. 


. I. The Ninth Circuit’s decision vacated the considered 
judgments of a state supreme court and state trial court. The 
Ninth Circuit justified its interference with the finality of the 
state court judgment on the basis of an expressed concern for 
absent class members. But this Court’s decision in Phillips 
Petroleum Co. v. Shutts, 472 U.S. 797 (1985), has defined the 
procedural protections necessary for absent class members. 
When absent class members are afforded full notice of a set- 
tlement, an opportunity to be heard with respect thereto, the 
right to opt out, and the guardianship of the settling and appel- 
late courts -- and ultimately this Court on direct appeal under 
28 U.S.C. § 1257(a) -- no more is required, or reasonably 
should be. Indeed, since avoidance of duplicative litigation is 
an impetus for the class action mechanism, courts should en- 
courage class action settlements releasing the broadest possi- 
ble range of claims. The Ninth Circuit evidently distrusted the 
state court’s evaluation of the fairness of the settlement, but 
such distrust has no place in our system of federalism. 


Il. The Delaware judgment is final and may not be un- 
dermined through collateral attack in a federal court. The Full 
Faith and Credit Act, 28 U.S.C. § 1738, so requires. Exceptions 
to the Full Faith and Credit Act are rare. This case involves 


14 


neither an express nor implied repeal of § 1738. See, e.g., Mar- 
rese V. American Academy of Orthopaedic Surgeons, 470 US. 
373, 381 (1985). There is no basis for i a congressional 
intent that claims under the Securities Exchange Act of 1934 
may not be settled and released by agreement of private par- 
ties in state court litigation. 


Ill. The Ninth Circuit established a novel test for deter- 
mining the preclusive effect of a state court judgment entered 
on a class action settlement releasing federal claims. Under 
the Ninth Circuit’s analysis, a state court can approve a class 
settlement only of claims that it can adjudicate. This test con- 
flates the entirely separate concepts of release and issue pre- 
clusion and strips state courts of the power to resolve disputes 
properly before them. 


IV. State court judgments are given preclusive effect in 
federal court through the Full Faith and Credit Act in order to 
advance the interests of federalism, comity, finality of judg- 
ments, and judicial efficiency. The costs of the Ninth Circuit's 
disturbance of the relationship between state and federal 
courts are enormously high: 

e The right of parties to resolve a legal dispute by contract 
is undermined. When a judgment is entered on a settle- 
ment, full faith and credit has the added effect of holding 
parties to their bargain. The Ninth Circuit decision allows 
parties to renege on a court-approved contract. 

e The legal system depends upon the finality of judgments. 
The Ninth Circuit decision eliminates finality with respect 
to prior state court class action settlements releasing federal 
claims as to which the statute of limitations has not run. 


° The Ninth Circuit decision allows class members to by-

A. The Ninth Circuit Decision Undermines Basic 

Principles of Federalism 


_ The principles underlying full faith and credit are constitu- 
tional. The Framers understood that fil! recognition of sister 
state judicial proceedings was an essential component of fed- 
eralism. See Underwriters Nat'l Assurance Co. v. North 
Carolina Life & Accident & Health Ins. Guar. Ass'n, 455 
US. 691, 703-04 (1982). To encourage the sense of federa- 
tion among the states, the Framers included a clause in the 
Constitution requiring each state to give “Full Faith and 
Credit . . . to the public acts, records, and judicial proceedings 
of every other state.” U.S. Const., art. IV, § 1. James Madison 
argued that the Full Faith and Credit clause was indispensable 
to promote “harmony and proper intercourse among the 
States” and was “a very convenient instrument of justice.” 
The Federalist No. 42, at 267, 271 (James Madison) (Clinton 
Rossiter ed., 1961). 


The first Congress enacted the Full Faith and Credit Act in 
1790 “specifically to insure that federal courts . . . would be 
bound by state judgments.” Kremer, 456 U.S. at 483 n.24; see 
also Davis v. Davis, 305 U.S. 32, 40 (1938). The Act ensures 
the “comity between state and federal courts that has been 
recognized as a bulwark of the federal system.” Allen v. 
MeCurry, 449 U.S. 90, 96 (1980); see also Growe v. Emison, 
113 S. Ct. 1075, 1082 (1993) (Full Faith and Credit Act em- 
bodies “elementary principles of federalism and comity”). 

Mindful of the federalist plan, this Court has consistently 
sought to promote: 


the notion of “comity,” that is, a proper respect for 
state functions, a recognition of the fact that the entire 


country is made up of a Union of separate state gov- 
ernments, and a continuance of the belief that the Na- 
tional Government will fare best if the States and their 
institutions are left free to perform their separate func- 
tions in their separate ways. This, perhaps for lack of a 
better and clearer way to describe it, is referred to by 
many as “Our Federalism,” and one familiar with the 
profound debates that ushered our Federal Constitution 
into existence is bound to respect those who remain 
loyal to the ideas and dreams of “Our Federalism.” 


Younger v. Harris, 401 U.S. 37, 44 (1971); see also Under-

union of states, each having its own judicial system capable of

fil. THE NINTH CIRCUIT TEST FOR DETER- 
MINING THE PRECLUSIVE EFFECT OF A 
STATE COURT SETTLEMENT RELEASING 
FEDERAL CLAIMS IS ENTIRELY MISDI- 
RECTED AND UNWORKABLE 


The Ninth Circuit held that “a court’s jurisdiction to extin- 
guish claims by class settlement should not exceed its juris- 
diction to extinguish claims by adjudication.” Pet. App. 34a. 
Thus, under the Ninth Circuit’s analysis a state court could 
approve the release of exclusively federal claims in individual 
and derivative suits, but it could not do so in class suits unless 
such claims “would have been extinguished by the issue 
preclusive effect of an adjudication of the state claims.” Pet. 
App. 33a (emphasis added). 


The Ninth Circuit’s analysis conflates the separate concepts 
of release and issue preclusion. A release is the contractual 
relinquishment of a right by a party or its representative. 
Black’s Law Dictionary 1289 (6th ed. 1990). A release is 
given legal effect under the regime of state contract law. 
Digital Equip. Corp. v. Desktop Direct, Inc., 114 S. Ct. 1992, 
2003 (1994); Kokkonen v. Guardian Life Ins. Co. of Am., 114 
S. Ct. 1673, 1677 (1994) (“enforcement of settlement agree- 
ment is for state courts”); PMX Indus., Inc. v. LEP Profit 
Int'l, 31 F.3d 701, 703 (8th Cir. 1994) (“A release or settle- 
ment agreement is a contract, and is construed under the legal 
principles applicable to the construction and interpretation of 
contracts.”). When a release is embodied in a state court 
judgment it must be accorded full faith and credit. See 
Grimes, 17 F.3d at 1561-63. In contrast, issue preclusion is a 
judicial doctrine that bars “relitigation of issues actually liti- 
gated and necessary to the outcome of the first action.” 
Parklane Hosiery Co. v. Shore, 439 U.S. 322, 326 n.5 (1979) 


38 


(emphasis added) (citations omitted); see also Marrese, 470 
U.S. at 376 n.1. 

The Ninth Circuit’s analysis is thus fundamentally misdi- 
rected: if what a court can adjudicate controls the parameters 
of what the parties can consensually decide to release, the 
defense of release will be subsumed by issue preclusion. Not- 
tingham, 925 F.2d at 34 (“[uJnless the [release] defense is to 
be written off as some vestigial remnant of an abandoned ju- 
risprudence -- and we think any such suggestion is fanciful” -- 
independent effect must be given to a release of federal 
claims). The invalidity of the Ninth Circuit’s “jurisdictional” 
analysis is underscored by the court’s acknowledgement that 
state courts have the power to approve the settlement of ex- 
clusively federal claims in individual and shareholder deriva- 
tive suits. See Pet. App. 37a-38a & n.32. If the Supremacy 
Clause prohibited a state court from resolving exclusively 
federal claims as a matter of jurisdictional power, a state court 
plainly could not approve the settlement of exclusively federal 
claims in any context, including in individual and derivative 
actions. 

A. The Ninth Circuit’s Limitation on the Preclusive 
Effect of a Negotiated Release Has No Basis in 
Prior Law 


The Ninth Circuit asserted that National Super Spuds, Inc. 
v. New York Mercantile Exchange, 660 F.2d 9 (2d Cir. 1981), 
and TBK Partners, Lid. v. Western Union Corp., 675 F.2d 456 
(2d Cir. 1982), “together provide the doctrinal framework for 
using issue preclusion” to limit the authority of a state court to 
approve a negotiated release. Pet. App. 33a. This assertion is 
demonstrably wrong. National Super Spuds and TBK address 
the authority of class representatives, not the power of a state 
court. Moreover, these cases confirm the capacity of author- 
ized class representatives to release on behalf of class mem- 
bers any claims that they possess in common with other class 
members -- even in the absence of an opt-out right. Nothing in 
National Super Spuds or TBK remotely suggests that issue 
preclusion has any bearing on -- much less restricts -- the 
scope of class action releases that may be negotiated by 
authorized representatives.

IV. THE NINTH CIRCUIT’S DECISION DISCOUR- 
AGES COMPREHENSIVE CLASS ACTION 
SETTLEMENTS, UNDERMINES THE FINAL- 
ITY OF JUDGMENTS, PERMITS PARTIES TO 
ABROGATE THEIR SETTLEMENT CON- 
TRACTS, AND PROMOTES UNNECESSARY 
LITIGATION IN THE FEDERAL COURTS 

In addition to advancing comity, the recognition of state 
court judgments approving class action settlements promotes 
consensual resolution of complex litigation, advances finality 
interests, holds litigants to the bargains they voluntarily make, 
conserves federal judicial resources and discourages duplica- 
tive and vexatious litigation. The Ninth Circuit’s decision un- 
dermines each of these objectives.

A. Encouraging Settlements

duction because, inter alia, it promotes “public policy [that] 
wisely encourages settlements” and unitary disposition of liti- 
gation). There is no reason to believe that class action settle-

respect less important, less reasonable or less fair than those 
reached under federal supervision. The Ninth Circuit’s deci- 
sion, however, assures that there will be fewer state court

federal claim.”®

B. Honoring the Right of Parties to Contract 


Defendants are entitled to rely on the binding effect of a ne- 
gotiated release. See v. Mendoza, 573 F.2d 1075, 
1078 (9th Cir. 1978) (“a litigant can no more repudiate a com-

contractual relationship”), cf’ PMX Indus., 31 F.3d at 703 (8th 
Cir. 1994) (holding that parties are bound to the broad terms of 
an unambiguous release, regardless of subjective intent). This 
reliance interest is destroyed if class members are permitted to 
accept the benefit of a settlement and renege on their promise 
not to sue. The Ninth Circuit’s decision intruded upon the par- 
ties’ consummated and court-approved agreement, and vitiated 
an integral part of the bargained-for consideration to be re- 
ceived by Matsushita.

This Court has enforced in a variety of contexts the principle

C. Preserving the Finality of Judgments 


The public policy favoring finality of judgments entered on 
class action settlements is at least as strong as the policy regard- 
ing the finality of judgments entered on a trial verdict. As a 
matter of immediate concern, the Ninth Circuit decision retro- 
actively threatens the finality and repose of any fully con- 
summated state class action settlement that released claims 
which the court approving the settlement did not have jurisdic- 
tion to adjudicate and which are not time-barred. Cf McCleskey 
v. Zort, 499 U.S. 467, 493 (1991) (noting “the law’s interest in 
finality”); 1B J. Moore, J. Lucas & T. Currier, Federal Prac- 
tice, J 0.405[2], at 189-90 (2d ed. 1984) (underlying policy of 
preclusion “is the objective of judicial finality”). 

The Ninth Circuit decision also opens the door to manipula- 
tion by class members who, as in this case, can obtain a settle- 
ment in state court and thereafter litigate in federal court. This 
threat is not confined to the securities field; the Ninth Circuit 


49 


invites collateral attacks on any case in which the state class re- 
leases claims within the exclusive jurisdiction of the federal 
courts, such as claims arising under the antitrust, patent, and en- 
vironmental laws. See note 36, supra. 


D. Minimizing Unnecessary Litigation in the 
Federal Courts 


The Ninth Circuit’s decision will increase class action liti- 
gation in the lower federal courts. Defendants will be uninter- 
ested in negotiating incomplete releases in state cases. Class 
action plaintiffs will, in turn, be required to file any colorable 
federal claim in federal court so that global disposition of 
cases may be achieved. Federal courts thus will become the 
forum for litigation traditionally handled by state courts, even 
when the core of the parties’ dispute centers on such tradi- 
tional state law claims as breach of directors’ fiduciary duties. 
See McCarren v. Town of Springfield, 464 U.S. 942, 944 
(1983) (“At a time when judges and observers are increas- 
ingly concerned with the work load of the federal courts, the 
application of principles of res judicata to disputes such as 
this might both conserve the time of the federal courts and 
make for a more orderly resolution of this and similar dis- 
putes.”) (Rehnquist, J., dissenting from denial of certiorari).

In sum, Matsushita negotiated a consensual settlement of 
the Delaware action, paid money for a comprehensive release, 
assumed the risk that shareholders might opt out and continue 
to prosecute their claims, and participated in a process in 
which the settlement was subjected to a fairness review by the 
Chancery Court and the Supreme Court of Delaware. In deny- 
ing full faith and credit to the judgment entered upon the Dela- 
ware settlement, the Ninth Circuit announced a new rule which 
radically and improvidently redefines the relationship between 
state and federal courts. As stated in Sandler Associates: 


[T]he state court-approved release forecloses this fed- 
eral action based on principles of comity between state 
and federal courts. To deny effect to a release of claims 
merely because it was given in an action commenced in 
state court would invite unprecedented consequences.
