QUESTIONS PRESENTED 


Matsushita’s 1990 acquisition of MCA Inc. gave rise to share- 
holder class action litigations in Delaware state court and later in 
California federal district court. The named plaintiffs in both 
litigations claimed to represent the former shareholders of MCA. 
The federal district court denied class action status to the federal 
litigations and entered summary judgment for defendant Matsu- 
shita. The parties to the Delaware state litigations negotiated an 
“opt out’’ class action settlement providing for the release of all 
claims, state and federal. A judgment on that settlement was 
entered by the Delaware Court of Chancery and affirmed by the 
Delaware Supreme Court. Thereafter, the Ninth Circuit Court 
of Appeals entertained an appeal from the order dismissing the 
federal actions sponsored by MCA shareholders who had 
released their federal claim for consideration pursuant to the 
Delaware settlement. The court of appeals refused to honor the 
release and refused to give full faith and credit to the state court 
judgment. The court of appeals held—contrary to the law of the 
First and Third Circuits—that state courts lack the power to 
approve a class settlement releasing exclusively federal claims. 
This case presents the following questions: 


1. Whether a federal court can withhold full faith and credit 
from a state court final judgment approving a class action settle- 
ment simply because the settlement released federal claims. 


2. Whether a federal court can automatically imply a private 
right of action under an agency regulation, here Securities and 
Exchange Commission Rule 14d-10, on the sole ground that the 
enabling statute has been held to be privately enforceable. 


3. Whether a statutorily-defined tender offer time period, with 
fixed parameters triggering other statutory regulations, can be 
judicially expanded to include a transaction that occurred 
outside the tender offer period.

Section 1738 of Title 28 provides, with respect to the authenti- 
cated acts, records and judicial proceedings of any State, that: 
Such Acts, records and judicial proceedings or copies 
thereof, so authenticated, shall have the same full faith and 
credit in every court within the United States and its 
Territories and Possessions as they have by law or usage in 
the courts of such State, Territory or Possession from 
which they are taken. 


SEC Rule 14d-10, 17 C.F.R. § 240.14d-10, provides, in rele- 
vant part: 

No bidder shall make a tender offer unless: (1) The tender 
offer is open to all security holders of the class of securities 
subject to the tender offer; and (2) The consideration paid 
to any security holder pursuant to the tender offer is the 
highest consideration paid to any other security holder 
during such tender offer. 


The complete text of Rule 14d-10 is set forth at App. 105a- 
106a. 


STATEMENT OF THE CASE 


Petitioners respectfully suggest that this case presents three 
issues worthy of certiorari. First, the court of appeals denied Full 
Faith and Credit to a final judgment entered on a class action 
settlement in Delaware state court, asserting a “policy” requiring 
that exclusively federal claims be resolved by settlement only in a 
federal court. This decision disables parties in a state court class 
action from reaching a global settlement of all claims or 
exchanging general releases where an exclusively federal claim 
has been or arguably could be asserted. It thus retroactively calls 
into question the finality of all state court class action settlements 
releasing federal claims as to which the statute of limitations has 
not run. By compromising the finality of judgments entered in 
state courts, the court of appeals intrudes on the ability of state 
courts to fully resolve cases involving both federal and state 


3 


claims. This interference with the ability of state courts to resolve 
complex litigation is particularly striking in this case because 
Delaware is recognized as having a significant interest in the 
regulation of corporate affairs, a matter primarily of state 
concern.! 

Second, the court of appeals implied a private right of action 
under SEC Rule 14d-10, concluding that when Congress enacted 
Section 14(d)(7) in 1968 it intended the federal courts to recog- 
nize an implied right of action under all of the rules promulgated 
thereunder, including Rule 14d-10, which the SEC did not adopt 
until 18 years later. This automatic implication of a private right 
of action contravenes bedrock principles of this Court and con- 
flicts with the approach of the District of Columbia, Third and 
Sixth Circuit Courts of Appeal. 


Finally, compounding these errors, the Ninth Circuit enlarged 
in an indeterminate way the scope of Rule 14d-10 by shifting the 
operative event that triggers regulation. The court of appeals 
extended the restriction that Rule 14d-10 imposes “during [a] 
tender offer’ to any transaction regardless of when it occurs, if 
the transaction may be deemed to be “an integral part” of a 
tender offer. This holding, which directly conflicts with the law of 
the Fourth Circuit, replaces a clear and certain standard (the 
defined tender offer period) with a subjective one (the “integral 
part” test), thereby injecting massive uncertainties into the fed- 
eral regulation of tender offers.

B. The State and Federal Actions 

Class actions were filed on behalf of MCA shareholders in the 
Delaware Court of Chancery in September 1990 upon the publi- 
cation of reports that Matsushita was seeking to acquire MCA. 
The class actions were consolidated and the Delaware court 
ordered expedited discovery. 


In December 1990, the first federal class action was filed in the 
United States District Court for the Central District of Califor- 
nia. That complaint, together with several subsequently-filed 
related actions, alleged that Matsushita’s tender offer violated 
Rule 14d-10, which requires equal consideration for all stock 
purchases made “during [a] tender offer.” In April 1991, the 
district court denied plaintiffs’ motion for class certification. 

In February 1992, the California district court granted sum- 
mary judgment to Matsushita and dismissed the federal com- 


plaints. Respondents appealed this decision to the Ninth Circuit 
Court of Appeals. 


5 


While the appeal was pending, the parties in the Delaware 
actions negotiated a settlement of all claims arising out of Mat- 
sushita’s acquisition of MCA. The settlement provided all share- 
holders the right to opt out of the settlement.” 

The Delaware Chancery Court certified a class for settlement 
purposes and approved a form of notice to the class that 
described in detail the federal claims asserted in the California 
district court actions and the status of the appeals in the Ninth 
Circuit. See App. 77a-101a. The notice made clear that approval 
of the Delaware settlement would release all state and federal 
claims arising out of the MCA transaction—specifically includ- 
ing those claims asserted in the California district court 
actions—and advised class members that they could preserve 
their state and federal claims by opting out of the settlement. 
App. 93a-94a, 96a-97a. In response to the class notice, only 19 of 
the more than 10,000 former MCA shareholders chose to opt 
out.’ 

The Delaware Chancery Court approved the settlement and 
entered a judgment releasing all claims of the class members who 
chose not to opt out. In determining that the settlement was “fair 
and reasonable,” the Chancery Court considered the value of all 
of the state and federal claims that would be released, acknowl- 
edged that particular care should be taken in reviewing a settle- 
ment that would bar claims pending elsewhere, noted the possi- 
bility of a reversal by the Ninth Circuit, and rejected an attack 
on the adequacy of the representation of the Delaware class. 
App. 63a, 67a-70a.

The jurisdiction of the district court was invoked under 28 
U.S.C. § 1331 pursuant to Section 27 of the Securities Exchange 
Act of 1934, 15 U.S.C. § 78aa. 


The federal complaints allege that the tender offer violated

serman transaction was entered into before the public tender

mary judgment in favor of Matsushita dismissing the federal 
actions. App. 52a-60a. 


During the pendency of the appeal of this decision to the Ninth 
Circuit, the Delaware Supreme Court affirmed the Delaware 
judgment approving the state court settlement. App. 76a. Matsu- 
shita invoked the Delaware final judgment in the Ninth Circuit 
as a bar to prosecution of the appeal by the parties who had 
participated in the Delaware settlement. Matsushita relied on the 
Full Faith and Credit Act, 28 U.S.C. § 1738, and the law of this 
Court enunciated in Phillips Petroleum Co. v. Shutts, 472 U.S. 
797 (1985), regarding the binding effect of opt out class action 
settlements. The Ninth Circuit, however, refused to grant full 
faith and credit to the Delaware judgment, holding that a class 
settlement involving the release of exclusively federal claims 
cannot be approved in state court. The Ninth Circuit reversed 
the order granting summary judgment to Matsushita, entered 
summary judgment for plaintiffs holding that the Wasserman 
transaction violated Rule 14d-10, and directed certification of a 
class consisting principally of the thousands of MCA shareholu- 
ers who had chosen not to opt out of the Delaware state court 
settlement and who had already accepted the proceeds of the 
Delaware settlement.

REASONS FOR GRANTING THE PETITION 


1. Full Faith and Credit. For nearly three decades the 
federal courts have uniformly held that state and federal claims 
can be released in a state court settlement. See, ¢.g., TBK 
Partners, Lid. v. Western Union Corp., 675 F.2d 456, 460 (2d 
Cir. 1982); Green v. Ancora-Citronelle Corp., 577 F.2d 1380, 
1383-84 (9th Cir. 1978); Abramson v. Pennwood Inv. Corp., 392 
F.2d 759, 762 (2d Cir. 1968). The court of appeals ignored this 
uniform case law and fashioned an unprecedented exception to 
the Full Faith and Credit Act in direct conflict with the law of 
the First and Third Circuits.‘ Tie court of appeals’ decision 
seriously impairs the ability of state courts to settle complex 
litigation. As a practical matter, it forces class action litigants 
into a federal forum because it is now wholly improvident for a 
defendant to settle a state class action raising allegations that 
could also be the basis for an exclusively federal claim. See, e.g., 
Raskin v. Birmingham Steel Corp., 1990 WL 193326 at *7 (Del. 
Ch. Dec. 4, 1990) (“[i]n many cases of this kind a fair settlement 
of a claim arising from the fairness of a merger will entail the 
release of federal securities law claims arising from the merger . . 
.. Settlement of stockholder class actions asserting breach of 
corporate directors fiduciary duties would hardly be practicable 
otherwise”) (citations omitted). Thus, state class actions are 
radically discouraged. The court's decision also opens the door to 
manipulation by class members who, as in this case, can obtain 
the benefits of a settic ment in state court and thereafter litigate 
in federal court. 

The legal system depends upon the finality of judgments. In 
order to enter judgment upon the settlement, the Delaware 
Chancery Court was obligated to and did find that the - oe 
adequately represented and that the settlement was fair an 
adequate. App. 73a-74a. Those findings were affirmed by the 
Delaware Supreme Court. This having been done, the legal effect 
of the settlement should have been determined once and for all;

2. Private Right of Action. The court of appeals is the first 


in the nation to hold that every regulation promulgated under a 
statute that has been held to be privately enforceable automati- 
cally confers its own private right of action—regardless of the 
language or purpose of that regulation. This conception is at odds 
with “settled interpretive principles regarding implied causes of 
action,” Central Bank v. First Interstate Bank, 114 S. Ct. 1439, 
1455 (1994), and conflicts with the law of other Circuits. The 
holding below, harking back to J./. Case Co. v. Borak, 377 U.S. 
426 (1964), authorizes the creation of new private rights that 
lack a basis in congressional intent. This case presents a timely 
opportunity to address the circumstances under which agency 
regulations may be enforceable by private suit. 


3. Tender Offer Regulation. The Fourth Circuit has recog- 
nized that Rule 14d-10, which regulates the payment of certain 
consideration “during [a] tender offer,” operates—just as a no 
parking sign operates—only during a defined period and not at 
other times. See Kahn v. Virginia Retirement Sys., 13 F.3d 110 
(4th Cir. 1993), cert. denied, 114 S. Ct. 1834 (1994). The parties 
here had stipulated that the Wasserman transaction was agreed 
to and consummated when no tender offer was pending. The 
court of appeals held, however, as a matter of law, that because 
the Wasserman transaction was “conditioned” on the successful 
completion of the public tender offer, it would be “deemed” to 
have taken place during the tender offer even though it did not in 
fact take place at that time. App. 17a-18a. The court of appeals 
superimposed on the statutorily-specified tender offer period an 
ill-defined extension of that period. The court held that since the 
Wasserman transaction “is deemed a part of the tender offer, the 
tender offer does not end, by definition, until [the Wasserman 
transaction] is performed [and] [pJerformance of [that transac- 
tion] . . . will therefore take place ‘during the tender offer... .”” 
App. 17a, n.19. Thus, the court of appeals sharply limited the 
ability of tender offerors to confidently structure transactions in 
accordance with clearly defined rules, thereby creating enormous 
uncertainty in the law.

adherence to this Court’s repeated teaching that “recognition of 
any private right of action for violating a federal statute must 
ultimately rest on congressional intent to provide a private rem-
