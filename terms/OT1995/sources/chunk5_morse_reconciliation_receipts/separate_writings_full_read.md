## Justice-specific grounds, remedy, and reservations

### Breyer, joined by O'Connor and Souter

**§5 minimum accepted ground.** The Act's text, history, and purpose cannot reasonably be read to exclude all party activity and thereby permit the familiar evasion from a white primary to an all-white convention or preprimary process. Congress knew *Smith v. Allwright*, *Terry v. Adams*, and the exclusionary Mississippi convention process; the Bingham amendment's “party office” language and history support coverage of the nominating activity at issue. “State” did not automatically exclude every political-party nominating activity because those constitutional precedents had already applied that concept to some such activity. This is a contextual construction of §§5 and 14(c)(1), not a declaration that every political party is the State for every purpose. Pp. 235–238.

The concrete application is critical: this convention was open to any voter declaring party loyalty but for the $45 fee; the Party had used the primary method in the immediately preceding Senate cycle, cancelling the scheduled 1990 primary when no opponent filed; it changed to the 1994 convention without preclearing the switch; and its convention nominee received state-law ballot-access and placement preferences. The challenged **fee** lies within the Act. The discussion of the switch supports the convention's resemblance to a primary; it is not a separately specified remedy or independent judgment invalidating that switch. Pp. 238–240.

**Limits and constitutional reservations.** The concurrence refuses to decide the general boundary between party activity and substitutes for state primaries, or every convention practice covered by the Act. It invokes *Presley*'s four categories—manner of voting; candidacy requirements and qualifications; electorate composition; creation/abolition of an elective office—and 28 CFR §51.7's exclusions for membership recruitment, campaigns, and platform drafting. Resolutions, platforms, and internal operational rules are described as **very likely**, not categorically, outside preclearance. First Amendment concerns may require still narrower limits for conventions. The fee is outside the area of greatest associational concern and resembles a kind of burden addressed by the Twenty-fourth Amendment; that analogy is not a merits ruling that this particular fee violates the Constitution. The difficult outer First Amendment and constitutional state-action questions remain open. Pp. 238–240.

**§10 threshold and remedy.** Breyer agrees that Congress intended private enforcement of §10. *Allen*'s rationale for §5 applies with similar force to §§2 and 10; differences of language and structure are not determinative. Congress's 1975 technical amendments and retention of §10, plus the stated importance of private enforcement, reinforce that conclusion. He **expressly expresses no view on the merits of the underlying §10 claim**. P. 240. His concurrence supports the Court's reversal/remand and §5 preclearance conclusion, while allowing the §10 claim to proceed without deciding that the fee is an unlawful poll tax. He supplies no separate injunction terms, damages/refund determination, or finding of racial purpose/effect.

**Thresholds not independently analyzed.** There is no independent standing, mootness, immunity, or direct-appeal-jurisdiction discussion in this concurrence. Its acceptance of judgment cannot be converted into a formal join in Stevens's threshold reasoning. O'Connor and Souter adopt the same entire concurrence, including its reservations; neither files an additional rationale or a broader historical join in this case.

### Scalia, joined by Thomas; also joining Thomas throughout

**Independent §5 statutory and constitutional grounds.** Scalia's own dissent starts with associational freedom, then advances four connected objections to the interpretations supporting judgment: (1) First Amendment overbreadth requires addressing a substantial range of affected activity, even if this fee were permissible in isolation; (2) failure to give boundaries creates chilling uncertainty; (3) preclearance is a prior restraint, demanding government permission even for lawful conduct and placing the burden of establishing nondiscriminatory purpose/effect on the party; and (4) constitutional avoidance counsels against finding that restraint in language referring only to States and political subdivisions. Pp. 241–246. He identifies the absence of a limiting construction or partial invalidation and rejects deferring all broader constitutional questions to later cases.

Scalia separately rejects the premise that Congress must have regulated every problem comprehensively: Congress may adopt partial measures. Even if §14's “party office” and §2's nomination language establish that **some** provision covers party activity, they do not establish that §5's preclearance requirement does so. Its text does not clearly impose this restraint on parties. Pp. 246–247. Note 4 explains that avoidance would defeat an otherwise ordinary same-meaning-across-sections inference; its final observation about reading §§2 and 5 naturally is not a §2 merits adjudication.

**Scope from the full Thomas join.** In addition to his own prior-restraint, overbreadth, uncertainty, and avoidance discussion, Scalia formally joins all of Thomas's separate grounds: statutory covered-entity exclusion; rejection of equating §5 with constitutional state action; alternative absence of state action here; alternative exclusion of conventions from §14(c)(1); further associational concerns; and §10's exclusive Attorney General enforcement. These are distinct historical grounds, not merely caution sufficient to avoid a broad rule.

**§10, remedy, and other thresholds.** Scalia's own dissent develops no §10 theory; his full Thomas join supplies the no-private-action position and the grounds in Part II summarized below. He dissents from reversal and would reject the §5 preclearance demand and the private §10 claim. He supplies no separate remedial order or independent standing/mootness ground. The fee's constitutional merits are not resolved in a separate constitutional judgment.

### Kennedy, joined by Rehnquist

**§5 statutory ground.** Section 5 regulates the State as a governing political body and its governmental units, not every person or entity whose conduct can count as state action under the Constitution. Kennedy endorses Thomas's explanation of the text and *Sheffield* at pp. 254–263, then emphasizes Congress's contrasting use of “person acting under color of law” in §11(a) and “whether acting under color of law or otherwise” in §11(b). The parallel §1983 cases show that Congress knew how to reach constitutionally attributable private conduct. It did not do so in §5. Pp. 248–250.

**Preserved governmental path.** Kennedy does not exclude every §5 claim involving a party's nominating practices. A State's unprecleared restructuring of election law to enable discriminatory party nominations could itself be a covered change, requiring **the State** to preclear. But no claim here challenges Virginia's primary-or-convention policy for failure of state preclearance; the challenged omission is the Party's failure to preclear its own $45 fee. A State-responsible practice and the Party's status as a covered §5 entity must remain separate. Pp. 251–252.

**Constitutional caution and express nondecisions.** The statutory case does not require defining Congress's full Thirteenth-, Fourteenth-, or Fifteenth-Amendment enforcement power, or determining whether direct constitutional state-action doctrine protects these appellants. *Smith* and *Terry* may bear on the constitutional claims still pending below. Kennedy preserves that possibility and declines to traverse it. P. 248. Associational freedom and the caution of *Cousins*, *O'Brien*, and related party cases provide further reasons against a sweeping construction; avoidance would be appropriate if the text were ambiguous, but he considers the actual text unambiguous. Pp. 250–251. Unlike Thomas's I.A.3, Kennedy does **not** decide that this convention necessarily fails the constitutional state-action test. Unlike Thomas's I.B, he does **not** adopt the separate conclusion that §14 excludes conventions categorically.

**§10, remedy, and other thresholds.** Kennedy expressly joins Thomas's Part II and its no-private-action conclusion. P. 247. He dissents from reversal of the statutory dismissal on the foregoing grounds while preserving the independent constitutional claims for their proper adjudication. He supplies no separate standing/mootness theory, no final constitutional ruling on the fee, and no alternate affirmative relief. Rehnquist joins all of Kennedy, and also all of Thomas; he does not join Scalia's separate dissent, although Thomas's I.C expressly adopts Scalia's prior-restraint analysis.

### Thomas, joined throughout by Rehnquist and Scalia; Kennedy only in Part II

**§5 antecedent covered-entity question, I.A.1.** “State or political subdivision” covers territorially defined governments and their governing organs. The Party is neither. Statutory references to territory and the definition of political subdivision reinforce that conclusion. *Sheffield* extends coverage to governmental bodies within a covered State, not private partisan organizations. *Williams*'s summary affirmance supplies precedential support for statutory exclusion. Past party submissions, especially those following the Attorney General's 1981 regulation, cannot establish a contrary congressional meaning or justify exponentially expanding submissions under the 60-day process. Pp. 253–263 and notes 1–6.

Thomas would give the regulation no deference because clear statutory text controls. The Party's failure to attack its validity and its counsel's concession do not bind the Court's construction: a litigant's tactical/legal error cannot determine a statute's meaning, and the question presented asks whether §5 requires preclearance. Pp. 258, 262–263. This is an express preservation/concession response, not an unmentioned issue or an assumed agreement to use the regulation as controlling.

**Separate I.A.2 statutory ground.** The Act's use of broader “under color of law” language in §11, unlike §5, and the contrast with §1983 defeat importing the full constitutional state-action doctrine into “State.” Congress intentionally distinguished governmental entities from private persons whose acts might be constitutionally attributable. Pp. 264–266 and notes 7–8.

**Alternative I.A.3 constitutional-attribution ground.** Even assuming §5 were coextensive with constitutional state action, the fee/convention is not fairly attributable to Virginia. *Smith* concerned a compulsory, comprehensively regulated primary, unlike the few permissive/deadline/certification requirements here. *Terry* concerned a de facto decisive election plus state-involved purposeful evasion; neither predicate appears here, and the Party's candidate lost the general election. The public-function cases require a traditionally **exclusive** governmental function. Private candidate selection is distinct from conducting an election. Virginia determines legal ballot-access qualifications; the Party chooses a nominee. Automatic access and other state benefits, or exercising a choice state law permits, do not by themselves convert the Party into the State. Pp. 266–276 and notes 9–18.

The discriminatory-potential requirement for a §5 change must not be confused with Thomas's claimed absence of constitutional state action: note 11 expressly distinguishes those inquiries. Notes 10 and 12 explain his readings of *Smith*, *Classic*, and *Moore*. Notes 13–15 reject importing unshown racial evasion, a conceded public electoral function, or state action from mere statutory benefits. Note 17 states his limit on Congress's Fourteenth-/Fifteenth-Amendment power over purely private behavior; note 18 rejects inferring coverage from a perceived statutory loophole. His preserved alternative is a suit against an appropriate **state official** challenging the State's ballot-access law; a State's discriminatory one-group access law would most likely be state action and subject to §5. Pp. 274–276. The dissent does not hold every constitutional claim against every party impossible.

**Independent I.B voting-definition ground.** Even if the Party were a State/state actor, §14(c)(1)'s enumeration of primary, special, and general elections omits conventions. Other federal election statutes expressly include conventions when Congress intends to. “Party office” does not erase the specified election types; an effective vote means one cast and fairly counted, not assurance that the voter's preferred nominee appears on the ballot. Covered candidacy and electorate-change precedents concern enumerated elections. Congress's 1982 expansion of §2's language did not amend §§5 or 14; harmonization is Congress's task. Pp. 277–282 and note 19.

**Additional I.C avoidance/association ground.** Beyond those textual grounds, preclearance burdens this convention's actual associational choices: funding through delegate fees instead of concentrating donor influence; choosing membership boundaries; and organizing to advance political aims. Denial of preclearance could compel admission of those unable or unwilling to pay. Thomas adopts Scalia's prior-restraint concern and doubts the construction could survive the scrutiny appropriate to severe burdens. He acknowledges that First Amendment association yields to Fifteenth Amendment protection where state-approved exclusion effectively denies participation in the actual election, but finds no such conflict absent state involvement and that effective exclusion. Excluding parties from §5 avoids these serious questions. Pp. 282–286. This is an additional reason for statutory construction, not a separate final decree holding §5 facially unconstitutional.

**§10 Part II, joined also by Kennedy.** Section 10 authorizes and directs the Attorney General alone to sue. Unlike §5's language protecting identified persons from unapproved changes, §10 contains a declaration of policy and a specified enforcement mechanism, not an individual statutory entitlement or a direct substantive ban on poll taxes. *Allen* therefore does not control. Congressional intent governs the implied-remedy inquiry; general assumptions about the contemporary implied-action context cannot displace this text. Sections 3 and 14(e), including references to an aggrieved person and attorney's fees, acknowledge private actions generally without creating one under §10. Congress's 1975 technical amendment does not establish private enforcement. Pp. 286–289 and notes 20–22.

Part II explicitly distinguishes an Attorney General §10 action using its special three-judge/direct-appeal procedure from private constitutional challenges before a single judge. It does not decide whether this fee is a poll tax or whether a constitutional challenge succeeds. Note 21 cautions against using past acceptance of private §2 suits to imply a §10 remedy; it does not decide a presented §2 cause-of-action question. Thomas would deny the private §10 cause and require no Party preclearance under §5. Pp. 289–290. No separate standing or mootness dismissal is proposed. Note 23 identifies unanswered consequences of treating parties as States—§5 declaratory proceedings, bailout, §2 liability, and federal examiners—rather than deciding those questions.

## Source differences and limitations

1. **Material structural omission in Cornell ZD2:** The raw HTML itself has empty paragraphs marked `globalstyle H1-A`, `H3`, `H2`, and `H1` where the official report supplies I/A, 1/2/3, B/C, and II. This is not an omission introduced by the archive's text extraction. Use the official structure above for Kennedy's partial join and proposition attribution. The full prose and all 23 footnotes are present in Cornell.
2. **Cornell is not a verbatim final-reporter transcription:** It retains slip-page cross-references and unresolved reporter citations; the official report supplies final page numbers and editorial wording/citation changes. Examples include Breyer's “Democrat National Convention” becoming “Democratic National Convention” at p. 236, identification of the Party by full name at p. 238, Thomas's Webster citation changing from p. 82 to p. 2461 at p. 254, and the corrected ordering of the newspaper citation in note 16 at p. 276. These examples do not alter the legal grounds or joins. Exact quotations and reporter pinpoints should use the official report. No exhaustive character-level diff is claimed.
3. **Shared anomalies must not become neutral facts:** Scalia's note 3 says “primary ballot” in both sources (official p. 244); the state-law benefit elsewhere under discussion is placement on the general-election ballot (e.g., official p. 273). The shared wording therefore is not an archive extraction mismatch and should not be repeated as an independently verified factual description. Thomas's note 18 cross-refers to note 14 when discussing constraints on enforcement power/state action (official p. 277); note 17 is the explicit enforcement-power discussion. Both sources preserve that cross-reference. Neither source was silently altered.
4. **No material legal or joinder conflict found in the completed reading:** The two sources agree on the separate authors, full and partial joins, substantive alternatives, §10 division, and material reservations. Cornell's missing section labels would be material if left unrepaired; the official report resolves them. This receipt does not claim independent verification of every underlying record assertion, quotation of another authority, or factual premise beyond the primary opinions.
