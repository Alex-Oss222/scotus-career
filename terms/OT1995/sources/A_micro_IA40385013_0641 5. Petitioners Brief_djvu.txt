The Fifth Circuit rendered its opinion on May 10, 
1995. Petitioner did not file a motion for rehearing. This 
Court has jurisdiction under 28 U.S.C. § 1254(1). Peti- 
tioner timely filed his petition for writ of certiorari on 
August 8, 1995 which was granted by this Court on 
November 22, 1995.

the cause may, in the discretion of the court, be trans-

STATEMENT OF THE CASE 


Petitioner Lloyd Henderson sustained injuries on 
August 27, 1991, while employed by the United States as 
a seaman aboard the M/V DELMONTE, a ship owned by 
the United States (R. 3-4). After exhausting administra- 
tive remedies, he filed suit against the United States on 
April 8, 1993, in the United States District Court for the 
Southern District of Texas, Galveston Division (R. 2, App. 
5-12). The court had jurisdiction under the Suits in 
Admiralty Act (“SAA”), 46 U.S.C. § 741-752 and the Pub- 
lic Vessels Act, 46 U.S.C. §§ 781-790. 


From the onset, Mr. Henderson experienced a series 
of difficulties obtaining the proper summons for the 
United States from the district clerk’s office (R. 163). At 
the same time Mr. Henderson filed his complaint, he

requested the appropriate summons forms and file 
stamped copies of the complaint (App. 42, 51). However, 
these summons forms were not sent to Mr. Henderson’s

received the completed summons from the district court

and complaint via certified mail to the United States 
Attorney General on May 19, 1993 (R. 163, App. 42). The 
Attorney General was served on May 25, 1993 (App. 42,

On May 25, 1993, Mr. Henderson’s complaint was 
forwarded to Constable Jack Abercia for service on the 
U.S. Attorney for the Southern District of Texas (App. 52, 
55-56). The summons intended for the United States 
Attorney, however, was still not in proper form at that 
point because the deputy district clerk had failed inad- 
vertently to place the seal of the court on the summons. 
On June 1, 1993, Constable Jack Abercia’s office wrote to 
Mr. Henderson’s counsel and informed him that Abercia 
required the Court's seal on each summons form prior to 
service (App. 52, 57-58). The constable’s office also 
advised Mr. Henderson’s counsel that it needed two addi- 
tional summons forms (App. 57-58). 


Mr. Henderson’s counsel again requested, via mail, 
additional summons forms from the U.S. District Court 
with the appropriate court seal (App. 52, 59-61). When 
they were not received, Mr. Henderson’s counsel's office 


called the Clerk, and informed the Clerk's office of the 
situation (App. 52). 


Mr. Henderson’s counsel again requested by mail the 
summons forms from the District Clerk’s office on August 
19, 1993 (App. 52, 62-64). The properly sealed summons were 
not received until August 25, 1993 (App. 52), although they 
were dated July 22, 1993 (App. 52, 65). The sealed summons 
were then transmitted a second time to Constable Abercia’s 
office on August 25, 1993 (App. 52, 66-67), two days before 
the statute of limitations elapsed. (R. 164). 


On August 30, 1993, Mr. Henderson filed a motion 
seeking an extension of time in which to serve the United 
States Attorney (R. 12-13, App. 13-14). The district court, 
after presumably finding good cause as required by Rule 
4, granted the extension and rendered an order allowing 
service on or before September 15, 1993 (R. 16, App. 15). 
The United States Attorney received personal service on 
September 3, 1993 (App. 33-34). 


On November 17, 1993, the United States moved to 
dismiss the case for want of jurisdiction due to a lack of 
“forthwith” service as required by the SAA (R. 34, App. 
16-38). Mr. Henderson filed his response to the motion on 
December 16, 1993 (App. 39-85). The district court origi- 
nally denied the motion, presumably finding that Rule 4 
governed the time for service in the case and that the 
court had the power to grant an extension (R. 107). 


Following the court’s ruling, however, the Fifth Cir- 
cuit delivered its opinion in United States v. Holmberg, 
holding that the SAA’s forthwith requirement rather than 
Rule 4 governs the time for service on the United States. 


19 F3d 1062 (Sth Cir. 1994), cert. denied, 115 S.Ct. 482 
(1994). On May 13, 1994, the government filed a second 
motion to dismiss based upon the Holmberg decision 
(App. 86-91). The district court reconsidered its ruling in 
light of Holmberg and granted the government's motion to 
dismiss (R. 189, Pet. App. 8). Although the dismissal was 
without prejudice, the statute of limitations had already 
elapsed (R. 189). 


Mr. Henderson appealed the district court’s decision 
to the United States Court of Appeals for the Fifth Cir- 
cuit. The Fifth Circuit affirmed the dismissal, merely reit- 
erating its opinion in Holmberg that the SAA‘s forthwith 
requirement rather than Rule 4 governs the time for ser- 
vice on the United States (Pet. App. 1). On August 8, 
1995, Mr. Henderson filed his Petition for Writ of Cer- 
tiorari in this Court. This Court granted the Petition on 
November 22, 1995. 


SUMMARY OF THE ARGUMENT 


The intent of Congress in enacting the SAA and Rule 
4 of the Federal Rules of Civil Procedure compels the 
conclusion that the 120 day service requirement in Rule 4 
supplants the “forthwith” service requirement of the 
SAA. The inherent conflict between the service provisions 
in the SAA and Rule 4 has inevitably led to a divergence 
of opinion among the circuit courts. The Third Circuit has 
held that Rule 4’s 120 day service requirement supersedes 
the SAA’s “forthwith” service provision because it is 
simply procedural; on the other hand, other circuits have 


held that the SAA‘s “forthwith” requirement represents a 
waiver of the government's sovereign immunity and can- 
not be modified by Rule 4 because it is substantive. 
However, those circuits that have held this provision to 
be substantive have erroneously based their holdings 
upon Second Circuit authority decided before 1966 when 
Congress specifically applied the federal rules of civil 
procedure to admiralty suits. Additionally, these courts 
have ignored the fact that at the time the SAA was 
enacted in 1920, there were no uniform rules in effect 
governing federal procedure. 


Any decision that the forthwith service provision of 
the SAA is substantive emasculates the intent of the 
legislature in enacting the SAA. The SAA was enacted by 
Congress in order to waive its immunity from suit by 
allowing in personam actions against the United States. 
The SAA specifically provides that suits brought under 
the Act “shall proceed and shall be heard and determined 
according to the principles of law and to the rules of 
practice .. . in like cases between private parties.” 46 
U.S.C. § 743. Congress even broadened its waiver of 
sovereign immunity in 1960 by removing the restriction 
as to merchant vessels. Furthermore, similar statutes in 
which the government has waived immunity such as the 
Federal Tort Claims Act and the Tucker Act are also 
governed by Rule 4 service procedures. The view that the 
SAA’s “forthwith” service requirement is substantive in 
essence defeats Congress's intent to waive immunity. 


The Rules Enabling Act through which the rules of 
procedure are promulgated specifically states that all con- 
flicting laws “shall be of no further force or effect.” 28 
U.S.C. § 2072. Congress therefore intended a valid rule 


9 


such as Rule 4 to supersede an inconsistent federal stat- 
ute. This Court has previously held in Hanna v. Plumer, 
380 U.S. 460 (1965), that Rule 4 which allows service on 
an executor supplanted a contrary state rule requiring in- 
hand service because matters of service are governed by 
the federal rules. Congressional intent to supercede the 
“forthwith” requirement of the SAA is further evidenced 
by the fact that present Rule 4 which includes the 120 day 
service requirement and “good cause” exception was 
drafted by Congress and not by this Court. Obviously, 
Congress desired to bring uniformity to the timeliness of 
service issue. 


The inevitable result of the Fifth and other circuits’ 
adoption of the “forthwith” service requirement rather 
than the 120 days provided by Rule 4 is a drastic varia- 
tion among the circuits as to what constitutes “forth- 
with.” This Court’s determination that the Rule 4 service 
provisions supplant the “forthwith” service requirement 
of the SAA would resolve this inconsistency and give 
effect to legislative intent. The lack of uniformity among 
the courts concerning the amount of time which is “forth- 
with” also raises due process concerns since many liti- 
gants are denied the right to proceed to trial on the 
merits. Furthermore, the “forthwith” requirement creates 
equal protection problems since all other federal actions 
involving the United States are subject to Rule . Cer- 
tainly, Congress did not intend that only seamen should 
be subjected to this draconian requirement while allow- 
ing those injured upon the land to have the advantage of 
the bright-line time limit in Rule 4. 

The Fifth Circuit's interpretation of the SAA’s service 
requirement also incorrectly restricts the district court's 


10 


power to extend the time for service under any circum- 
stances. Even if the “forthwith” service requirement 
applies to suits brought under the SAA rather than Rule 
4, the district court should maintain the power to extend 
the time for service in appropriate circumstances. In an 
analogous context, this Court has permitted federal 
courts to toll the limitations period in a federal statute 
providing for substantive liability. American Pipe & Con- 
struction Co. v. Utah, 414 U.S. 538, 556-559 (1974). The 
facts of this particular case further illustrate the need for 
a definitive rule which would allow the courts to extend 
the time period for service under the SAA. Given the 
problems Mr. Henderson confronted in attempting to 
serve the United States, the district court’s extension of 
time for serving the government was proper and should 
be honored by this Court.

A. The Fifth Circuit's Interpretation Of The SAA’s 
Forthwith Service Requirement Conflicts With 
Third Circuit Precedent. 


The manner of service on the United States under the 
SAA and Rule 4 is essentially the same. Both require that 
the plaintiff personally serve the United States Attorney

time for such service, but simply provides that service be

contrast, the Second, Fifth, Ninth, and Eleventh Circuits 
have concluded that the SAA’s forthwith requirement 
applies regardless of Rule 4’s 120-day requirement. E.g. 
City of New York v. McAllister Brothers, Inc., 278 F.2d 708,

1388 (9th Cir. 1976), cert. denied, 430 U.S. 945 (1977);

B. The Fifth Circuit's Interpretation Of The SAA’s 
Service Requirement As Substantive Frustrates 
Congressional Intent. 


1. The Provisions Of The SAA Negate An 
Unnecessarily Strict Interpretation Of The 
Forthwith Service Requirement. 


The Fifth Circuit’s interpretation of the SAA’s forth- 
with service requirement as substantive, like that of the 
Second, Ninth, and Eleventh Circuits, fails to capture 
Congressional intent. Prior to 1916, the government 
enjoyed immunity from suits alleging damages inflicted 
by government owned and operated vessels. Recognizing 
the inequity of declining to compensate those damaged 
by government vessels, Congress passed the Shipping 


17 


Act of 1916, which provided that U.S. vessels employed 
as merchant vessels would be subject to all laws, regula- 
tions and liabilities to which private merchant vessels 
were subjected. Id. However, when later litigants began to 
use in rem actions to seize government vessels, Congress 
passed the SAA in 1920 in order to allow in personam 
actions against the government.” In essence, Congress 
broadened the waiver of immunity in suits involving 
damages inflicted by public vessels by enacting the SAA. 


From the outset, the plain language of the SAA 
declared Congress’s intent that the government should be 
treated in the same manner as private litigants. Section 
743 of the SAA specifically provides that “[s]uch suits 
shall proceed and shall be heard and determined accord- 
ing to the principles of law and to the rules of practice 
obtaining in like cases between private parties.” This 
provision plainly reveals Congress’s intent to treat gov- 
ernmental and private vessels the same. 


Continuing to allow the government to enjoy the 
forthwith service requirement, while private owners and 
operators of vessels are subject to the service require- 
ments set out in Rule 4, defeats Congressional intent in 
enacting the SAA to place the government on equal terms 
with private litigants. See 46 U.S.C. § 742. In fact, this 
Court, in interpreting a different provision of the SAA, 
noted: “Congress has expressly declared that when sued 
under the Suits in Admiralty Act the United States is to have

5. Alternatively, Rule 4 Was Enacted Pursuant 
To The Federal Rules Of Civil Procedure 
Amendments Act Of 1982 And Superseded 
The Provisions Of The SAA. 


The circumstances surrounding the enactment of 
Rule 4 also compel a finding that Rule 4 supplants the 
provisions of the SAA. The Federal Rules of Civil Pro- 
cedure are usually not the product of direct congressional 
legislation. As a general rule, the federal rules are pro- 
mulgated by this Court pursuant to the Rules Enabling 
Act, 28 U.S.C. § 2072. Historically, Congress has acceded 
to the recommendations of the Supreme Court and 
adopted the rules as drafted by this Court. However, in 
adopting the 120-day requirement of Rule 4 in 1982, 
Congress exercised its power to reject the changes pro- 
posed by the Supreme Court and instead rewrote Rule 4 
legislatively. 


On April 28, 1982, this Court transmitted to Congress 
several proposed amendments to the Federal Rules of 
Procedure including the amendments to Rule 4 with the 
120 day service requirement.2? These amendments were 
to have taken effect on August 1, 1982. However, because 
of numerous complaints concerning proposed Rule 4,7! 
Congress then enacted Public Law 97-227, postponing the 
effective date of the proposed amendments to Rule 4 until 
October 1, 1983. All other amendments to the Rules of 
Procedure took effect on August 1, 1982 as scheduled.

When the court in Amella, supra, questioned the 
“dubious assumption” that the Federal Rules of Civil 
Procedure could amend an act of Congress, 732 F.2d at

was drafted by Congress. Implicit in the court’s reasoning 
in Amella and other circuit decisions in which the courts 
have found the “forthwith” requirement to be substan- 
tive, is that such rights cannot be altered except by Con- 
gress. However, this is precisely what Congress did in 
1982 when it rewrote Rule 4 legislatively. Also see George

a statutory command under the Federal Rules of Civil

General principles of statutory construction further 
support the view that Rule 4(j) applies, and was intended 
to apply, generally to all actions filed in federal courts. 
When a general statute is enacted after a specific statute 
and is incompatible with the earlier specific statute, it is 
construed to supersede the specific statute. Preiser v. 
Rodriguez, 411 U.S. 475, 489-90 (1973). Furthermore, prior 
specific statutes are considered repealed by implication 
“when a comprehensive revision of a particular subject is 
promulgated. ...” 1A J. SUTHERLAND, StaTuTORY CONSTRUC- 
TION, § 23.15, at 376 (Sth ed. 1992). 


When Congress established a 120-day period within 
which a complaint could be served, Congress intended to 
bring uniformity to the timeliness of service issue. Congress 
is presumed to be aware of existing statutes and judicial 
decisions when it enacts new legislation, and is presumed to 
intend to achieve a consistent body of law. See Miles v. Apex 
Marine Corp., 498 U.S. 19, 32 (1990). Thus, when Rule 4 was 
enacted, Congress is presumed to have been aware of the 
“forthwith” service provision in the SAA and the decisions 
construing that statute. If Congress intended there to be any 
exception to the general rule it was prescribing, it certainly 
could have created exceptions to Rule 4. However, no such 
intention appears in the language of Rule 4 or in the legisla- 
tive history of the Rule. E.g., 128 Conc. Rec. 30929-30937, 
32476-32477, 32904-32905, 33132 (1982). Because Rule 4 was 
legislatively written by Congress, this Court should conclude 
that it replaces the “forthwith” service provision.

C. The Fifth Circuit’s Interpretation Of The SAA’s 
Service Requirements Leads To Inconsistent 
Results. 


Application of the forthwith service requirement 
rather than Rule 4’s 120-day requirement leads to incon- 
sistent results because the term “forthwith,” in the words 
of one court is “inherently ambiguous.” O'Halloran, 817 F. 
Supp. at 831. Courts across the country vary widely in 
their determinations of what is and what is not forthwith 
service. E.g., Holmberg, 19 F.3d at 1065 (holding that ser- 
vice on the United States 106 days following filing suit 
was not forthwith); Kenyon, 676 F.zd at 1231 (holding that 
service on the United States 60 days after filing suit was 
not forthwith); Owens, 541 F.2d at 1388 (holding that 
service on the United States 58 days after filing suit was 
not forthwith); O'Halloran, 817 F. Supp. at 831 (holding 
that service on the United States 77 days after filing suit 
was not forthwith); Libby, 657 F. Supp. at 1253 (holding 
that service on the United States 21 days after filing suit 
was forthwith). This bewildering variation among the 
courts in interpreting the term “forthwith” serves only to 
confuse litigants. 


Prior to the 1982 amendments to Rule 4, the timeli- 
ness of service of process was judged by a “due dili- 
gence” standard, except in cases under the SAA. Stanga v. 
McCormick Shipping Corp., 268 F.2d 544, 550 (Sth Cir. 
1959); Grammenos v. C.M. Lemos, 457 F.2d 1067, 1070 (2d 
Cir. 1971). While “due diligence” and the “forthwith” 
language of the Act were defined differently by the 
courts, both shared a common infirmity: neither had a

clear definition of what was “due diligence” and “forth- 
with.” This Court has often noted that courts and liti- 
gants are best served by bright-line rules, especially in 
cases involving harsh consequences such as the loss of a 
right to proceed to trial on the merits. E.g., Houston v. 
Lack, 487 U.S. 266, 267 (1988) (adopting bright-line rule 
recognizing inmate's notice of appeal filed upon receipt 
by prison authorities); Budinich v. Becton Dickenson & Co., 
486 U.S. 196, 202 (1988) (“courts and litigants are best 
served by the bright-line rule, . . . that a decision on the 
merits is a ‘final decision’ for purposes of § 1291"). By 
enacting the 120-day service requirement, Congress obv:- 
ously intended to uniformly define the term for service in 
all federal litigation. 


The lack of uniformity among the courts concerning 
the amount of time which is “forthwith” under the SAA

U.S. Const. Amend. V. Nothing in the language of Rule

Il. The Fifth Circuit's Interpretation Of The SAA’s Ser- 
vice Requirements Restricts The District Courts’ 
Power To Extend The Time For Service Under Any 
Circumstances. 


The Fifth Circuit held that the district court's exten- 
sion of the time for service in this case had no effect 
because the SAA’s forthwith service requirement is juris- 
dictional in nature. 51 F.3d at 576. In other words, accord- 
ing to the Fifth Circuit, district courts lack the power to 
extend the forthwith service requirement under any cir- 
cumstances. Thus, even the government's own failure to 
provide the necessary summons will not serve as a basis 
for enlarging the time to properly effect service. 


Even if the forthwith service requirement applies to 
suits brought under the SAA, rather than Rule 4, the 
district courts should maintain the power to extend the 
time for service in appropriate circumstances. The Fifth 
Circuit previously addressed an analogous issue in 
McCormick v. United States, 680 F.2d 345 (1982). That case 
involved a suit under the SAA brought against the United 
States after the statutory limitations period had elapsed. 
The United States argued that because the SAA reflects a 
waiver of sovereign immunity, the limitations period 1s 
jurisdictional in nature and cannot be tolled under any 
circumstances. Id. at 350. The Fifth Circuit rejected the 
United States’ argument, relying heavily on this Court's 


39 


decision in American Pipe & Construction Co. v. Utah, 414 
U.S. 538 (1974). 


In addressing the issue of whether a limitations 
period in a federal statute providing a new cause of 
action could be tolled, this Court in American Pipe con- 
cluded: 


[T}he mere fact that a federal statute providing 
for substantive liability also sets a time limita- 
tion upon the institution of suit does not restrict 
the power of the federal courts to hold that the 
statute of limitations is tolled under certain cir- 
cumstances not inconsistent with the legislative 
purpose. 


Id. at 559. Heeding American Pipe, the Fifth Circuit held in 
its own decision on the issue: 


We cannot accept the government's position, 
and the district court's holding that the SAA 
limitations provisions can never be tolled. We 
hold that the two-year limitation on suits under 
the SAA can be tolled under appropriate cir- 
cumstances where to do so would not defeat the 
purpose of the provision, which is to encourage 
those with claims against the United States to 
present their claims promptly and diligently, 
and where injustice to the plaintiff would other- 
wise result. 


McCormick, 680 F.2d at 351. Likewise, federal courts 
should possess the power to extend the forthwith service 
requirement under the SAA in appropriate circumstances 
despite the fact that the SAA’s forthwith service require- 
ment may be jurisdictional in nature. 


The facts of this case further illustrate the need for a 
precise rule which would allow the courts to extend the 
time period for service in suits under the SAA. Here, 
Henderson immediately requested the appropriate sum- 
mons forms from the district clerk's office at the time he 
filed his complaint on April 3, 1993 (App. 42, 51). How- 
ever, it was the district clerk’s own lack of diligence and 
failure to include the proper seal on the summons which 
resulted in the delay in service upon the government 
(App. 42, 51-52, 57-64). In fact, Mr. Henderson's counsel

52, 57-58). Although new summons forms were imme- 
diately requested by Mr. Henderson's counsel upon 
learning of the defect, the substitute summons forms 
were not received until August 25, 1993, again because of 
delay by the district clerk's office (App. 52, 59-64). It 
would be ironic indeed to disallow a litigant to receive 
additional time to effect service upon the government 
when it is the government's own delay which resulted in 
the need for that extension.
