## Submitted issue and actual judgment

This is review of a discovery-based dismissal sanction after the Government declined to comply with the district court’s selective-prosecution discovery order. The en banc Ninth Circuit sustained the discovery ruling and resulting dismissal. It did **not** find these defendants victims of an established constitutional violation, acquit them, or decide their guilt. The precise question is the evidentiary threshold for obtaining discovery about alleged racial selection for federal prosecution, and application of that threshold to the developed record. The Government attacks the discovery predicate, not independently the appropriateness of the sanction selected upon refusal. An explicit “with prejudice” term is not authenticated by the readable orders and must not be supplied.

The five Black defendants faced cocaine-base conspiracy/distribution and firearm charges after a joint local-police/ATF investigation. The proceedings were pretrial. The sources specifically establish that **state charges were never filed against these defendants** (JA 157). Accordingly, federal-versus-state selection is the asserted injury; a completed transfer after dismissal of their state prosecutions is not a fact of this record.

Both courts described the discovery order as covering people charged with **both** cocaine-base and federal firearm offenses, with racial identity, investigating agencies, and criteria for selecting federal prosecutions, over the specified three-year period. The actual September 8 transcript confirms both counts and describes “1989 through 1992” (JA 162). A later response to counsel seeking crack-only cases is ambiguous (JA 163); the published appellate description remains the source-supported statement of the adjudicated order. The exact scope need only be revisited if a proposed mandate turns on extending or narrowing that boundary.

The district court’s discretion operates within a legally valid threshold. Review for abuse of discretion therefore does not make a disputed legal requirement unreviewable, and plenary consideration of that requirement does not authorize selecting among unsupported factual accounts.

## Evidence that must stay distinct

**Defense office sample.** Beller’s July 17 declaration reports 24 Black defendants and no non-Black defendant in the defined cocaine-base sample. The underlying files were federal public defender cases **closed during 1991**, excluding specified out-of-district and early retained-counsel matters. This is neither all federal prosecutions filed in 1991 nor a census of district offenders. It gives the racial composition of one prosecuted sample, but does not by itself identify the composition of the pool eligible for equivalent federal charging, the number passed over, or matched armed-crack offenders prosecuted by the State. The statement that all races commit all offenses at a relevant rate is part of the lower majority’s inference, not an established demographic fact.

**Additional defense material.** Reed describes direct professional experience with Black federal defendants and other-race state crack-sale defendants, including repeat offenders. O’Connor relays a treatment professional’s experience with white and minority crack users/dealers. Their scope, first-hand versus relayed foundation, and degree of matching to the federal offenses differ. Neither supplies every drug-quantity, firearm, evidentiary, or criminal-history characteristic of a particular comparator. That is a genuine evidentiary limitation, not proof that no comparator exists. The lower majority reports no Government admissibility objection below; this does not make every hearsay submission categorically sufficient in future litigation.

**Government’s developed response.** The initial prosecutor’s inability to explain the sample is not the whole record. On reconsideration, Scheper offered case-specific reasons involving amount, repeated transactions, multiple participants, joint federal investigation, firearms, strong recordings, threats, and criminal histories, and denied racial selection. Investigators described why relatively few of their local cases were referred federally and reported many Black offenders prosecuted by the State. These are sworn explanations and assertions to assess; they are not appellate findings that every proffered allegation is true or every decision race neutral. Government declarations also identify non-Black federal crack defendants outside or disputedly related to the sample’s time and closing criteria. Those examples matter to any broader claim that the district prosecuted only Black defendants, without logically disproving the sample’s carefully bounded count.

**Material outside the original record.** The Berk–Campbell study discussed in the panel dissent was not before the district court. The Government disputed judicial notice; the en banc majority did not rest on that study. It cannot silently become the missing comparison population or an adjudicated fact. The newspaper article was expressly discounted by the district judge.

**District consideration.** The judge said the statistics raised a question about motivation and that criteria disclosure could address it. She also said she reviewed all parties’ evidence (JA 217–218). The litigants dispute the extent to which she relied on supplemental declarations. A neutral account should report those statements and the dispute, not remove the declarations from the record or attribute reliance solely to assumptions.

## Governing provisions independently verified

The complete event-date Rule 16 was independently recovered from the [official 1996 United States Code, title 18 appendix](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/USCODE-1996-title18/html/USCODE-1996-title18-app.htm), saved in `rule16_1996_code_extract.txt`. This resolves the original source handoff’s incomplete-rule retrieval limitation.

Rule 16(a)(1)(C) requires inspection/copying of the enumerated documents and objects within Government possession, custody, or control that “are material to the preparation of the defendant’s defense or are intended for use by the government as evidence in chief at the trial, or were obtained from or belong to the defendant.” Those are alternative textual triggers. The materiality question remains where neither other trigger applies.

Rule 16(a)(2), except as provided in (a)(1)(A), (B), (D), and (E), excludes discovery under that Rule of Government attorney/agent internal reports and memoranda made in connection with investigation or prosecution of the case; it separately limits Government witness/prospective-witness statements by reference to 18 U.S.C. §3500. Paragraph (a)(3) preserves its specified grand-jury exceptions. A request for existing aggregate racial information, a request for charging criteria, and a request for internal deliberative memoranda do not automatically implicate these limits in identical ways.

Rule 16(d)(1) permits denial, restriction, deferral, or another appropriate order on a sufficient showing, including a written showing inspected by the judge alone; material supporting a granted ex parte order must be sealed and preserved for appellate review. Rule 16(d)(2) authorizes specified responses to failure to comply with the Rule and “such other order as it deems just under the circumstances.” This independently verified language does not establish that the district judge expressly invoked that paragraph as the basis for the actual sanction, or answer whether the original discovery duty existed.

The 1993 amendment added expert disclosures, and the 1994 amendment addressed statements of organizational defendants. The operative (a)(1)(C), protective-order, and sanction language relevant here was not newly created by those changes. Do not retroject the expert-disclosure provision into the 1992 order as a source of entitlement. No modern restyling or later discovery expansion governs.

Rule 17(c), as quoted in the safe merits source, allows designated documentary/object subpoenas subject to quashing or modification if compliance is unreasonable or oppressive. Its enforcement has independent predicates. The Government specifically disputes that its earlier state-subpoena discussion concedes unconditional access to state records. Brady, compulsory process, and supervisory authority are additional respondent arguments; none is established here as an automatic substitute for the contested discovery showing.

## Actual positions and alternatives

**Government’s supported threshold.** Its submission seeks a substantial, concrete preliminary showing concerning discriminatory effect and purpose, expressly **less than a prima facie merits case**. In the absence of direct discriminatory evidence, it seeks credible evidence that relevantly similar different-race offenders were not federally prosecuted. It accepts that direct intent evidence or, in an appropriate case, comparison involving a different offense can matter. Thus its actual proposal is not accurately described as a universal demand for a named, perfectly identical comparator regardless of all other evidence. Its strongest application argument concerns the sample’s denominator, mismatch between ordinary crack cases and these armed, substantial, well-supported charges, and the specific rebuttal explanations. General regularity and enforcement interests support caution, not constitutional immunity.

**Respondents’ discovery/merits distinction.** Their strongest position is that asking for records needed to test a supported discrimination concern must require less than proving the discrimination itself. Martin identifies access obstacles and proposes staged disclosure, criteria first, redaction, in-camera review, and use of accessible sources. Rozelle emphasizes specific facts supporting a reasonable, colorable inference and district-court judgment. They do not claim any unsupported allegation requires unrestricted discovery. The challenge is to articulate an objective threshold that gives these concerns operative effect without making any racial imbalance in a selected sample sufficient by itself.

**Rule 16 deserves its own analysis.** Martin argues that information material to a genuine selective-prosecution defense falls within (a)(1)(C), subject to textual exclusions and protection. The United States replies that materiality itself incorporates the preliminary showing required for this defense and expressly denies that its position creates an additional category categorically exempt from discovery (reply pp. 2–8, independently read). Its reply calls Martin’s Rule 16 objection new in this litigation. A neutral disposition should address any actual presentation concern before reaching a new categorical interpretation; it must not attribute to the Government or the court below a blanket “outside Rule 16” holding they did not adopt. A reasoned interpretation might resolve threshold materiality without deciding every possible meaning of “defense.”

**Sustaining the order is a live path.** One could conclude that the defined sample, supplemental professional observations, asserted access disparity, and the judge’s consideration of the response together satisfy a discovery threshold appreciably below merits proof, and that ordering this information lay within permissible discretion. That path must explain why the inference concerns the relevant federal selection rather than drug use or general offending, why the Government’s developed explanations do not defeat the preliminary showing, and how the actual scope remains lawful. It does not establish discrimination on the merits.

**Setting the order aside is a live path.** One could require a more reliable showing linking racial difference to comparable federal-selection decisions, while allowing circumstantial proof and alternatives to named comparators, and find this sample and declarations inadequate under that rule. That path must distinguish meaningful preliminary proof from demanding information only obtainable after discovery, assess all the submitted evidence and responses, and identify the actual legal or discretionary error. It should neither declare that statistical evidence can never suffice nor make an unsupported demographic assumption in the opposite direction.

**A bounded remand is a separate path.** If the adopted legal rule materially differs from the lower court’s or if a necessary part of the order must be separated from the rest, a mandate can return the existing request for that lawful assessment. It must identify the genuine legal correction or unresolved source-supported issue. It cannot order discovery simply to cure this research team’s omissions, compel the parties to invent new comparators, or presume that criteria-only disclosure falls outside all threshold and privilege concerns. Whether actual further submissions are permitted is an ordinary legal implementation question, not an automatic right created by this preflight.

## Date-eligible precedent and Justice-specific support

**Wayte, 470 U.S. 598, 607–610 & n.5 (1985).** The operative predivergence text was independently read. Prosecutorial choice is broad but constitutionally constrained; effect and purpose are required, and awareness of impact differs from choosing a course because of that impact. The decision expressly did **not** decide discovery entitlement. It supplies the underlying elements and institutional considerations, not an already fixed discovery quantum. Stevens and O’Connor joined that majority. The dissent’s concern about deciding a claim before required discovery is obtained is a date-eligible argument, not a majority holding.

**McCleskey, 481 U.S. 279, 292–299 (1987).** The pertinent predivergence equal-protection reasoning was checked. The decision’s exceptionally-clear-proof discussion concerns inferring purposeful discrimination from the Baldus study in individualized capital decisions; it distinguishes settings where statistical inference is permitted. O’Connor and Scalia joined the majority; Stevens dissented. Whether and how that reasoning informs a preliminary discovery threshold in a local charging record is an extension requiring explanation. It cannot simply convert discovery into a completed merits adjudication or create a categorical prohibition on statistics.

**Wade (May 18, 1992), actual simulated law.** Souter’s Part A, with all nine then serving, recognizes constitutional review despite the Government’s statutory discretion over a substantial-assistance motion. Part B, joined by Stevens, O’Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, and Thomas among its eight supporters, requires a substantial threshold showing of improper motive for discovery or a hearing; useful assistance plus generalized accusations does not suffice. That is concrete, controlling discovery authority at its specified sentencing stage. Stone’s separate reservation does not erase the eight-Justice rule. Application here must assess the difference between an assistance-only proffer and this racial-pattern evidence, rather than assume *Wade* already resolved selective-charging comparator proof.

**Kimberlin (June 12, 1995), actual simulated law.** Ginsburg’s unanimous opinion, including Breyer and every present Associate, rejects a categorical direct-evidence requirement for unconstitutional motive while preserving constitutional elements, specific nonconclusory support, lawful discovery limits, and the qualified-immunity defense. This makes a direct-admission-only standard unavailable by simple inference from institutional concerns. It does not abolish preliminary screening or transplant ordinary civil discovery wholesale into criminal prosecution. Ginsburg’s authorship and Breyer’s express join supply concrete sources for their treatment of evidentiary form and safeguards, without forecasting either vote here.

**Williams (May 4, 1992), actual simulated law.** Stevens, O’Connor, and Thomas joined the narrower supervisory duty and dismissal formulation; Stone’s qualifying support creates the precise common propositions recorded in Holdings. The Court recognizes bounded judicial protection against materially misleading known-evidence omissions and preserves *Bank of Nova Scotia* prejudice. It supplies no categorical immunity for prosecutorial decisionmaking and no automatic dismissal upon every discovery dispute. Neither its duty nor its sanction elements simply transfer from grand-jury presentation to selective-prosecution discovery. The prior case’s constitutional-disclosure question remains open.

**J.E.B. (April 19, 1994), actual simulated law.** Stevens, O’Connor, Souter, and Ginsburg join the staged peremptory-challenge proof rule; Kennedy independently supports the prohibition and inquiry. It preserves the distinction between an initial showing, an explanation, and the objector’s ultimate persuasion burden. Its known venire and particular strike setting differs from a charging pool not yet identified. The analogy supports careful separation of stages; it does not establish the same first-stage showing for this case. Scalia and Thomas’s disagreement there does not determine the race-based charging issue here.

**Mezzanatto (January 18, 1995), actual simulated law.** The Government’s historical brief cites that case for an expansive regularity statement. The actual holding permits a knowing and voluntary, specifically bounded impeachment waiver under Rules 410/11(e)(6), with individualized challenges preserved. It does not create a new categorical presumption resolving this discovery dispute. Its real text-and-background method is available at its actual scope.

These authorities furnish each Associate’s actual writings or joins and the strongest competing legal considerations. They do not produce modeled votes, a coalition, or a new controlling rule by tallying unrelated cases.
