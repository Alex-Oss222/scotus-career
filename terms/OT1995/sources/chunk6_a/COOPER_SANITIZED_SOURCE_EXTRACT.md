## Lower proceeding and actual statute

Cooper v. State, 889 P.2d 293 (Okla. Crim. App. 1995), No. F-92-533, January 10, 1995; rehearing denied February 13, 1995. The lower court affirmed first-degree murder conviction and death sentence from Oklahoma County No. CRF-89-5084, Richard J. Freeman, trial judge. Jury verdict May 22, 1992; formal judgment June 5, 1992. The offense concerned Harold Sheppard's stabbing death during burglary. The controlled federal issue is the clear-and-convincing incompetence burden, not all independently rejected guilt/sentencing claims.

Okla. Stat. tit.22 §1175.4(B), quoted by the parties, says: “The court, at the hearing on the application, shall determine, by clear and convincing evidence, if the person is incompetent. The person shall be presumed to be competent for the purposes of the allocation of the burden of going forward with the evidence.” The trial judge's transcript quotation also says “allocation of the burden of proof and burden of going forward with the evidence.” The lower opinion states that Oklahoma places clear-and-convincing persuasion on the defendant. The statutory proof quantum and its operative allocation are thus directly presented; the modest variation in the parties' excerpt does not create a separate unresolved burden rule.

The lower opinion and respondent brief pp14–18 describe the remaining statutory machinery. Section1175.1 defines competence through present ability to understand charges/proceedings and effectively and rationally assist in defense. The person, defense counsel, prosecutor or court may initiate inquiry; the application must state facts sufficient to raise doubt (§1175.2). A hearing and sufficiently supported doubt lead to examination (§1175.3). The examiners address appreciation of charges, rational assistance, ability to regain competence within reasonable time, mental illness/need for treatment and dangerousness. The final competency hearing permits presence, cross-examination and jury demand (§1175.4); statements receive statutory use protection with the described impeachment qualification. Incompetence suspends criminal proceedings; treatment/assistance and restoration mechanisms have separate predicates (§§1175.5–.6). These are source descriptions, not a determination that every state-law or constitutional predicate was met here.

## Competency chronology, competing evidence and preservation

1. In August1990, Dr.Edith King, a state-employed clinical psychologist, evaluated Cooper and reported incompetence; he was committed to Eastern State Hospital. Petitioner's brief pp2–3 and lower opinion JA63–64 describe serious thought disorder, possible hallucinations, disorientation, difficulty focusing and apparent misunderstanding of the prosecution. This earlier finding was not a permanent adjudication of all later capacity.
2. At the December1990/January1991 postexamination hearing, King described the earlier condition and improvement but could not say he was competent that hearing day. Dr.Jeanne Russell, who conducted a hospital screening interview and reviewed records/staff reports, testified to competence and malingering. Hospital entries reflected knowledge of charges, courtroom roles, consequences, criminal experience and possible insanity defense. The judge found competence. Petitioner's brief says no separate express malingering finding was made; the lower opinion upheld the finding.
3. Shortly before the May1992 trial, counsel again raised abnormal behavior and inability to communicate. Counsel had at one point acknowledged possible feigning, then renewed concerns after investigators and later events. The lower opinion discusses the trial judge's isolated comment conflating sanity at the offense with trial competence and calls it an injudicious statement contradicted by his later protective conduct; that appellate characterization is not independent proof of a constitutionally sufficient inquiry.
4. The May4–5 hearing included Dr.Philip Murphy's examination and testimony, inmates, jailers and other evidence. Murphy diagnosed a major affective disorder, episodic and treatable, and testified that although some manipulation/malingering was present, mental illness prevented rational cooperation with counsel. His report of fear of counsel and confusion was not an opinion that every observed act was genuine. Inmates described repetitive cleaning, feces-related behavior and speaking to absent persons. Jailers described ordinary communication and no such observed abnormalities. The State also showed two pro se federal civil-rights filings and apparent understanding of served papers. Petitioner's brief pp4–10; respondent pp2–12; lower opinion JA64–66; Murphy testimony JA8–26 and jailer testimony JA26–37.
5. At hearing close, JA42–43 (underlying May4–5 transcript271–272), the judge quoted clear-and-convincing evidence, distinguished “not normal” from incompetent, said he respected Murphy and did not dispute the diagnosis given the limited examination, then stated: “I don't believe he has carried the burden by clear and convincing evidence of his incompetency” and directed trial. His actual words also suggested a client might be unable to help the defense yet not incompetent. This is the transcript, not a source finding that the evidence was exactly equipoised or that the judge would find incompetence by a preponderance.
6. JA44–49 reproduces trial discussion of Cooper sitting apart, counsel's inability to communicate, and another inquiry after feces-related behavior. Counsel sought mistrial/commitment or delay for independent reevaluation and again objected to the burden. The judge denied further relief. The source does not furnish a subsequent hearing applying a different proof quantum.

The appellate court separately upheld (a) the legal burden and (b) the factual determinations under that burden. It said the second hearing had stronger defense evidence but credited the availability of the State's lay account and held there was no abuse of discretion in finding Cooper had not met his burden. It did not rule that expert testimony must control. At JA64 n.4 it explains that the trial-level argument that the May hearing was only a preliminary sufficient-doubt hearing was abandoned on appeal, where both hearings were described as postexamination proceedings. That preserves the distinction between the presented final proof quantum and a different preliminary-inquiry claim. A separate jury-waiver objection was rejected below; it is not silently added to this granted issue.

## Actual reported positions, concessions and requested remedies

Petitioner brief pp14–15, 16–32 and46 argues that common-law and contemporary practice protect against trial when the greater weight of evidence shows incompetence; a higher defendant burden allocates more error to a person whose capacity to assist in proving the claim is in question. It distinguishes the preponderance allocation from this higher quantum, emphasizes attorney cooperation and trial reliability, and advances a capital-reliability argument. Its conclusion requests reversal and remand for a new trial. That request is not a source showing that retrospective adjudication is feasible or infeasible.

Respondent brief pp12–21 and27–32 accepts that a factually incompetent person cannot constitutionally be tried, but argues that states may select reliable factfinding procedures and that the legal prohibition does not itself constitutionalize a more-likely-than-not standard. It relies on the possibility of malingering, opportunities for counsel/expert assistance, repeated inquiries, delay's effects on prosecution, and the person's interest against wrongful commitment. It argues that a higher burden screens manipulation and that a genuinely incompetent defendant ordinarily can meet it. It invokes Addington and Jones concerning commitment, Leland and Patterson on proof allocation, and Ohio v.Akron Center's clear-and-convincing bypass standard. Those are litigant arguments; this extract does not adopt their equivalence or correctness.

The State also says continuing inquiry can correct changed competence during trial, cites Drope, and stresses that competence is not an offense element. Its argument analogizes commitment protections and competency findings; the different direction of the burden is visible in the argument itself. The source position regarding psychotropic medication relies on a historical description of Riggins; **use actual simulated Riggins instead of that historical legal description**.

Petitioner's argument relies on English and early American practice, federal law and most states' standards. Federal18U.S.C.§4241(d)'s preponderance standard and historical sources are cited in the filings; the current statute should be authenticated if its exact language is quoted. No empirical rate of malingering or error is established by these advocacy statements.
