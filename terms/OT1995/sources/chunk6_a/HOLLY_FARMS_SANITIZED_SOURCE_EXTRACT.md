## Provisions and issue boundaries

NLRA §2(3), 29 U.S.C. §152(3), includes employees generally but excludes “any individual employed as an agricultural laborer,” alongside separately stated exclusions. Congress’s recurring appropriations rider ties that term to FLSA §3(f). The Board brief identifies the then-current rider as Pub. L. No. 103-333, tit. IV, 108 Stat. 2570 (1994), and cites *Bayside Enterprises, Inc. v. NLRB*, 429 U.S. 298, 300 n.6 (1977), for the recurring enactments since 1946. This identifies the operative linkage; the brief is not itself an official statutory publication.

FLSA §3(f), 29 U.S.C. §203(f), provides:

> “Agriculture” includes farming in all its branches and among other things includes the cultivation and tillage of the soil, dairying, the production, cultivation, growing, and harvesting of any agricultural or horticultural commodities (including commodities defined as agricultural commodities in section 1141j(g) of Title 12), the raising of livestock, bees, fur-bearing animals, or poultry, and any practices (including any forestry or lumbering operations) performed by a farmer or on a farm as an incident to or in conjunction with such farming operations, including preparation for market, delivery to storage or to market or to carriers for transportation to market.

The statute thus separately identifies farming operations and practices performed “by a farmer or on a farm” incident to or in conjunction with “such farming operations.” Source: petitioner brief pp. 2–3; Board brief pp. 12–14. The exact disjunctive “or” matters; a defective OCR quotation in the agency appendix must not replace the statutory text.

NLRA §10(e), 29 U.S.C. §160(e), bars consideration of an objection not urged before the Board, its member, agent, or agency, unless extraordinary circumstances excuse the omission. This matters to one argument below and should be assessed before treating that argument as preserved.

## Operations and record

Holly Farms operates an integrated poultry business. It owns chicks raised to approximately seven weeks/marketable size by independent contract growers. The growers supply the facilities and perform the feeding and care. Ownership of the birds stays with Holly Farms. Live-haul crews then catch, load, and transport the mature birds to Holly Farms’ processing plant. The contested workers include chicken catchers, forklift operators, and truckdrivers. The lower dissent lists 118 catchers, 12 forklift operators, and 27 drivers; other summaries use approximate workforce numbers. A typical crew contains nine catchers, a forklift operator, a driver, and a supervisor. Catchers put chickens into coops; the forklift operator loads the coops; the driver hauls the birds to processing. These differences in task and location are material and should not disappear into a generic description of “poultry employees.” Sources: 48 F.3d 1360, agriculture discussion and dissent; petitioner brief pp. 3–8; Board brief statement.

The live-production manager supervises live haul, hatcheries, and the feedmill, up to the processing operation. His testimony relates catching schedules to processing shifts: the company seeks to keep the plant supplied and reduce the interval between catching and processing, which affects bird weight. Drivers drop trailers at scales; live-haul personnel handle holding and rotation, with processing receiving employees later taking the trailers for processing. JA 28a–29a. These are actual record facts bearing on functional integration, not merely counsel’s characterization.

The government reproduces a growing contract in its appendix. It expressly explains that the contract was marked as an exhibit and the subject of testimony, but was not formally moved into evidence (Board brief pp. 3–4 n.2). The contract should therefore not be described as an admitted documentary exhibit. Provisions discussed include the grower’s duty to apply skill and judgment to raising the birds to marketable weight, be present during catching/removal, and prepare the premises/equipment for the crew. The existence of these obligations does not resolve the legal characterization of the crew’s own work.

Hatchery, egg, and pullet operations were classified separately. Their treatment does not establish a blanket rule that every employee of an integrated poultry firm is covered or excluded. Roaring River feedmill/feed-haul/mechanic employees were included in the bargaining unit; their coverage is not the live-haul question presented here.

## Agency rationale and procedural posture

A representation petition was filed February 16, 1989. The Regional Director distinguished hatchery-related agricultural work from live haul. As to “live haul employees, designated as chicken catchers, who work out of the processing plant,” he concluded that neither primary nor secondary agriculture applied. He reasoned that when an employer contracts with farmers for care and feeding, its status as farmer ends as to that activity, citing *Imco Poultry*, 202 N.L.R.B. 259; he also found sufficient evidence of the live-haul workers’ functional integration with processing-plant employees to warrant inclusion with them. JA 21a–23a. The fresh review should distinguish these stated agency grounds from later elaborations in counsel’s brief.

The Board directed an election in a unit including the live-haul and Roaring River employees and excluding office employees, guards, and supervisors. JA 40a, 44a. The July 27, 1989 election resulted in a 95–95 tie after a determinative challenged ballot was resolved. The ALJ found an earlier union authorization-card majority and serious unfair labor practices making a fair rerun unlikely, and recommended a *Gissel* bargaining order. The ALJ acted March 12, 1992; the Board’s May 28, 1993 decision sustained unfair-labor-practice findings and the bargaining order. Member Oviatt dissented on the agricultural classification. Sources: lower decision; Board brief statement; relevant agency appendices.

Tyson acquired Holly Farms’ stock on July 18, 1989. Successor liability and numerous unfair-labor-practice details were litigated below, but they are not independently opened by the live-haul statutory question. The Fourth Circuit enforced the Board’s order. Judge Niemeyer dissented on agricultural classification and a separate successorship issue. The unit’s inclusion of live-haul employees is consequential to the challenged bargaining order; a conclusion about those employees does not erase unrelated findings involving covered workers without a separate remedial basis.

## Competing positions supported by the sources

**Petitioners.** The text’s “by a farmer or on a farm” means a processor’s employee can perform secondary agriculture on a farm even though the employer is not itself a farmer. Catching, cooping, and loading birds prepare them for market; drivers deliver them to market. Petitioners invoke the legislative treatment of independent threshing crews and the Department of Labor regulations. They argue the Board’s functional-integration approach improperly converts a statutory occupational exclusion into a test driven by the employer’s processing business. They further argue that Holly Farms resumes its farming role when it retakes possession of the birds; continuous title provides additional support in their view. Their merits brief also argues that catching/loading is primary agriculture as “harvesting.” Sources: petitioner brief pp. 8–15 and agriculture arguments; reply; lower dissent.

**Board.** Secondary agriculture requires the stated place/actor connection and an incident-or-conjunction relationship to the relevant farming operation. It reads “such” as preserving that relationship. It does not argue that the “on a farm” alternative disappears whenever the employer is not a farmer. It contends that the growers perform the raising, whereas the live-haul crews serve a distinct processing operation after the grower’s raising activity is complete. The growers do not employ or direct the crews. Continuous ownership does not make Holly Farms the entity doing the raising; retaking possession for processing does not itself resume poultry husbandry. Truckdrivers largely work off-farm, and “delivery to market” still must satisfy the statutory connection. Sources: Board brief pp. 8–15, 19–30; lower majority.

**Union.** The respondent union emphasizes the equipment, schedules, and staffing of the crews in relation to the processing plant’s operations. It characterizes live haul as the first phase of processing, not an incident of the growers’ farming. These are advocacy descriptions supported in part by the schedule and handling testimony; do not elevate every characterization into an agency finding. Union brief pp. 2–4 and argument.

**Preservation qualification.** The Board says the primary-agriculture/“harvesting” theory was not presented to the Board, the court of appeals, or in the petition, and first appeared in the merits brief. It invokes §160(e) and *Woelke & Romero Framing, Inc. v. NLRB*, 456 U.S. 645, 665–666 (1982). Its footnote explains that the lower court called the point conceded, but the more precise procedural statement is that it was not raised: the cited court-of-appeals opening brief p. 12 and reply p. 18 presented secondary agriculture. Board brief pp. 14–15 & n.13. This is a concrete preservation issue requiring treatment; a runtime shorthand saying no threshold issue is identified should not erase it. It does not foreclose the preserved secondary-agriculture question.

## Regulations and available precedent

Both sides rely on Labor Department interpretations in 29 C.F.R. pt. 780, particularly:

- §780.126: when dealers/processors contract with farmers to raise poultry and retain title, the farmer performs primary agriculture; the dealer/processor does not become a raiser simply on that basis. Employees’ work on a farm incident to raising can nevertheless qualify as secondary agriculture, with cross-reference to the secondary-agriculture provisions.
- §780.129: secondary practices must be performed by a farmer or on a farm, concern the relevant farmer’s operations or farming operations on that farm, and be incident to or in conjunction with them.
- §780.134: practices that cannot be performed on a farm, such as delivery to market, do not qualify when performed by a nonfarmer merely because they concern farm products.
- §§780.143–.144: the relation to farming depends on the circumstances; work not performed by or for the farmer is an indication against the required relationship, and an independently organized business can be distinct from the farming operation. The line cannot always be drawn with precision.
- §§780.150–.151(k): preparation for market must satisfy the other secondary-agriculture requirements; listed poultry practices include culling, grading, cooping, and loading. Petitioners emphasize the express list, while the Board emphasizes its conditional setting.

These are descriptions with source pinpoints in the Board’s pp. 19–30 and petitioners’ statutory arguments, not substitutes for an official regulation edition if exact quotation is needed. The statutes themselves control.

Waialua Agricultural Co.*, 349 U.S. 254 (1955); *Mitchell v. Budd*, 350 U.S. 473 (1956); *Bayside Enterprises, Inc. v. NLRB*, 429 U.S. 298 (1977); and *Johnston v. Cotton Producers Association*, 244 F.2d 553 (5th Cir. 1957).
