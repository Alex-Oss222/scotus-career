## Correct issue, posture, and disposition boundary

The question concerns authority to grant this postverdict acquittal after counsel filed one day beyond the then-operative deadline, not whether federal district courts lose all Article III criminal jurisdiction when a Rule 29 period expires. Rule 29(c) permitted a motion within seven days after jury discharge or within additional time **fixed during that seven-day period**. Rule 45(a)’s computation excluded intervening weekends for this short period; the July 13, 1993 verdict/discharge produced a July 22 deadline, and counsel filed July 23. The Government timely objected. There was no trial-stage motion, reserved decision, or timely extension. These facts exclude both a timely-renewal theory and an authorized-reservation theory on this record.

The district court first denied the late motion on sufficiency grounds, then changed its view at the October 14 sentencing hearing and entered an October 28 acquittal. It acknowledged lateness and relied on lack of Government prejudice and prevention of injustice. The court of appeals reversed for want of Rule 29 authority and directed reinstatement of the verdict and sentencing; it did **not** decide the sufficiency question, although both parties had briefed it. The district judge’s changed inference from the trial evidence is not an established finding of factual innocence or a new-evidence innocence claim.

The source extraction establishes intervening June 27, 1995 sentencing and a July 7 amended judgment. A disposition in April 1996 must account for those proceedings. It should not mechanically command a first sentencing as though none had occurred, infer that the sentence has been served, or order release without a lawful basis. The record does not establish the defendant’s precise present custody status. That uncertainty does not prevent resolution of the authority question and is not a litigant’s failure to prove an element of it.

Under predivergence *United States v. Wilson*, 420 U.S. 332 (1975), review seeking restoration of an existing guilty verdict, rather than a new trial, is not categorically barred by double jeopardy. An authority ruling still does not decide the unreviewed sufficiency issue. The disposition should state the consequence for the acquittal order and identify whatever ordinary further treatment of properly available sufficiency or sentencing matters its grounds actually require. A new trial is not the automatic remedy for a ruling that the late motion was unauthorized; neither is a new trial compatible with an eventual controlling determination of legally insufficient trial evidence.

## Rules, versions, and actual source of power

- Rule 29(a) expressly permits court-initiated acquittal after the evidence closes; its relationship to Rule 29(c)’s postverdict procedure is disputed. Petitioner argues the operative words contain no explicit terminal cutoff and that the caption cannot withdraw that power. Government answers that a free-standing postdischarge route would defeat (c)’s structure and Rule 45’s express limitation.
- Rule 45(b)’s ordinary excusable-neglect enlargement excludes Rules 29, 33, 34, and 35 except on the conditions those rules state. Its express exception must be addressed before invoking fairness or excusability.
- Rule 2 requires just determination, simplicity, fairness, and elimination of unjustifiable expense and delay. Whether it supports a permissible construction is distinct from whether it independently overrides an express restriction.
- Former Rule 57, the version relevant to the 1993 conduct, authorizes practice where no provision governs, consistent with the Rules. The amendment effective December 1, 1995 is separately verified in `RULE57_DATED_SOURCE_NOTE.md`. It permits practice consistent with federal law, federal rules, and local rules; its nonwillful-form protection concerns **local** rules, and its actual-notice protection concerns requirements absent from federal law/rules and local rules. The official pending-case clause applies the amendment only insofar as just and practicable. No universal retroactivity or national-deadline exemption is inferred.
- Section 1651 supplies appropriate extraordinary writs in aid of jurisdiction; it is not simply a second label for an otherwise prohibited Rule 29 extension. Section 2255 and appellate review have separate conditions, addressed below.

## Strongest competing paths

**Specific rule and timely objection.** The Government’s narrowest substantial position is that the stated Rule 29(c)/45(b) conditions govern this new, untimely motion and cannot be displaced by recharacterizing its disposition as sua sponte, inherent, or fair. This does not require holding that every deadline is jurisdictional or resolving the outer limits of court-initiated action **within** seven days. Nor does it require deciding a timely motion’s later reconsideration or abolishing every inherent power. The prejudice of filing one day late is different from whether the Rules confer an excuse power.

**Independent pre-sentence authority.** Petitioner’s substantial alternative is a limited power, while the criminal cause remains before the district court and before sentence, to prevent judgment on legally insufficient evidence despite counsel’s short delay. The textual Rule 29(a) claim should be answered on its own terms, with its relation to (c), not dismissed solely because no timely defense motion existed. The limited proposal invokes the judge’s firsthand trial knowledge and a serious constitutional interest; it is not merely a generalized request to disregard rules. Its difficulties are the express extension restriction, the absence of any reservation, the need to define why sentence rather than the specified deadline supplies the boundary, and the district judge’s own earlier contrary sufficiency conclusion. The fact that sentencing later occurred does not erase the historical pre-sentence setting of the challenged acquittal.

**Constitutional adequacy of remaining process.** A conviction unsupported by legally sufficient evidence raises *Jackson v. Virginia*, 443 U.S. 307 (1979), concerns. That does not establish a constitutional right to this particular late district motion. Conversely, references to appeals or §2255 are not self-proving demonstrations that every otherwise valid claim will receive equivalent review. The appropriate analysis distinguishes a reasonable opportunity to challenge sufficiency, counsel’s alleged default, the standards governing remaining review, and any actual constitutional injury. It should avoid deciding the disputed sufficiency facts under an authority-only grant or converting counsel’s characterization into an innocence finding.

**Extraordinary writ and assurance-based alternatives.** Coram nobis must satisfy its own exceptional grounds, including the relation to ordinary available remedies; *United States v. Morgan*, 346 U.S. 502 (1954), does not furnish a routine alternative deadline. The record reports no predeadline judicial assurance inducing this late filing, so *Harris Truck Lines* and *Thompson* do not supply that predicate merely because the judge later reconsidered. No finding about an unreported private conversation is invented. A remand demanding new proof of hypothetical assurances would lack a source-supported trigger.

## Independently checked predivergence authorities

*United States v. Smith*, 331 U.S. 469, 473–476 (1947), was independently read in its operative reasoning. It rejects the contention that Rule 33 limits the defendant’s motion while leaving an indefinite judicial power to grant identical new-trial relief on the court’s initiative. It stresses both the rule’s structure and finality after appellate affirmance. Petitioner can distinguish that completed appellate posture from a pre-sentence acquittal; a decision relying on *Smith* should explain why its rule-structure reasoning nonetheless extends, rather than pretend the cases have identical facts. Its old description of habeas as unlimited in time cannot be used as an April 1996 statutory statement.

*Fallen v. United States*, 378 U.S. 139, 142–144 (1964), was independently checked. The ill, incarcerated, effectively unrepresented defendant did all reasonably possible to transmit his notice within the appeal period; delay in prison mailing was not chargeable to him. Rule 2 informs that construction. It supplies petitioner a genuine purposive-reading argument, but not an established general excusable-neglect power for counsel’s admittedly late motion. The four-Justice concurrence treated timely delivery to prison officials as timely filing, further illustrating the distinction between defining compliance and forgiving noncompliance.

*Bank of Nova Scotia v. United States*, 487 U.S. 250, 254–256 (1988), was independently checked. Supervisory authority cannot circumvent Rule 52(a)’s harmless-error command; the Rule binds within its lawful scope. Scalia’s concurrence accepts supervision of proceedings before the court, while reserving broader direct supervision or prosecutor-discipline authority. This supports analysis of conflict with an actual rule, not a categorical statement that no inherent authority exists.

The clean source extraction verifies Kennedy’s *Arizona v. Manypenny*, 672 F.2d 761 (9th Cir. 1982), as permitting reconsideration of a **timely** Rule 29 motion while jurisdiction remained and expressly reserving other contexts. That opinion is a Justice-specific historical source, not Supreme Court law or a precommitted answer to the present missing-timeliness condition.

## Actual simulated law and Justice-specific support

**Pioneer (March 24, 1993).** The canonical bankruptcy holding and Standards were checked independently. The five-Justice Stone opinion, including Stevens and Kennedy, permits a full excusability assessment where Rule 9006(b)(1) authorizes it and attributes counsel’s conduct to the client. It expressly preserves (b)(2) prohibitions and (b)(3) specific-rule limitations. Therefore neither those Justices’ support for flexible excusability nor the result for a late claim establishes a Rule 29 exception. The authority/exception distinction is directly useful here.

**United States v. Williams (May 4, 1992).** The actual simulated Court recognizes a bounded supervisory duty and dismissal authority for known, directly negating, materially distorting grand-jury omissions, with the specified probable-cause and *Bank of Nova Scotia* prejudice conditions. Stevens, O’Connor, and Thomas joined the narrower merits formulation; Stone supplied the qualified additional support recorded in Holdings. This prevents importing the historical postdivergence categorical no-duty approach. It supplies a real source for considering bounded supervision, while its rule-consistent, prejudicial-misconduct setting does not itself decide power to disregard Rules 29/45.

**Olano (April 26, 1993).** O’Connor’s rule, joined by Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, and Stone, distinguishes forfeiture from intentional waiver and permits only conditioned, discretionary Rule 52(b) correction. A missed filing is not automatically knowing relinquishment. Possible appellate correction is not automatic relief and does not authorize the district court to enlarge Rule 29 on identical terms. The Government’s acknowledged uncertainty over differences between ordinary sufficiency and plain-error review remains material to the alternative-process argument.

**Herrera (January 25, 1993) and Medina (June 22, 1992).** The former supplies only a majority-supported rejection of that particular delayed, inconsistent new-evidence submission, with no general controlling freestanding-innocence burden or denial of such a right. The latter permits the defendant’s preponderance burden and corresponding presumption at an adequate competency hearing; Kennedy’s principal opinion and O’Connor/Souter’s concurrence share those bounded propositions. Neither case establishes that a legally insufficient conviction may stand, creates a free-floating fairness exception, or resolves this federal-rule authority question. Historical advocates’ broader postdivergence descriptions must be replaced by this actual law.

**Libretti (November 7, 1995).** The PUBLIC projection was read. All nine support the distinction between actual informed waiver and the statutory authority to impose punishment: consent can establish predicates but cannot create withheld power. O’Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Thomas, and Breyer support the particular forfeiture-classification/waiver holdings; Souter and Ginsburg preserve actual-understanding limits; Stevens’s remand position emphasizes independent assurance of statutory authority. These are useful sources for the separate concepts of authority and forfeiture, not an automatic conclusion about Rule 29.

**Lonchar (April 1, 1996).** The PUBLIC projection was read. All nine reject a free-standing equitable terminal sanction that bypasses governing habeas statutory and rule predicates while preserving lawful distinct stay discretion. Stevens, Kennedy, Souter, Ginsburg, and Breyer also support protection necessary to complete actually required process when execution would defeat it. O’Connor, Scalia, and Thomas reserve that broader conditional explanation; Stone does not join it. The two directions are both material: general equity cannot supplant the framework, and process genuinely required by law must remain meaningful. The first-petition/execution setting and pre-AEDPA statutory versions limit the analogy.

## April 24 statutory change and collateral channels
