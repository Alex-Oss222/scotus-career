A. Introduction. 


Respondent does not contest the most important 
point in this case: the peculiarly high burden that Okla- 
homa places on defendants to demonstrate their incom- 
petence to stand trial is unprecedented at common law 
and entirely foreign (until recently) to traditional Ameri- 
can criminal practice. Neither federal law nor the law of 
forty-six of the fifty states places so high a burden on 
defendants. This burden violates the fundamental right 
not to be tried while incompetent and corollary constitu- 
tional rights implicit in this fundamental right.! 


These corollary constitutional rights include: effective 
assistance of counsel, Strickland v. Washington, 466 U.S. 668 
(1984); waiving the privilege against compulsory self-incrimina- 
tion by taking the witness stand, Boykin v. Alabama, 395 U.S. 238 
(1969); the right to confront one’s accusers, Pointer v. Texas, 380 
U.S. 400 (1965); the right to testify on one’s own behalf, Rock v. 
Arkansas, 483 U.S. 44 (1987); acceptance of the guidance of coun- 
sel, Godinez v. Moran, U.S. 113 S.Ct. 2680 (1993) (citing 
Faretta v. California, 422 U.S. 806 (1975)); the right to remain 
silent without penalty for doing so, Griffin v. California, 380 U.S. 
609 (1965); and other rights and factors encompassed in these 
corollary constitutional rights which are relevant to the truth- 
seeking function. See Brief of Amicus National Association of 
Criminal Defense Lawyers 9-17; Riggins v. Nevada, 504 U.S. 127, 
139-45 (1992) (Kennedy, J, concurring in judgment).

B. Reliance On This Court’s Precedents Is Mis- 
placed And Sound Policy Fails To Justify Sup- 
port For Respondent's Position. 


Respondent relies on Addington v. Texas, 441 U.S. 418 
(1979), but that reliance is, to say the least, misplaced. 
Addington held that, in order to adequately protect an 
individual’s constitutional right to liberty, due process 
required that the government bear the high burden of clear 
and convincing evidence. 441 U.S. at 431-33. Addington in 
no way offers support for the proposition that the individ- 
ual should bear that high burden before his constitutional 
right will be honored; if anything, Addington’s solicitude 
for the constitutional rights of the individual argues pos- 
itively against Respondent's position. 


Accepting Respondent's position would mean that 
we are no longer asking that an individual share equally 
with the state the risk of error for an erroneous compe- 
tence finding, but saying the state’s interest is signifi- 
cantly more important than the defendant's fundamental 
right not to be tried while incompetent. This standard 
does exactly what Addington forbids: it strips away an 
individual's liberty interest — in this case Petitioner’s 
fundamental constitutional right not to be tried while 
incompetent - without minimal constitutional protec- 
tions. The Respondent asks this Court to condone a prac- 
tice that allows it to try factually incompetent 
individuals, contrary to the purpose of the Due Process 
Clause to protect individual interests, and contrary to this 
Court’s concern that we “jealously guard” the fundamen- 
tal right not to be tried while incompetent. Drope v. Mis- 
souri, 420 U.S. 162, 172, 173 (1975). 


This Court has used the clear and convincing stan- 
dard not as Respondent simplistically implies (i.e., when 
the interests at stake are deemed to be more substantial 
than the mere loss of money [Brief of Respondent 25), 
but more precisely, when the risk of harm to individual 
liberty is sufficiently important to require additional pro- 
tection against an erroneous decision. For example, a 
state could not satisfy the Addington standard by passing 
a civil commitment law that required an individual to 
prove he was not committable by clear and convincing 
evidence. The harm created by the clear and convincing 
standard is that a factually incompetent defendant will be 
adjudged in a trial he does not understand and in which 
he cannot even minimally participate. The Constitution 
does not tolerate this severe impairment of the truthseek- 
ing function of the criminal trial. 


Raising the standard does not reduce the risk of error, 
it merely reassigns it by requiring more incompetent 
defendants to stand trial. That is, the rate of error is not 
reduced; it is only the consequences of the errors that 
change. Indeed, as a logical matter, it may increase the 
number of mistaken competency findings by requiring 
the trier of fact to reject its own beliefs about the truth of 
the matter when those beliefs do not rise to the clear and 
convincing standard. 


Respondent’s reliance on Ohio v. Akron Center for 
Reproductive Health, 497 U.S. 502 (1990), is similarly mis- 
placed. There, this Court upheld the placement of a clear 
and convincing evidence burden upon a minor seeking a 
judicial bypass of a parental consent requirement. The 
Court did so, however, because of a critical fact omitted 


from Respondent's analysis: the judicial bypass pro- 
cedure was an ex parte one in which no litigant was 
present to advance state interests that might militate 
against the minor’s claim.? Thus, Akron is readily distin- 
guishable. 


The state is a most active litigant in the competency 
procedure. Adjudication of a defendant’s incompetence 
does not occur until the state has had every opportunity 
to develop the facts and make its position known. The 
state, if it chooses, in preparation for a competency hear- 
ing, may observe a defendant at a state hospital or jail 
mental ward 24 hours a day, seven days a week. It can 
subject the defendant to interviews with and evaluations 
by mental health professionals. The state can run count- 
less medical tests including testing blood levels after 
medication treatment. It can administer any and all avail- 
able psychological tests. The state has complete access to 
observation and continued observation of the defendant. 
No defense attorney or defense mental health expert will 
have any greater opportunity to develop evidence of 
incompetence.

C. A Uniform Standard Of Proof Has No Effect On 
The Committability Of Incompetent Individ- 
uals. 


Respondent implies that a criminal defendant who 
cannot meet the Addington clear and convincing test for 
civil commitment, but who establishes by a prepon- 
derance of the evidence he is incompetent and thereby 
suspends the criminal trial, may somehow be placed in a 
legal “limbo.” Brief of Respondent 31; Brief of Amici 13. 


The issues Respondent seeks to compare are not par- 
allel, much less identical. They are two different substan- 
tive standards that examine two different issues. The 
purpose of a criminal competency evaluation is to deter- 
mine whether an individual lacks the capacity to under- 
stand the nature of the proceedings and the ability to 
assist his counsel in preparing a defense. Dusky v. United 
States, 362 U.S. 402 (1960); Okla. Stat. tit. 22, 
§§ 1175.1-1175.8 (1991 & Supp. 1995). Civil commitment, 
on the other hand, requires existence of a mental disabil- 
ity which meets the code’s definition and potential dan- 
gerousness to either himself or others. O'Connor v. 
Donaldson, 442 U.S. 563, 575 (1975); Okla. Stat. tit. 43A, 
§§ 1-103(14), 5-401 (Supp. 1995). Thus, the fact that a 
preponderance standard exists on the issue of incompe- 
tence has absolutely no bearing on the civil determina- 
tion. Even when a defendant meets the Dusky standard by 


10 


clear and convincing evidence, beyond a reasonable 
doubt, or beyond all doubt, it remains theoretically possi- 
ble that the state would be unable to civilly commit him. 


Respondent has not shown and cannot show that this 
is a real-world problem. The state has made no showing 
that people charged with serious crimes are in this legal 
limbo. Respondent has provided no evidence of experi- 
ence from the forty-six states or the Federal Government, 
which have standards lower than clear and convincing 
proof for incompetence, indicating practical problems 
which would justify Respondent’s hypothetical concerns. 


In sum, comparing criminal incompetence to civil 
commitment is like comparing apples to oranges. Having 
identical standards of proof for commitment and compe- 
tence has no effect on the committability of incompetent 
defendants. Moreover, having different standards will not 
stand in the way of the commitment of any individual 
who needs commitment while the state seeks to restore or 
establish competence.“ Clearly, identical standards of 
proof will not cure the state’s purported legal limbo.”

CONCLUSION 


Respondent's placement of the clear and convincing 
burden on defendant can find no support in history, 
tradition, precedent or policy. In short, it finds no support 
in any of the sources to which this Court turns in ascer- 
taining the meaning of due process. It is no wonder, then, 
that Respondent is left to claim that “the due process 
provided to determine competence is left to the states.” 
Brief of Respondent 13. Under Respondent's no-holds- 
barred view of due process, it presumably would be 
constitutional for a state to require defendants to demon- 
strate their incompetency to stand trial by proof beyond a 
reasonable doubt, or for that matter by proof beyond any 
and all doubt. The due process clause is not, however, a 
carte blanche to the states. While it is for each state to 
prescribe, in the first instance, its rules of practice, it is 
ultimately for this Court to determine whether those 


14 


practices accord with fundamental principles of due pro- 
cess — or, conversely, whether they fail to “jealously 
guard” the fundamental rights of the individual, Pate 383 
U.S. at 385-86, and thus offend principles of justice that 
are “so rooted in the traditions and conscience of our 
people as to be ranked as fundamental.” Medina v. Califor- 
nia, 505 U.S. 437, 112 S.Ct. 2572, 2577 (1992) (quoting 
Patterson v. New York, 432 U.S. 197, 202 (1977)). 


Under the appropriate test, as stated in Medina, the 
practice that Oklahoma has adopted offends due process. 
As no one disputes, Oklahoma’s practice will bring to 
trial as competent defendants who would be deemed 
incompetent in the overwhelming majority of American 
jurisdictions. It is difficult in the extreme to see how such 
a practice can be deemed “adequate” to protect an indi- 
vidual’s fundamental constitutional right not to stand 
trial while incompetent. It is a gross departure from the 
accepted range of historical practice, and it finds no 
support in precedent or sound reason.
