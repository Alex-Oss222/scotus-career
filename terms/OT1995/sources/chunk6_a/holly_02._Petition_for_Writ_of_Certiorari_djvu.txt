STATEMENT OF CASE 


For many years, Petitioner Holly 
Farms ("Holly") has been engaged in 
the production and marketing of poul- 
try products at various facilities 
throughout the United States, includ- 
ing Monroe and Wilkesboro, North Car- 
olina; Glen Allen, Harrisonburg, and 
Temperanceville, Virginia; and Center 
and Seguin, Texas. Its operations 
have included hatcheries, live- haul 
operations, feed mills, feed haul 
operations, maintenance facilities, 
processing plants, and transportation 
operations. (A-227-29.) 

A. The Transportation Operations 

In early January 1989,’ Chauf- 


feurs, Teamsters and Helpers, Local 


> All dates are 1989 unless otherwise

Union Nos. 29, 71, 355, 391, 592, 
657, and 988 (hereinafter collective- 
ly “the Union") filed a representa- 
tion petition with the National Labor 
Relations Board ("NLRB" or “Board") 
seeking to represent the drivers and 
yardmen employed by Holly in its long 
haul transportation operations at 
Monroe and Wilkesboro, North Caro- 
lina; Glen Allen, Harrisonburg, and 
Temperanceville, Virginia; and Center 
and Seguin, Texas. The NLRB con- 
ducted a secret-ballot election, 
which resulted in the Union’s being 
certified on March 24 as the collec- 
tive bargaining representative for 
these transportation employees. 
(A-229, A-233.) 

On April 11, Holly began negoti- 


ating with the Union for a collective 


bargaining agreement to cover the 
long haul transportation employees. 
The parties stipulated that these 
negotiations were conducted in good 
faith. (A-236, A-597-98.) 

While these negotiations were 
continuing, Petitioner Tyson Foods, 
Inc. ("Tyson"), was negotiating to 
purchase the stock of Holly. Tyson, 
like Holly, was engaged in the pro- 
duction and marketing of poultry 
products at various locations 
throughout the United States. Its 
operations included hatcheries, live- 
haul operations, feed mills, feed 
haul operations, maintenance facili- 
ties, processing plants, and trans-~- 
portation operations. 

Although Tyson and Holly were 


engaged in similar businesses, the 


transportation operations of the two 
companies were fundamentally differ- 
ent. Holly’s long haul transporta- 
tion operation existed primarily to 
deliver Holly products to Holly cus- 
tomers. The long haul operations at 
Holly operated on a cost basis and 
were largely controlled by the pro- 
duction schedule at their respective 
processing plants. (A-667-68.) Each 
plant maintained a dispatch office 
from which loads were assigned and 
trucks were dispatched. Drivers and 
their trucks were dispatched on a 
first-in, first-out basis, and an 
“extra board" was maintained from 
which additional drivers could be 
selected when needed to haul a load 
or team up with another driver to 


haul a load on a trip that would re- 


quire a single driver to exceed his 
allotted drive time. (A-666, A-669- 
70.) 

Although Holly tried to lower 
its transportation costs by securing 
backhaul business, it was largely 
unsuccessful. Approximately 50 per- 
cent of Holly’s return miles were 
"deadhead," or empty, miles, result- 
ing in an annual estimated loss of 
$5 million to $6 million. (A-73-74.) 

Tyson’s transportation opera- 
tions were not represented by any 
union and were much larger than 
Holly’s, consisting of approximately 
550 drivers compared to Holly’s 220 
drivers. (A-666-67.) In contrast to 
Holly’s transportation system, Tyson 
Transportation operated as an inde- 


pendent, profit-seeking enterprise. 


Tyson hauled only 35 percent of 
Tyson’s products, with the balance 
being hauled by common carriers, al- 
lowing Tyson to limit its hauling to 
areas where it had established back- 
haul customers, which created greater 
backhaul revenues. Tyson’s empty 
mileage ratio was between 7.5 percent 
and 12 percent, and Tyson Transporta- 
tion has generated a profit every 
year since 1980. (A-117-18, A-601, 
A-667.) 

Unlike Holly, Tyson operated as 
a common carrier, and each trip made 
by a driver consisted of multiple 
legs in order to generate backhaul 
revenue. Whereas Holly drivers would 
typically be gone three to four days 
at a time, Tyson drivers were fre- 


quently gone for ten days to two 


weeks at a time. Tyson’s fleet man- 
ager concept enabled trucks to be 
coordinated through a central loca- 
tion rather than being dispatched 
from each plant. (A-117-18, A-669- 
83.) 

On July 18, Tyson purchased the 
stock of Holly. When Tyson purchased 
Holly’s stock, it made no immediate 
changes in Holly’s transportation 
division. Holly continued to operate 
as a separately identifiable company. 
For two months after the acquisition, 
nothing changed for the Holly trans- 
portation employees, all of whom con- 
tinued on the Holly payroll, under 
the same terms and conditions of em- 
ployment. (A-101.) The parties 
stipulated that during this two-month 


period, Holly continued to engage in 


meaningful negotiations with the 
Union for a contract. Agreements 
were reached on some additional is- 
sues, but no final contract was 
achieved. (A-94-99, A-598-628.) 

On September 11, Tyson made a 
decision to discontinue the Holly 
methods of transportation, which were 
continuing to lose money, and to 
merge these operations with Tyson’s 
transportation operations under the 
Tyson method of operations. It was 
further decided that all Holly trans- 
portation employees would be offered 
employment in the newly integrated 
operation under Tyson’s established 
terms and conditions of employment. 
(A-617-18.) 

On September 12, Holly informed 


the Union that its transportation 


10 


operations would cease to exist on 
September 22 and that Holly drivers 
would be receiving offers of employ- 
ment with Tyson Transportation, to be 
effective on September 22. Holly 
declined to bargain over the integra- 
tion decision, but offered to bargain 
over the effects of the integration. 
Holly, however, took the position 
that the Tyson terms and conditions 
of employment were part of the inte- 
gration decision and not part of the 
effects of that decision. The Union 
rejected this offer. (A-97-99.) 
Concurrent with the announcement 
to the Union, Holly notified all 
drivers that Tyson was taking over 
the transportation operation and ad- 
vised the drivers that they were be- 


ing offered jobs by Tyson. (A-99.) 


11

the employees accept Tyson’s offer of 
employment. (A-779.) All but 47 of 
Holly’s 220 transportation employees 
accepted employment with Tyson. 
These 47 drivers declined to accept 
employment under the Tyson terms and 
conditions of employment. (A-631- 
36.)

A temporary terminal was estab-

hauling operations for the Monroe,

C. The NLRB Proceedings 

The NLRB acquired jurisdiction 
under Sections 10(a) and (b) of the 
NLRA, 29 U.S.C. §§ 160(a) & (b) 
(1973). Pursuant to Section 10(c) of 
the Act, 29 U.S.C. § 160(c) (Supp. 
1995), a hearing was held before 
Administrative Law Judge ("ALJ") 
Robert M. Schwarzbart beginning on 
November 27, 1989, and concluding on 
May 2, 1990. 

The ALJ issued his decision on 
March 12, 1992. He concluded, inter 
alia, that Tyson became a successor 
to Holly’s bargaining obligation on 


July 18; that Tyson unlawfully failed 


18 


to bargain over the decision to inte- 
grate the two transportation opera- 
tions and its effects; that Tyson 
unlawfully unilaterally imposed its 
terms and conditions of employment on 
the former Holly drivers, thereby un- 
lawfully constructively discharging 
the 47 drivers who rejected these 
terms; that Tyson unlawfully withdrew 
recognition from the Union; and that 
the strike of October 1 was an unfair 
labor practice strike. The ALJ also 


concluded that a bargaining order 


under NLRB v. Gissel Packing Co., 395 


U.S. 575, reh’g denied, 396 U.S. 869 
(1969), should be issued in the live- 


haul bargaining unit where the Union 
had lost an election. (A-206-935.) 
Exceptions to the ALJ’s decision 


were filed with the Board. On May

Board agreed with the ALJ that Tyson 
became a successor to Holly’s bar- 
gaining obligation on July 18. (A-

cluded that the decision to integrate 
the two transportation divisions in-

D. The Court Of Appeals Proceedings 


On June 7, 1993, Petitioners 
timely filed a petition for review 
with the United States Court of Ap- 
peals for the Fourth Circuit. On 
July 19, 1993, the Board filed a 
cross-application for enforcement of 
its order. The court of appeals ac- 
quired jurisdiction pursuant to Sec- 
tions 10(e) and (f) of the Act, 29 
U.S.C. §§ 160(e) & (f) (1973). These 
provisions specify no time in which a 
party must seek review of a Board 
order. 

On March 10, 1995, a panel of 
the court of appeals enforced the 
Board’s order. Judge Niemeyer dis- 
sented in part. Judge Niemeyer con- 
Cluded that "the majority opinion 


Significantly misconstrues the law of 


22 


successorship liability when it re- 
fuses to recognize the existence of 
two corporate entities for the two 
months after the stock purchase by 
Tyson." (A-55.)
