STATEMENT OF THE CASE 
The Petitioner, Byron Keith Cooper, was charged in Oklahoma 


County Case Number CRF-89-5084 with Murder in the First Degree. 
The death penalty was sought by the prosecution. On May 22, 1992 
the jury found the Petitioner guilty of the charge and assessed the 
penalty of death. On June 5, 1992 the trial court formally imposed 
the Judgment and Sentence. The Oklahoma Court of Criminal Appeals 
delivered its Opinion in said cause on January 10, 1995, wherein 
Petitioner’s conviction and death sentence were affirmed. 

In affirming Petitioner’s sentence of death, the Oklahoma 
Court of Criminal Appeals rejected Petitioner’s argument that by 
placing a burden of proof on him of clear and convincing evidence 
to determine incompetency his Eighth and Fourteenth Amendment 
rights were violated. The Court of Criminal Appeals held that 
“forcing a criminal defendant to prove by clear and convincing 
evidence he is incompetent is not subject to proscription under the 
Due Process Clause, because it does not “offend some principle of 
justice so rooted in the tradition and conscience of our people as 
to be ranked as fundamental. Medina, 505 U.S. at Cooper vy. 
State, 889 P. 2d 293 (Ok1.Cr.1995). 


This Court should grant certiorari to resolve the issue 
of whether a state may, consistent with the Eighth and 
Fourteenth Amendments, allocate a level of proof as hi 
as clear and convincing evidence on a defendant 
determining his competency to stand trial. 


In Oklahoma, the competency statutes mandate that an accused 
is presumed competent, as a matter of law, and has the burden of 


-j3- 


proving incompetency (and disproving competency). The court shall 
determine by clear and convincing evidence if the defendant is 
incompetent. Okla. Stat. tit. 22, § 1175.4(B) (Supp.1994). A 
clear and convincing burden placed on an accused to prove 
incompetency is such a harsh standard that it violates Petitioner’s 
right to due process under the Fourteenth Amendment. 

Prior to trial, in August, 1990, Petitioner was examined by 
state psychologist Dr. Edith King and determined by the court to be 
incompetent to stand trial. (C.H. 4, 32). Petitioner was sent to 
Eastern State Hospital in Vinita, Oklahoma, to receive psychiatric 
treatment before trial. Upon his return from Vinita, Petitioner 
underwent a post-competency hearing to determine if he was, at that 
time, competent to proceed with the action against him. (C.H. 3) 
(Dec. 20, 1990; Jan. 18, 1991). The court determined that, under 
Oklahoma law, Petitioner was competent to stand trial. (C.H. 101) 
(Dec. 20, 1990; Jan. 18, 1991). Thereafter, prior to trial, 
Petitioner had another hearing on his competency after appearing in 
jail coveralls and saying his clothes his attorney brought were 
burning him. Additionally, Petitioner’s hair was poked out and 
going in every direction and “he looks like heck" according to his 
counsel. (C.H. 3, 4) (May 4, 5, 1992).

This Court has consistently ruled that state laws controlling 
competency procedures must be “adequate” to provide meaningful and 
reliable safeguarding of an accused’s right not to be subjected to 


Medina v. California, 505 


„ 112 S.Ct. 2572 (1992), rehearing denied, 113 8. ct. 


(1992); Drope v. Missouri, 420 U.S. 162, 95 S.Ct. 896 (1975); Pate 
vy. Robinson, 383 U.S. 375, 277, 86 S.Ct. 836, 838 (1966); 
Const. amends. V, VI, VIII, XIV. 

The clear and convincing standard of proof required of a 
defendant is unconstitutional in that it fails to provide 
anticipatory, protective procedures which minimize the risk that an 
incompetent person will be subjected to criminal procedures and 
offends the recognized principles of fundamental fairness. 
violates “powerful social standards of a progressive society." 


Medina vy. California, 112 S.Ct. 2572, 2582 (1992) (Justice o/ connor 
and Justice Souter concurring in judgment). 

Petitioner was forced to attempt to prove that he was 
incompetent at the time of trial by evidence which is “clear and 
convincing." Okla. Stat. tit. 22, § 1175.4(B) (Supp.1994). 
Oklahoma standard jury instructions define the burden of proof 
followed by the judge in the instant case: 

When I say that a party has the burden of proving any 

proposition by clear and convincing evidence, I mean that 

you must be persuaded, considering all the evidence in 

the case, that the proposition on which the party has 

this burden of proof is true, and that this conclusion is 

unmistakable and free from serious or substantial doubt 

as to its correctness. OUJI-CIV 3.2 (emphasis added) 

Invoking this standard of proof resulted in the failure of 
Petitioner’s strong argument of incompetency, because it was not 
strong enough to hurdle the burden of being “unmistakable.” 

The correct burden of proof that should have been applied in 
Petitioner’s competency proceeding, is that of a preponderance of 
the evidence, which is defined as being more probably true than not 
true. The stricter standard of "clear and convincing" invoked by 
Oklahoma constitutes an inadequate anticipatory protective 
procedure, and is therefore violative of the Petitioner’s rights to 
due process pursuant to U.S. Const. amend. XIV. 

The case of Medina v. California, 505 U.S. _, 112 S.Ct. 2572 
(1992), rehearing denied, 113 S.Ct. 19 (1992), deals with placement 
of the burden of proof in competency proceedings. In Medina, this 
Court held that the California statute, which mandated a defendant 


to prove his competency, did not violate fundamental fairness as 


required by the Due Process Clause of U.S. Const. amend. XIV. The 
major factor that distinguishes Medina from the facts presently at 
issue, is that the California statute only required preponderance 
of the evidence as the standard of proof, while the Oklahoma 
statute requires clear and convincing evidence as its standard of 
proof. 

In the present case, Petitioner was determined by the court to 
be incompetent to stand trial in August, 1990. It is therefore 
against fundamental principles of fairness to demand that 
Petitioner take responsibility for proving by clear and convincing 
evidence his incompetency in further competency proceedings, when 
it has been established that his ability to do so is questionable. 

Petitioner, after he was adjudicated incompetent, was required 
to prove that he was still incompetent by clear and convincing 
evidence. Although a Legislature has the power to decide the 
allocation of the burdens of producing evidence and of persuasion, 
it is not empowered to allocate these burdens in a way that 
“offends some principle of justice so rooted in the traditions and 
conscience of our people as to be ranked as fundamental." Speiser 
vy. Randall, 357 U.S. 513, 78 S.Ct. 1332, 1341 (1958). 

In litigation, it is clear the level of the burden of proof 
may be the determinative factor in the outcome of an issue. Cf, 
Spe.gitr vy. Randall, 357 U.S. at 525, 78 S.Ct. at 1342 (1958). 
Placing a clear and convincing burden on a defendant, especially 
one who has already been determined incompetent, places a 


substantial risk that an incompetent individual may be convicted or 


-7J7- 


executed. This procedure flies in the face of the notions of 
fundamental fairness in the judicial system, in that it is the 
exact opposite of what due process guarantees to an accused. U.S. 
Const. amend. XIV. 

Subjecting Petitioner to a criminal proceeding, especially one 
for which death is the penalty, without a fair and constitutional 
finding that Petitioner was competent to stand trial, violates due 
process, and constitutes cruel and unusual punishment pursuant to 
U.S. Const. amends. VIII and XIV.

a state district court failed to give an instruc-

Kentucky Supreme Court reversed, feeling 
such an instruction was mandated by Taylor

failure must be evaluated in light of the 
totality of the circumstances—including all 
the instructions to the jury, the arguments of

also Hyatt v. State, 779 P.2d 993, 995 (Oki.

sal. As noted above, it seems clear the flight 
instruction was given here not based on Ap-

crime. The evidence showed when Appellant

crack cocaine. He was arrested for posses- 
sion of drug paraphernalia based on this 
charge. 


Therefore, Mitchell is not applicable here,

charged. See Compton u State, 74 Okl.Cr.

consciousness of guilt and for the purpose of 
evading arrest). Accordingly, we find any 
error in giving the flight instruction was 
harmless, as the misdirection of the jury has 
not “resulted in a miscarriage of justice,” or 
has not constituted “a substantial violation of 
a constitutional or statutory right.” 20 0. 8.

difficulty is that the words ‘moral certain- 
ty add nothing to the words ‘beyond a 
reasonable doubt,’ as one may require ex- 
planation as much as the other. We also 
observe that Gray, supra, as well as a 
number of more recent cases, holds that it 
is not necessary for the trial court to de- 
fine what is meant by a ‘reasonable doubt,’ 
and trial courts have been repeatedly ad- 
monished against giving any instruction 
attempting to define the term ‘reasonable 
doubt.’ 


In more recent years, trial courts have 
been severely criticized by this Court for 
attempting to define the term ‘reasonable 
doubt.’ See Bell u State, Okl. Or., 381 P.2d 
167 (1963); and, it has even beer held 
reversible error to so define this difficulty- 
of-definition term. Wilson v. State, Okl. 
Cr., 403 P.2d 262 (1965); Lee u State, 
Okl. Or., 430 P.2d 858 (1967). 
We reiterate the phrase ‘reasonable doubt’ 
is self-explanatory, and definitions thereof 
do not clarify its meaning, but rather tend 
to confuse a jury; therefore, instructions 
defining it are unnecessary, and should not 
be given. The same logic should apply in 
attempts in voir dire examinations to ex- 
plain to the jury what is meant by ‘reason- 
able doubt.’ 

Templer, 494 P.2d at 671-72. See also Victor 

v. Nebroska, 511 U.S. ——, 114 S.Ct. 1239, 

127 L.Ed.2d 583 (1994). 


Accordingly, Appellant’s sixth proposition 
is without merit. |

prejudice or any other arbitrary factor. 


VIII. CONCLUSION 


Finding no error warranting reversal or 
modification, the judgment and sentence for 
Murder in the First Degree is AFFIRMED.
