The National Association of Criminal Defense Lawyers 
{hereinafter NACDL] is a non-profit corporation with a

The NACDL is greatly concerned by the State of 
Oklahoma’s position, which seeks to undermine the constitutional 
right not to be tried and sentenced while incompetent. In the 
experience of the members of the NACDL, it is not possible to 
provide reasonably effective representation to defendants who are 
mentally incapable of understanding the charges against them and 
of assisting in their defense. The State’s position, if accepted by

information that would lead to their acquittal at trial. The State’s 
position would also undermine the reliability of capita) sentences 
because defendants who are unable to assist their attorneys are — 
by definition — unable to bring relevant mitigating circumstances 
to the attention of their counsel and the courts.

SUMMARY OF ARGUMENT 


Requiring a capital defendant to prove incompetency to 
stand trial by a evidentiary standard as high as clear and 
convincing is a violation of the Fourteenth Amendment's Due 
Process Clause, as well as the Eighth Amendment's prohibition 
against cruel and unusual punishments, which is applied to the 
States through the Fourteenth Amendment. 


The high evidentiary standard results in a fundamentally 
unfair trial, in violation of the Due Process Clause. In Medina v. 
California, 505 U.S. 437 (1992), the Court focused on historical 
tradition and contemporary norms to determine whether a 
procedure was fundamental to a fair trial. Nevertheless, Medina 
members of the Court as to the extent that the interests of the 
parties should be included in the due process analysis. Amicus 
curiae submits that in assessing whether a State’s procedure results 
in an unacceptable risk of a fundamentally unfair trial, the Court 


4 


should balance the interesis of the parties and consider historical 
tradition and contemporary norms. 


Applying this test, the Due Process Clause prohibits the 
State from requiring a defendant to prove incompetency by a 
standard higher than preponderance of the evidence. Any higher 
standard leads to a substantially greater likelihood of factually 
incompetent defendants being forced to trial. A lawyer 
representing an incompetent defendant will be unable to provide 
the effective assistance of counsel required by the Sixth 
Amendment because so many aspects of the defense are based 
upon the client’s ability to cooperate and provide information. 
Given the inexactitude of psychiatric diagnoses, such a high 
standard means that many factually incompetent defendants will be 
unable to prove their own incompetency.- The interests of the State 
in prosecuting swiftly are outweighed by the compelling interests 
of defendants not to be tried while incompetent, and by the fact 
that defendants found incompetent are committed to mental health 
facilities, and hence pose no danger to the community. 


Further, there is a strong common law tradition supporting 
preponderance of the evidence as the appropriate standard of proof 
to determine whether an accused is competent to stand trial. At 
common law, an incompetent defendant could not be tried. The 
standard of proof of competency has traditionally been 
preponderance of the evidence because the procedures to determine 
competency in criminal cases were modeled on the civil 
competency processes. Also, contemporary norms, as evidenced 
by the practice of the overwhelming number of jurisdictions, favor 
a preponderance of the evidence standard.

A State's calibration of proof violates the Due Process 
Clause of the Fourteenth Amendment if it offends some 
fundamental and deeply-rooted principle of justice. Medina v. 
California, 505 U.S. 437, 446 (1992); Patterson v. New York, 432

the Court have not always agreed, however, on the extent to which 
the parties’ interests ought to be weighed. Amicus curiae submits 
that the Court should expressly consider the relative harms to the 
State and to the accused in determining whether a high standard of 
proof denies a person a fundamentally fair trial. The due process 
calculus ought to include more than a search through history. It

In Medina, however, the Court moderated its mode of 
analysis. Ruling that the accused can be required to bear the 
burden of proving mental incompetency, a five-justice majority 
announced that “the Mathews balancing test does not provide the 
appropriate framework for assessing the validity of state procedural 
rules which . . . are part of the criminal process." Medina, 505 
U.S. at 443. The majority opinion in Medina focuses, to a large 
extent, on historical tradition. Nevertheless, in its opinion, the 
Court does appear to balance the interests at stake. See id. at 448 
("we turn to consider whether the rule transgresses any recognized 
principle of ‘fundamental fairness’ in operation" (citation omitted)); 
id. at 449 (discussing the relationship between a claim of 
incompetency and a defense of not guilty by reason of insanity). 
Indeed, in her concurrence in Medina, Justice O’Connor notes, "! 
read the Court's opinion to allow some weight to be given 
countervailing considerations of fairness in operation, 
considerations much like those we evaluated in Mathews." Id. at 
454 (O’Connor, J., concurring). Justice O’Connor found the 
Mathews test "a useful guide". Jd. at 453. Her opinion identifies 
a number of relevant considerations in determining whether the 
placement of the burden of proof on the defendant is fundamentally 
unfair, including whether the placement of the burden of proof on 
the State is necessary to enforce a further right protected by the 
Constitution, such as the right to be tried while competent. /d. at 
455. 


Though the majority in Medina places great weight on 
historical tradition, subsequent decisions rely more extensively on 
other considerations. The issue in Parke v. Raley, 506 U.S. 20, 
113 S.Ct. 517 (1992) was whether the defendant could be required 
to bear the burden of challenging prior convictions under a 
recidivist statute. Writing for the Court, Justice O’Connor cites 
Medina and discusses histori *| tradition and contemporary 
practice. Raley, 113 S.Ct. at 524-25. But the Court’s opinion 
also fully considers the interests at stake. It notes the State’s 
interests in punishing habitual criminals. /d. at 522. It addresses 


the requirement that a guilty plea be knowing and voluntary, as 


held in Boykin v. Alabama, 395 U.S. 238 (1969). Raley, 113 
S.Ct. at 523. Finally, the opinion weighs the relative ability of 
each side to reconstruct records of prior convictions. /d. at 524- 
25. 


Later the same Term, the Court decided Godinez v. 
Moran, _ U.S. _, 113 S. Ct. 2680 (1993). In ruling that the 
decisions to plead guilty and to waive counsel do not require a 
higher level of competency than that necessary to stand trial, the 
Court again considers the interests of the parties. The Court 
discusses the competency required to waive certain rights in the 
context of the nature of those underlying rights. Godinez, 113 S. 
Ct. at 2686-87. All criminal defendants must make important 
decisions once proceedings have been initiated. /d. at 2686. 
Decisions to plead guilty are not more complicated than other 
decisions that the accused must make. /d. And, "[t}his being so, 
we can conceive of no basis for demanding a higher level of 
competence for those defendants who choose to plead guilty." /d. 
In addition, "a trial court must satisfy itself that the waiver of [the 
defendant's] constitutional rights is knowing and voluntary," /d. at 
2687. The Court’s extensive discussion of the interests of the 
parties prompted a concurrence that restates the holding in Medina, 
and focuses on historical tradition. /d. at 2688, 2689-91 
(Kennedy, J., concurring). 


In deciding whether a heightened standard violated Mr. 
Cooper’s right to due process, the court below placed undue 
reliance upon historical tradition. See Cooper v. State, 889 P.2d 
293, 301-02 (Okla. Crim. App. 1995). This Court’s analysis has 
not been so narrowly cabined. In determining the process due in 
criminal cases, the Court should consider the interests of both the 
State and the accused at least where, as here, the interests of the 
accused are those independently protected by the Constitution. 
After all, "[t}he concept of due process is, ‘perhaps, the least 
frozen concept of our law — the least confined to history and the 


most absorptive of powerful social standards of a progressive 
society."" Medina, 505 U.S. at 454 (O’Connor, J., concurring), 


9 


quoting Griffin v. Illinois ,351 U.S. 12, 20-21 (1956) (Frankfurter, 
J., concurring in judgment). Where a State’s procedure 
substantially affects a defendant’s ability to exercise rights that are 
independently protected by the Constitution, the impact of the 
procedure on those rights ought to be part of the due process 
calculus. 


B. The balance of equities strongly favors a 
preponderance of the evidence standard. 


1. An accused has compelling interests in 
being tried while competent and in 
receiving the effective assistance of 
counsel. 


The petitioner here, and defendants in similar situations, 
have two separate but related constitutional interests that are 
threatened by a standard of proof as high as clear and convincing 
evidence. 


The first interest is the constitutional right to be tried while 
competent. An accused’s competency is essential to a fair criminal 
proceeding. In Drope v. Missouri, 420 U.S. 162 (1975), the Court 
acknowledged the long-accepted rule that "a person whose mental 
condition is such that he lacks the capacity to understand the nature 
and object of the proceedings against him, to consult with counsel, 
and to assist in preparing his defense may not be subjected to a 
trial" and held that this prohibition "is fundamental to an adversary 
system of justice.” Drope, 420 U.S. at 171-72; see also American 
Bar Association Criminal Justice Mental Health Standards, Std. 7- 
4.1(6) (1989) [hereinafter ABA Mental Health Standards]

Kennedy has explained, “[clompetence to stand trial is 
rudimentary, for upon it depends the main part of those rights 
deemed essential to a fair trial, including the right to effective 
assistance of counsel... ." Riggins v. Nevada, 504 U.S. 127, 


10 
139-40 (1992) (Kennedy, J., concurring in the judgment). 


A second interest at stake, as identified by Justice 
Kennedy, is the Sixth Amendment right to effective assistance of 
counsel. A defendant who is tried while incompetent will likely 
also be denied his or her Sixth Amendment right because he or she 
will not be able to communicate sufficiently with an attorney to 
enable that attorney to provide effective representation. As the 
Court has stated, "[t}he purpose of the Sixth Amendment guarantee
