INTEREST OF AMICI 


Amici States, through their respective attorneys 
general, share an interest in preserving a cornerstone principle 
of federalism: that States be permitted reasonable latitude to 
develop criminal prosecution procedures that promote speedy 
and just trial of serious crimes. At the same time, amici 
acknowledge their commitment to obey the constitutional 
command that a criminal defendant not be tried unless he or 
she understands the nature of the proceedings, and is 


reasonably able to assist defense counsel. See Dusky v. 
United States, 362 U.S. 402 (1960) (per curiam). 


STATEMENT OF THE CASE 


Amici adopt the case statement of respondent State of 
Oklahoma. We observe that the evidence of Cooper’s guilt 
appears compelling, while evidence of Cooper’s incompetency 
to stand trial is weak. On direct appeal, the Oklahoma Court 
of Criminal Appeals found the record to be "virtually 


brimming with evidence Appellant was malingering." Cooper 
v. State, 889 P.2d 293, 313 (Okla. Crim. App. 1995). 


Amici note Oklahoma’s recitation that the trial court 
found Cooper incompetent in August 1990, and ordered him 
hospitalized. Several months later, the court found Cooper 
competent, applying Oklahoma’s presumption of competency 
and finding that presumption not overcome by "clear and 
convincing” evidence. In his merits brief, Cooper does not 
emphasize the August 1990 incompetency finding; he simply 
argues that the "clear and convincing” incompetency proof 
standard is unconstitutional in all situations. Following suit, 
this brief responds to that general argument. 


SUMMARY OF ARGUMENT 


Consistent with its stated due process approach, this 
Court should deferentially review Oklahoma’s statutory 
requirement of clear and convincing proof for incompetency 
to proceed. Deference is also proper because Cooper facially 
challenges Oklahoma’s proof standard, which requires him to 
demonstrate that the statute could not be constitutional under 
any circumstance. Further, trial competency and related 
issues are now governed by a comprehensive matrix of 
constitutional law; federalism principles counsel respect for 
State lawmaking in the interstices of this legal matrix. 


The “clear and convincing" incompetency proof 
standard reasonably accommodates conflicting legal and 
pragmatic pressures. The public and criminal defendants have 
a powerful interest in expeditious criminal prosecutions. 
Criminal defendants also have an interest against being 
erroneously found incompetent. Requiring that incompetency 
be clearly and convincingly proven, rather than proven merely 
by a preponderance of evidence, protects all these interests. 
Additionally, constitutional rules governing competency- 
related issues can create a dispositional “limbo” in which a 
defendant may be found incompetent to proceed, yet the State 
is barred from effectively treating the defendant to restore 
competency. This problem may be reduced by requiring that 
incompetency to proceed be proven by clear and convincing 
evidence, which is also the constitutionally required proof 
standard for civilly committing mentally ill persons. 


The requirement that incompetency be clearly and 
convincingly proven does not impair basic rights of a criminal 
defense. Most particularly, a defendant remains entitled to an 
inquiry whenever a doubt exists about competency; this 
reduces the risk that a truly incompetent defendant will be 
tried and convicted. And the "clear and convincing” 
incompetency proof standard does not affect the right to raise 
mental illness-related defenses at trial. In fact, because the 
"clear and convincing" standard guards against compelled 
psychotropic medication of a criminal defendant, it can aid 
such defenses by its tendency to assure that the defendant’s 
baseline mental condition will be observed at trial. In light 
of all these concerns, it is reasonable, and constitutional, to 
require clear and convincing proof of incompetency to 
proceed. 


ARGUMENT 


THE "CLEAR AND CONVINCING" STANDARD 
FOR PROVING INCOMPETENCY SERVES 
IMPORTANT INTERESTS AND REASONABLY 
ACCOMMODATES CONFLICTING LEGAL 
AND PRAGMATIC PRESSURES; THEREFORE, 
IT IS CONSTITUTIONAL 


At the outset, Amici acknowledge the problem 
seemingly posed by this case: By requiring that 
incompetency to proceed be proven by "clear and convincing" 
evidence, Oklahoma’s statute, Okla. Stat. Ann. tit. 22 § 
1175.4B (Supp. 1995), requires a criminal defendant who is 
"more likely than not" incompetent, yet is not "clearly and 
convincingly” so, to face trial. This problem forms the basis 
for Cooper’s arguments before this Court. 


Despite this apparent problem, Oklahoma is not alone 
in requiring "clear and convincing" proof of incompetency. 
Connecticut, Pennsylvania, and Rhode Island also use this 
standard. Conn. Gen. Stat. § 54-56d(b) (1995); Pa. Stat. 
Ann., Tit. 50, § 7402(c), (d) (Supp. 1995); R.I. Gen. Laws. 
§ 40.1-5.3-3 (Supp. 1994). The remaining States, by statute 
or case law, generally utilize the “preponderance” standard 
(see appendix I of this brief). The question, then, is whether 
constitutional principles permit the “clear and convincing" 
stan «@ for proving incompetency. Amici respectfully 
answer in the affirmative. 


Amici begin by identifying the parameters of the 
constitutional analysis. Several important principles define 
the nature of this Court’s review. 


A 


5 First, the pertinent constitutional provision is the Due 
Process Clause, U.S. Const. Amend. XIV. Cooper raises both 
Due Process and Eighth Amendment-based challenges to the 
“clear and convincing" standard for proving incompetency. 
However, the latter provision is not implicated, because the 
question of competency to proceed arises prior to a guilt 
finding. See Ingraham v. Wright, 430 U.S. 651, 671-72 n. 40 
(1977) (Eighth Amendment scrutiny not triggered absent 
criminal conviction). A separate body of Eighth Amendment 
law addresses incompetency to be executed. See Ford v. 
Wainwright, 477 U.S. 399 (1986). Therefore, just as criminal 
trial of an incompetent person is barred solely by due process, 
Drope v. Missouri, 420 U.S. 162, 172-73 (1975), the manner 
in which a State decides competency to proceed--even in a 
capital case--involves only due process analysis. 


Next, amici will not address whether Oklahoma’s 
particular definition of "clear and convincing" evidence makes 
that burden of proof heavier than understood by this Court in 
Addington v. Texas, 441 U.S. 418 (1979). In Addington, this 
Court described "clear and convincing" proof as an 
"intermediate" standard, between the traditional civil 
“preponderance” standard and the criminal "beyond reasonable 
doubt" standard. Jd at 424. Cooper argues that only the 


lighter "preponderance" standard may be used to determine 
incompetency to proceed. However, he also contends that as 
defined by Oklahoma’s stock jury instruction at the time of 
his competency inquiry, "clear and convincing" proof may 
pose a heavier burden than understood by this Court in 
Addington (Br. of Petitioner at 28). Amici respectfully defer 
to the arguments of Cooper and of Oklahoma to decide the 
latter contention. For purposes of this brief, the amici States 
presume that clear and convincing proof is an intermediate 
standard, as stated in Addington. We will argue that so 
understood, it is an appropriate standard for deciding that a 
defendant is incompetent to proceed. 


Cc 


Turning to the standard of review, amici observe that 
Cooper’s burden of persuasion before this Court is very 
heavy, for two reasons. First, it appears from the Oklahoma 
court’s opinion and from Cooper’s certiorari petition that 
Cooper facially attacks Okla. Stat. Ann. tit. 22 § 1175.4B; no 
"as applied" argument is offered. Cooper’s opening merits 
brief is also broadly framed, articulating no “as applied" 
analysis. "A facial challenge to a legislative Act is, of course, 
the most difficult challenge to mount successfully, since the 
challenger must establish that no set of circumstances exists 
under which the Act would be valid." United States v. 
Salerno, 481 U.S. 739, 745 (1987) (emphasis added). 


Second, this Court has held that State legislative 
decisions receive judicial deference under the Due Process 
clause. Unless a statute compromises a deep-rooted, common 
law rule, this Court will not strike it merely because some 
reasonable person might find the legislature’s decision 
unwise. The Court followed this principle in Medina v. 


6 


California, 505 U.S. 437 (1992), when it approved 
California’s decision to allocate the burden of proving 
incompetency to proceed upon the party alleging 
incompetency--in that case, the defendant. "[A] state 
procedure ‘does not run foul of the Fourteenth Amendment 
because another method may seem to our thinking to be fairer 
or wiser or to give a surer promise of protection to the 
prisoner at the bar." Jd at 451 (quoting Smyder v. 
Massachusetts, 291 U.S. 97, 105 (1934)). 


Deference to Oklahoma’s legislative judgment is 
particularly appropriate in this case. Just as common law 
does not allocate the burden of proof to either party, Medina, 
505 U.S. at 446-47, there appears to be no common law rule 
for degree of proof in competency-to-proceed inquiries. 
Deeming such inquiries analogous to ordinary civil 
proceedings, the "preponderance" standard has been applied, 
but no searching rationale for this practice has been 
expressed. See Charles E. Torcia, Wharton’s Criminal 
Procedure § 426, at 785 & n. 20 (13th ed. 1991). Compare 
Drope, 420 U.S. at 171 (explaining common law basis of rule 
against trial of incompetents). See also Bruce J. Winick, 
Restructuring Competency to Stand Trial, 32 U.C.L.A. L. 
Rev. 921, 968 (1985) ("A procedural due process right to a 
determination of competency is not the equivalent of a 
substantive due process right to be immune from trial while 
incompetent .. ."). Therefore, assignment of the "clear and 
convincing" standard for proving incompetency does not, by 
itself, compromise any deep-rooted right. 


Further, a detailed matrix of rules already exists for 


assessing competency to proceed. If competency is in doubt, 
a trial court must undertake an inquiry. See Drope, 420 U.S. 
at 173 ("reasonable cause" to doubt competency requires 


inquiry); Pate v. Robinson, 383 U.S. 375, 385 (1966) ("bona 
fide question" requires inquiry). The States have painstaking 
procedures for competency inquiries, generally similar to 
recommendations of the Model Penal Code and the American 
Bar Association (see appendix I of this brief). The inquiry 
includes careful clinical evaluation, aided if necessary by 
inpatient observation. After clinical evaluation, there is a 
court hearing to legally determine whether the defendant is 
competent. Model Penal Code §§ 4.04 through 4.06 (1985); 
Il American Bar Ass'n, Standards for Criminal Justice §§ 7- 
4.1 through 7-4.15 (2d ed. 1986). See also Okla. Stat. Ann. 
tit. 22 §§ 1175.2 through 1175.8 (1986 & Supp. 1995). 


This rule matrix extends to related issues. Under the 
Constitution, a State has very limited authority to compel a 
criminal defendant to take psychotropic medication--even if 
arguably necessary to maintain competency. Riggins v. 
Nevada, 504 U.S. 127, 135 (1992). Additionally, a State 
cannot confine an incompetent defendant indefinitely; if 
competency cannot be restored within a reasonable time, the 
State must initiate civil commitment proceedings, or else 
release the defendant. Jackson v. Indiana, 406 U.S. 715, 
731-39 (1972). Civil commitment, in turn, requires “clear 
and convincing” proof, generally of mental illness plus 
dangerousness. Addington, 441 U.S. at 433. A defendant 
who is tried and acquitted on insanity-type grounds can only 
be confined so long as he or she remains both mentally il! 
and dangerous. Foucha v. Louisiana, 504 U.S. 71, 77 (1992). 
If a convicted defendant appears mentally ill, notice and an 
adversary hearing are required before involuntary transfer to 
a mental health facility can occur. Vitek v. Jones, 445 U.S. 
480, 495 (1980). There are also constitutional limits on 
prison authorities’ ability to control disruptive inmate 


behavior with psychotropic medication. Washington v. 
Harper, 494 U.S. 210, 227 (1990). 


Given this extensive rule matrix, maximum deference 
should be granted to State decisions in the remaining 
interstices of the law dealing with competency and related 
issues. “The essence of federalism is that states must be free 
to develop a variety of solutions to problems and not be 
forced into a common, uniform mold." Addington, 441 U.S. 
at 431. Cf United States v. Lopez, _US.__, 115 S. Ct. 
1624, 1626 (1995) ("first principles" of federalism counsel 
against excessive federal government intrusion into areas of 
primary state responsibility). Put another way, the decision 
of Oklahoma and several other States to require "clear and 
convincing" proof of incompetency need only “adequately” 
protect the core requirement that incompetent persons not face 
criminal trial, Drope, 420 U.S. at 172; it need not be a 
perfect or ideal choice, Medina, 505 U.S. at 451. Therefore, 
if any reasonable justification can be found for that decision, 
this Court should uphold it. 


As amici now demonstrate, several such justifications 
exist. These justifications are based upon important public 
interests--interests that are arguably shared by criminal 
defendants. They also relate to circumstances that can be 
created by the above-described matrix of related law.

First, the States have a powerful, legitimate interest in 
trying criminal cases expeditiously. See Winston v. Lee, 470 
U.S. 753, 762 (1985) ("the community’s interest in fairly and 


accurately determining guilt or innocence . . . is of course of 
great importance”); JIlinois v. Allen, 397 U.S. 337, 347-48 
(1970) (Brennan, J., concurring) ("Constitutional power to 
bring an accused to trial is fundamental to a scheme of 
‘ordered liberty’ and prerequisite to social justice and peace"); 
Cooper, 889 P.2d at 303 ("The State has great interest in 
assuring its citizens a thorough and speedy judicial process . 

."). And, of course, criminal defendants have a 
constitutional right to a "speedy trial," U.S. Const. Amend. 
VI. Whenever a criminal trial is delayed, the preservation of 
evidence, and hence accuracy of the trial proceedings, 
becomes a problem for both parties.

incompetency to proceed that reduces the risk of an erroneous 
incompetency finding, and thereby reduces the risk of an 
unnecessary trial delay. The "preponderance" standard admits 
a high risk of an erroneous incompetency finding. See 
Addington, 441 U.S. at 426-27 ("preponderance" standard for

This Court’s Riggins decision adds a new, fair trial- 
related dimension to the legal tension between finding a 
defendant incompetent and attempting to restore competency. 
In Riggins, this Court reversed the capital murder conviction 
of a defendant who was involuntarily treated with the 
psychotropic medication Mellaril (thioridazine) prior to and 
during trial; thus medicated, the defendant raised an insanity 
defense, which the jury rejected. This Court held that the 
compelled administration of psychotropic medication, not 
proven “necessary to accomplish an essential state policy," 
504 U.S. at 138, violated the defendant’s Sixth and 
Fourteenth Amendment rights to effective counsel and a fair 
trial. That holding was supported by the possibility that the 
medication negatively affected the defendant’s appearance. 
testimony, and ability to follow the proceedings and consult 
with counsel. /d at 137. Along the way, Court suggested 
that a "less intrusive alternatives" inquiry is required before 
a criminal defendant can be compelled to take psychotropic 
medication while trial is under way. /d. at 135-36.

Riggins thus poses a special dilemma for State 
lawmakers, who know that in order to be effective, treatment 
to restore mental competency will commonly require 
psychotropic medication. See Winick, 32 U.C.L.A. L. Rev. 
at 945; American Psychiatric Ass’n, Practice Guidelines for 
the Treatment of Patients with Substance Use Disorders: 
Alcohol, Cocaine, Opioids, Am. J. Psychiatry Vol. 152, No. 
11, at 7-9, 29-30, 36-37, 40-42 (Nov. 1995 Supplement); 
American Psychiatric Ass’n, Practice Guidelines for the 
Treatment of Patients with Bipolar Disorder, Am. J. 
Psychiatry Vol. 151, No. 12, at 5-14 (Dec. 1994 Supplement); 
American Psychiatric Ass’n, Practice Guidelines for Major 
Depressive Disorder .: Adults, Am. J. Psychiatry Vol. 150, 
No. 4, at 7-11 (April 1993 Supplement); M. Mostert & R. 
Boshes, "Pharmacological Treatment of Schizophrenia," in 
Psychiatry Diagnosis & Therapy (J. Flaherty et al. 2nd ed. 
1993), at 101-13. Although Riggins reserved the question 
whether psychotropic medication may be involuntarily 
administered to maintain defendant competency, 504 U.S. at 
136, it clearly warns lawmakers that potent constitutional 
concerns must be overcome if such treatment can be justified. 


The State of Utah has encountered this dilemma. In 
1990, a noncapital murder defendant who calls himself 
"Captain Nemo" was adjudicated incompetent to proceed, 
which only requires a preponderance of the evidence under 
Utah Code Ann. § 77-15-5(10) (1995), and was accordingly 
committed to the Utah State Hospital for treatment under 
Utah Code Ann. § 77-15-6 (1995). While thus committed, 
the defendant refused psychotropic medication, and obtained 
a federal court order barring such treatment. See Woodland 
v. Angus, 820 F. Supp. 1497, 1500-01 (D. Utah 1993). 
Heavily relying upon Riggins, the federal court held that even 
though the defendant was "not competent to make an 


15 


informed medical decision" about treatment with psychotropic 
medication, 820 F. Supp. at 1504, and even though the State 
of Utah had a "significant" interest in trying him on the 
criminal charges, id. at 1511, the State could not involuntarily 
medicate the defendant to restore his competency. See id. at 
1508-1519. Not until 1994 was "Captain Nemo" found 
competent to proceed, tried, and convicted. His state appeal 
is now pending, and is expected to raise both competency and 
"speedy trial" issues. 


Cases like "Captain Nemo" add a pragmatic concern 
to the legal dilemma created by Vitek, Riggins, and the other 
above-cited case law. An incompetent criminal defendant 
who occupies mental hospital space while refusing efficacious 
treatment denies that space to somebody who may benefit 
from it; he or she may also endanger hospital staff and other 
patients. State lawmakers and the clinicians who operate 
mental institutions are loath to "warehouse" such individuals, 
by confining them without effective treatment. To help 
resolve all these conflicting pressures, lawmakers could select 
the "clear and convincing" standard for proof of a criminal 
defendant’s incompetency to proceed. Just as this standard 
provides legal justification for civil commitment, it may also 
provide sufficient justification for clinically appropriate use 
of psychotropic medication to restore competency. Even if 
the standard does not entirely resolve such problems, it can, 
and should, be viewed as a reasonable, and therefore 
constitutional, legislative effort to do so. 


Il 
Amici now revisit the problem acknowledged in Point 


]--that a defendant who is "more likely than not" incompetent 
may face trial. That problem is more theoretical than real, 


16 


because the "clear and convincing" incompetency proof 
standard operates within a procedural matrix that adequately 
prevents trial of a truly incompetent defendant. See Leland 
v. Oregon, 343 U.S. 790, 793 (1952) ("To determine the 
merits of this challenge, the statute must be viewed in its 
relation to other relevant [State] law and in its place in the 
trial of this case"). Several features are pertinent. 


A 


First, the "clear and convincing" standard for proving 
incompetency comes into play only after extensive inquiry, 
consisting of clinical evaluation and an adversarial, judicial 
hearing. That inquiry is designed to gather and present 
comprehensive evidence bearing upon the elements of 
competency: Does the defendant understand the proceedings, 
and can the defendant reasonably assist counsel? Given such 
searching inquiry, a truly incompetent defendant should 
readily be found clearly and convincingly so. See Cooper, 
889 P.2d at 303 ("A truly incompetent defendant, through his 
attorneys and experts, can prove incompetency with relative 
ease"). Therefore, the clear and convincing standard is 
reasonable. 


Next, Oklahoma’s statutes, in accord with this Court’s 
holdings in Pate and Drope, require that competency be re- 
considered whenever a doubt arises: "The court, at any time, 
may initiate a competency determination on its own motion, 
without an application, if the court has a doubt as to the 
competency of the person." Okla. Stat. Ann. tit. 22 § 
1175.2A (1986). As reflected in the Oklahoma appellate 
court’s opinion, trial counsel can re-raise the competency 
question at any time, and did so in this case. Cooper, 889 
P.2d at 312. The statutes of Connecticut, Pennsylvania, and 


17 


Rhode Island are in accord. Conn. Gen. Stat. § 54-56d(c) 
(1995) (inquiry may be requested any time "it appears that 
the defendant is not competent . . ."); Pa. Stat. Ann., Tit. 50, 
§ 7402(c), (d) (Supp. 1995) (inquiry upon "prima facie 
question of incompetency"); R.I. Gen. Laws § 40.1-5.3-3 
(Supp. 1994) (inquiry allowed whenever "it appears that the 
defendant is not competent”). 


This ongoing duty to watch for signs of incompetency 
provides a "fail-safe" guard against an erroneous finding of 
competency that might result from an initial failure to find 
incompetency by clear and convincing evidence. A court’s 
direct observations during trial will provide additional data 
about the defendant’s mental status. See Winick, 32 
U.C.L.A. L. Rev. at 953-54. The trial court may thus find 
itself clearly and convincingly persuaded that the defendant 
is incompetent, and stay the prosecution accordingly. 
Naturally, such disposition is most likely to occur if the 
defendant is, in the court’s initial opinion, more likely than 
not incompetent. Conversely, the unfolding trial may dispel 
judicial doubts about the defendant’s competency. In either 
instance, the trial court’s contemporaneous observations will 
likely be placed on record, allowing fully-informed appellate 
review. Such was apparently the disposition in this case, 
allowing the Oklahoma appellate court to confirm that Cooper 
was not incompetent, but instead was malingering. Cooper, 
889 P.2d at 313. These procedures substantially reduce the 
risk of an erroneous competency finding, and thus adequately 
protect the core right not to be tried while incompetent. 


It is also important to recognize defendant’s rights that 
are not affected by the "clear and convincing" standard to 


18 


prove incompetency. There is no infringement upon insanity- 
type defenses, recognized in all the States that require clear 
and convincing proof of incompetency to proceed. Okla. 
Stat. Ann. tit. 22 §§ 1161 through 1170 (1986 & Supp. 
1995); Conn. Gen. Stat. § 53a-13 (1995); Pa. Stat. Ann., Tit. 
42, R. Crim. P. 305C(1)(b) (1989), Tit. 42, § 7402(e)(4)(iii)- 
(iv) (Supp. 1995); RI. Gen. Laws § 40.1-5.3-4 (1990). 
Defenses such as duress, compulsion, entrapment, and the like 
are not affected, or are only indirectly affected. There is no 
infringement upon the right to present mitigating evidence of 
mental disorder at sentencing. There is no impairment of the 
protection against executing an incompetent person--which, as 
earlier explained, is a discrete inquiry under Ford v. 
Wainwright, 477 U.S. 399 (1986). 


In fact, some of the foregoing defenses are more likely 
to succeed if the defendant appears mentally impaired to the 
fact finder or sentencer. Concurring in the Riggins judgment, 
Justice Kennedy suggested that a criminal defendant has what 
might be termed a constitutional "right to appear insane" at 
trial, and that involuntarily medicating a defendant is akin to 
manipulating material evidence. 504 U.S. at 141-45 
(Kennedy, J., concurring). The “clear and convincing" proof 
standard for incompetency to proceed favors sending a 
marginally competent (or marginally incompetent) defendant 
to trial, where he or she will be observed in a baseline, 
unmedicated mertal condition. Given the Sixth Amendment 
rationale of both the main Riggins opinion and Justice 
Kennedy’s concurrence, such presentation of the marginally 
competent defendant can actually assist the defense. 


Ability to assist defense counsel is, of course, an 


element of competency to proceed under Dusky. But Riggins 
paradoxically suggests that some degree of incompetency can 


19 


itself assist the defense. Additionally, although it has 
suggested that an incompetent defendant is constructively 
"absent" from the proceedings, Drope v. Missouri, 420 U.S. 
162, 171 (1975), this Court has also held that a defendant's 
right to be present at trial "is a condition of due process to 
the extent that a fair and just trial would be thwarted by his 
absence, and to that extent only." Smyder v. Massachusetts, 
291 U.S. 97, 107-08 (1934), overruled on other grounds, 
Duncan v. Louisiana, 391 U.S. 145 (1968). It may therefore 
be significant that Cooper does not allege that his trial, even 
though disrupted from time to time by his behavior, reached 
an inaccurate or otherwise unjust verdict. 


It has also been observed that the common law rule 
against trying incompetents arose during an era when there 
was no right to counsel in most criminal cases; accordingly, 
optimal mental acuity of the defendant was essential in order 
to raise a defense. Because counsel is now constitutionally 
guaranteed, the defendant’s personal competency is now a 
somewhat less critical feature of a fair trial than it was at 
common law. Winick, 32 U.C.L.A. L. Rev. at 952-53. 
Given all of this, and without disputing the fundamental 
nature of the rule against trying incompetents, it appears that 
the constitutionally permissible presumption of competency, 
Medina, can be moderately strengthened. The "clear and 
convincing” standard for proving incompetency so strengthens 
that presumption, but not to an insurmountable degree. 


In sum, the "clear and convincing" standard for 
proving incompetency to proceed does not erode the settled 
rule against trial of incompetent persons. Instead, it is a 
reasonable response to conflicting pressures, and difficult 
circumstances, that arise under modern law. Applied in the 
context of elaborate protective procedures, this proof standard 


20 


adequately protects the fundamental right to a fair trial. 
Therefore, it is constitutionally permissible. 


CONCLUSION 
For the foregoing reasons, amici believe that the 
judgment of the Oklahoma Court of Criminal Appeals should 
be AFFIRMED.

parties, and other evidence bearing upon competency may be 
heard. Nearly every State provides for some form of limited 
commitment upon a finding of incompetency to proceed.
