JURISDI 


Jurisdiction of this Court rests upon 28 U.S.C. § 1257 
(a) (1988), Petitioner having asserted below and assert- 
ing here a deprivation of rights secured by the Constitu- 
tion of the United States. The petition for certiorari was 
filed July 13, 1995, and granted on October 5, 1995.

STATEMENT OF THE CASE 


1. Pre-Trial Proceedings 


Byron Keith Cooper was charged with Murder in the 
First Degree on December 4, 1989 for the September 4, 
1989 stabbing death of Mr. Harold Sheppard during the 
commission of a burglary. Mr. Cooper became a suspect 
after using Mr. Sheppard’s J.C. Penney card. A watch and 
‘wo cameras belonging to Mr. Sheppard were found in 
the home where Mr. Cooper had been staying. At Mr. 
Sheppard’s house, authorities found Cooper's fingerprints 
on a flashlight, a metal can, a watch case and a carton of 
cigarettes. Serious questions regarding Mr. Cooper’s com- 
petency to stand trial arose thereafter and reappeared 
repeatedly throughout the trial court proceedings. 


a. The August, 1990, Incompetency Determination 


In early August, 1990, counsel sought and obtained a 
judicial order to evaluate Mr. Cooper’s competency to 
stand trial, because of serious concerns about Cooper’s 
mental competency. (Record 336-37, 344-45). Dr. 
Edith King, a clinical psychologist employed by the Okla- 
homa Department of Mental Health, examined Cooper 
shortly thereafter and determined he was incompetent (o 
stand trial. Among other things, she found him to be 
inattentive, irrational, incoherent, delusional, unable to 
focus on her questions, and unable to communicate effec- 
tively. (Transcript of Competency Hearing of December 
20, 1990 and January 18, 1991, [hereinafter “C.H.I”] 
4-30). After the prosecution made no effort to contest 
this evaluation the court committed Cooper to Eastern 
State Hospital, a state mental health facility, to be treated. 


b. The Second Exploration into Competency 


Upon his return from the hospital, another hearing on 
Mr. Cooper’s competency was held in December, 1990, 
and January, 1991. Dr. King testified to her earlier find- 
ing of incompetency and told the court that in her opinion, 


3 


Cooper displayed a thought disorder and possibly experi- 
enced auditory and/or visual hallucinations. (C.H.I 7). 
She concluded that during her evaluation Cooper's dis- 
orientation and confusion were not feigned. (C.H.I 19). 
At the time of her testimony, Dr. King observed Cooper 
seemed oriented and no longer had the wild, out-of-control 
look he had when she had examined him earlier. (C.H.I 
27-28, 32). 


To rebut Mr. Cooper’s evidence of incompetency, the 
prosecution called Eastern State Hospital psychologist 
Jeanne Russell. Russell did not actually participate on the 
team that treated Mr. Cooper, but she had conducted a 
screening interview two and a half months after his ad- 
m.ssion and evaluated his progress at the hospital. (C.H.I 
38-40, 48). In Russell’s opinion, Cooper understood the 
nature of the charges, was able to consult with counsel, 
and was not a mentally ill person as defined by statute. 
Moreover, she opined he was malingering and competent 
to stand trial. (C.H.I 43-44, 51). 


On the basis of this record, the court found Mr. Cooper 
competent to stand trial. (C.H.I 101). The court made 
no finding Cooper was malingering. 


c. The Third Exploration into Competency 


More than one year and three months later, at the close 
of a motion hearing, Cooper’s lead counsel again raised 
the issue of incompetence. (App. 3-7). Counsel stated 
he was ready for trial, but was concerned about Cooper’s 
bizarre behavior both in the courtroom and in the 
county jail and that two investigators from the Okla- 
homa County Public Defender’s Office who had spiut 
considerable time with Cooper believed he was “cracking 
up.” Counsel recognized he was not a doctor, acknowl- 
edged that Cooper might be faking, and offered the prose- 
cution the opportunity to pick any doctor to assess Mr. 
Cooper and indicated he would pay for it. 


4 


The trial judge responded that he was not inclined to 
revisit his finding of fifteen months earlier: 


We're talking about two things. Insanity as a defense 
to a crime or incompetence to assist counsel. So, if 
an incompetent person is convicted of a crime thet 
he committed when he was sane, I don’t see any 
harm’s done. (App. 6). 


d. The Fourth Exploration into Competency 


Trial on the merits was scheduled to begin on May 4, 
1992. But on that day again, Cooper exhibited strange 
behavior that suggested he was not competent to stand 
trial. As his attorney described his appearance, “his hair's 
poked out and going in every direction and it’s unkept and 
he looks like heck.” (Transcript of Pre-Trial Motions and 
Competency Hearing May 4 and 5, 1992 [hereinafter 
“C.H.II"] 3-4). Counsel also learned that Cooper had 
adamantly refused to change from his jail attire to court 
clothes. Cooper later told the court that the court clothes 
burned him. /d. 


With this development, defense requested further in- 
quiry into Mr. Cenper’s competency and informed the 
court that another evaluation was necessary. (C.H.II 32). 
Counsel based this request not only upon Cooper’s un- 
kempt appearance and recent bizarre behavior, but also 
upon his mental health history, his family history with 
schizophrenia, his abject developmental history, his ada- 
mant refusal to talk to counsel during the preceding two 
months, and the opinions of two public defender investi- 
gators that Mr. Cooper was a deeply disturbed individual. 
(C.H.II1 26-28, 32-36). During this discussion, Mr. 
Cooper assumed the fetal position in court. (C.H.II 35). 


Later that day a hearing commenced on Mr. Cooper’s 
competency to stand trial. Over the next day and a half, 
the defense presented several witnesses, lay and expert, 
who described Mr. Cooper as wholly unable to cooperate 
with counsel, to participate meaningfully in the proceed- 


5 


ings, or even to care appropriately for himself. The de- 
fense first called Mr. Cooper to testify. (See C.H.II 39- 
85). He failed to respond to a majority of questions, de- 
spite considerable cajoling. (See, e.g., C.H.II 47-49). 
The questions he did answer indicated that he was afraid 
of his own counsel. Mr. Cooper repeatedly stated his 
counsel was trying to kill him and cause him bodily harm. 
(C.H.II 42-43, 46, 57-61, 71). He accused his iawyer, 
the prosecutor, and two other persons of trying to choke 
him with a rope. (C.H.II 57-61). Moreover, his responses 
to a number of questions had no basis in reality.’ 


During this examination, defense counsel produced a 
drawing made by Mr. Cooper of “Noryb,” a spirit with 
whom Mr. Cooper claimed to communicate. Counsel agreed 
to return the drawing if Cooper would let the judge speak 
with Noryb. During this protracted negotiation, Cooper 
lapsed into unintelligible speech. (C.H.II 50). He did tell 
the court Noryb had stopped a conspiratorial attempt to 
choke him. (C.H.II 59, 62-64). Finally, he became so 
paranoid and distraught during the questioning regarding 
Noryb that as defense counsel approached him, he backed 
up out of the witness chair and fell backwards over the 
railing, jackknifed between the witness box and the wall, 
striking his head against the marble wall. (C.H.II 69-70, 
84; App. 37-38).?

SUMMARY OF ARGUMENT 


The trial of a person more likely than not incompetent 
violates principles of justice “so rooted in the traditions 
and conscience of our people as to be ranked as funda- 
mental.” Patterson v. New York, 432 U.S. 197, 202 
(1977). The right of a defendant to be tried only if 
competent is fundamental to our adversary system of 
justice. History teaches us that the trial of an incompetent 
has been condemned by civilized society for centuries. 
Competency to stand trial in England in the 1700s through 
the present has been judged by the civil standard of 
proof, a preponderance of evidence standard. 


Early American cases and statutes fail to apply a clear 
and convincing standard of proof on competency determi- 
nations. Numerous later cases in the late 1800s and 
early 1900s enunciated a standard of preponderance of 
the evidence. This same standard applies in almost all 
states today and is the appropriate test in all Federal 
courts by statute. 


Unlike the narrow class of cases where an individual’s 
competence is in equipoise, Oklahoma’s standard actually 
contemplates the trial of someone who is more likely than 
not incompetent. Such a policy has no historical pedi- 
gree and obviously fails to adequately protect the funda- 
mental right not to be tried while incompetent. The de- 
termination that a defendant is competent to proceed 
implicates a host of constitutional principles that demand 
protection commensurate with their fundamental status. 


Moreover, application of this standard not only under- 
protects the fundamental right to be free from trial while 
incompetent, it also denigrates the sound administration 
of justice by establishing dual standards for competency, 
one to stand trial and another for all other competency 
issues such as competency to enter a guilty plea or waive 
the assistance of counsel. 


15 


The trial of a person more probably than not incom- 
petent is contrary to due process concerns of reliability in 
the fact-finding process and accuracy .of verdicts. The 
higher the standard of proof applied, the more likely an 
erroneous finding of competency will be made. This is 
contrary to the protective purposes behind the clear and 
convincing standard, to ensure a heightened level of pro- 
tection for life and liberty interests. 


Finally, heightened reliability and accuracy interests in 
Capital sentencing proceedings are undermined by the trial 
of one more likely than not incompetent. The demeanor 
of an incompetent person and his inability to provide 
counsel with vital mitigating evidence prevents a jury from 
making a reasoned, moral judgment as to his life.

GREATER WEIGHT OF THE EVIDENCE DEMON-

Our Constitution bars trial when a citizen’s mental 
condition is such that he lacks the capacity to understand 
the nature and object of the proceedings against him, to 
consult with counsel, or to assist in preparing his defense. 
Drope v. Missouri, 420 U.S. 162, 171 (1975); Medina v. 
California, US. , 112 S.Ct. 2572, 2579 (1992). 
This fundamental right is directly traceable from the gen- 
uine concerns for the mentally disabled expressed as early 
as the tourth century B.C. by Greek and Roman physic- 
ians and philosophers. Samuel J. Brakel, John Parry, & 
Barbara A. Weiner, The Mentally Disabled and the Law 
9, 9-12 (3d ed. 1985). 


These concerns were long ago embraced by English 
common law. As this Court noted recently:

A rule that allows the state to force a delusional or

The fundamental right not to be tried while incompetent

B. Fundamental Fairness Prohibits the Trial of an 
Individual Who Is More Likely Than Not Incom- 


petent. 


Mr. Cooper has just demonstrated that history forbids 
the trial of incompetent individuals and that one is in- 
competent if the evidence so preponderates. These views 
reflect the age-old understanding that to try a person who 
cannot comprehend the proceedings and defend himself 
is both unfair and unproductive to the ends of justice. 
See Dusky v. United States, 362 U.S. 402 (1960). It is 
no wonder that history refuses to endorse a standard of 
proof higher than preponderance. Such an inappropriately 
high standard would substantially increase the likelihood 
that constitutionally incompetent defendants would be 
convicted and in some instances condemned to death. 


This Court, in approving allocation of the burden of 
proof on the defendant in Medina, noted: 


Under California law, the allocation of the burden of 
proof to the defendant will affect competency deter- 
minations only in a narrow class of cases where the 
evidence is in equipoise; that is, where the evidence 
that a defendant is competent is just as strong as the 
evidence that he is incompetent. 


Medina, 112 S.Ct. at 2579. Unlike the California scheme, 
which had only a marginal effect on the substantive right 
not to be tried while incompetent, Oklahoma’s clear and 
convincing standard necessarily impacts upon a larger 
class of people, and guarantees that some who make a 
strong showing of incompetence and persuade the fact- 
finder he is likely incompetent must nonetheless proceed 
to trial. At the time of Petitioner's trial, Oklahoma's

This Court has repeatedly held that state laws con-

“powerful social standards of progressive society.” Medina, 
112 S.Ct. at 2582 (O'Connor, J., joined by Souter, J.,

In Parke v. Raley, —— U.S. ——, 113 S.Ct. 517

(“Against the historical status quo, I read the Court's

(Kennedy, J., concurring in judgment). 


In determining whether Oklahoma's standard offends 
due process considerations of fairness in its operation, it 
is useful to look at the effect this requirement has on the 
fundamental right not to be tried while incompetent, the 
reliability of the fact-finding process in determining com- 
petency, the ability of a defendant to meet this evidentiary 
burden in light of the legitimate state concerns about 
access to evidence and feigning incompetency, and the 
social cost to the fundamental right not to be tried while 
incompetent. 


No compelling or adequate justification exists for aban- 
doning the common law view that if one is more likely 
incompetent, he is incompetent. Initially, it is important 
to realize the State does not have to prove anything to 
prevail. On the other he od, for the defendant to prevail 
under a preponderance standard he must prove he is more 
likely than not incompetent. The defense must garner all 
available evidence of incompetency and provide the fact- 
finder with opinions of experts, prior opinions of doctors, 


28 


nurses, lay testimony, any other observations, hospital 
records, and any other relevant evidence. 


A preponderance standard also adequately addresses 
the concerns that an inconclusive examination will un- 
fairly benefit the defendant because such an examination 
will fall far short of satisfying the defendant’s burden. 
The defendant will have a strong incentive to present alli 
available evidence to the examiner as well as the fact- 
finder, to avoid an inconclusive examination. Cf. Medina 
v. California, 112 S.Ct. at 2582 (O’Connor, J., concurring 
in judgment). The defendant, to prove incompetence, 
must persuasively show the factfinder that the greater 
weight of the evidence shows incompetency. Otherwise 
the evidence will be in equipoise (or less) and the defend- 
ant will not prevail. 


In determining whether a clear and convincing standard 
violates considerations of fairness, it is important to realize 
like many psychological inquiries, competency evalua- 
tions are “in the present state of mental sciences . . . at 
best a hazardous guess however conscientious.” Solesbee 
v. Balkcom, 339 U.S. at 23 (1950) (Frankfurter, J., 
dissenting); see also Ake v. Oklahoma, 470 U.S. 68, 80- 
81 (1985); Addington, 441 U.S. at 430; Drope, 420 U.S. 
at 176; Anthony Highmore, A Treatise on the Law of 
Idiocy and Lunacy 202 (1807) (“nothing is more diffi- 
cult than to prove the fact of insanity”). The uncertainty 
of psychological inquiries strongly suggests a clear and 
convincing standard grossly underprotects the fundamental 
right to avoid trial while incompetent.

to him by the process of placing on the other party 
the burden . . . of persuading the fact-finder... .

3. Important constitutional rights of individuals 
are violated by the Oklahoma standard. 


The high burden that Oklahoma places upon defend- 
ants creates an unacceptably high risk that incompetent 
individuals will be forced to trial. The common law never 
tolerated so high a risk. Nor did any American jurisdic- 
tion during the nineteenth century or the first three- 
fourths of the twentieth century. Today, neither federal 
law nor the law of forty-six of the fifty states will tolerate 
so high a risk. There are compelling reasons for this. The 
determination that a defendant is competent to proceed 
implicates a host of constitutional principles that merit 
protection commensurate with their fundamental status. 
As Justice Thomas noted for the Court in Godinez, 113 
S.Ct. at 2686, the defendant who is determined competent 
to stand trial is an individual who subsequently will face 
many critical choices of constitutional dimension. He must 
decide whether to accept the guiding hand of counsel 
which the Constitution assures him, or to instead waive 
that right and choose self-representation. /d. at 2687. He 
must decide whether to plead guilty, and thereby waive 
his fundamental right to a trial. /d. at 2686. Additional 
choices are bound up in that question: 


He will ordinarily have to decide whether to waive 
his “privilege against compulsory self-incrimination,” 

Boykin v. Alabama, 395 U.S. 238, 243 . . . (1969), 
by taking the witness stand; if the option is available,
