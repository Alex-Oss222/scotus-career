STATEMENT OF THE CASE 


Petitioner, Byron Keith Cooper, was charged on 
December 4, 1989 with first degree murder for the stabbing 
death of Harold Sheppard during the commission of a 
burglary in violation of Okla.Stat.tit. 21, § 701.7 (1991) in 
Case No. CF-82-5084 in the District Court of Oklahoma 
County before the Honorable Richard W. Freeman, District 
Judge. Petitioner was represented by counsel. The jury 
returned a verdict of guilty and, after a sentencing stage 
proceeding, found the existence of five (5) aggravating 
circumstances: that Petitioner had previously been convicted 
of a felony involving violence; that the murder was 
committed to avoid arrest or lawful prosecution; that the 
murder was especially heinous, atrocious, or cruel; that the 
murder was committed by ome serving a term of 
imprisonment; and the existence of a probability that 
Petitioner would pose a continuing threat to society. The 
jury then recommended a sentence of death. The trial court

From this Judgment and Sentence, Petitioner perfected

SUMMARY OF THE ARGUMENT 


In 1980, the State of Oklahoma adopted a set of statutes 
to govern procedures for determining a criminal defendant's 
competency to stand trial. Okla.Stat.tit. 22, § 1175.4 (1991) 
provides that once it is determined there is doubt as to the 
defendant’s competency, a defendant is evaluated. 
Thereafter, a post-examination competency hearing is held. 
At that hearing, the court shall determine, by clear and 
convincing evidence, if the person is incompetent. The 
party who raises the issues shall bear the burden. 


Petitioner claims the clear and convincing standard 
adopted by Oklahoma and utilized in this case violates due 
process as it allows the trial of a person "more likely than 
not incompetent". That a factually incompetent defendant 
shall not be subjected to trial is a principle of justice “so 
rooted in the traditions and conscience of our people as to be 


ranked as fundamental". Medina v. California, 505 U.S. 
437, 112 S.Ct 2572 (1992). 


Every defendant is presumed competent to stand trial and 
while this Court has determined that incompetent defendants 
should not proceed to trial, the Court has not allocated to the 
states the manner in which incompetency is to be 
determined. States are free to adopt their own standards of 
proof consistent with their own concepts of public policy and 
fairness. 


Although due process requires basic procedural 


13 


safeguards be observed, balancing of society’s interest 
against those of the accused has been left to the legislative 
branch. The clear and convincing intermediate level of 
proof adopted by the Oklahoma Legislature is sufficient to 
protect the values of society and is an accurate reflection of 
how the risk of error should be distributed between the 
litigants. 


THE CONSTITUTION PROTECTS A 
CRIMINAL DEFENDANT FROM BEING TRIED 
WHILE INCOMPETENT; THE DUE PROCESS 
PROVIDED TO DETERMINE INCOMPETENCE 
IS LEFT TO THE STATES. 


At the core of our criminal justice system is a 
presumption that every defendant is competent to stand trial. 
Medina v. California, 505 U.S. 437, 112 S.Ct. 2572 (1992). 
It is only when a defendant’s mental condition is such that he 
lacks the capacity to understand the nature and object of the 
proceedings against him, to consult with counsel, or to assist 
in preparing his defense, that he is exempted from being 
tried. Drope v. Missouri, 420 U.S. 162, 171 (1975), Pate 
y. Robinson, 383 U.S. 375 (1966). While this Court has 
determined that incompetent defendants should not proceed 
to trial, it has not directed the states as to the manner in 
which to determine incompetency. This Court has held the 
allocation of the burden of proof to a criminal defendant to 
prove incompetence does not violate due process. Medina, 
112 S.Ct. at 2577. 


14 


A defendant not only has a right not to be tried while 
incompetent, he also has a right not to be committed without 
due process. It is clear that "commitment for any purpose 
constitutes a significant deprivation of liberty that requires 
due process protection". Jones v, United States, 463 U.S. 


354, 361 (1983), quoting Addington v. Texas, 441 U.S. 418, 
425 (1979). Therefore, a state must have a constitutionally 


adequate purpose for the confinement. Jones, 463 U.S. at 
361. 


Oklahoma has balanced these concerns in the adoption of 
its competency statutes. Okla.Stat.tit. 22, § 1175.1 (Supp. 
1992) defines competency as "the present ability of a person 
arrested for or charged with a crime to understand the nature 
of the charges and proceedings brought against him, and to 
effectively and rationally assist in his defense”. 


Under Oklahoma law, the question of the incompetence 
of a person may be raised by the person, the defense 
attorney, or the district attorney, by an application for 
determination of competency. The court may also initiate a 
competency determination on its own motion and direct the 
processing of the application. | Okla.Stat.tit. 22, § 
1175.2(A). 


The application shall allege that the person is 
incompetent to undergo further proceedings, and shall state 
facts sufficient to raise a doubt as to the competency of the 
person. Okla.Stat. tit. 22, § 1175.2 (1991). The court 
shall hold a hearing on the application and, if the court finds 
there is a doubt as to the competency of the defendant, the 
court shall order the person to be examined by "doctors or 


15 


appropriate technicians". Okla.Stat. tit. 22, § 1175.3 (Supp. 
1993). 


These medical health professions are required to answer 
a series of five questions: 


1. Is this person able to appreciate the nature of the 
charges against him? 


2. Is this person able to consult with his lawyer and 
rationally assist in the preparation of his defense? 


3. If the answer to question 1 or 2 is no, can the person 
attain competency within a reasonable time if provided with 
a course of treatment, therapy or training? 


4 Is the person a mentally ill person or a person 
requiring treatment as defined by Section 3 of Title 43A? 


5. If the person were released without treatment, therapy 
or training, would he probably pose a significant threat to 
the life or safety of himself or others? 

Okla. Stat.tit. 22, § 1175.3 (Supp. 1993). 


After these determinations are made, a hearing on the 
competency of the person shall be held. The court, at the 
hearing on the application, shall determine, by clear and 
convincing evidence, if the person is incompetent. If 
incompetency is found, the criminal proceedings are enjoined

"Clear and convincing" is a intermediate level burden

reliability in the result. See Addington v. Texas, 441 U.S.

On the other hand, the interest of the defendant in a 
criminal case are so great that they have been protected by 
standards of proof designed to exclude as nearly as possible 
the likelihood of an erroneous judgment. This is 
accomplished by requiring that the state prove the guilt of an 
accused beyond a reasonable doubt. In Re Winship, 397 
U.S. 358 (1970). 


The clear and convincing standard, however, is an 
intermediate standard typically used in those civil cases

at 424. Although there is no empirical data on how jurors 
understand these differing standards of proof, "the standard 
of proof [at a minimum] reflects the value society places on 
individual liberty". Addington, 441 U.S. at 425.

Under the concept of federalism, the states are free to 
tailor their own standards of proof as applicable to the 
petticular interests at stake, bearing in mind that other 
protections afforded in the statute are a factor in determining 
the risks involved. In Addington, 441 U.S. at 431, this 
Court s‘ated, "(t]he essence of federalism is that states must 
be free 1o develop a variety of solutions to problems and not 
be forced into a common, uniform mold". In Addington, 
this Court, while finding the "clear and convincing" standard 
constitutionally required, held that the determination of the 
precise burden equal to or greater than the "clear and 
convincing” standard was a matter of state law which was 
left to the Texas Supreme Court. Id. at 433. As statutes 
vary from state to state, procedures to protect due process 
must also be allowed to vary. 


Competency is not an element of the offense which the 
State must prove in order to convict but is a threshold issue 
necessary to be resolved in order to prevent a violation of 
due process through conviction of a person incompetent to 
stand trial. While this Court has been diligent in protecting 
a defendant’s due process rights, it has not, and should not 
ignore the power of the individual states to adopt their own 
procedures for determining incompetency.
