## Claim, record and procedural posture

Herbert Markman owns U.S. Reissue Patent33,054, reissued from4,550,246, “Inventory Control and Reporting System for Drycleaning Stores”; Positek holds the drycleaning license. Westview makes the DATAMARK/DATASCAN system; Althon uses it. The district action was filed under35U.S.C.§281, seeking damages under§284 among other relief. The infringement trial was separated from validity and damages. The patent appears at JA293–301; accused-system testimony JA9–46; patent-lawyer testimony JA46–145; inventor testimony JA160–195; Westview-president evidence JA208–214 and brochures JA302–307.

Claim1 includes a data input device, processor/memory with “means to maintain an inventory total,” transaction-associated indicia, a bar-code printer, at least one scanner, and the concluding function “whereby said system can detect and localize spurious additions to inventory as well as spurious deletions therefrom.” Claim10 depends on claim1 and specifies an alphanumeric input keyboard. The claim and specification repeatedly refer to articles associated with transactions; specification descriptions include monitoring status, location and throughput, and reconciling article additions/deletions. The prosecution history includes an amendment adding the detect/localize language to overcome an obviousness rejection and an explanation referring to adding input articles and subtracting output articles. The reissue application discussed tracking individual articles and grouped transactions.

The accused system records clothing descriptions long enough to print the ticket, then retains invoice number, date and cash total; it does not retain a permanent garment listing. DATASCAN reads bar codes against invoice information. The parties disputed whether “inventory” must include physical clothing or could be satisfied by cash/invoice totals. They did not dispute that the accused system fails the claim if “inventory” requires clothing as part of its meaning. The lower opinion ¶¶24–38,72–80 gives the complete claim and construction rationale.

The jury was instructed to determine claim meaning using specifications, drawings, file histories and how persons of ordinary skill understand terms. It found infringement of claims1 and10, but not14. Westview had moved for a directed verdict at the close of petitioners' case; the trial judge reserved the motion and later entered JMOL/noninfringement on the ground that the claims required clothing inventory. Judgment was entered October1,1991 in E.D.Pa.No.91-0940. The Federal Circuit affirmed that judgment en banc. The pending review does not itself reopen claim14, decide validity, award damages or find a different accused-system operation.

## Complete lower opinion topology and material reasoning

Chief Judge Archer wrote for Rich,Nies,Michel,Plager,Lourie,Clevenger and Schall. Mayer and Rader concurred in the judgment separately; Newman dissented. Bryson did not participate in the lower appeal. These are lower judges only.

The eight-judge majority classified claim construction as a legal task for the court, with de novo appellate review. It emphasized public notice, patent/specification/prosecution-history integration,35U.S.C.§112's written description and distinct-claim requirements, and consistent scope against different defendants. It allowed extrinsic evidence to educate the court regarding technical language but said that such evidence could not vary the public claim; it treated the ultimate meaning as legal even after considering it. It affirmed the actual clothing-based construction. Its principal historical authorities were Hogg v.Emerson,47U.S.437; Silsby v.Foote,55U.S.218,225–26; Winans v.Denmead,56U.S.330; Winans v.NewYork & Erie,62U.S.88; Bischoff v.Wethered,76U.S.812; Heald v.Rice,104U.S.737; Coupe v.Royer,155U.S.565; MarketStreet Cable,155U.S.621; and Singer v.Cramer,192U.S.265. It distinguished factual identity/infringement determinations from claim interpretation and analogized the public patent grant to a statute. It preserved the jury's comparison of an interpreted claim to the accused device.

Mayer accepted an ultimate legal construction but rejected categorical removal of genuinely disputed subsidiary facts. His opinion relied on Graham v.JohnDeere,383U.S.1,17; the interpretive qualifications in Silsby,Bischoff,Heald and written-instrument cases; Rule52(a); and ordinary jury/JMOL review. It distinguished patent-law expert opinion from factual evidence, subjective inventor intent from probative technical usage, and genuine disputes from manufactured disagreement. His concurrence agreed with the judgment on this record rather than the majority's universal classification.

Rader expressly found no substantial evidence supporting the proposed cash-total interpretation: the claim/specification/history foreclosed it; after-the-fact inventor testimony could not create the dispute, and legal expert opinion was not evidence. He would affirm without deciding whether other cases can present subsidiary factual questions. This is an express narrower judgment ground, not an invented litigation option.

Newman distinguished legal effect (“construction”) from factual technical meaning (“interpretation”), stressed the trier's assessment of technical evidence, and argued that neither technological complexity nor labeling factual work as law could remove jury functions. She disputed the majority's reading of historical cases, distinguished bench/equity opinions, and treated ordinary deferential review and jury protection as applicable in patent litigation. Her proposed remedy was remand for the trial judge initially to apply the ordinary test whether substantial credible evidence could support a reasonable jury, or whether JMOL/new trial should result. She did not simply direct an automatic infringement judgment for Markman.

## Parties' actual positions and material concessions

Petitioner merits pp2–7 identifies evidence that industry participants used “inventory” for cash/invoices, including Westview's advertising and its president's admissions; it contends an artisan could treat batches/transactions as inventory without retaining every garment description. It stresses that only one scanner is required and that a preferred embodiment cannot automatically limit the claim. Summary pp14–17 argues that historical patent meaning disputes went to juries and that extrinsic evidence involves ordinary credibility/factfinding; policy about consistency cannot defeat preserved constitutional allocation. Its conclusion requests reversal and remand for proceedings in the district or appellate court consistent with the ruling, not an immediate damages award.

Respondent merits summary pp16–18 offers both a record-specific affirmance and a categorical construction position. It says no real scientific expert conflict exists here, petitioners' patent attorney gave legal conclusions, and the intrinsic record has one reasonable meaning. Footnote11 expressly says judgment is correct whether claim interpretation is always legal or sometimes has subsidiary facts; alternatively the verdict lacks substantial evidentiary support. Respondents also defend judicial construction after extrinsic explanatory evidence, emphasizing historical evolution, the essence of jury trial rather than every procedural detail, public notice and national consistency. Their claim that statutory clarity eliminates legitimate ambiguity is argument, not an established record fact. They request affirmance of the JMOL judgment.
