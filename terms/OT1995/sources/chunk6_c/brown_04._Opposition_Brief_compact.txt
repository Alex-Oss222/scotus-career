CONSTITUTIONAL PROVISION INVOLVED 
The relevant provision of Article III of the United States 
Constitution provides, “The judicial Power shall extend to all 
Cases, in Law and Equity, arising under this Constitution, the 
Laws of the United States, and Treaties made, or which shall be 
made, under their Authority; to all Cases affecting Ambassadors, 
other public Ministers and Consuls; to all Cases of admiralty and 
maritime Jurisdiction; to Controversies to which the United 
States shall be a Party; to Controversies between two or more 
States; between a State and Citizens of another State; between 
Citizens of the same State claiming Lands under Grants of 
different States, and between a State, or the citizens thereof, and 
foreign States, Citizens or Subjects.” U.S. Const. art. III, § 2. 
STATEMENT OF THE CASE 
Petitioner United Food and Commercial Workers Union 
Local 751 (“Union”), on behalf of its members, sued Respondent 
Brown Group, Inc. d/b/a Brown Shoe Company (“Brown Shoe”) 
on June 16, 1992, in the United States District Court for the 
Eastern District of Missouri, alleging a violation of the Worker 
Adjustment and Retraining Notification Act (“WARN Act”), 29 
U.S.C. $§ 2101-2109. The basis of the Union’s complaint was 
that Brown Shoe’s January 17, 1992 notice to its employees of the 
closure of its facility in Dixon, Missouri failed to provide 
sufficient or appropriate notice to those employees of Brown 
Shoe’s intention to close its Dixon plant. 
The Union was the collective bargaining representative of 
the unionized work force of the Dixon, Missouri shoe 
manufacturing facility of Brown Shoe.’ Brown Shoe’s non-

On July 29, 1992, Brown Shoe filed a motion to dismiss the 
complaint on the grounds that the Union lacked standing to assert 
the individual monetary claims of its members. In its motion to 
dismiss, Brown Shoe argued that the Union did not have 
associational standing to bring the action on behalf of its 
members. Brown Shoe also argued that the Union should not be 
permitted to amend its complaint to add individual “aggrieved 
employees” as plaintiffs in order to establish subject matter 
jurisdiction. In response, the Union ignored the issue of the 
addition of parties or amendment of the complaint and instead 
argued that it did, in fact, have associational standing. It did not 
seek leave to amend its complaint to add individual plaintiffs at 
any time prior to the district court’s ruling on the motion to 
dismiss. The district court, on April 12, 1993, granted Brown 
Shoe’s motion to dismiss, concluding that it was without 
jurisdiction because the Union had “not alleged injury to itself” 
and lacked constitutional standing to bring a WARN Act claim for 
monetary damages on behalf of its members. The district court's 
dismissal of the Union’s complaint was without prejudice.

“In its original brief supporting the motion to dismiss, defendant

only after the Court ruled against plaintiff that plaintiff sought

Union permission to amend, concluding that “Plaintiff's 
unexplained delay in seeking to amend is particularly 
troublesome.” (App. at 18a). 
The Union appealed the district court's order dismissing the 
case and its order denying its motion to alter or amend judgment 
to the United States Court of Appeals for the Eighth Circuit. In 
affirming the district court’s order, the Eighth Circuit held that 
the Union had failed to meet its burden of establishing either 
direct or associational standing, and that, therefore, the district 
court had properly dismissed the Union’s complaint for lack of 
jurisdiction. (App. at 8a, 11a). With respect to the Union’s direct 
standing to assert its own rights, the Eighth Circuit held that the 
relief requested by the Union in its complaint would not redress 
the union’s asserted injury. (App. at 7a). It rejected the Union’s 
argument that its “catch-all prayer for relief” would redress the 
injury resulting from Brown Shoe’s alleged failure of notice to 
the Union. (App. at 7a). The Eighth Circuit reasoned, “We do not 
think that the boilerplate remedies language . . . , broad and non- 
specific as it is, comports with the constitutional requirement of 
5 
redressability envisioned by the Supreme Court in Allen [v. 
Wright, 468 U.S. 737 (1984)] and its other cases on the subject.” 
(App. at 7a). The court also specifically rejected the Union's 
argument that the language of the WARN Act granting a union a 
right to sue to enforce the Act “relieve[s] the union of its 
obligation to meet the constitutional requirement of standing.” 
(App. at 7a). 
With respect to the Union’s associational standing to assert 
the monetary damage claims of its members, the Eighth Circuit 
held that the Union had failed to meet the third part of the test for 
associational standing set forth by this Court in Hunt v. 
Washington State Apple Advertising Comm'n, 432 U.S. 333, 343 
(1977), i.e., that “neither the claim asserted nor the relief 
requested requires the participation of individual members in the 
lawsuit.” (App. at 9a). The Eighth Circuit reasoned that the relief 
the Union requested — backpay and benefits for its members — 
would require individualized proof because of the varying times 
of termination of each employee as alleged in the complaint as 
well as the fact that the employees “were paid on a piecework 
basis.” (App. at 10a). 
The Eighth Circuit also upheld the district court’s denial of 
the Union’s motion to alter or amend judgment and rejected the 
Union’s argument that it should have been permitted to amend its 
complaint after that complaint had been dismissed. (App. at 11la- 
12a). The Eighth Circuit reasoned that because “the union has

to amend — in spite of having been put on notice (by the opposing 
party, no less) of the possible need to amend,” the district court 
had not abused its discretion in denying the Union’s motion to

SUMMARY OF THE ARGUMENT 
The Petitioner in this case has not presented this Court with 
any grounds for the granting of a petition for writ of certiorari. 
There is not one issue involved in this case and upon which the 
Eighth Circuit ruled which has not been long settled by this Court 
and the federal courts of appeals. There is no disagreement 
between the federal appellate courts on the issue of a union’s 
standing under the WARN Act to assert the monetary damage 
claims of its members under the Act. Nor is there an important 
question of federal law which has not been, but should be, settled 
by this Court or has been decided in a way that conflicts with the 
applicable decisions of this Court. 
The Eighth Circuit did nothing more in this case than apply 
this Court’s well-established principles of constitutional 
standing. Its decision that the Union lacked standing to assert its 
own claims was based on its application of the test for 
constitutional standing set forth by this Court. The Eighth 
Circuit’s holding that the Union had also failed to establish that it 
had standing to assert the individual monetary claims of its 
members under the test set forth in Hunt v. Washington State 
Apple Advertising Comm'n, 432 U.S. 333 (1977), was entirely 
consistent with this Court’s holding in Warth v. Seldin, 490 U.S. 
422 (1975), that the test for associational standing reflects 
constitutional rather than prudential concerns. Thus, the Eighth

REASONS FOR DENYING THE WRIT 
A. The Eighth Circuit’s Ruling Raises No “Fundamental 
And Recurring Separation Of Powers Issue” Which This 
Court Need Decide. 
The Eighth Circuit's determination that this Union had not 
adequately invoked the jurisdiction of the federal courts by 
failing to establish in its pleading a case or controversy under 
Article III of the United States Constitution was compelled by 
this Court’s long-settled and well-established precedent. Its 
holding is entirely consistent with the long line of federal cases 
which require a litigant to establish in its pleadings that it has 
standing to invoke the federal court’s jurisdiction, whether that 
litigant is seeking to enforce its own rights or, as an association, 
the rights of its members. Indeed, it was the Union's own failure 
to establish in its complaint its standing to assert its own rights 
and the rights of its members which compelled dismissal of its 
complaint rather than the application of an incorrect 
constitutional analysis on the part of the Eighth Circuit. 
The Union’s petition for a writ of certiorari is premised on a 
fundamental misperception of the principles of standing 
established by this Court and applied by the federal courts of 
appeals. The underlying premise of the Union's argument to this 
Court — as it was in the courts below — is that because Congress, 
in the WARN Act, granted unions the right to sue for violations of 
the statute, it also granted unions standing to sue under the 
WARN Act. This argument ignores the consistent holdings of 
this Court that Congress cannot by statute eliminate the 
requirements of Article III. Lujan v. Defenders of Wildlife, 504 
U.S. 555, 560 (1992); Gladstone, Realtors v. Village of Bellwood, 
441 U.S. 91, 99 (1979); Warth v. Seldin, 422 U.S. 490, 499 
(1975); Trafficante v. Metropolitan Life Ins. Co., 409 U.S. 205, 
209 (1972). Thus, in Gladstone, this Court held, “In no event, . . . 
may Congress abrogate the Art. III minimum: A plaintiff must 
always have suffered a distinct and palpable injury to himself . . . 
that is likely to be redressed if the requested relief is granted.” 
441 U.S. at 100 (quoting Simon v. Eastern Kentucky Welfare 
Rights Organization, 426 U.S. 26, 38 (1976)). And in Warth, this 
Court held, “Congress may grant an express right of action to 
persons who otherwise would be barred by prudential standing 
rules. Of course, Art. III’s requirement remains: the plaintiff 
must still allege a distinct and palpable injury to himself, even if it 
is an injury shared by a large class of other possible litigants.” 422 
U.S. at 499. 
Thus, a congressional grant of a right to sue does not in itself 
establish the standing of a litigant under Article III of the 
Constitution. Congress could not in the WARN Act confer 
standing on this union either expressly or impliedly in an extra 
constitutional manner so that the Union could assert either its 
own rights or the rights of its members under the statute. While 
Congress could in the WARN Act grant the union a right to sue 
and thus eliminate prudential considerations attached to 
standing, it could not eliminate the constitutional requirements of 
Article III. These constitutional requirements are precisely those 
that the Eighth Circuit applied. 
Therefore, the “fundamental and recurring separation of 
powers issue” which the Union contends is raised by the Eighth 
9 
Circuit's ruling in this case is one that has long been settled by this 
Court. Congress does not have the ability, through the exercise of 
its Article I legislative powers, to confer standing to a litigant 
beyond the requirements of Article III. A case or controversy 
must stil] exist and it is the province of the federal courts to 
determine whether the requirements of Article ITI have been met. 
Cf. Marbury v. Madison, | Cranch 137 (1803). The Eighth Circuit 
correctly determined that “the WARN Act's provision that 
designates the union as a proper plaintiff -o assert violations of 
the Act in federal court does not relieve tne union of its obligation 
to meet the constitutional requirement of standing.” (App. at 7a). 
The Eighth Circuit's ruling is entirely consistent with this Court's 
holdings in Warth, Lujan and Gladstone that Congress may not 
eliminate the requirements of Article III. Because there is no 
fundamental constitutional question which must be answered by 
this Court, there is no valid basis for granting the Union's 
petition.

C. The Eighth Circuit’s Holding Is Consistent With This 
Court’s Well-Established Principles Of Standing. 
Article III of the Constitution restricts the judicial power of 
the federal courts to actual “cases” or “controversies” and the 
issue of standing is one at the heart of Article III. Lujan v. 
Defenders of Wildlife, 504 U.S. 555, 560 (1992). As this Court 
explained in Warth v. Seldin, 422 U.S. 490, 498 (1975), “In 
essence the question of standing is whether the litigant is entitled 
to have the court decide the merits of the dispute or of particular 
issues. ... [I]t is founded in concern about the proper — and 
properly limited — role of the courts in a democratic society. This 
Court further explained the principles of standing in Allen v. 
Wright, 468 U.S. 737, 752 (1984), when it stated: 
Determining standing in a particular case 
may be facilitated by clarifying principles or 
even clear rules developed in prior cases. 
Typically, however, the standing inquiry 
requires careful judicial examination of a 
complaint’s allegations to ascertain whether 
the particular plaintiff is entitled to 
adjudication of the particular claims asserted. 
Is the injury too abstract, or otherwise not 
appropriate, to be considered judicially 
cognizable? Is the line of causation between 
the illegal conduct and injury too attenuated? 
Is the prospect of obtaining relief from the 
injury as a result of a favorable ruling too 
speculative? 
14 
(Citing Valley Forge Christian College v. American United For 
Separation of Church and State, Inc., 454 U.S. 464, 472-73 
(1982)). 
This Court has differentiated between constitutional (Article 
ITT) standing and prudential limitations on standing. Lujan, 504 
U.S. at 560; Gladstone, 441 U.S. at 99-100; Warth, 422 U.S. at 
498. The requirement of constitutional standing seeks to ensure a 
concrete Article III “case or controversy” by focusing on the 
plaintiff's “harm.” Warth, 422 U.S. at 498-99. Prudential 
standing limitations prevent a court from adjudicating “questions 
of broad social import where no individual rights would be 
vindicated and limit access to the federal court to those litigants 
best suited to assert a particular claim.” Gladstone, 441 U.S. at 
100. With respect to prudential considerations, Congress may 
grant a right to sue to persons who otherwise might be barred by 
prudential limitations. Gladstone, 441 U.S. at 100; Warth, 422 
U.S. at 501; Sierra Club v. Morton, 405 U.S. 727 (1972). 
Congress may not, however, expand by statute the case or 
controversy limitations imposed upon litigants by Article III. 
Gladstone, 441 U.S. at 100; Trafficante, 409 U.S. at 209. As this 
Court held in Valley Forge, “[O)}f one thing we may be sure: 
Those who do not possess Art. III standing may not litigate as 
suitors in the courts of the United States.” 454 U.S. at 475-76. 
This Court has also distinguished immediate or direct 
standing from associational standing. Warth, 422 U.S. at 511. 
Immediate or direct standing exists when a plaintiff is asserting 
its own rights; associational standing involves enforcement of the 
rights of some or all of an association's members. See Hunt v. 
Washington State Apple Advertising Comm'n, 432 U.S. 333 
(1977); Warth, 422 U.S. at 511. The Court has not differentiated 
between the associational standing of a voluntary organization 
with that of involuntary organization, as the Union urges it to do 
in this case. In either case, a plaintiff must demonstrate “such a 
personal stake in the outcome of the controversy as to warrant his 
15 
invocation of federal court jurisdiction and to justify exercise of 
the court’s remedial powers on his behalf.” Warth, 422 U.S. at 
498-99 (quoting Baker v. Carr, 369 U.S. 186, 204 (1962)). 
The questions set forth in Allen and the test set forth in Warth 
and Hunt are precisely those which the Eighth Circuit addressed 
and applied in this case. Its conclusion — that the Union lacked 
constitutional standing to assert the monetary damage claims of 
its members under the WARN Act — is in accord with this Court's 
decisions.

the organization's purpose; and (c) neither the claim asserted nor 
the relief requested requires the participation of individual 
members in the lawsuit.” 432 U.S. at 343. Thus, it is clear that the 
Hunt test is & constitutional rather than prudential one. There is 
nothing in either Warth or Hunt which indicates that this Court 
believed any of the elements of the test to be anything other than 
constitutional. 
Consequently, the issue which the Union urges support the 
grant of a writ of certiorari in this case — whether the third prong 
of the Hunt test is prudential or constitutional — is one that has 
long ago been decided by this Court. Given this Court's rulings in 
Warth and Hunt, there is no question but that the third prong of the 
Hunt test is constitutional and was correctly applied by the Eighth 
Circuit.

(b) The Union Is Not Exempt From Article III 
Standing Requirements Merely Because It Is 
The Statutory Representative Of Its Members. 
The Union further argues that the Eighth Circuit erred “by 
assuming that the associational standing doctrine as stated in 
Brock and Hunt is fully applicable to a situation, such as the one 
here, where Congress has prescribed detailed procedures for 
appointing an entity as the representative of the interests of a 
class of individuals.” (Petition at 20). The Union's argument 
appears to be in that it should not be required to establish 
constitutional standing as a part of its pleadings simply because it 
is a statutory representative under the National Labor Relations 
Act. 
A similar argument was rejected by the Ninth Circuit in 
United Union of Roofers, Waterproofers and Allied Trade No. 40 
v. Insurance Corp. of America, 919 F.2d 1398 (9th Cir. 1990). In 
that case, the Ninth Circuit, relying on the third prong of the Hunt 
test, affirmed the district court's ruling that a union plaintiff 
lacked standing to recover money damages for the unpaid wages 
of its members from a surety bond payment. /d. at 1399-1400. 
The union urged that it had “special representative obligations” 
to its members “that make it an appropriate association to sue for 
damages on behalf of its members.” Jd. The Ninth Circuit 
rejected this argument, holding, “Even assuming, arguendo, that 
a union does have a special representative duty to its members 
that distinguishes it from many other associations, there is 
absolutely no authority that such a factor would negate the 
required application of the test established in Hunt.” Jd. The court 
concluded, “{I)t is clear that individual Union members will have 
to participate at the proof of damage stage. There is no escaping 
the fact that the Union in this case cannot overcome the third 
hurdle placed before it by Supreme Court precedent.” /d. 
This Court has also refused to apply a “sliding scale” of 
constitutional standing which would vary according to the claims 
asserted. Valley Forge, 454 U.S. at 484. In Valley Forge, the 
plaintiffs argued that because the establishment clause of the First 
Amendment was more “fundamental” than other constitutional 
clauses, the standard for determining whether plaintiffs had 
standing should be lessened. Jd. This Court rejected that 
argument, holding that “([tJo the extent the Court of Appeals 
relied on a view of standing under which the Art. III burdens 
diminish as the ‘importance’ of the claim on the merits increases, 
we reject that notion.” Jd. It also added that “we know of no 
principled basis on which to create a hierarchy of constitutional 
values or a complementary ‘sliding scale’ of standing which 
might permit respondent to invoke the judicial power of the 
United States.” Id. 
The Union urges this Court to grant its petition in this case in 
order to establish the principle that “a separate judicial inquiry” 
into standing is not necessary when the plaintiff is a statutory 
representative. The Union has not, however, pointed to any 
“principled basis” on which to create “sliding scale” of 
constitutional standing which varies according to the 
congressional status of the party asserting the claim. This Court 
has held repeatedly that Coagress may not abrogate Article III's 
requirement that there be a case or controversy. Lujan, 504 U.S. at 
560; Allen, 468 U.S. at 751; Gladstone, 441 U.S. at 91; Warth, 
422 U.S. at 501; Trafficante, 409 U.S. at 209. Were the Court to 
ignore its precedent in this area and adopt the Union's reasoning, 
it would effectively eviscerate the requirements of Article III of 
the Constitution. 
It is significant that the Union has no authority and can point 
to no basis for the granting of a petition for writ of certiorari to 
adopt the novel and unprecedented theory that it is not subject to 
the case or controversy requirement of Article III of the 
Constitution merely because it is the statutory representative of 
its members. Such a theory is directly contrary to this Court’s 
29 
rulings and constitutes an inadequate basis for the granting of the 
Union's petition. 
(c) The Eighth Circuit’s Holding Is Not 
Inconsistent With This Court’s Opinion In 
Hoosier Cardinal. 
The Union argues finally that the Eighth Circuit's analysis is 
flawed because it is inconsistent with this Court’s holding in 
International Union, United Auto Workers v. Hoosier Cardinal 
Corp., 383 U.S. 696 (1966), in which this Court held that a union 
may sue under the Labor Management Relations Act for the 
monetary damages of its members under a collective bargaining 
agreement. (Petition at 23-24). 
The Eighth Circuit’s ruling is not, however, inconsistent 
with this Court’s opinion in Hoosier Cardinal. As the Eighth 
Circuit recognized in its opinion, the claims in Hoosier Cardinal 
were made pursuant to the terms of a collective bargaining 
agreement, to which the union plaintiff was a signatory. 383 U.S. 
at 699. (App. at 8a). Thus the union in Hoosier Cardinal had 
standing because it was seeking “to vindicate employee rights 
under a contract the union had obtained,” and because its interest 
in the case “implements no more than the established doctrine 
that the union’s role in the collective bargaining process does not 
end with the making of the contract.” Jd. at 700. Here, the 
individual claims of its members which the Union pursued in its 
complaint were not brought pursuant to the collective bargaining 
agreement between the Union and Brown Shoe but under the 
statutory provisions of the WARN Act. There was no privity of 
contract and the rights of the union protected by the collective 
bargaining agreement were not implicated, as they were in 
Hoosier Cardinal. In accordance with the Court's holding in 
Hoosier Cardinal, the federal courts have held that individual 
employees do not have standing to enforce their Section 301 
rights because they are not parties to the collective bargaining 
30 
agreement between the union and the employer. Cleveland v. 
Porca Co., 38 F.3d 289 (7th Cir. 1994); Katir v. Columbia 
University, 15 F.3d 23 (2d Cir. 1994); Martin v. Youngstown Sheet 
& Tube Co., 911 F.2d 1239, 1244 (7th Cir. 1990); Vosch v. Werner 
Continental Inc., 734 F.2d 149, 154 (3d Cir. 1984), cert. denied, 
469 U.S. 1108 (1985). 
The law of standing set forth by this Court is clear and 
consistent. The Eighth Circuit's ruling was in harmony with the 
precedent of this Court, which it was bound to follow, as well as 
the holdings of the other federal courts of appeals. There is, 
therefore, no valid basis for the granting of the Union’s petition 
for a writ of certiorari.
