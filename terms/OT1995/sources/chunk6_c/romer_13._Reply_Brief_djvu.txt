REPLY BRIEF OF PETITIONERS 


Stripped of rhetoric, Respondents’ arguments present 
two themes: (1) the Court should overrule James v. 
Valtierra and extend beyond race the narrow Hunter ex- 
ception to rational basis review of internal state govern- 
ment restructurings; and (2) Amendment 2 fails rational 
basis review—that is, Amendment 2 “is so unrelated to 
the achievement of any combination of legitimate purposes 
that [the Court] can only conclude that the [Colorado 
voters’] actions were irrational.” Vance v. Bradley, 440 
U.S. 93, 97 (1979) (emphasis added ). 


Respondents’ arguments fail because they are premised 
not upon law, but rather upon a parade of horribles de- 
rived from imagined interpretations and applications of 
Amendment 2. In following this approach, Respondents 
have simply ignored the facial nature of their challenge— 
Amendment 2 has never been applied, much less in the 
extreme manner hypothesized by Respondents. Amend- 
ment 2 is facially valid so long as at least some of its 
applications would be constitutional, see, e.g., United 
States Vv. Salerno, 481 U.S. 739, 744-45 (1987); Re- 
spondents’ concern that Amendment 2 might be misused 
can be addressed only in the context of an “as applied” 
challenge by an affected individual. 


All that is at issue here is whether Coloradans can 
divest their elected representatives of authority to deal 
with a particular isswe—protections for homosexuals and 
bisexuals that go beyond those afforded by federal law— 
and instead insist that this question of public policy be 
resolved solely by the people on a statewide basis. Under 
the established jurisprudence of this Court, which stands 
on bedrock principles of federalism, such ordering of a 
State’s internal affairs is subject to heightened scrutiny in 
a federal equal protection challenge on/y if it classifies on 
the basis of race or infringes a fundamental constitutional 
right. Amendment 2, which furthers numerous legiti- 
mate—even compelling—state purposes, infringes no

avow any reliance here on suspect class.’ Under settled 
law the judgment below must be reversed. 


I. THE HUNTER EXCEPTION IS LIMITED TO 
RACIAL CLASSIFICATIONS. 


Far from effectuating a sweeping reworking of the 
American political process, Hunter v. Erickson, 393 U.S. 
385 (1969), established a narrow exception to the tradi- 
tional rules of judicial restraint that apply to federal equal 
protection challenges to a State’s structuring of its own 
government. The only area where this Court has con- 
doned close scrutiny of such state decisions is in the con- 
text of race. But the exceptional judicial review engen- 
dered by racial classifications cannot lead to the conclusion 
that every restructuring of state government, regardless 
of the group affected, is inherently suspect. To the con- 
trary, recognizing the “unique nature of state decisions 
that ‘go to the heart of representative government,’ ” 
Gregory Vv. Ashcroft, 501 U.S. 452, 461 (1991), this 
Court has steadfastly applied the opposite presumption.

B. Respondents’ claim that James can be overruled

equally flawed. Extending Hunter’s narrow exception to 
any “independently identifiable” group allows the excep-

hands of the federal judiciary. Every law classifies and 
thus every law affects “independently identifiable” 
groups, if the person defining the group employs any 
imagination at all. In James, the group directly harmed 
comprised individuals who supported subsidized housing, 
and the readily identifiable sub-group was the poor. Here, 
those immediately affected by Amendment 2 are voters 
who want anti-discrimination policy set at the local level; 
an identifiable sub-group is homosexuals and bisexuals. 
Amendment 2 treats political issues, not individuals dif- 
ferently, and thus reveals that once the search for “in- 
dependently identifiable” groups is allowed to proceed 
outside of the unique race-specific context in which it 
arose, that inquiry becomes meaningless.'”
