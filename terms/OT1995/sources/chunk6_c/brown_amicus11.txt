THE AMERICAN FEDERATION OF GOVERN-

SUMMARY OF ARGUMENT 


This case will decide whether a labor union has stand- 
ing to enforce the Worker Adjustment and Retraining 
Notification (WARN) Act, 29 U.S.C. §§ 2101-09, 
against an employer who had failed to provide advance 
notice of layoffs to either the union or the workers repre- 
sented by that union. In denying standing to Local 751 
of the United Food and Commercial Workers Union 
(UFCW), despite its conclusion that the union had al- 
leged an injury to itself, the court below erroneously 
determined that “[a]n award of monetary damages to any 
aggrieved employees would do nothing to remedy the 
alleged failure of notice to the UFCW.” United Food 
and Commercial Workers Int'l Union, Local 751 vy. 
Brown Group, Inc., 50 F.3d 1426, 1430 (8th Cir. 1995). 
This determination reflected a flawed conception of the 
purposes of WARN, a faulty understanding of the role 
which monetary relief plays in furthering WARN’s pur- 
poses, and the imposition of an extremely restricted view 
of the role that unions serve as a representative of their 
members’ interests. 


Acting upon its erroneous reading of WARN’s purpose 
and remedies, the Eighth Circuit panel failed to give 
proper recognition to the Congressionally-established in- 
tegral role of labor unions as integral in effectuating the 
purposes of WARN. Amici submit that the Eighth Cir- 
cuit panel’s constricted application of standing principles 
will not withstand scrutiny in the light of the structure, 
legislative history, and substantive legal rights of WARN. 


Properly considered, labor union standing to enforce 
WARN falls well-within this Court’s Article III] standing 
jurisprudence, even in the absence of express Congres- 
sional authorization to unions to sue to enforce WARN. 


7 


See International Union, UAW vy. Brock, 477 U.S. 274 
(1986). Amici submit that the Eighth Circuit panel 
erred by ignoring Congress’ mandate that unions, as “rep- 
resentatives”, may sue on behalf of their members.' How- 
ever, assuming that Congress had not included such a 
provision, Amici argue that the UFCW sstill satisfied 
Article III's core requirements, and the test articulated 
in Hunt v. Washington State Apple Advertising Comm'n, 
432 U.S. 333 (1977). Amici therefore fully support the 
arguments set forth in the UFCW'’s brief, but focus 
herein primarily upon this mistaken assumption. 


At the most fundamental level, unions clearly suffer 
cognizable injuries to their interests when advance notice 
of layoffs is not provided. The Eighth Circuit panel failed 
to recognize that union enforcement of WARN’s civil 
penalties acts both as punishment for the statutory viola- 
tion and as a deterrent to ensure future compliance with 
WARN’s advance notice requirements. This failure re- 
sulted in the erroneous conclusion that the union’s injuries 
are not redressable through a suit for monetary relief on 
behalf of affected workers. Indeed, pursuant to WARN, 
that is the only possible redress. Further, this failure re- 
sulted in the erroneous conclusion that union enforcement 
of WARN to collect civil penalties is analogous to ac- 
tions for compensatory damages, and therefore that the 
individual participation of union members was required. 
These errors led to a misapplication of standing doctrine 
as it has been developed by this Court. 


For these reasons, amici join with petitioner UFCW in 
urging this Court to reverse the judgment dismissing this 
case for lack of standing. 


‘Congress provided in WARN that: “A person seeking to 
enforce such liability, including a representative of employees or 
a unit of local government aggrieved under paragraph (1) or (3), 
may sue either for such person or for other persons similarly 
situated, or both, in any district court of the United States for 
any district in which the violation is alleged to have occurred, or 
in which the employer transacts business.” 29 U.S.C. § 2104(a) (5).

ARGUMENT 


UNIONS HAVE STANDING TO REPRESENT THEIR 
MEMBERS IN SUITS ENFORCING THE WORKER 
ADJUSTMENT AND RETRAINING NOTIFICATION 
ACT. 


A. Failure to Provide Advance Notice of Layoffs or Plant 
Closing in Compliance with WARN Harms Unions as 
Well as Their Members. 


a. The Eighth Circuit panel erroneously assumed that 
in the absence of the statutory provision allowing a union 
to sue as the “representative” of dislocated workers, 29 
U.S.C. § 2104(a)(5), that the UFCW could not satisfy 
Article III association standing principles. In fact, the 
opposite is true: A lawfully recognized union may assert 
its bargaining unit members’ WARN claims so long as 
the Article III “case or controversy” requirement is met. 


The touchstone here is that the injury is direct, and its 
nature precise. Significantly, this case satisfies the heart 
of Article III; namely, the requirement that the associa- 
tion’s members suffer a “distinct and palpable injury.” 
Warth v. Seldin, 422 U.S. 490, 500-01 (1975); Havens 
Realty Corp. v. Coleman, 455 U.S. 363, 372 (1982). 
Further, the union’s interests are protected by WARN, 
making the UFCW a “litigant entitled to have the court 
decide the merits of the dispute.” Warth, 422 US. at 
498. Indeed, the lower court’s approach is contrary to 
this Court’s charge that “standing is gauged by the spe- 
cific common-law, statutory, or constitutional claims that 
a party presents.” IJnternational Primate Protection 
League v. Administrators of Tulane Educ. Fund, 500 
U.S. 72, 77 (1991). See Gladstone, Realtors v. Village 
of Bellwood, 441 U.S. 91, 99 (1979); Havens Realty 
Corp. v. Coleman, 455 U.S. 363 (1982). 


The sine qua non of standing jurisprudence is that 
the entity seeking standing first possess a legally enforce- 
able right. Associated Gen. Contractors, Inc. v. Cali- 


9 


fornia State Council of Carpenters, 459 U.S. 519, 540 
(1983) (“[iJn each case [a union's] injury must be 
analyzed to determine whether it is of the type that the 
| ] statute was intended to forestall.”); Tennessee Elec. 
Power Co. v. Tennessee Valley Auth., 306 U.S. 118, 
137-8 (1939); Alabama Power Co. v. Ickes, 302 U.S. 
464, 480 (1938). This Court has also looked at the 
directness or indirectness of the asserted injury. Asso- 
ciated Gen., 459 U.S. at 540. 


Where a statute is silent as to whom an appropriate 
litigant should be, this Court has permitted representation 
in accordance with standards established under common 
law or statutes of general application. Whitmore v. 
Arkansas, 495 U.S. 149, 163 (1990) (third party repre- 
sentation permissible where the standards under the com- 
mon law are satisfied); 13 C. Wright, A. Miller, Federal 
Practice and Procedure § 3531.9 at 627 (West 1984) 
(formal representative such as trustees, guardians and 
personal representatives may protect the interests of those 
they represent). 

b. “The actual or threatened injury required by Ar- 
ticle IIl may exist solely by virtue of ‘statutes creating 
legal rights, the invasion of which creates standing. .. . ” 
Warth, 422 U.S. at 500. The right possessed here, is at 
its heart, one which lies in the federal statutorily estab- 
lished role of unions as the exclusive representative of 
workers under the National Labor Relations Act. 29 
U.S.C. § 151 (1995); See also 29 U.S.C. §§ 157 and 
159 (1995); Eastex, Inc. v. NLRB, 437 U.S. 556, 564 
(1978).° When a union sues to redress a violation of 
WARN, it acts as the representative of the employees’ 
own choosing for the “purpose of . . . other mutual aid 
and protection.” 29 U.S.C. §§ 151 and 157 (1995). 
Indeed, protection and enforcement of employees’ rights 
is a union's raison d'etre.

is simply not an individual notice statute and that unions 
themselves are damaged when they do not receive notice. 
First and foremost, advance notice permits unions to 
more effectively exercise their right to represent their 
members through collective bargaining about the plant 
closing decision and its effects. See First National Main-

assist its members in seeking new employment, replace- 
ment income, and adjustment services. This assistance to 
members enables the union to fulfill its responsibilities to 
advance its members economic interests, and, in turn,

to improved future organizing opportunities with those 
members in their new workplaces as well as enhanced

to assist its members, the union’s capacity to act as an 
effective advocate for its members is weakened. See 
OPEIU, Local 2 v. FDIC, 962 F.2d 63 (D.C. Cir. 
1992).

680, 681 (“{BJargaining over the effects of a decision

Further, local or district unions may reside within a geo-

Not Required by the Rights Asserted nor the Relief

wages, WARN’s monetary relief provisions most closely 
resemble civil penalties intended to ensure compliance

part of the affected workers.”

tion is by no means trivial. First of all, the lower court’s 
restrictive understanding of the role played by monetary 
relief in WARN enforcement undergirds its determina- 
tion that monetary relief for affected workers would not 
redress the injuries inflicted upon the UFCW by the lack 
of advance notice. 50 F.3d at 1430. Instead, an anoma-

Second, this restrictive understanding divorced WARN 
from its central role in Congress’ worker adjustment 
scheme, and undergirds its determination that the individ- 
ual participation of UFCW members was required. 
Viewed in proper context, WARN’s civil penalties were 
intended to encourage employer compliance with the stat- 
ute’s advance notice requirements—including its role for 
unions in adjustment assistance and retraining.

Accordingly, any monetary relief obtained by unions

reason, individual participation of union members is not 
necessary, and monetary relief to affected members in a

CONCLUSION 


The decision below rested upon a fundamental miscon- 
ception of the role of labor unions in this country, creat- 
ing an artificial gap between the interests of workers and 
unions, and one which stands in contrast to national labor 
policy as codified in our federal laws. WARN is, in the 
final analysis a statute designed to protect collective inter- 
ests. Violations of that law are redressable through the 
civil penalties created by Congress.
