## Identity, text, and lower-court procedural statements

Romer v. Evans, Supreme Court No. 94-1039, scheduled merits event May 20, 1996. Review concerns the Colorado Supreme Court's October 11, 1994 affirmance of a permanent injunction against Amendment 2, Nos. 94SA48 and 94SA128, 882 P.2d 1335. Argument date supplied by runtime: October 10, 1995. All nine current Justices are the event roster; no recusal established.

Amendment 2's complete text, reproduced in the lower merits opinion App. B-1 and in the joint appendix:

> No Protected Status Based on Homosexual, Lesbian, or Bisexual Orientation. Neither the State of Colorado, through any of its branches or departments, nor any of its agencies, political subdivisions, municipalities or school districts, shall enact, adopt or enforce any statute, regulation, ordinance or policy whereby homosexual, lesbian or bisexual orientation, conduct, practices or relationships shall constitute or otherwise be the basis of or entitle any person or class of persons to have or claim any minority status quota preferences, protected status or claim of discrimination. This Section of the Constitution shall be in all respects self-executing.

The State's electorate approved it November 3, 1992; results were certified December 16. The predecision lower court reports 813,966–710,151 (53.4%–46.6%). Plaintiffs brought the state action November 12, 1992. Individuals and the listed local governmental entities sought to prevent enforcement. Preliminary relief preceded the merits trial; the merits judgment now reviewed permanently enjoins enforcement. Colorado's *Evans I*, 854 P.2d 1270 (1993), held strict scrutiny applicable to a state measure fencing an identifiable class out of equal political participation. The 1994 majority adhered to that standard and assessed the State's asserted justifications.

The 1994 majority's own descriptions establish these source facts:

- At minimum, Amendment 2 repeals existing state/local antidiscrimination measures based on sexual orientation and prevents similar or greater future protections absent a state constitutional amendment. Its specific state-law reach remains important: the parties dispute “special” protection versus ordinary legal protection. Neither the government's nor plaintiffs' broadest characterization is a finding simply because stated in a brief.
- The merits trial rejected suspect/quasi-suspect classification on its political-powerlessness reasoning. Plaintiffs did not appeal that ruling. Individual respondents expressly state they do not concede its correctness. The lower majority did not decide that constitutional classification.
- Plaintiffs presented rational-basis invalidity as an alternative; the trial and majority did not decide it because they applied strict scrutiny. Erickson's dissent did address rational-basis review. Individual respondents' merits brief pp. 12–15 presses it here.
- The State did not reassert its child-welfare justification on the merits appeal. The lower court's other listed asserted interests concerned factionalism, governmental organization, resources for suspect classes, personal/familial/religious privacy, and avoidance of subsidizing a special-interest group's political objectives. Morality was separately argued and treated as sufficiently presented by the majority.
- The majority treated religious liberty as compelling but found the amendment not narrowly tailored. It referred to religious exemptions (including Denver's), intimate-association and small owner-occupied housing exceptions, and the wide range of nonintimate commercial and governmental contexts covered. It found no demonstrated connection between prohibiting ordinary nondiscrimination protection and familial authority to teach values.
- The majority reports trial findings that actual Denver/Wisconsin experience did not support the asserted cost and enforcement impairment. The State's contrary material was described as theory or prediction. The dissent regarded limited state enforcement budgets, uniformity, and religious freedom as rational reasons and would reverse/vacate the injunction.
- The State requested severance of orientation language from conduct/practices/relationships. The majority rejected it: the four descriptions target the same class; the remainder was not autonomous. Its severability rule asks autonomy and enacting-body intent. No federal court finding about a new narrowing construction is established.
- The majority rejected the State's Tenth Amendment claim that the people's constitutional amendment power validates an otherwise federal-rights violation.
- Scott joined the majority and separately proposed a Privileges or Immunities right to assemble/petition, discussing Slaughter-House. That is a lower separate opinion, not controlling Supreme Court law or automatically a preserved new claim.
- Erickson disagreed with the identified fundamental right and read Hunter/Seattle as race-specific; he invoked James v. Valtierra, Gordon v. Lance, and Crawford, favored rational-basis review, and would vacate the injunction. His use of historical Heller must be replaced with actual simulated Heller before any Supreme Court modeling.

## Briefing statements, attributed rather than adopted

State merits pp. 9–12: characterizes the measure as preemption of special protections and allocation of a divisive issue to the statewide electorate; argues no disenfranchisement, ballot denial, or prohibition on seeking a new state amendment; reads Hunter/Seattle as race cases and James as a refusal to extend; opposes a broad identifiable-group entitlement to every governmental decision level. It proposes rational bases of finite enforcement resources, privacy/religious/associational burdens, minimal regulation, and statewide uniformity. Those are arguments, not findings about the precise effect of every local ordinance.

Individual respondents' merits pp. 12–15: argue unequal political access subject to strict scrutiny and, independently, irrational blanket foreclosure of one group's eligibility for nondiscrimination protection. They distinguish a mere loss on a substantive policy from selectively closing ordinary lawmaking channels; dispute fit between statewide total foreclosure and the State's proposed interests. They also endorse an amicus argument that the total group-specific withdrawal of protection is itself an equal-protection defect. Their brief states some First Amendment claims remain in the trial court. No final adjudication of those claims is recovered here.

The local governmental respondents' separate merits brief defends equal participation in the governmental process, drawing on Reynolds, Hunter, and Seattle and the dangers of majoritarian closure. Its argument begins printed p. 10.
