STATEMENT OF THE CASE 


The Worker Adjustment and Retraining Notification Act 
(“WARN”), 29 U.S.C. § 2101-09, provides that cov- 
éred employers may not order a “plant closing or mass 
layoff,” as statutorily defined (29 U.S.C. § 2101(a)(2) & 
(3)), without providing notice sixty days beforehand “to 
each representative of the affected employees at the time 
of the notice or, if there is no such representative at that 
time, to each affected employee.” 29 U.S.C. § 2102(a) 
(emphasis supplied). “Representative” is defined in the 
statute as “an exclusive representative of employees within 
the meaning of section 159(a) or 158(f) of [the National 
Labor Relations Act (NLRA)].. .” 


The WARN Act goes on to provide (a) that if an em- 
ployer “orders a plant closing or mass layoff in violation 
of section 2102,” the employer is liable “to each aggrieved

and benefits, (29 U.S.C. § 2104(a)(1)); and (b) that “a 
person seeking to enforce such liability, including a repre- 
sentative of employees . . . aggrieved Under paragraph (1) 
. . . May sue” in district court (29 U.S.C. § 2104(a)(5) 
(emphasis supplied) ).

SUMMARY OF ARGUMENT 


I. Where a standing issue arises in a federal case 
between two private parties the pertinent question—and 
the only pertinent question—is whether the case will be 
“presented in an adversary context and in a form his- 
torically viewed as capable of resolution through the ju- 
dicial process.” Flast v. Cohen, 392 US. 83, 94-95 
(1968). In making this determination, the initial task in 
a case brought under a statute is to determine whether 
the statute itself authorizes the plaintiff to bring a lawsuit 
of the kind before the court. 


Here, the WARN Act specifically authorizes unions as 
exclusive representatives of the affected employees to 
bring suit where the employer fails to provide the notice 
to the union mandated by the statute in the event of a 
plant closing or mass layoff, and specifically provides as 
the only remedy to the union in such a lawsuit recovery 
on behalf of the represented employees of statutory mone- 
tary payments. Those payments are in the nature of liqui- 
dated damages, designed in part to deter employer viola- 
tions and in part to compensate the group of employees 
represented by the union for the lost opportunity to re- 


5 


spond effectively to the plant closing or mass layoff. Pro- 
viding monetary relief payable to the affected employees 
where the union sues for a violation suitably redresses the 
injury to the union by reason of the violation since that 
right was created to facilitate the union's role in aiding 
and assisting the represented employees adversely affected 
by the closing or layoff. 


Given this statutocty structure, there is no doubt that 
the union in this case has standing. Where there is a 
specific statutory grant of litigating authority such as that 
here, the only pertinent standing limitations are those 
derived directly from Article II] of the Constitution. 
Warth v. Seldin, 422 U.S. 490, 500-501 (1975). The 
bedrock Article III requirement is that the litigant “must 
allege personal injury fairly traceable to the defendant's 
allegedly unlawful conduct and likely to be redressed by 
the requested relief.” Allen v. Wright, 468 U.S. 737, 751 
(1984). The WARN Act statutory scheme alone is 
sufficient to demonstrate that each aspect of this Article 
III standard is met here. 


In particular, where there is a common law or statu- 
tory legal duty owed to the plaintiff which will, if carried 
out, enable the plaintiff to advance a third party's interests, 
and the defendant breaches that duty, the law recog- 
nizes that a suit by the plaintiff pursuing relief, monetary, 
individualized, or otherwise, that redounds to the finan- 
cial benefit of the third party is one that is appropriate 
for judicial resolution. Third party beneficiary cases, for 
example, have precisely that structure, as do cases in 
which the government is accorded statutory authority to 
sue for relief for individuals and cases brought by unions 
under § 301 of the Labor Management Relations Act, 29 
U.S.C. § 185, to recover for represented employees the 
wages and working conditions provided for in a collective 
bargaining agreement on an employer breach of the agree- 
ment. /nternational Union UAW vy. Hoosier Cardinal 
Corp., 383 U.S. 696, 699-700 (1966). In all of these in- 


stances, given the precise nature of the legal duty owed 
to the plaintiff and the consequent injury caused by its 
breach, it has always been recognized that monetary relief 
for the benefit of third parties will redress the plaintiff's 
legal injury for the purposes of the standing doctrine. 

Il. The Eighth Circuit in analyzing this case ignored 
the point that the employer's legal duty ran to the union, 
not to the affected employees individually. As just noted, 
that point decides this case. Even on its own terms, how- 
ever, the Eighth Circuit was wrong. The statutory desig- 
nation of unions as representatives authorized to bring a 
WARN Act suit on behalf of affected employees (as well 
as for many other purposes under both this statute and the 
federal labor laws) is one of those designations—recog- 
nized as proper in the federal courts—‘“by statute or the 
settled practice of the courts, [through which] the plaintiff 
is permitted to sue for the benefit of another.” Tyler v. 
Judges of the Court of Registration, 179 U.S. 405, 406 
(1900). Such designations are typically bounded by com- 
mon law or statutory criteria governing the appointment 
of the representative as well as by some duty of the kind 
imposed on fiduciaries to provide for adequacy of repre- 
sentation. The WARN Act designation of unions which 
are representatives of employees under the federal labor 
laws as statutory representatives under the WARN Act 
itself shares those characteristics. That being so, all the 
requirements recognized in this Court's standing jurispru- 
dence are met in this case. 

Against this background, the Eighth Circuit erred in 
treating this case as part of the “associational standing” 
line of cases. Those cases are only one subcategory of 
the larger category of “representational standing” cases. 
Associational standing is concerned with the situation in 
which the association in question has not been recognized 
as a representative for litigation purposes by any statutory 
or common law scheme, but relies instead on the volun-

for authority to represent its members in litigation. It is

only in the associational standing cases that the Court has 
been required to develop its own prudential criteria as to 
when such implicit representation will be recognized. 
When, as here, the authorization to bring suit is made

no application. Rather, in the authorized representative

And the WARN Act statutory scheme shows that the

Introduction 


Last term, this Court summarized the substantive and 
enforcement provisions of the WARN Act as follows: 


With some exceptions and conditions, WARN for- 

bids an employer of 100 or more employees to “order 

a plant closing or mass layoff until the end of a 60- 

day period after the employer serves written notice 

of such an order.” 29 U.S.C. § 2102(a). The em- 

ployer is supposed to notify, among others, “each 

affected employee” or “each representative of the af- 

fected employees.” 29 U.S.C. §2102(a)(1). An 

employer who violates the notice provisions is liable 

for penalties by way of a civil action that may be 

brought “in any district court of the United States 

for any district in which the violation is alleged to have 

occurred, or in which the employer transacts busi- 

ness,” § 2104(a)(5). The class of plaintiffs includes 

aggrieved employees (or their unions, as representa- 

tives), ibid., who may collect “back pay for each day 

of violation,” § 2104(a)(1)(A), “up to a maximum 

of 60 days,” § 2104(a)(1). [North Star Steel Co. 

v. Thomas, ——— U.S. ——, 115 S. Ct. 1927, 1929 
(1995) (emphasis supplied ).] 

As this summary indicates, the WARN Act establishes an 

interrelated scheme of substantive requirements and en- 

forcement provisions specifically providing that unions 

chosen as employee representatives pursuant to the federal 


10 


labor laws governing collective bargaining retain that rep- 
resentative role for WARN Act purposes, including 
for purposes of bringing suits that provide the statutory 
“back pay” remedy for violation of the notice provision. 
See 29 U.S.C. §2101(a)(4) (defining “representative” 
as “an exclusive representative of within the meaning 
{the National Labor Relations Act] or the [Railway 
Labor Act].”) 


The statute itself on its face thus provides a specific 
Congressional answer to the fundamental question raised 
by the standing inquiry in the federal courts, viz., “whether 
the person whose standing is challenged is a proper party 
to request an adjudication of a particular issue.” Flast 
v. Cohen, 392 U.S. 83, 99-100 (1968). The issue pre- 
sented by this case is whether, as the Eighth Circuit held, 
Congress is on some basis precluded by the “case or con- 
troversy” requirement of Article III of the Constitution 
from so providing. 

In North Star Steel, of course, this Court proceeded 
on the understanding that there is no such limitation on 
Congress’ authority to structure a statutory scheme along 
the lines of the WARN Act for delineating rights and 
duties and prescribing remedies and enforcement proce- 
dures. One of the two consolidated cases there was 
brought by a union and sought to recover the applicable 
statutory monetary relief for the employees the union 
represented. Crown Cork & Seal Co. v. United Steel- 
workers of America, Supreme Court No. 94-835. Yet the 
Court suggested no disquiet with the union's standing in 
its opinion even though the standing issue had been 
raised at oral argument (1995 WL 311715, Oral Argu- 
ment, at p. 11) and would, of course, have been jurisdic- 
tional if meritorious. See National Organization for 
Women Inc. v. Scheidler, US. , 114 S. Ct. 
798, 802 (1994). 


That understanding could not have been more correct. 
The instant case, on its face—and, as we will show, at its 
core--is a straightforward “case or controversy” of a 
kind long recognized by the law. 


li 


The plaintiff union, a private party, has, by reason of 
its status, a federal statutory right to a notice from the 
defendant employer, also a private party, when the de- 
fendant formulates an intent to act in a manner that 
affects the plaintiff's interests. The notice is designed to 
enable the plaintiff to respond by initiating a course of 
conduct that protects and advances those interests. The 
complaint here alleges that the defendant formulated the 
requisite intent—and, indeed, acted on it—and that the 
defendant did so without providing the plaintiff the requi- 
site notice, thereby violating the plaintiff's statutory rights 
and causing the plaintiff legal injury. And, the complaint 
seeks the remedy the statute provides to redress such 
violations. 

This case is thus a classic private lawsuit for a specific, 
cencrete violation of a statutorily created right. It is not 
a lawsuit raising a constitutional claim or a challenge to 
government action that diverges from the historic “case 
or controversy” paradigm and by reason of that diver- 
gence tests the limits of our litigation system, or of our 
tripartite government structure, or of our federalism. In- 
deed, in this case, any separation of powers concerns cut 
squarely in the direction of upholding Congress’ legislative 
power to create the entirely rational scheme for enforcing 
a statutory obligation running from one set of private 
parties to another. Rostker v. Goldberg, 453 US. 57, 
64 (1981) (since “Congress is a co-equal branch of gov- 
ernment” the Court “accords ‘great weight to the deci- 
sions of Congress’” when those decisions are constitu- 
tionally challenged ). 


The only complexity here is that the private, statutory 
right in question runs to the plaintiff as—and because 
of—its status as a representative of a discrete group of 
individuals—the defendant’s employees—and that pre- 
cisely because of the representative nature of the notice 
right and the employee protective purpose served by that 
right, the sole statutory remedy for the violation takes the 
form of required monetary payments by the defendant 
to those employees. 


12 


The courts below proceeded on the theory that Article 
III of the Constitution generates two absolute rules: (a) 
that lawsuits brought by A, a rights holder, against B, an 
alleged violator of A’s rights, in which monetary relief on 
a finding of a violation is payable to C (or to C, D, E, 
etc.) are not justiciable “cases or controversies” because 
A lacks standing to prosecute the case; and (b) that Con- 
gress cannot, consistent with the standing requirement, 
designate a representative with authority to litigate on 
behalf of a group of individuals where the relief is mone- 
tary payments to those individuals. 


The Eighth Circuit's conclusion in each of these re- 
gards is contrary to the continuous recognition in num- 
erous legal contexts that such lawsuits through authorized 
representatives are justiciable “cases or controversies” 
and is wholly without foundation in this Court’s “stand- 
ing” jurisprudence. Very simply stated, where Congress 
creates a cause of action and provides a remedial scheme 
for violations like that here, providing for litigation 
through an authorized representative who itself is owed 
duties under the statute, the Congressional enactment 
itself is sufficient to establish the plaintiff's Article III 
standing.

This WARN Act Suit, And To Seek The Monetary 
Remedy To Employees The Act Decrees, By Reason 
Of The Express Statutory Grant To The Union Of 
Litigating Authority To Vindicate The Union’s Ex-

The Representational Nature Of That Notice Right.

B. All the foregoing being so, we turn to an analysis 
of the WARN Act and of its litigation scheme. 


The WARN Act became law in 1988, long after the 
federally-created-and-regulated system governing employee 
workplace representation through a union was in place. 
The WARN Act added to the preexisting law limited 
statutory rights concerning plant closings and mass lay- 
offs, actions which effectively terminate the employees’ 
employment tenure and any collective bargaining agree- 
ment entered into on the employees’ behalf by their 
exclusive representative. In making that addition, Con- 
gress determined that where the workforce has chosen 
an exclusive representative for collective bargaining as 
provided for in the National Labor Relations Act 
(NLRA) or Railway Labor Act (RLA) and a closing 
or mass layoff occurs, the protection of employee inter- 
ests should continue to be entrusted to the statutory rep- 
resentative otherwise responsible for representing the em- 
ployees with regard to their employment. 


(1) Statutory Representative: The WARN Act there- 
fore defines “representative” as the exclusive representa- 
tive chosen by employees pursuant to the National Labor 
Relations Act or Railway Labor Act. 29 U.S.C. § 2101 
(a)(4). 

Such statutory representatives are “designated or se- 
lected [by the employees] in a unit appropriate for such 
purposes . . . for the purposes of collective bargaining in 
respect to rate of pay, wages, hours of employment, or 
other conditions of employment” and, as noted above, 
are the “exclusive” representative for such purposes. 
NLRA § 9(a), 29 U.S.C. § 159(a); see also RLA § 2, 
Fourth, 45 U.S.C. § 152, Fourth. The process for desig-

for by statute and administered by the National Labor

National Mediation Board). NLRA §9, 29 U.S.C. 
§ 159; RLA § 2, Ninth, 45 U.S.C. § 152, Ninth. It is 
therefore an unfair labor practice under the NLRA for 
an employer to recognize as an exclusive representative a

The WARN Act, as we have seen, extends the author-

collective bargaining agreements to enforcing WARN Act

the Railway Labor Act, the WARN Act provides that as 
to certain matters—there, negotiating collective agree-

tive capacity and to the union alone. In both instances, 
because of its exclusive role vis-a-vis the employer, the 
union is in a decidedly better position than the employ-

the employer breaches its legal duties.

ployees or a total of 500 employees). 


Finally, just as many cases brought on a collective 
bargaining agreement will involve common issues of 
contract interpretation affecting the employees as a 
group, it is also the case under the WARN Act that 
almost all of the substantial legal issues likely to arise 
in litigation—for example, whether the plant closing or 
mass layoff was “caused by circumstances not reasonably 
foreseeable at the time that notice would have been re- 
quired” or whether “the employer reasonably believed in

or business” (29 U.S.C. §§$ 2102(b) & (c))—will be 
common to the affected employees as a group.

WARN Act, as in LMRA § 301, it would be “artificial 
and pointless to cut off [the union’s] representative func- 
tions at the courthouse door.” United Federation of

In sum, the WARN Act provision authorizing unions as

Statutory scheme as a whole. The means by which “rep-

The union representatives that may sue to recover mone-

their terms and conditions of employment and of enforc-

are likely to be common to the represented employees as 
a whole, while the monetary sum payable to aggrieved 
employees is set through a purposely simplified calcula-

a single employee.

has never overridden an explicit statutcry or traditional

does not provide appropriate redress to A for the legal 
injury to A’s interests underlying the case of action. In 
large part, we submit, that legal result follows from the in-

breadth of the constitutional authority of legislatures and 
common law courts to create remedial schemes to vin- 
dicate legal rights and to correct legal injuries. 


The choice of a form of relief to “redress” a particular 
injury depends upon practical considerations very much 
the province of legislatures and common law courts. In one 
sense, for example, a damages award never “redresses” a 
plaintiff's injury directly. Compensatory damages do not

restore a severed leg or reverse the pain caused by the 
injury. Nor does such an award necessarily induce the 
particular wrongdoer who caused the injury to fulfill his 
duty of care toward the plaintiff, either retrospectively or 
in the future. Thus the form and nature of monetary 
remedies for a wrong is the product of a societal judgment 
on how to further a set of interests which include those 
of the plaintiff and those of society at large in deterring 
similar conduct in the future. How those interests are to 
be furthered depends on the nature and the purpose of 
the particular right and on the overall legal scheme of 
which that right is but one part. 


That being so, the law has never limited monetary 
remedies for the defendant’s failure to perform a legal 
duty due to the plaintiff to remedies that flow to the 
plaintiff herself, and not to other individuals in whose 
well-being the plaintiff has a legally-recognized interest. 
Rather, courts and legislatures have appreciated that 
where a duty owed to one person is intended at least in 
part to aid that person in forwarding another individual's 
(or individuals’) welfare, the failure of a defendant to 
perform the duty owed is best vindicated by judicial re- 
lief that flows to the beneficiary rather than to the holder 
of the legal right.

The upshot of this analysis is that where there is a 
common law or statutory legal duty owed to the plaintiff 
which will, if carried out, enable the plaintiff to act on 
its legally recognized interest in furthering a third party's 
welfare, and where that duty has been breached, an other- 
wise appropriate plaintiff can pursue relief, monetary, in- 
dividualized, or otherwise, which redounds to the financial! 
benefit of the third party. Put another way, in such situa- 
tions, given the precise nature of the plaintiff's legal right 
and of the concrete legal duty owed by the defendant 
to the plaintiff, and the consequent legal injury caused by 
the defendant's violation, monetary relief for the benefit 
of third parties will redress the plaintiffs legal injury. 
The plaintiff thus has Article III standing to bring and 
prosecute the lawsuit.

Hunt crystalizes the associational standing inquiry 
through a three-part judicially-created standard to fill the 
resulting void: 


[A]n association has standing to bring suit on 
behalf of its members when: (a) its members would 
otherwise have standing to sue in their own right; 
(b) the interests it seeks to protect are germane to 
the organization’s purpose; and (c) neither the claim 
asserted nor the relief requested require the partici- 
uation of individual members in the lawsuit. [432 
U.S. at 343.] 


The Eighth Circuit's conclusion that the union here 
lacks standing, rested on the third prong of the Hunt 
standard. Because individual monetary damages were 
being sought, the court below concluded, “the relief 
requested requires the participation of individual members 
in the lawsuit.” Hunt, 432 U.S. at 343, cited at Pet. 
App. 9a." 


The first Hunt requirement—which assures that there 
is the requisite concrete injury to the beneficiary, so 
that the case is one in which the judicial power can be 
exercised with sufficient focus and specificity-—is, to be 
sure, part of the constitutional standing inquiry, and 
applies, as we have seen, in all representational cases. 
See pp. 39-40, supra. The second two associational 
standing criteria, however, have no independent role 
where “by statute or the settled practice of the courts, 
the plaintiff is permitted to sue for the benefit of another.” 
Tyler v. Judges of the Court of Registration, supra, 179 
U.S. at 406.

is appropriate to regard the organization as a surrogate
