## Governing text and current law

The Seventh Amendment provides: “In Suits at common law, where the value in controversy shall exceed twenty dollars, the right of trial by jury shall be preserved, and no fact tried by a jury shall be otherwise re-examined in any Court of the United States, than according to the rules of the common law.” Both clauses matter: initial allocation and later reexamination are distinct.

Section 112's particular-and-distinct-claim requirement and written description are identified in the lower reasoning and the respondent appendix excerpt. Their public-notice function supports a judicial-construction argument but does not by itself resolve every jury question. The presence of means-plus-function words does not authorize a universal §112 paragraph 6 holding; that independent statutory issue is not the granted question. Sections 281/284 explain the infringement action and damages requested, not a particular constitutional allocation.

**Wooddell**, Dec. 4, 1991, canonical Federal Courts—Civil jury right: a legal compensatory claim carries jury protection notwithstanding accompanying equitable requests; historical analogy and, more importantly, the legal/equitable remedy inquiry from Terry apply. The holding was joined by Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, and Souter among all eight participating Justices. It does not mean judges cannot interpret the governing legal instrument in a jury action.

**Jury determination of criminal materiality**, Standards and Tests, present operation effective June 19, 1995, Gaudin: the judge states statutory meaning and the legal standard; the criminal jury determines the materiality element beyond reasonable doubt. Labeling application a mixed question cannot remove an element. All eight current non-Stone Justices joined Scalia's Court opinion. Its Fifth/Sixth Amendment allocation and criminal burden do not transfer wholesale to claim interpretation; its warning against conclusory law/fact labels and preservation of judicial interpretation/sufficiency functions remain relevant.

**Thompson v. Keohane**, Nov. 29, 1995, current neutral/Public Projection: ultimate Miranda custody receives independent federal review while supported historical facts and credibility receive the actual §2254(d) protection. O'Connor's Court opinion, joined by Stevens, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Ginsburg and Breyer, uses the function of the issue and the decisionmakers' capacities; it does not make every ultimate inference legal. Thomas's public dissent treats the reasonable-person inference there as factual, rejects uniformity alone as dispositive, and distinguishes the legal standard from its fact-specific application. No habeas presumption or burden becomes patent law by analogy.

Safe prior sources independently inspected: Tull, 481 U.S. 412, particularly 417–427 and Scalia's separate writing; Terry, 494 U.S. 558, particularly 564–565, the Court's remedy discussion, Stevens's concurrence, and Kennedy's dissent. Tull protects the essence of jury trial while allowing a different allocation of a statutory civil penalty amount; Scalia, with Stevens, contests that separation. Terry's Court holdings protect legal compensation; its division concerns how to perform historical analogy. The source text expressly limits some practical-compatibility considerations to administrative/specialized equity settings. Neither decision permits removing a historically protected jury function simply because patent cases are complex.

Strong objection: preserving factfinding does not allow extrinsic testimony to contradict an unambiguous public claim or make patent-law opinions into evidence.
