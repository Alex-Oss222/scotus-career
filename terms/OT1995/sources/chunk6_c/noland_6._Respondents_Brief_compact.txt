STATEMENT OF THE CASE 
1. First Truck Lines, Inc. (“FTL”) filed its chapter 11 
petition on April 10, 1986 (Pet. App. 2a). FTL’s chapter 11 plan 
was confirmed by order entered June 2, 1987 (Resp. App. 3a). 
The nonpecuniary loss tax penalty claims of the Internal 
Revenue Service (“IRS”) at issue are for the tax period ended 
December 31, 1987 and for the post-confirmation tax quarters 
ended September 30, 1987, March 31, 1988, and June 30, 1988, 
respectively (Resp. App. 7a). The case was converted to chapter 
7 on August 1, 1988, and Thomas R. Noland was appointed 
Trustee for FTL (Pet. App. 2a). Petitioner and the Trustee applied 
to the bankruptcy court for a determination of the priority, if any, 
to be accorded to the nonpecuniary loss tax penalty claims of the 
IRS (Resp. App. 11a, 12a).

The court of appeals followed the analysis of the bankruptcy

principles of equitable subordination included Comstock, supra, 
Pepper, supra, and Taylor, supra, as well as one appellate case, 
In re Stirling Homex Corp., 579 F.2d 206 (2d Cir. 1978), cert.

former Bankruptcy Act, 11 U.S.C. § 93(j) (repealed), the absence 
of case law equitably subordinating tax penalty claims prior to 
the enactment of the Bankruptcy Code was because 
nonpecuniary loss tax penalty claims were not allowed by law

broadly to disallow both secured and unsecured claims for tax 
penalties against the estate of the bankrupt (Pet. App. 12a-13a, 
citing Simonson v. Granquist, 369 U.S. 38 (1962)). Finally, the 
6

nonpecuniary loss tax penalty claims (Pet. App. 13a-14a, citing 
Burden v. United States, 917 F.2d 115 (3d Cir. 1990); In re Virtual 
Network Services Corp., 902 F.2d 1246 (7th Cir. 1990); and 
Schultz Broadway Inn v. United States, 912 F.2d 230 (8th 
Cir. 1990)).

Congress intended the punitive nonpecuniary loss tax penalties 
to be subject to equitable subordination pursuant to 11 U.S.C. 
§ 510(c) (Pet. App. 14a-17a).

INTRODUCTION AND 
SUMMARY OF ARGUMENT 
The court of appeals held that a postpetition punitive 
nonpecuniary loss tax penalty claim may on acase by case basis 
be subordinated pursuant to 11 U.S.C. § 510(c)(1) to the claims 
of parties who gave value to the debtor. This holding is consistent 
with the plain meaning of 11 U.S.C. § 510(c)(1), is consistent 
with longstanding bankruptcy policy that provides similar 
treatment to similar claims, and is consistent with providing 
superpriority pursuant to 11 U.S.C. § 364(c) over any or all 
administrative expense claims to parties who support a trustee or 
debtor in possession in the operation of a business during a 
chapter 11 case. 
Tax ties are intended to punish persons who have been 
guilty pc default or wrong; the enforcement of penalties 
against bankruptcy estates is disfavored because it would punish 
entirely innocent creditors rather than the delinquent taxpayers. 
Simonson v. Granquist, 369 U.S. 38, 40-41 (1962). Wherees 
compensatory pecuniary loss tax penalty claims are treated like 
9 
other pecuniary loss claims, including compensatory pecuniary 
loss tax claims, the Bankruptcy Code subordinates disfavored 
punitive nonpecuniary loss tax penalty claims. See 11 U.S.C. 
§ 507(a)(7)(G);' 11 U.S.C. § 726(a)(4). It is also clear from the 
remainder of the statutory scheme that nonpecuniary loss tax 
penalty claims are disfavored and may be treated differently 
from pecuniary loss claims. 
The lower courts have held that the postpetition 
nonpecuniary loss tax penalty claims in this case are 
administrative expenses under 11 U.S.C. § 503(b)(1)(C). The 
decisions of other courts which determine the allowance of 
postpetition claims as administrative expense claims but do not 
address whether those allowed claims can be subordinated, and 
the prior decisions of this Court permitting the subordination of 
the pecuniary loss claims of dominant shareholders who 
engaged in misconduct or breached their fiduciary duties to other 
creditors, are not controlling in this case whose only issue is the 
subordination of allowed postpetition punitive nonpecuniary 
loss tax penalty claims. 
Nonpecuniary loss tax penalty claims, by their very nature, 
are »ysceptible to subordination pursuant to 11 U.S.C. 
§ 510(c)(1) without a finding of misconduct on the part of the 
claimant. It is also evident from the legislative history that the 
development of the principles of equitable subordination is left 
to the courts. It would be improper to carve out a broad exception 
to 11 U.S.C. § $10(c) for tax penalties when the statute on its face

Although it is clear from the plain meaning of 11 U.S.C. 
§ 726(a) that the principles of equitable subordination may be 
applied to any claim in a chapter 7 case, the term “principles of 
equitable subordination” is not defined in the Bankruptcy Code. 
Petitioner requests that this Court create an exception to exclude 
all tax penalties from the operation of 11 U.S.C. § 510(c)(1) 
unless there is inequitable conduct by the IRS. Such an exception

prevent injustice or unfairness in the administration of a 
bankruptcy estate suggest that misconduct is not required if there 
is either injustice or unfairness as contemplated by the Taylor or 
Pepper decisions. 
The third decision of this Court addressing equitable 
subordination, Comstock v. Group of Institutional Investors, 335 
U.S.211 (1948), also arose under the former Bankruptcy Act and 
applied the teachings of Taylor and Pepper to another dominant 
shareholder situation. Once again, a pecuniary loss claim was 
involved. Although there was domination of the subsidiary, 
Justice Jackson, speaking for the five Justice majority, delivered 
the opinion of the Court and stated that “[i}t is not mere existence 
of an opportunity to do wrong that brings the rule [equitable 
subordination] into play; it is the unconscionable use of the 
opportunity afforded by the domination to advantage itself at the 
injury of the subsidiary that deprives the wrongdoer of the fruits 
of his wrong.” 335 U.S. at 229. Subordination was therefore 
denied. In criticizing the majority decision, the four dissenting 
Justices pointed out that “[t]he equities which form the Deep 
Rock doctrine relate not alone to matters of bad faith. They are 
also concerned with the essential fairness and propriety of 
transactions from an objective standpoint. Pepper v. Litton, [308 
U.S. at) 306.” Comstock, 335 U.S. at 237-38 (Murphy, J., 
dissenting). 
Taylor, Pepper, and Comstock, each supra, share the 
common thread that subordination may be proper against 
dominant shareholders who have suffered pecuniary losses but

engage in misconduct or breach their fiduciary duties to other 
creditors. The application of the principles of equitable 
subordination to nonpecuniary loss tax penalty claims does not 
require any departure from that general rule because the 
nonpecuniary loss tax penalty claims are punitive rather than 
compensatory, and are thus by their very nature disfavored and 
susceptible to subordination.’ Such a result is consistent with 
both the former and current bankruptcy laws, with prior 
decisions of this Court, and with the legislative history to 11 
U.S.C. § 510.
