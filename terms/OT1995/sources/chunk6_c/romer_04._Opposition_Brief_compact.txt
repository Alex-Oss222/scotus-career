QUESTION PRESENTED 
Whether an initiated state constitutional amendment 
that forbids the state and all local governments from 
adopting or enforcing any law or policy protecting les- 
bians, gay men and bisexuals from discrimination vio- 
lates the Equal Protection Clause of the Fourteenth 
Amendment because it burdens the fundamental right of 
lesbians, gay men and bisexuals to participate in the 
political process and it fails rationally to advance a legiti- 
mate governmental purpose.

STATEMENT OF THE CASE 
| On November 3, 1992, a majority of Colorado voters 
| approved a state constitutional amendment (“Amend- 
ment 2”) that provides: 
| Neither the State of Colorado, through any of its 
| branches or departments, nor any of its agen- 
| cies, political subdivisions, municipalities or 
| school districts, shall enact, adopt or enforce 
| any statute, regulation, ordinance or policy 
| whereby homosexual, lesbian or bisexual orien- 
tation, conduct, practices or relationships shall 
constitute or otherwise be the basis of, or entitle 
any person or class of persons to have or claim 
any minority status, quota preferences, pro- 
tected status or claim of discrimination. This 
Section of the Constitution shall be self-execut- 
ing. 
The amendment deprives all branches and departments 
of state government (the legislative, executive, and judi- 
| cial), state agencies (including state universities and col- 
leges), political subdivisions of the state (including 
counties), municipalities and school districts of the power 
to remedy “any claim of discrimination” based on “homo- 
sexual, lesbian or bisexual orientation.” Thus, as

variety of public and private contexts and immunize such 
discrimination from regulation or redress at any level of 
government.!

anti-discrimination laws and policies? and to prohibit the

participation within the meaning of Hunter. Evans is con- 
sistent with this Court's precedents and is not worthy of 
certiorari review under Sup. Ct. R. 10.1(c).

The state, however, contends that the practical effect

Apparently, the state maintains that any legal classifica-

This contention is wrong. If the state were to enact 
new laws putting other, special burdens on lesbians, gay

legislation intended to benefit or protect gay, lesbian or

See Sup. Ct. R. 10.1(a)-(b).

COURT PRESENTS NO THREAT TO THE RIGHT 
OF DEMOCRATIC SELF-GOVERNANCE. 
The state contends that Evans will disrupt democratic 
decision-making and “inject| | the judiciary into a broad 
range of issues heretofore reserved for state legislatures 
or to Congress.” Pet. at 16. This contentior \ also lacks 
review. 
At the outset of its argument, the state correctly notes 
that every law will affect some persons more than others 
and that every law affects some identifiable group of 
persons - namely, the group of persons defined by the 
“ ia ah _ die sisi 
16 
statute. For instance, a state-wide speed limit for motor 
vehicles may be described as singling out all drivers who 
exceed the speed limit. However, the holding of Evans is 
limited to cases in which the law “fences out” a group 
that can be identified independently of the law being 
challenged. The limit on Evans’ holding underscores the 
essential principle that the equal protection clause of the 
Fourteenth Amendment forbids the exclusion of persons 
from the political process, not the preemption of issues. 
~ 
This principle strikes the proper balance between the 
right of the majority to engage in democratic self-govern- 
ment and the right of all persons to participate in the 
political process. Persons are not excluded from political 
participation simply because they fail to secure a majority 
of votes on a single issue: political loss is a necessary part 
ci the political process, in which different factions con- 
tend for different government measures, sometimes win- 
ning and sometimes losing, but are never excluded totally 
from the process of presenting proposals for consider- 
ation by democratic institutions. Hunter, 393 U.S. at 
393-94. 
All five of the constitutional provisions cited by the 
state in support of its contention, pet. at 16-19, preempt 
issues; they do not exclude any independently identifiable 
group. Indeed, the state’s five examples indicate that 
Amendment 2 is unique in its total exclusion of one 
constituency from all political fora in Colorado. 
For instance, Colorado’s constitutional term limit 
excludes the incumbents of all factions and parties 
equally: it fences out no particular group, nor does it 
17 
target any particular constituency. To use Justice Harlan’s 
words in Hunter, a limit on terms 
obviously does not have the purpose of protect- 
ing one group to the detriment of all others. It 
will sometimes operate in favor of one faction; 
sometimes in favor of another. 
393 U.S. at 393-94. 
As the state notes, term limits prevent “supporters of 
particular incumbents,” pet. at 18, n.12, from re-electing 
their preferred candidates. However, this group - “sup- 
porters of particular incumbents” — is not independently 
identifiable: like supporters of particular types of bonded 
indebtedness, one “can discern no independently identi- 
fiable group” that favors incumbency as such. Gordon, 403 
U.S. at 5. 
Of course, a term limit has a disparate impact on the 
aggregate of all persons who wish to re-elect incumbents. 
But these persons are not identifiable independent of 
their desire to accomplish what the challenged provision 
forbids. The group, therefore, is not “independently iden- 
tifiable” — that is, identifiable as a group independent of 
its members’ opposition to term limits. 
A similar analysis applies to all of the other state 
constitutional provisions cited by the state. In each case, 
the “group” affected is defined by nothing more than its 
members’ support for legislation prohibited by the consti- 
tutional provision. See Colo. Const. art. V, § 50 (restric- 
tions on state funding of abortions); art. XIV, § 3 and art. 
XX, § 1 (restrictions on annexation by Denver); art. XVIII, 
§ 9 (restrictions on locations where gambling is legal); art. 
XX, § 7 (limitation on structure of Denver school district). 
18 
In no case does the cited provision deliberately single out 
a specific constituency having an identity that transcends 
a narrow political controversy and bar normal political 
institutions from considering any laws or policies 
intended to benefit that constituency. These provisions 
burden a group that is defined only by support of the 
forbidden program or law; they do not exclude an inde- 
pendently identifiable group from participation in the 
political process. 
By contrast, Amendment 2 singles out in its text a 
group of persons — “homosexuals, lesbians, and bisex- 
uals” — who comprise a group independent of any shared 
interest in enacting what Amendment 2 forbids. Lesbians, 
gay men, and bisexuals are identifiable as a group by 
their sexual orientation, a trait that is unrelated to the 
existence of anti-discrimination laws or Amendment 2. 
See testimony and exhibits addressing the genetic and 
social origins of sexual orientation, 13:242-338, 371-427; 
14:468-91. 
It would be difficult to imagine a more deliberate 
exclusion of an independently identifiable group’s inter- 
ests from consideration in the political process. By the 
Same token, however, Evans provides no basis for the 
extension of its principles to laws other than those that 
are similar to Amendment 2. 
Prohibiting legislative, judicial, or executive protec- 
tion of a group’s civil rights uniquely burdens the 
group’s participation in the political process. Such a dras- 
tic curtailment of effective political participation prevents 
a constituency from using the democratic process to pro- 
tect itself from majoritarian excesses.

The decision in Evans reflects a continuing theme in 
this Court’s jurisprudence: courts should not be reluctant 
to overturn the will of the people if its expression is 
unconstitutional. See, e.g., Hunter, 393 U.S. 385; Reitman v. 
Mulkey, 387 U.S. 369 (1967); Lucas v. Colorado Gen’l Assem-

(1963); see Colo. Const. art. II, § 2 (the people of Colorado 
may “alter and abolish their constitution and form of 
government whenever they may deem it necessary to 
their safety and happiness, provided, such change be not 
repugnant to the constitution of the United States”).

Colorado Supreme Court’s decision by considering the

plaintiffs’ other equal protection theory: that Amendment 
2 does not rationally advance a legitimate governmental 
purpose. See Yee v. Escondido, 503 U.S. __, 118 L.Ed.2d 
153, 169-70 (1992); Robert L. Stern, Eugene Gressman, 
Stephen M. Shapiro & Kenneth S. Geller, Supreme Court

respondent has the right to urge any argument in support

The district court did not rule on the plaintiffs’ argument 
that Amendment 2 fails to rationally advance any legiti- 
mate governmental purpose. The plaintiffs, however, pre-

ther the compelling interests advanced by the state nor 
any legitimate purpose. 
At a minimum, equal protection requires that distinc- 
tions drawn by the government be “rationally related to a 
legitimate governmental interest.” Heller v. Doe, 509 U.S.

athy arising out of hostility or groundless stereotypes is 
not a legitimate purpose justifying a law under the equal 
protection clause. Palmore v. Sidoti, 466 U.S. 429, 434

413 U.S. 528, 534 (1973). If this Court grants certiorari 
review of the decision of the Colorado Supreme Court, it
