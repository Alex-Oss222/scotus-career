STATEMENT OF CASE 


The issue in this case is whether a state constitutional 
amendment that forbids the state government and all 
local governments from adopting or enforcing any law or 
policy protecting gay people from discrimination on the 
basis of their sexual orientation violates the Equal Protec- 
tion Clause of the Fourteenth Amendment. Amendment 
2, adopted by Colorado voters in 1992, provides, 


Neither the State of Colorado, through any of its 
branches or departments, nor any of its agen- 
cies, political subdivisions, municipalities or 
school districts, shall enact, adopt or enforce 
any statute, regulation, ordinance or policy 
whereby homosexual, lesbian or bisexual orien- 
tation, conduct, practices or relationships shall 
constitute or otherwise be the basis of, or entitle 
any person or class of persons to have or claim 
any minority status, quota preferences, pro- 
tected status or claim of discrimination. This 
Section of the Constitution shall be in all 
respects self-executing.

B. Legislative History of Amendment 2. 


Dissatisfied with the give and take of the normal politi- 
cal process affecting anti-discrimination laws and policies, 
Colorado for Family Values (“CFV”) proposed Amendment 2 
as a ballot initiative amending the state constitution. Amend- 
ment 2 differed fundamentally from previous efforts to 
repeal anti-discrimination measures: it altered the jurisdic- 
tion of all governmental entities in Colorado by barring all 
branches and all levels of government from protecting gay 
people from any form of public or private discrimination in 
any context. In CFV’s words, the purpose of Amendment 2 
was to “close the lid on the entire issue of [civil rights for gay 
people] here in Colorado.” R.15:853, J.A.182. As the Colorado 
Supreme Court later determined, Amendment 2 “sought to 
deny” the right of gay people “to participate equally in the 
eps process.” 882 P.2d at 1349, Pet. App. B-22, Pet. App. 


In the ensuing ballot campaign, CFV relied on two 
arguments to persuade voters to enact Amendment 2. 
First, CFV portrayed all gay men and lesbians as “mor- 
ally depraved” persons who undermine “traditional fam- 
ily values and structures.” Pl.Ex. 11 at 32-33, R.6:55; P1.Ex. 
12 at 55, R.6:56. CFV’s campaign brochure asserted that 
gay people sexually molest children as part of “homosex- 
uals’ lifestyle.” Df.Ex. W (lodged concurrently with Joint 
Appendix) at 2, R.8:117. CFV’s campaign materials sent to 
pastors also emphasized Biblical passages as justification 
for discrimination against gay people. R.8:85. CFV 
asserted that “gay behavior” is what the Bible calls “sin,” 
R.16:888, P1.Ex. 12 at 113, and that homosexuality is “an 
abomination” to “the authority of God.” R.16:1062-63. 


Second, CFV argued that laws protecting gay people 
from discrimination provided them with “special rights” 


8 


not available to other Colorado residents. As an officer of 
CFV later conceded, the phrase “special rights” was a 
“meaningless term” designed as a campaign slogan 
rather than an accurate description of Amendment 2’s 
legal effects. R.16:879, J.A.102.8 


C. Scope and Meaning of Amendment 2. 


Amendment 2’s plain language prohibits “any 

. Claim of discrimination” made pursuant to “any 
statute, regulation, ordinance or policy” whenever the 
discrimination for which the claimant seeks relief is based 
on “homosexual, lesbian, or bisexual orientation, con- 
duct, practices, or relationships.”? This prohibition on 
remedies for discrimination is expressly unlimited. The 
amendment bars state and local government from prohib- 
iting discrimination of any kind and in any context if it is 
based on a person’s gay, lesbian or bisexual orientation — 
including government employment, the sale of insurance, 
or provision of governmental services like police protec- 
tion, where government expressly prohibits arbitrary dis- 
crimination directed against other groups.

1. The Fourteenth Amendment Prohibits 
States from Gerrymandering Governmental 
Structure for the Purpose of Depriving

As the Colorado Supreme Court noted, this Court has 
consistently applied a heightened standard of review to 
state laws when such laws deprive members of particular 
groups of equal participation in the day-to-day political 
process. For instance, when state laws disenfranchise a 
particular group, the Court applies strict judicial scrutiny. 
Kramer v. Union Free School Dist., 395 U.S. 621, 627 (1969); 
Cipriano v. City of Houma, 395 U.S. 701 (1969). Similarly, 
the Court applies strict scrutiny to state laws that place 
voters in unequally populated districts and prevent them 
from casting equally weighted votes. Reynolds v. Sims, 377 
U.S. 533 (1964). Likewise, the Court applies such scrutiny 
to laws that gerrymander the boundaries of even equi- 
populous districts to ensure the defeat of a political 
group. Davis v. Bandemer, 478 U.S. 109 (1986); see also

it prevents only one such group from being the benefici-

regard to the group protected by the action, then no one 
is denied an equal opportunity to participate in the politi-

obtaining the excluded category of government action. 


Of course, even structural provisions that impartially

warrants heightened scrutiny to effectuate the guarantees 
of the Equal Protection Clause.

c. Heightened Scrutiny is Required Only

The State contends that the Fourteenth Amendment 
cannot prohibit state constitutional provisions that target 
the interests of particular groups because any such prohi- 
bition will have “sweeping” and “devastating” conse- 
quences. State Br. 34. According to the State, the principle 
of impartiality in government structure recognized in the 
decision below would require that heightened judicial 
scrutiny be applied to all “routine federal and state legis-

The State and amici exaggerate the reach of the deci- 
sion below because they ignore the fundamental limit on 
the decision contained in the jurisprudence on which it is 
based. As explained in Gordon and in Justice Harlan’s 
concurrence in Hunter, the Fourteenth Amendment bars 
only discriminatory laws that target particular groups. A 
law does not deprive anyone of equal protection unless it 
treats persons unequally, by forbidding laws from being 
enacted or applied for the benefit of some groups while 
allowing the same laws to be enacted and applied for the 
benefit of other groups. Washington v. Seattle School Dis- 
trict, 458 U.S. at 474 n. 17, 480 n. 23. Therefore, if constitu- 
tional provisions are “grounded in neutral principles” 
and exclude issues across the board in an impartial man- 
ner, then such provisions are unaffected by the Four- 
teenth Amendment. 


Such a doctrine concerning the gerrymandering of 
governmental jurisdiction is analogous to this Court's 
application of the Fourteenth Amendment to the gerry- 
mandering of electoral districts or to ballot access regula- 
tions. Every arrangement of electoral districts and every 
ballot access regulation_burdens some group of voters 
and candidates who would have been more successful

under a different scheme. However. such arrangements of 
electoral districts do not violate the Fourteenth Amend- 
ment unless they single out some political group in a 
discriminatory manner. See Johnson v. De Grandy, 114 S.Ct. 
2647, 2658 (1994) (districting plan does not dilute minor- 
ity’s vote if it provides “equal political opportunity” even 
if minority voters would be more successful under a 
different scheme); Davis v. Bandemer, 478 U.S. at 131; 
Burdick v. Takushi, 504 U.S. 428, 434 (1992) (heightened 
scrutiny does not apply if “a state election law imposes 
only ‘reasonable, nondiscriminatory restrictions’ upon the 
First and Fourteenth Amendment rights of voters”) 
(emphasis added). 


In determining whether a particular state law dis- 
criminates against a particular group in its arrangement 
of the political process, it is necessary to examine both 
the purpose and the effect of the provision. Davis v. 
Bandemer, 478 U.S. at 127. If the provision simply has a 
disparate impact on the efforts of one political group and 
does not facially or deliberately burden that group, then 
it is not unconstitutionally discriminatory under the 
Fourteenth Amendment. See, e.g., Davis v. Bandemer, 478 
U.S. at 127, 134, 142; City of Mobile v. Bolden, 446 U.S. 55 
~. (1980); Washington v. Davis, 426 U.S. 229 (1976); Hunter, 
393 U.S. at 394-95 (Harlan, J., concurring). Likewise, 
unless the discriminatory effects of a challenged law are 
substantial, it cannot be said to unconstitutionally burden 
a particular group. See, e.g., Salyer Land Co. v. Tulare Lake 
Basin Water Storage Dist., 410 U.S. 719, 728 (1973) (where 
water district has “disproportionate effect on land- 
owners, state can deny non-landowners ballot for dis- 
trict’s board”); Ball v. James, 451 U.S. 355, 371 (1981) 
(same); Davis v. Bandemer, 478 U.S. at 132 (to violate the 
Fourteenth Amendment, gerrymandering must “consis- 
tently degrade . . . a group of voters’ influence over the 
political process as a whole”).

A. If the Breadth and Selectivity of the Burdens 
Imposed by a Classification Can Only Be 
Explained by Antipathy Toward a Particular 
Group, Then the Classification Does Not Ratio- 
nally Serve Any Legitimate Purpose. 


At a minimum, equal protection requires that distinc- 
tions drawn by the government be “rationally related to a 
legitimate governmental interest.” United States Depart- 
ment of Agriculture v. Moreno, 413 U.S. 528, 533 (1973); see 
also City of Cleburne v. Cleburne Living Center, 473 U.S. 432, 
446 (1985). Under this standard of equal protection 
review, the Court engages in a two-part inquiry: the 
Court asks first whether a classification is intended to 
serve a legitimate state purpose, and second, whether the 
classification is rationally related to that purpose. 


The State argues in its brief that Amendment 2 serves 
several legitimate purposes. None of those asserted pur- 
poses is rationally advanced by Amendment 2, and none 
of them can mask its true purpose: to harm a politically 
unpopular group. Before canvassing the State’s asserted 
purposes, however, it is important to review two general 
principles for assessing their validity. 

First, in assessing whether a purpose is legitimate, 
this Court consistently has held that antipathy arising out 
of hostility or groundless stereotypes is not a legitimate 
purpose justifying a law under the Equal Protection 
Clause. Accordingly, the Court presumes that a state law 
is valid only “absent some reason to infer antipathy.” 
F.C.C. v. Beach Communications, 113 S. Ct. 2096, 2101 (1993) 
(quoting Vance v. Bradley, 440 U.S. 93, 97 (1979)). As this 
Court stated in Palmore v. Sidoti, 466 U.S. 429, 433 (1984), 
“[p]rivate biases may be outside the reach of the law, but 
the law cannot, directly or indirectly, give them effect” 
(emphasis added). This Court repeatedly has asserted 
that antipathy, by itself, is not a legitimate purpose even 
for non-racial discrimination. See, e.g., Cleburne, 473 U.S. 
at 447-48; Moreno, 413 U.S. at 534 (“a bare . . . desire to 


37 


harm a politically unpopular group cannot constitute a 
legitimate government interest”) (emphasis in original). 

If bare antipathy toward a group could justify dis- 
crimination against that group, then the requirements of 
the Equal Protection Clause would be meaningless. By 
definition, every classification serves the purpose of dis- 
criminating against the group that the classification bur- 
dens. Therefore, if the “rational basis” test has any 
content whatsoever, it cannot permit discrimination 
solely for discrimination’s sake. 


In determining whether a classification illegitimately 
imposes discrimination against the members of a group 
as an end itself, one important consideration is the 
breadth of the discrimination imposed.”6 If the legal clas- 
sification insulates from legal remedy public or private 
discrimination against some group without limitation or 
qualification, then it is impossible to attribute any pur- 
pose to the classification apart from a generalized desire 
to discriminate. By its terms, such indiscriminate imposi- 
tion of burdens reveals no attempt to draw a distinction 
in pursuit of some specific policy: rather, such a law 
declares a characteristic to be sufficient justification for 
discrimination regardless of any specific purpose. Such 
discrimination for the sake of discrimination is illegiti- 
mate. See Citizens for Responsible Behavior v. Superior Court, 
1 Cal. App. 4th 1013, 1027-33, 2 Cal. Rptr. 2d 648, 656-60 
(Cal. App. 1991). 

Second, it is not enough that a classification has an 
incidental tendency marginally to advance a legitimate 
purpose; rather, there must be some plausible justifica- 
tion for the particular classification selected and some 
reasonable expectation that the classification will in prac- 
tice achieve the purpose. See Heller v. Doe, 113 S. Ct. 2637, 
2643 (1993) (to be rational, a classification “must find 
some footing in the realities of the subject addressed”);

C. The Selectivity, Severity and Legislative His- 
tory of Amendment 2 Demonstrate that Its Bur- 
dens on Gay People Can Only Be Attributable 
to Antipathy, an Illegitimate State Purpose. 


The issue in this case is not whether it might be 
rational to discriminate on the basis of sexual orientation 
in some specific context for some specific legitimate pur- 
pose. The Court can and should leave for another day the 
question of whether some legitimate purpose can justify 
such limited discrimination. Rather, the question in this 
case is whether there is a rational basis for constitu- 
tionally insulating every type of public and private dis- 
crimination against gay people in every context from any 
remedy, regardless of the circumstances or purposes of 
such discrimination. Amendment 2 provides blanket 
immunity for discrimination based on gay orientation. 
Therefore, in order to uphold Amendment 2, the Court 
would have to find that such orientation is always a 
rational basis for any sort of discrimination. 


48 


Such a holding, like Amendment 2 itself, would be 
unprecedented: no classification, however rational in 
some circumstance, is always a rational basis for discrimi- 
nation. Rather, the foundation of the “rational basis” test 
is that law-makers and enforcers ought to possess the 
flexibility to determine the specific circumstances under 
which some non-suspect classification might be pru- 
dently employed. Cleburne, 473 U.S. at 445. Amendment 2 
rejects all such flexible and nuanced approaches and 
declares that gay orientation is always, in effect, a ratio- 
nal basis for discrimination. Such a sweeping law cannot 
be explained by any specific interest in pursuing some 
specific policy. Rather, a blanket prohibition on protection 
in every context, even unforeseen, indicates only a pur- 
pose to engage in discrimination for discrimination’s 
sake. As explained above, such a purpose is not legiti- 
mate under the Equal Protection Clause.
