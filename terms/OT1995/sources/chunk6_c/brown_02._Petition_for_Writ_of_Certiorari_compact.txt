STATEMENT OF THE CASE 
The Worker Adjustment and Retraining Notification 
Act (WARN), 29 U.S.C. §§ 2101-09, provides that cov- 
ered employers may not order a “plant closing or mass 
layoff,” as statutorily defined (29 U.S.C. § 2101(a)(2) 
& (3)), without providing notice sixty days beforehand 
“to each representative of the affected employees at the 
4 
time of the notice or, if there is no such representative 
at that time, to each affected employee.” 29 U.S.C. 
§ 2102(a) (emphasis supplied). “Representative” is de- 
fined in the statute as “an exclusive representative of em- 
ployees within the meaning of section 159(a) or 158(f) 
of [the National Labor Relations Act (NLRA)].. .” 
WARN goes on to provide that: (a) if an employer 
“orders a plant closing or mass layoff in violation of sec- 
tion 2102,” the employer is liable “to each aggrieved em- 
ployee who suffers an employment loss” for back pay 
and benefits, (29 U.S.C. §2104(a)(1)), and (b) “a 
person seeking to enforce such liability, including a rep- 
resentative of employees . . . aggrieved under paragraph 
(1)... may sue” in district court (29 U.S.C. § 2104 
(a)(S) (emphasis supplied) ). 
On January 17, 1992, Brown Group, Inc. d/b/a Brown 
Shoe Co. (“Brown Shoe”) mailed a written notice to 
William T. Treece of the United Food and Commercial 
Workers International Union (“International”), which 
announced that Brown Shoe’s Dixon, Missouri plant 
would permanently close and 277 employees would be 
discharged as a result of the closing. Brown Shoe stated 
in the letter that the company would begin discharging 
employees on March 20, 1992. Brown Shoe already had 
begun discharging employees before sending the January 
17 notice, discharged additional employees on January 
20, 1992, and continued to discharge employees during 
February and March, 1992. 
On June 16, 1992, United Food and Commercial 
Workers Union Local 751 (“Local 751” or the “Union”), 
petitioner herein, filed a complaint in the United States 
District Court for the Eastern District of Missouri, alleg- 
ing that the Union was the “exclusive representative of the 
[Dixon] employees” on January 17, 1992 and at all other 
times material to the complaint as defined in WARN, 29 
U.S.C. § 2101(a)(4). Jurisdiction was alleged under 28 
U.S.C. § 1331 and 29 U.S.C. § 2104(a). A copy of the 
o* 
é 
5 
then current collective bargaining agreement between 
Local 751 and Brown Shoe was attached to the com- 
plaint. The Union also alleged that it, not William 
Treece or the International, had a right to notice of the 
Dixon closing sixty days before the closing was ordered 
in accordance with 29 U.S.C. § 2102(a)(1), and that 
Brown Shoe had in any event failed to continue the em- 
ployment of most of the represented employees for a sixty- 
day period following the notice. An appendix to the 
complaint identified those Dixon employees who are ag- 
grieved employees under § 2104(a). 
The complaint demanded that: (1) Brown Shoe pay 
each aggrieved employee back pay and fringe benefits for 
each day of employnient loss the employee suffered prior 
to the expiration of the sixty-day notice period; (2) Local 
751 recover from Brown Shoe the cost of this action, 
including reasonable attorneys’ fees; and (3) Local 751 
recover such other and further relief on its behalf and 
that of the aggrieved employees as may be proper. 
On April 12, 1993, the District Court issued a memo- 
randum and order granting Brown Shoe’s motion to dis- 
miss on the ground that the Union lacked standing to sue 
for damages for the represented employees. Local 751 
thereupon filed a motion to alter or amend judgment ask- 
ing leave to file an amended complaint seeking declaratory 
relief and adding affected individuals as parties. That 
motion was denied by the District Court on March 7, 
1994. 
On appeal, the Eighth Circuit affirmed the District 
Court’s standing ruling. The Court of Appeals recognized 
that the UFCW is a statutory “representative” of the 
aggrieved employees. But the Court held that while the 
Union “is entitled to bring suit under the WARN Act, 
[] it does not necessarily follow that the union has stand- 
ing.” Pet. App. 4a. 
The Court of Appeals went on to hold that although 
the failure to provide an adequate, timely notice to the 
6 
Union, as required by the statute, may “allege{] an injury 
to the union itself . . . that is concrete and actual” (Pet. 
App. 6a), the Union does not have direct standing. 
The statutory remedy for inadequate notice—back pay 
and benefits for aggrieved employees—said the Eighth 
Circuit, “would do nothing to remedy the alleged failure 
of notice to the UFCW.” Pet. App. 7a. The Eighth 
Circuit went on to deny the Union standing to assert the 
right of the employees the Union represents to monetary 
relief, relying on the third prong of the associational 
standing test articulated in Hunt v. Washington State 
Apple Advertising Commission, 432 U.S. 333, 343 
(1977 )—viz., whether “the claim asserted [or] the relief 
requested recuirea the participation of the individual mem- 
bers in the lawsuit.” Pet. App. lla. Finally, the Court 
of Appeals affirmed the district court’s denial of leave to 
amend the complaint. Pet. App. 12a. 
REASONS FOR GRANTING THE WRIT 
The five courts of appeals—other than the court below 
—in which a case has been litigated by a union seeking 
monetary relief for the employees the union represents 
to redress an employer violation of the Worker Adjust- 
ment and Retraining Notification Act, have proceeded 
on the understanding that WARN provides for—and the 
Constitution permits—such suits, have exercised jurisdic- 
tion over the case, and have reached the merits. 
This Court, after noting the question of the union’s 
standing in oral argument, did the same last year in 
North Star Steel Co. v. Thomas, US. , 115 
S.Ct. 1927 (1995). North Star Steel, the firsts WARN 
litigation to come to this Court, involved two consolidated 
cases, one of which, like this case, was brought by a un- 
ion seeking to recover the applicable statutory monetary 
relief for the employees the union represented. See 
United Steelworkers of America v. Crown Cork & Seal 
7 
Co., 32 F.3d 53, 55 (3d Cir., 1994) affirmed sub nom. 
North Star Steel Co. v. Thomas, supra.’ 
The Eighth Circuit has now gone the other way and 
has invalidated Congress’ determination to provide for 
these WARN suits by unions on the theory that this 
Statutory cause of action offends the Constitution’s 
“standing” rules. The constitutional standing question thus 
raised is both a recurring one—necessarily arising in 
numerous WARN cases—and one that goes to the heart 
of the WARN statutory scheme as a whole. Since North 
Star Steel’s approach and result evince this Court’s un- 
derstanding that WARN suits by unions are a proper 
part of the statutory scheme, the Eighth Circuit’s con- 
trary determination warrants immediate review to clarify 
the law in this regard. 
The Eighth Circuit’s constitutional ruling, moreover, 
raises a fundamental and recurring separation of powers 
issue. There are a number of federal statutes that pro- 
vide for litigation by a representative designated by stat- 
ute who is empowered to seek monetary relief on behalf of 
particular individuals. This Court has proceeded on the

The question of whether Article III limits Congress’ 
legislative power to determine whether a representative 
may prosecute an action for monetary relief on be- 
half of individuals who have suffered injury in fact, and 
if so, under what circumstances, raises a most basic and 
sensitive issue concerning the relationship between Con- 
gress and the federal courts. The federal courts “have 
no more right to decline the exercise of jurisdiction which 
is given than to usurp that which is not given. The one 
or the other would be treason to the Constitution.” 
Cohens v. Virginia, 6 Wheat. 262, 404 (U.S. 1821) 
(Marshall, C.J.). Whether Congress’ law-making powers 
are to be restricted on the view of the constitutional stand- 
ing requirement adopted below is therefore a quintessen- 
tial question for this Court to decide. 
Given the nature of the question presented here and 
of the background law, in the balance of this petition 
we develop two points. 
First, Congress in crafting WARN expressly deter- 
mined that employer violations of the requirement that 
plant closing/mass layoff notices are to be provided to 
unions designated by a group of employees as their 
exclusive representative can be met by a suit by the union 
against the employer and that the union in such a suit can 
seek monetary relief payable to the represented employees 
on account of the violation. Such suits are, indeed, at the 
very heart of the statutory enforcement scheme. Congress’ 
intent to provide for WARN cases of the kind the Union 
brought here, in other words, could not be more clear. 
Thus, the decision below that Congress cannot, consistent 
with Article III’s standing requirements, enact such a 
cause of action is a direct frontal attack on Congress’ 
Article [ legislative powers. Pp. 9-14, infra. 
9 
Second, the Eighth Circuit’s constitutional ruling rests 
on the assumption that for the purposes of the standing 
doctrine, a case in which Congress has determined that 
a person that has a representational status is to be em- 
powered to bring suit seeking monetary relief payable to 
the individuals that person represents is on all fours with 
a case in which Congress has made no such determina- 
tion and the person bringing suit is proceeding under 
some general heading of jurisdiction. That assumption 
is contrary to this Court’s overall “standing” jurisprudence, 
which distinguishes between “constitutional” standing re- 
quirements and “prudential” standing requirements and 
which recognizes that “Congress may, by legislation, ex- 
pand standing to the full extent permitted by Art. III, 
thus permitting litigation by one ‘who otherwise would be 
barred by prudential standing rules.’ . . ,” (Gladstone 
Realtors v. Village of Bellwood, 441 U.S. 91, 100 
(1979)). The result reached below moreover, puts into 
question many statutory and common law schemes ana- 
logous to that provided for in WARN. Indeed, to take 
the analogy closest to hand, the Court of Appeal’s consti- 
tutional ruling cannot be squared with this Court's deci- 
sion in International Union, UAW v. Hoosier Cardinal 
Corp., 383 U.S. 696 (1966) which recognizes union suits 
to redress employer violations of collective bargaining 
agreements in which the union seeks monetary relief on 
behalf of the adversely affected employees. 
At the same time, there is no case in this Court squarely 
treating with Congress’ constitutional authority to provide 
for suits by a representative for individual monetary relief 
of the kind at issue here. And, the court of appeals law is 
neither clear nor consistent. The importance of this con- 
stitutional issue and the absence of an authoritative ruling 
directly in point underline the need for review of this case 
by this Court. Pp. 14-24, infra.
