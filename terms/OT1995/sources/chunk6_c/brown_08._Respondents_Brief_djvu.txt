STATEMENT OF THE CASE 


On January 17, 1992, Respondent Brown Group, Inc., d/b/a 
Brown Shoe Company (“respondent” or “Brown’’), sent a notice 
to William L. Treece, Assistant Director for Footwear, Food and 
Manufacturing of the United Food and Commercial Workers 


= 


International Union, advising that Brown’s Dixon, Missouri, 
shoe manufacturing plant would be permanently closed begin- 
ning on March 20, 1992 (JA 62-63).' The notice was furnished 
pursuant to the Worker Adjustment and Retraining Notification 
Act, 29 U.S.C. §§2101-09 (“WARN”). The addressee, Treece, 
was the official who had signed the collective bargaining agree- 
ment with Brown on behalf of the international union (JA 61). 


On June 16, 1992, Petitioner United Food and Commercial 
Workers Union Local 751 (“petitioner” or “the Union”) filed suit 
against Brown in the United States District Court for the Eastern 
District of Missouri under WARN, alleging that the January 17th 
notice failed to give sufficient or appropriate notice of 
respondent's intention to close its Dixon plant (JA 7). 


The ten-paragraph complaint asserted that the notice was not 
in compliance with WARN because Treece was “not the exclu- 
sive representative of the affected employees” (JA 9 47), while 
elsewhere petitioner referred to January 17, 1992, as “the date on 
which [respondent] provided [petitioner] written notice of the 
permanent closing of the Dixon plant pursuant to §2102(a) of the 
WARN Act” (JA 8 46). 


The suit identified petitioner as the “employee representative” 
for 277 union members employed at the plant “on whose behalf 
the relief prayed for herein is sought” (JA 8 45). The Union 
asserted that Brown began laying off plant workers on Janu- 
ary 31, 1992, and continued the layoffs during February and 
March, and that those persons were “aggrieved employees under 
the WARN Act entitled to relief as set forth in §2104 of the Act” 
(JA 9448, 9). Attached to the complaint was a copy of the union 
contract (JA 11-61) and achart listing the names and the last day 
of work for the 277 employees (JA 64-73). 


‘Brown Group, Inc., is a publicly traded corporation that has no parents 
and no subsidiaries that are not wholly-owned. 


The prayer of the complaint requested that Brown be required 
to “pay each of the aggrieved employees backpay and fringe 
benefits, including but not limited to, vacation pay, medical 
expenses, and contributions to the pension plan, for 60 days or 
for each day of each employee's employment loss . . . .” (JA 9- 
10 410). The Union sought no relief for itself but did request 
costs, including reasonable attorneys’ fees, and added a boilerplate 
prayer for “such other and further relief on its behalf and that of 
the aggrieved employees as may be proper” (JA 10). 


Brown filed a motion to dismiss pursuant to Rule 12, Fed. R. 
Civ. P., challenging the Union's standing to maintain this action 
(JA 74). On April 12, 1993, the district court (Judge Edward L. 
Filippine) granted the motion and dismissed the complaint, 
concluding that the court was without jurisdiction because the 
Union had “not alleged injury to itself” and lacked constitutional 
standing to bring a WARN Act claim for monetary damages on 
behalf of its members, none of whom had been joined as parties. 
820 F. Supp. 1192.’ 


On appeal, the Eighth Circuit affirmed in a unanimous opinion 
authored by Judge Pasco M. Bowman for a three-judge panel. 50 
F.3d 1426. The court held that the Union had failed to establish 
either direct or associational standing and that the district court 
therefore had properly dismissed the complaint for lack of 
jurisdiction. With respect to the Union’s direct standing to assert 
its own rights, the Eighth Circuit determined that the relief 
requested by the Union would not redress the Union’s supposed 
injury. /d. at 1430. It rejected the Union's argument that the

Although the Union’s brief to the Eighth Circuit expressly 
disavowed any reliance on the concept of “associational stand-

The Union's petition for rehearing and suggestion for rehear-

The Petition for Certiorari did not challenge that portion of the ruling.

SUMMARY OF THE ARGUMENT 


The WARN Act requires an employer to give 60 days’ 
advance notice of a proposed plant closing or mass layoff. If the 
affected employees are represented by a union, the notice is to be 
sent to the union; the local unit of government must also be 
notified. Section 2104(a) authorizes lawsuits to enforce the 
obligation to provide an appropriate notice. Paragraph (1) of 
§2104(a) creates a cause of action belonging to, and authorizes 
back pay and certain lost benefits for, “each aggrieved employee 
who suffers an employment loss as a result of such closing or 
layoff.” Paragraph (3) imposes a “civil penalty of not more than 
$500 for each day of such violation,” payable to the local 
community where the plant is situated. Paragraph (5) provides 
venue for actions brought in federal court by persons “aggrieved 
under paragraph (1) or (3)” of §2104(a) 


In this case, the Union filed suit “on behalf of’ 277 union 
members at respondent's Dixon plant, who were otherwise 
identified as “the aggrieved Brown Shoe employees on whose 
behalf the relief prayed for herein is sought” (JA 8). The 
complaint requested back pay and benefits for the affected 
employees (all of whom had individual piece-work wage rates) 
and attorneys’ fees. The Union did not assert that it had suffered 
any injury as a result of the allegedly defective notice; it did not 
seek any remedy for itself; nor did it indicate in its complaint how 
or if the relief prayed for would redress any injury the Union 
might have suffered. 


The plain language of WARN does not support the mainte- 
nance of this action by the Union, which admittedly was not 
“aggrieved under paragraph (1) or (3)" of §2104(a). The 
legislative history of WARN reveals (a) that the thrust of the 
Statute was to require notice for the benefit of affected employees 
and local communities, not unions; (b) that in the case of union 
workers, notice was to be given to the union for the benefit of the 
members as a convenience to the employer so that it would not 


need to provide hundreds or thousands of personal notices; and 
(c) that, to the extent that the “representative” referred to in 
§2104(5) is the union, such a designation does not reflect a 
conscious conferral of authority to sue but is a mere vestige in a 
venue provision from earlier bills that would have given the 
union an independent right and allowed the union to recover a 
penalty for violation of that right. 


The Union lacks standing because it has not pleaded — and 
cannot show — any “injury-in-fact.” This Court's standing 
jurisprudence requires a plaintiff to demonstrate, inter alia, that 
one of its “legally-protected” interests has suffered an injury that 
is distinct, concrete, real and immediate, not abstract. Warth v. 
Seldin, 422 U.S. 490 (1975); Allen v. Wright, 468 U.S. 737 
(1984); Lujan v. Defenders of Wildlife, 504 U.S. 555 (1992). 


Here, the furnishing of an allegedly defective notice did not 
cause constitutional “injury” to petitioner because the Union was 
a mere conduit of the notice to its members; it suffered nothing 
more than a “procedural injury” of the type held insufficient to 
support standing in Lujan. The Union's complaint did not 
contain the kind of specific allegations of interference with its 
ability to function or to carry on its business that are necessary 
for an organization to have standing to sue in its own right. 
Petitioner cannot bootstrap itself into Article II] by claiming in 
its brief that a timely notice would have facilitated performance 
of its duties as collective bargaining representative because it did 
not plead any such “injury,” and nothing in the language or 
history of WARN suggests that Congress designed the notice to 
serve those purposes anyway. Moreover, in its collective bar- 
gaining agreement with Brown, petitioner specifically recog- 
nized the right of the employer “to close a plant permanently” 
and waived any right to negotiate concerning a plant-closing 
decision or its effects. 


Standing is also wanting because the remedy sought in the 
complaint — back pay and certain benefits for plant workers — 


will not redress the alleged failure of the Union to receive an 
appropriate or timely notice. To “redress” the Union's proce- 
dural “injury” would require ordering the plant to remain open 
until 60 days after the furnishing of a proper notice, but Congress 
has specifically prohibited injunctive relief in §2104(b). Any 
suggestion that back pay toemployees would redress the Union's 
putative “injury” is necessarily speculative and thus inadequate 
for Article III] purposes. Lujan, 504 U.S. at 561; Simon v. Eastern

A. The WARN Act Does Not Specifically Designate a 
Union as a Proper Litigant for Back Pay for Ag- 
grieved Plant Workers. 


In 29 U.S.C. §2104(a)(1) Congress provided that an employer 
who fails to furnish a proper notice of a plant closing is liable “to 
each aggrieved employee who suffers an employment loss as a 
result of such closing.” An “aggrieved employee” is defined in 
§2104(a)(7) as an employee who does not receive, directly or 
indirectly, the notice called for by §2102(a)(1). Section 2104(a)(1) 
thus limits the congressionally-created cause of action for a 
violation of the §2102(a)(1) notice provision to the individual 


“aggrieved” employees. 


Petitioner argues that paragraph (5) of §2104(a) authorizes 
any union to seek WARN back pay for its members, but the 
literal language of that provision does not support such an 
interpretation. Paragraph (5) provides as follows: 


“A person seeking to enforce such liability, including a 
representative of employees or a unit of local government 
aggrieved under paragraph (1) or (3), may sue either for 
such person or for other persons similarly situated, or both, 
in any district court of the United States for any district in 
which the violation is alleged to have occurred, or in which 
the employer transacts business.” (Emphasis added.) 


The parties authorized to sue under this provision are those 
“aggrieved under paragraph (1) or (3).” Paragraph (1) provides 
back pay and certain benefits for “each aggrieved employee who 
suffers an employment loss as a result of such closing or layoff.” 
Paragraph (3) imposes a “civil penalty of not more than $500 for 
each day of such violation,” payable to the community where the 
plant is located. Obviously, a union as the statutory “represen- 
tative of employees” is not “aggrieved under paragraphs (1) or 
(3).” It is therefore not entitled to bring a suit for back wages or 
for the fine payable to the local governmental unit. 


= 


Petitioner essentially contends that anyone mentioned in 
paragraph (5) automatically has standing to sue for a violation of 
WARN. But if that interpretation were correct, an affected unit 
of local government could also bring an action for back wages for 
the employees, and, presumably, a union could sue for the fine 
payable to the local community — scenarios obviously incon- 
sistent with congressional intent, principles of grammar and 
syntax, and common sense. Moreover, the language authorizing 
suit to be brought “for such person or other persons similarly 
situated” cannot include the union as employee representative, 
for unions are not “similarly situated” with their members. 


Hence, the statutory structure itself refutes the foundational

a venue provision, designed to ensure that suits to enforce the 
rights created by §§2104(a)(1) and (3) can be freely brought 
anywhere the employer transacts business, as well as the more 
obvious situs of the violation.

For standing purposes, a plaintiff must plead and prove that it 
has suffered an actionable “injury,” which must be “distinct,” 
“palpable” and “concrete,” “real and immediate,” not “abstract.” 
See Warth, 422 U.S. at 501; Allen, 468 U.S. at 755-56; Schlesinger, 
418 U.S. at 220-21; City of Los Angeles v. Lyons, 461 U.S. 95, 
102 (1983). And the injury must be to a “legally-protected 
interest.” Lujan, 504 U.S. at 560. Petitioner’s claim does not 
pass this test. 


This “injury-in-fact” requirement imposes the obligation on a 
would-be plaintiff to allege “specific, concrete facts demonstrat- 
ing that the challenged practices harm him.” Warth, 422 U.S. at 
508 (emphasis in original). Quoting Linda R.S. v. Richard D., 
410 U.S. 614, 617 (1973), the Court in Warth also stated: 


“The Art. III judicial power exists only to redress or 
otherwise to protect against injury to the complaining party, 
even though the court’s judgment may benefit others collat- 
erally. A federal court’s jurisdiction therefore can be 
invoked only when the plaintiff himself has suffered ‘some 
threatened or actual injury resulting from the putatively 
illegal action.’” 422 U.S. at 499. 


Accord, Gladstone, Realtors, 441 U.S. at 99. 


Yet in its complaint petitioner specifically and repeatedly 
alleged that it was suing “on behalf of” the affected plant 
workers. Nowhere did it plead that respondent’s actions had 
caused the Union itself any harm. Whatever the statutory 
intendment, in this case this union did not plead that its failure to 
receive an appropriate notice caused any injury to itself. Its 
complaint was therefore properly dismissed because it did not 
plead the first element required for constitutional standing.

C. Petitioner Has Not Established That Any Alleged 
Injury to It Was Redressable in This Case. 


In addition to “injury-in-fact,” Article II] requires that the 
injury asserted must be likely to be redressed or remedied by the 
relief sought. Allen, 468 U.S. at 751 (“relief from the injury must 
be ‘likely’ to follow from a favorable decision”); Simon, 426 
U.S. at 38 (plaintiff must show “an injury to himself that is likely 
to be redressed by a favorable decision”); Lujan, 504 U.S. at 561 
(likelihood of redress must not be merely speculative). This 
element recognizes that Article III judicial power “exists only to 
redress or otherwise to protect against injury to the complaining 
party, even though the court’s judgment may benefit others 
collaterally.” Warth, 422 U.S. at 499. Hence, a claimant must 
demonstrate “that he personally would benefit in a tangible way 
from the court’s intervention.” /d. at 508. 


The redressability component “examines the causal connec- 
tion between the alleged injury and the judicial relief requested.” 


—=— 


Allen, 468 U.S. at 753 n.19. This necessarily entails analysis of 
the specific relief sought by the would-be litigant against the 
background of its “injury.” Petitioner here prayed for a monetary 
award to the former Dixon plant workers and did not seek 
anything for itself for its putative “injury.” But back pay for 
employees would not, and could not as a matter of law, redress 
any supposed “injury” to petitioner attributable to a defective 
notice of proposed plant closing. If any such “injury” existed, it 
could be repaired only by an order which in some fashion would 
delay the time for closing the Dixon plant until 60 days after 
delivery of a proper notice. No such injunction was sought here 
for the obvious reason that WARN expressly prohibits it. 29

B. Principles of Third-Party-Beneficiary Contracts 
and Enforcement Actions by Government Agen-

The Union also seeks comfort in two other classes of cases 
which it claims support, by analogy, its standing to sue in this

Petitioner first maintains that the law of third-party-benefi- 
ciary contracts justifies a suit on behalf of its members, even 
though it is not seeking any relief for itself. This argument is far 
afield. In the first place, the third-party-beneficiary doctrine 
arises by definition in contract, not in tort or by statute, as here. 
It is based upon an express agreement by two contracting parties, 
A and B, to confer a benefit upon a third party, C. Most of the 
litigation in the law of third-party beneficiaries involves C’s 
right to assert claims under the contract against A and/or B. 


The Union cites no cases in which B — the promisee — was 
permitted to sue for damages for C without asserting some right 
to relief for itself. The only authority proffered by petitioner is 
Corbin on Contracts, §812, where the author makes reference to 
a “few” cases in the United States “that allow the promisce to sue 
as ‘trustee’ for the beneficiary, and to recover not merely his own 
damages but also the damages suffered by the beneficiary by 
reason of the promisor’s breach of contract.” (Emphasis added. ) 
The author also cautions that some states have provided by 
Statute for such an action. /d.

The third-party-beneficiary analogy is wide of the mark for at 
least few reasons: (1) the right-to-sue issue in those cases is 
more acs urately couched in terms of state-law principles of “real

(4) there is no authority for allowing the promisee to assert 
damage claims on behalf of a large number (say, 277) of would- 
be third-party beneficiaries.

C. Cases Involving Traditional “Representatives” Do 
Not Support the Union’s Standing. 

Petitioner’s Point II espouses the theory that the existence of 
various traditional “representative” plaintiffs, whose Article III 
standing has never been examined, justifies its standing here 
even in the absence of “personal” injury to it. The Union 
acknowledges that its theory would rewrite all of this Court’s 
modern standing cases (Br. 37). Forexample, in Simon, 426 U.S. 
at 39, the Court rejected this very proposition: 


“The necessity that the plaintiff who seeks to invoke judi- 
cial power stand to profit in some personal interest remains 
an Art. III requirement.” 


In Lujan, too, the Court reemphasized that part of the “irreduc- 
ible constitutional minimum of standing” is the requirement 
“that the party seeking review be himself among the injured.” 
504 U.S. at 560, 563. 


Properly read, the “representative” cases are entirely (or at 
least almost always) consistent with existing Article III doctrine. 
The would-be representative is situated so that it satisfies Article 
III, including the “personal” injury requirement, for itself. The 
Union is simply not equivalent to any of these “traditional” 
representatives. 


Petitioner invokes the Wright & Miller treatise for the propo- 
sition that “representative” actions are commonplace and that, 
by analogy, a union can assert damage claims for its members 
(Br. 36). Ina lengthy footnote (id. at 36-37 n.9), petitioner cites 
numerous cases involving trustees, executors, guardians, receiv- 
ers, administrators, etc., most of which did not even discuss 
standing.” The difference between those cases and the present 
one is fundamental and determinative: trustees, receivers, ex- 
ecutors and administrators have title to the property or assets in 
question and, by definition, have a personal legal interest at 
stake. It is therefore fully consistent with the requirement of 
“personal injury” that they have the right to conduct litigation 
aimed at increasing the worth of that property and to defend 
actions designed to impair its value. Scott, The Law of Trusts 
§2.6 (4th ed. 1987). Here, there is no estate or property that the 
Union owns or is charged with protecting. 


Cases involving guardians usually are predicated on a judicial 
determination that the ward is not capable of carrying on his or 
her own affairs, including the conduct or the defense of litiga- 
tion, and that a “next friend” should be appointed. Indeed, even 
in capital cases the Court has ruled that a “next-friend” does not 
have standing to assert the rights of acondemned prisoner unless 
he provides “an adequate explanation — such as inaccessibility,

mantle on behalf of affected plant workers. Those workers, after 
all, are given the right to sue in their own behalf if they sochoose.
