U.C.M.J. ARTICLE 118 MAY NOT BE IMPLE-

The government asserts that the ‘‘linchpin’’ of peti- 
tioner’s argument is that Uniform Code of Military Justice 
{hereinafter U.C.M.J.] article 118, 10 U.S.C. § 918, is a 
*‘nullity’’ after Furman v. Georgia, 408 U.S. 238 (1972). 
Resp. Br. 10. The government is incorrect, however, 
because petitioner's argument is not that Article 118's 
death-sentence provision is ‘‘null,"’ but simply that it 
cannot be effectuated in a constitutional manner unless and 
until, as the Eighth Amendment requires, Congress 
establishes aggravating factors to guide the sentencer’s 
discretion. See United States v. Woolard, 981 F.2d 756, 
759, reh'g denied, 990 F.2d 819 (Sth Cir. 1993); United 
States v. Harper, 729 F.2d 1216, 1225 (9th Cir. 1984). 

Many death penalty statutes, both federal and state, were 
in effect in this country before Furman. Those statutes 
were constitutional when written, but this Court determined 
that, due to the unfettered discretion these statutes allowed 
judges and juries to exercise in capital cases, the death 
penalty was being applied in a racist and arbitrary manner. 
408 U.S. at 242 (Douglas, J., concurring). This Court thus 
held that the death penalty as applied violated the Eighth 
Amendment. 408 U.S. at 239-40. 

The government and its amici try in vain to exempt 
U.C.M.J. article 118 from Furman's reach, but Furman 
requires that the unfettered discretion of sentencing panels 
in the military, or anywhere, be circumscribed by 
appropriate standards and the rule of law. The government

the people—establish aggravating circumstances. Indeed,

aggravating circumstances may be “‘legislative or court- 
imposed.’’3 Resp. Br. 30 (quoting Zant v. Stephens, 462

stated that ‘‘statutory aggravating circumstances play a 
constitutionally necessary function at the stage of legisla-

eligible for the death penalty.’’ 462 U.S. at 878 (emphasis

because this Court has never indicated that any court may 
create the aggravating factors that make a defendant 
‘death eligible.”’ This Court has simply allowed state 
appellate courts to impose narrowing constructions on 
otherwise vague aggravators. The aggravating circum- 
stances themselves, however, were enacted not by the 
courts, but by state legislatures expressing the people’s 
will.

C. THE PRESIDENT HAS NO INHERENT 
AUTHORITY TO ESTABLISH AGGRAVATING 
FACTORS. 


The government attempts to circumvent the need for an 
explicit delegation of authority. See Pet. Br. 11-12. It 
asserts that the ‘‘Commander-in-Chief’s constitutional 
authority easily encompasses,’ Resp. Br. 16, the authority 
to create aggravating factors in capital cases and, therefore, 
the ordinary constitutional limitations on the power of 
Congress to confer law-making authority on the Executive 
Branch are less stringent. Resp. Br. 14-19. The government 
is wrong. The President has no inherent authority to 
establish punishments. In fact, this Court unambiguously 
declared in Solorio v. United States, 483 U.S. 435, 446 
(1987), that ‘‘Congress, and not the Executive, was given 
authority to make rules for the regulation of the Armed 
Forces.’’ Solorio specifically refutes the government’s 
argument that the President has inherent powers in the 
courts-martial context. See also Schick v. Reed, 419 U.S. 
256, 267 (1974) (‘Of course, the President may not 
aggravate punishment’’). Moreover, the Constitution itself 
specifically reserves to Congress the power ‘‘to make rules 
for the government and regulation of the land and naval 
forces.’’ U.S. Const. Art. I, § 8, cl. 14. 

This Court’s opinions in United States v. Mazurie, 419 
U.S. 544 (1975); United States ex rel. Knauff v. 
Shaughnessy, 338 U.S. 537 (1950); United States v. 
Curtiss-Wright Export Corporation, 299 U.S. 304 (1936); 
United States v. Chemical Foundation, Inc., 272 U.S. | 
(1926); and Cargo of the Brig Aurora v. United States, 11 


7 


U.S. 382 (1813), all of which the government cites, Resp. 
Br. 14, do not support its assertion that the President has 
any inherent authority in this area. In Mazurie, this Court 
held that Congress may delegate authority to regulate 
alcoholic beverages in Indian territory to tribal authorities. 
In Knauff, although recognizing that the executive has 
inherent authority in the arena of foreign affairs to exclude 
aliens, the Court upheld the executive’s action based on a 
specific delegation of authority from Congress. Likewise, 
in Chemical Foundation, this Court upheld the Executive 
Department's sale of patents, copyrights, and trade-marks 
seized from German citizens based on a specific delegation 
of authority. 

The most illuminating case the government relies upon 
is Curtiss-Wright, 299 U.S. 304, in which the Court held 
there was no violation of the doctrine of separation of 
powers when Congress delegated authority to the President 
to determine whether an embargo on the sale of arms and 
munitions to belligerents in the Chaco war was necessary. 
In deciding the issue, this Court recognized not only that 
there was a specific delegation of authority, but also that in 
the arena of foreign affairs the President’s inherent 
authority is at its pinnacle. Curtiss-Wright, 299 U.S. at 
315-16. Thus it is clear that none of these cases are 
relevant to the internal domestic affair of governing the 
armed forces within the geographical limits of the United 
States during peacetime. 

This Court has recognized that the President does have 
some limited inherent authority as Commander-in-Chief. 
See, e.g., Department of the Navy v. Egan, 484 U.S. 518 
(1988); Ex Parte Quirin, 317 U.S. 1, 10 (1942); Swaim v. 
United States, 165 U.S. 553 (1897). However, this Court 
has never recognized any inherent authority to establish 
criminal offenses, procedures for courts-martial, or crimi- 
nal penalties. Indeed, this Court specifically recognized 
long ago that ‘‘it has not yet been definitely established to 


8 


what extent the President, as Commander-in-Chief of the 
armed forces, or his delegates, can promulgate, supplement 
or change substantive military law as well as_ the 
procedures of military courts in time of peace, or in time 
of war.’’ Reid v. Covert, 354 U.S. 1, 38 (1957).4 
Moreover, as is apparent from Kent v. Dulles, 357 U.S. 
116 (1958), no alleged inherent authority as Commander- 
in-Chief alters the requirement for an explicit delegation. 
In Kent, this Court held that the President’s inherent 
authority in foreign relations and national security does not 
override individual rights, despite the fact that this Court 
has repeatedly recognized that the President’s powers are 
greatest in the area of foreign relations. See Knauff, 338 
U.S at 542; Curtiss-Wright, 299 U.S. at 320. It is apparent, 
therefore, that if the President’s broad inherent authority in 
foreign affairs is insufficient to permit less stringent review 
when an individual’s personal rights have been infringed, 
then his limited authority as Commander-in-chief does not 
justify less stringent review under these circumstances.*

Matters of ‘‘substance’’ and matters of **procedures”’ 
are much talked about in the books as though they

Neither ‘‘substance’’ nor ‘‘procedure’’ represents the

related at vest ....

a particular matter is ‘characterized either as ‘substantive’

The government simply ignores the facts and the legal 
issue involved in Dobbert and urges the Cour to 
mindlessly rely on ‘‘terminological niceties."’ Guaranty 
Trust Co., 326 U.S. at 110. In Dobbert, this Court was 
concerned with whether an ex post facto violation had 
occurred when the state applied a capital statute enacted 
after the offense was committed and which changed the 
capital sentencing structure in Florida from jury sentencing 
to judge sentencing. Dobbert, 432 U.S. at 287-92. Dobbert 
did not address the nature of aggravating circumstances. 
The issue before the Dobbert Court was simply whether 
‘the change in the role of the judge and jury in the 
imposition of the death sentence in Florida ... constitutes 
an ex post facto violation.’ /d. at 292. This Court 
determined only that the change from jury sentencing to 
judge sentencing was procedural for purposes of evaluat-

the government sadly takes language out of context. In

G. PURSUANT TO ARTICLES I AND II OF THE

Both the government and its amici apparently suggest

First and foremost, petitioner does not argue that some

H. THE NATURE OF THE AGGRAVATING 
FACTORS SELECTED BY THE PRESIDENT IS 
IRRELEVANT TO THE DETERMINATION OF 
WHETHER THERE IS A CONSTITUTIONAL 
VIOLATION IN THIS CASE. 


In its concluding paragraph, the government asserts that 
‘‘many of the aggravating factors [Congress] adopted for 
capital homicide offenses [in 18 U.S.C. § 3592] were 
virtually identical to those earlier identified by the 
President’’ in R.C.M. 1004(c). Resp. Br. 46. Petitioner 
does not dispute this claim, but, standing alone, it is 
irrelevant. The government goes further, however, and 
declares: ‘‘With the actions of the President and Congress 
so closely aligned, petitioner has given this Court no sound 
reason to overturn the President’s judgment in promulgat- 
ing RCM 1004.”’ Resp. Br. 47. 

This assertion fails to grasp the significance of the issues 
before this Court. Petitioner has not challenged—and this 
Court did not grant the writ of certiorari to review—the 
nature of the aggravating factors in R.C.M. 1004(c). 
Simply stated, the supposed contemporary ‘‘alignment’’ 
between the President and Congress does not justify and 
cannot withstand the violation of the separation of powers. 
The President was—and is—without power to create ag- 
gravating factors to define the class of servicemembers 
eligible for the death penalty. 

The President has authority to convene courts-martial, to 
prosecute cases as capital, to select the members, to select 
the judge, and to approve the resulting death sentence or 


20 


commute it. Pet. Br. 48-49. The government now asks this 
Court to hold that the President has the inherent authority 
to create law by defining the class of persons eligible for 
the death penalty. This “‘blending of functions in one 
branch of the Government is the objectionable thing which 
the draftsmen of the Constitution endeavored to prevent by 
providing for the separation of governmental powers.’’ See 
Reid v. Covert, 354 U.S. at 39. The government’s request 
must therefore be rejected.
