QUESTIONS PRESENTED 


I. Whether, because Congress’ legislative responsibility to 
establish aggravating factors in military capital cases 
involves individual liberties protected by the Fifth and 
Eighth Amendments, an explicit delegation of legislative 
power to the President is necessary. 


Il. If an explicit delegation of legislative authority is not 
required, whether an implicit delegation of Congress’ 
legislative power to establish aggravating factors may be 
divined from various and sundry provisions of the Uniform 
Code of Military Justice.” 


Ill. If there has been such an implicit delegation of 
authority to the President, whether Congress has 
established constitutionally adequate standards to guide the 
President’s exercise of legislative authority. 


IV. Whether it is consistent with the Framer’s intent, the 
dictates of Articles I and II of the Constitution, and the 
Eighth Amendment to permit Congress to delegate to the 
President its responsibility to define aggravating factors 
which will define the class of servicemembers eligible for 
the death penalty.

JURISDICTION 


This Court's jurisdiction is invoked pursuant to 28 
U.S.C. § 1259(1), the United States Court of Appeals for 
the Armed Forces (formerly the United States Court of 
Military Appeals) having reviewed this case pursuant to 
Uniform Code of Military Justice [hereinafter U.C.M.J.] 
article 67(a)(1), 10 U.S.C. § 867(a)(1). 


CONSTITUTIONAL, STATUTORY AND 
REGULATORY PROVISIONS INVOLVED 


This case involves: 

1. U.S. Const. art. I, § 1, which provides, in pertinent 
part, ‘‘All legislative Powers herein granted shall be vested 
in a Congress of the United States....°’ 

2. U.S. Const. art. I, § 8. cl. 14, which provides, in 
pertinent part, ‘““The Congress shall have Power. . .To 
make Rules for the Government and Regulation of the land 
and naval Forces... .”’ 

3. U.S. Const. art. Il, § 1, cl. 1, which provides, in 
pertinent part, ‘“The executive Power shall be vested in a 
President of the United States... .”’ 

4. U.S. Const. art. Il, § 2, cl. 1, which provides, in 
pertinent part, ““The President shall be Commander in 
Chief of the Army and Navy of the United States... .” 

5. The Fifth Amendment to the United States Constitu- 
tion, which provides, in pertinent part, ‘‘No person shall 
be. . .deprived of life, liberty, or property, without due 
process of law.” 7 

6. The Eighth Amendment to the United States Consti- 
tution, which forbids the infliction of ‘‘cruel and unusual 
punishments.” 

7. U.C.M.J. article 18, U.S.C. § 818, which establishes 
the jurisdiction of general courts-martial. J.A. 139.7 


2J.A. refers to the Joint Appendix.

8. U.C.M.J. article 36, 10 U.S.C. § 836(a), which allows 
the President to prescribe procedural rules for courts- 
martial. J.A. 140. 

9. U.C.M.J. article 56, U.S.C. § 856, which allows the 
President to prescribe maximum punishments for non-

10. U.C.M.J. article 118, 10 U.S.C. § 918, which

11. Manual for Courts-Martial, United States, 1984,

used in capital cases under the U.C.M.J. J.A. 132-38. 


STATEMENT OF THE CASE 


Dwight Loving was serving as a Private (El) in the 
United States Army at Fort Hood, Texas, at the time of his 
offenses and trial before a general court-martial. He was 
found guilty of attempted murder, premeditated murder, 
felony murder, and four specifications of robbery, in 
violation of U.C.M.J. articles 80, 118(1) and (4), and 122, 
10 U.S.C. §§ 880, 918(1) and (4), and 922, respectively. 
On April 3, 1989, the court-martial panel announced three

SUMMARY OF ARGUMENT 


Article I, § 8, cl. 14 of the United States Constitution 
grants to Congress the exclusive and plenary power to 
make all laws to be applied to servicemembers in the 
military justice system. Moreover, the Constitution, this 
nation’s history and this Court’s opinions evidence that the 
President, as Commander in Chief, has no inherent 
peacetime authority to encroach upon the strictly legislative 
task of establishing aggravating factors for military capital 
cases. Furthermore, the creation of aggravating factors, 
necessary elements in the legislati< determination of 
whether the death penalty is an appropriate punishment in 
a particular case, potentially impinges upon a service- 
members rights guaranteed by the Fifth and Eighth 
Amendments. It is for this reason that only an explicit 
delegation of legislative power by the Congress would 
justify the President’s exercise of legislative power. 
Because Congress has not explicitly delegated its power in 
this area, the President's creation of aggravating factors 
violates the separation of powers doctrine. 

Congress has not implicitly delegated its authority to the 
President. In order to find an implicit delegation, the lower 
court ignored the fact that the military was granted 
jurisdiction to try servicemembers for the capital offense of 
murder in peacetime within the geographical limits of the 
United States for the first time in 1950. The lower court 
also disregarded Congress’ overriding concern, when 
enacting the U.C.MJ., that the power of military 
commanders in the military justice system be limited. 
Finally, the lower court completely disregarded this 
Court’s rules of statutory construction, which disfavor any 
inference of Congressional intent delegating authority to 
the President to determine which servicemembers shall be 
eligible for the death penalty. 


6 


Congress has not set forth an intelligible principle 
guiding and limiting the President’s exercise of legislative 
power. Article 36 of the U.C.M.J. refers only to principles 
of law applied in the United States district courts and there 
were no such capital aggravating factors at the time of the 
President's promulgation of R.C.M. 1004(c), with the sole 
exception of factors applicable only to aircraft piracy. 
Moreover, Article 36 excuses the President from following 
even this guidance by allowing compliance only in so far 
as the President ‘‘considers practicable.’ The lack of an 
intelligible principle guiding the President’s discretion 
renders any purported implicit delegation of authority 
unconstitutional. 

Finally, any Congressional delegation of authority to 
establish aggravating factors in capital cases is unconstitu- 
tional. The intent of the Framers in Article 1, § 8, cl. 14 
and the Eighth Amendment was for Congress to make all 
policy judgments concerning what acts would be punished 
as criminal offenses and what the punishment should be 
for those offenses. The determination of what offenders 
will be eligible for the death penalty is a fundamental 
policy judgment which must be made by Congress as the 
elected representatives of American society. While Con- 
gress may delegate some technical or complex decisions, 
the determination of aggravating factors is not a decision 
which falls into this category. Indeed, with the exception 
of R.C.M. 1004(c), no aggravating factors have been 
established outside the halls of the legislatures—state and 
federal—in the modern era of capital punishment. Even 
assuming that Congress may delegate its authority, it may 
not make that delegation to the President because such a 
delegation places in the President, as Commander in Chief, 
legislative, executive, and judicial powers. This blending of 
functions is precisely what the Framers endeavored to 
avoid.

428 U.S. at 183 (Opinion of Stewart, Powell, and Stevens, 
JJ.); see also Zant v. Stephens, 462 U.S. 862, 877 n.15 
(1983); McCleskey v. Kemp, 481 U.S. 279, 319 (1987). 
Thus, the death penalty is ‘‘ultimately understood only as 
an expression of the community's outrage—its sense that 
an individual has lost his moral entitlement to live.”’ 
Spaziano v. Florida, 468 U.S. 447, 469 (1984) (Stevens, J., 
joined by Brennan and Marshall, JJ., concurring in part and 
dissenting in part). 

Moreover, because the legislature is the voice constituted 
to speak for the people in all instances, including 
expressing moral outrange, this Court has long held that it 
is legislatures which must determine whether the death 
penalty is to be applied to American citizens. For instance, 
in Gregg, 428 U.S. at 175-76 (Opinion of Stewart, Powell, 
and Stevens, JJ.) (quoting Furman, 408 U.S. at 383 
(Burger, C.J., dissenting)), this Court emphasized, ‘‘[I]}n a 
democratic society legislatures. . .are constituted to respond 


20 


to the will and consequently the rm ral values of the 
people.’’ Likewise, Justice Powell declared in his dissent 
in Furman, 408 U.S. at 418, that the decision to use capital 
punishment is uniquely ‘‘an area squarely within the 
historic prerogative of the legislative branch—both state 
and federal—to protect the citizenry through the designa- 
tion of penalties for prohibitable conduct.’’ See also Harris 
v. Alabama, _ U.S. —_, 115 S.Ct. 103!, 1035 (1995); 
Penry v. Lynaugh, 492 U.S. 302, 331, (1989); Stanford v. 
Kentucky, 492 U.S. 361, 370 (1989); Thompson y. 
Oklahoma, 487 U.S. 815, 821 (1988). Therefore, the 
determination of aggravating factors to ‘‘genuinely narrow 
the class of persons eligible for the death penalty,’’ Zant, 
462 U.S. at 877, in the Armed Forces, is uniquely the 
function of Congress. See Gregg, 428 U.S. at 176 (quoting 
Gore v. United States, 357 U.S. 386, 393 (1958)) (the 
specification of punishments is ‘‘peculiarly [a] question{ ] 
of legislative policy’’); see also Whalen v. United States, 
445 U.S. 684, 689 (1980). 

Given the need for policy decisions and moral judgments 
involved in determining who shall be eligible for a death 
sentence, the delegation in U.C.M.J. article 36 to establish 
‘‘procedures”’ is clearly not an explicit delegation support- 
ing the President's creation of aggravating factors in 
capital cases. See Greene, 360 U.S. at 507 (only ‘‘explicit 
action by lawmakers’’ is sufficient to delegate authority to 
infringe on Fifth Amendment rights to ‘‘life, liberty, and 
property’’). Likewise, nothing in the legislative history 
establishes that Congress intended in Article 36 to grant 
the President broad policy making authority. 

Moreover, in determining the implications of these 
provisions on the capital punishment process, it is 
necessary to consider the timing of Congress’ actions in 
enacting these provisions. All of these articles were part of 
the Uniform Code of Military Justice as passed in 1950— 
22 years before this Court's decision in Furman—and they 


21 


have not been substantially amended since that time.'’ 
Congress did not intend (and could not have intended), in 
1950, to grant the President the authority tc establish 
aggravating factors in military capital cases satisfying 
Eighth Amendment principles which did not emerge for 
more than twenty years. Articles 18, 36, and 56 and their 
legislative history, therefore, provide no support for the 
lower court’s decision.

425 (1944).

principle’’ guiding the delegated power. In all other

IV. PURSUANT TO THE DOCTRINE OF SEPARA- 
TION OF POWERS AND THE EIGHTH AMEND- 
MENT OF THE UNITED STATES CONSTITU- 
TION, THE ESTABLISHMENT OF 
AGGRAVATING FACTORS TO BE USED IN 
MILITARY CAPITAL CASES TO NARROW THE 
CATEGORY OF PERSONS ELIGIBLE FOR THE 
DEATH PENALTY IS A LEGISLATIVE DECISION 
VESTED EXCLUSIVELY IN CONGRESS AND 
CANNOT BE DELEGATED TO THE PRESIDENT. 


In our tripartite system of government, the Congress 
Shall write the laws (Article I), the Executive shall 
implement the laws (Article II), and the Judiciary shall 
interpret the laws (Article III). Moreover, ‘‘the intent of 
the Framers [was] that the powers of the three great 
branches of the National Government be largely separate 
from one another.’’ Buckley v. Valeo, 424 U.S. 1, 120 
(1976). Article I, § 1, specifically vests ‘‘{a}ll legislative 
powers” in the Congress. While Congress may under some 
circumstances seek assistance from the other branches, the 
extent and character of that assistance is delineated by the 
structure of the Constitution, specific constitutional limita- 
tions, as well as by ‘‘common sense and the inherent 
necessities of the governmental co-ordination.’ J.W. 


38 


Hampton, Jr. & Co. v. United States, 276 U.S. at 406; see 
also Union Bridge Co. v. United States, 204 U.S. 364 
(1907). 

In the military context, just as in the civilian context, the 
Constitution provides that the Congress must make all 
laws, i.e., the Congress must legislate. U.S. Const. art. I, 
§ 8, cl. 14. The Congress is specifically charged *‘to make 
rules for the government and regulation of the land and 
naval forces.’ /d. This unqualified power is as plenary as 
the other grants of authority in Article I, Solorio, 483 US. 
at 441, 446, and vests in ‘‘Congress, rather than the 
Executive, authority to make rules for the government of 
the military,”” id. at 446, ‘‘including, of course, the 
punishment of military offenses,” Duke & Vogel, 13 
Vand. L. Rev. at 447-48; see also Grant W. Nelson & 
James E. Westbrook, Court-Martial Jurisdiction Over 
Servicemen for ‘‘Civilian’’ Offenses; An Analysis of 
O'Callahan v. Parker, 54 Minn. L. Rev. 1, 18 (1969). 

Likewise, in the military context, just as in the civilian 
context, the Constitution provides that the President is the 
executive or the Commander in Chief, i.e. the President 
must implement the laws. U.S. Const. art. II, § 2, cl. 1. 
The only difference in treatment for members of the armed 
forces contemplated by the Framers is that servicemembers 
are tried not by Article III courts but by courts created by 
the Congress under its Article | powers. Weiss, 114 S.Ct. 
at 755. Thus, in determining who shall make the laws and 
who shall implement them, the members of the military are 
not, and can not, be treated differently than other citizens 
of this country.

1. The Framers of the Constituticn intended 
that Congress, not the President, make the 
rules and regulations for the Armed Forces. 


The Framers of the Constitution were cognizant and 
fearful of military court-martials even for members of the 
military. As this Court recognized in Reid v. Covert, 354 
U.S. at 23-24, *‘The Founders envisioned the army as a 
necessary institution, but one dangerous to liberty if not 
confined within its essential bounds. Their fears were 
rooted in history.’’ See also Lee v. Madigan, 358 U.S. at 
232 (*‘The attitude of a free society toward the jurisdiction 
of military tribunals—our reluctance to give them authority 
to try people for nonmilitary offenses—has a long 
history’). 

The Framers were mindful that English rulers tried to 
rule the country through the army and without the input of 
Parliament. Contrary to the common law, soldiers were 
tried in peacetime by court-martial for non-military 
offenses, including capital offenses. This pattern was 
continued until 1689 when the Declaration of Rights 
placed control of the military in the legislature. Duke & 
Vogel, at 444. Parliament exercised its authority by 
limiting the peacetime power of courts-martial over 
servicemembers. In the first Mutiny Act, Parliament 
allowed courts-martial, in peacetime, only for mutiny, 
sedition, and desertion which were punishable by death. 
Solorio v. United States, 483 U.S. at 442 (citing Mutiny 
Act of 1689, 1 Wm. & Mary, ch. 5). 

This country’s own history was marred by military 
excesses on behalf of the Executive. In the early days of 


43 


this nation, American colonists were tried by courts-martial 
and executed without regard to the existence of civil laws 
and civil courts to punish all offenses. Reid, 354 U.S. at 
27-28 (citing 1 Wells, The Life and Public Services of 
Samuel Adams, 231). Because of this history, the Framers 
placed control of the military, including the policy 
judgments concerning the offenses and punishments to be 
applied in military courts, in Congress. Duke & Vogel, at 
447-48. The ‘‘profound fear and distrust of military power 
[under the control of the executive],”’ is reflected in the 
plain language of the Constitution. Reid, 354 U.S. at 29 
(citing Art. I, §§8 and 9; Art. Il, § 2: Art Ill; and 
Amendments II, III, V, and VI): see also Solorio, 483 U.S. 
at 446 (English history shows a Strong desire to transfer 
the control of court-martial jurisdiction from the executive 
to the legislature, thus in our Constitution “Congress, and 
not the Executive, was given authority to make rules for 
the regulation of the Armed Forces’’). 


2. The Framers of the Constitution intended 
in the Eighth Amendment to prohibit all 
punishments that were not specifically au- 
thorized by the legislature. 


The specific language of the Eighth Amendment 
prohibits ‘‘cruel and unusual punishments.’’ While there 
has been much debate about other aspects of this 
amendment, it is clear that the Framers intended to prohibit 
all punishments that were not determined and specifically 
authorized by the legislature. 

The precursor of the Eighth Amendment is the ‘‘cruell 
and unusual Punishments’’ clause of the English Declara- 
tion of Rights of 1689. Harmelin y. Michigan, 501 U.S. 
957, 965 (1991) (Opinion of Scalia, J. joined by 
Rehnquist, CJ.). The English provision, in turn, was 
prompted by the abuses of Lord Chief Justice Jeffreys, 


44 


who while sitting as a judge during the Stuart reign of 
James Il, created punishments that were not ‘‘authorized 
by the common-law precedent or statute."" Jd. at 968; 
Furman v. Georgia, 408 U.S. at 317-19 (Marshall, J., 
concurring). The Preamble to the Declaration wi Rights 
declared that James II had endeavored to ‘“‘subvert’’ the 
“laws and liberties of this kingdom’’ by arbitrarily 
‘assuming and exercising a power of dispensing with and 
suspending of laws and the execution of laws, without 
consent of parliament.’’ The Declaration then continued by 
denouncing ‘‘arbitrary and illegal courses’’ and “‘illegal 
and cruel punishments’’ in prosecutions in the Court of the 
King’s Bench. Declaration of Rights, | Wm. & Mary, 
Sess. 2, ch. 2 (1689) (quoted in Harmelin, 501 U.S. at 
969). These statements were prompted by a number of 
cases in which various types of punishment, which had not 
been authorized by common law or Parliament, were 
pronounced by Judges of the King’s Bench. Harmelin, 501 
U.S. at 969-71; see also Granucci, “‘Nor Cruel and 
Unusual Punishments Inflicted:’’ The Original Meaning, 
57 Calif. L. Rev. 839 (1969). 

The most prominent example of executive and judicial 
abuse of authority was the case of Titus Oates, who was 
convicted of perjury in 1685. He had been convicted of 
giving false testimony during the ‘‘Popish Plot’’ trials of 
1678-79, and had been sentenced to be defrocked of his 
“Canonical Habits,’ to serve a term of life imprisonment, 
to pay a large fine, to be twice severely whipped, and to 
stand in the pillory at various times during the year. This 
sentence was illegal, however, in several specifics, 
including the fact that only an ecclesiastical court could 
strip a person of ‘‘Canonical Habits’’ and there were no 
precedents authorizing whipping or life imprisonment for 
perjury. /d. (citing | Journals of the House of Lords 367 
(May 31, 1689), quoted in Second Trial of Titus Oates, 10 
How. St. Tr. 1227, 1325 (K.B. 1685)). Four years later,
