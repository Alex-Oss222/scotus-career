## Loving, No. 94-1966

Companion source: [loving_neutral_source_copy.md](loving_neutral_source_copy.md).

**Correction 1 — disputed authorization.** Replace the statement that Congress “delegated ... authority over ... sentencing factors” with: “Congress enacted UCMJ provisions authorizing specified punishments and conferring rulemaking and punishment-limiting powers; whether those provisions authorize these capital aggravating factors, and whether that authority is constitutionally delegable and sufficiently bounded, is disputed.”

**Correction 2 — questions actually stated in the papers.** The petition, questions i, presents whether Congress may delegate its legislative responsibility to establish military capital aggravators consistently with Article I and the Eighth Amendment; whether it implicitly did so; and whether it supplied constitutionally adequate standards. Petitioner merits questions I–IV divide the second question into asserted necessity of explicit delegation and possible implicit delegation. The government's question asks whether the President had authority to promulgate RCM 1004 where the UCMJ otherwise expressly authorizes death. Statutory authorization, required clarity, adequate guidance, constitutional nondelegability and the Article II alternative are separate supported components. **Sources:** petition i; petitioner merits i, 3–50; government merits i, 14–47; reply.

**Correction 3 — lower reasoning and material record.** CAAF, 41 M.J. 213, affirmed convictions and sentence and rejected the delegation challenge under Curtis and Matthews. The Army Court of Military Review had affirmed, 34 M.J. 956, with reconsideration denied, 34 M.J. 1065. Review is under 28 U.S.C. §1259(1). **Sources:** petition appendix 141a–142a, 229a–256a. Petitioner requests reversal of the CAAF judgment, merits 50; government requests affirmance, merits 47. Neither request supplies an acquittal, release, new guilt trial or immediate execution.

Loving, an Army private at Fort Hood, was convicted of premeditated murder of Bobby Sharbino, felony murder of Christopher Fay, attempted murder and four robbery specifications. Fay was an active-duty soldier driving a taxi for extra money; his killing occurred on Fort Hood. Sharbino was a retired Army sergeant killed in Killeen. The eight-member panel unanimously imposed death, dishonorable discharge and forfeiture of all pay and allowances on April 3, 1989. Overlapping specifications dismissed as multiplicitous are not additional retained convictions. The panel unanimously found three aggravators: premeditated murder during robbery, RCM 1004(c)(7)(B); another Article 118 murder in the same case, (c)(7)(J); and actual perpetration of the felony-murder killing, (c)(8). **Sources:** petitioner merits 3–4; government merits 5–7; petition appendix 8a–9a, 253a–254a.

**Correction 4 — applicable versions and statutory limits.** The governing primary texts are reproduced in JA 132–151. Official 1994 Code copies corroborate the statutes but do not substitute for earlier applicable language.

- Article 118, JA 145: only specified premeditated and felony-murder alternatives in clauses (1) and (4) carry the death-or-life provision. Do not describe all Article 118 murder as capital. The 1992 clause-(3) wording amendment is not the charged alternative.
- Article 18, JA 139: general courts-martial's punishment authority remains under presidential limitations and extends to death only where the Code specifically authorizes it. The separate law-of-war provision and judge-only capital-jurisdiction exception are not the eight-member-panel issue here.
- Article 36, JA 140: presidential procedure/proof rules must, so far as practicable in the President's judgment, apply generally recognized federal criminal principles/evidence rules, and may not conflict with the UCMJ. The applicable pre-1990 subsection (b) requires uniformity insofar as practicable **and reporting to Congress**. The later Code omits that reporting clause. Whether these capital eligibility factors fall within “procedures” is disputed.
- Article 56, JA 141: punishment may not exceed presidentially prescribed limits for the offense. Whether categorical limits on death eligibility fit that authorization is disputed.
- Article 106a, JA 142–144: Congress's specific espionage aggravator provision and reference to additional presidential factors inform opposing statutory arguments; it is not the murder offense of conviction and cannot replace the distinct Article 118 question.
- RCM 1004, JA 132–138, is the pre-1991 version. Death requires an expressly capital offense, unanimous conviction, a specified aggravator proved beyond reasonable doubt and unanimously found, and unanimous agreement that admissible aggravation substantially outweighs extenuation/mitigation. Eligibility and broader sentencing aggravation are distinct. The accused has broad mitigation latitude. Notice is required before arraignment, with the stated late-notice/proof exception unless specific prejudice and inadequacy of a continuance/recess are shown. Later felony-murder amendments are not substituted.
- Article 55 prohibits the specified corporal punishments and other cruel or unusual punishment; irons are allowed only for safe custody. Article 71(a) requires presidential approval before execution and does not permit suspension of the death part; subsection (c) has separate finality requirements. **Sources:** official `UCMJ55_1994` and `UCMJ71_1994` files. Adjudication of this challenge is not approval or an execution order.

**Competing grounds retained.** Petitioner disputes authority to make substantive capital-selection choices, requires a clear congressional delegation, attacks the sufficiency of guidance and asserts impermissible concentration of legislative, prosecutorial, military-judicial and clemency functions. The government invokes Articles 18/36/56 within Congress's own Article 118 punishment authorization, military practice and discipline, civilian principles, statutory safeguards, later legislative treatment and presidential functions. It separately asserts independent Article II power or uses overlapping executive responsibility to support a less demanding delegation inquiry. **Sources:** petitioner merits 3–50; government merits 14–47; reply. These alternatives are arguments, not established statutory conclusions.

Government merits nn.5–7 does not quarrel in this case with Matthews's limited additional-aggravator requirement while preserving the distinction between that assumption and universal incorporation of civilian capital doctrine. It says petitioner does not dispute that these factors would sufficiently narrow if Congress enacted them. A legal assumption for resolving these claims adds no record fact. A free-standing Article II ground remains separate from using the President's military role only to assess an enacted delegation.

Use the image PDF if a precise assertion in a damaged passage becomes material; do not infer the missing words. No historical assertion from those damaged passages is necessary to the corrections stated here.
