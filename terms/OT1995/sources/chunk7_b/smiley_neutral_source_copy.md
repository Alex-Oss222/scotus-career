## OT1995-068 — Smiley v. Citibank (South Dakota), N.A.

**Event:** 1996-06-03 | **Category:** MERITS | **Action:** Argued merits decision

**Citation:** 517 U.S. 735

**Docket(s):** No. 95-860

### SECTION I — NEUTRAL MODELING PACKET

- **Case Name:** Smiley v. Citibank (South Dakota), N.A.
- **Citation / Docket:** 517 U.S. 735; No. 95-860
- **October Term:** 1995
- **Entry Stage / Event Type:** Inventory-authorized merits review; decision pending in this simulation.
- **Question Presented:** Petitioner presents three questions: (1) whether Congress intended South Dakota’s legislative definition of “interest” to define the federal term “interest” in §30 of the National Bank Act, 12 U.S.C. §85, and thereby displace other States’ contract laws limiting the form or the amount of liquidated damages for late payments on revolving credit accounts; (2) whether Congress may constitutionally delegate to South Dakota the power to define the federal lending term “interest” for all fifty States so as to preempt other States’ contract laws; and (3) whether, as a matter of federal law, “interest at the rate” in §85 includes contingent, sum-certain penalty charges (late fees), so as to preempt state limitations on the form or the amount of contractual liquidated damages on revolving credit accounts. Respondent contends that only the third question is actually presented and would phrase it as whether “interest” in §85 includes charges by a national bank for a borrower’s failure to make timely payment of amounts due on credit card accounts.
- **Authorized Review Scope:** The stated questions and the parties’ scope and preservation contentions govern the submitted merits review. Respondent contends that the first two questions are not actually presented (see Preservation / Threshold / Vehicle Issues).
- **Legal Area:** Banking law, federal preemption and administrative law — meaning of “interest” in National Bank Act §30 (12 U.S.C. §85), with related nondelegation and agency-deference questions
- **Court and Judgment Under Review:** Supreme Court of California, 11 Cal. 4th 138, 900 P.2d 690 (judgment entered September 1, 1995), 5–2, affirming the Court of Appeal (26 Cal. App. 4th 1767, decided over a dissent), which had affirmed the Superior Court’s order granting Citibank’s motion for judgment on the pleadings. The Superior Court first denied the motion; on Citibank’s petition for a writ of mandate, the Court of Appeal directed it to grant the motion or show cause, and it then granted the motion. The majority (Justice Mosk) stated that “[t]he issue is not the existence of preemption under section 85,” because under *Marquette* a borrower’s State’s conflicting law “must, of course, give way to the federal statute,” and concluded that §85 “interest” “could include ... a late payment fee, payable contingently in the event of default after maturity,” relying on the legal usage of “interest” in 1864, the Act’s most-favored-lender purpose and the Comptroller of the Currency’s interpretations; it held that §85 permits a national bank to collect such charges if lenders in its home State may, so that contrary California law is preempted. The court also stated that Congress has “entrusted the question of the lawfulness of a national bank’s late payment fees to its home state and to its home state alone,” and that “Congress has made no such delegation” to the States (Pet. App. 30). The parties read the decision differently. Petitioner says it in effect let South Dakota law define “interest” and “rate” and thus the scope of preemption, applied the presumption against preemption only to the existence and not the scope of preemption, and reversed that presumption (“Had Congress intended to limit preemption, it would have doubtless made itself plain”); she also says the court presumably declined to defer to the Comptroller because of inconsistencies while noting agreement with its current position. Citibank says the court decided the federal meaning of “interest” and disclaimed any delegation. Two justices dissented; petitioner describes them as concluding that §85 “interest” does not include the sum-certain contract penalties at issue and that a national bank’s power to impose such charges rests on 12 U.S.C. §24 rather than §85, and Citibank describes the dissent as resting on a “plain meaning” of “interest” limited to periodic percentage charges.
- **Requested Supreme Court Disposition / Relief:** Petitioner asks that the California Supreme Court’s judgment be reversed; respondent asks that it be affirmed. Any decision must stay within the presented issues and source-authorized remedy.
- **Material Facts and Record:** Citibank (South Dakota), N.A. is a national bank located in Sioux Falls, South Dakota, with no branches elsewhere; from there it issues Visa, Mastercard and other credit cards to customers nationwide. Its card agreements generally provide for percentage finance charges on certain balances and for late-payment charges, which may be flat or percentage-based. Petitioner Barbara Smiley, a California resident, held two Citibank cards. Each agreement imposed a monthly finance charge on certain balances (a percentage of the average daily balance or a 50-cent minimum, whichever was larger), which she does not challenge. The Classic Card agreement imposed a $15 late charge if the minimum payment was more than 25 days overdue. The Preferred Card agreement imposed $6 if the minimum payment was more than 15 days overdue and, if still delinquent at 30 days, an additional charge of the greater of $15 or 0.65% of the current balance, assessed again for each succeeding month in which the minimum payment remained more than 30 days overdue. South Dakota law defines interest as “the compensation allowed by law for the use, or forbearance, or detention of money or its equivalent, including without limitation, points, loan origination fees, credit service or carrying charges, charges for unanticipated late payments, and any other charges, direct or indirect, as an incident to or as a condition of the extension of credit” (S.D. Codified Laws §54-3-1), and provides that, unless a maximum is specifically established elsewhere in the code, there is no maximum interest rate or charge when the parties establish it by written agreement (§54-3-1.1). Petitioner does not dispute that Citibank is located only in South Dakota or that South Dakota law permits these charges. On July 7, 1992, she filed a putative class action in Los Angeles Superior Court alleging that the late charges are unlawful penalties under Cal. Civ. Code §1671, which, for consumer contracts, makes a liquidated-damages provision void unless the parties agree on a presumed amount of damage in a case where it would be impracticable or extremely difficult to fix the actual damage; Citibank describes the California standard as limiting late charges to a “fair average compensation” for the lender’s anticipated loss, as estimated in a lender study, and says petitioner seeks a trial on whether the charges meet it. The complaint alleges that the fees bear no relation to the outstanding balance, the amount owed, the number of days late or the bank’s processing costs, are imposed in addition to continuing finance charges, and accompany a contractual default that the bank does not waive even if the fee is paid. Citibank describes them as charges for keeping the bank’s money past its due date that reflect delinquent borrowers’ higher funding, collection and loss costs. The case was decided on the pleadings; there are no findings on the fees’ purpose, relation to cost or market effects. California legislation effective January 1, 1995 (Cal. Fin. Code §§4000–4001) permits banks located in California to assess specified credit-card late charges; Citibank says its charges are consistent with those charges, while petitioner says certain Preferred Card fees are imposed twice per billing period in violation of §4001 and that under the decision below those limits would not apply to out-of-state national banks.
- **Preservation / Threshold / Vehicle Issues:** *Scope of the questions.* Both parties state that the meaning of “interest” in §85 is a question of federal law; Citibank adds that a State can neither enlarge nor shrink the federal definition. Citibank contends that petitioner’s first two questions are therefore not presented, because neither it nor the California Supreme Court claims that South Dakota law defines the federal term, and there has been no delegation. Petitioner replies that the first two issues remain pivotal because Citibank’s reading still lets the home State’s law determine which charges are included in the exported “rate” and thus the preemptive scope of §85. *Posture.* Review is of a judgment on the pleadings; petitioner contends that Citibank’s economic and reliance assertions are outside the record. *The Comptroller’s regulation.* 12 C.F.R. §7.4001(a) (61 Fed. Reg. 4849, 4869) states that “interest” as used in 12 U.S.C. §85 “includes any payment compensating a creditor or prospective creditor for an extension of credit, making available of a line of credit, or any default or breach by a borrower of a condition upon which credit was extended,” including “numerical periodic rates, late fees, not sufficient funds (NSF) fees, overlimit fees, annual fees, cash advance fees, and membership fees,” but ordinarily not appraisal fees, premiums and commissions attributable to insurance guaranteeing repayment, finders’ fees, fees for document preparation or notarization, or fees incurred to obtain credit reports. According to the Comptroller’s preamble (61 Fed. Reg. 4858), paragraph (b) clarifies that one looks to state law to determine what lending charges are permitted for the most favored lender and thus for national banks, and paragraph (c), added in the final rule after comments from Arkansas commenters, confirms that the federal definition does not change a State’s definition of interest (or how it is used) solely for state-law purposes, so that a late fee allowed but not counted as interest under home-State law may be charged and exported but is not treated as interest in evaluating compliance with that State’s usury limits. The rule was proposed on March 3, 1995 (60 Fed. Reg. 11924, 11929), before the California Supreme Court’s September 1, 1995 judgment, which, according to the preamble, cited the agency’s recent interpretive opinions and the proposed rule as consistent with its reasoning. After notice and comment it was published in final form on February 9, 1996, with a stated effective date of April 1, 1996, after that judgment and petitioner’s November 30, 1995 petition. The preamble describes the final rule as substantially identical to the proposal apart from paragraph (c), as reflecting current case law and the Comptroller’s interpretive letters, and as reflecting positions the agency has taken as amicus curiae in pending litigation. *Deference.* Petitioner contends that the regulation, which she says was issued on the eve of argument, receives no deference: in §86 Congress committed private §§85–86 actions to the courts without delegating to the Comptroller authority to expand, contract or define their scope (*Adams Fruit Co. v. Barrett*, 494 U.S. 638), and the question is one of statutory construction that the plain meaning resolves. She argues that the Comptroller’s views have been too inconsistent to warrant deference (*INS v. Cardoza-Fonseca*), citing a June 25, 1964 letter from Comptroller Saxon that, answering an inquiry about charges on consumer auto financing, listed “[c]harges for late payments” among charges “which would not properly be characterized as interest”; a 1988 Deputy Chief Counsel letter treating the home State’s law as defining §85 “interest”; a February 17, 1995 Chief Counsel letter treating federal law as defining “interest” to include loan-related charges, which she says was issued without the notice that 12 U.S.C. §43 requires for preemption opinions; and the final rule’s own description, as she quotes it, of a “significant change.” She argues that the proposal was issued “[i]n direct response to this and related litigation” and that its timing raises the specter of manipulation of pending litigation. In her reply brief she adds that the agency does not justify its interpretation by expertise and rests its definition on lower-court decisions (“current case law”); that the regulation is not a permissible construction because it classifies fees by label rather than substance, draws no principled line between included and excluded fees, does not address prepayment penalties, attorneys’ fees or court costs, and through paragraph (c) treats the same fee as interest for exportation but not for a home-State ceiling; that 12 U.S.C. §93a confers no preemptive rulemaking authority, and the proposal did not describe each affected state law as §43 requires; that deference is inappropriate where preemption of nonconflicting state law is at stake; that any deference must be discounted because the regulation is a post hoc, litigation-inspired reaction to credit-card fee litigation in which the agency appeared as amicus for the banks; and that it cannot be applied retroactively to control this litigation without an express congressional grant of retroactive rulemaking authority (*Bowen v. Georgetown University Hospital*, 488 U.S. 204). These reply contentions postdate Citibank’s brief, and the supplied sources contain no response from Citibank to them. Citibank responds that, where language and purpose do not resolve the question, the Court defers to the responsible agency’s permissible construction and gives the Comptroller’s reasonable reading of an ambiguous National Bank Act term “controlling weight” (*Chevron*; *NationsBank*); that no express delegation of interpretive authority is required because the Comptroller, as exclusive supervisor of national banks, examines and enforces their compliance with §85, and *NationsBank* deferred on that basis (quoting *Clarke* and *Investment Co. Institute v. Camp*); that the agency’s expertise in lending mechanics and banks’ and States’ reliance on its views make deference especially apt; that the notice-and-comment regulation codifies longstanding positions, citing agency letters from 1946 onward on set-up, collection, service and delinquency charges, annual fees and late fees, including a 1988 letter to Iowa specifically addressing Citibank’s late fees; that an agency’s present views receive deference even if they have been revised (*NationsBank*; *Good Samaritan Hospital v. Shalala*); that the 1964 letter did not refer to §85 and, according to the Comptroller, concerned state law, as the California Supreme Court also concluded, and read naturally says only that such charges were allowed in addition to the state percentage rate; that the 1988 letter reached the same conclusion on late fees by a different analytical path, and the agency has never concluded otherwise; and that the “significant change” quotation is misleading because the proposal notice (60 Fed. Reg. 11929) shows the agency did not regard codification as a change in its legal views.
- **Petitioner’s Merits Contentions:** Petitioner argues that the presumption against preemption, grounded in federalism, applies to the scope as well as the existence of preemption and requires a clear statement before §85 displaces evenhanded state contract and consumer-protection law; §85 says “interest at the rate,” not “relating to” loan charges, and §1671 places no ceiling on the interest rate Citibank may charge, so its indirect economic effect on loan pricing creates no conflict. The most-favored-lender principle (*Tiffany*) concerns only the rate of interest, “and not ... the character of contracts” national banks may make (*National Bank v. Johnson*; *Evans v. National Bank*); a national bank’s power to impose late fees derives from its contract and incidental powers under 12 U.S.C. §24, which remain subject to nondiscriminatory state law that does not prohibit or significantly interfere with them (*First National Bank v. Kentucky*; *McClellan v. Chipman*; *Anderson National Bank v. Luckett*). In reply she adds that the Act separately bars a home State’s discrimination against national banks, so a home State could not favor other lenders on late fees in any event. If home-State law determined which charges are “interest,” §85 would give one State unlimited power to set lending law for the nation, raising Article I nondelegation and Article IV concerns that call for a narrow federal construction (*First National Bank v. Dickinson*; *Panama Refining Co. v. Ryan*; *Thomas v. Washington Gas Light Co.*). As a federal term, “interest at the rate” means compensation for the use or forbearance of money measured by time and the amount owed (modern and mid-nineteenth-century dictionaries; *Deputy v. du Pont*); “rate” in §85 refers to time-based charges, and §86’s “twice the amount of interest” shows that Congress distinguished a rate from a sum-certain penalty; and the common law distinguished interest from sum-certain contract penalties, which were governed by liquidated-damages law rather than usury (*Tayloe v. Sandiford*; *Lloyd v. Scott*; *Spain v. Hamilton’s Administrator*; *Loudon v. Taxing District*; *New Orleans Insurance Co. v. Piaggio*; *Merchants’ National Bank v. Sevier*), as she reads *United States v. Texas* to confirm. She also relies on the National Currency Act’s reference to “interest for delay in the payment of money,” the 1864 debates’ references to annual percentage rates, the 1933 discount-rate alternative in §85, DIDA legislative history disclaiming any intent to override state limits on “late charges,” Regulation Z’s separation of late charges from finance charges, and the rule that §§85–86 are strictly construed because they impose penalties (*Tiffany*). She accepts that interest may include periodic percentage rates, definite up-front charges that can be amortized over the loan term, and delay damages based on the unpaid balance and measured by time, and does not contend that interest must always be stated as a percentage. In reply she states that her objection is not to the flat form or the amount of the fees but that they cannot be reckoned at the outset by time and the amount owed, so a bank could not know whether a fee would exceed a percentage ceiling until it was imposed. She reads *Citizens’ National Bank v. Donnell* as turning on compounding and *Daggs v. Phoenix National Bank* as limited to time-based rates, argues that *Greenwood Trust* and the decisions following it erred, and contends that Citibank’s economic and reliance arguments are outside the pleadings record and belong to Congress.
- **Respondent’s Merits Contentions:** Citibank argues that the case presents a single question of statutory construction and does not implicate broader preemption or federalism theory. In 1864, as now, the ordinary legal meaning of “interest” included all agreed compensation “for the use or forbearance of money, or as damages for its detention” (*Brown v. Hiatts*; see *Redfield v. Ystalyfera Iron Co.*; *Shoemaker v. United States*), including new or increased charges triggered by failure to pay on time, as contemporaneous statutes, dictionaries and cases show; courts counted flat charges as interest against percentage ceilings because otherwise those ceilings could be evaded. “Rate” then and now included a “[p]rice or amount stated or fixed on anything” (Webster, 1828), and the discount-rate clause added in 1933 did not narrow the original language. Whether a charge is a “penalty” bears on its enforceability, not on whether it is interest: *Donnell* rejected a national bank’s argument that an overdraft charge was a penalty rather than §85 interest, petitioner’s authorities do not define interest, and *United States v. Texas* addressed only the common-law right to prejudgment interest. Under *Daggs*, which disapproved the reading of *Johnson* that petitioner advances, a national bank in a State that permits any agreed charge has the same federal right, so whether a charge is excessive or noncompensatory is for the home State’s law. The most-favored-lender purpose of protecting national banks from “unfriendly state legislation” (*Tiffany*) requires including such charges, since otherwise a home State could let favored lenders charge late fees while denying them to banks (California formerly allowed state-chartered credit unions a flat $5 late fee while subjecting banks to standards that might impose lower limits), and the 1864 Congress chose “allowed” over “established” to give national banks whatever charges private parties could contract for. Once late fees are interest, *Marquette* establishes that a borrower’s State’s contrary law “must, of course, give way”; the presumption against preemption is one guide to congressional intent, not a clear-statement rule, and deserves little weight because no significant historic police power of the borrower’s State is at stake, borrowers having long obtained credit from out-of-state lenders on terms governed by the lender’s law. Citibank reads petitioner as acknowledging that percentage charges, including increased default percentages, and up-front flat charges are §85 interest, and argues that this leaves no principled basis for reserving flat default charges to the borrower’s State, as the Preferred Card’s “$15 or 0.65%” charge illustrates, and that Congress did not intend to fragment regulation of a single lending package. DIDA, whose operative language parallels §85, and TILA’s disclosure regime are irrelevant to §85’s meaning. Citibank also points to intense competition among card issuers and to the benefits of differentiated charge packages for timely payers and borrowers with weak credit histories; petitioner responds that these assertions are unsupported in the pleadings record.

**MCI Telecommunications**

**Current rule:** Section 203(b)(2) permits the Commission, in its discretion and for good cause shown, to modify requirements made by or under §203 in particular instances or by general order applicable to special circumstances or conditions; no required notice period may exceed 120 days. That authority permits adjustments to filing form, contents, timing or place, including consequential and class-wide technical changes, but not replacement of mandatory filing throughout the nondominant long-distance sector with an optional system. The inquiry examines the obligation and whether its essential operation is adjusted or removed, not a numerical market-share threshold. Good cause alone cannot authorize the replacement. The Act’s exception for connecting carriers preserves their separate inspection duties; §203(c)’s allowance for otherwise lawful authority does not confer unlimited dispensing power.

**John Hancock**

**Current rule:** A guaranteed-benefit policy itself is a plan asset; the insurer’s underlying assets are not deemed plan assets solely because it issued the policy only to the extent the policy genuinely guarantees the amount of benefits. A nonguaranteed component does not gain the exclusion from general-account placement. Plan-asset status subjects the insurer to §3(21)(A) fiduciary duties to the extent it exercises the specified authority or control over management or disposition; breach, causation, loss and remedy remain separate. McCarran-Ferguson does not displace ERISA’s express insurance-related duties. Compatible state insurance regulation survives the saving clause, but a concrete conflicting obligation cannot control over ERISA itself. An agency interpretation cannot erase the exclusion’s component limitation.

**Good Samaritan Hospital**

**Current rule** (S2, “Medicare corrective adjustments and permissible interpretive change”; present operation effective 1993-06-07): Section 1395x(v)(1)(A)(ii) commands suitable retroactive corrective adjustments when aggregate reimbursement for a provider and period proves inadequate or excessive. The Secretary may construe that command as reconciling interim payments to sums payable under lawful reasonable-cost methods and ceilings, without an individualized exception whenever actual costs exceed the ceiling. Authorized exceptions and independent challenges to unlawful methods remain. Interpretive change can reduce weight without automatically invalidating a considered permissible construction; text, delegation and reasons must be examined. Unsupported litigation positions neither replace required agency explanation nor gain automatic controlling weight.

**NationsBank**

**Holding:** The business of banking under 12 U.S.C. §24 Seventh is not exhausted by its enumerated activities, and the Comptroller's reasonable construction may include financial-intermediary agency sales of the fixed, variable and combined annuity products covered by this approval. The construction must remain within banking and its incidental powers and comply with enacted restrictions; this conditioned brokerage approval authorizes no underwriting, issuance or guarantee of the insurers' obligations and no unrelated commercial enterprise.

**Proposition-level authority:** Opinion of the Court (Souter), eight Justices; Stone-Zsela concurs in both judgments only.

**Treatment of earlier authority:** Clarke v. Securities Industry Association, 479 U.S. 388 — applies its substantial respect for reasonable Comptroller banking-law construction and brokerage analysis to this agency activity. Chevron, 467 U.S. 837 — applies deference within the ambiguity left after the enacted banking boundaries are determined; clear prohibitions still govern. Investment Company Institute v. Camp, 401 U.S. 617 — preserves substantive banking restrictions and the need for an actual reasoned agency judgment. MCI and Brown v. Gardner — preserve text-first review and actual statutory limits; neither displaces permissible construction within a genuine delegation.

**Holding:** The Comptroller reasonably classifies the annuity agency products covered by this approval as investment instruments outside the insurance category of 12 U.S.C. §92 for this statutory purpose, so §92 does not invalidate the approval. The Court does not decide whether §92 exclusively limits genuine insurance-agency activity to banks in qualifying small places, and it neither repeals that provision's conditions nor determines classification under a distinct state insurance law.

**Proposition-level authority:** Opinion of the Court (Souter), eight Justices; Stone-Zsela concurs in both judgments only.

**Limits and questions not reached:** The Comptroller's approval separately requires advertising, promotional materials and customer descriptions to disclose that the annuities are not bank products and are not FDIC insured, together with a signed purchaser acknowledgment of agency status and lack of federal insurance. Brokerage does not issue, underwrite or guarantee an insurer's undertaking. Applicable banking, securities and insurance requirements otherwise remain.

When §92 applies, it requires the national bank to be located and doing business in a place of no more than 5,000 inhabitants under the last preceding decennial census; the insurer must be authorized by the State where the bank is located; and the agency must operate under Comptroller rules. It permits solicitation and sale of insurance and collection of premiums for agreed fees or commissions, but prohibits the bank from assuming or guaranteeing premium payment and separately prohibits guaranteeing the truth of the insured applicant's statements. Those statutory terms are not the same as this approval's disclosure conditions. Section 24's distinction between without-recourse, customer-order/customer-account securities transactions and own-account activity remains; no own-account investment exception is adjudicated. No individual customer's contract, universal annuity classification or separate state-law preemption dispute is resolved.

**Town & Country (earlier effective OT1995 decision)**

**Operative rule:** A worker who performs or seeks ordinary compensated service for an employer is not excluded from NLRA §2(3) merely because a union also pays the worker to organize. After independent examination of the statutory boundary and compatible common-law service, the Board permissibly treats union pay and organizing obligations as consistent with employee status.

**Agency-review sequence as applied:** “At Chevron's first step, the statute does not unambiguously impose the employers' categorical exclusion. MCI requires that judicial inquiry before deference. Within the resulting statutory range, the Board reasonably regards compatible work and organizing as simultaneous service.” Precedent treatment: Chevron — “applied only after independent identification of the statutory range”; MCI — “preserves judicial statutory limits.”

**Proposition-level authority:** NLRB v. Town & Country Electric, Inc., 516 U.S. 85, November 28, 1995 (OT1995 Record `terms/OT1995/records/NLRB_v_Town_and_Country_Electric_Inc_merits_1995-11-28.md`, Public Projection). Justice Ginsburg’s Opinion of the Court, joined in full by Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas and Breyer; 9–0.

**Limits:** The decision construes NLRA §2(3); it states no rule specific to banking statutes, preemption or retroactive agency rules.
