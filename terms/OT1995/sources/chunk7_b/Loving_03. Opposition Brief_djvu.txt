STATEMENT 


Petitioner was convicted by a general court-martial 
of premeditated murder, felony murder, attempted 
murder, and robbery (four specifications), in violation 
of Articles 118, 80, and 122, Uniform Code of Military 
Justice (UCMJ), 10 U.S.C. 918, 880, and 922. The 
court sentenced petitioner to death, a dishonorable 
discharge, and forfeiture of all pay and allowances. 
The United States Army Court of Military Review 
affirmed. 34 M.J. 956, reconsideration denied, 34 M.J. 
1065 (A.C.M.R. 1992) (Pet. App. 229a-265a). The 
United States Court Appeals for the Armed Forces 
affirmed. Pet. App. la-227a. 

1. In United States v. Matthews, 16 M.J. 354 
(C.M.A. 1983), the United States Court of Military 
Appeals (now the United States Court of Appeals for 
the Armed Forces) analyzed the constitutionality of 
the military’s death penalty provisions, Article 118, 
UCMJ, 10 U.S.C. 918. After reviewing this Court’s 
cases, the court concluded that certain common fea- 
tures appeared in a constitutionally valid death penal- 
ty procedure: a bifurcated sentencing proceeding; spe- 
cific aggravating circumstances; selection of and 
findings on the particular aggravating circumstances 
used by the sentencer to impose the death penalty; 
unrestricted opportunity for the defendant to present 
mitigation and extenuation evidence; and mandatory 


appellate review of the appropriateness of the sen- 
tence. /d. at 377. 

The Matthews court found all of those safeguards 
present in the military system except for specific 
aggravating factors.' The court held that because 
court-martial mem!ers were not required to identify 
specifically the aggravating factors relied upon in 
assessing the death sentence, it was impossible for 
appellate courts to determine whether the members 
had made an individualized sentencing determination 
based on the character of the defendant and the 
circumstances of the crime. 16 M.J. at 379. The court 
indicated that either Congress or the President, in 
his capacity as Commander in Chief and/or pursuant 
to the powers that Congress delegated to him, could 
take corrective action. Jd. at 380 (citing Art. 36, 
UCM, 10 U.S.C. 836). 


! The court noted (1) that the military follows a bifurcated 
sentencing procedure; (2) that “(cljertain aggravating circum- 
stances, such as premeditation, specific intent, and murder 
during commission of specified felonies, must be found by the 
court members * * *. These findings identify the instances in 
which an accused is eligible for the death penalty. After the 
findings, evidence may be submitted to identify other 
aggravating circumstances.” 16 M.J. at 378 (citation omitted); 
(3) that the defendant has an unlimited opportunity to put on 
evidence in extenuation and mitigation; (4) that there is 
mandatery review of the facts, law, and sentence appro- 
priateness followed by a mandatory review of the law by the 
Court of Military Appeals (now the Court of Appeals for the 
Armed Forces); and (5) that the President, who can take any 
lesser action on the sentence, must ultimately approve any death 
sentence. Jhid. See generally A. Sullivan, The President's 
Power to Promulgate Death Penalty Standards, 125 Mil. L. 
Rev. 143 (1989). 


The President responded by promulgating Rule for 
Courts-Martial (RCM) 1004, see Pet. App. 354a-360a 
(reproducing RCM 1004), which amended the Manual 
for Courts-Martial to provide procedures for adjud- 
icating cases in which the death penalty is authorized 
for homicide by Article 118 of the Uniform Code of 
Military Justice, 10 U.S.C. 918. See Manual for 
Courts-Martial, United States, 1984 (Manual). RCM 
1004, which had been circulated for public comment 
before the decision in Matthews, rectified the 
deficiency identified in that opinion by enumerating 
specific aggravating factors, at least one of which the 
court-martial members must unanimously find in 
order to impose the death penalty. RCM 1004 also 
provides that all members of the court-martial -must 
concur that the aggravating circumstance(s) substan- 
tially outweigh any extenuating or mitigating cir- 
cumstances before death can be adjudged. The Presi- 
dent caused the 1984 Manual for Courts-Martial and 
its Rules for Courts-Martial to be issued by “virtue of 
the authority invested in [him] as President by the 
Constitution of the United States and by Chapter 47 
of Title 10 of the United States Code [i.e., the 
UCMJ].” Exec. Order No. 12,473, 3 C.F.R. 201 (1984 
comp.). 

2. During the evening of December 11, 1988, 
petitioner, an Army private stationed at Fort Hood, 
Texas, robbed at gun point two different 7-Eleven 
convenience stores in Killeen, Texas. Pet. App. 3a, 
231a; Pet. 4-5. In the first robbery, petitioner fired 
two shots into the counter and soda fountain, which 
were behind the cashier; he obtained $38. In the 
second robbery, petitioner fired a bullet into the cash 
register, and demanded that the cashier put money in 


a paper bag. As he ran out, petitioner fired a round 
back into the store. He obtained approximately $52 
from the second robbery /d. at 3a, 231a. 

When the 7-Eleven robberies netted him only a 
small amount of cash, petitioner conceived a plan to 
rob taxicab drivers. On the evening of December 12, 
1988, petitioner called a taxicab to take him from a 
store in Killeen to his barracks in Fort Hood. The 
driver of the taxicab was an active-duty -soldier, 
Christopher Fay, who was working for extra money. 


Petitioner directed Fay to a secluded area on vet 


Hood, where he demanded Fay’s money at gun poin 
After receiving some money from Fay, petitioner shot 
him in the back of the head. While looking at the 
bleeding wound, petitioner cocked his pistol and again 
shot Fay in the back of the head. Fay died. Pet. App. 
da-4a, 231la-232a. 

Petitioner fled to his barracks and, after counting 
the money he obtained from Fay, called a second 
taxicab. That cab was driven by a retired Army 
sergeant, Bobby Sharbino. Petitioner directed Shar- 
bino to a secluded street in Killeen, where he robbed 
Sharbino of his money pouch, wallet, and other 
personal effects. Petitioner then ordered Sharbino to 
lie down on a car seat and shot him in the head, killing 
him. Pet. App. 4a, 232a. 

Later that evening, petitioner took his girlfriend to 
a nightclub in Killeen. Upon leaving, petitioner and 
his girlfriend entered a taxicab driven by Howard 
Harrison. After dropping his girlfriend off near her 
residence, petitioner directed Harrison to a secluded 
area and robbed him at gun point. Petitioner then 
attempted to kill Harrison. Harrison struggled with 


petitioner, disarmed him, and fled. Pet. App. 4a-5a, 
232a-233a. 

Acting on leads provided by Harrison, law 
enforcement agents, including agents of the Army 
Criminal Investigation Command (CID), arrested 
petitioner in the afternoon of December 13, 1988. 
Petitioner was taken to the CID office, where, after 
being advised of his rights, he made a full videotaped 
confession describing his crimes in detail. Searches 
of the area surrounding petitioner’s girlfriend’s resi- 
dence, and of the residence and other places frequent- 
ed by petitioner, resulted in the recovery of the 
weapon used in the murders and robberies, spent and 
unspént bullets, petitioner’s blood-stained jacket, and 
effects taken from the murder victims. Pet. App. 232a 
n.2, 233a, 5a, 7a. Later, while incarcerated at the U.S. 
Army facility at Fort Riley, Kansas, petitioner told a 
fellow inmate that he had committed the first murder 
“to see if he could get away with it,” and the second 
murder “because it was fun,” and that “if he had to do 
it over, the only difference is he wouldn’t get caught.” 
Id. at 68a-69a. 

An eight-member court-martial unanimously found 
petitioner guilty of the premeditated murder of 
Sharbino and of the felony murder of Fay. By a non- 
unanimous vote, the court-martial also found peti- 
tioner guilty of attempting to murder Harrison, of 
robbing Sharbino and Harrison, and of robbing the 7- 
Eleven stores. Pet. App. 8a-9a.° After a hearing 


conducted in accordance with RCM 1004, the court- 


martial unanimously found, as aggravating factors, 


that the premeditated murder of Sharbino was com- 
mitted while petitioner was engaged in a robbery (see 
RCM 1004(c)(7)), that in the felony murder of Fay 
petitioner acted as the triggerman (see RCM 
1004(c)(8)), and that petitioner, having been found 
guilty of the premeditated murder of Sharbino, was 
found guilty of another murder in the same case (see 
RCM 1004(c)(7)(J)). Pet. App. 253a-254a. The court- 
martial unanimously sentenced petitioner to death, a 
dishonorable discharge, and forfeiture of all pay and 
allowances. The convening authority approved the 
findings and sentence. Pet. App. 9a, 230a." 

3. The Army Court of Military Review affirmed 
the findings and sentence. Pet. App. 229a-256a. In 
accordance with Article 66, UCMJ, 10 U.S.C. 866, the 
court independently weighed the evidence and 
“agree[d| with each of the aggravating factors found

found “that any extenuating and mitigating circum-

vating circumstances.” IJbid.

does not sufficiently narrow the class of death-

his authority under the Constitution and the UCMJ

The Curtis court concluded that under the Uniform 
Code of Military Justice, the President has long 
“play[ed] the major role in determining what punish- 
ments may be imposed by courts-martial.” Pet. App. 
362a-363a. It held that several provisions of the

Relying on Mistretta v. United States, 488 U.S. 361

Petitioner also argued that only a court-martial 
panel composed of 12 members can constitutionally 


impose the death penalty. The Army Court of 
Military Review found that claim meritless on the

that claim on the grounds (1) that the jury-trial

rejected, on the authority of Curtis and Matthews,

ARGUMENT 


1. Petitioner contends that Congress has not 
explicitly or implicitly delegated to the President any 
authority to prescribe aggravating factors (Pet. 14- 
15), and, if Congress has done so, such delegation is 
unconstitutional because that authority may not be 
delegated at all (Pet. 9-11), because the delegation 
impermissibly combines in the President the power to 
make and enforce laws (Pet. 11-14), or because Con- 
gress failed to set forth sufficient standards to guide 


10 


the President’s exercise of his authority (Pet. 19-23). 
Those claims lack merit and do not warrant review. 

a. In Furman v. Georgia, 408 U.S. 238 (1972), this 
Court held that the death penalty cannot be imposed 
under procedures that create a substantial risk that 
it will be inflicted in an arbitrary manner. See, e.g., 
McCleskey v. Kemp, 481 U.S. 279, 301 (1987); 
California v. Brown, 479 U.S. 538, 541 (1987); Gregg 
v. Georgia, 428 U.S. 153, 188 (1976) (lead opinion). 
Contrary to petitioner’s contention (Pet. 8-9), Fur- 
man did not hold unconstitutional Congress’s judg- 
ment that the death penalty is a lawful punishment 
for premeditated or felony murder in the military 
justice system. See Art. 118, UCMJ, 10 U.S.C. 918. 
Furman requires only that appropriate safeguards 
exist in order for Article 118 to be implemented in a 
constitutional manner. Compare Dobbert v. Florida, 
432 U.S. 282, 293-294, 297-298 (1977) (rejecting argu- 
ment that there “was no death penalty ‘in effect’ in 
Florida” at the time of defendant’s crime, because 
Florida had not yet acted to comply with the dictates 
of Furman; upholding retroactive application of 
Florida’s post-Furman capital sentencing law on the 
ground that the changes were merely “procedural”). 

The President had ample authority to prescribe the 
safeguards that will result in the constitutional 
application of the statute that Congress enacted. In 
addition to his constitutional authority as “Com- 
mander in Chief of the Army and Navy,” U.S. Const. 
Art. II, § 2, Cl. 1, three provisions of the Uniform 
Code of Military Justice make clear that the Presi- 
dent has the power to devise sentencing procedures 
for capital cases. Article 18, UCMJ, 10 U.S.C. 818, 
which sets forth the jurisdiction of general courts- 


1] 


martial, provides that such courts-martial “may, 
under such limitations as the President may pre- 
scribe, adjudge any punishment not forbidden by this 
chapter.” Article 36, UCMJ, 10 U.S.C. 836, empowers 
the President to prescribe “trial{] and post-trial 
procedures * * * for cases triable in courts-martial” 
by regulations that “shall, so far as [the President} 
considers practicable, apply the principles of law 
* * * generally recognized in the trial of criminal 
cases in the United States district courts.” And 
Article 56, UCMJ, 10 U.S.C. 856, authorizes the 
President to limit the maximum punishment a court- 
martial may impose. See, e.g., United States v. 
Prescott, 2 C.M.A. 122, 124-125, 6 C.M.R. 122, 124-125 
(1952). Together, those Articles authorize the Presi- 
dent to prescribe whatever procedures are necessary 
to assure that Article 118 can be implemented consti- 
tutionally. 

Any doubt as to whether the UCMJ delegates to the 
President authority to promulgate RCM 1004 was 
eliminated by Congress’s enactment in 1985 of Article 
106a, UCMJ, 10 U.S.C. 906a, which makes it a capital 
offense for a servicemember to engage in espionage. 
That provision lists three specific aggravating fac-
