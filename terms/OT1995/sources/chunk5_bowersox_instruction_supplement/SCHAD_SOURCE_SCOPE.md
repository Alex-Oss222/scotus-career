# Schad: scope of the Beck instruction holding

**Authority:** *Schad v. Arizona*, 501 U.S. 624 (1991), decided June 21, 1991. Part III, 645–648, is the opinion of the Court, authored by Justice Souter. Its status differs from the plurality's separate verdict-specificity analysis in Parts I–II. Source: [official U.S. Reports PDF](SCHAD_USREPORTS-501-624.pdf); [complete extracted text](SCHAD_USREPORTS-501-624.txt).

**Holding.** The jury's evidence-supported option to convict of noncapital second-degree murder satisfied *Beck v. Alabama*, 447 U.S. 625 (1980), despite the absence of a robbery instruction. *Schad* rejected the asserted entitlement under *Beck* to instructions on every evidence-supported lesser included noncapital offense. 501 U.S. at 645–648. It did not hold that any nominal lesser-offense instruction suffices.

**Material setting.** The prosecution submitted premeditated murder and robbery felony murder. The defense maintained that the circumstantial evidence established theft of the victim's property, not participation in the killing. The jury received verdict forms for first-degree murder, second-degree murder, and acquittal. The trial court had refused a requested theft instruction; the preserved claim in the Supreme Court concerned robbery, which the petitioner characterized as included within robbery murder. Id. at 628–630, 645 & n.10.

**Reason and precise reach.** *Beck* addressed the distortion created by an all-or-nothing choice between capital conviction and acquittal: jurors convinced of some serious crime might convict capitally to avoid releasing its perpetrator. Here the jurors had a noncapital conviction option. The Court addressed the defense's specific objection that second-degree murder could not express its theory that someone else killed the victim. It reasoned that jurors unconvinced of either degree of murder, but convinced of robbery and unwilling to acquit, would have no rational basis to select capital murder instead of second-degree murder. The second-degree instruction therefore sufficed to ensure reliability in this case. Id. at 646–648.

The Court immediately limited that reasoning: Beck is not necessarily satisfied by “just any lesser included offense, even one without any support in the evidence.” The petitioner conceded that the evidence supported second-degree murder; the Court relied on that concession. Id. at 648. The decision thus rejects a requirement for a separate noncapital option corresponding to each prosecution theory in these circumstances, while preserving the requirement of evidentiary support for the option actually supplied.

**Separate writing and reserved matters.** Justice White's dissent argued that second-degree murder was included only within the premeditation theory, leaving felony murder without a legitimate intermediate option. He would require robbery instructions because robbery was an element of felony murder and the evidence supported robbery without murder; he proposed lesser-offense instructions for each theory advanced by the prosecution. Id. at 660–662 & nn.5–6. That position is dissenting, not the Court's rule. Justice Scalia expressly joined Part III without a separate limitation on its Beck analysis. Id. at 652.

The Court resolved the federal instruction claim without deciding whether robbery actually qualified as a lesser included offense under Arizona law; it described that status as petitioner's characterization. It also expressly declined to decide whether the theft-instruction claim had been waived. Id. at 645 & n.10. This source does not decide an instruction claim where no evidence-supported noncapital lesser option was supplied, nor establish an across-the-board right to all lesser offenses. It supplies no facts or disposition for any other case.
