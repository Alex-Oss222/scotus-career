# Foman v. Davis — Rule 15(a) source scope

Source: *Foman v. Davis*, 371 U.S. 178 (1962), decided December 3, 1962. Official United States Reports PDF: <https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/USREPORTS-371/pdf/USREPORTS-371-178.pdf>. Local source: [FOMAN_USREPORTS-371-178.pdf](FOMAN_USREPORTS-371-178.pdf); complete text extraction: [FOMAN_USREPORTS-371-178.txt](FOMAN_USREPORTS-371-178.txt). Citations below use printed reporter pages; PDF pages 1–6 correspond to reporter pages 178–183.

This is a source-only statement of Foman's rule and limits. It supplies no conclusion about the pending matter, no attributed lower-court rationale in that matter, and no judicial commitments. The Rule 15(a) wording quoted here is the wording reproduced in the 1962 opinion.

## Rule and application actually stated in Foman

The Court stated:

> Rule 15 (a) declares that leave to amend “shall be freely given when justice so requires”; this mandate is to be heeded.

371 U.S. at 182. It continued:

> If the underlying facts or circumstances relied upon by a plaintiff may be a proper subject of relief, he ought to be afforded an opportunity to test his claim on the merits. In the absence of any apparent or declared reason—such as undue delay, bad faith or dilatory motive on the part of the movant, repeated failure to cure deficiencies by amendments previously allowed, undue prejudice to the opposing party by virtue of allowance of the amendment, futility of amendment, etc.—the leave sought should, as the rules require, be “freely given.”

Id. The qualification matters: the liberal amendment rule coexists with reasons that can justify denial, expressly including **undue delay**. Foman's list is illustrative, as its “such as” and “etc.” indicate. Its concluding formulation preserves district-court discretion while requiring a justification:

> Of course, the grant or denial of an opportunity to amend is within the discretion of the District Court, but outright refusal to grant the leave without any justifying reason appearing for the denial is not an exercise of discretion; it is merely abuse of that discretion and inconsistent with the spirit of the Federal Rules.

Id.

Foman's application concerned a complaint dismissed on statute-of-frauds grounds, followed the next day by motions to vacate and amend to seek quantum meruit recovery for performance of the obligations underlying the asserted oral agreement. Id. at 179. The Court stated that “the amendment would have done no more than state an alternative theory for recovery.” Id. at 182. It held erroneous the affirmance of the denial of the motion to vacate in order to permit that amendment. Id. The separate appeal-notice holding appears at pages 181–182 and does not supply an additional amendment restriction. The separate memorandum at page 183 would have left the amendment issue with the court of appeals; it supplies no alternative substantive undue-delay test.

## An actual stated reason and an apparent justification are distinct

The controlling language is “apparent or declared reason,” followed by “any justifying reason appearing for the denial.” Id. at 182. A **declared reason** is one the deciding court actually gave, whose attribution requires a source for that statement. An **apparent justification** is a justification discernible from the supported record under the rule, and must be identified as such rather than attributed as the deciding court's actual reasoning.

Foman therefore does not make an expressly articulated reason the only possible justification. Equally, an unknown actual rationale does not establish either that the court articulated none or that no justification could appear from the record. A source gap about the denial order must remain a source gap. Foman's own discussion does not authorize inventing findings, motives, prejudice, or a rationale to bridge it.

At page 181, the opinion recounts the court of appeals' view that the record did not show the circumstances before the district court and that this prevented a finding of abuse. That is the opinion's account of the court of appeals' reasoning; it is not an announcement that a missing account of district-court reasons invariably forecloses review. The Court proceeded to examine the record and apply the Rule 15(a) standard at page 182.

## What the supplied timing facts permit one to examine

The parent research instruction reports that the amendment was requested after four years, discovery was complete, a magistrate's report and recommendation had issued, and the court was ready to rule. These propositions are **supplied record descriptions, not facts independently verified by this Foman-only extraction**. The actual rationale for denying amendment is reported as unknown. Foman contains none of these facts concerning the pending matter.

If independently supported in the neutral record, those timing and procedural-stage facts can be considered in examining the expressly recognized possible justifications of undue delay and undue prejudice. A permissible analytical inquiry can ask what the amendment would add at that stage, whether further proceedings would be necessary, what the record shows about the timing and any explanation for it, and whether the record establishes prejudice attributable to allowing the amendment. Those are analytical questions developed from the rule, not findings or a mandatory checklist prescribed by Foman.

Foman does not specify a period that automatically constitutes undue delay, hold that completed discovery or a pending recommendation establishes such delay, or decide the significance of this combination of procedural events. Thus “four years” and the reported stage of proceedings must not silently become findings of **undue** delay, bad faith, dilatory motive, or undue prejudice. Nor do those facts establish that the district court actually relied on any of these grounds. Conversely, the unknown actual rationale alone must not silently become a finding that no apparent justification exists. This source note does not decide whether the supplied circumstances amount to a sufficient apparent justification.

The unresolved propositions remain the content of the actual denial rationale and whatever additional facts are necessary to evaluate a claimed apparent justification. Their significance can be assessed only within the supported record and applicable review rules. This note supplies neither an outcome nor a remedy for the pending matter.
