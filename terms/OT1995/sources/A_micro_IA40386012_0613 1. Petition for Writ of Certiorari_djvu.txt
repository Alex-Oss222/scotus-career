QUESTIONS PRESENTED FOR REVIEW 


After a doctor misdiagnosed infant Joseph Hodge’s 
bone infection as “suspected child abuse”, the Carroll 
County (Maryland) Department of Social Services “ruled 
out” physical child abuse, but maintained a hard-copy 
file on the case and computer-listings on its Automated 
Master File (“AMF”). Respondent State officials then 
repeatedly denied petitioners David and Marsha Hodge 
(parents of Joseph) access to the file and the AMF data, 
assuring them that: (i) they were exonerated of child 
abuse; and (ii) they were not entitled to a hearing under 
Maryland Code, Family Law § 5-715, because the AMF is 
supposedly not a “central registry” and they (the 
Hodges) were supposedly not “suspects”. For nearly 
three years, however, the AMF secretly listed the case as 
“indicated” for sexual child abuse. Moreover, respon- 
dents knew that the AMF is a “central registry” and did 
treat the adult Hodges as “suspects”, but denied those 
facts as part of an illegal policy aimed at defeating the 
rights of the Hodges (and thousands of others) to a 
§ 5-715 hearing. Under these circumstances, 


I. Did respondents’ conduct in maintaining false 
AMF listings of “indicated” sexual abuse, denying a 
§ 5-715 hearing, and misrepresenting the facts so shock 
the conscience and offend the community’s sense of fair 
play and decency as to violate the Hodges’ rights of 
substantive due process? 


ii 
QUESTIONS PRESENTED FOR REVIEW - Continued 


II. Did respondents’ maintenance of a file and AMF 
listings on a case with no evidence : any child abuse, 
and secret maintenance of false AMF listings of “indi- 
cated” sexual abuse, abridge the Hodges’ rights of famil- 
ial privacy? 


III. Did respondents’ illegal policy of falsely deny- 
ing that the AMF is a “central registry” and that the 
Hodges (and others) are “suspects”, so as to evade the 
adjudicatory procedure mandated by § 5-715, deprive the 
adult Hodges of due process of law under Logan v. Zim- 
merman Brush Co., 455 U.S. 422 (1982)? 


IV. Do respondents have qualified immunity from 
liability for damages under 42 U.S.C. § 1983, where they 
lacked discretionary authority under Maryland law to 
engage in any of the actions described above, and where 
those actions: 


A. violated the Hodges’ rights to substan- 
tive due process; 


B. abridged the Hodges’ rights of familial 
privacy; and 


C. deprived the adult Hodges of due pro- 
cess of law under Logan v. Zimmerman Brush Co.? 


V. Are the Hodges’ claims for declaratory and 
injunctive relief moot, where: 


A. respondents maintained false AMF list- 
ings of “indicated” sexual child abuse on the 
Hodges for almost three years;

Maryland law to keep AMF listings “confiden-

C. the Court of Appeals erroneously ruled

D. nothing prevents respondents from

VI. Are the Hodges entitled to expunction of the

relevant provisions of law appears in App. at 82a.

Qualified immunity “is an accommodation by the 
courts to the ‘conflicting concerns’ of, on one hand, gov- 
ernment officials seeking freedom from personal mone- 
tary liability and harassing litigation and, on the other 
hand, injured persons seeking redress for the abuse of 
official power.” Hodorowski v. Ray, 844 F.2d 1210, 1216 (Sth 
Cir. 1988) (quoting Anderson v. Creighton, 483 U.S. 635, 638 
(1987)). As such, “whether an official protected by quali- 
fied immunity may be held personally liable for an 
allegedly unlawful official action generally turns on the 
‘objective legal reasonableness’ of the action . . . assessed 
in light of the legal rules that were ‘clearly established’ at 
the time it was taken.” Anderson, 483 U.S. at 639 (quoting 
Harlow v. Fitzgerald, 457 U.S. 800, 818-19 (1982)). We have

IV. 


In conclusion, the Hodges have not demonstrated a 
violation of any federal constitutional or statutory right 
of familial privacy. The confines of that right were not so 
clearly established that, even if Defendants’ acts did 
impinge the Hodge family’s zone of privacy, they could 
objectively or reasonably have known that their conduct 
violated the Due Process Clause. The absence of any

A. Eleventh Amendment Immunity 


The Eleventh Amendment provides the States and 
their agents with absolute immunity from suit in the 
federal courts. Edelman v. Jordan, 415 U.S. 651, 662-63, 94 
S.Ct. 1347, 1355-56, 39 L.Ed.2d 662 (1974). When consider- 
ing whether that immunity precludes federal claims 
against a particular local entity, courts must examine the 
essential nature of that entity under state law. Mt. Healthy 
City Bd. of Educ. v. Doyle, 429 U.S. 274, 280, 97 S.Ct. 568, 
572-73, 50 L.Ed.2d 471 (1977). 


The United States Court of Appeals for the Fourth 
Circuit already has examined Maryland’s social services 
system in the context of Eleventh Amendment immunity, 
and has concluded that the county departments of social 
services are agencies of the State for Eleventh Amend- 
ment purposes. Keller v. Prince George's County, 923 F.2d 
30, 32 (4th Cir.1991); see also Williams v. Anderson, 753 
F.Supp. 1306, 1311 (D.Md.1990) (holding Kent County 
Department of Social Services immune from suit under 
the Eleventh Amendment). Accordingly, the Eleventh 
Amendment precludes this action against CCDSS. 


To the extent that the plaintiffs seek monetary dam- 
ages, the Eleventh Amendment also precludes claims in 
federal court against the individual defendants in their 
official capacities. Edelman, 415 U.S. at 663, 94 S.Ct. at 
1355-56. However, an exception to immunity exists for 
federal claims for purely prospective equitable relief, 
even if such claims might have an ancillary effect on the 
State Treasury. See Quern v. Jordan, 440 U.S. 332, 337, 99 
S.Ct. 1139, 1143, 59 L.Ed.2d 358 (1979) (citing Ex Parte 
Young, 209 U.S. 123, 28 S.Ct. 441, 52 L.Ed. 714 (1908)). 


30a 


Finally, absolute immunity under the Eleventh Amend- 
ment does not extend in any event to individual state 
officials sued in their individual capacities. 


In this case, the plaintiffs have named Jones, Katz, 
and Colvin in both their official and individual capacities. 
Within the Eleventh Amendment framework outlined 
above, the plaintiffs may seek equitable relief against the 
individual defendants in their official capacities and 
monetary relief against those defendants in their individ- 
ual capacities. Accordingly, this Court will not grant the 
defendants’ motion to dismiss those claims based on 
Eleventh Amendment immunity.

42 U.S.C. § 1983 


Section 1983 provides for the imposition of liability 
on any person who, acting under color of state law, 
deprives another of rights, privileges, or immunities 
secured by the Constitution or the laws of the United 
States. To state a claim under § 1983, the plaintiffs must 
show both (1) that the offending conduct was committed 
by a person acting under color of state law, and (2) that 
such conduct deprived the plaintiffs of rights secured by 
the Constitution or laws of the United States. Parratt v. 
Taylor, 451 U.S. 527, 535, 101 S.Ct. 1908, 1912-13, 68 
L.Ed.2d 420 (1981), overruled in part on other grounds, 
Daniels v. Williams, 474 U.S. 327, 106 S.Ct. 662, 88 L.Ed.2d 
662 (1986). The parties in this case agree that the individ- 
ual defendants at all times acted “under color of state 
law.” 


Section 1983 “ ‘is not itself a source of substantive 
rights,’ but merely provides ‘a method for vindicating 
federal rights elsewhere conferred.’ ” Graham v. Connor, 
490 U.S. 386, 393-94, 109 S.Ct. 1865, 1870, 104 L.Ed.2d 443 
(1989) (quoting Baker v. McCollan, 443 U.S. 137, 144 U.S. 
137, 144 n. 3, 99 S.Ct. 2689, 2694 n. 3, 61 L.Ed.2d 433 
(1979)). In particular combination with the Due Process 
Clause of the Fourteenth Amendment, § 1983 allows 
plaintiffs to assert three kinds of federal claims: (1) claims 
for the deprivation by state officials of any of the specific 
protections defined in the Bill of Rights; (2) claims under 
the substantive component of the Due Process Clause 
relating to certain arbitrary wrongful government action; 
and (3) claims under the procedural component of the 
Due Process Clause relating to deprivations of life, lib- 
erty, or property without due process of law. Zinermon v.

Burch, 494 U.S. 113, 125, 110 S.Ct. 975, 983, 108 L.Ed.2d 
100 (1990). Here, { 51 of the complaint clearly sets out 
claims of the third variety listed above. In such a case, the 
first inquiry is whether the defendants deprived the 
plaintiffs of a protected liberty or property interest; if so, 
this Court must determine whether the process afforded 
to the plaintiffs conformed to the constitutional minimum 
under circumstances. Cleveland Bd. of Educ. v. Loudermill, 
470 U.S. 532, 538, 105 S.Ct. 1487, 1491, 84 L.Ed. 2d 494 
(1985). 


Familial Privacy 


As one of the fundamental concepts in the Constitu- 
tion, the term “liberty” broadly encompasses interests 
more far-reaching than mere freedom from bodily 
restraint. Board of Regents v. Roth, 408 U.S. 564, 572, 92 
S.Ct. 2701, 2706, 33 L.Ed.2d 548 (1972) (citing Meyer v. 
Nebraska, 262 U.S. 390, 399, 43 S.Ct. 625, 626-27, 67 L.Ed. 
1042 (1923)). Nevertheless, the term does not extend lim- 
itlessly to every conceivable individual interest that 
might impinge on one’s pursuit of happiness in a free 
society, Roth, 408 U.S. at 569-70, 92 S.Ct. at 2705, but 
instead derives meaning from “existing rules or under- 
standings that stem from an independent source such as 
state law.” Id. at 577, 92 S.Ct. at 2709; see also Paul v. Davis, 
424 U.S. 693, 710-11 96 S.Ct. 1155, 1165-66, 47 L.Ed.2d 405 
(1976). 


For many years, courts have recognized that the Due 
Process Clause encompasses a broad right of personal 
privacy. Whalen v. Roe, 429 U.S. 589, 598-600, 97 S.Ct. 869, 
876, 51 L.Ed.2d 64 (1976) (recognizing an “individual 


40a 


interest in avoiding disclosure of personal matters,” and 
an “interest in independence in making certain kinds of 
important decisions”); Fadjo v. Coon, 633 F.2d 1172, 1175 
(Sth Cir.1981) (privacy interest extends to information 
gathered during criminal investigation); United States v. 
Westinghouse Elec. Corp., 638 F.2d 570, 577 (3rd Cir.1980) 
(privacy interest extends to medical records). Further, 
courts have acknowledged that such a right embraces 
interests in familial privacy. See, e.g., Smith v. Organization 
of Foster Families for Equality and Reform, 431 U.S. 816, 842, 
97 S.Ct. 2094, 2108, 53 L.Ed.2d 14 (1977) (due process 
clause protects ‘private realm of family life which the 
state cannot enter’ (quoting Prince v. Massachusetts, 321 
U.S. 158, 166, 64 S.Ct. 438, 442, 88 L.Ed. 645 (1944))); Bohn 
v. County of Dakota, 772 F.2d 1433, 1435-36 (8th Cir.1985) 
(familial privacy in context of parent identified as sus- 
pected child abuser). 


Analogizing to the protected liberty interest in per- 
sonal privacy acknowledged by the Supreme Court, the 
protected liberty interest in familial privacy encompasses 
an “interest in avoiding disclosure of personal matters.” 
Whalen, 429 U.S. at 599, 97 S.Ct. at 876. That interest, also 
described as a “right to confidentiality,” Fadjo, 633 7.2d at 
1175 (quoting Plante v. Gonzalez, 575 F.2d 1119, 1152 (5th 
Cir. 1978)), is implicated when government shares private 
information or when government collects private infor- 
mation in the absence of any legitimate state interest for 
such collection. Fadjo, 633 F.2d at 1175.

of child abuse, the plaintiffs in Bohn filed suit under 
§ 1983 claiming a deprivation of their rights to familial 
privacy. On the issue of whether the parents sufficiently

With respect to the defendants’ other argument, “it is 
well settled that violations of state law cannot provide 
the basis for a due process claim.” Weller v. Department of 
Social Services, 901 F.2d 387, 392 (4th Cir.1990). However, 
where state officers deprive an individual of a protected 
liberty or property interest in accordance with estab- 
lished state procedures, one may attack those procedures 
as violative of the Due Process Clause. Logan v. Zimmer- 
man Brush Co., 455 U.S. 422, 102 S.Ct. 1148, 71 L.Ed.2d 265 
(1982). 


In Parratt v. Taylor, 451 U.S. 527, 101 S.Ct. 1908, 68 
L.Ed.2d 420 (1981) and Hudson v. Palmer, 468 U.S. 517, 104 
S.Ct. 3194, 82 L.Ed.2d 393 (1984), the Supreme Court 
explained that where a state official’s random and 
unauthorized actions result in a deprivation of a pro- 
tected liberty or property interest, adequate postdepriva- 
tion remedies provided by the State would satisfy the 
requirements of due process, even where the state official 
acted intentionally. Hudson, 468 U.S. at 533, 104 S.Ct. at 
3203. In contrast, an “established state procedure” 
resulted in the deprivation at issue in Logan, and the 
Court in that case held that postdeprivation remedies 
would not suffice, and that the plaintiff could challenge 
the state procedure. Logan, 455 U.S. at 436, 102 S.Ct. at 
1158 (“Logan is challenging not the Commission’s error, 
but the ‘established state procedure’ that destroys his 


52a 


entitlement without according him proper procedural 
safeguards”). 


In the case at bar, the individual defendants deprived 
the plaintiffs of their protected interest in familial privacy 
by placing into the AMF a record of the Hodges’ case in 
the absence of any legitimate state interest in maintaining 
such a record. That deprivation occurred pursuant to an 
established state procedure that refuses to recognize the 
AMF as a central registry or to accord to persons sus- 
pected of child abuse the procedural safeguards con- 
tained in § 5-715. The only postdeprivation remedy 
available to the plaintiffs comes in the form of expunge- 
ment after their record has remained on the AMF for five 
years; in light of the nature of the deprivation, this Court 
finds such a remedy wholly inadequate. The procedures 
contained in § 5-715 have indisputable value in discover- 
ing and preventing unwarranted deprivations such as the 
one at issue in this case. Finally, although requiring notice 
and a hearing before placing a person’s name on a central 
registry will lead to a significant fiscal and administrative 
burden on the State, “it is at least awkward to find that 
procedures the state, through its legislative body, has 
imposed upon itself would be unduly burdensome for the 
state.” Riccio v. County of Fairfax, Virginia, 907 F.2d 1459, 
1468 (4th Cir.1990). Accordingly, this Court holds that the 
plaintiffs have stated a claim under § 1983 based on the 
defendants’ refusal to comply with the procedural 
requirements of § 5-715.

C. Qualified Immunity 


Qualified immunity acknowledges that subjecting public 
officials with discretionary functions to insubstantial lawsuits 


58a 


burdens not only the defendant officials, but society as a 
whole. Harlow v. Fitzgerald, 457 U.S. 800, 814, 102 S.Ct. 2727, 
27%, 73 L.Ed. 2d 396 (1982). Balanced against that concern, 
however, is the recognition that, “[i]n situations of abuse of 
office, an action for damages may offer the only realistic 
avenue for vindication of constitutional guarantees.” Harlow, 
457 U.S. at 814, 102 S.Ct. at 2736; see Butz v. Economou, 438 
U.S. 478, 506, 98 S.Ct. 2894, 2910-11, 57 L.Ed. 2d 895 (1978) 
(“it is not unfair to hold liable the official who knows or 
should know he is acting outside the law”) (citing Scheuer v. 
Rhodes, 416 U.S. 232, 94 S.Ct. 1683, 40 L.Ed.2d 90 (1974)). 
Accordingly, public officials enjoy qualified immunity from 
liability under § 1983 unless their actions violated “clearly 
established statutory or constitutional rights of which a rea- 
sonable person would have known.” Harlow, 457 U.S. at 818, 
102 S.Ct. at 2738. In other words, “in light of pre-existing law 
the unlawfulness must be apparent.” Anderson v. Creighton, 
483 U.S. 635, 638, 107 S.Ct. 3034, 3038, 97 L.Ed.2d 523 (1987). 
Finally, the defense of qualified immunity does not shield 
public officials from liability in their official capacities, but 
only in their individual capacities. Kentucky v. Graham, 473 
U.S. 159, 166-67, 105 S.Ct. 3099, 3105, 87 L.Ed.2d 114 (1985). 


As noted above, the plaintiffs in this case have stated 
a claim against the individual defendants under § 1983 
for the deprivation of the plaintiffs’ protected liberty and 
property interests in familial privacy and in the pro- 
cedures established by Maryland law. With regard to the 
first of those interests, not only was the existence of a 
protected interest in familial privacy “clearly established” 
in January 1989, when CCDSS first learned that Joseph 
Hodge could not possibly have suffered child abuse, but 
the Supreme Court in 1977 noted that “[o]ur decisions 


59a 


establish that the Constitution protects the sanctity of the 
family precisely because the institution of the family is 
deeply rooted in this Nation’s history and tradition.” 
Moore v. East Cleveland, Ohio, 431 U.S. 494, 503, 97 S.Ct. 
1932, 1937-38, 52 L.Ed.2d 531 (1977); see also Smith v. 
Organization of Foster Families for Equality and Reform, 431 
U.S. 816, 842, 97 S.Ct. 2094, 2108-09, 53 L.Ed.2d 14 (1977) 
(acknowledging the existence of a ‘private realm of fam- 
ily life which the state cannot enter’) (quoting Prince v. 
Massachusetts, 321 U.S. 158, 166, 64 S.Ct. 438, 442, 88 L.Ed. 
645 (1944)). Similarly, the Eighth Circuit in 1985 recog- 
nized that collecting information relating to suspicions of 
child abuse implicated the “privacy and autonomy of 
familial relationships.” Bohn, 772 F.2d at 1435. 


Equally “clearly established” in January 1989 were 
the procedural safeguards set out in Md.Fam.Law Code § 
5-715 and its supporting regulations governing the inclu- 
sion of records of cases of suspected child abuse on the 
central registry. To the extent that the individual defen- 
dants in this case disregarded those procedures as part of 
an “established state procedure,” the plaintiffs may assert 
their claims in this case. 


The individual defendants argue that they made 
every effort in good faith to comply with Maryland law, 
and that therefore they enjoy qualified immunity in this 
case. However, the proper inquiry for qualified immunity 
in this case is whether the individual defendants reason- 
ably complied with federal law, not state law. Obviously, 
compliance with state law cannot absolve a public official 
of liability in connection with the deprivation of clearly 
established rights under the Constitution.

D. Conclusion 


Before leaving the defendants’ motion to dismiss or 
for summary judgment and considering the plaintiffs’ 
counterpart motion, this Court will review its holding. 
On the grounds of Eleventh Amendment immunity, this 
Court will dismiss CCDSS from the case. After careful 
consideration of the record in this case, this Court con- 
cludes that the plaintiffs have stated a claim under § 1983 
based on the alleged deprivation of their protected inter- 
est in familial privacy and in connection with their chal- 
lenge to the established state procedure refusing to 
recognize the AMF as a central registry within the mean- 
ing of § 5-715. Finally, this Court rejects the argument 
that the individual defendants enjoy qualified immunity 
to liability in their individual capacities. Indeed, taking 
all of the evidence in this case in the light most favorable 
to the plaintiffs, the unlawfulness of the defendants’ 
actions was clearly apparent. See Anderson v. Creighton, 
483 U.S. at 638, 107 S.Ct. at 3038.

no otherwise admissible evidence that would support an

records of cases of suspected child abuse. See COMAR 
07.02.07.02B(1) 


Moreover, the defendants have not raised a genuine 
issue of material fact in connection with the issue of 
whether persons listed on the AMF are “suspected child 
abusers,” especially in the face of the affidavits of Charles 


7 The parties disagree as to the evidentiary value of

Bosley, Cassandra Hillmon, and Selena Thomas, submit-

III. CONCLUSION 


In January 1989, the Hodges brought their three- 
month-old son into the hospital for treatment of a bone 
infection. Because of a misdiagnosis, CCDSS conducted 
an investigation. Although child abuse was ruled out to a 
medical certainty, and despite the procedural require- 
ments of Md.Fam.Law Code § 5-715, CCDSS placed a 
record of the Hodges’ case into the AME, a central regis- 
try of cases of suspected child abuse, without notifying 
the Hodges or affording them an opportunity for a hear- 
ing. Today, this Court holds that the entry of that case file 
onto the AMF, and the established State procedure that 
facilitated the entry, deprived the Hodges of their pro- 
tected interest in familial privacy without due process of 
law. This Court will execute an Order on this date in 
accordance with the foregoing Memorandum.

MEMORANDUM 


Despite unmistakable evidence that showed con- 
clusively that the plaintiffs, David and Marsha Hodge, 
had not abused their child, Joseph Hodge, in any manner 
whatsoever, the defendants, the Carroll County Depart- 
ment of Social Services (“CCDSS”), M. Alexander Jones, 
Director of CCDSS, Alan Katz, Assistant Director of 
CCDSS, and Carolyn Colvin, Director of the Maryland 
Department of Human Relations (“MDHR”), kept a 
record of the case on the Automated Master File, 
(“AMF”), a statewide comprehensive computerized 
database containing, inter alia, records of cases of sus- 
pected child abuse. By a Memorandum and Order dated 
September 21, 1992, this Court dismissed CCDSS from the 
case, granted in part and denied in part the remainder of 
the motion of the defendants to dismiss or for summary 
judgment, and granted the plaintiffs’ motion for inter- 
locutory summary judgment on the issue of liability. Pres- 
ently pending before this Court are the cross-motions of 
the parties to alter or amend that judgment. 


66a 


I. MOTIONS UNDER RULE 59%e) 


Although in their motions the parties fail to address 
the standards governing motions to alter or amend judg- 
ment under Rule 59(e) of the Federal Rules of Civil Pro- 
cedure, this Court briefly will survey the law in that field. 
In appropriate cases, a motion under Rule 59(e) invites 
the court to revisit its prior rulings under any one of 
three permissible grounds: an intervening change in con- 
trolling law; new evidence previously unavailable; or the 
need to correct a clear error of law or prevent manifest 
injustice. Natural Resources Defense Council v. United States 
E.P.A., 705 F. Supp. 698, 702 (D.D.C 1989), vacated on other 
grounds, 707 F. Supp. 3 (D.D.C. 1989). In this case, neither 
party seeks review under either of the first two grounds; 
instead, the defendants assert that this Court repeatedly 
erred as a matter of law, while the plaintiffs seek to 
clarify the terms of the Memorandum dated September 
21, 1992, by means of a broadly worded amendment. 


The decision to reexamine the judgment for alteration 
or amendment is committed to the sound discretion of 
the trial court. Doe v. Heckler, 580 F. Supp. 1224, 1227 (D. 
Md. 1984). In the exercise of that discretion, this Court 
may refuse to consider previously unraised arguments, 
Seamon v. Vaughan, 921 F.2d 1217, 1220 (11th Cir. 1990), or 
to permit relitigation of matters already decided. United 
States v. Western Elec. Co., Inc., 690 F. Supp. 22, 25 (D.D.C. 
1988). 


Having reviewed these principles, this Court now 
will proceed to a close examination of the pending 
motions. 


67a 


II. DEFENDANTS’ MOTION TO ALTER OR AMEND 


In their motion, the defendants assert that this Court 
erred in four rulings of law. Specifically, the defendants 
argue first, that the facts in this case do not implicate the 
Hodges’ right to familial privacy; second, that the facts in 
this case do not implicate Md. Fam. Law Code Ann. 
§ 5-715; third, that the defendants enjoy qualified immu- 
nity; and fourth, that this Court erroneously granted 
summary judgment in favor of the plaintiffs despite the 
existence of genuine issues of material fact. This Court 
will consider each of those assertions in turn. 


A. Familial Privacy 


The defendants do not deny that the Due Process 
Clause of the Fourteenth Amendment protects a broad 
liberty interest in familial privacy, closely analogous to 
personal privacy, which includes a strong interest in 
avoiding the disclosure of personal matters, an interest 
sometimes described as a “right to confidentiality.” The 
collection and maintenance by the government of highly 
personal and private information, such as information 
relating to child abuse investigations, implicates that 
interest. Accordingly, when it collects and maintains such 
volatile information, the government must take care not 
to deprive families of their protected interests, for exam- 
ple, by ensuring against improper disclosure of that 
information to the general public. 


Conceding that common ground, the defendants 
argue that this Court erred when it held that maintaining 
information relating to the Hodges’ case deprived the 
plaintiffs of their protected liberty interest in familial 


68a 


privacy. Essentially, the defendants argue that the gov- 
ernment may maintain records of private information, 
even in the absence of any legitimate need for that infor- 
mation, so long as the information remains confidential 
and undisclosed to the general public. 


In support of that argument, the defendants rely 
heavily on the decision of the United States Supreme 
Court in Whalen v. Roe, 429 U.S. 589 (1977). The plaintiffs 
in Whalen, users and prescribers of Schedule II phar- 
maceuticals, challenged a New York statutory scheme 
that provided for the collection and maintenance in a 
computerized database of records that, inter alia, identi- 
fied users and prescribers of such drugs. Reversing the 
district court and upholding the scheme, the Court in 
Whalen emphasized two points: first, that New York con- 
fidentiality law expressly provided for a criminai penalty 
in the event of improper disclosure of information to the 
general public; and second, that collecting and maintain- 
ing such records furthered the legitimate governmental 
objective of monitoring the valid medical use of Schedule 
II drugs and controlling the improper use of those drugs. 


The Court carefully considered the plaintiffs’ argu- 
ment that their protected privacy interests were deprived 
simply by the maintenance of the records, i.e., the dis- 
closure of the information in those records only to those 
governmental employees authorized under the confiden- 
tiality laws to access the information. Rejecting that argu- 
ment, the Court wrote: 


Unquestionably, some individuals’ concern for 
their own privacy may lead them to avoid or to 
postpone needed medical attention. Neverthe- 
less, disclosures of private medical information 


69a 


to doctors, to hospital personnel, to insurance 
companies, and to public health agencies are 
often an essential part of modern medical prac- 
tice even when the disclosure may reflect unfa- 
vorably on the character of the patient. 
Requiring such disclosures to representatives of 
the State having responsibility for the health of 
the community, does not automatically amount 
to an impermissible invasion of privacy. 


Whalen, 429 U.S. at 602 (footnote omitted). By qualifying 
the last sentence with the word “automatically,” the 
Court announced that, even where confidentiality laws limit 
access to government records, the maintenance of records of 
private information by the government could result in a 
deprivation of privacy. Clearly, the decision in Whalen 
does not afford the defendants the comfort that they seek. 


The holding in Whalen raises the question: when 
would merely maintaining records of private information 
offend the right to confidentiality? The answer is unre- 
markable: when the government has no legitimate pur- 
pose for maintaining the private information in those 
records. 


Reassuringly, few courts have considered the claim 
that government has collected and maintained records of 
private information in the absence of any legitimate need 
for that information, perhaps because the proposition 
against such a practice is so basic. In United States v. 
Westinghouse Elec. Corp., 638 F.2d 570 (3rd Cir. 1980), the 
United States Court of Appeals for the Third Circuit 
recognized the fundamental logic supporting the rule 
that private information should remain protected until 
“the government has advanced a need to acquire the 


. 


70a 


information.” Id. at 578. See also id. (“In the cases in which 
a court has allowed some intrusion into the zone of 
privacy surrounding medical records, it has usually done 
so only after finding that the societal interest in dis- 
closure outweighs the privacy interest on the specific 
facts of the case”). 


In Fadjo v. Coon, 633 F.2d 1172 (5th Cir. 1981), the 
United States Court of Appeals for the Fifth Circuit came 
to a similar conclusion. In Fadjo, a state attorney conduct- 
ing a criminal investigation collected some extremely sen- 
sitive private information from the plaintiff that 
subsequently was disclosed to the general public. Eval- 
uating the two claims asserted in the complaint in that 
case, the court held: 


Fadjo clearly states a claim under the confiden- 
tiality branch of the privacy right. He does not 
claim that the state lacked authority to obtain 
personal information from him while pursuing a 
criminal investigation. However, even if the 
information was properly obtained, the state 
may have invaded Fadjo’s privacy in revealing 
it [to the public]. Alternatively, although the 
state could compel Fadjo’s testimony it could 
delve into his privacy only in pursuit of aims 
recognized as legitimate and proper. Implicit in 
both formulations of the complaint is the allega- 
tion that no legitimate state purpose existed suf- 
ficient to outweigh the invasion into Fadjo’s 
privacy. 


Fadjo, 633 F.2d at 1175 (footnote omitted). Citing to the 
holding of the court in Fadjo that “the state .. . could 
delve into his privacy only in pursuit of aims recognized 
as legitimate and proper,” this Court wrote, “[the right to 


7la 


confidentiality] is implicated when government shares 
private information or when government collects private 
information in the absence of any legitimate state interest 
for such collection.” Memorandum dated September 21, 
1992, at 13-14. 


The defendants argue alternatively that the legiti- 
mate need for collecting the information in this case 
supports their maintaining the records after any need for 
the records evaporated. But even where confidentiality 
laws limit access to records of private information, the 
government may not maintain such records of private 
information without directly implicating the right to con- 
fidentiality, which in turn requires some legitimate need 
on the part of the government for maintaining those 
records. Not an overwhelming need, nor a compelling 
need, nor even a substantial need - a rational, permiss- 
ible, legitimate need. In all of the papers filed with this 
Court, the defendants in this case have failed to suggest a 
rational, permissible, legitimate need for the information 
contained in the Hodges’ case file, and despite a tho- 
rough and independent inquiry, this Court cannot con- 
ceive of any. See Memorandum dated September 21, 1992, 
at 16-17. Accordingly, this Court rejects the defendants’ 
argument and reaffirms its holding that the facts of this 
case implicate the Hodges’ protected liberty interest in 
familial privacy. 


B. Section 5-715 


The defendants further argue that Md. Fam. Law 
Code Ann. § 5-715 does not apply to the AMF or to this 
case because, first, the AMF is not a central registry, and 


72a 


second, the AMF does not identify any person as a “sus- 
pected child abuser.” This Court examined this issue 
thoroughly the first time, see Memorandum dated Sep- 
tember 21, 1992, at 7-8, 20-24, and will not revisit the 
issue except to address a few of the points raised by the 
defendants in their present motion. 


Filing a second affidavit from Linda Heisner, Director 
of the Office of Family and Children’s Services at the 
Social Services Administration within MDHR, the defen- 
dants assert that her affidavits, and those filed by Katz, 
“deserve more weight” than the affidavits in support of 
the plaintiffs’ arguments. At this stage of the proceedings, 
however, this Court does not “weigh the evidence [or] 
determine the truth,” but instead, construing all of the 
evidence in the light most favorable to the non-moving 
party, asks “whether reasonable jurors could find by a 
preponderance of the evidence that the [non-moving 
party] is entitled to a verdict.” Anderson v. Liberty Lobby, 
Inc., 477 U.S. 242, 249, 252 (1986). 


This Court closely examined the statements con- 
tained in the Heisner and Katz affidavits. In her first 
affidavit, Heisner asserted that “no central registries exist 
today, nor have they since 1986.” However, applicable 
Maryland law and regulations belie that conclusion. In 
another part of her affidavit, Heisner attested: 


The AMF system contains listings of services 
provided to families. The services are listed in 
code. The family’s service data is entered along 
with the names of all persons residing in the 
recipient’s household. Even in cases where the 
services received were child protective services, 


73a 


the system does not identify anyone as a sus- 
pected abuser. This is true even in cases where 
abuse is found to be indicated and a person has 
been adjudicated a child abuser. 


As noted in the Memorandum dated September 21, 1992, 
although the AMF does not explicitly label any of the 
case associates as a “Suspected Child Abuser,” the appli- 
cable regulations assign that label indirectly. CCDSS pro- 
vides CPS services only in cases of suspected child abuse, 
COMAR 07.02.07.03A; thus, every case of CPS services is 
a case cf suspected child abuse. “Child abuse” is defined 
as physical injury or sexual molestation inflicted on a 
child either by a parent or by a guardian. COMAR 
07.02.07.02B(3); thus, every case of CPS services involves 
suspicions that a parent or guardian has physically 
injured or sexually molested a child. Where, as in this 
case, the AMF displays a record of indicated sexual child 
abuse and lists only the parents, one cannot help but 
conclude that one or both of the parents is a suspected 
child abuser, even in the absence of an explicit label. 
Moreover, to the extent that one considers the AMF to 
include the “hard copy” case files, documents in those 
files titled “Report of Suspected Child Abuse” leave no 
doubt about the nature of the investigation in this case. In 
short, taking the statements in Heisner’s affidavit as true, 
those statements do not raise a genuine issue of material 
fact regarding whether the AMF is a central registry 
under Maryland law (it is) or whether it identifies any 
persons as suspected child abusers (it does). 


An examination of the statements in the affidavit 
filed by Katz reveals similarly true statements that fail to 


74a 


raise any genuine issues of material fact. For example, 
Katz makes the statement: 


It would be highly inappropriate on the part of 
anyone using only the AMF to assume that 
either David R. Hodge or Marsha A. Hodge, or 
both, were suspected of committing physical or 
sexual abuse on Joseph E. Hodge. 


Of course, such an assumption would be inappropriate 
because there was no abuse whatsoever in the Hodge 
case. Katz does not attest that no person ever has made 
such an inappropriate assumption, however. Katz also 
does not explain how to interpret an AMF display of a 
record of indicated physical abuse with only one case 
associate. In such a case, where the investigator has dis- 
covered credible, specific, and unrefuted evidence show- 
ing that physical abuse was committed by a caretaker 
through cruel or inhumane treatment or by a malicious 
act, COMAR 07.02.07.08B(1), one can conclude that the 
case associate at least is suspected of child abuse. 


Finally, Katz attests: 


The Plaintiffs’ pleadings assume that anyone 
whose name appears on the Automated Master 
File in a Child Protective Services’ case is de 
facto a suspect in a child abuse or neglect inves- 
tigation. This is totally incorrect. The Auto- 
mated Master File system does not list, nor is it 
intended to list, suspected or confirmed child 
abusers or neglectors. It is impossible using an 
AMF printout by itself to draw a conclusion as 
to who the suspected child abuser or neglector, 
if any, might have been. In fact, in some cases, 
such as the Hodge case, the suspected abuser is 
not known. 


Eee 


75a 


As noted above, however, and at the risk of repetition, 
every case of CPS services is a case of suspected child 
abuse, and therefore every case of CPS services involves 
suspicion of abuse by a parent or guardian; assertions to 
the contrary cannot be credited. Accordingly, to the 
extent that the AMF contains the names of individuals 
other than the parents or guardians of a child, the AMF 
contains names of individuals who are not suspected of 
child abuse, because only a parent or guardian can com- 
mit that offense. As also noted above, however, although 
the display on the AMF does not explicitly label any case 
associates as “suspected child abusers,” in many cases the 
display provides that information indirectly, and the hard 
copy case file dispels all uncertainty. In light of the undis- 
puted facts and the inescapable conclusions of law that 
follow from them, it simply is not impossible in every 
case to determine, even from the AMF display alone, the 
identity of a suspected child abuser. 


C. Qualified Immunity 


The defendants also argue that they enjoy qualified 
immunity from liability in this case because the applica- 
ble law was not “clearly established.” Of course, the right 
to familial privacy and the protections of § 5-715 were 
clearly established as of January 1989. This Court 
addressed that issue completely in the Memorandum 
dated September 21, 1992, and will not revisit it except to 
address one argument. 


The defendants complain that no case cited by this 
Court “concerned the issue of whether a child protective 
services agency’s maintenance of records concerning its 


ae 


76a 


investigation of an unsubstantiated report of child abuse 
violates the family’s right of privacy.” Memorandum in 
Support of Motion, at 12-13 (emphasis in original). But as 
noted above, it is well-established first, that a protected 
liberty interest in familial privacy exists and closely par- 
allels an individual’s interest in privacy protected by the 
Due Process Clause; second, that no agency of any kind 
may maintain records of private information without 
implicating the protected liberty interest in privacy; and 
third, where an agency insists on maintaining such 
records in the absence of any legitimate need for those 
records, the right of privacy is deprived. 


D. Genuine Issues of Material Fact 


Finally, the defendants argue that this Court failed to 
credit the assertions contained in the affidavits filed by 
Heisner and Katz. As discussed more completely above, 
this Court credited those affidavits but found that they 
raised no genuine issues of material fact to preclude a 
finding of liability. In light of all of the foregoing, there- 
fore, this Court will deny the defendants’ motion to alter 
or amend.

Motion, at 1-4. Concluding that the Hodges lack standing

Plaintiffs in federal court must satisfy the threshold 
requirement imposed by Article III of the United States 


79a 


Constitution by alleging a case or controversy, U.S. Const. 
art. III, § 2, cl. 1, for this Court has no power to issue 
advisory opinions, United States v. Fruehauf, 365 U.S. 146, 
157 (1961), and only may decide “questions presented in 
an adversary context and in a form historically viewed as 
capable of resolution through the judicial process.” Flast 
v. Cohen, 392 U.S. 83, 95 (1968). 


In this case, the plaintiffs asserted claims under 42 
U.S.C. § 1983 in connection with the inclusion of a record 
of their case on the AMF despite uncontested medical 
evidence that precluded any possibility of child abuse. 
Further, the plaintiffs asserted that the inclusion of their 
names on the AMF occurred without notice and a hearing 
as required by § 5-715. These claims raised controversies 
between the parties concerning, inter alia, the existence 
and scope of the right to familial privacy under the Due 
Process Clause, and the applicability of § 5-715 to the 
defendants’ actions. 


In their motion to alter or amend, the plaintiffs seek 
relief essentially in the form of a declaratory judgment 
that would “indicate” the proper form of notice and the 
proper way to conduct a hearing when, in the future, the 
defendants seek to place more records of other cases of 
suspected child abuse on the AMF. However, under the 
Declaratory Judgment Act, 28 U.S.C. § 2201(a), this Court 
may not make such an indication in the absence of a 
constitutional controversy. Aetna Life Ins. Co. v. Haworth, 
300 U.S. 227, 239-40 (1937). Nothing about this case 
requires a decision as to the proper form of notice or the 
proper conduct of a hearing under § 5-715, in light of the 
undisputed fact that the defendants denied the plaintiffs 
notice and a hearing of any kind. 


80a 


Another consideration prevents this Court from 
granting the relief that the plaintiffs seek. The “individual 
States have broad latitude in experimenting with possible 
solutions to problems of vital local concern.” Whalen, 429 
U.S. at 597. The notice and hearing provisions of § 5-715 
and the supporting regulations promulgated by the 
MDHR represent “a considered attempt to deal with such 
a problem” — namely, child abuse. Although this Court 
stands ready to interpret those statutes and regulations 
when necessary, this Court is ill-equipped to step into the 
shoes of the Maryland General Assembly or the MDHR 
and pass new regulations to deal with the troubling prob- 
lem of child abuse in Maryland.

Having considered the motions to alter or amend 
judgment, this Court remains unpersuaded, and will 
deny both of those motions. This Court will execute an 
Order on this date in accordance with the foregoing 
Memorandum.

(a) In general. — In this subtitle the following words

(b) Abuse. - (1) “Abuse” means:

§ 5-702. Legislative policy. 


The purpose of this subtitle is to protect children who 
have been the subject of abuse or neglect by: 


(1) mandating the reporting of any suspected 
abuse or neglect; 


(2) giving immunity to any individual who 
reports, in good faith, a suspected incident of abuse or 
neglect; 


(3) requiring prompt investigation of each 
reported suspected incident of abuse or neglect; 


(4) causing immediate, cooperative efforts by 
the responsible agencies on behalf of children who have 
been the subject of reports of abuse or neglect; and 


(5) requiring each local department to give the 
appropriate service in the best interest of the abused or 
neglected child. (1987, ch. 635, § 2.)

.08 Disposition of Investigation of Suspected Child 
Abuse and Neglect. 


A. Based on its investigation, the local department 
shall determine whether a report of suspected child abuse 
or neglect is found to be “indicated” or “unsubstanti- 
ated”. 


B. Indicated Child Abuse. 


(1) An “indicated” report of child abuse means 
one in which there is credible and specific evidence, 
which has not been satisfactorily refuted, that: 


(a) Physical injury resulted from cruel or 
inhumane treatment or a malicious act by a caretaker or 
household or family member; or 


(b) Sexual abuse of a child occurred and 
was committed by a caretaker or household or family 
member; or 


(c) The nature, extent and location of injury 
make cruel or inhumane treatment or a malicious act the 
likely cause; or 


(d) There are physical or behavioral indica- 
tions that a child has been sexually abused, including but 
not limited to, genital trauma, pregnancy, sexually trans- 
mitted diseases, and patterns of behavior typically associ- 
ated with sexual abuse. 


(2) The determination that abuse is “indicated” 
does not require a court conviction for the crime of child 
abuse or the existence of proof sufficient to obtain such a 
conviction. In making this determination, the local 
department shall consider whether: 


94a 


(a) Explanations of the incident under 
investigation are inconsistent over time, or do not match 
the developmental age and capabilities of the child; or 


(b) There is a history of prior unexplained 
injuries in the household or family which may indicate 
patterns of domestic violence. 


C. Unsubstantiated Child Abuse. An “unsubstanti- 
ated” report of child abuse is one which does not meet 
the criteria established for an “indicated” finding. In 
choosing this disposition, the worker shall document the 
extent to which any of the following factors apply to the 
situation: 


(1) There is no current injury; 


(2) There is no credible account by the sus- 
pected victim or a witness; 


(3) There is no indication of past injury or sex- 
ual abuse; 


(4) There is credible evidence that injuries have 
been accidentally caused, or did not result from a cruel, 
inhumane or malicious act; or 


(5) It is not possible to complete the investiga- 
tion due to an inability to gain access to the child or to 
obtain relevant facts regarding the incident.
