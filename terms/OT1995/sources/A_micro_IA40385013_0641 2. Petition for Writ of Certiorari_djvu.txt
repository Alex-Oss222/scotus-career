STATEMENT OF THE CASE 


Petitioner Lloyd Henderson sustained injuries on 
August 27, 1991, while employed by the United States as 
a seaman aboard the M/V DELMONTE, a ship owned by 
the United States (R 3-4). After exhausting administrative 
remedies, he filed suit against the United States on April 
8, 1993, in the United States District Court for the South- 
ern District of Texas, Galveston Division (R 2).° Jurisdic- 
tion arose under the Suits in Admiralty Act (“SAA”), 46 
U.S.C. § 741-752 and the Public Vessels Act, 46 U.S.C. 
§§ 781-790. 


Mr. Henderson experienced a series of difficulties 
obtaining a proper summons for the United States from 
the district clerk’s office (R 163). As a result, Mr. Hender- 
son did not serve his complaint via certified mail on the 
United States Attorney General until May 19, 1993, over a 
month after filing suit (R 163). The summons intended for 
the United States Attorney, however, was still not in 
proper form at that point because the deputy district 
clerk had failed inadvertently to place the seal of the 


> On motion by the United States, the case was later trans- 
ferred to the Eastern District of Texas, Beaumont Division (R 1). 


court on the summons. A state constable noticed the 
defect and, after a further series of difficulties with the 
district clerk’s office, Mr. Henderson finally obtained a 
properly sealed summons on August 25, 1993, just two 
days before the statute of limitations elapsed (R 164). 


Mr. Henderson then filed a motion seeking an exten- 
sion of time in which to serve the United States Attorney 
(R 12-13). The district court, after presumably finding 
good cause as required by Rule 4, granted the extension 
and rendered an order allowing service on or before 
September 15, 1993 (R 16). The United States Attorney 
received personal service on September 3, 1993. Thereaf- 
ter, the United States moved to dismiss the case for want 
of jurisdiction due to a lack of “forthwith” service as 
required by the SAA (R 34). The district court originally 
denied the motion, presumably believing that Rule 4 
governed the time for service in the case and that the 
court had the power to grant an extension (R 107). Fol- 
lowing the court’s ruling, however, the Fifth Circuit 
delivered its opinion in United States v. Holmberg, holding 
that the SAA’s forthwith requirement rather than Rule 4 
governs the time for service on the United States. 19 F.3d 
1062 (5th Cir. 1994), cert. denied, 115 S.Ct. 482 (1994). The 
district court reconsidered its ruling in light of Holmberg 
and granted the government’s motion to dismiss without 
prejudice but after the statute of limitations had elapsed 
(R 189). Mr. Henderson appealed to the Fifth Circuit to no 
avail. The | ‘th Circuit merely reiterated its opinion in 
Holmberg that the SAA’s forthwith requirement rather 
than Rule 4 governs the time for service on the United 
States.

REASONS FOR GRANTING THE WRIT 


A. This Court Should Grant the Writ Because the 
Fifth Circuit’s Interpretation of the SAA’s 
Forthwith Service Requirement Conflicts with 
Third Circuit Precedent. 


The manner of service on the United States under the 
SAA and Rule 4 is the same. Both require that the plain- 
tiff personally serve the United States Attorney for the 
district in which the suit is filed* and, additionally, serve 
the Attorney General by mail.5 Rule 4 and the SAA col- 
lide, however, with respect to the time for service on the 
United States. The SAA fails to provide any specific time 
for service on the United States, but rather simply pro- 
vides that service be “forthwith.” Rule 4, on the other 
hand, provides a specific time limit of 120 days in which 
to accomplish service and permits the district court to 
extend the time upon a showing of “good cause.” 


Thus the question of which rule regarding the time for 
service on the United States arises in suits brought under the 
SAA. The Third Circuit has determined that Rule 4’s 120-day 
requirement supersedes the SAA’s forthwith requirement. 
Jones & Laughlin Steel, Inc. v. Mon River Towing, Inc., 772 F.2d 
62, 66 (3d Cir. 1985). Several district courts have held like- 
wise. E.g., Libby v. United States, 657 F. Supp. 1251, 1252 (M.D. 
Fla. 1987), aff'd, 840 F.2d 818 (11th Cir. 1988); Phillips v. United 
States Army Corps of Engineers, 629 F. Supp. 967, 971 (S.D. 


4 After the filing of this suit, Congress amended Rule 4 to 
permit the United States Attorney to designate agents to receive 
process and to permit service on the United States Attorney by 
mail. 

5 The SAA specifies registered mail; Rule 4 permits regis- 
tered or certified mail. 


Miss. 1986). Other circuits addressing the issue, however, 
have concluded that the SAA’s forthwith requirement 
applies regardless of Rule 4’s 120-day requirement. E.g. City 
of New York v. McAllister Brothers, Inc., 278 F.2d 708, 710 (2d 
Cir. 1960); Owens v. United States, 541 F.2d 1386, 1388 (9th Cir. 


1976), cert denied, 430 U.S. 945 (1977); Holmberg, 19 F.3d at 
1065. 


For the most part, the conflict arises from the deter- 
mination of whether the SAA’s forthwith service require- 
ment is substantive or simply procedural. Those courts 
that consider the forthwith requirement substantive hold 
that it applies without regard to Rule 4 because the 
Federal Rules of Civil Procedure do not modify substan- 
tive rights. E.g., Battaglia v. United States, 303 F.2d 683, 686 
(2d Cir.), cert. dism’d, 371 U.S. 907 (1962). Those courts 
that consider the forthwith service requirement pro- 
cedural hold that Rule 4’s 120-day requirement supplants 
the SAA’s forthwith requirement because the Federal 
Rules supplant all conflicting procedural requirements in 
federal courts. E.g., Jones & Laughlin Steel, 772 F.2d at 66. 


Significantly, the leading precedents construing the 
SAA’s forthwith requirement were decided prior to the 
application of the Federal Rules to admiralty suits in 
1966. See Amendments to Rules of Civil Procedure for the 
United States District Courts, reported in 383 U.S. at 1029, 
1030-1031 (1966). For instance, in McAllister Brothers, the 
plaintiff served process on the United States Attorney 
more than two months after filing suit. 278 F.2d at 708. 
The court held that the plaintiff's failure to comply with 
the SAA’s forthwith service requirement deprived the 
district court of jurisdiction and compelled dismissal. Id. 
at 710. 


Two years later in Battaglia v. United States, the court 
reached an even harsher result. 303 F.2d at 683. There, the 
plaintiff served the United States Attorney on the same 
day he filed the complaint. The plaintiff failed to serve 
the Attorney General by registered mail, however, until 
sometime later. The court construed the SAA to require 
both forthwith service on the United States Attorney and 
forthwith service by registered mail on the Attorney Gen- 
eral. Accordingly, the court held that the lack of forthwith 
service warranted dismissal. Id. at 686. The rationale for 
the decision was that the forthwith service requirement 
constitutes a prerequisite to the United States’ waiver of 
sovereign immunity. 


In 1966, Congress amended the Federal Rules of Civil 
Procedure to apply them to admiralty suits. Amendments 
to Rules of Civil Procedure for the United States District 
Courts, reported in 383 U.S. at 1030-1031. Following the 
application of the Federal Rules to admiralty suits in 
1966, the Ninth Circuit nonetheless adopted the long-held 
view of the Second Circuit that forthwith service of pro- 
cess constitutes a matter of jurisdiction. Owens, 541 F.2d 
at 1388; see also Barrie v. United States, 615 F.2d 829, 830 
(9th Cir. 1980) (following Owens); Kenyon v. United States, 
676 F.2d 1229, 1231 (9th Cir. 1981) (following Owens); 
Amella v. United States, 732 F.2d 711, 713 (9th Cir. 1984) 
(following Owens). More recently, the Eleventh and Fifth 
Circuits also have adopted the view that forthwith ser- 
vice constitutes a matter of substance unaffected by the 
Federal Rules. Libby v. United States, 840 F.2d 818 (11th 
Cir. 1988); Holmberg, 19 F.3d at 1065; Henderson, 51 F.3d at 
576. 


The Third Circuit, however, has used a more reason- 
able analysis in part because the other circuits have relied 
on the law before the Federal Rules applied to admiralty 
suits. In Jones & Laughlin Steel, the plaintiff filed suit on 
January 18, 1984, and sent notice to the appropriate fed- 
eral agency the next day but failed to serve the Attorney 
General and United States Attorney until late March, well 
within the 120 days required by Rule 4 but not “forth- 
with” as required by the SAA. 772 F.2d at 66. The court 
logically concluded that the SAA’s forthwith service 
requirement constituted a matter of procedure rather 
than substance. Id. at 66. In so concluding, the court 
relied on the fact that Congress unified admiralty pro- 
cedure and the Federal Rules in 1966. See Amendments to 
Rules of Civil Procedure for the United States District 
Courts, reported in 383 U.S. at 1030-1031. Thus, the court 
reasoned that the provisions for service of process found 
in Rule 4 should govern service on the United States 
under the SAA. Specifically, the court held: 


This congressional enactment of a uniform 120- 
day period for accomplishing service of process 
{under Rule 4] must be read in light of 28 U.S.C. 
§ 2072, [the Rules Enabling Act] which states 
that “jajll laws in conflict with such rules shall 
be of no further force or effect after such rules 
have taken effect.” Rule 4(j) therefore super- 
sedes the Suits in Admiralty Act’s requirement 
of forthwith service. 


772 F.2d at 66. 


At least two district courts have adopted the Third 
Circuit’s reasoning. Phillips, 629 F. Supp. at 971; Libby, 657 
F. Supp. at 1252. Other district courts, though bound by 


10 


circuit precedents to the contrary, have expressed agree- 
ment with the Third Circuit’s construction of the SAA. 
For instance, in O'Halloran v. United States, the court 
stated: “This Court finds the Third Circuit approach rea- 
sonable, particularly in light of the inherently ambiguous 
nature of the term forthwith. This Court also believes that 
rejecting the argument that Sec. 742 is jurisdictional leads 
to a fairer result. However, the Ninth Circuit has consid- 
ered and rejected this very argument.” 817 F. Supp. 829, 
832 (N.D. Cal. 1993), aff'd, 45 F.3d 436 (9th Cir. 1994). See 
also Hall v. Maritime Administration, Civ. Action 92-3030, 
1993 U.S. Dist. Lexis 5753 (E.D. La. 1993) (following Fifth 
Circuit precedent and concluding “with great reluctance 

that plaintiff's complaint must be dismissed). Only this 
Court can resolve this conflict and mandate a uniform 
rule for the timely service of process on the United States 
in suits brought under the SAA. 


B. This Court Should Grant the Writ Because the 
Fifth Circuit’s Interpretation of the SAA’s 
Forthwith Service Requirement Frustrates Con- 


gressional Intent. 


The Fifth Circuit’s interpretation of the SAA’s forth- 
with service requirement as substantive, like those of the 
Second and Eleventh Circuits, fails to capture Congress's 
intent. Prior to 1916, the government enjoyed immunity 
from suits alleging damages inflicted by government 
owned and operated vessels. Re cognizing the inequity of 
declining to compensate those damaged by government 
vessels, Congress passed the Shipping Act of 1916, later 
modified in 1920 and renamed the SAA. 39 Stat. 728, 
730-31; 46 U.S.C. § 742. The Shipping Act, and the SAA 
after it, provided that U.S. vessels employed as merchant 


11 


vessels would be subject to all laws, regulations and 
liabilities to which private merchant vessels were subject. 
ld. This provision reveals Congress's consent to suits 
involving damages inflicted by government vessels and 
Congress's intent to treat governmental and private ves- 
sels equally. 


Congress's specific intent in including the forthwith 
service requirement in the SAA is less clear. In fact, the 
legislative history of the Act lacks any explanation for the 
forthwith service requirement. As noted above, some 
courts have opined that the SAA’s forthwith service 
requirement constitutes an integral part of the govern- 
ment's waiver of sovereign immunity, and that Congress 
intended that immunity be maintained when service on 
the government is not forthwith. E.¢., Battaglia, 303 F.2d 
at 685; Kenyon, 676 F.2d at 1232. 


This view of the forthwith requirement stems from 
the rule of statutory construction that “statutes which 
waive immunity of the United States from suit are to be 
construed strictly in favor of the sovereign.” McMahon v. 
United States, 342 U.S. 25, 27 n.5 (1951). No rule of statu- 
tory construction requires, however, that each term 
within a statute waiving immunity be strictly construed. 
In fact, this Court has noted that narrowly construing 
specific terms of such a statute can defeat Congress's 
general intent to waive immunity. For instance, in Indian 
Towing Co. v. United States, this Court warned against 
“import(ing] immunity back into a statute designed to 
limit it.” 350 U.S. 61, 69 (1955). Echoing this Court's 
warning in Indian Towing, the court in Weiss v. United 
States, emphasized that a presumption in favor of immu- 
nity “does not mean that there must be a niggling and 


12 


obnubilative adherence to the narrowest meaning of the 
words of *he Act when the patent intent of Congress” is 
to waive tmunity. 168 F. Supp. 300, 301 (D.N.J. 1958). 


Even Justice Friendly, who concurred in an opinion 
holding that the forthwith requirement constitutes an 
integral part of the government's waiver of immunity, 
remarked: 


I should have supposed that, once Congress gave 
the basic consent to sue the United States, as it did 
in the first sentence of Sec. 2 of the Suits in Admi- 
ralty Act, it was content to have the courts decide 
the effect of various procedural lapses in the same 
manner as “if such vessel were privately owned or 
operated.” The Act was “a comprehensive waiver 
by the United States” whereby “the government, in 
entering the shipping world as a participant, sim- 
ply assumes a normal role in the antecedent pat- 
tern.” 


Battaglia, 303 F.2d at 686 (citation omitted) (quoting Gilm- 
ore and Black, Admiralty 773-774 (1957)). 


Congress's more likely intent for including the forth- 
with requirement in the SAA was simply to provide a 
time frame for service because no external rule regarding 
the timeliness of service existed when Congress enacted 
the SAA in 1920. The Federal Rules did not come into 
effect until 1938 and did not apply to admiralty suits until 
1966. See 28 U.S.C. § 2072; Amendments to Rules of Civil 
Procedure for the United States District Courts, reported 
in 383 U.S. at 1030-31. When the Rules came into effect, 
Congress expressed in the Rules Enabling Act that all 
procedural laws in conflict with the new Rules “shall be 
of no further force or effect.” 28 U.S.C. § 2072. Thus, the 


13 


Rules supplanted the SAA’s forthwith service require- 
ment. This view is reflected by Justice Boochever in his 
concurrence in Kenyon. 676 F.2d at 1231. Specifically, he 
States: 


[I}f freed from the bounds of stare decisis, |! 
would hold that the service provision of Section 
742 [the forthwith requirement] does not consti- 
tute an integral part of the substantive waiver of 
sovereign immunity, but is a mere procedural 
provision necessary at the time of the statute's 
enactment, to effectuate that waiver. 


Id. at 1232. 


The details of service of process are just the type of 
procedural requirements the Federal Rules of Civil Pro- 
cedure were designed to supplant. In fact, this Court has 
previously construed the details of service of process as 
procedural. In Hanna v. Plumer, this Court decided that 
Rule 4, in allowing substituted service on an executor by 
leaving the process at his usual place of abode, sup- 
planted a contrary state rule requiring in-hand service 
upon an executor. 380 U.S. 460 (1965). The rationale 
behind the decision in Hanna was that such details consti- 
tuted matters of procedure governed by the Federal 
Rules. Id. at 473. Similarly, in resolving a conflict between 
service provisions found in the Tucker Act, a predecessor 
of the Federal Tort Claims Act, and Rule 4, the Eastern 
District of New York determined that the latter governed. 
United States v. American Surety Co., 25 F. Supp. 700, 702 
(E.D.N.Y. 1938). Specifically, the court stated: “It is to be 
presumed that the Supreme Court considered the prob- 
lem of whether or not Sections 762 and 763 of 28 U.S.C.A. 


14 


[the Tucker Act] were procedural or substantive and con- 
cluded that the procedure outlined therein could be 
altered by the new rules.” /d. 


Concluding that the forthwith service requirement is 
anything other than procedural seems particularly illogi- 
cal in light of the other service requirements found in the 
SAA. Justice Boochever illustrates this point in his con- 
currence in Kenyon: 


Section 742 [of the SAA] specifies even the man- 
ner of mailing “by registered mail to the Attor- 
ney General of the United States.” Certainly that 
provision is not to be considered as jurisdic- 
tional and would be superseded by the Federal 
Rule provision that states that a copy of the 
summons and complaint may be sent by “regis- 
tered or certified mail to the Attorney General of 
the United States.” 


676 F.2d at 1232. 


Also persuasive in reaching the conclusion that the 
SAA’s forthwith service requirement is merely procedural 
is the fact that the 1937 notes of the Advisory Committee 
pertaining to Rule 4 state that the Rule is intended to 
“provide a uniform and comprehensive method of service 
for all actions against the United States or an officer or 
agency thereof.” Federal Civil Judicial Procedure and 
Rules at 22 (West 1995). Additionally, the Advisory Com- 
mittee listed several federal statutes providing for service 
on the United States which the Committee believed the 
Rules superseded. /d. at 1252. The SAA did not appear on 
the list but likely only because the Rules did not then 
apply to admiralty cases. For these reasons, the more 


15 


likely intent of Congress in including the forthwith ser- 
vice requirement in the SAA was merely to provide a 
procedural guideline in the absence of the Federal Rules. 
Now that the Federal Rules apply in admiralty suits, they 
supplant the SAA’s contrary forthwith requirement. 


Moreover, continuing to allow the government to 
enjoy the forthwith service requirement, while private 
owners and operators of vessels are subject to the service 
requirements set out in Rule 4, defeats Congress's intent 
in enacting the SAA to place the government on equal 
terms with private litigants. See 46 U.S.C. § 742. In fact, 
this Court, in interpreting a different provision of the 
SAA, noted: “Congress has expressly declared that when 
sued under the Suits in Admiralty Act the United States is to 
have its procedural rights determined and governed in the same 
manner as private parties.” United States v. Isthmian S.S. Co., 
359 U.S. 314, 324 (1959) (emphasis added). The Third 
Circuit, in deciding that Rule 4 rather than the SAA’s 
forthwith requirement governs the time for service on the 
United States, similarly relied on Congress's intent in 
enacting the SAA to treat the United States as a private 
litigant. Jones & Laughlin Steel, 772 F.2d at 64.

Lloyd Henderson filed a personal injury suit against 
the United States under the Suits in Admiralty Act (the 
“SAA”), 46 U.S.C. app. §§ 741-752 (1988), and the Public 
Vessels Act (the “PVA”), 46 U.S.C. app. §§ 781-790 (1988). 
Pursuant to Rule 12(b)(1) of the Federal Rules of Civil 
Procedure, the district court dismissed Henderson's com- 
plaint for lack of subject-matter jurisdiction. Henderson 
appeals the district court’s dismissal, and we affirm. 


App. 2 


Lloyd Henderson, a merchant mariner, was injured 
while working aboard a vessel owned and operated by 
the United States. On April 8, 1993, Henderson filed a 
personal injury suit against the United States under the 
SAA and PVA. Forty-seven days later, on May 25, 1993, 
the Attorney General of the United States received by 
mail a copy of the complaint. On August 30, 1993, Hen- 
derson filed a motion for postponement in the district 
court, in which he claimed that documents necessary to 
complete service of process on the government had been 
lost in the mail. The district court granted Henderson's 
motion, but ordered that he complete service within fif- 
teen days. The United States Attorney for the district in 
which the action was brought was personally served with 
the complaint on September 3, 1993, 148 days after Hen- 
derson had filed the suit. 


The United States moved to dismiss Henderson's suit 
for lack of subject-matter jurisdiction, arguing that § 742 
of the SAA requires that plaintiffs effect service of pro- 
cess in suits brought against the government under the 
SAA “forthwith.” The district court denied the motion 
without opinion. The United States subsequently 
renewed its motion to dismiss, citing United States v. 
Holmberg, 19 F.3d 1062 (5th Cir.), cert. denied, __ US. 
__, 115 S. Ct. 482, 130 L. Ed. 2d 395 (1994). The district 
court dismissed Henderson’s complaint without preju- 
dice for lack of subject-matter jurisdiction.

Henderson appeals, arguing that (1) the district court 
granted him an extension of time in which to effect ser- 
vice of process on the Government; (2) given the prob- 
lems he had with the mail, he completed service of 
process on the Government reasonably forthwith; (3) his 
service on the Attorney General was forthwith and satis- 
fied the service requirements of § 742; and (4) the district 
court’s dismissal of his complaint violated his right to 
due process.!

In United States v. Holmberg, 19 F.3d 1062 (5th Cir.), 
cert. denied, _ U.S. _, 115 S. Ct. 482, 130 L. Ed. 2d 395 
(1994), we addressed the question of whether the forth- 
with service requirement in § 742 is procedural and, thus, 
superseded by the Federal Rules of Civil Procedure. We 
held that the requirement of forthwith service is a condi- 
tion of the government's waiver of sovereign immunity 
and, therefore, a jurisdictional prerequisite. Id. at 1064-65; 
accord Libby v. United States, 840 F.2d 818 (11th 
Cir.1988); Amella v. United States, 732 F2d 711 (9th 
Cir.1984); Battaglia v. United States, 303 F.2d 683 (2d Cir.) 
cert. dismissed, 371 U.S. 907, 83 S. Ct. 210, 9 L. Ed. 2d 168 
(1962). But see Jones & Laughlin Steel, Inc. v. Mon River 
Towing, Inc., 772 F.2d 62 (3d Cir.1985) (holding forthwith 
service requirement to be procedural). Because § 742’s 
forthwith service requirement is a jurisdictional prerequi- 
site, it is not superseded by the Federal Rules of Civil 
Procedure and could not have been modified by Rule 4(j). 
See Holmberg, 19 F.3d at 1064 (holding that Rule 4(j) does 
not superseded [sic] § 742’s forthwith requirement). Thus 
the district court’s decision to grant Henderson fifteen 
additional days in which to meet the forthwith service 
requirement of Rule 4(j) did not affect either the court’s

subject-matter jurisdiction or Henderson's obligations 
under § 742. 


Henderson contends next that, given his problems 
with the mail, he completed service of process on the 
Government reasonably forthwith, and that the district 
court’s implied finding that he showed “good cause” for 
the delay in service supports his contention. Henderson 
also cites as support our statement in United States v. 
Bradley, 428 F.2d 1013 (5th Cir.1970), that in the context of 
another statute, the term “forthwith” was “deliberately 
undefined . . . to allow courts to interpret it in a context of 
‘reasonableness,’ on a case by case basis.” Id. at 1015. In 
Holmberg, however, we concluded that “under any defi- 
nition, service in 103 or 106 days is not forthwith.” 
Holmberg, 19 F.3d at 1065. Thus, even if we interpret the 
term in a context of reasonableness, and even if the 
district court found that Henderson showed good cause 
for the delay, completing service in 148 days is not forth- 
with. 


Cc 


Lastly, Henderson argues that his service on the 
Attorney General was forthwith and satisfied the service 
of process requirements of § 742. He contends that “the 
purpose of the [forthwith] requirement is to provide rea- 
sonably prompt notice of the suit to the United States,” 
and that his service on the Attorney General provided the 
government with that notice. Under § 742, both service of 
the complaint on the U.S. Attorney and the mailing of a 


App. 7 


copy to the Attorney General are required to complete 
service of process on the government. See § 742. “The 
word ‘forthwith’ applies both to the service of a copy of 
the complaint on the United States Attorney and to the 
mailing of a copy of the complaint by registered mail to 
the Attorney General of the United States.” 1 Martin J. 
Norris, The Law of Maritime Personal Injuries, § 8.4 (4th 
ed. 1990). See, e.g., Battaglia v. United States, 303 F.2d 
683, 686 (2d Cir.), cert. dismissed, 371 U.S. 907, 83 S. Ct. 
210, 9 L. Ed. 2d 168 (1962) (holding that the term “forth- 
with” applies to both the service and the mailing). Thus, 
while we do not address whether Henderson’s service on 
the Attorney General was forthwith, we conclude that 
Henderson's service on the Attorney General alone did 
not satisfy the forthwith service requirement of § 742. 


For the foregoing reasons, we DENY the Govern- 
ment’s motion to dismiss Henderson's appeal, but 
AFFIRM the district court’s dismissal of Henderson’s 
omplaint.

F.3d at 1064-1065. Although “forthwith” has no precise 
time line, the Fifth Circuit has held that 103 days is not 
forthwith. Holmberg, 19 F.3d at 1065. 


Because the plaintiff affected service on the United 
States 150 days after filing suit, service was not forthwith. 
Therefore, this court lacks subject matter jurisdiction, and 
the case must be dismissed. Accordingly, it is
