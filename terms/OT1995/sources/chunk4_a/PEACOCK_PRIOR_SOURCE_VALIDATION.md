## Massachusetts Laborers' Health & Welfare Fund v. Starrett Paving Corp., 845 F.2d 23 (1st Cir. 1988)

Primary text: https://static.case.law/f2d/845/html/0023-01.html ; machine-readable equivalent https://static.case.law/f2d/845/cases/0023-01.json . Independently cross-checked against https://openjurist.org/845/f2d/23 . All returned HTTP 200 with TLS verification enabled.

Safe complete text: /tmp/starrett_cap_primary_verified.txt . Page-anchored primary HTML: /tmp/starrett_cap_verified.html . CAP JSON: /tmp/starrett_cap_verified.json . OpenJurist source and extracted opinion-only text: /tmp/starrett_primary_verified.html and /tmp/starrett_primary_verified.txt . Prefer CAP where OpenJurist has OCR errors or an uncorrected parenthetical.

Breyer authored the court opinion; panel: Campbell, Chief Judge, Breyer, and Selya. The complete primary text records no separate writing or withheld assent by a panel member. Decided April 25, 1988; the same reporter entry includes the complete May 26, 1988 memorandum and order denying rehearing. Do not silently omit the latter.

Holding, 24–26: §1145's federal duty to contribute applies to an employer already obligated to make the contributions; it does not independently impose the corporation's contribution obligation on its president/sole shareholder solely because of that office/ownership. The individual had made no personal promise; veil piercing was virtually conceded not to be at issue. Summary judgment against him reversed and case remanded.

Expressly undecided, 24–25: whether the officer is an “employer” under §1002(5). Breyer said the court would likely accept the broader FLSA-like reading if required to decide it, but only assumed employer status for the case. That tentative preference is not a holding adopting an economic-reality test for ERISA.

Alter ego, 26–27: the construction leaves room for an owner already liable under state or other relevant federal law to be an employer obligated to contribute. This is a preserved alternative, not an adjudication that these facts satisfy any veil-piercing test. Rehearing specifically accepts that Alman v. Danin used federal substantive law looking to congressional policy and a federal three-factor test; state law may furnish guidance. The court modified its wording to make clear that the relevant source can be state law OR appropriate federal law. It is inaccurate to report Starrett as categorically requiring state-law veil piercing.

Civil enforcement, 27: in rejecting rehearing, the court held that §1132 allows enforcement of plan terms or other substantive provisions but does not itself create the legal obligation to make contributions. A plan, or the plan with other law, may create the obligation; §1132 does not independently authorize recovery from an unrelated person. The parties identified only §1145 as their substantive source.

Source limitation: no holding here concerns §1109(b)'s before/after-fiduciary temporal limitation, whether a particular act was a fiduciary act, or the scope of relief for an independently established fiduciary breach. “Heretofore or hereafter” appears only in a quoted definition of a plan, not a temporal fiduciary-liability holding.

## Connors v. P & M Coal Co., 801 F.2d 1373 (D.C. Cir. 1986)

Primary text: https://static.case.law/f2d/801/html/1373-01.html ; machine-readable equivalent https://static.case.law/f2d/801/cases/1373-01.json . Independently cross-checked against https://openjurist.org/801/f2d/1373 . All returned HTTP 200 with TLS verification enabled.

Safe complete text: /tmp/connors_cap_primary_verified.txt . Page-anchored primary HTML: /tmp/connors_cap_verified.html . CAP JSON: /tmp/connors_cap_verified.json . OpenJurist source and extracted opinion-only text: /tmp/connors_primary_verified.html and /tmp/connors_primary_verified.txt .

Decided September 12, 1986. Buckley authored the opinion for the court; panel: Ginsburg, Scalia, and Buckley. The full text contains no separate opinion or withheld assent. This is an unqualified panel court opinion, not an opinion authored by Scalia or Ginsburg.

Holding, 1374, 1376–1378: Title IV/MPPAA withdrawal liability does not attach to corporate owner-officers merely by reason of ownership, operational control, or active conduct of legitimate corporate business. The court rejects the proposed interest-and-control/economic-reality route to personal statutory-employer liability in that setting. It relies on language, limited-liability background, statutory liquidation/bankruptcy protections, and concern about deterring plan participation. Orders denying the individuals summary judgment and granting the trustees summary judgment reversed.

Expressly undecided, 1377: the scope of “employer” under Title I and the general applicability of Title I definitions to Title IV. The opinion should not be reported as deciding either question categorically.

Alter ego, 1378: the court expressly preserves personal liability for owners who are alter egos or satisfy traditional veil-piercing principles. The actual alternative-ground resolution is that the trustees neither alleged that basis nor supplied evidence for it; therefore the individuals cannot be held liable on that basis here. The case does not define a comprehensive ERISA veil-piercing test or decide a successful application of one.

Source limitation: no temporal fiduciary-status or fiduciary-breach-remedy holding. No resolution of ancillary jurisdiction over a separate later action.

## Calero-Toledo v. Pearson Yacht Leasing Co., 416 U.S. 663 (1974)

Complete official opinion PDF supplied by parent after TLS-verified retrieval: https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/USREPORTS-416/pdf/USREPORTS-416-663.pdf . Read complete extracted text /tmp/chunk4_prior_forfeiture/Calero_Toledo.txt (and source /tmp/chunk4_prior_forfeiture/Calero_Toledo.pdf), including all footnotes, White concurrence, Stewart statement, and Douglas dissent.

Brennan for Burger, White, Marshall, Blackmun, Powell, and Rehnquist throughout; Stewart joins Parts I and II. White concurrence joined by Powell concerns additional contexts in which a prior hearing may be unnecessary. Stewart's statement would hold forfeiture of an innocent and nonnegligent owner's property contrary to Fifth/Fourteenth Amendments for reasons of Douglas's dissent. Douglas agrees on Puerto Rico's three-judge-court status but dissents on the merits.

Actual holdings: Puerto Rican statutes are state statutes for three-judge-court purposes (669–676); in the described movable-property seizure setting, prior notice/hearing could be postponed (676–680); the forfeiture statutes, applied to the yacht on this record, were not unconstitutional merely because the lessor was uninvolved in and unaware of the lessee's illegal act (680–690). Part III expressly rejects the lower court's uncompensated-taking analysis (680).

Entrustment versus knowledge, 689–690: absence of knowledge/involvement was conceded; appellee nevertheless voluntarily entrusted possession to the lessees, and made no allegation or proof of doing all it reasonably could to avoid unlawful use. Consent to possession is analytically distinct from consent to unlawful activity.

Reservations, 689–690: Court says it would be difficult to reject the constitutional claim of an owner whose property was taken without privity/consent; similarly for one proving both lack of involvement/awareness AND all measures reasonably expected to prevent the proscribed use. The latter could make forfeiture lack legitimate purpose and be unduly oppressive. These are serious-constitutional-question reservations, not resolved constitutional exemptions or a finally defined due-care test. The opinion provides no exhaustive preventive-measures checklist.

Statutory scope, 665 n.1, 687 n.25: quoted enactment reaches conveyances transporting or facilitating transport, sale, receipt, possession, or concealment of controlled substances. The opinion notes judicially recognized common-carrier and unlawful-possession exceptions parallel to federal statutory provisions; do not conflate those statutory constructions with a newly held federal constitutional exception.

Instrumentality/compensation limits: the yacht's alleged unlawful use was the basis for forfeiture, and the Court discusses preventing renewed illegal use, deterrence, and economic penalties (686–688). It does not announce or apply a separate “meaningful instrumentality connection” constitutional test. Douglas's observation about one cigarette and trivia (693) is dissent, not majority finding of an unconstitutional minimum-connection violation. The majority's rejection of compensation here remains limited by its stated constitutional reservations; it does not validate every confiscation labeled forfeiture.

## Van Oster v. Kansas, 272 U.S. 465 (1926)

Official source supplied after TLS verification: https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/USREPORTS-272/pdf/USREPORTS-272-465.pdf ; complete text read at /tmp/chunk4_prior_forfeiture/Van_Oster.txt ; PDF at same base path .pdf. Stone authored the Court opinion; no separate writing or dissent recorded.

Holding, 467–468: due process permits state forfeiture of a vehicle used to transport illegal liquor when an innocent owner entrusted possession/use to the wrongdoer, although the unlawful transportation was unauthorized. Such rules may induce care and avoid the need to adjudicate collusion. The police-power/federal-revenue distinction does not create a constitutional barrier in this setting.

Express reservation, 467: unnecessary to decide confiscation where the lawbreaker appropriated and used innocent persons' property without the owner's consent. This is consent to possession/use generally, not a requirement of knowing authorization of criminal use.

Other actual matters, 468–469: broader state reach than National Prohibition Act §26 does not invalidate it; evidence sufficiency and effect of the wrongdoer's later acquittal are treated as state procedural questions; jury claim both not properly raised and unsubstantial. No separate compensatory-taking holding and no general judicially formulated instrumentality test.

## J. W. Goldsmith, Jr.-Grant Co. v. United States, 254 U.S. 505 (1921)

Official source supplied after TLS verification: https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/USREPORTS-254/pdf/USREPORTS-254-505.pdf ; complete text read at /tmp/chunk4_prior_forfeiture/Goldsmith.txt ; PDF at same base path .pdf. McKenna authored the Court opinion; McReynolds dissents without an accompanying written rationale.

Holding, 508–511, 513: Rev. Stat. §3450 authorizes forfeiture of the retained-title seller's interest in an automobile actually used to remove/conceal untaxed spirits despite the seller's lack of knowledge, notice, or reason for suspicion; this application is constitutional. It is actual illegal use, not proof of the titleholder's guilt, that triggers this statute's forfeiture. §§3460–3461 do not modify the statutory result (512).

Reservations, 512: Court expressly does not decide broad hypothetical application to a Pullman sleeper with a passenger's bottle or an ocean steamer carrying an innocently received package; it also reserves property stolen or otherwise taken without the owner's privity/consent. Thus it would overread the case to say the Court approved any incidental criminal presence anywhere on an asset, or resolved theft-based forfeiture. It likewise would overread it to say the case held a particular freestanding constitutional instrumentality standard. McReynolds's bare dissent supplies no stated doctrinal ground.

## United States v. Fuller, 409 U.S. 488 (1973)

Official source supplied after TLS verification: https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/USREPORTS-409/pdf/USREPORTS-409-488.pdf ; complete text read at /tmp/chunk4_prior_forfeiture/Fuller.txt ; PDF at same base path .pdf. Rehnquist authored the Court opinion, joined by Burger, Stewart, White, Blackmun. Powell dissented, joined by Douglas, Brennan, Marshall. Full dissent and all footnotes read.

Actual holding, 490–494: eminent-domain compensation for fee land need not include incremental value from aggregating it with government-owned grazing land available only under revocable permits whose governing statute creates no property right. Congress supplied no statutory compensation beyond the constitutional requirement here.

General reasoning and explicit qualification, 492–493: prior cases go far toward a general principle that government as condemnor need not pay for value it created or could destroy through a governmental authority other than eminent domain. The Court immediately warns the principle cannot be pushed to its ultimate logical conclusion; value from completed public works and ordinary proximity to public facilities remain distinct. The majority's working rule rests on a revocable permit to use government-owned neighboring land as part of a privately controlled unit.

Source-scope warning: Fuller is not a forfeiture case and does not directly hold that validly forfeited private property requires no compensation. It supplies an analogy about distinct governmental authority and compensable interests, qualified as stated. Powell's dissent disputes exclusion of real location value where grazing permits remained unrevoked; it is not a forfeiture opinion either.
