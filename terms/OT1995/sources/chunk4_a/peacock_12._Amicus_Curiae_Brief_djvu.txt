C. The Delinquency Problem Faced By Taft-Hartley 
Funds 


The trustees of the IPF have adopted written collection 
procedures which insure that employers are given an 
initial opportunity to pay delinquent contributions and, 
if the delinquent sums are not collected, that the matters 
are then referred to counsel for action. In 1994, there 
were approximately 3,200 delinquent IPF employers, and 
323 delinquencies in the hands of counsel, of which 86 
ultimately had to be litigated. Section 502(e)(2) of 
ERISA (29 U.S.C. § 1132(e)(2)) provides that the 
IPF may institute all its collection cases in Washington, 
D.C. “where the plan is administered,” regardless of where 
the participating employer is located. This provision en- 
ables the IPF to bring all such cases in its home judicial 
district, resulting in a great savings to the plan in court 
costs and a great tactical advantage in resolving delin- 
quencies in the IPF’s home forum.”

For trust funds such as the IPF, there is little difficulty

SUMMARY OF ARGUMENT 


The question presented by this case is whether federal 
courts can continue to apply in ERISA cases the well- 
established doctrine of federal common law veil piercing 
to allow beneficiaries and funds to recover benefits due 
them under ERISA. This Court has consistently disre- 
garded the corporate form and pierced the veil when 
required to prevent fraud or injustice or to avoid defeat- 
ing legislative policies. The veil piercing doctrine is a 
broad “equitable principle,” or a “qualification or denial” 
of the principle of limited liability. It is not, as the court 
below recognized, itself a “cause of action”.

The federal common law of veil piercing has been 
uniformly recognized and applied by the courts of appeals 
in ERISA delinquency cases. The circuits have all adopted 
a parallel three-factor federal common law veil piercing 
test, which “demands a substantial showing” by a plain- 
tiff that each factor is present. The Fourth Circuit prop- 
erly applied this familiar test to respondent Thomas’ evi- 
dence, which proved that Petitioner Peacock siphoned off 
the assets of Tru-Tech, a corporation he controlled, for 
his own personal benefit and to deny his employees the 
ERISA benefits Tru-Tech owed them. Given the difficul- 
ties of uncovering corporate machinations like Peacock’s, 
and the substantial evidentiary burden a plaintiff must 
shoulder, affirmance of the judgment below will not open 
the litigation floodgates or expose corporate shareholders 
to unwarranted liability. 


The decision below does not create a new “cause of 
action,” ERISA remedy or category of persons subject to 
ERISA’s liability, or otherwise run afoul of Mertens v. 
Hewitt Associates, US. » 113 & Ce. 2063 
(1993) and Massachusetts Mutual Life Insurance Co. v. 
Russell, 473 U.S. 134 (1985). Once the court of appeals 
pierced Tru-Tech’s corporate veil due to Peacock’s abuse 
of the corporation, Peacock and Tru-Tech became “one 
for all purposes.” United States v. Lehigh Valley R.R., 220 
U.S. 257, 272, 274 (1911). The court below properly 
found him liable under ERISA, not because he is an 
ERISA fiduciary in his own right, but because he is 
True-Tech, a conceded ERISA fiduciary. 


Taft-Hartley plans faced such daunting problems col- 
lecting delinquent employer contributions that Congress 
amended ERISA in 1980 to give it even more “teeth.” 
Against this background, reversal of the Fourth Circuit 
would take away a weapon ERISA plans had even before 
the 1980 amendments, and run counter to Congress’ ex- 
press intent to facilitate plan collections from delinquent 
employers, including those who loot their corporations. 


7 


Every court to have addressed the issue in an ERISA 
case has recognized the veil piercing doctrine, even those 
which, consistent with Mertens, have simultaneously re- 
buffed attempts to expand ERISA’s definition of “em- 
ployer” in order to reach corporate officers. Indeed, these 
decisions affirmatively, and correctly, find that to sanction 
corporate abuses like Peacock’s in the ERISA context 
would thwart Congress’ discernible intent. 


Contrary to Petitioner’s argument, state law cannot 
govern here. First, collection of employer delinquencies 
can only be brought in federal court. Second, because 
Peacock stands in the shoes of a fiduciary in breach, 
Thomas’ second action clearly “relates to” an ERISA 
plan, and state law would be preempted by ERISA. 


Under Peacock’s theory, the veil piercing doctrine could 
be used in an ERISA case only if the corporate officer 
was brazen or witless enough to abuse the corporate form 
(and the beneficiaries or fund lucky enough to learn of 
the abuses) before an adverse judgment was entered 
against the corporation. If neither event occurred before 
judgment, the federal doctrine (and a federal forum) 
would be unavailable. But the availability of veil piercing 
in ERISA cases cannot turn on the fortuity of the timing 
of the “corporate strategies” employed by schemers like 
Peacock, if only because such a rule would reward those 
who are the cleverest and most sophisticated in the use 
of the veil to disguise their siphoning off of corporate 
assets. 


Finally, reversal here would deprive Taft-Hartley and 
other ERISA funds of important procedural and tactical 
advantages Congress was careful to afford them, such 
as the right to sue employers in the district where the plan 
is administered, and to effect nationwide service of process. 
What Peacock advocates would not only strip away these 
statutory protections, but would also mean that every 
ERISA delinquency case involving veil piercing would be 


brought in two states, in two forums and under two sets 
of laws—first in federal court (and under federal law) 
where the Plan is administered, and then, under state law, 
in whatever state the employer happened to be located. 
This patchwork of litigation, with its attendant expense 
and difficulty for the funds, is obviously desirable for 
corporate manipulators like Peacock (whose goal it is to 
cheat their employees out of ERISA benefits), but harm- 
ful to fund beneficiaries. Allowing Peacock to reach that 
goal in this fashion is contrary to the letter and the spirit 
of ERISA. 


ARGUMENT 


I. THE ORIGINS AND NECESSITY OF THE CORPO- 
RATE VEIL PIERCING DOCTRINE 


Chief Justice Marshall observed nearly 200 years ago 
that a corporation is “an artificial being, invisible, intangi- 
ble, and existing only in contemplation of law.” Trustees 
of Dartmcuth College v. Woodward, 17 U.S. (4 Wheat.) 
250, 303 (1819). The venerable doctrine of “piercing the 
corporate veil” is a necessary reality check on this legal 
fiction, and its attendant privilege of limited liability. As 
Professor Wormser (who apparently popularized the term 
“veil piercing”) has written, 


[s]ince the element of personality is an extraordinary 
privilege conferred upon the corporation by the law, 
and involves the employment of a fiction, . . . “[the 
corporate form] must be used for legitimate business 
purposes and must not be perverted”... . 


I. Maurice Wormser, Disregard of the Corporate Fiction 
and Allied Corporation Problems 8-9 (New York: Baker, 
Voorhis & Co. 1927) (citation omitted). Accordingly, 


the fiction will be disregarded and the law will look 
to see the men and facts behind the fiction whenever 
it is employed “to defraud creditors, to evade an 
existing obligation, to circumvent a statute, to achieve 


9 
or perpetuate monopoly, or to protect knavery and 
crime.” 
Id. at 40. 


These doctrines are likewise firmly embedded in the 
jurisprudence of this Court, which has 


long recognized “the broader equitable principle that 
the doctrine of corporate entity, recognized generally 
and for most purposes, will not be regarded when 
to do so would work fraud or injustice.” Taylor v. 
Standard Gas Co., 306 U.S. 307, 322 (1939). In 
particular, the Court has consistently refused to give 
effect to the corporate form where it is interposed to 
defeat legislative policies. 


First Nat'l City Bank v. Banco Para El Comercio Ex- 
terior De Cuba, 462 U.S. 611, 629-30 (1983). See also 
Anderson v. Abbott, 321 U.S. 349, 362 (1944); Lehigh 
Valley, 220 U.S. at 271-74. 


Limited liability of corporations, then, is a privilege, 
just as the veil-piercing exception is a prophylactic limita- 
tion on that privilege. This exception has been variously 
termed an “equitable principle” (First Nat'l City Bank, 
462 U.S. at 629), a “qualification or denial” of the 
principle of limited liability (Anderson, 321 U.S. at 
362), an “exception” to the “rule” of limited liability 
(Frank H. Easterbrook & Daniel R. Fischel, Limited 
Liability and the Corporation, 52 U. Chi. L. Rev. 89 
(1985)), a “doctrine” (id. at 109), or a “disregard” of 
the “fiction” of the corporate form (Wormser, supra, at 
40). But an attempt to pierce the corporate veil 


“is not itself a cause of action but rather is a means 
of imposing liability on an underlying cause of ac- 
tion ... .” 1 William Meade Fletcher, Fletcher 
Cyclopedia of the Law of Private Corporations § 41, 
at 603 (perm. ed. rev. vol. 1990).

(emphasis added).

Il. THE TEST FOR VEIL PIERCING IN ERISA CASES 
IS WELL-DEFINED AND DIFFICULT TO MEET 


The doctrine of piercing the corporate veil has been 
applied by many courts in ERISA delinquency cases. As 
articulated in Alman v. Danin, 801 F.2d 1, 4 (1st Cir. 
1986), relied upon by the court below (Thomas, 39 F.3d 
at 504), a court will look to three factors in determining 
whether to pierce the corporate veil: (1) the respect paid 
by the individuals to the corporate identity; (2) the fraud- 
ulent intent of the principals; and (3) the degree of in- 
justice that would be visited on the litigants by recognizing 
the corporate identity. Accord United Elec. Workers v. 
163 Pleasant St. Corp., 960 F.2d 1080, 1092-93 (1st Cir. 
1992) (Breyer, Selya and Cyr, JJ.). The other courts 
of appeals have adopted a substantially similar federal 
common law veil piercing test in ERISA cases. See, e.g., 
cases cited in id.; see also Operating Eng’rs Pension Trust 
v. Reed, 736 F.2d 513, 515 (9th Cir. 1984) (Kennedy, 
J.). 


That three-factor test “demands a substantial showing” 
by a plaintiff that each factor is present. United Elec., 
960 F.2d at 1092-93. To meet that burden, a litigant 
must prove, for example, that corporate formalities and 
independence were ignored (Laborers Clean-Up Contract 
Admin. Trust Fund v. Uriarte Clean-Up Serv., Inc., 736 
F.2d 516, 524 (9th Cir. 1984)); that an owner looted 
the company for the owner’s personal benefit (Laborers’ 
Pension Trust Fund v. Sidney Weinberger Homes, Inc., 
872 F.2d 702, 705 (6th Cir. 1988)); that the firm had 
“wholly inadequate capitalization” (Lowen v. Tower 
Asset Management, Inc., 829 F.2d 1209, 1221 (2d Cir. 
1987)); or that the owner abused the corporate form 
“in an inequitable fashion to avoid its obligations” (Con- 
tractors, Laborers, Teamsters & Eng’rs Health & Welfare 
Plan v. Hroch, 757 F.2d 184, 191 (8th Cir. 1985)). The 
Fourth Circuit here did no more than apply this familiar 
test to Thomas’ proof which, as the court of appeals 


11 


found (in a holding Peacock did not appeal to this 
Court), established that Peacock 


engaged in a “corporate strategy” with “the specific 
intent and purpose . . . [of] siphon{ing] off [Tru- 
Tech’s] assets to favored creditors owned or con- 
trolled by Peacock and to defeat collection of the 
Thomas judgmert.” 


Thomas, 39 F.3d at 505. 


Permitting funds such as the IPF to continue to pursue 
unscrupulous individuals like Peacock will not subject 
officers and shareholders of legitimate corporations to 
wholesale imposition of ERISA liability. The “piercing” 
doctrine will continue to come into play only when a 
plaintiff makes a “substantial showing” that there has 
been a total disregard of the corporate form, or that the 
corporation has been converted into a vehicle to defraud 
beneficiaries. In (and only in) those circumstances, the 
perpetrators will be denied the protection of the corporate 
veil, not because there is an independent “cause of action” 
against them under a “piercing” theory, but because the 
wrongdoer is no longer entitled to the protection of the 
legal fiction of the corporate shield, and will be deemed 
to be the corporation and, as such, liable under ERISA 
for the payment of contributions. 


Ill. THE UNIVERSALLY RECOGNIZED FEDERAL 
VEIL PIERCING DOCTRINE FOLLOWED BY THE 
FOURTH CIRCUIT IS CONSISTENT WITH ERISA 
AND THIS COURT’S TEACHINGS 


Petitioner cites no case (because to our knowledge there 
is none) in which a court has refused to apply the veil 
piercing doctrine to any other federal statute. Peacock 
nonetheless argues that Congress chose ERISA as its first 
vehicle to forbid federal courts from piercing the corpo- 
rate veil, thereby allowing abuses of the corporate form 
designed to evade ERISA obligations, to be remedied 
(if at all) in the state courts. 


12 


Petitioner never comes to grips with the fact that every 
court facing the issue has recognized that veil piercing is 
available in an appropriate ERISA breach of fiduciary 
duty case, as in all others. See, e.g., Massachusetts 
Laborers’ Health & Welfare Fund v. Starrett Paving Corp., 
845 F.2d 23, 26-27 (1st Cir. 1988) (Breyer, J.); United

IV. THIS COURT SHOULD DECLINE PETITIONER’S

The problem of employer delinquencies was so acute 
and posed such an economic threat to the well-being of 
Taft-Hartley funds that in 1980, the statute was amended 
to give it “teeth.” See Ryan, 767 F.2d at 1173-74. Na- 
tional funds like the IPF must chase thousands of delin- 
quent employers every year. The most difficult delin- 
quency cases do not involve employers with ongoing 
operations, but employers who are “here today and gone 
tomorrow.” More and more, this phenomenon of the 
vanishing or insolvent employer is attributable to fraud 
rather than any economic malaise. In those instances 
when an employer has disregarded the corporate form 
in order to perpetrate a fraud on creditors, including the

fund, the doctrine of piercing the corporate veil has 
proven to be the only effective weapon.” 


This is not a case in which beneficiaries or Taft-Hartley 
funds are asking the Court for expanded powers under 
ERISA. The doctrine of piercing the corporate veil is so 
well-established and so widely accepted by the courts that 
it has been used for decades to combat corporate fraud 
in ERISA cases. Peacock now asks this Court to turn 
back the clock by taking that weapon away from the funds 
and relegating them to the vagaries of state litigation. 
Such action not only would be contrary to the policy of 
ERISA, but would be unfair to those that Congress ex- 
pressly sought to protect.
