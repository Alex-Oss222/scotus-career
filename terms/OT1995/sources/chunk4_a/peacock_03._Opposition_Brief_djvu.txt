RESPONDENT'S BRIEF IN OPPOSITION 


STATEMENT OF THE CASE 


This is a lawsuit brought by the Respondent, Jack L. 
Thomas (“Thomas”) to pierce Tru-Tech, Inc.’s corporate 
veil in order to reach the Petitioner, D. Grant Peacock 
(“Peacock”), an officer and shareholder of Tru-Tech. 


Thomas, a former employee of Tru-Tech, originally 
brought a class action suit against Tru-Tech and Peacock 
under the Employee Retirement Income Security Act of 
1974 (ERISA), 29 U.S.C. § 1001, et seg. The district 
court ruled in favor of the plaintiff class as against Tru- 
Tech but held Peacock was not liable as a plan fiduciary. 
Thomas v. Tru-Tech, Inc., No. 87-2243-3, 1988 U.S. 
Dist. LEXIS 15929, 1988 WL 212511 (D.S.C. Nov. 28, 
1988). The United States Court of Appeals for the 


2 


Fourth Circuit affirmed the district court’s opinion. 
Thomas v. Tru-Tech, Inc., 900 F.2d 256 (4th Cir. 1990) 
(unpublished disposition; full text at 1990 WL 48865). 
Thomas then commenced this current litigation against 
Peacock individually, seeking to pierce Tru-Tech’s cor- 
porate veil. 


Thomas sued Peacock individually because Peacock and 
his attorney, Alan H. Finegold, schemed to siphon off 
Tru-Tech’s assets in order to prevent the collection of 
the judgment against Tru-Tech. Money was transferred 
from Tru-Tech to Peacock’s wife, to Peacock’s lawyer 
and to Peacock’s business, Peacock, Williams & Company 
(“P,W & C”). At a Tru-Tech Board meeting held on 
March 6, 1989, Peacock agreed to pay $130,000 of 
Tru-Tech funds to P,W & C. Although Peacock claimed 
that this transfer was to repay P,W & C for money paid 
to Tru-Tech’s president to manage Tru-Tech’s affairs, the 
district court noted that the evidence showed that P,W & C 
had previously written off that debt. On July 1, 1989, 
the Tru-Tech Board of Directors, of which Peacock 
was by then the sole member, approved a transfer of 
$47,000 to P.W & C. In February of 1990, Peacock 
officially resigned as a Director of Tru-Tech, but he con- 
tinued to sign checks drawn on Tru-Tech’s bank accounts 
for months to come. Based on transfers of funds such 
as the ones described, the district court found that Pea- 
cock failed to observe the corporate form and depleted 
Tru-Tech’s assets in order to avoid collection of the 
Thomas judgment and in order to enrich himself. The 
district court ruled that it was proper for Thomas to 
pierce Tru-Tech’s corporate veil in order to reach Pea- 
cock. Peacock appealed to the Fourth Circuit which 
affirmed the district court’s decision. Thomas v. Peacock, 
39 F.3d 493 (4th Cir. 1994). 


In a unanimous opinion written by Judge Donald 
Russell and joined by Judge Widener and Senior Judge 
Chapman, the Fourth Circuit held that the federal court 
had subject matter jurisdiction over the case because it 


3 


arose under ERISA. Furthermore, the Court ruled that 
the district court did not err by applying a federal com- 
mon law standard for piercing the corporate veil. 


The Fourth Circuit reasoned that the equitable doctrine 
of piercing the corporate veil was “necessarily subsidiary 
to some primary cause of action,. ... Thus, ... nota 
mere procedural rule relating to ‘how’ a judgment is to 
be enforced, but is, rather, a substantive rule of liability.” 
39 F.3d at 500. The court took great pains to ensure 
that its ruling was in compliance with H. C. Cook Co. v. 
Beecher, 217 U.S. 497, 30 S. Ct. 601 (1910). Thomas’ 
effort to pierce the corporate veil was not found to be 
independent of his prior litigation and, thus, not in conflict 
with Beecher. 39 F.3d at 501. Some circuits have based 
federal ancillary jurisdiction in veil-piercing actions on 
the proposition that this Court’s decision in Beecher may 
lack continuing vitality. Argento v. Village of Melrose 
Park, 838 F.2d 1483 (7th Cir. 1988); Skevofilax v. 
Quigley, 810 F.2d 378 (3d Cir. 1987) (en banc), cert. 
denied, 481 U.S. 1029, 107 S. Ct. 1956 (1987). The 
Fourth Circuit, by contrast, did not challenge Beecher 
but simply distinguished it on its facts. Whereas Thomas 
alleges that Peacock wrongfully ignored corporate for- 
malities and improperly transferred funds in an effort to 
evade the judgment against Tru-Tech, Beecher involved 
a suit to enforce a judgment against officers of a corpora- 
tion who were acting within their legal capacity. 39 F.3d 
at 501. 


After establishing that jurisdiction was proper in federal 
court, the Fourth Circuit then ruled that applying the 
federal common law rule for piercing the corporate veil 
was appropriate in ERISA cases. While recognizing that 
Mackey v. Lanier Collection Agency & Service, 486 U.S. 
825, 108 S. Ct. 2182 (1988), held that “ERISA does not 
provide an enforcement mechanism for collecting judg- 
ments won” in suits brought by plan beneficiaries, pursu- 
ant to ERISA § 502(a)(1)(B), (d), 29 U.S.C. § 1132 
(a)(1)(B), (d), against ERISA benefit plans, the Fourth 


4 


Circuit noted that piercing the corporate veil is not 
merely a mechanism for collecting a judgment. 39 
F.3d at 503. Because Thomas seeks to pierce the cor- 
porate veil to determine who is liable under ERISA, the 
court held that ERISA preempts state law rules of veil- 
piercing. /d. Therefore, the federal common law rule 
of veil-piercing must apply in ERISA actions. The Fourth 
Circuit adopted the rule articulated by the First Circuit 
in Alman v. Danin, 801 F.2d 1 (1st Cir. 1986),’ finding 
its reasoning persuasive and in accordance with the veil- 
piercing rules followed in other circuits. 39 F.3d at 503. 


For these reasons, the Fourth Circuit found that the 
district court had properly asserted ancillary jurisdiction 
and had properly applied the federal common law veil- 
piercing standard when piercing the corporate veil under 
ERISA. 

SUMMARY OF ARGUMENT 


First, the Fourth Circuit’s decision is in keeping with 
this Court’s decisions in Beecher and Kokkonen vy. 
Guardian Life Insurance Co., 114 S. Ct. 1673 (1994), 
over the scope of federal ancillary jurisdiction. In addi- 
tion, there is no split among the circuits as to the very 
narrow question of ancillary jurisdiction over an action 
to pierce the corporate veil in order to collect a judge- 
ment in an ERISA case. Simply put, it would be pre- 
mature for this Court to grant Peacock’s petition because 
so few courts have addressed this narrow issue. Nor has 
the impact of 28 U.S.C. § 1367 been felt on this issue. 
So even if this Court disagrees and finds a split among 
the circuits, that split may disappear once courts have 
an opportunity to apply and interpret § 1367. 


Second, the Fourth Circuit’s decision is not contrary to 
this Court’s decisions in Mertens v. Hewitt Associates, 113 


'In Alman, the First Circuit reasoned that because ERISA does 
not attach great weight to corporate form, the “corporate entity 
may be disregarded in the interests of public convenience, fairness 
and equity.” 801 F.2d at 3 (quoting Town of Brookline v. Gorsuch, 
667 F.2d 215, 221 (1st Cir. 1981) ). 


5 


S. Ct. 2063 (1993) or Mackey. The equitable doctrine 
of piercing the corporate veil is a substantive rule of 
liability, not a mere collection action. Furthermore, there 
is no confusion among the circuit courts as to piercing 
the corporate veil in ERISA cases. The petitioner sees 
confusion and discord where none exists. The Court 
should deny the petition for a Writ of Certiorari. 


REASONS FOR DENYING THE PETITION 


I. THE FOURTH CIRCUIT’S DECISION IS NOT IN 
CONFLICT WITH THIS COURT’S DECISIONS IN 
BEECHER OR KOKKONEN. 


The Fourth Circuit found that it had ancillary juris- 
diction over Thomas’ attempt to pierce the corporate veil. 
This decision is not in conflict with Beecher. In Beecher, 
a corporate patent holder brought a patent infringement 
action in federal court. While the suit was pending, the 
directors of the defendant corporation voted to continue 
sales despite allegations in the lawsuit that the sales were 
in violation of the. plaintiff's patent. Although winning 
the patent infringement case, plaintiff was unable to col- 
lect from the defendant corporation because it had be- 
come insolvent. Plaintiff then filed another lawsuit in 
federal court against the directors of the defendant cor- 
poration, alleging that the defendant directors had voted 
to continue sales with the purpose of rendering the com- 
pany insolvent in the event that the plaintiff was success- 
ful in its patent infringement suit. The Court found that 
the plaintiff was attempting “to make the defendants 
answerable for the judgment already obtained,” 217 U:S. 
at 498, 30 S. Ct. at 601-02, and held that the case did not 
fall within the federal court’s ancillary jurisdiction. Jd. 
at 499, 30 S. Ct. at 602. 


Beecher is distinguishable from the case before this 
Court. Beecher dealt with litigation that was truly in- 
dependent from the prior litigation. Thomas’ effort to 
pierce the corporate veil is not independent of his prior 


6 


litigation. As the Fourth Circuit noted, “an attempt to 
pierce the corporate veil is necessarily subsidiary to some 
primary cause of action.” 39 F.3d at 500. By its very 
nature, an attempt to pierce the corporate veil cannot be 
independent of the primary cause of action. 


Nor is the Fourth Circuit’s decision in conflict with 
Kokkonen. As Peacock himself concedes, Kokkonen is 
not controlling. Kokkonen addresses the federal court's 
jurisdiction to enforce a settlement agreement from a 
prior federal case. The litigation over the settlement 
agreement was for breach of a contract and was purely 
independent from the prior federal case. In Kokkonen, 
the Court noted that 


Generally speaking we have asserted ancillary juris- 
diction (in the very broad sense in which that term 
is sometimes used) for two separate, though some- 
times related, purposes: (1) to permit disposition by 
a single court of claims that are, in varying respects 
and degrees, factually interdependent; and (2) to 
enable a court to function successfully that is, to 
manage its proceedings, vindicate its authority, and 
effectuate its decrees. 


114 S. Ct. at 1676 (citations omitted). As to the first pur- 
pose enunciated by Kokkonen, by its very nature an at- 
tempt to pierce the corporate veil is factually dependent 
upon the earlier claims under ERISA. As to the second 
purpose for ancillary jurisdiction, by attempting to pierce 
the corporate veil, Thomas is clearly asking the federal 
court to effectuate its decree in the earlier action. This 
is the type of situation envisioned by the Court in Kok- 
konen as warranting the exercise of ancillary jurisdiction: 


“The situation would be quite different if the parties’ 
obligation to comply with the terms of the settlement 
agreement had been made part of the order of dis- 
missal—either by separate provision (such as a pro- 
vision ‘retaining jurisdiction’ over the settlement 
agreement) or by incorporating the terms of the set- 
tlement agreement in the order. In that event, a 


— TT ew - 


<= 


7 


breach of the agreement would be a violation of the 
order, and ancillary jurisdiction to enforce the agree- 
ment would therefore exist.” 


Id. at 1677. That is close to the situation presented 
here. The district court found that Peacock failed to 
observe the corporate form and deliberately siphoned off 
Tru-Tech’s assets with the specific intent and purpose of 
defeating the collection of the judgment against Tru-Fech. 
Clearly, the federal courts may exercise jurisdiction over 
an action to pierce the corporate veil in order to hold 
liable one who has been actively and purposefully at- 
tempting to defeat a legitimate effort to collect a lawful 
judgment. To conclude otherwise would cast into doubt 


a court's ability to vindicate its authority and to effectu- 
ate its decrees. 


Il. THERE IS NO MEANINGFUL SPLIT IN THE CIR- 
CUITS CONCERNING FEDERAL ANCILLARY 
JURISDICTION OVER ACTIONS TO PIERCE THE 
CORPORATE VEIL IN ORDER TO COLLECT 
PRIOR JUDGMENTS UNDER ERISA. 


Peacock cites Sandlin v. Corporate Interiors, Inc., 972 
F.2d 1212 (10th Cir. 1992), Sederquist v. Court, 861 
F.2d 554 (9th Cir. 1988), Berry v. McLemore, 795 F.2d 
452 (Sth Cir. 1986), Redding Ford v. California State 
Bd. Of Equalization, 722 F.2d 496 (9th Cir. 1983). 
Fidelity & Casualty Co. v. Reserve Ins. Co., 596 F.2d 
914 (9th Cir. 1979), and National Westminster Bank 
U.S.A. v. Cheng, 751 F. Supp. 1158 (S.D.N.Y. 1990) 
as cases demonstrating a split in the circuits. The facts 
of these cases are distinguishable from the facts currently 
before the Court. Not one of those cases deals with an 
effort to pierce the corporate veil under ERISA. 


Sandlin actually supports the Fourth Circuit’s decision 
to find ancillary jurisdiction. Although the Tenth Circuit 
found no adequate basis for ancillary jurisdiction when it 
applied the Beecher standard to the facts before it. the 
court noted that ancillary jurisdiction may well be found 


8 


if the alter ego claims were more closely connected to the 
underlying claim. Furthermore, the court indicated that 
ancillary jurisdiction may be based upon a successor 
theory if the plaintiff could allege “post-judgment trans- 
fers of property.” 972 F.2d at 1217 (citations omitted ). 
The facts that the Tenth Circuit foresaw as being able 
to provide ancillary jurisdiction exist in this case. 
Thomas’ allegations that Peacock was the alter ego for 
Tru-Tech are irreversibly bound to the merits of the 
initial litigation and involve post-judgment transfers of 
property in an effort to defeat collection of the initial 


judgment. 


Nor can it be said that Berry is in direct conflict with 
the Fourth Circuit’s decision. Berry involves a garnish- 
ment action brought against the alleged indemnitors of a 
judgment debtor. The sole connection between the alleged 
indemnitors and the initial litigation was an indemnity 
contract with the defendants in the initial case. The al- 
leged indemnitors played no role in the initial litigation. 
Such cannot be said about Peacock and the initial litiga- 
tion brought by Thomas. 


Peacock argues that the Second Circuit should also be 
counted among the circuits in direct conflict with the 
Fourth Circuit’s ruling. He reaches this conclusion on 
the basis. not of a case decided by the Second Circuit, 
but on a case decided by the district court in the South- 
ern District of New York. National Westminster Bank, 
like the case before this Court, involves an effort to pierce 
the corporate veil in order to collect a judgment. The 
district court found no nexus between the original litiga- 
tion and a subsequent veil-piercing action, and, therefore, 
found no ancillary jurisdiction. Even if this decision is 
at odds with the decision rendered by the Fourth Circuit, 
it is unreasonable to suggest that, on the basis of this one 
decision from a district court, the Second Circuit is 
at odds with the Fourth Circuit. Peacock indicates that 
the decision in National Westminster Bank, standing 
alone, is indicative of the Second Circuit’s position be- 


9 


cause the principle relied upon by the district court was 
derived from the Second Circuit’s opinion in Manway 
Constr. Co. v. Housing Auth. Of the City of Hartford, 
711 F.2d 501 (2d Cir. 1983). As Peacock notes, how- 
ever, Manway involves totally different facts from this 
case. National Westminster Bank simply cannot predict 
the position the Second Circuit will take when presented 
with a veil-piercing action under ERISA to collect a 
prior judgment. 


Likewise, it is ludicrous to suggest that the Fourth 
Circuit is at odds with the Ninth Circuit on this issue 
considering that the Ninth Circuit held in Blackburn 
Truck Lines v. Francis, 723 F.2d 730 (9th Cir. 1984), 
that the federal court had jurisdiction over a suit brought 
to pierce the corporate veil in order to reach the owners 
of ae corporations in order to collect a prior judg- 
ment. 


Il. IT WOULD BE PREMATURE FOR THIS COURT 
TO ADDRESS THE ISSUE OF ANCILLARY JURIS- 
DICTION OVER AN ACTION TO PIERCE THE 
CORPORATE VEIL IN ORDER TO COLLECT A 
PRIOR JUDGMENT UNDER ERISA. 


Granting Peacock’s petition would be premature. First 
of all, there simply have been too few cases decided 
which address this issue. In his efforts to demonstrate a 
split in the circuits, Peacock has cited cases which are 
not close enough factually to warrant comparison. This 
is not merely a case to collect a judgment; it is an action 
to pierce the corporate veil to collect a judgment in an 
ERISA action. Opinions which examine the issue of 
ancillary jurisdiction in litigation brought to collect prior 
judgments, but not involving attempts to pierce the cor- 
porate veil under ERISA, simply do not address the 
narrow issue in this case. 


Furthermore, few courts have had an opportunity to 
address this issue since 28 U.S.C. § 1367, the statute 


10 


codifying ancillary and pendent jurisdiction, went into 
effect on December 1, 1990. Even if this Court were to 
determine that a significant split in the circuits exists on 
the issue of ancillary jurisdiction, there is no way of 
knowing at this time whether the split would remain once 
courts have had an opportunity to apply § 1367. This 
new statute may well result in greater uniformity in the 
circuits for all issues dealing with what used to be known 
as pendent and ancillary jurisdiction. Until more appel- 
late courts are confronted with actions to pierce the cor- 
porate veil in order to collect a judgment in an ERISA 
action and until more courts have had an opportunity to 
apply and interpret 28 U.S.C. § 1367, the issue presented 
to this Court is simply not ripe for consideration. It 
would be premature for this Court to grant Peacock’s 


petition. 


IV. THE FOURTH CIRCUITS DECISION TO APPLY 
FEDERAL COMMON LAW _ VEIL-PIERCING 
STANDARDS IS NOT IN CONFLICT WITH THIS 
COURT’S DECISIONS IN MERTENS OR MACKEY, 
NOR IS THERE CONFUSION ABOUT VEIL- 
PIERCING UNDER ERISA IN THE CIRCUIT 
COURTS. 


In Mertens, the Court held that ERISA does not pro- 
vide for actions seeking monetary damages against non- 
fiduciaries. The Fourth Circuit’s decision does not con- 
flict with this holding. Thomas is not engaging in an 
independent action for monetary damages; he is engaging 
in an equitable action to satisfy a prior judgment. Fur- 
thermore, as discussed above, the veil-piercing action is 
dependent upon the prior litigation. It is not an inde- 
pendent action at all. 


Nor does the Fourth Circuit’s decision conflict with 
Mackey. Peacock relies heavily upon the statement in 
Mackey that “ERISA does not provide an enforcement 
mechanism for collecting judgments won” in actions 
brought against plans by proported beneficiaries or in 


1] 


actions brought by general creditors of the plan. 486 
U.S. at 833, 108 S. Ct. at 2187. As the Fourth Circuit 
noted, however, Peacock’s reliance upon this statement is 
misplaced. Mackey does not preclude the application of 
4 federal common law veil-piercing rule in ERISA ac- 
tions. An attempt to pierce the corporate veil is not a 
mere enforcement mechanism for collection of a judg- 
ment. Thomas’ action seeks to apply the general pur- 
poses and principles of ERISA to pierce Tru-Tech’s cor- 
porate veil in order to hold Peacock liable for the actions 
of his alter ego. The fact that this case is not a mere 
collection action is brought home by the realization that 
a collection action under state law may yet have to be 
brought against Peacock to collect the judgment against 
him if the Fourth Circuit’s decision stands. 


Finally, Peacock argues that there is a “broad and 
continuing confusion” over piercing the corporate veil in 
ERISA actions. There is no such confusion. The courts 
are in consensus that it is proper to pierce the corporate 
veil to enforce a judgment when there is evidence of 
wrongdoing and a failure to observe the corporate form. 
The cases Peacock cites demonstrate this. In /nternational 
Brotherhood of Painters and Allied Trades Union y. 
Kracher, 856 F.2d 1546 (D.C. Cir. 1988), the court 
concluded its opinion by emphatically stating: “[{w]e 
Stress again that this case involves no claim of alter ego 
or conduct that might result in the necessity to pierce the 
corporate veil.” Jd. at 1550, n.28. In Connors v. P & M 
Coal Co., 801 F.2d 1373 (D.C. Cir. 1986), the court 
noted that in general “shareholders and officers may be 
held personally liable for their corporations’ obligations 
under ERISA if they have acted as the alter egos of their 
corporations or otherwise met the requirements that 
justify piercing the corporate veil under traditional com- 
mon law principles.” /d. at 1378. In Operating Engi- 
neers Pension Trust v. Reed, 726 F.2d 513 (9th Cir. 
1984), the court recognized that an individual should be 
held personally liable for trust fund obligations of a 


12 


corporation controlled by that individual only if there has 
been a failure to observe the proper corporate form, and 
there is fraud involved. /d. at 515. Clearly these cases 
are not in conflict with the Fourth Circuit’s decision. 
Courts are not reluctant to pierce the corporate veil when 
they are confronted with fraud and injustice. See Alman, 
801 F.2d 1 (1st Cir. 1986). 


CONCLUSION 


Review by this Court of the Fourth Circuit’s decision 
would be unwarranted and premature. There currently 
exists no significant conflict between the Fourth Circuit’s 
decision and the decisions of this Court and other cir- 
cuits. Furthermore, the impact of 28 U.S.C. § 1367 has 
yet to be felt on the issues presented by this case.
