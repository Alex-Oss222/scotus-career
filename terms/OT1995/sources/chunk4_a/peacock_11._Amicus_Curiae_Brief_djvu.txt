INTEREST OF AMICI CURIAE 
The American Association of Retired Persons (AARP) is 
a nonprofit membership organization of more than 32 million 
persons, age 50 or older, working or retired, that is dedicated to 
addressing the needs and interests of older Americans. AARP 
seeks through education, advocacy and service to enhance the 
quality of life for all by promoting independence, dignity and 


2 
purpose. As a method of promoting independence, AARP 
attempts to foster the economic security of individuals as they age. 
Through educational and advocacy efforts, AARP seeks to 
increase the availability, security, equity, and adequacy of private 
and public pension plans. 

Since 1985, as part of its advocacy efforts, AARP has 
filed numerous briefs in the federal district and appellate courts 
and in the U.S. Supreme Court on behalf of older workers and 
retirees concerning employment and benefits under the Employee 
Retirement Income Security Act (ERISA) and the Age 
Discrimination in Employment Act (ADEA). In 1993, AARP 
established its Pension Equity Project to advocate for security and 
equity in retirement benefits and to educate the public about the 
need for greater safeguards in the pension system. 

The National Employment Lawyers Association (NELA) 
is a voluntary organization, founded in 1985, of over 2,300 
attorneys who specialize in representing individuals in 
controversies arising out of the workplace. It is the country’s only 
professional membership organization comprised of lawyers who 
represent employees in cases involving employment 


discrimination, employee benefits, wrongful discharge, and other 


3 
employment-related matters. NELA has devoted itself to 
supporting precedent-setting litigation remedial legislation affecting 
the rights of individuals in the workplace. 

AARP and NELA are qualified to brief this Court on the 
implications of the decision in this case, having participated as 
amicus curiae in numerous cases involving ERISA and other 
employment laws, including John Hancock Mut. Life Ins. Co. v. 
Harris Trust & Savings Bank, 114 S.Ct 517 (1993), and the 
leading decision on ERISA benefit claims, Firestone Tire & 
Rubber Co. v. Bruch, 489 U.S. 101 (1989). 

AARP’s members, NELA members’ clients, and other 
older Americans depend on the protections that ERISA, 29 U.S.C. 
§ 1001 et seq., imposes on private employer-sponsored employee 
benefit plans. In particular, ERISA’s protections, and the ability 
to enforce these protections, are of vital concern to older workers 
and retirees, since the quality of their lives in retirement depends 
heavily on their receipt of pension benefits. Indeed, individuals 
who receive pensions are less likely to fall into poverty than those 


who do not.” And, those individuals who receive pensions are

BACKGROUND 

It is important to step back and look at what this case is 
really about. Quite simply, after having its rights adjudicated 
during a trial and reviewed by a court of appeals, a defendant 
consciously chose to take unscrupulous actions to evade the court’s 
judgment. The facts of this case warrant emphasis because of 
respondent’s derision of the court’s power and authority. 

Respondent, Jack L. Thomas, on behalf of himself and 
others similarly situated, brought a lawsuit against Tru-Tech, Inc. 
and D. Grant Peacock, alleging various breaches of fiduciary 
duties. After a bench trial, the district court held that Petitioner 
Peacock, based on his status as director and officer without more, 
was not a plan fiduciary. However, the court also held that Tru- 
Tech was a plan fiduciary that had committed breaches of 
fiduciary duties. The court awarded the class of plan participants 
$187,628.93. Thomas v. Tru-Tech, Inc., No. 87-2243-3, 1988 
U.S. Dist. LEXIS 15929, at *19-22 (D.S.C. Nov. 28, 1988). 
Upon appeal, the Fourth Circuit affirmed the district court in all 
respects. Thomas v. Tru-Tech, Inc., 900 F.2d 256 (4th Cir. 
1990),

not otherwise liable. Jd. at 499. The court specifically rejected 
Petitioner’s argument that Mertens v. Hewitt Associates, 113 S. 
Ct. 2063 (1993), foreclosed this action against him. Instead, the 
Fourth Circuit held that although Mertens did not permit actions 
for monetary damages against nonfiduciaries, this case was not “an 
independent action for monetary damages against a nonfiduciary, 
but an equitable attempt to satisfy a previous judgment entered 
against a fiduciary." Thomas v. Peacock, 39 F.3d at 502. 

Amici support the Fourth Circuit’s holding that defendants 
may not take deceptive, evasive or fraudulent actions to evade a 


federal court judgment, including an ERISA judgment. Amici

to fashion equitable remedies to defeat such actions. Thus, amici 
agree with the Fourth Circuit’s conclusion that Mertens is 
inapplicable to this case. 

Because the parties and other amici have fully briefed 
other issues before the Court, this brief focuses on the narrow 
issue of the inapplicability of Mertens to this case.*

We find ourselves incredulous that Petitioner intentionally 
misconstrues both ERISA and this Court’s decisions interpreting 
that statute in an effort to condone his wrong-doing. What is at 
stake here is a third party’s ability to transfer corporate assets 
from a corporation he solely owns to himself in order to evade a 
court judgment.” The Fourth Circuit properly recognized the 
significance of this dilemma when it approved the use of corporate 
veil-piercing to find a third-party who transfers assets to himself 


to prevent satisfaction of the judgment. See Kokkonen v. Guardian 
Ins. Co. of America, supra at n. 5. v. Life 


14 
in order to evade a court judgment liable for that judgment. 
Without such recognition, parties will assail the court’s power and 
authority, and the judicial system will erode. 

Mertens does not require the federal courts to relinquish 
their power and authority in order to prevent fraudulent transfers 
of assets. Mertens focused on the meaning of ERISA § 502(a)(3) 
and its interrelationship with other sections of ERISA. 
Although the Court observed in Mertens that "ERISA is a 
comprehensive and reticulated statute,” id. at 2066, and discussed 
the "six carefully integrated civil enforcement provisions" of 
ERISA § 502(a), id. at 2067, quoting, Massachusetts Mutual Life 
Insurance Co. v. Russell, 473 U.S. 134, 146 (1985), it did not 
confront the manner in which parties may enforce adjudicated 
ERISA rights. 

Neither ERISA’s statutory language nor ERISA’s 


legislative history focuses on what happens after ERISA rights

As recognized by the Fourth Circuit, this Court’s decision 

in Mackey v. Lanier Collection Agency & Service, Inc., 486 U.S. 
825, 833 (1988), supports the federal court’s exercise of 
jurisdiction in this action. If this Court were to accept Petitioner’s 
reading of Mackey, ERISA judgments would not be collectable in 
federal court. This reading would lead to the incongruous result 
that a federal court ERISA judgment must be enforced in state 
court. 

The fact that the judgment in this case adjudicated rights 
under ERISA is immaterial to the ability of, and necessity for, the 
district court to vindicate its power and authority. Indeed, it 
makes no difference under what federal statute the judgment was 
adjudicated. Where any defendant attempts to evade a judgment, 
the Federal Rules of Civil Procedure and the judicial system have 
established equitable remedies to defeat such actions, and they 
should be used. This Court should not allow Petitioner Peacock 


16 
or any other defendant to use ERISA to shield him from his 


wrongdoing.
