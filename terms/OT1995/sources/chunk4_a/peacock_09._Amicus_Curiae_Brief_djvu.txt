impose personal liability on a nonfiduciary by piercing the

BRIEF OF THE NATIONAL ASSOCIATION OF 
REAL ESTATE INVESTMENT MANAGERS 
AS AMICUS CURIAE IN SUPPORT OF PETITIONER 


INTEREST OF THE AMICUS CURIAE 


The National Association of Real Estate Investment 
Managers (“NAREIM™”) is an association of approximately 60 
firms that manage large, equity-oriented real estate 
investments or act as investment and asset managers to 
various institutional holders of such real estate. Because 
ERISA pension plans are major investors in real estate, 


2 


NAREIM member firms often serve as investment manager 
fiduciaries of these plans. As a result, they have a strong 
interest in ensuring that any duties or liabilities imposed under 
ERISA with respect to such activities are consistent with the 
language of the statute and predictable in application. 
Specifically, shareholders (both corporate and individual) of 
real estate investment managers are ERISA fiduciaries only 
when they expressly assume that obligation or function as 
fiduciaries. Nonfiduciary shareholders thus have a substantial 
concern about being saddled with ERISA liability based on 
their corporate ownership rather than the established tests of 
fiduciary status. ' 


SUMMARY OF ARGUMENT 


Respondent’s veil-piercing action cannot proceed against 
petitioner for two independent reasons. First, this case does 
not fall within the ancillary jurisdiction of the federal courts. 
Adjudication of petitioner’s liability is not necessary to 
vindicate the federal court’s authority or to effectuate its 
decrees. There has been no allegation that anyone bound by 
the original judgment has flouted or failed to comply with that 
judgment. Rather, this “ancillary” action resulted in a 
completely different judgment against a. different party 
premised on different facts and a different legal theory. This 
Court’s controlling precedent precludes the assertion of 
ancillary jurisdiction when a judgment creditor seeks to 
require a nondiverse third party to pay the judgment. H.C. 
Cook Co. v. Beecher, 217 U.S. 497 (1910). 


The action to impose liability on petitioner also lacks 
factual interdependence with the fiduciary action that 
preceded it. Determining the issue of fiduciary liability 
required an inquiry into whether petitioner or others exercised

3 


discretionary authority and control over the management and 
administration of fund assets. By contrast, the veil-piercing 
action required an inquiry into a shareholder's dominance of 
his corporation and observation of the corporate form; indeed, 
it focused on conduct that occurred after the original 
judgment. 


Second, even if ancillary jurisdiction exists, the Fourth 
Circuit’s theory of implied liability is inconsistent with the 
explicit and careful distinctions in ERISA between the duties 
of those who are fiduciaries under the statute and those who 
are not. These distinctions have very significant practical 
implications. ERISA fiduciaries are subject to a stringent 
standard of care for their own conduct. Fiduciaries also can 
be held liable for acts and omissions of cofiduciaries as well 
as for violations of ERISA’s prophylactic and highly technical 
prohibited transaction rules. These obligations cannot be 
contracted away even if the fiduciary acts in good faith and 
in the best interest of the plan and its participants and 
beneficiaries. The Fourth Circuit’s expansion of the scope of 
such liability to the shareholders of fiduciaries undercuts 
ERISA’s fundamental distinctions between fiduciaries and 
nonfiduciaries and is likely to increase materially the cost to 
investment managers of doing business. 


In imposing ERISA liability on petitioner, a nonfiduciary, 
the Fourth Circuit did not purport to find either a specific 
cause of action or a specific source of authority of nonfidu- 
ciary duty under the statute, for there is none. It simply 
borrowed a liberal veil-piercing rule adopted in the totally 
different context of regulating the obligations of employers 
under ERISA. As this Court only recently held, however, 
there is a strong presumption against federal courts creating 
new causes of action or remedies within the ERISA statutory 
scheme. Mertens v. Hewitt Associates, 113 S. Ct. 2063, 
2067 (1993). 


The Fourth Circuit offered essentially no rationale for 
overcoming that presumption. In fact, allowing the action 


4 


created here would upset the delicate balance of competing 
interests legislated by Congress; it would render ERISA 
liabilities wholly unpredictable; and it would ignore statutory 
language showing that Congress has already regulated the 
relationship among plans, fiduciaries and their shareholders. 
Finally, there is a fundamental inconsistency between 
ERISA’s functional definition of fiduciary status and 
corporate veil-piercing when used to impose liability on a 
nonfiduciary. 


ARGUMENT 


I. ANCILLARY JURISDICTION DOES NOT 
ENCOMPASS A _ PLAINTIFF’S UNRELATED 
POST-JUDGMENT CLAIMS AGAINST THIRD 
PARTIES 


The Fourth Circuit's jurisdictional ruling vastly expands 
the scope of ancillary jurisdiction to include new and different 
claims, brought after the original action, involving parties and 
facts different from the original action. Ancillary 
jurisdiction, as it has been framed by this Court, does not 
reach so fear. 


This Court has identified two purposes served by the 
ancillary jurisdiction of the federal courts: (1) to permit 
disposition by a single court of claims that are factually 
interdependent; and (2) to enable a court to manage its 
proceedings, vindicate its authority, and effectuate its decrees. 
Kokkonen v. Guardian Life Ins. Co. of America, 114 S. Ct. 
1673, 1676 (1994). Neither circumstance exists here. 


A. The Expansion Of The Federal Court’s Limited 
Jurisdiction Permitted Below Is Unnecessary To 
Enable The Court To Vindicate Its Authority 


The Fourth Circuit did not hold that ancillary jurisdiction 
was necessary to vindicate the federal court’s authority. The 
assertion of jurisdiction was not designed to enforce the 
judgment of a federal court. Rather, it was designed to enter 


5 


a new judgment holding a third party responsible for the 
liabilities imposed by the original judgment. 


To be sure, federal courts have broad authority to enforce 
their judgments. See Riggs v. Johnson County, 73 U.S. 166, 
187 (1867); Bank of the United States v. Halstead, 23 U.S. 
(10 Wheat.) 51 (1825). For example, federal courts have 
unquestioned jurisdiction to issue orders forcing a party to 
Carry out the terms of the court’s judgment. See, e.g., Dugas 
v. American Surety Co., 300 U.S. 414, 428 (1937); Supreme 
Tribe of Ben-Hur v. Cauble, 255 U.S. 356, 367 (1921); Root 
v. Woolworth, 150 U.S. 401, 410-413 (1893). The 
enforcement proceeding enables the court to ensure that its 
rulings are given effect and that the parties bound by the 
judgment comply with it. See id. at 410-412. 


The holdings of this Court make clear, however, that 
federal jurisdiction to enforce judgments in supplemental 
proceedings is not unlimited. Kokkonen, 114 S. Ct. at 1676- 
1677. Specifically, this Court has never held that jurisdiction 
exists to allow a plaintiff in a subsequent action to bring a 
claim that requests relief of a “different kind” or that rests on 
a “different principle.” Dugas, 300 U.S. at 428; see, e.g., 
Beecher, 217 U.S. at 499; Hale v. Allinson, 188 U.S. 56, 81 
(1903). In fact, the Court has expressly rejected as “expan- 
sive” dicta the notion that a federal court has jurisdiction to 
provide precisely the kind of relief respondent seeks here. In 
Kokkonen, the Court declined to endorse a definition of 
ancillary jurisdiction as 

“[a] bill filed to continue a former litigation in the same 

court . . . to obtain and secure the fruits, benefits and 

advantages of the proceedings and judgment in a former 
suit in the same court by the same or additional parties 
. Or to obtain any equitable relief in regard to, or 
connected with, or growing out of, any judgment or 
proceeding at law rendered in the same court * * *.” 


6 


114 S. Ct. at 1676 (quoting Julian v. Central Trust Co., 193 
U.S. 93, 113-114 (1904)). 


The Fourth Circuit acknowledged that federal courts do 
not have jurisdiction over “independent” enforcement actions. 
Pet. App. 15a.’ The court of appeals concluded, however, 
that respondent’s veil-piercing action was not independent, 
because the action was a derivative claim that attempted to 
determine whether petitioner, a corporate shareholder, could 
be held liable for a pre-existing judgment entered against the 
corporation. Jd. at 13a. The court reasoned that 


[bJecause identification of those parties who are liable is 
necessarily antecedent to procedural enforcement of the 
judgment against those parties, and because Riggs 
establishes that federal courts have “ancillary” jurisdic- 
tion over enforcement of judgments, it would seem that 
the federal courts’ “ancillary” jurisdiction would extend 
to the instant attempt to pierce the corporate veil. 


Ibid. This reasoning plainly misconceives the breadth of 
ancillary jurisdiction and cannot be reconciled with this 
Court’s decision in H.C. Cook Co. v. Beecher, 217 U.S. 497 
(1910). 


Beecher involved a suit by a judgment creditor of a 
corporation found liable for patent infringement who, unable 
to collect against the corporation, sued its directors. The 
judgment creditor alleged that the directors took actions 
during the pendency of the patent infringement proceeding to 
render the corporation insolvent in the event an adverse 
judgment was entered. After emphasizing that the second 
action did not deal with the patent but only the existence of 
the judgment, the Court flatly disagreed with the contention

that the action was “ancillary” to the judgment in the former 
suit, “which, of course, it is not.” 217 U.S. at 498-499. 
The Court held that “if the directors are under obligations by 
Connecticut law to pay a judgment against their corporation, 
that is not a matter that can be litigated between citizens of 
the same state, in the [federal courts].” Jd. at 499. Indeed, 
Beecher was a more compelling case for the exercise of 
ancillary federal jurisdiction, because the plaintiff charged 
that the defendants’ activities, which occurred while the 
patent suit was being litigated, were intended to frustrate the 
effect of the federal judgment. 


This Court has never departed from or questioned the 
holding of Beecher, and there is no reason for it to do so 
now. Indeed, the Court's recent decision in Kokkonen 
demonstrates the continuing vitality of the principles 
enunciated in Beecher. See 114 S. Ct. at 1676. Simply 
stated, ancillary jurisdiction, as framed by Beecher and 
Kokkonen, does not enable a federal court to entertain every 
subsequent action by a plaintiff to identify an individual or 
entity against whom an existing judgment might be satisfied. 
Rather, a federal court lacks ancillary jurisdiction to hear a 
claim where the plaintiff seeks a different kind of substantive 
relief. Beecher involved the question — not at issue in the 
original action — whether corporate shareholders were under 
a state-law obligation to pay a patent judgment already 
obtained against their corporation. This case involves the 
question — also not at issue in the original action — whether 
a controlling shareholder has an obligation to satisfy an 
ERISA judgment already obtained against his corporation. 
Beecher clearly held that the federal courts lack jurisdiction 
to hear this kind of ancillary claim. 217 U.S. at 497. 


The Fourth Circuit sought to avoid the holding in 
Beecher in two ways, neither of which is persuasive. First, 
although the court of appeals observed that “the doctrine of 
piercing the corporate veil is not a mere procedural rule 
relating to ‘how’ a judgment is to be enforced, but is, rather, 


8 


a substantive rule of liability” (Pet. App. 12a), it ignored the 
obvious implication of this observation — that respondent was 
attempting to secure a different kind of relief. Instead, the 
court reasoned that any effort by a plaintiff to satisfy a prior 
federal judgment fell within the federal court’s jurisdiction 
because it was “necessarily antecedent” to procedural 
enforcement of the judgment. /d. at 13a. This reasoning 
ignores the fact that “[a] distinction must be drawn between 
those acts which are necessary to enforce a judgment or order 
and those which are done merely for the sake of procedural 
convenience and bear no relationship to the court’s earlier 
exercise of constitutional or statutory jurisdiction.” Eagerton 
v. Valuations, Inc., 698 F.2d 1115, 1119 (11th Cir. 1983). 
The Fourth Circuit’s rationale would allow a claimant to use 
the federal courts to pursue anyone against whom an insolvent 
fiduciary might have a claim as “necessarily antecedent” to 
enforcing the federal judgment.’ 


The wrong alleged in this case is not one that imperils 
the operation of the federal court. See Kokkonen, 114 S. Ct. 
at 1676. In Kokkonen, the Court noted that “the only order 
here was that the suit be dismissed, a disposition that is in no 
way flouted or imperiled by the alleged breach of the 
settlement agreement.” Jd. at 1677. Similarly, here the 
federal court’s order was that Tru-Tech, Inc. ("Tru-Tech") 
was liable to respondent under ERISA and that petitioner was 
not liable, an order that is neither flouted nor imperiled by 
petitioner’s unwillingness to assume the debts of Tru-Tech.

Just as the Kokkonen plaintiff asserted a purely state law 
claim, so does respondent. If, as respondent argues, 
petitioner improperly stripped Tru-Tech of its assets before 
the judgment could be satisfied, his remedy is a state court 
action to void the fraudulent transfers or pierce the corporate 
veil. Such an action would require the court to resolve new 
and different factual issues between new parties. Federal 
courts do not have the power to entertain such e/forts. As 
Beecher makes clear, a plaintiff who brings a veil-piercing 
claim against a controlling shareholder is not attempting to 
enforce the federal judgment received against the corporation; 
rather, he is using the federal judgment to create new state 
law liabilities against a stranger to the federal judgment. 


Second, the Fourth Circuit asserted that “Beecher is 
factually distinguishable from the case at bar.” Pet. App. 
15a. The court of appeals stated that “the instant case 
involves allegations that [petitioner] deliberately engaged in 
improper transfers and improperly ignored corporate 
formalities with the specific purpose of evading the already- 
extent [sic] judgment against Tru-Tech, [while] Beecher in- 
volved an allegation that corporate directors, acting within 
their legal capacity, voted to continue sales of a product 
which was the subject of a then-pending patent infringement 
suit.” Ibid. We submit that the jurisdictional power of the 
federal courts to adjudicate substantively different claims in 
enforcement actions cannot rest on so slender a factual 
distinction. As just discussed, the question instead turns on 
whether the plaintiff is merely attempting to execute the prior 
judgment, or whether the plaintiff is attempting to impose 
liability on new parties on the basis of new grounds that 
involve the litigation of new and different questions. That is 
unquestionably the case here.

B. The Veil-Piercing Action Lacks The Factual 
Interdependence With The ERISA Action To 
Support Ancillary Jurisdiction 

An exercise of ancillary jurisdiction also is inappropriate 

here because the underlying ERISA action and the subsequent 
veil-piercing action involve altogether different factual issues. 
The ERISA action involved the question whether Tru-Tech 
complied with its fiduciary duty in administering an ERISA 
plan. See Pet. App. 3a-4a. The veil-piercing action, by 
contrast, involved the question whether Tru-Tech and 
petitioner possess separate personalities.* No precedent of 
this Court justifies considering the veil-piercing action to be 
ancillary to the ERISA action.° 


A fiduciary duty determination under ERISA and a veil- 
piercing determination not only are governed by different 
legal principles but also are highly fact-intensive. In 
assessing petitioner’s fiduciary liability, the trial court looked 
at evidence of the business decisions made by Tru-Tech’s 
officers and directors in establishing and administering the 
ERISA plan. The court expressly found that “[nJeither D. 
Grant Peacock & Co. nor D. Grant Peacock exercised 
discretionary authority and control over the management and 
administration of the plan and disposition of its assets,” and 
thus were not plan fiduciaries. Thomas v. Tru-Tech, No. 87-

In the veil-piercing action, the trial court looked at 
entirely different facts: evidence of moneys owed to and by 
Tru-Tech, transfers of notes and consideration paid therefor, 
and company accounting practices for periods before and after 
the original judgment. Pet. App. 40a-42a. This inquiry had 
virtually no overlap with the fiduciary duty inquiry. The 
factual interdependence between the two actions necessary to 
support ancillary jurisdiction is clearly lacking. 


Rather than adopting an expansive view of federal 
jurisdiction to enforce judgments, this Court and the lower 
courts have generally asked whether the subsequent 
enforcement action arises from a common nucleus of 
operative facts with the underlying federal proceeding and 
therefore can be said to form part of the same constitutional 
case. See Owen Equipment & Erection Co. v. Kroger, 437 
U.S. 365, 371 (1978); United Mine Workers v. Gibbs, 383 
U.S. 715, 725 (1966). These courts have found that the 
requisite factual nexus is lacking when the plaintiff asserts 
unrelated, fact-specific, disputed claims, after the end of the 
principal litigation.°

A. ERISA Unambiguously Delineates Who Shall Bear 
The Burdens Of Fiduciary Responsibility 


Congress devoted an entire subpart of ERISA to the 
subject of “Fiduciary Responsibility,” §§ 401-414, 29 U.S.C. 
§§ 1101-1114. The core subject of this regulation, a 
“fiduciary” to a plan, must comply with detailed obligations 
set forth in the statute. The duties and responsibilities of 
fiduciaries include “the proper management, administration, 
and investment of [plan] assets, the maintenance of proper 
records, the disclosure of specified information, and the 
avoidance of conflicts of interest.” Massachusetts Mut. Life 
Ins. Co. v. Russell, 473 U.S. 134, 142-143 (1985). A 
fiduciary is “personally liable” for any breach of the 
“responsibilities, obligations, or duties imposed upon 
fiduciaries” by Title I, § 409(a), 29 U.S.C. § 1109(a). The 
fiduciary is also barred from causing the plan to engage in 
certain “prohibited transactions,” § 406(a), 29 U.S.C. 
§ 1106(a), and from engaging in other prohibited transactions 
involving self-dealing or conflict of interest. See § 406(b), 
29 U.S.C. § 1106(b). Several remedial provisions provide 
broad rights of action to both private parties and the Secretary 
of Labor against errant plan fiduciaries. See, e.g., § 502, 29 
U.S.C. §§ 1132. 


The statute succinctly provides three ways to become an 
ERISA fiduciary. First. the plan instrument must provide for 
at least one “named fiduciary.” See § 402(a), 29 U.S.C. 
§ 1102(a). Second, the plan instrument may authorize named 
fiduciaries to designate other persons to carry out certain 
fiduciary responsibilities under the plan. See § 405(c)(1)(B), 
29 U.S.C. § 1105(c)(1)(B). Persons so designated are 
deemed to be fiduciaries under ERISA. See § 3(21)(A), 29 
U.S.C. § 1002(21)(A).

definition of fiduciary status. See § 3(21)(A), 29 U.S.C. 
§ 1002(21)(A). Even if a corporation or an individual is not 
named or specifically designated as a fiduciary, that 
corporation or individual functionally will take on fiduciary

persons subject to fiduciary duties — and to damages — 
under section 409 [29 U.S.C. § 1109].” Mertens, 113 S. Ct. 
at 2071. ' 


This broad functional definition of fiduciary status cuts 
through the labels in corporate structures. For example, 
members of a board of directors of a corporation are treated 
as fiduciaries to the extent that their activities fall within 
Section 3(21). See 29 C.F.R. § 2509.75-8, D-4 (1994); 
Martin v. Harline, 15 E.B.C. (BNA) 1138 (D. Utah 1992); 
Newton v. Van Otterloo, 756 F. Supp. 1121, 1132 (N.D. Ind. 
1991). Indeed, an officer of a corporation that is a named 
fiduciary of a plan may, in acting for the corporation, also be 
regarded as a fiduciary individually. Compare Confer v. 
Custom Eng’g Co., 952 F.2d 34 (3d Cir. 1991) (corporate 
officer not a fiduciary individually), with Kayes v. Pacific 
Lumber Co., 51 F.3d 1449 (9th Cir. 1995) (corporate officer 
who acts in a fiduciary capacity may be held liable 
individually as a fiduciary).

ERISA'’s distinction between fiduciary and nonfiduciary 
status is the essential guide for parties dealing with pension 
plans, such as the members of NAREIM. For example, an 
investment firm may be appointed by a named fiduciary to 
serve as an “investment manager” of the assets of the plan. 
See § 402(c)(3), 29 U.S.C. § 1102(c)(3). An investment 
manager under ERISA is a fiduciary with power over plan 
assets, which is registered as an investment advisor under the 
Investment Advisers Act of 1940, or is a bank or insurance 
company, and which has acknowledged in writing its 
fiduciary status. See § 3(38), 29 U.S.C. § 1002(38). Due to 
the functional definition of a fiduciary under ERISA, 
however, the possible extent of fiduciary status is not limited 
to the corporate entity appointed investment manager. Thus, 
other corporate entities, individuals affiliated with the 
investment manager, and even the shareholders are charged 
under ERISA with knowing that if they act as fiduciaries, 
they must both meet the obligations of and be subject to 
liability as fiduciaries. 


The practical implications of the distinction between 
fiduciary and nonfiduciary status are substantial. For 
example, in an investment relationship that is not governed by 
ERISA, the parties may define the standard of care by 
contract and agree to exculpation (on a showing of good faith, 
or absent proof of negligence) as to the performance of the 
investment. Under ERISA, however, an investment manager 
is held to a very high statutory standard of care, § 404(a)(1), 
29 U.S.C. § 1104(a)(1), which cannot be varied by the 
parties, and exculpation of an investment manager is void as 
against public policy. § 410(a), 29 U.S.C. § 1110(a); see 
Kayes, 51 F.3d at 1460. 


Furthermore, even if the investment manager’s conduct 
satisfies ERISA’s standard of care generally, under ERISA 
the investment manager still must comply with the statute’s 
highly detailed prohibited transaction rules (§ 406(a), 29 
U.S.C. § 1106(a)), which bar fiduciaries from causing the 


16 


plan to enter into investments that directly or indirectly 
involve parties in interest.’ In addition, a fiduciary may be 
liable for breaches committed by cofiduciaries. Implementing 
procedures to comply with ERISA’s stringeat rules and 
purchasing fiduciary liability insurance to protect against 
inadvertent violations of ERISA increase the operating costs 
of investment managers.. Thus, there are very tangible 
reasons for investment managers, and the corporations and 
individuals related to them, to understand precisely when they 
are deemed to be fiduciaries subject to fiduciary liability 
under ERISA and when they are not. 


Until now, that guidance has been provided by the clear 
statutory language of ERISA and by more than 20 years of 
interpretation of that language by the Department of Labor 
and the courts. The decision below would introduce massive 
confusion by imposing liabilities on nonfiduciaries that are 
equivalent to those that fiduciaries have assumed expressly.

1. The Fourth Circuit’s Reasoning Lacks Support 
in the Statute 


The Fourth Circuit did not attempt to locate a specific 
relevant source of nonfiduciary duty in ERISA or a cause of 
action in the language of the statute. Rather, the court of 
appeals appears to have relied entirely on ERISA’s general 
purpose of protecting employee benefits as the basis for a 
new common law rule imposing liability on nonfiduciaries. 
Under ERISA, however, “vague notions of a statute’s ‘basic 
purpose’ are * * * inadequate to overcome the words of its 
text regarding the specific issue under consideration.” 
Mertens, 113 S. Ct. at 2071. 


The Fourth Circuit did not explicitly acknowledge that it 
was sanctioning the imposition of fiduciary liability on a 
nonfiduciary. As a result, the court of appeals did not 
address the open issue, post-Mertens, of the liability of a 
nonfiduciary under Section 502(a)(3) of ERISA for knowing 
participation in a fiduciary breach. Accordingly, the court 
below avoided a confrontation with the post-Mertens decisions 
in three other circuits that have decided against such knowing 
participation liability of a nonfiduciary. See Reich v. Rowe, 
20 F.3d 25 (Ist Cir. 1994) (nonfiduciary accounting service 
provider); Reich v. Continental Casualty Co., 33 F.3d 754, 
757 (7th Cir. 1994), cert. denied, 115 S. Ct. 1104 (1995)

statutory duties of care, solely because of the corporation’s 
status as a fiduciary under ERISA. Given these distinctions 
under the structure of ERISA, it was plainly inappropriate to 
follow Alman and import veil-piercing notions here.

C. Veil-Piercing To Impose Liability On A 
Nonfiduciary Is Inconsistent With ERISA’s 
Functional Definition Of A Fiduciary 


The functional definition of a fiduciary in Section 3(21) 
of ERISA is an explicit and dynamic statutory mechanism for 
attributing fiduciary responsibility to the true, behind-the- 
scenes wielders of power over a plan. It is therefore 
protective: if the “named” fiduciary really is acting as a 
fiduciary in name only, the functional definition pins fiduciary 
duties — and thus personal liability — upon additional parties. 
In this way, the functional definition functions very much like 
the common law doctrine of veil-piercing, placing substance 
over form. 


However, veil-piercing analysis differs from the 
functional ERiSA analysis in such a way that it would be 
anomalous to use veil-piercing to impose fiduciary liability on 
an individual (such as petitioner) who has already been found 
to be a nonfiduciary under ERISA. Though procedurally 
similar, the two analyses differ in substance. Veil-piercing is 
a traditional state-law method of assigning substantive liability 
against a person or entity when that person or entity exercises 
such complete control over a corporation that the corporate 
form is adjudged to be a sham. See Pet. App. lla. By 
contrast, the analysis required by ERISA looks beyon¢ issues 
of general corporate control to the particular type of control 
specified in Section 3(21) as triggering fiduciary duties. This 
factual link to plan assets and affairs is essential to describing 
an ERISA cause of action, because fiduciary duties and 
obligations make sense only in terms of their relation to a 
plan.'° In other words, it makes perfect sense to have situa-
