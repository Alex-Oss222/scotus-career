STATEMENT OF THE CASE 
A. Background 


Congress enacted the National Voter Registration Act 
of 1993 (“NVRA”) in order to establish uniform non- 
discriminatory voter registration “procedures that will 
increase the number of eligible citizens who register to 
vote in elections for Federal office.” 42 U.S.C. 
§ 1973gg(b)(1). Consistent with Congress’ objectives, the 
overwhelming majority of states have now implemented 
this legislation with resounding success. Over five mil- 
lion new voters, of diverse backgrounds and political 
affiliations, have been added to the rolls. 

Every judge from every court to consider the ques- 
tion has concluded without difficulty that the NVRA is 
constitutional. The State of California nevertheless per- 
sists in its challenge, despite over a century of Supreme 
Court precedent that squarely and consistently upholds 
Congress’ broad authority to regulate federal elections. 
Article I, Section 4 of the Constitution, while recognizing 
that the States play a role in regulating federal elections, 
explicitly empowers Congress “at any time” to “make or 
alter such Regulations” as it deems appropriate. In an 
unbroken string of decisions, dating back to Ex Parte 
Siebold, 100 U.S. (10 Otto) 371, 392 (1879), this Court has 
held that Article I, Section 4 vests in Congress “para- 
mount” authority to regulate federal elections. 

Expressly pursuant to this settled authority, Congress 
enacted the NVRA. Declaring that “the purpose of our 
election process is not to test the fortitude and determina- 
tion of the voter, but to discern the will of the majority,” 
Congress established uniform nondiscriminatory registra- 
tion standards. See S. Rep. No. 103-6, 103rd Cong., 1st 
Sess. 3 (1993). The NVRA provides that states requiring 
advance registration to vote in federal elections must 
provide for registration by (1) mail-in application, (2)

simultaneous application with driver’s license or state ID 
card applications or renewals, and (3) application at dis- 
ability and public assistance agencies. 42 U.S.C. 
§§ 1973gg-2 to 1973gg-5. The NVRA also describes pro- 
cedures and standards governing the maintenance of 
voter registration rolls. 42 U.S.C. § 1973gg-6. Covered 
states were generally required to comply with the NVRA 
by January 1, 1995. 42 U.S.C. § 1973gg note. 


Through the NVRA, Congress informs the states that 
if they choose to mandate advance registration for federal 
elections, which they are not required to do, they must 
implement specified registration procedures. The NVRA 
promotes the straightforward concept that voter registra- 
tion is simply a matter of maintaining lists. Recognizing 
that states already maintain lists at motor vehicle and 
public assistance offices, the NVRA directs that those 
agencies assist in the voter registration process. 


B. Procedural History 


Respondents agree generally with Petitioners’ state- 
ment of “The Proceedings Below” set forth at pages 7-11 
of the Petition. Respondents note that, with the exception 
of South Carolina, those few other states listed by the 
Petitioners as having challenged the constitutionality of 
the NVRA have since abandoned their challenges. 


C. Legislative History of the NVRA 


The right to vote is a fundamental right under our 
Constitution. Reynolds v. Sims, 377 U.S. 533, 555 (1964). 
Through the NVRA, Congress sought to protect this fun- 
damental right and to address the nationwide problem of 
discriminatory and unfair voter registration procedures 
for federal elections. 42 U.S.C. § 1973gg. The NVRA’s 
legislative history and text both demonstrate a purpose to

make registration more accessible. Finding that voter reg-

voter turnout and that contemporary registration rates 
are abysmally low, the Senate concluded: 


[R]egistration procedures in the United States

are employed by some states. . . . The Voting

The House echoed the Senate’s conclusion. Determin- 
ing that existing local voter registration laws were, in 
some instances, just as restrictive and discriminatory as 
the unlawful practices prohibited by the Voting Rights 
Act of 1965, the House Report stated that the NVRA was 
needed to reduce “obstacles to voting to the absolute 
minimum while maintaining the integrity of the electoral 
process.” H.R. Rep. No. 103-9, 103rd Cong., 1st Sess. 3 
(1993). 


The findings and purposes set forth in the Act also 
recognize the significant public interest in increasing 
voter participation. Noting that discriminatory and unfair 
registration laws have a “direct and damaging effect on 
voter participation” in federal elections, the NVRA states 
that its objectives are to increase the rate of voter registra- 
tion, enhance voter participation in federal elections, and 
“protect the integrity of the electoral process.” 42 U.S.C. 


§ 1973gg(a) & (b). 


D. Statement of Facts 


The overwhelming majority of states have recognized 
the laudatory purposes of the NVRA as well as Congress’ 
power to legislate in this area and, accordingly, have 
faithfully implemented this legislation. But, as the district 
court noted: “The State of California and Governor 
Wilson [took] actions . . . tantamount to failure to comply 
with the NVRA.” App. at 251.! 


Recognizing that preparation for implementing the 
NVRA would take considerable time, then-Acting Secre- 
tary of State Tony Miller sent the Governor a letter on 
January 31, 1994, warning that California was in danger 
of not meeting the January 1, 1995, deadline because it 
had yet to develop an implementation plan. See App. at 
65-66. At about the same time, the California Department 
of Motor Vehicles (“DMV”) cautioned the Governor that 
the state was running out of adequate preparation time 
for implementing the NVRA. See App. at 68-72. 


Despite these advance warnings, the State failed to 
develop a comprehensive plan for implementing the 
NVRA. Nor did it seek any judicial resolution of the law’s 
constitutionality. Instead, in August 1994, the Governor 
issued an Executive Order directing state officials not to 
comply with the NVRA except “to the extent federal 
funding is made available.”2 App. at 57-58. Meanwhile, in

supported by more than 100 years of precedent in this 
Court affirming beyond doubt Congress’ authority over 
federal elections, including the registration of voters. 
Given this authority, there is simply no reasonable basis 
for questioning the NVRA’s constitutionality. 


Furthermore, no conflict exists among the courts of 
appeals on the issue presented. Every judge from every 
court to consider the question has concluded that the 
NVRA fits comfortably within Congress’ constitutionally 
delegated powers. The Seventh and Ninth Circuits, as 
well as district courts in California, Illinois, Michigan, 
Kansas, South Carolina, Pennsylvania, and Virginia, are 
unanimous in concluding that Congress’ power to enact 
the NVRA is authorized by the plain language of Article 
I, Section 4. As Chief Judge Posner stated in Association of 
Community Organizations for Reform Now (ACORN) v. 
Edgar, 56 F.3d 791, 794 (7th Cir. 1995), “the Constitution 
itself is clear enough to enable us to resolve the case.” 


Finally, because the Constitution gives Congress “the 
whip hand” with respect to the regulation of federal 
elections, id., every court to consider the issue has easily 
dismissed any Tenth Amendment concerns. As Judge 
Sneed explained, in distinguishing the Tenth Amendment 
question raised in New York v. United States, 505 U.S. 144 
(1992): “The flaw in the State of California’s argument is 
that it ignores Article I, section 4, which, unlike the 
commerce power in Article I, section 8, empowers Con- 
gress to impose on the states precisely the burden at

3. The legislative history of Article I, Section 4 
permits regulations by both federal and state 
governments, but makes any federal regulation 
paramount 


The legislative history of Article I, Section 4 provides 
additional support for this Court’s consistent holdings 
that Congress has been constitutionally afforded the final 
say in the regulation of federal elections. In fact, Article I, 
Section 4 was the subject of intense debate at the various 


21 


state ratifying conventions of 1787-1789. At that time, 
elections within the meaning of Section 4 were “those 
wherein Senators should be chosen by legislatures and 
Representatives by voters possessing ‘the qualifications 
requisite for electors of the most numerous branch of the 
state legislature.’ ” Newberry v. United States, 256 U.S. at 
249-50 (1920) (quoting U.S. Const. Art I, §§ 2, 3). Thus, 
many states feared that granting Congress the power to 
regulate the times, places, and manner of electing Con- 
gressmen “might be extended to and embrace the regula- 
tion of the election of the members of the state 
legislatures who were to exercise the power to elect Sena- 
tors.” Id. at 268. 


On the other hand, proponents of the provision 
believed that consigning the power to regulate federal 
elections exclusively with the States would place the exis- 
tence of the federal government entirely at the mercy of 
the States. “They could at any moment annihilate it by 
neglecting to provide for the choice of persons to admin- 
ister its affairs.” The Federalist No. 59, at 363 (Alexander 
Hamilton) (Clinton Rossiter, ed. 1961); see also The Federal- 
ist No. 60, at 366 (Hamilton) (“an uncontrollable power 
over the elections to the federal government could not, 
without hazard, be committed to the State legislatures”). 


In the end, granting Congress paramount power over 
federal elections was considered indispensable, and those 
favoring congressional authority over federal elections 
prevailed. See J. Story, Commentaries on the Constitution of 
the United States 280-292 (1st ed. 1833) (cited in Or-gon v. 
Mitchell, 400 U.S. at 119 n.2). Alexander Hamilton 
explained that Section 4’s “propriety rests upon the evi- 
dence of this plain proposition, that every government 
ought to contain in itself the means of its own preserva- 
tion.” The Federalist No. 59, at 362 (emphasis in original).

B. IN VIEW OF THE UNBROKEN LINE OF CASES 
ESTABLISHING CONGRESS’ AUTHORITY OVER 
FEDERAL ELECTIONS, THIS CASE PRESENTS 
NO SUBSTANTIAL TENTH AMENDMENT QUES- 
TION 


Petitioners also seek review to assert their claim that 
the NVRA is unconstitutional under the Tenth Amend- 
ment. But given that this Court has so clearly and fre- 
quently construed Article I, Section 4 to accord Congress 
paramount authority over the regulation of federal elec- 
tions, there is nothing “reserved” to the states so as to 
raise a Tenth Amendment question. Any other result 
would constitute a radical departure from over a century 
of settled Tenth Amendment jurisprudence, both as to 
holdings and methodology of adjudication. 


Application of the Tenth Amendment is to be deter- 
mined by “inquir[ing] whether an Act of Congress is 
authorized by one of the powers delegated to Congress in 
Article I of the Constitution.” New York v. United States, 
505 U.S. 144, 155 (1992) (citing Perez v. United States, 402 
U.S. 146 (1971) and McCulloch v. Maryland, 17 U.S. (4 
Wheat.) 316 (1819)). “If a power is delegated to Congress 
in the Constitution, the Tenth Amendment expressly dis- 
claims any reservation of that power to the States... . ” 
New York v. United States, 505 U.S. at 156. 


Because the NVR was enacted pursuant to a consti- 
tutional provision that explicitly grants Congress the 


24 


power to regulate the states, it cannot offend the constitu- 
tionally-mandated division of authority between the 
states and the federal government. To assert otherwise, as 
Petitioners necessarily have been compelled to argue to 
this Court, would render Article I, Section 4 nugatory: 
Congress, in fact, would not have the power at all to 
“make or alter” the States’ regulations. Instead, State 
executives and legislatures would command ultimate 
authority to dictate how federal elections are conducted 


and basic tenets of federalism would be flipped on their 
head. 


By their very role in our democratic scheme, federal 
elections, the existence of which are dependent upon the 
Constitution, could never have been within the 
“reserved” powers of the States. The Tenth Amendment 
simply provides that “powers not delegated to the United 
States by the Constitution . . . are reserved to the States.” 
Thus, this Court in Mitchell held that Congress could not 
regulate state elections because the “Constitution 
reserves to the States the power to set voter qualifications 
in state and local elections.” Mitchell, 400 U.S. at 125. Yet, 
as this Court recently observed, “No state can say, that it 
has reserved, what it never possessed.” U.S. Term Limits, 
Inc., 115 S.Ct. at 1854 (quoting Justice Story). 


Because the ultimate authority to regulate federal 
elections was “never possessed” by the states, the Tenth 
Amendment is simply irrelevant here: 


For the election of Senators and Representatives 
in Congress is a Federal function; whatever the 
states do in the matter they do under authority 
derived from the Constitution of the United 
States. The reservation contained in the 10th 
Amendment cannot properly operate upon this 
subject in favor of the state governments; they 


25 


could not reserve power over a matter that had 
no previous existence. 


Newberry, 256 U.S. at 281 (White, J., dissenting).

Although Petitioners attempt to make much of the 
fact that the NVRA involves state officers whose tradi- 
tional responsibilities are unrelated to elections, this fact 
is of no constitutional import - particularly since the 
officers involved already perform functions comparable 
to those required by the NVRA. “In no matter should 
[courts] pay more deference to the opinion of Congress 
than in its choice of instrumentalities to perform a func- 
tion that is within its power.” National Mutual Insurance 
Co. v. Tidewater Transfer Co., 337 U.S. 582, 603 (1949) 
(opinion of Jackson, J.). As Chief Justice Marshall stated 
with regard to Congress’ power under the “necessary and 
proper” clause: “Let the end be legitimate, let it be within 
the scope of the constitution, and all means by which are 
appropriate, which are plainly adapted to that end, which 
are not prohibited, but consist with the letter and spirit of 
constitution, are constitutional.” McCulloch v. Maryland, 
17 U.S. (4 Wheat.) 316, 421 (1819). 


Nor does the NVRA represent the first time that 
Congress has imposed duties on state officers other than 
election officials. Congress previously has exercised its 
Section 4 powers in very specific terms, for example, to 
tell state legislatures “to meet in joint convention, and

meaningful practical consideration in implementation. 
But while the federal government does in fact assume-a 
portion of the costs involved in implementing the NVRA,

burdens are much exaggerated, which entities should 
bear the expenses of a program enacted pursuant to 
congressional authority is a legislative policy decision, 
not a matter of constitutional dimension for the courts.

federal funding not a Tenth Amendment issue); Hodel, 452

CONCLUSION 


Questions relating to the scope of congressional 
power often implicate novel and important constitutional 
issues that should be addressed. This case does not pre- 
sent such an issue. This case involves a clear constitu- 
tional mandate that has been consistently affirmed by this 
Court. Time and time again, when asked to consider 
Article I, Section 4, this Court has reiterated Congress’ 
“paramount” authority to regulate federal elections, a 
power that includes the registration of voters. There is no 
reason for this Court to say again what it already has said 
clearly and forcefully. Accordingly, the Court should 
deny the State’s Petition.
