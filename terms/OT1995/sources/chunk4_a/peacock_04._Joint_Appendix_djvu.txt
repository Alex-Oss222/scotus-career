ORDER 


This class action was brought under the Employee 
Retirement Income Security Act of 1974, 29 U.S.C. 
§ 1001, et seg. The plaintiff class consists of former 
salaried employees of the defendant Tru-Tech, Inc. The 
plaintiffs claim that the defendants represented to the 
class that they would be covered by a pension plan. The 
pension plan was to be effective on October 1, 1981 
according to a summary plan descripiton which was dis- 
tributed to the class in August of 1982. The majority of 
the class had been previously employed by the Draper 
Division of Rockwell International and their former serv- 
ice with Draper was to be counted toward vesting with 
Tru-Tech. 


The amended complaint alleges that each of the defend- 
ants exercised discretionary authority, control or respon- 
sibility over the administration and management of the 


4 


plan and, therefore, were fiduciaries under the plan. The 
amended complaint then alleges that they breached their 
fiduciary duties to the plaintiff class by: 


1. Failing to properly fund the plan. 
2. Failing to have the plan approved by IRS. 


3. Using the tentative disapproval by IRS as a pre- 
text to terminate the plan and refund contributions to the 
defendant. 


Two other causes of action allege breach of contract 
and civil conspiracy. The defendants filed a general 
denial. 


The primary contention of the plaintiffs is as follows: 
After initiating the plan Tru-Tech began to experience 
financial difficulties. They obtained a deferment of con- 
tributions. The IRS then notified Tru-Tech that its pro- 
nosed 10 year cliff vesting schedule was not acceptable. 
A previous resolution of the Board of Directors had au- 
thorized the executive officers of Tru-Tech to make tech- 
nical or administrative amendments to the plan. Tru- 
Tech’s President and Secretary executed an amendment 
to the plan pursuant to this resolution, changing the vest- 
ing schedule to 4-40. The plan was then given prelimi- 
nary approval by IRS. Subsequently, plaintiffs allege that 
the defendants conspired together to convince the IRS 
that this amendment was without authority. In doing so, 
they misrepresented to the IRS that the President had 
executed the resolution for his own financial benefit. They 
also failed to disclose the previous resolution of the board 
authorizing the executive officers to make technical or 
administrative amendments to the plan. Later they con- 
snired together to prevent the class from learning of these 
developments.

DISCUSSION OF THE EVIDENCE 
AND FINDINGS OF FACT 


It is clear from the evidence that Tru-Tech represented 
to its salaried employees that a pension plan had been 
enacted for their benefit. Both Thomas and Wilcock testi- 
fied that this was the case. In addition, Tru-Tech admitted 


17 


as much in their July 23, 1984 letter to the IRS stating 
that “we promised the salaried employees a pension plan 
and agreed to credit them with previous service at Rock- 
well for calculation of vesting.” [Plaintiffs’ Exhibit #17]. 
Wilcock testified that the purpose of this representation 
was to retain the key employees and that they were 
successful because no key employees were lost. 


Therefore, I find as a fact the following: 


1. Tru-Tech represented to its salaried employees that 
they would be covered by a pension plan similar to the 
plan they enjoyed with Rockwell with service at Rockwell 
credited toward vesting. The purpose of this plan was to 
induce the former Rockwell employees to remain with 
Tru-Tech and such purpose was successfully obtained. 
Tru-Tech achieved additional benefits from the existence 
of the plan through tax deductions. [See Plaintiffs’ Ex- 
hibit #26]. 


With respect to the specifics of the vesting schedule, 
there is no reference to any discussion by the Board of 
Directors of any specific vesting schedule at the meeting 
where the plan was approved. [Plaintiffs’ Exhibit #21]. 
Mr. Peacock testified that he could not recall any dis- 
cussion of a 10 year vesting schedule with the Board of 
Directors [Peacock deposition, 12 (8-10)]. Mr. Wilcock 
testified that there was no discussion of any specific vest- 
ing schedule by the Board of Directors and it appears that 
almost two-thirds of the affected employees had more than 
10 years of vested service by 1984 when Tru-Tech sought 
to scuttle the plan. [See Plaintiffs’ Exhibit #1]. There- 
fore, it is difficult to credit Tru-Tech’s contention to 
the IRS in Mr. Williams’ July 23, 1984 letter that the 4-40 
schedule would have provided at that time less “incentive 
to retain valuable employees.” By then the vast majority 
of the key salaried employees listed in Plaintiffs’ Exhibit 
#1 would have been fully vested under either schedule. 
The proof is much more convincing that the primary 
reason Tru-Tech refused to acknowledge the 4-40 amend- 
ment was to obtain a return of the pension funds. [See 
Plaintiffs’ Exhibit #23 and testimony of Robert Johnson]. 


18 


This is underscored by Mr. Williams’ letter of August 22, 
1984 in which he requests an adverse determination letter 
so that Tru-Tech “may move on to a consideration of 
alternative ways of providing retirement benefits for our 
employees.” [Plaintiffs’ Exhibit #8]. Admittedly this was 
never done. 


Consequently, I find as a fact the following: 


2. The Tru-Tech Board of Directors made no specific 
specification of a particular vesting schedule but rather 
gave a general approval of a pension plan using “proto- 
type documents developed by Connecticut General Life 
Insurance Company.” [Plaintiffs’ Exhibit #21, pages 66- 
67]. 


3. Because of the extent of previous vesting service 
earned with Rockwell, there was no significant difference 
in the incentive to retain valuable employees between the 
10 year cliff and 4-40 vesting schedules at the time Tru- 
Tech attempted to repudiate the 4-40 amendment in 
July through October, 1984. 


4. The true reason for Tru-Tech’s repudiation of the 
4-40 vesting amendment was to obtain a return of the 
pension funds previously paid into the plan and not to 
provide an incentive to retain valuable employees. 


In its July 23, 1984 letter to the IRS, Tru-Tech strongly 
implies that Mr. Wilcock as President of Tru-Tech orig- 
inated the 4-40 amendment for the purpose of furthering 
his own financial interests. To the contrary, the evidence 
clearly shows that the 4-40 amendment was initiated 
by the IRS, drafted by the Eckenrode Agency as agent for 
Tru-Tech, passed by Tru-Tech’s corporate counsel for 
approval, executed by Wilcock and Myers and then re- 
turned to IRS by Eckenrode. The Eckenrode representa- 
tive testified that either he or Wilcock referred it for 
approval to Tru-Tech’s counsel and Tru-Tech’s counsel's 


19 


own notes reflect that he was advised of the proposed 
amendment in the spring of 1983 by B.J. Myers. [Plain- 
tiffs’ Exhibit #23, 4 3]. 


Therefore, based on this evidence, I make the following 
additional finding of fact: 


5. The 4-40 vesting amendment was requested by IRS, 
prepared by Tru-Tech’s insurance agent, approved by 
Tru-Tech’s counsel, executed by Tru-Tech’s executive offi- 
cers Wilcock and Myers, and returned to IRS by Tru- 
Tech’s insurance agent. 


The next question concerns the authority of Wilcock 
and Myers to execute the amendment. As executive offi- 
cers of Tru-Tech they had previously been given authority 
by the Board of Directors to execute technical and admin- 
istrative amendments which may be requested by IRS. In 
the sense that it is used here, I interpret the term “tech- 
nical” to refer to the degree of substance or materiality 
of the amendment. While vesting provisions certainly may 
be considered to be substantive as opposed to technical 
in some situations, as where there is no previous credit 
toward vesting, there are other situations where vesting 
amendments are obviously technical. 


If all the key employees had 10 years of service and 
were therefore vested under either schedule. §: would be 
difficult to argue that the amendment would be material 
and therefore substantive as opposed to technical. Here 
it appears that at least two-thirds of the key employees 
would have been vested under either schedule, if not at 
the time of the amendment in September of 1983, cer- 
tainly by the time the amendment was repudiated by Tru- 
Tech in the period of July through October of 1984. 
Therefore, under the circumstances, I have concluded 
that the amendment from 10 year cliff to 4-40 vesting 
was more technical than substantive in light of the fact 
that the vast majority of the key employees as reflected in 


20 


Plaintiffs’ Exhibit #1 would have been vested under 
either schedule. 


This finding is consistent with Plaintiffs’ Exhibit #22 
which Tru-Tech Chairman Grant Peacock presented “sev- 
eral technical amendments” including an amendment to 
the hourly pension plan changing the “service require- 
ment” from 10 to 15 years. [See Plaintiffs’ Exhibit #22, 
page 342]. The “service requirement” is similar in sub- 
stance to the vesting schedule and, if a change in the 
“service requirement” is a “technical amendment,” it fol- 
lows that an amendment changing the vesting schedule 
may be technical as well. 


In addition, it is the general rule that a corporation may 
be bound where its officer or agent acts without, or in 
excess of, his actual authority if he acts within the scope 
of apparent authority with which his corporation has 
clothed him. See C.J.S. Corporations § 994. Here the 
defendant Grant Peacock as Chairman of the Board of 
Tru-Tech has testified that he generally relied upon Mr. 
Wilcock to administer the plan and that he never at- 
tempted to limit Wilcock’s authority to make representa- 
tions to the IRS or to the beneficiaries of the plan. [See 
testimony of Peacock, infra.]. 


Therefore, I make the following additional findings of 
fact: 


6. The September 14, 1983 amendment to the Tru- 
Tech pension plan as set forth in Plaintiffs’ Exhibit #4 
converting to a 4-40 vesting schedule was, under the cir- 
cumstances, a technical amendment within the meaning 
of the December 4, 1981 resolution of the Tru-Tech 
Board of Directors as set forth in Plaintiffs’ Exhibit #21 
at page 67. 


7. Wilcock and Myers had both actual and apparent 
authority to bind Tru-Tech in its dealings with and repre- 
sentations to IRS and the plan beneficiaries with refer- 


21 


ence to the existence of the Tru-Tech salaried pension 
plan and the validity of the 4-40 vesting amendment. 


29 U.S.C. § 1053(c) specifically provides that once a 
vesting schedule becomes effective, it cannot be changed 
to a less favorable vesting schedule. Obviously the 10 
year cliff vesting schedule is less favorable than the 
4-40. Therefore, the question arises as to whether the 
4-40 vesting schedule amendment became effective. The 
pension plan [Plaintiffs’ Exhibit #13] in paragraph 15.7 
on page 34 provides for approval by the Internal Revenue 
Service as follows: 


Notwithstanding any other provisions of this Plan, 
the Employer’s adoption of this Plan is subject to 
the condition precedent that the Employer’s Plan 
shall be approved and qualified by the Internal Rev- 
enue Service as meeting the requirements of § 401 (a) 
of the Internal Revenue Code... . 


The plaintiffs contend that the plan was “approved 
and qualified” within the meaning of this section and 
defendants contend that it was not. The Plaintiffs point 
to the language of the attachment to the initial IRS 
adverse determination letter stating that “the waiver of 
minimum funding and the amendment to the plan was 
approved on 5 30/84 as a National Office Technical Ad- 
vice Memorandum” [Plaintiffs’ Exhibit #11, page 38]. 
They also point to the language of Tru-Tech’s July 23, 
1984 letter which specifically states that following the 
4-40 amendment “the IRS subsequently approved the 
plan. [Plaintiffs’ Exhibit #6]. 


Paragraph 15.7 of the plan makes no reference to a 
“final approval” by IRS but only to an approval and 
qualification by IRS that the plan is “meeting the re- 
quirements of § 401(a) of the Internal Revenue Code.” 
It is obvious from the language of Plaintiffs’ Exhibit #11 
that following the 4-40 amendment the plan received at 
least preliminary approval by the IRS and it is further 


22 


obvious that after the 4-40 amendment the plan did meet 
the requirements of § 401(a) of the Internal Revenue 
Code. For this reason, I have concluded that the plan 
did, in fact, receive the “approval by the Internal Rev- 
enue Service” specified in paragraph 15.7 and further that 
after such approval the attempted reversion to the 10 
year cliff vesting schedule [See Plaintiffs’ Exhibit #10] 
was invalid under paragraph 15.3 of the plan which pro- 
vides as follows: 


CONDITIONS OF AMENDMENT. Neither the 
Sponsor nor Employer shall make any amendment 
which would cause the Plan to lose its status as a 
qualified plan within the meaning of § 401(a) of 
the Internal Revenue Code. [Plaintiffs’ Exhibit #13, 
page 33]. 


Based on the above, I make the following additional 
findings of fact: 


8. Following the adoption of the 4-40 vesting schedule 
the plan did, in fact, meet the requirements of § 401(a) 
of the Internal Revenue Code and was, in fact, approved 
and qualified by the Internal Revenue Service within 
the meaning of paragraph 15.7 of the plan. 


9. The subsequent attempted amendment by the Tru- 
Tech Board of Directors back to the 10 year cliff vesting 
schedule was invalid under the provisions of paragraph 
15.3 of the plan. 


With respect to the question of who are plan fiduciaries, 
section 1.26 of the plan states that the term fiduciary 
includes any fiduciary “designated by the employer in 
its adoption agreement.” [Plaintiffs’ Exhibit #13, page 
5]. The adoption agreement designates Tru-Tech as the 
named fiduciary [Plaintiffs’ Exhibit #12, page 4, 4 VIII}. 
The question remains as to whether the remaining de- 
fendants exercised discretionary authority or control over 
the management or administration of the plan or the 
disposition of its assets. 


23 


While Tru-Tech shared offices in Pittsburgh with Grant 
Peacock & Co., Inc. and certain individuals were officers 
of both companies, there is no suggestion that Grant 
Peacock & Co., Inc. exercised any authority or control 
in connection with the Tru-Tech salaried pension plan or 
for that matter, any of the affairs of Tru-Tech. 


Grant Peacock’s position as a director and officer of 
Tru-Tech would not alone be sufficient to make him a 
fiduciary. The Labor Department in an interpretative 
bulletin addressing this issue states in part as follows: 


D-4 Q: In the case of a plan established and main- 
tained by an employer, are members of the board of 
directors of the employer fiduciaries with respect to 
the plan? 


A. Members of the board of directors of an em- 
ployer which maintains an employee benefit plan will 
be fiduciaries only to the extent that they have re- 
sponsibility for the functions described in Section 
3(21)(A) of the Act. 


x of * 


D-5 Q: Is an officer or employee of an employer 
or employee organization which sponsors an em- 
ployee benefit plan a fiduciary with respect to the 
plan solely by reason of holding such office or em- 
ployment if he or she performs none of the functions 
described in Section 3(21)(A) of the Act? 


| a 


ERISA Interpretative Bulletin 75-8, 29 C.F.R. § 2509. 
75-8 (1983) (emphasis added). A number of circuit 
courts have adopted the same or a similar approach. See, 
e.g., Sommers Drug Stores Co. Employee Profit Sharing 
Trust v. Corrigan Enterprises, Inc., 793 F.2d 1456 (Sth 
Cir. 1986), cert. denied, 107 S.Ct. 884 (1987), 107 
S.Ct. 1298 (1987); Trenton v. Scott Paper Co., 832 
F.2d 806 (3rd Cir. 1987). 


24 


Even where an individual or corporation is a plan 
fiduciary, not all actions are fiduciary in nature: 


When an employer acts as a plan fiduciary, it wears 
“two hats,” and assumes fiduciary status only when 
it functions as a fiduciary and not when it is con- 
ducting business not regulated by ERISA. The De- 
partment of Labor has taken the position that “settlor 
functions” relating to the formation, design and ter- 
mination of plans are not fiduciary activities. 


Ogden v. Michigan Bell Telephone Co., 657 F.Supp. 328, 
333 (E.D. Mich. 1987) (citations omitted). 


At most, Grant Peacock, as chairman of the board of 
Tru-Tech, was involved along with the other members 
of the board of directors in the business decisions relating 
to the formation and design of the salaried pension plan. 
These were not fiduciary decisions, but were business deci- 
sions relating to the design features of the pension plan. 
Therefore, I make the following finding of fact: 


10. Neither D. Grant Peacock & Co. nor D. Grant 
Peacock exercised discretionary authority and control over 
the management and administration of the plan and the 
disposition of its assets. 


Based cn the above findings of fact, I make the follow- 
ing conclusions of law on the issue of liability: 


CONCLUSIONS OF LAW 


1. The purpose of ERISA is to protect the interest of 
the participants in the pension plan. See 29 U.S.C. 
1001(b). Section 1104(a) provides that a fiduciary 
should discharge his duties with respect to the plan solely 
for the interest of the participants and beneficiaries and 
for the exclusive purpose of providing benefits. In addi- 
tion, Article XI, § 11.1 of the plan provides as follows: 


GENERAL FIDUCIARY STANDARD OF CON- 
DUCT. Each Fiduciary of the Plan shall discharge 


25 


his duties hereunder solely in the interest of the 
Participants and their Beneficiaries and for the ex- 
clusive purpose of providing benefits to Participants 
and their Beneficiaries and defraying reasonable ex- 
penses of administering the Plan... . [Plaintiffs’ 
Exhibit #13, page 27]. 


2. The defendant Tru-Tech constituted a fiduciary of 
the plan within the meaning of 29 U.S.C. § 1002(21) 
(A). Neither D. Grant Peacock & Co. nor D. Grant 
Peacock were fiduciaries under that section. 


3. As a fiduciary, Tru-Tech may be liable under 29 
U.S.C. § 1109(a) which provides as follows: 


Any person who is a fiduciary with respect to a plan 
who breaches any of the responsibilities, obligations, 
or duties imposed upon fiduciaries by this [Act] shall 
be personally liable to make good to such plan any 
losses to the plan resulting from each such breach 


4. Tru-Tech represented to the plan participants that 
the plan was in effect and they relied upon such repre- 
sentation. 


5. The 4-40 vesting amendment executed on Septem- 
ber 14, 1983 [Plaintiffs’ Exhibit #4] was valid and re- 
sulted in an approval by the Internal Revenue Service 
within the meaning of paragraph 15.7 of the plan. 


6. The amendment by which Tru-Tech attempted to 
revert to the 10 year cliff vesting schedule violated the 


provisions of paragraph 15.3 of the plan and of 29 U.S.C. 
§ 1053(c) and was therefore invalid. 


7. The resulting return of the plan proceeds to Tru- 
Tech and Tru-Tech’s failure to fund the plan as pro- 
vided in the plan documents were the result of a breach 
of fiduciary duty by Tru-Tech. which proximately resulted 
in damages to the plan and its beneficiaries. 


26 


8. The common law claims of fraud and civil con- 
spiracy (Counts III and IV) are pre-empted by ERISA. 
Pilot Life Ins. Co. v. Dedeaux, 107 S.Ct. 1549, 1553 
(1987); Salomon v. Transamerica Occidental Life Ins. 
Co., 801 F.2d 659, 660-61 (4th Cir. 1986). 


9. The class is therefore entitled to damages, attorneys 
fees and costs under 29 U.S.C. § 1132(g) as to Counts I 
and II (ERISA). 


FINDINGS OF FACT AND CONCLUSIONS 
OF LAW AS TO DAMAGES 


The plaintiffs’ expert on damages was Ralph Sayre, an 
actuary who specializes in administration and valuation 
of pension plans. Mr. Sayre testified that he had been 
furnished appropriate payroll records of Tru-Tech to- 
gether with the interest rates the pension funds would 
have earned had they been properly invested with Con- 
necticut General. According to Mr. Sayre’s report [Plain- 
tiffs’ Exhibit #25] the class members would be entitled 
to damages in the amount of $205,407.20 as of Decem- 
ber 31, 1987. A copy of Mr. Sayre’s schedule of dam- 
ages is attached as Exhibit A. Mr. Sayre’s report reflects 
that all participants in the pension plan were terminated 
after Tru-Tech went out of business during the 1984 
plan year. He testified that due to the large number of 
terminated employees, he treated the plan as having a 
“partial termination” which results in all employees being 
100% vested. This treatment is consistent wtih § 15.5 
of the plan [Plaintiffs’ Exhibit #13] which provides that 
upon a “partial termination” of the plan all participants 
shall be 100% vested and entitled to a distribution of 
their account. 


Based on the above, I make the following findings of 
fact regarding damages: 


1. All class participants as reflected on Exhibit A are 
100% vested in their account balances. 


27 


2. The schedule of class participants account balances 
as reflected on Exhibit A in the amount of $205,407.20 
is the amount of damages the class is entitled to as of 
December 31, 1987 and it also correctly reflects the 
damages recoverable by each member of the class. 


3. The defendants are also liable for pre-judgment in- 
terest from December 31, 1987. The 52 Week T-Bill 
Rate Changes, January 1, 1975 to Date, issued by the 
Administrative Office of the United States Courts reflect 
that the rate on January 14, 1988 was 7.14%. Applying 
this rate to the damages of $205,407.20 from January 1 
through October 30, 1988, yields $12,221.73 of appli- 
cable pre-judgment interest, thereby entitling the class 
participants to a total sum of $217,628.93. Prior to trial, 
the plaintiffs settled with the defendant Connecticut Gen- 
eral for the sum of $30,000.00, which has previously 
been approved by the court. Accordingly, Tru-Tech is 
liable for the sum of $187,628.93. 


4. 29 U.S.C. Section 1132(g) provides for reasonable 
attorneys fees and costs. I find that plaintiffs are entitled 
to reasonable attorneys fees and costs. The attorneys 
for the plaintiff class shall submit their petition for attor- 


neys fees and costs within 45 days of the date of this 
order. 


Based on the above, the Clerk of Court shall enter 
judgment against the defendant Tru-Tech, Inc. in the 
amount of $187,628.93. The plaintiff shall have no judg- 
ment against the defendant D. Grant Peacock and D 
Grant Peacock & Co. 


IT IS SO ORDERED.

CHAPMAN, Circuit Judge: 


This appeal arises out of a class action brought by 
Jack L. Thomas (“Thomas”) against Tru-Tech, Inc. 
(“Tru-Tech”), D. Grant Peacock (“Peacock”), Grant 
Peacock and Co. Inc. (“GP&C”) and Connecticut Gen- 
eral Life Insurance Company (“CGLIC”) alleging, inter 
alia, breach of fiduciary duty under the Employment [sic] 
Retirement Income Security Act of 1974, 29 U.S.C. 
$$ 1001 et seq. (1989) (“ERISA”). The district court, 
following a trial to the bench, held Tru-Tech liable for 
breach of fiduciary duty, but found that Peacock and 
GP&C were not fiduciaries under the Plan and thus could 
not be liable for breach of fiduciary duty. Thomas appeals, 
arguing that the district court erred in finding that Peacock 
and GP&C were not fiduciaries. Tru-Tech appeals, argu- 
ing that the district court erred in finding that the amend- 


Scere 


34 


ment to the Plan was authorized by Tru-Tech’s Board of 
Directors and that Tru-Tech’s Plan was approved by the 
IRS. We conclude that the district court did not clearly 
err as to its findings of fact and made no errors of law. 


I 


Located in Spartanburg, South Carolina, Tru-Tech was 
formed in 1981 to take over a textile machinery business 
previously operated by Rockwell International Corpora- 
tion. Tru-Tech’s Chairman of the Board was Peacock, 
who also owned and operated GP&C, a Pittsburgh com- 
pany that was initially a business brokerage business and 
later a company managing other business interests of 
Peacock and his business associates. Tru-Tech shared 
Offices in Pittsburgh with GP&C. 


In a resolution of the Board of Directors dated Decem- 
ber 4, 1981, Tru-Tech adopted a pension plan for its 
salaried workers (“Plan”). This Plan provided for ten- 
year “cliff” vesting under which an employee would be- 
come fully vested following ten years of service. The 
Board did not discuss or specify the “cliff” vesting sched- 
ule, but gave its general approval of the Plan. Although 
it stated an effective date of October 1, 1981, the adop- 
tion of the Plan was “subject to the condition precedent

Thomas argues that the district court clearly erred in 
finding that neither Peacock nor GP&C were liable as 
fiduciaries under ERISA. The Act allows suits for breach 
of fiduciary duty only against persons who are fiduciaries. 
29 U.S.C. § 1109(a) (1989); Gelardi v. Pertec Com- 
puter Corp., 761 F.2d 1323, 1324-25 (9th Cir. 1985). 
To identify a fiduciary, one looks first to the terms of 
the plan, which must “provide for one or more named 
fiduciaries who jointly or severally shall have authority 
to control and manage the operation and administration 
of the plan.” 29 U.S.C. § 1102(a)(1). See also ERISA 
Interpretive Bulletin 75-8, 29 C.F.R. § 2509.75-8 (1983) 
(“Each plan must, of course, have at 'cast one named 
fiduciary ....”). If a plan designates a person or en- 
tity a “named fiduciary,” then such person or entity is 
presumptively a fiduciary and subject to liability under 
ERISA. See Yeseta v. Baima, 837 F.2d 380, 384 (9th 
Cir. 1988). In this case, Paragraph 1.26 of the Plan 
states that the term “named fiduciary” means “the Ad- 
ministrator and any other Fiduciary designated by the 
employer in its adoption agreement and any successor 
thereto.” The Adoption Agreement designated Tru-Tech 
as the administrator and named fiduciary, and Tru-Tech 
subsequently appointed W. A. Williams as Plan Admin- 
istrator at its December 4, 1981, board meeting. Thus, 
neither Peacock nor GP&C are fiduciaries within the ex- 
press terms of the Pian.

However, analysis does not stop here. The Act ex- 
tends the scope of the term “fiduciary” to those who 
actually exercise discretionary authority, control or re- 
sponsibility over a plan. Section 1002(21)(A) defines a 
fiduciary as follows: 


[A] person is a fiduciary with respect to a plan to 
the extent (i) he exercises any discretionary author- 
ity or discretionary control respecting management 
of such plan or exercises any authority cr control 
respecting management or disposition of its assets, 

. or (iti) he has any discretionary authority or 


discretionary responsibility in the administration of 
such plan. 


29 U.S.C. § 1002(21)(A). The phrase “to the extent” 
indicates that “in defining ‘fiduciary’ Congress was pecu- 
liarly concerned with realities, as distinguished from mere 
formalities.” Fulk v. Bagley, 88 F.R.D. 153, 162 
((M.D.N.C. 1980). We must focus “on the function 
performed, rather than on the title held.” Blatt v. Mar- 
shall and Lassman, 812 F.2d 810, 812 (2d Cir. 1987). 
See also H.R. Rep. No. 1280, 93d Cong., 2d Sess., 
reprinted in 1974 U.S. Code Cong. & Ad. News 4639, 
5038, 5103 (“[T]he definition includes persons who have 
authority and responsibility with respect to the matter in 
question, regardless of their formal title.”). 


As a result of this pragmatic inquiry, a person not 
designated as a fiduciary by a plan may yet be a fiduci- 
ary under ERISA if that person exercises powers listed 
in Section 1002(21)(A). However, an officer or direc- 
tor of a corporation is not a fiduciary merely because 
of his or her position in the corporation, as an interpre- 
tive bulletin issued by the Labor Department explains. 
This bulletin reads in pertinent part:

respect to the plan?

[1002](21)(A) of the Act.

described in Section [1002](21)(A) of the Act? 

A: No.
