STATEMENT 


A. Overview. 


Respondent Jack Thomas sued Petitioner Grant ‘Pea- 
cock for breach of fiduciary duty under ERISA. Petitioner 
was exonerated, but a judgment was entered against his 
co-defendant, Tru-Tech, Inc. (“Tru-Tech”). Unable to 
collect the judgment from Tru-Tech, respondent then filed 
this second, independent lawsuit against petitioner to 


3 


“Pierc[e] the Corporate Veil Under ERISA and Applica- 
ble Federal Law.” J.A. 49 (Amended Complaint). In 
this second suit, respondent sought to impose personal 
liability on petitioner for the judgment rendered solely 
against Tru-Tech in the earlier lawsuit, on the basis of 
acts that took place after entry of that judgment. 


At issue in this case are (a) the existence of the federal 
common law veil-piercing remedy said by the courts 
below to arise out of ERISA; and (b) the power of a 
federal court to entertain a separate lawsuit such as this, 
where there is no independent basis of federal jurisdiction. 


This action was begun with a new complaint, a new civil 
action number, and new service of process; it involved 
new parties, new factual allegations and new theories of 
recovery. Its sole link to the prior case and to the federal 
courts is that it seeks to identify strangers to the prior 
judgment who may have a responsibility to satisfy that 


judgment on some legal theory independent of the terms 
of the judgment itself. 


Petitioner argued below that ERISA does not provide 
for a cause of action to “pierce the corporate veil” in 
order to collect a judgment entered in a prior lawsuit 
brought pursuant to ERISA: Respondent’s claim did not 
refer in any way to an ERISA plan or invoke any of 
ERISA’s remedial provisions; its sole connection to ERISA 
was that it sought to collect a judgment obtained in a 
lawsuit that invoked ERISA. The district court disagreed, 
holding that respondent had stated a viable ERISA claim 
that permitted recovery against petitioner. 


The Fourth Circuit affirmed, but with a twist to the 
analysis. The court of appeals did not simply hold, as did 
the district court, that respondent had stated a claim under 
ERISA that would confer federal question jurisdiction. 
The court instead based federal court jurisdiction here 
solely on the existence of federal jurisdiction in the prior 


4 


action. Despite the absence of any common nucleus of 
operative fact between the two lawsuits, that court con- 
cluded that a federal court has “ancillary jurisdiction” 
over a separate lawsuit brought for the purpose of impos- 
ing a prior federal court judgment on a person other than 
the judgment debtor from the prior suit. 


Notwithstanding its unwillingness to conclude, as re- 
spondent urged, that the claim here stated an ERISA 
cause of action, the Fourth Circuit went on to hold, with- 
out reference to any statutory source, that ERISA pro- 
vides the rule of decision where an attempt is made to 
pierce the corporate veil to impose an ERISA judgment 
on a shareholder of a judgment debtor: “we believe that 
ERISA preempts any state law of veil-piercing.” Pet. 
App. 20a. The court concluded that ERISA does not 
attach great weight to corporate form, and that a “liberal 
veil-piercing standard” is appropriate in ERISA actions. 
Id. at 23a.

2. On Appeal. 


The Fourth Circuit approached the case on a funda- 
mentally different tack from the district court, separating 
the question of federal subject matter jurisdiction from the 
existence of a federal common law remedy under ERISA. 
Accordingly, the court of appeals held that federal subject 
matter jurisdiction exists even without the existence of a 
cause of action arising out of ERISA. 


The court first observed that there was no basis for 
federal court jurisdiction “other than the fact that the 
suit seeks to enforce a judgment obtained pursuant to 
ERISA.” Pet. App. lla n.6. The court then began its 
analysis of subject matter jurisdiction, first characterizing 
the nature of an action to pierce the corporate veil: It “Is 
not simply an attempt to collect a preexisting liability 
against an entity already found liable under the relevant 
substantive law; it is, rather, an attempt to impose such 
a preexisting liability upon an entity not otherwise liable. 
Pet. App. 12a. Thus, the doctrine of piercing the cor- 
porate veil is “a substantive rule of liability.” Id. It 
“seeks to identify those parties against whom the judgment 
can be satisfied.” Jd. at 13a. Finally, because “identifi- 
cation of those parties who are liable is necessarily ante- 
cedent to procedural enforcement of the judgment against 
those parties,” the court of appeals concluded that it had 
“ancillary” jurisdiction over “the instant attempt to pierce 
the corporate veil.” /d.

SUMMARY OF ARGUMENT 


I. By federalizing the law of veil piercing in order to 
enhance ERISA plaintiffs’ ability to find a solvent party 
to pay an ERISA judgment, the Fourth Circuit has done 
what the express teachings of this Court and the clear 
intent of Congress forbid. Given ERISA’s comprehensive 
nature, courts should not, as the court of appeals did 
here, “infer causes of action in the ERISA context.” 
Mertens v. Hewitt Associates, 113 §. Ct. 2063, 2071 
(1993). 


Nor is there any need for a federal common law of 
ERISA veil piercing. ERISA itself contains a mechanism 
for reaching beyond the corporate veil. Its definition of 
“fiduciary” imposes liability without regard to the corpo- 
rate form. Petitioner was here expressly adjudged not to be 
a fiduciary; if respondent failed in his attempt to reach 
beyond the corporate veil to petitioner under the express 
terms of ERISA, he should not be allowed to do so under 
a judicial expansion of the statute. 


Particularly pertinent here is Mackey v. Lanier Collec- 
tion Agency & Service, 486 U.S. 825, 833 (1988), in 
which the Court explained that “ERISA does not provide 
an enforcement mechanism for collecting judgments won” 
in ERISA cases. If ERISA does not provide for the sim- 
ple enforcement of ERISA judgments, it surely cannot 
be held to contain implicitly an even more complex 
mechanism for the imposition of those judgments on a 
third party. 


The conclusion from Mackey that state law provides 
the forum and the remedy for disputes such as this one 
is reinforced by this Court’s recent pronouncements on 
the scope of ERISA’s preemption provision. Contrary to 
the Fourth Circuit’s holding, ERISA does not preempt 
the state law of veil piercing. The claim here—that peti- 
tioner improperly transferred assets from a corporation 
previously held liable under ERISA—has no “connection 
with or reference to” an ERISA plan. Metropolitan Life 


13 


Ins. Co. v. Massachusetts, 471 U.S. 724, 739 (1985). 
Nor would application of state law rules to such a dispute 
interfere with the goal of a “nationally uniform adminis- 
tration of employee benefit plans.” New York Siate Conf. 
of Blue Cross & Blue Shield Plans v. Travelers Ins. Co., 
115 S. Ct. 1671, 1678 (1995). 


State courts are more than competent to adjudicate this 
quintessentially state-law matter of whether a corporate 
veil should be disregarded. While it may be that the 
appropriate state law remedy would be the avoidance of 
preferential transfers, rather than vcil piercing, since peti- 
tioner was not Tru-Tech’s alter ego at the time of the 
ERISA wrong, the fact remains that respondent would 
have had available state law remedies. There is no need 
to federalize this realm of the law. 


Accordingly, it was error to look to ERISA for the 
substantive rule of decision, and ERISA cannot supply 
subject matter jurisdiction in this case. 


II. These deficiencies are not remedied by the invo- 
cation of the federal courts’ “ancillary jurisdiction.” The 
action here is “more than just a continuation or renewal 
of the [prior] suit, and hence requires its own basis for 
jurisdiction.” Kokkonen v. Guardian Life Ins. Co., 114 
S. Ct. 1673, 1676 (1994). 


Indeed, this Court has previously held that “an at- 
tempt to make [new] defendants answerable for the judg- 
ment already obtained” is not “ancillary to the judgment 
in the for.wer suit.” H.C. Cook Co. v. Beecher, 217 U.S. 
at 498-99. Beecher controls here and requires reversal. 


Beecher's rule is plainly correct. Because the only link 
between this case and Tru-Tech is that respondent's pur- 
pose is to obtain payment on the Tru-Tech judgment 
from someone, and because this effort is based on post- 
judgment activities, there is no “common nucleus of oper- 
ative fact” that would make the two actions part of the 
same constitutional case. Owen Equip. & Erec. Co. v. 
Kroger, 437 U.S. 365, 371 (1978). 


14 


Nor can an effort to find additional parties to pay a 
judgment that the judgment debtor cannot satisfy be 
considered necessary “to enable a court to function suc- 
cessfully.” Kokkonen, 114 S. Ct. at 1676. A search for 
deeper pockets may be desirable for the plaintiff who 
holds the judgment, but it cannot be called essential to 
the functioning of the court. Again, the state courts are 
perfectly suited to this effort. Whatever the extent to 
which necessity can expand a federal court’s Article Il 
jurisdiction, it will not go this far. 


The Fourth Circuit’s use of ancillary jurisdiction in- 
vokes a federal court’s inherent power to turn into a fed- 
eral question a matter Congress chose not to include 
within ERISA. This circumvents congressional will, and 
both Article III and prudential limits on jurisdiction. The 
decision would open the gates to the full panoply of suits 
over the responsibility of third parties—under a variety of 
theories—for previously rendered federal court judg- 
ments. Neither necessity nor convenience counsels in favor 
of such a rule; Article III forbids it.

B. This Case Does Not Fall Within the Established 
Categories of Ancillary Jurisdiction Recognized by 
This Court. 


In Kokkonen, this Court provided a taxonomy of an- 
cillary jurisdiction: the doctrine has been used (i) to 
allow unified disposition of claims that are factually in- 
terdependent, and (ii) to provide necessary protection of 
a court’s ability to function successfully. 114 S. Ct. at 
1676. Each of these species of ancillary jurisdiction has a 
constitutional as well as a prudential dimension. 


The federal courts are courts of limited jurisdiction, 
empowered to entertain cases only where there is both 
constitutional and congressional authorization. Kokkonen, 
114 S. Ct. at 1675; Owen, 437 U.S. at 371-73. Article 
III, Section 2 of the Constitution defines the limit of 
federal judicial power. It extends that power to, among 
other controversies, “all Cases . . . arising under . . . the 
Laws of the United States.” Until the proponent of ju- 
risdiction establishes a basis for jurisdiction, “[iJ]t is to 
be presumed that a cause lies outside this limited jurisdic- 
tion.” Kokkonen, 114 S. Ct. at 1675. 


The Fourth Circuit reversed this presumption, conclud- 
ing that there was “simply no practical reason to treat 
this litigation as separate from the initial litigation.” Pet. 
App. 17a-18a. The court reached this conclusion without 
expressly considering whether this case and Tru-Tech were 
a part of the same constitutional case or controversy.” 
After recognizing that the only basis for federal jurisdic- 
tion was “the fact that the suit seeks to enforce a judg- 
ment obtained pursuant to ERISA,” Pet. App. lla n.6, 
the court inquired into whether this case and Tru-Tech

The first type of ancillary jurisdiction identified by

primary test enunciated by this Court for determining

is no factual overlap between the two cases, there is no 
efficiency in bringing the second matter into federal court. 
As was the case here, the federal court will do exactly 
what a state court faced with the same case would do. 
It will conduct a full trial. The putatively ancillary case 


cannot be decided on the basis of the record in the prior 
action.

C. Congress Did Not Provide in ERISA for Federal 
Jurisdiction Over a Claim Such as This One. It 
Follows That Ancillary Jurisdiction Over This 
Claim Is Not “Necessary” to a Court’s Functioning 
in the Context of ERISA. 


ERISA is a comprehensive statute containing its own 
grant of federal jurisdiction. We have shown that ERISA 
makes no provision for maintenance of an action like this 
one, and indeed that its preemption provision leaves intact 
whatever state law claims respondent might have for the 
acts of which he complains. Congress did not deem it 
necessary for purposes of ERISA that an action such as 
this one be tried in federal court.™

D. Prudential Limits Counsel Against the Exercise of 
Ancillary Jurisdiction Here. 


Assuming arguendo that the Article III judicial power 
could in theory be stretched to reach a proceeding of this 
kind, that would not be the end of the matter. As this 
Court has recognized in such areas as standing and ripe- 
ness, it has discretion to set prudential limits on the exer- 
cise of jurisdiction. See, e.g., Reno v. Catholic Soc. Servs., 
Inc., 113 S. Ct. 2485, 2495 n.18 (1993) (ripeness); 
Nordlinger v. Hahn, 505 U.S. 1, 17 (1992) (stand- 
ing); Wyoming v. Oklahoma, 502 U.S 437, 450 (1992) 
(“we have imposed prudential and equitable limitations 
upon the exercise of our original jurisdiction”). Such 


43 


prudential limits are particularly appropriate to judicially 
created doctrines such as necessary ancillary jurisdiction. 


Prudential considerations strongly counsel reversal here. 
The fundamental rationale of the Fourth Circuit was that 
a federal judgment confers federal jurisdiction over addi- 
tional lawsuits that seek to identify any potentially re- 
sponsible parties, and not just those already expressly 
adjudged liable. One can envision a wide variety of cir- 
cumstances in which a judgment creditor from a federal 
question lawsuit (from antitrust to RICO to securities 
law) might seek to expand the pool of potentially re- 
sponsible parties. Under the Fourth Circuit’s rule, any such 
suit, proceeding on any legal theory of vicarious liability 
or asset tracing, would be ancillary to the prior federal 
suit because the second suit would seek to determine who 
is responsible for the judgment. This rule would open the 
federal courts to all manner of collection actions, which 
would have to proceed under state-law theories ™ or (as 
happened here) judge-made versions of the same theories 
that would have to be invented for each state-law theory. 
Moreover, depending upon the perceived purpose of the 
federal statute giving rise to the original judgment, the 
federal common law as to a given collection theory might 
well vary from one statute to the next. None of this is 
necessary to the functioning of the federal courts, nor is it 
consistent with the limits of Article III or sound principles 
of judicial administration. Ancillary jurisdiction does not 


give a successful plaintiff a permanent ticket of entry to 
the federal courts.
