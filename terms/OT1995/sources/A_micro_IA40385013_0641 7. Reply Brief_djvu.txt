ARGUMENT AND AUTHORITIES 


I. THE 120 DAY SERVICE REQUIREMENT OF RULE 
4 SUPPLANTS THE FORTHWITH SERVICE RE- 
QUIREMENT IN THE SAA. 


A. Rule 4(j) Conflicts With The “Forthwith” Service 
Requirement In The SAA. 


The United States argues that there is in fact no conflict 
between the SAA and Rule 4(j) and that effect can be 
given to both statutes. The United States contends that 
Rule 4(j) and Section 742 of the SAA are not in conflict 
because Rule 4(j) does not necessarily confer a right to 
an absolute 120 days in which to serve the complaint. 
(Br. for the United States at 18). The United States 
also argues that although the rules may produce differing 
results, this is not enough to establish that the statutes 


2 


are in conflict. See Radzanower v. Touche, Ross & Com- 
pany, 426 U.S. 148, 155 (1976); United States v. Batch- 
elder, 442 U.S. 114, 121 (1979). (Br. for the United 
States at 19-20). 


Although Rule 4 allows 120 days for service, under 
the SAA, any time period greater than approximately 58 
days is not “forthwith.” (Br. for Petitioner at 34). Be- 
cause courts have interpreted the term “forthwith” to al- 
low only approximately half the time for service as 
allowed under Rule 4, the rules are inconsistent. The 
United States’ argument also ignores the fact that Rule 4 
allows an extension of time for service upon a showing 
of “good cause,” while the “forthwith” requirement does 
not. In other words, under Rule 4, a court has the dis- 
cretion to extend the time limit for service, while under 
the SAA, the court must dismiss the suit upon a finding 
that service was not “forthwith.” 


The United States also relies upon Supreme Court au- 
thority indicating that as long as the statutes are not 
repugnant, the Court must give effect to both. See Ruck- 
elshaus v. Monsanto Company, 467 U.S. 986, 1018 
(1984); Connecticut National Bank v. Germain, 503 U.S. 
249, 253 (1992); Morton v. Mancari, 417 U.S. 535, 
550-551 (1974).’ (Br. for the United States at 17). 


1 None of the cases relied upon by Petitioner involves a conflict 
between a federal rule of procedure and a federal statute. These 
cases involve repeals by implication, in direct contrast to the pres- 
ent case which involves a federal rule promulgated pursuant to 
the Rules Enabling Act which expressly repealed al! conflicting 
laws. See, e.g., Penfield Co. v. Securities and Exchange Commis- 
sion, 330 U.S. 585, 589 n.5 (1947) (“where a Rule of Civil Pro- 
cedure conflicts with a prior statute, the rule prevails”); Griffith 
v. N.L.R.B., 645 F.2d 1194, 1197 n4 (9th Cir. 1976) (“federal 
statutes are superseded by conflicting federal rules.”); American 
Federation of Musicians v. Stein, 213 F.2d 679, 685- 686 (6th Cir. 
1954) (“the Federal Rules of Civil Procedure have the force and 
effect of statutes, and if there is a conflict between procedure pro- 
vided in an earlier Act of Congress and that provided by the rules, 
the former must yield to the latter”). 


3 


However, this Court has held that a construction of a 
Statute that produces incongruous results should not be 
accepted. NLRB v. Lion Oil Co., 352 U.S. 282, 287 
(1957); United States v. Dow, 357 U.S. 17, 25 (1958). 
Statutes should be interpreted to avoid untenable distinc- 
tions and unreasonable results whenever possible. Ameri- 
can Tobacco Co. v. Patterson, 456 U.S. 63, 71 (1982). 
While a statuary waiver of sovereign immunity is to be 
strictly construed, congressional adoption of broad statu- 
tory language authorizing suits against the United States 
is not to be thwarted by an unduly restrictive interpreta- 
tion. Canadian Aviator Limited v. United States, 324 
U.S. 215, 222 (1945). Likewise, where an other'vise 
acceptable construction of a federal statute would raise 
serious constitutional problems, the United States Supreme 
Court will construe the statute to avoid such problems un- 
less such construction is plainly contrary to the intent of 
Congress. New York v. United States, 505 U.S. 144 
(1992); Concrete Pipe & Products, Inc. v. Construction 
Laborers Pension Trust, 508 U.S. 602 (1993). There- 
fore, this Court should conclude that Rule 4 supplants 
the “forthwith” requirement in the SAA. 


1. The Provisions Of The SAA Negate An Unnec- 
essarily Strict Interpretation Of The “Forth- 
with” Service Requirement. 

The United States argues that requiring “forthwith” 
service in admiralty cases is not inconsistent with the 120 
day time limit in Rule 4. In other words, the government 
contends that it has an interest in expeditious service in 
admiralty litigation involving the government and it is not 
inconsistent with Rule 4 to require immediate service in 
certain cases. (Br. for the United States at 21). How- 
ever, the “forthwith” requirement does not appear in any 
other statute involving a waiver of the government's sov- 
ereign immunity. E.g., 28 U.S.C. §§ 1346(b), 2671- 
2680; (Br. for Petitioner at 26). In fact, similar stat- 
utes based upon the government’s waiver of sovereign 


4 


immunity such as the Federal Tort Claims Act are gov- 
erned by Rule 4. This claim is also inconsistent with the 
fact that seamen are considered “wards of admiralty.” 
See, Garrett v. Moore-McCormack Co., 317 U.S. 239, 
246 (1942). 


Equally untenable is the United States’ contention that 
the SAA does not provide for general uniformity of treat- 
ment of governmental and private defendants in admiralty 
litigation. (Br. for the United States at 23). In short, 
the United States claims that the SAA specifies different 
rules than applicable to private admiralty litigation in that 
suits under the SAA: (1) are subject to a shorter statute 
of limitations; (2) trial by jury is not permitted under 
the SAA; and (3) the statute provides for prejudgment 
interest at the rate of 4%. (Br. for the United States at 
23-24). Although the government concedes that Section 
743 of the SAA provides that such suits “shall proceed 
and shall be heard and determined according to the prin- 
ciples of law and to the rules of practice obtaining in like 
cases between private parties,” the government indicates 
that this provision only applies “to questions that the act 


does not specifically address.” ( Br. for the Government 
at 23). 


First, section 743 does not, as the government asserts, 
specify that the provision only applies to matters the Act 
does not address. Second, the provisions of the SAA cited 
by the Government are primarily substantive provisions 
and do not involve matters of procedure. By enacting 
Rule 4 of the Federal Rules of Civil Procedure, Congress 
intended to create a “uniform and comprehensive method 
of service in all actions against the United States.” H. 
Doc. 588, Notes To tHe RULES OF CiviL PROCEDURE 
For THE District COURTS OF THE UNITED STaTEs, 75th 
Cong., 3d Sess. (1938). In 1966, the federal rules, in- 
cluding Rule 4, were specifically applied to admiralty liti- 
gation. 112 Conc. Rec. 4053 (1966); H. Doc. 391, 
89th Cong., 2d Sess. (1966). Therefore, Congress in- 


5 


tended the federal rules of procedure to supercede incon- 
sistent statutory provisions governing service. 


In fact, the Clarification Act of March 24, 1963, ch. 
26, § 1, 57 Stat. 45, which the government discusses as 
providing an action for claims arising from employment 
in the crew of vessels owned by the United States through 
the Maritime Administration also supports Petitioner’s 
claim. (Br. for the United States at 6-7). The statute 
provides that “officers and members of crews (hereinafter 
referred to as “seamen”) employed on United States or 
foreign flag vessels as employees of the United States 
through the War Shipping Administration shall, with re- 
spect to . . . death injuries, illness, maintenance and 
cure, loss of effects, Uetention or repatriation, . . . have 
all of the rights, benefits, exemptions, privileges, and lia- 
bilities, under law applicable to citizens of the United 
States employed as seamen on privately owned and op- 
erated American vessels.” (emphasis supplied). The lan- 
guage in this statute that seamen are to have “all the 
rights, benefits, exemptions, privileges, and _ liabilities, 
under law applicable to citizens of the United States” 
further supports Petitioner’s position that the legislature 
did not intend to create different rules of procedure gov- 
erning seamen than private parties. This provision clearly 
establishes Congress’s intent to place the government and 
private parties on equal terms. 


The United States also tries to distinguish an opinion 
upon which Petitioner relies, United States v. Isthmian 
S.S. Co., 359 U.S. 314, 324 (1959), in support of his 
claim that in enacting the SAA, Congress intended to 
treat seamen injured aboard government vessels and pri- 
vate litigants equally. (Br. for the United States at 24, 
n.22). The principle issue in that case was whether in 
an action brought under the SAA against the Govern- 
ment to recover costs incurred in transporting cargo, the 
government could file a cross-libel to recover on an unre- 
lated debt owed by the claimant. The Supreme Court 


6 


concluded that the Government could not maintain their 
cross-libel within the same suit and was required to bring 
a separate action in a United States District Court. /d. 
at 324. The Court reasoned that Congress expressly de- 
clared that when sued under the Suits in Admiralty Act, 
the United States is to have its procedural rights deter- 
mined and governed in the same manner as private par- 
ties. Id. at 324. 


The Court later concluded that the district court im- 
properly failed to award interest as provided in the SAA, 
and that Congress intended to cover interest awards 
affirmatively and not have them controlled by the general 
command that the suit “shall proceed and shall be heard 
and determined according to the principles of law” ap- 
plicable to private parties. Jd. The United States ap- 
parently takes the position that because this language in 
the SAA is inapplicable to allow other statutes to govern 
interest awards, this provision is also inapplicable to the 
“forthwith” requirement. However, an award of interest 
clearly involves a matter of substantive law and not pro- 
cedure, which is the issue in this case. Furthermore, there 
is no comprehensive federal code or rules governing in- 
terest awards in all cases, in marked contrast to service 
of process which is governed by the federal rules of civil 
procedure at issue here. Consequently, this language of 
the SAA also supports the claims made by Petitioner in 
this case. 


2. Because The Federal Rules Of Procedure Were 
Not In Effect At The Time The SAA Was En- 
acted, The Service Provisions Of Rule 4 Are 


Controlling. 


The United States also argues that Petitioner’s claim 
that the “forthwith” requirement was included in the SAA 
because no rules of procedure existed at that time is con- 
tradicted by the provision in Section 743 that SAA litiga- 
tion “shall proceed and shall be heard and determined 
according to the principles of law and to the rules of 


7 


practice obtaining in like cases between private parties.” 
(Br. for the United States at 25). In other words, the 
government asserts that by including the “forthwith” re- 
quirement, Congress created an exception to the rule that 
the time for service would be governed by the rules appli- 
cable to private parties. Again, the language in Section 
743 supports Petitioner’s claim because it indicates Con- 
gress’s intent that suits against the Government should 
proceed in the same manner as suits between private par- 
ties. Clearly, without any rules of procedure in effect at 
the time the SAA was enacted, Congress had to provide 
some guidelines governing service on the Government. 


In fact, the legislative history upon which the United 
States relies in its response support the claims raised by 
petitioner in this case. In its brief, the United States 
references a hearing in 1910 before the subcommittee of 
the Senate Judiciary Committee in which the “forthwith” 
requirement is briefly mentioned. (Br. for the United 
States at pp. 4-5 n.5); AMENDMENTS TO ADMIRALTY 
Law: HEARINGS BEFORE THE SUBCOMMITTEE OF THE 
SENATE COMMITTEE ON THE JupIciaRY, 61st Cong., 2d 
Sess. 16 (1910). During that hearing Senator Brandegee 
inquires when service should occur. In response, Archi- 
bald G. Thacher, Procter In Admiralty, who appeared 
before the subcommittee replied, “I should say serve ‘forth- 
with’. There is no reason why it should be not be done 
promptly after the filing of the libel.” According to the 
government, the next version of the bill contained the re- 
quirement that the libel be served “forthwith” and there 
is no other legislative history discussing the term “forth- 
with.” Although this discussion is cited by the United 
States, it supports Petitioner’s position that at the time 
the SAA was enacted there were no rules of procedure 
governing service of process; consequently, this provision 
was included because otherwise there was no time limit 
for service of process. 


The United States also argues that Petitioner’s reliance 
on the 1937 Advisory Committee notes to Rule 4 is mis- 


placed because the notes merely state that Rule 4 “pro- 
vides a uniform and comprehensive method of service for 
all actions against the United States.” (Br. for the 
United States at 19, n.18); H. Doc. 588, Notes To THE 
RULES OF CrviL PROCEDURE For THE DistricT CourRTS 
OF THE UNITED STATES, 75th Cong., 3d Sess. (1938). 
The Government’s conclusion that the term “method” 
does not include timing of service is incorrect, especially 
given the fact that Rule 4 included a requirement that 
service was to be made “forthwith” when the rules of pro- 
cedure were adopted in 1938. H. Doc. 460, 75th Cong., 
3d Sess. (1938). Although Petitioner states in his brief 
that the “method” of service on the United States under 
the SAA and Rule 4 are “essentially the same” (Petition- 
er’s Br. at 10), the two statutes are different in that the 
SAA requires service by registered mail to the Attorney 
General of the United States, while Rule 4 allows service 
by registered or certified mail to the Attorney General of 
the United States. Even though these distinctions are not 
in issue in the present case, Petitioner does not concede 
that there is no conflict between the manner of service 
in these provisions. 


The United States also attempts to distinguish the Third 
Circuit’s decision in Jones & Laughlin Steel, Inc. v. Mon 
River Towing, Inc., 772 F.2d 62, 66 (3d Cir. 1985), 
on the grounds that the decision was merely dictum since 
the Third Circuit held that it lacked jurisdiction under 
the SAA and that jurisdiction existed only under the 
Federal Tort Claims Act. (Br. for the United States 
at 12 n.13). This is an incorrect interpretation of this 
opinion, since the sole issue in the case was whether in 
an admiralty suit against the United States under the 
SAA, Rule 4 supersedes the “forthwith” requirement of 
the SAA. 772 F.2d at 63. The court in Jones & 
Laughlin Steel noted in the case that it believed that 
because the plaintiff did not allege that a vessel of the 
United States caused its damage, the applicable statute 
was the FTCA. /d. at 65. However, because this argu-

ment was never raised, the Court addressed the merits of 
the district court’s interpretation of the SAA. /d. The 
court precisely held that Rule 4 governs service in actions 
based on the FTCA and the SAA. /d. at 66. As in Jones 
& Laughlin Steel, this Court should find that the service

B. The Application Of Rule 4(j) To The SAA Is In 
Harmony With The Rules Enabling Act And Rule 
82 Of The Federal Rules Of Civil Procedure. 


The United States argues that to construe Rule 4(j) 
as superseding the SAA’s requirement of “forthwith” serv- 
ice would expand the jurisdiction of the district court in 
violation of the Rules Enabling Act, 28 U.S.C. § 2072, and 
Rule 82 of the Federal Rules of Civil Procedure. (Br. for 
the United States at 26). In support of this argument the 
government relies upon authority discussing that sovereign 
immunity is jurisdictional in nature and that statutes which 
waive immunity of the United States are to be strictly 
construed in favor of the sovereign. See United States v. 
Mitchell, 445 U.S. 535, 538 (1980); McMahon vy. United 
States, 342 U.S. 25, 27 (1951); FDIC v. Meyer, 114 
S.Ct. 996, 1000 (1994). 


However, in a precisely analogous case, the Supreme 
Court in Mississippi Publishing Corporation v. Murphree, 
326 U.S. 438, 443 (1946), considered whether Rule 4(f) 
of the Rules of Civil Procedure which provided that proc- 
ess “may be served anywhere within the territorial limits of 
the state in which the district court is held” contravenes 
the provisions of the Rules Enabling Act which states that 
the rules “shall neither abridge, enlarge, or modify the sub- 
stantive rights of any litigant,” or Rule 82 which provides 
that the rules “shall not be construed to extend or limit the 
jurisdiction of the district courts... .” The court stated 
that Rule 4(f) and Rule 82 must be construed together, 
and that Rule 82 refers to venue and jurisdiction of the 


10 


subject matter of the district court, while Ruie 4(f) con- 
cerns the means of bringing the defendant before the court 
which already has venue and jurisdiction of the subject 
matter. Id. at 444-445. The Court reasoned that Rule 
4(f) does not enlarge or diminish the venue of the district 
court, or its power to decide the issues of the suit, which is 
jurisdiction of the subject matter to which Rule 82 must 
be taken to refer. “Rule 4(f) serves only to implement the 
jurisdiction over the subject matter which Congress has 
conferred, by providing a procedure by which the defend- 
ant may be brought into court at the place where Congress 
has declared that the suit may be maintained.” /d. at 445. 
Therefore, the ourt concluded that Rule 4(f) and Rule 
82 were consistent. /d. 


The Court also concluded that Rule 4(f) is consistent 
with the Rules Enabling Act. The Court reasoned that: 


Undoubtedly, most alterations of the rules of prac- 
tice and procedure may and often do affect the rights 
of litigants. Congress’ prohibition of any alteration 
of substantive rights of litigants was obviously not 
addressed to such incidental effects as necessarily 
attend the adoption of the prescribed new rules of 
procedure upon the rights of litigants who, agreeably 
to rules of practice and procedure, have been brought 
before a court authorized to determine their rights. 
... The fact that the application of Rule 4(f) 
will operate to subject petitioner's rights to adjudi- 
cation by the district court . . . will undoubtedly affect 
those rights. But it does not operate to abridge, 
enlarge or modify the rules of decision by which that 
court will adjudicate its rights. It relates merely to 
the manner and the means by which a right to re- 
cover... is enforced. In this sense the rule is a rule 
of procedure and not of subsiantive right, and is not 
subject to the prohibition of the Enabling Act. /d. 
at 446. (citations omitted). 


As in Murphree, Rule 4(d) does not abridge any substan- 
tive rights and is consistent with the Rules Enabling Act 
and Rule 82.

Il. THE FAILURE TO APPLY RULE 4 TO THE SAA 
RESTRICTS THE DISTRICT COURT'S POWER TO 
EXTEND THE TIME FOR SERVICE UPON A 
SHOWING OF GOOD CAUSE. 


Finally, the United States criticizes the fact that the 
complaint in this case was filed in the Galveston Division 
of the Southern District of Texas rather than in the Hous- 
ton Division. (Br. for the United States at 9). Suits 
under the SAA may be brought “in the district court of 
the United States for the district in which the parties so 
suing, or any of them, reside or have their principal place 
of business in the United States, or in which the vessel or 
cargo charged with liability is found.” 46 U.S.C. § 742. 
There is no dispute that the complaint in this case was 
filed in the Southern District of Texas where Petitioner 
resided. (App. 5). The statute does not require that the 
complaint be filed in a particular division of the district 
where the claimant resides. Additionally, this claim is 
raised for the first time in this Court. 


Equally disingenious is the United States’ challenge to 
Petitioner's method of obtaining the appropriate summons 
forms and complaint by mail and argument that Petition- 
er’s failure to follow routine practice resulted in the lack of 
forthwith” service. The United States asserts that Peti- 
tioner should have: (1) obtained summons forms and pre- 
pared the summons forms before the complaint was filed; 
and ( 2) filed the complaint by hand, thus facilitating 
immediate issuance and receipt of a signed, sealed sum- 
mons. (Br. for the United States at 9-10, 30-31 ). How- 
ever, there is nothing in the rules of procedure which 
prohibit a request for the appropriate forms by mail. The 
district court obviously concluded that diligence was ex- 
ercised when it initially granted Petitioner an extension 
of time to serve the government. (App. i5). The record 
is also clear that it was the district clerk’s delay in forward- 


ing the appropriate forms which resulted in the delay j 
service. (App. 52, 59-67). "ae 


12 


As discussed above, Petitioner believes that Rule 4 
supplants the “forthwith” requirement of the SAA. Allter- 
natively, Rule 4 of the Federal rules of civil procedure 
should be read in conjunction with the SAA so that the 
district court can determine whether service is “forthwith” 
based upon the standard in Rule 4. This Court’s adop- 
tion of Rule 4 in cases involving the SAA would promote 
justice by allowing district courts to extend the time for 
service upon a showing of “good cause,” such as existed 
in this case. 


CONCLUSION 


For the reasons set forth in this brief, the judgment of 
the court of appeals should be reversed and the case should 
be remanded to the district court for a trial on the merits.
