The City’s Opposition buttresses Beadle’s position that the 
court of appeals applied the wrong legal standard — one

1. The City doesn’t refute that the Eleventh Circuit’s 
decision conflicts with the undue hardship analysis of Brown 
v. General Motors Corp., 601 F.2d 956, 960 (8th Cir. 1979) 
(see Petition at 11, 14, 17), as well as with decisions of the 
Third, Sixth, and Seventh Circuits in Protos v. Volkswagen of 
America, Inc., 797 F.2d 129, 133 (3d Cir. 1986); McDaniel 
v. Essex Int’l, 696 F.2d 34, 37, 38 (6th Cir. 1982); and 
Redmond v. GAF Corp., 574 F.2d 897, 903-04 (7th Cir. 
1978) (see Petition at 14-15, 17-18). The City chooses to 
ignore these cases. 
Instead, the City rejects the theme of Brown, Protos, and 
Redmond that an employer must either reasonably 
accommodate an employee’s religious needs (42 U.S.C. § 
2000e(j)) or demonstrate an "undue hardship." ‘The City would 
replace this statutory requirement with a "more than de 
minimis burden" benchmark. It erroneously rejects Beadle’s 
argument that burden is not the same as costs, Opp. at 18, 
claiming "Hardison and its progeny have held that undue 
hardship can be defined in terms of both economic costs to the 
employer and the burden on the employer such as loss of 
efficiency,’ increased risk, decreased employee morale." 
Opp. at 20.

The City misconstrues Justice O’Connor’s reference to 
private employers in Caldor. Of course, Justice O’Connor 
was not suggesting existence of a greater Establishment Clause 
concern with public employers. Quite the reverse. As Justice 
O’Connor noted in Wallace v. Jaffree, 472 U.S. 38, 83 
(1985): 
The text of the Free Exercise Clause speaks of laws 
that prohibit the free exercise of religion. On its 
face, the Clause is directed at government 
interference with free exercise. Given that concern, 
one can plausibly assert that government pursues

Free Exercise Clause values when it lifts a 
government-imposed burden on the free exercise of 
religion. Ifa statute falls within this category, then 
the standard Establishment Clause test should be 
modified accordingly. It is disingenuous to look for 
a purely secular purpose when the manifest objective 
of a statute is to facilitate the free exercise of 
religion by lifting a government-imposed burden. 
Contrary to the City’s contention, a government employer has 
a greater accommodation burden because of the free exercise 
implications.° 
3. Contrary to the City’s assertion, Beadle does not claim 
"unless the employer suffers ‘costs’ in terms of monetary 
costs, then no matter what other economic burden the 
employer may suffer in accommodating the employee," there 
is no undue hardship. Opp. at 11. Rather, Beadle relies upon 
the Third, Sixth, Seventh, and Eighth Circuits’ interpretation 
that Hardison’s "more than de minimis cost" language relates 
only to tangible economic costs suffered by the employer, 
such as payment of premium wages or a demonstrable loss of 
efficiency. The legal standard remains “undue hardship." 
The City extends the "more than de minimis cost" analysis 
to such nebulous factors as "decrease in employee morale" 
(Opp. at 18), citing EEOC v. Heil-Quaker Corp., 55 Fair. 
Empl. Prac. Cas. (BNA) 1895 (M.D. Tenn. 1990), and Cook 
SAs National Jewish Commission on Law and Public

against the City of Tampa under the First and Fourteenth 
Amendments.
