QUESTIONS PRESENTED 
1. Does the court of appeals decision conflict with the 
holding in Brown v. General Motors Corp. , 601 F.2d 956 (8th 
Cir. 1979), and the teaching of this Court in Trans World 
Airlines, Inc. v. Hardison, 432 U.S. 63 (1977), that an 
employer can be relieved of its obligation to reasonably 
accommodate an employee’s religious practices only if the 
available accommodations involve more than de minimis 
tangible costs, such as payment of premium wages or a 
demonstrable loss of efficiency? 
2. Does the court of appeals decision reflect a serious 
trend by lower courts to erroneously lower the “undue 
hardship" standard to Title VII’s religious accommodation 
requirement so employers need do nothing when asked for 
such an accommodation, a trend only this Court can reverse? 
3. Does the court of appeals decision commit manifest 
error in creating, by judicial fiat, an exemption from the 
religious accommodation requirement of Title VII for 
municipal law enforcement agencies?

STATEMENT OF THE CASE 
A. Relevant Facts 
1. Background 
Aston Beadle is a member of the Seventh-day Adventist 
Church. A tenet of that faith forbids secular labor during its 
Sabbath (from sundown Friday to sundown Saturday). 
Adventists refrain from routine work during the Sabbath, 
although they may engage in emergency or humanitarian work 
(App. at 2a). 
Born and raised in Jamaica, Beadle worked as a policeman 
in that country for two years before coming to the United 
States. Choosing law enforcement as his life’s work, Beadle 
served a brief time with the Hillsborough (Florida) County 
Sheriff’s Office but because he could not work on his Sabbath 
lost his job (App. at 12a).! Beadle then sought other law 
enforcement positions. 
2. The Department’s Refusal to Accommodate 
'Beadle’s action against the Hillsborough County Sheriff's 
office was tried by the same Magistrate Judge, Charles R. 
Wilson, as heard this case. There the magistrate judge held for 
the Sheriff’s office, using the same analysis as employed here 
and concluding the neutral rotating shift system utilized by the 
Sheriff’s Department constituted a reasonable accommodation. 
A Petition for Writ of Certiorari in that case was docketed in 
this Court on March 31, 1995, as Aston A. Beadle v. 
Hillsborough County Sheriff's Dept, et al., No. 94-1610. 
Because both the Hillsborough County Sheriff's Department 
case and this case employed similar analyses, the cases should 
be consolidated for oral argument. 
4 


[PDF PAGE 17]
On January 26, 1987, Beadle successfully completed the 
Tampa police officer’s civil service examination. In an 
interview, he informed the Department he was a Seventh-day 
Adventist and could not work on his Sabbath. The 
Department informed Beadle it was "not required to make any 
special allowance for him." (App. at 2a; PX 9 at 1). Captain 
Walker of the Department’s Personnel Bureau, on April 28, 
told Beadle that he would not be accommodated and would not 
be hired unless willing to work at the time and day scheduled 
(PX 11 at 1; Walker Dep. R2-64-41:3-14). As a result of 
Beadle’s Sabbath accommodation request, Walker 
recommended he not be hired (App. at 13a). 
Consequently, the Police Chief asked the City Attorney for 
a legal opinion on the Department’s obligation to 
accommodation Beadle (Walker Dep. R2-64-24:23-27:11). On 
January 4, 1988, the opinion concluded the Department was 
not required to provide an accommodation for Beadle’s 
religious beliefs (App. at 13a). 
On February 4 during his final interview, the Department 
told Beadle he would not be recommended for hire because he 
refused to work during his Sabbath (R4-47:4-49: 1; 95:7-96:5). 
Nevertheless, early the following week, the Department asked 
Beadle if he was still interested in attending the police 
academy. Beadle indicated he was and received instructions 
to report for employment preliminaries (R4-50:21-52:4). 
Beadle reported for orientation on February 19, 1988. In 
June he successfully completed the course of study at the 
Tampa Police Academy. Although the Department was aware 
of Beadle’s religious accommodation needs, it nonetheless 
"randomly assigned" him to Sergeant Cook’s training squad, 
a position which had Tuesdays, Wednesdays, and Thursdays 
off. Sergeant Dawson’s training squad, to which Beadle could 


[PDF PAGE 18]
have been assigned, had Thursdays, Fridays, and Saturdays 
off (Cook Dep. R2-66-118:7-19; R4-199:12-25). Because of 
the assignment to Cook’s training squad, Beadle was 
scheduled to work on his Sabbath (App. at 14a). 
During his first week of field training, Beadle requested 
Saturday off and was permitted to be absent without pay. But 
the Police Chief informed Beadle further requests would not 
be granted (R2-60:7-13; 214:1-12; App. at 15a). 
The following week the Department denied Beadle’s request 
for Saturday off. He then requested a transfer to another 
squad, but the Department denied this request, explaining if 
Beadle’s request for a specific day off was granted, the 
Department would have to grant other similar requests (App. 
at 15a). 
At a meeting on January 21 or 22, the Police Chief 
demanded Beadle “work [his] shift." Under protest, Beadle 
resigned (App. at 15a). 
3. Feasible No-Cost Means of Accommodation 
Recruits in field training worked four 10-hour «ays, 
followed by three consecutive days off. The shift rotated 
forward one day each week every 56 days (App. at 3a). All 
concede if Beadle had been assigned to Sergeant Dawson's 
squad, initially he would have had Saturdays off. Recruits 
usually spent the first six weeks and the last two weeks with 
one field training officer while rotating to one or more other 
field training supervisors with different schedules for the 
interim 8-week period. Consequently, had Beadle rotated with 
the other members of his squad without an accommodation, he 
eventually would have been assigned to work some Saturdays 
(App. at 15a, 26a). 


[PDF PAGE 19]
Sergeant Cook indicated trainees were assigned to a regular 
squad rather than being placed in a training squad. Under this 
arrangement, two field training officers were assigned per 
squad throughout the district. This program was no different 
from what would have occurred had Beadle been assigned to 
Sergeant Dawson’s squad for two 56-day cycles, alternating 
between two training officers (Cook Dep. R2-66-43: 13-44: 10; 
125:18-126:17). The Department unfortunately refused this 
scheduling device to accommodate Beadle. 
According to Sergeant Cook, such a training program was 
not detrimental to the recruit or to the Department (Cook Dep. 
R2-66-127:15-128:16). Although the training would have 
been essentially the same, it was not used to accommodate 
Beadle because it "was not the policy at the time.” (Cook 
Dep. R2-66-129:12-20). Colonel Fox conceded Beadle could 
have been accommodated throughout his training by being 
assigned to Sergeant Dawson’s squad but explained: "That's 
just the way we did business." (R4-200:2-201:4). 
The trial court found "a possible permanent accommodation 
{after completion of field training] would have been to permit 
Beadle to work at what was known as the ‘downtown squad. ’" 
(App. at 18a-27a). The court also noted another possible 
permanent accommodation would have been an assignment as 
a background investigator. Both were traditionally Monday 
through Friday positions (App. at 18a).

treatment of Beadle violated Title VII of the Civil Rights Act 
of 1964 (id.):

C. The Trial Court’s Decision 
The trial court found Beadle established a prima facie case 
of religious discrimination under Title VII (App. at 23a). As 
to Beadle’s 16-week field training program, the court said: 
1. "Beadle was not assigned to the training squad that had 
the weekend days off, even though it was known that he was 
a Seventh-day Adventist who did not wish to work on 
Saturdays." (App. at 14a); 
2. "Had Beadle been assigned to Sergeant Dawson's 
squad instead of Sergeant Cook’s, he would have his days off 
on Thursday, Friday, and Saturday during the first 56-day 
cycle." (App. at 15a); 
3. "[NJo reason was given [by the Department] for 
Beadle’s assignment to Sergeant Cook’s rather than Sergeant 
Dawson’s squad other than it was a random assignment and no 
accommodation would be made for Beadle." (App. at 15a); 
4.  "{O]ther than granting him his first Saturday off of his 
training period, the City made absolutely no accommodations 
whatsoever to Beadle’s religious needs other than the benefits 
of its neutral rotating shift system." (App. at 26a); and 
5. The Police Department refused to accommodate Beadle

because if Beadle’s "request for a specific day off was 
granted, the Department would then have to concern itself 
with accommodating similar requests made by others." (App.

The court did not disagree that after his 16-week field 
training Beadle could have been permanently accommodated 
since "other positions were available in the City of Tampa

Like its decision in Beadle v. Hillsborough County Sheriff's

scheduling system." (App. at 27a). The court explained: 
[T]he Court concludes that requiring the Department 
to grant shift exceptions to Beadle would be to incur 
a greater than de minimis cost because of the public

with police work. 
(App. at 27a).

D. The Court of Appeals Decision 
The Eleventh Circuit noted that "[rjelying on Beadle v. 
Hillsborough County Sheriff’s Dep’t, 29 F.3d 589 (11th Cir. 
1994), the city and Department argue that a neutral rotating 
shift system is necessarily a reasonable accommodation." 
(App. at 8a). The court distinguished this case from 
Hillsborough where voluntary swaps were permitted. But the 
Tampa Police Department made no such allowance (App. at 
8a). 
The court of appeals observed: "Aside from granting Beadle 
his first Saturday off from work, the magistrate found that the 
Department did nothing to accommodate Beadle other than the 
neutral rotating shift system." (App. at 8a). But the court 
promptly discarded the trial court’s "reasonable 
accommodation" conclusion while affirming its "undue 
hardship" conclusion (App. at 8a): 
We conclude that the magistrate court did not err 
when it found that requiring the Department to grant 
shift exceptions would result in a greater than de 
minimis cost and that the City had met its obligation 
under Title VII. 
(App. at 10a). The Eleventh Circuit acknowledged that 42 
U.S.C. § 2000e(j) provides "[t]he employer violates [Title 
VII] unless it ‘demonstrates that [it] is unable to reasonably 
accommodate . . . an employee’s. . . religious observance 
or practice without undue hardship on the conduct of the 
employer’s business.’" (App. at 6a-7a) (citing Ansonia Bd. of 
Educ. v. Philbrook, 479 U.S. 60, 68 (1986)). 
Two related points flow from the Eleventh Circuit’s 
conclusion. First, it mistakenly expands the Hardison "de 
10 


[PDF PAGE 23]
minimis" costs standard to include "not only monetary 
concerns, but also the employer’s burden in conducting its 
business." (App. at 7a). Second, it further expands "de 
minimis" to include "health, safety, and welfare of its 
citizenry" (App. at 9a) despite a record silent concerning such 
facts; and a Hardison decision which focused on tangible costs 
to the employer. 
REASONS FOR GRANTING THE WRIT 
INTRODUCTION 
The Eleventh Circuit’s decision lowers the standard an 
employer must meet in satisfying its accommodation duty. In 
so doing, the court ignores congressional language and intent. 
The decision also misinterprets this Court’s holding in Trans 
World Airlines, Inc. v. Hardison, 432 U.S. 63 (1977); and 
directly conflicts with the Eighth Circuit Court of Appeals’ 
decision in Brown v. General Motors Corp., 601 F.2d 956 
(8th Cir. 1979). The Eleventh Circuit’s decision (and its 
earlier decision in Beadle v. Hillsborough County Sheriff's 
Dep’t, 29 F.3d 589 (11th Cir. 1994), employing nearly 
identical reasoning) negates Title VII's _ religious 
accommodation requirement. The court shrinks the meaning 
of "undue hardship" to “more than de minimis burden" and 
then makes a per se finding, without reference to the language 
of Title VII, that law enforcement agencies cannot 
accommodate without exceeding such a minimal burden. 
The Eleventh Circuit decisions reflect a serious and growing 
trend by lower courts to expand the “undue hardship" 
exception to Title VII's religious accommodation requirement 
in such a manner.that employers, as here, need do nothing 
when confronted by an employee’s request for an 
accommodation. These decisions also illustrate how 
11 


[PDF PAGE 24]
governmental agencies, relying on counsel’s interpretation of 
the case law, can ignore their accommodation requirements. 
A grave risk exists that the Eleventh Circuit’s twin opinions 
will be interpreted by other courts and employers to say law 
enforcement agencies and others are exempt from Title VII's 
religious accommodation requirements. 
Moreover, this Petition asks whether Hardison’s de minimis 
cost holding should be clarified due to its misapplication by 
lower courts and in light of evolving Establishment Clause 
jurisprudence. For example, Estate of Thornton v. Caldor, 
Inc., 472 U.S. 703, 710 (1985), suggests the Establishment 
Clause permits a workplace accommodation unless it "would 
cause the employer substantial economic burdens or when the 
employer’s compliance would require the imposition of 
significant burdens on other employees required to work in 
place of the Sabbath observers." (Emphasis supplied). 
I. THE COURT OF APPEALS ERRONEOUSLY 
LOWERED THE STATUTORY "UNDUE HARDSHIP" 
STANDARD APPLIED IN TITLE VII RELIGIOUS 
ACCOMMODATION CASES. 
The decision below replaces Congress’ "reasonable 
accommodation without undue hardship“ standard with a 
judicially fashioned "more than de minimis burden" standard, 
thus gutting the religious accommodation requirement of Title 
VII. This act of judicial activism is antithetical to Congress’ 
intent and directly opposes EEOC’s interpretation of the 
statute. 
In 1972 Congress, without opposition, amended Title VII of 
the Civil Rights Act of 1964 providing in section 701(j) (42 
U.S.C. § 2000e(j)): 
12 


[PDF PAGE 25]
The term "religion" includes all aspects of 
religious observance and practice, as well as belief,

B. Reasonable Accommodation and Undue Hardship 
Title VII of the Civil Rights Act of 1964, as amended, 
prohibits an employer from discriminating against an 
employee on the basis of “race, color, religion, sex, or 
nation origin.” 42 U.S.C. § 2000e-2(a)(1). The statute 
defines the term “religion” to include “all aspects of 
religious observance and practice, as well as belief.” 42 
U.S.C. § 2000e(j). “The employer violates the statute 


[PDF PAGE 49]
7a 
unless it ‘demonstrates that [it] is unable to reasonably 
accommodate .. . an employee’s . . . religious observ- 
ance or practice without undue hardship on the conduct 
of the employer’s business.’” Ansonia Bd. of Educ. v. 
Philbrook, 479 U.S. 60, 68, 107 S.Ct. 367, 372, 93 L.Ed.2d 
305 (1986) (quoting 42 U.S.C. 2000e(j)). 
Recognizing that the phrases “reasonable accommoda- 
tion” and “undue hardship” are not defined under the 
statutes, “[eJach case necessarily depends upon its own 
facts and circumstances, and in a sense every case boils 
down to a determination as to whether the employer has 
acted reasonably.” United States v. City of Albuquerque, 
545 F.2d 110, 114 (10th Cir.1976), cert. denied, 433 U.S. 
909, 97 S.Ct. 2974, 53 L.Ed.2d 1092 (1977). Once an 
employer demonstrates that it reasonably accommodated 
an employee’s religious needs, the statutory inquiry 
ends. Ansonio, 479 U.S. at 68, 107 S.Ct. at 371-72. “[T]he 
extent of undue hardship on the employer’s business is 
at issue only where the employer claims that it is unable 
to offer any reasonable accommodation without such 
hardship.” Id. at 68-69, 107 S.Ct. at 372. The Supreme 
Court has described “undue hardship” as any act requir- 
ing an employer to bear more than a “de minimis cost” 
in accommodating an employee’s religious beliefs. Trans 
World Airlines, Inc. v. Hardison, 432 U.S. 63, 84 n. 15, 
97 S.Ct. 2264, 2277 n. 15, 53 L.Ed.2d 113 (1977). The 
Court has also recognized that the phrase “de minimis 
cost” entails not only monetary concerns, but also the 
employer’s burden in conducting its business. Jd.

background investigations for hiring. These positions are 
basically Monday to Friday jobs. 
At the trial, City command officers testified that as- 
signments to a particular training squad without rotation 


[PDF PAGE 61]
19a 
is detrimental to the consistency of the training and results in lack of proper training. According to the City, it is extremely important to the continuity of the train- ing that the police officers be trained under different 
field training officers. 
After his termination, Beadle timely filed a charge of discrimination with the Equal Employment Opportunity 
Commission, received his right to sue letter, and initiated

CONCLUSIONS OF LAW 
1. Waiver and Release 
As a preliminary threshold issue, the Court must con- sider whether the release that Beadle signed, in which 
he agreed to waive his right to sue the City in return for a release from his purported financial obligation for the cost of his training, was valid under the applicable law. 
Employees may validly waive their Title VI rights in private settlements with their employers if their consent 
to a release is both knowing and voluntary. Alexander v. Gardner-Denver Company, 415 U.S. 36, 52, 94 S.Ct. 1011, 1021, 39 L.Ed.2d 147 (1974). Although the Circuits 
are split as to the appropriate source of law to be used in determining the validity of a waiver, the authority most 
prominently cited, and the leading authority is Bormann 
v. A T & T Communications, Inc., 875 F.2d 399 (2d Cir. 
1989). This case sets forth a “totality of the circum- 
stances” test to assess the validity of a particular release. 
The Court in Bormann indicates that the following 
factors must be considered: (1) the plaintiff’s education 
and business experience, (2) the amount of time the plaintiff had possession of or access to the agreement 
before signing it, (3) the role of the plaintiff in deciding 
the terms of the agreement, (4) the clarity of the agree- 
ment, (5) whether the plaintiff was represented by 
counsel or consulted with an attorney, and (6) whether 


[PDF PAGE 62]
20a 
the consideration given in exchange for the waiver 
exceeds employee benefits to which the employee was 
already entitled by contract or law. Jd. at 403, (quoting 
EEOC v. American Express Publishing Corporation, 681 
F.Supp. 216, 219 (S.D.N.Y. 1988). The fifth factor is 
modified to include “whether an employer encourages or 
discourages the employee to consult an attorney and 
whether the employee had a fair opportunity to do so.” 
Bormann, at 403. (citation omitted). Bormann addition- 
ally states that this list of factors is not exhaustive, but 
provides guidance for determining whether a particular 
release is knowing and voluntary. 
The City argues that the release is valid because plain- 
tiff was an educated man, was told that he did not have 
to sign the release if he did not want to, and that the 
terms of the release are clear. The City further argues 
that Beadle thought he was being discriminated against 
when he signed the release, had filed a charge of dis- 
crimination with the EEOC and had spoken with an 
attorney about a charge of religious discrimination 
against his former employer the Hillsborough County 
Sheriff’s Office. Because Beadle had access to two 
separate attorneys and understood the consequences of 
the release, the City argues that the terms of the release 
should be enforced. 
However, having considered the circumstances in their 
totality, the Court is convinced that the release was not 
entered into knowingly and voluntarily under the totali- 
ty of the circumstances. Beadle had possession of the 
release for no more than thirty minutes and as few as 
ten minutes before he signed it. He had absolutely no 
role whatsoever in negotiating the terms of the release. 
Although he could possibly have contacted his counsel, 
and there is no evidence to suggest that he was coerced 
to sign the release, he was not represented by counsel 
nor did he exercise his opportunity to consult with an 
attorney. Although he was not discouraged from consult- 


[PDF PAGE 63]
2la 
ing with an attorney, the Court is unconvinced that he had a fair opportunity to do so, given the stressful situa- tion he faced. The evidence indicates that Beadle, at the time he executed the waiver, was suffering from severe financial and domestic problems. He was terminated 
from his prior position with the Hillsborough County Sheriff’s Office, he was undergoing marital and domestic problems at home and his car had been repossessed. He was faced with the difficult choice of the surrender of his job or the surrender of his religion. 
More importantly, the Court is not convinced that there was an actual financial obligation due from Beadle to the City between the time of his termination (or resignation) and the execution of the waiver. According to the “Binding Contract For Reimbursement Of Hiring and Training Expenses” that Beadle signed at the time of the commencement of his employment, liability for train- ing expenses would have arisen only in the event that he accepted employment with another law enforcement 
agency within the State of Florida within twenty-four 
months from the commencement of full-time service asa police officer subsequent to the completion of the field training period. At the time that Beadle signed the waiver of release, he thought that there was an existing financial obligation which he owed to the City. Pegram’s 
conversations with him at the time of his resignation led him to believe that he owed the City money. Additional- 
ly, the Court is not convinced that a financial obligation 
was due to the City prior to the commencement of full-time service after completion of the field training 
program. Further, the release is not mutual in that a representative of the City did not sign it, agreeing to refrain from holding Beadle responsible from any finan- 
cial obligation. Considering all of these factors in totali- 
ty, the Court is of the opinion that Beadle did not intelli- 
gently and voluntarily sign the release waiving his Title 
VII rights.

2. The Title VII Claim 
The parties stipulated in their pretrial statement that 
this Court has jurisdiction of this action pursuant to 42 
U.S.C. § 2000e-5(f)(3). The parties further agree that at 
all times pertinent to this action, the City was Beadle’s 
“employer” and that Beadle was an “employee” as those 
terms are defined in 42 U.S.C. §§ 2000e(b) and 2000e(f), 
respectively. Additionally, the parties stipulate that 
Beadle satisfied the administrative prerequisites to 
maintaining this action. 
Title VOI of the Civil Rights Act of 1964, as amended, 
42 U.S.C. § 2000e-2(a)(1), makes it unlawful for an 
employer to discriminate against an employee or pro- 
spective employee on the basis of that individual’s “race, 
color, religion, sex, or national origin.” The term “reli- 
gion” includes “all aspects of religious observance and 
practice, as well as belief, unless an employer demon- 
strates that he is unable to reasonably accommodate to 
an employee’s or prospective employee’s religious ob- 
servance or practice without undue hardship on the 
conduct of the employer’s business.” 42 U.S.C. § 
2000e(j). 
To establish a prima facie case of religious discrimina- 
tion, a plaintiff must demonstrate: (1) that he or she had 
a bona fide belief that compliance with an employment 
requirement would be contrary to his or her religious 
belief or practice; (2) that he or she informed the em- 
ployer about the conflict; and (3) that he or she was 
discharged or penalized for failing to comply with the 
conflicting employment requirement. Brener v. Diagnos- 
tic Center Hospital, 671 F.2d 141, 144 (5th Cir. 1982) '.

[PDF PAGE 65]

Murphy v. Edge Memorial Hospital, 550 F.Supp. 1185, 1187-88 (M.D. Ala. 1982). The City does not contest the sincerity of Beadle’s religious beliefs. Further, the Court finds from the evidence that Beadle’s religious beliefs, with respect to Saturday work requirements, conflict with the employment requirements of the City and that Beadle informed the City of the conflict. The Court further finds that based upon the record of facts, Beadle was constructively discharged since he was forced into an involuntary resignation as a result of the inability to comply with his religious beliefs. See Young v. South- western Savings & Loan Association, 509 F.2d 140 (5th Cir. 1975).? 
After a Title VII plaintiff has established a prima facie case, the defendant must show that it offered the plain- tiff a reasonable accommodation of his or her religious practices, or that any accommodation of the plaintiff’s practices would result in undue hardship to the employ- er. 42 U.S.C. § 2000e(j). Murphy v. Edge Memorial Hospi- tal, supra, 550 F.Supp. at 1188. 
“The duty to accommodate requires that the employer take affirmative steps to explore and implement alterna- tives which would satisfy an employee’s religious prac- tices without (1) compromising the employment entitle- ments of other employees or (2) conferring a privilege on the employee the cost of which is more than de minimis.” Murphy v. Edge Memorial Hospital, supra, 550 F.Supp. at 1188. 
Since a prima facie case is established, the specific issue presently before the Court is whether the City could have reasonably accommodated Beadle’s religious needs without incurring undue hardship. To resolve this 
* Pursuant to Bonner v. City of Prichard, Alabama, supra, deci- sions of the former Fifth Circuit entered prior to the split establishing the Eleventh Circuit are binding in the Eleventh Circuit. 


[PDF PAGE 66]
24a 
issue, the Court relies upon TransWorld Airlines, Inc. v. 
Hardison, 432 U.S. 63, 97 S.Ct. 2264, 53 L.Ed.2d 113 
(1977). In that case, Larry G. Hardison, a follower of the 
Worldwide Church of God, sued TransWorld Airlines 
(TWA) and the International Association of Machinists 
and Aerospace Workers, AFL-CIO (IAM), claiming that 
TWA violated Title VII by failing to reasonably accom- 
modate his religious beliefs. Hardison, like Beadle, 
belonged to a religion requiring observance of the Sab- 
bath from sundown Friday to sundown Saturday. TWA 
discharged him without accommodating his request to 
have these days off since shift assignments at its Kansas 
City, Missouri facility were based on a seniority system 
in accordance with a collective bargaining agreement. 
Due to Hardison’s comparatively low seniority, the 
union’s unwillingness to modify the system on Hardison’s 
behalf, Hardison’s religious belief could not meet an 
accommodation absent payment of overtime to another 
substitute employee or forcing the supervisor to take 
Hardison’s place. The TWA facility to which Hardison 
was assigned operated twenty-four hours a day through- 
out the year. 
The Supreme Court found that the evidence estab- 
lished an absence of volunteers to relieve Hardison on 
Saturdays and to give him Saturdays off, would have 
deprived another employee of his or her shift preference 
“at least in part because he did not adhere to a religion 
that observed the Saturday Sabbath.” TransWorld Air- 
lines, Inc. v. Hardison, supra, 432 U.S. at 81, 97 S.Ct. at 
2274. The Court concluded: “Absent a discriminatory 
purpose”, the operation of a neutral scheduling system 
which was designed to meet the needs of all employees, 
“cannot be an unlawful employment practice even if the 
system has some discriminatory consequences.” Trans- 
World Airlines, Inc. v. Hardison, supra, 432 U.S. at 82, 
97 S.Ct. at 2276. The Court found that an employer is 
not required by Title VII to alter such a neutral schedul- 
ing system to meet the religious practices of an employ- 


[PDF PAGE 67]
25a 
ee, but may satisfy the reasonable accommodation re- quirement by demonstrating efforts to accommodate an employee within the neutral system. According to the Court in Hardison, “[i]t would be anomalous to conclude that by ‘reasonable accommodation’ Congress meant that an employer must deny the shift and job preference of some employees, as well as deprive them of their con- tractual rights, in order to accommodate or prefer the religious needs of others, and we conclude that Title VII does not require an employer to go that far.” Trans- World Airlines, Inc. v. Hardison, supra, 432 U.S. at 81, 97 S.Ct. at 2274. The Court evaluates reasonable accom- modation in light of the employer's ability to accommo- date an employee within the framework of the system established without denying the benefits of the system to other employees or incurring a greater than de mini- mis cost. As stated in Murphy v. Edge, supra, an accommodation is not required under Title VII when: (1) the accommodation will deprive the benefits of the neutral scheduling system to any other employee, or (2) will require the employer to incur a greater than de minimis cost.” Murphy v. Edge Memorial Hospital 550 F.Supp. at 1189. 
As stated in Williams v. Southern Union Gas Compa- ny, 529 F.2d 483, 489 (10th Cir.), cert. denied, 429 U.S. 959, 97 S.Ct. 381, 50 L.Ed.2d 325 (1976), the phrases “reasonably accommodate” and “undue hardship” are relative terms that cannot be given any hard and fast meaning. “Each case necessarily depends upon its own facts and circumstances, and in a sense every case boils down to a determination as to whether the employer has acted reasonably.” United States v. City of Albuquerque, 545 F.2d 110, 114 (10th Cir.) cert. denied, 433 U.S. 909, 97 S.Ct. 2974, 53 L.Ed.2d 1092 (1976). 
In the United States v. City of Albuquerque, Id., the religious discrimination issue was faced by a public employer, i.e., the City’s Fire Department. In that case, 


[PDF PAGE 68]
26a 
the Court noted that “the ‘business’ was that of fire 
suppression, and such is obviously a matter of great 
public interest.” Jd. at 114. The interest is “in protecting 
the lives and property of a dependent citizenry”. To that 
extent, that Court determined that Courts should “go 
slow in restructuring . . . employment practices.” Id. 
In the present case, Beadle was assigned to an initial 
training squad which gave him Tuesdays, Wednesdays 
and Thursdays off but not the days of his Sabbath. Had 
he been assigned to the other training squad, he would 
have had his Sabbath days off initially, but since the 
training squads are rotated, ultimately he would have 
been required to work on a Sabbath Day. Unlike his prior 
employment, (Hillsborough County Sheriff’s Office), the 
City did not allow Beadle to swap days off with other 
employees, relying upon the integrity of its training 
program which requires exposure to a variety of training 
officers. Clearly, other than granting him his first Satur- 
day off of his training period, the City made absolutely 
no accommodations whatsoever to Beadle’s religious 
needs other than the benefits of its neutral rotating shift 
system. The City contends, however, that because of the 
particular nature of police work, accommodating an 
employee outside of the neutral rotating shift system 
would deprive the benefits of the system to other 
employees and result in a greater than de minimis cost 
to the City. Although the cases reflect the view that the 
trial court must evaluate reasonable accommodation and 
undue hardship in light of the employment context of 
each case, the Court is unable to find any authority 
which would require a public health or safety agency to 
grant such an employee an exception from the custom- 
ary neutral rotating shift system used by law enforce- 
ment agencies in order to accommodate the Sabbath 
requirements of the officer. 
In an action by a FBI agent against the United States 
Department of Justice claiming religious discrimination 


[PDF PAGE 69]
27a 
under Title VII due to denial of Sabbath observance 
requirements, the Seventh Circuit concluded “[c]om- pelled, as it is by Title VII, to have one rule for all of the diverse religious beliefs and practices in the United States, the FBI may choose to be stingy with exceptions 
lest the demand for them overwhelm it.” Ryan v. U.S. Department of Justice, 950 F.2d 458, 462 (7th Cir.), cert. denied, 112 S.Ct. 2309, 119 L.Ed.2d 230 (1991). 
Beadle argues that other positions were available in the City of Tampa Police Department, such as what is known as the “downtown Squad”, assignment to which would accommodate his religious beliefs, but that the City took an “intransigent” position by failing to afford 
him an assignment to such a position. However, Beadle never gave the system an opportunity or a chance to work for him. He may have been eligible for such an assignment after completion of his training. Deputy Chief Curtis Lane encouraged him to do so but Beadle’s conscience would not allow him to do so. 
The Court interprets TransWorld Airlines, Inc. v. Hardison to provide that Title VII does not require a law enforcement agency of the nature of the City of Tampa Police Department to grant its law enforcement officer a permanent weekly religious accommodation to the exclu- 
sion of his or her fellow officers. The City of Tampa Police Department is a 24-hour a day, seven-day a week, 
365-day a year agency with public health, safety and welfare responsibilities. The Court is unable to conclude 
that the accommodation of a transfer of Beadle to another training shift would have deprived another 
police trainee of the benefits of the neutral scheduling 
System since the evidence did not establish that a swap was necessary, at least at the training stage. However, 
the Court concludes that requiring the Department to grant shift exceptions to Beadle would be to incur a greater than de minimis cost because of the public 
health, safety and welfare considerations associated with 


[PDF PAGE 70]
28a 
police work. Title VII does not require the employer to 
assure the individual the opportunity to observe religious 
practices at all costs. See Ansonia Board of Education v. 
Philbrook, 479 U.S. 60, 107 S.Ct. 367, 93 L.Ed.2d 305 
(1986). In the present case, the City met its obligation 
under Title VII when it offered the neutral shift rotating 
system as a reasonable accommodation to Beadle. 
Accordingly, the Court directs the Clerk to enter 
judgment for the defendant.

(a) Purpose of this section. 
This section clarifies the obligation imposed by Title VII 
of the Civil Rights Act of 1964, as amended, (sections 
701(j), 703 and 717) to accommodate the religious practices 
of employees and prospective employees. This section does 
not address other obligations under Title VII not to dis-

may exist pursuant to constitutional, or other statutory

The legal principles which have been developed with re- 
spect to discrimination prohibited vy Title VII on the bases 
of race, color, sex, and national origin also apply to re- 
ligious discrimination in all circumstances other than where 
an accommodation is required.

ployee or prospective employee, unless the employer 
demonstrates that accommodation would result in undue 
hardship on the conduct of its business.’ 
(2) Section 701(j) in conjunction with § 703(c), imposes 
an obligation on a labor organization to reasonably accom-

The following list is an example of areas in which flex- ibility might be introduced: flexible arrival and departure times; floating or optional holidays; flexible work breaks; use of lunch time in exchange for early departure; stag- gered work hours; and permitting an employee to make up time lost due to the observance of religious practices.* 
(iii) Lateral Transfer and Change of Job Assignments. 
When an employee cannot be accommodated either as to his or her entire job or an assignment within the job, employers and labor organizations should consider whether or not it is possible to change the job assignment or give the employee a lateral transfer.

(e) Undue Hardship. 
(1) Cost. 
An employer may assert undue hardship to justify a

§ 1605.3 Selection practices. 
(a) Scheduling of Tests or Other Selection Procedures. When a test or other selection procedure is scheduled at a time when an employee or prospective employee cannot attend because of his or her religious practices, the user of the test should be aware that the principles enunciated in these guidelines apply and that it has an obligation to accommodate such employee or prospective employee un- less undue hardship would result. 
(b) Inquiries Which Determine An Applicant’s Availa- bility to Work During An Employer’s Scheduled Working Hours. 
(1) The duty to accommodate pertains to prospective employees as well as current employees. Consequently, an employer may not permit an applicant’s need for a reli- gious accommodation to affect in any way its decision whether to hire the applicant unless it can demonstrate that it cannot reasonably accommodate the applicant’s re- ligious practices without undue hardship.
