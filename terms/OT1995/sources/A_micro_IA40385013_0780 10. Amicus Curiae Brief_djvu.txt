SUMMARY OF ARGUMENT 


Development of the appropriate standard for appellate 
review of district court determinations of mixed questions 
of fact and law turns on an assessment of whether the 
trial court or the appellate court is “better positioned” to 
decide the particular issue. The assessment takes account 
of the characteristics of the question under review and 
the respective institutional advantages of the federal trial 
and appellate courts in deciding it. When a mixed ques- 
tion of fact and law involves application of a standard 
that is “non-technical” in nature to an infinite variety of 
fact situations—such that a decision in one case will sel- 
dom serve as a useful precedent for others—the question 
is appropriately committed to the district courts, subject 
to deferential, clearly erroneous review on appeal. 


Probable cause and reasonable suspicion determina- 
tions require a “non-technical,” totality of the circum- 
stances analysis, judged from the perspective of an ex- 
perienced law enforcement officer. In //linois v. Gates, 462 
U.S. 213 (1983), this Court rejected the two-prong test 
that the Court had developed in Aguilar v. Texas, 378 
U.S. 108 (1964), and Spinelli v. United States, 393 U.S. 
410 (1969), for evaluating when information from an 
informant provided probable cause, because probable 
cause is a “fluid concept” that is “not readily, or even 
usefully, reduced to a neat set of legal rules,” noting that 
“one determination will seldom be a useful ‘precedent’ for 
another.” /d. at 232, 238 n.11. 


The district courts are in the superior position to make 
the totality of the circumstances probable cause determi- 
nation, a weighted assessment of physical and testimonial 
evidence under well-developed, though necessarily general, 
principles. The clearly erroneous standard strikes the 
proper balance for appellate review of probable cause 
and reasonable suspicion determinations, in that it assigns 
primary responsibility for making this non-technical deter- 


8 


mination to the district courts, while preserving the ability 
of the courts of appeals to correct both legal mistakes 
and unambiguously erroneous applications of probable 
cause principles. 


Although the parties suggest that this Court’s prior 
decisions have “expressly indicated” that probable cause 
determinations regarding warrantless searches or arrests 
should receive “independent review,” it is not so. This 
Court has never directly confronted the question. In any 
event, “independent review” is not necessarily inconsistent 
with review under the clearly erroneous standard. 


Adoption of the clearly erroneous standard for review 
of probable cause and reasonable suspicion determinations 
in federal criminal cases would imply no denigration of 
Fourth Amendment rights. The clearly erroneous stand- 
ard of review would apply equally to appeals by defend- 
ants following final judgment and to pre-trial appeals by 
the government where a suppression motion has been 
granted. A decision as to the appropriate standard of 
appellate review is Fourth Amendment neutral. 


Nor would adoption of the clearly erroneous standard 
on appeal undermine the preference for warrants. The 
clearly erroneous standard mandates deference only to 
decisions of the district courts as to whether there was or 
was not probable cause. It does not accord deference to 
law enforcement officers searching without a warrant. 
The advantages of obtaining a warrant would remain. 
Evidence gathered in good faith pursuant to a facially 
valid warrant is admissible even if a reviewing court is 
convinced that the magistrate’s decision to issue it lacked 
a substantial basis. And in cases where the probable 
cause issue is close, doubts are resolved in favor of validat- 
ing the warrant. No such advantages apply in non-warrant 
cases. 


Were this Court to require de novo appellate review 
of federal district court probable cause and reasonable

9 


suspicion determinations, two types of anomalies would 
arguably result. First, the Court would be requiring 
greater appellate scrutiny of decisions made by the district 
courts after full adversary hearings than is given to the 
seemingly less reliable decisions of magistrates who issue 
warrants on the basis of ex parte affidavits. Second, the 
Court would be requiring greater appellate scrutiny of 
federal district court probable cause and reasonable sus- 
picion determinations than is applied in reviewing the 
sufficiency of the evidence to support findings of guilt in 
federal criminal cases. This would be an inversion of the 
priorities in effect established by this Court in other 
settings. See Jackson v. Virginia, 443 U.S. 307 (1979); 
Stone v. Powell, 428 U.S. 465 (1976).

A. Developing the Standard of Appellate Review 


De novo review means a fresh determination of an issue 
that accords no weight or deference to any prior resolu- 
tion of it. See, e.g., Renegotiation Board v. Bannercraft 
Clothing Co., Inc., 415 U.S. 1, 23 (1974); United States 
v. First City National Bank, 386 U.S. 361, 368 (1967); 
see also Raddatz, 447 U.S. at 690 (Stewart, J., dissenting). 


By contrast, clearly erroneous review requires the appel- 
late court “to uphold any district court determination that 
falls within a broad range of permissible conclusions.” 
Cooter & Gell v. Hartmarx Corp., 496 U.S. 384, 400 


McConney represent the most extensive discussions of the issue 
by the courts of appeals. 


A number of courts of appeals have taken inconsistent approaches 
over time. For example, while the First Circuit now reviews the 
conclusion as to the existence of probable cause de novo, United 
States v. Zapata, 18 F.3d 971, 975 (1st Cir. 1994), it had previ- 
ously taken the contrary view, United States v. Rodriguez-Morales, 
929 F.2d 780, 783 (1st Cir. 1991) (“[W)hether police activity is 
reasonable in any particular set of circumstances is almost in- 
variably a factbound inquiry. The nisi prius court sees and hears 
the witnesses and will ordinarily develop a superior ‘feel’ for what 
transpired. Hence appellate oversight is correspondingly defer- 
ential; the court of appeals reviews the district court's findings 
of fact following a suppression hearing, including mixed fact/law 
findings, under the clearly erroneous test.”), cert. denied, 502 U.S. 
1030 (1993). 


Similarly, the Sixth Circuit now takes the position that de novo 
review is required, United States v. Padro, 52 F.3d 120, 122 (6th 
Cir. 1995), whereas it had previously used the clearly erroneous 
standard, United States v. Sangineto-Miranda, 859 F.2d 1501, 1508 
(6th Cir. 1988). It has been suggested that the Sixth Circuit 
shifted its appellate review standard in response to a supposed 
“hint” in Williams v. United States, 500 U.S. 901 (1991), in which 
the Court granted certiorari, vacated and remanded the case to 
consider the Solicitor General’s position that de novo appellate 
review is required. See United States v. Sims, 975 F.2d 1225, 1238 
(6th Cir. 1992), cert. denied sub nom. Gardner v. United States, 
507 U.S. 932 (1993); see also United States v. Williams, 949 F.2d 
220, 221-22 (6th Cir. 1991) (on remand), cert. denied, 504 U.S. 
958 (1992).

B. Appellate Review of Probable Cause and Reasonable 
Suspicion Determinations 


The starting point for analysis of the appropriate stand- 
ard of review is an examination of the nature of probable 
cause and reasonable suspicion determinations. The an- 
cient term, “probable cause,” enshrined in the Fourth 
Amendment, refers to a quantum of proof, the point at 
which, considering the facts and circumstances, a particu- 
lar matter is deemed sufficiently probable. To quote Chief 
Justice Marshall: 


(T]he term “probable cause,” according to its usual 
acceptation, means less than evidence which would 
justify condemnation; and, in all cases of seizure, 
has a fixed and well known meaning. It imports a 
seizure made under circumstances which warrant 
suspicion.

Probable cause is sometimes defined in part by stating 
what it is not. See Illinois v. Gates, 462 U.S. 213, 235 
(1983) (probable cause is not a “[fJinely tuned standard{ | 
such as proof beyond a reasonable doubt or by a pre-

U.S. 410, 419 (1969) ). 


Probable cause never exists in a vacuum. The question 
is always probable cause with respect to what, such as 
whether there is probable cause to believe a particular 
individual arrested is the perpetrator of the crime, or that 
certain known conduct amounts to a crime, or that con- 
traband is hidden in a particular automobile. The fact 
that it is applied to a myriad of situations means that 
developing a set of rules to cover all situations is 
impracticable. 


It would, of course, be helpful to the district courts 
and law enforcement officers were this Court or the courts 
of appeals able to formulate a set of rules to provide 
specific guidance for making probable cause determina- 
tions. And, for a time, the Court attempted to do just 
that with respect to when information from informants, 
together with other facts, amounted to probable cause. 
But it is instructive that that effort ultimately foundered.

D. Fourth Amendment Values 


The parties suggest that the fact that probable cause 
and reasonable suspicion determinations are often co- 
terminous with determinations as to whether the Consti- 
tution was violated weighs heavily toward de novo review 
on appeal. Brief for the United States at 29; Brief for 
Petitioners at 32. But this Court has never held that de 
novo review of “constitutional facts” is the general rule. 
Rather, it has affirmatively stated the opposite, that “an 
issue does not lose its factual character merely because 
its resolution is dispositive of the ultimate constitutional 
question.” Miller v. Fenton, 474 U.S. at 113 (citing 
Dayton Board of Education v. Brinkman, 443 U.S. 526, 
534 (1979) (finding of intent to discriminate subject to 
clearly erroneous standard of review) ). 


Bose Corp. and Harte-Hanks Communications are not 
to the contrary. Bose Corp., in holding that de novo 
appellate review of actual malice determinations is re- 
quired, emphasized the Court's historic role in perform- 
ing a fully independent factual review in First Amendment 
cases in particular. 466 U.S. at 499, 503-11. Harte- 
Hanks, which re-affirmed Bose Corp., specifically under- 
scored the “unique character of the interest protected by 
the actual malice standard” in public official defamation 
cases, adding that “[t}his value must be protected with 
special vigilance.” 491 U.S. at 686, 687.

It might be suggested that to adopt the clearly erroneous

Nor would adoption of the clearly erroneous standard 
of review remove law enforcement officers’ incentives to 
obtain a warrant. Ample advantages would remain. Evi- 
dence gathered pursuant to a facially valid warrant is 
admissible even if a reviewing court is convinced that the 
magistrate’s decision to issue it lacked a substantial basis. 
See United States v. Leon, 468 U.S. 897 (1984). And 
unlike in non-warrant search cases, where the probable

validating a warrant. See Illinois v. Gates, 462 U.S. at 
237 n.10; United States v. Ventresca, 380 U.S. 102, 109 
(1965). In sum, a law enforcement officer who obtains a 
warrant before searching will justifiably have great com- 
fort in the admissibility of any evidence gathered. To 
search without a warrant entails greater risk. 


E. Anomalies That Would Result From De Novo Review 


If the Court were to require de novo review of probable 
cause and reasonable suspicion determinations in cases 
where there was no warrant, two types of anomalies would 
arguably result. 


31 


First, this Court would be requiring the courts of 
appeals to apply greater scrutiny to probable cause deter- 
minations made in non-warrant cases after a full adversary 
hearing in the district court than to seemingly less reliable 
determinations made on the basis of ex parte affidavit 
presentations in warrant cases. See Massachusetts v. Up- 
ton, 466 U.S. 727, 733 (1984); Illinois v. Gates, 462 U.S. 
at 238-39. While the Court might well regard such a 
result to be the unavoidable byproduct of a desire to 
encourage warrants, it would nonetheless appear to be 
a less than balanced allocation of appellate judicial re- 
sources. See Spears, 965 F.2d at ?70 (discussing Judge 
Posner's concurrence in United States v. McKinney, 919 
F.2d 405, 422 (7th Cir. 1990) ). 


A second type of anomaly that would emerge from 
requiring de novo review of probable cause and reasonable 
suspicion determinations in federal cases is that greater 
appellate scrutiny would then be given to such determina- 
tions than to findings of guilt following a bench trial. 
It has long been the rule in the federal courts that, in 
reviewing the sufficiency of the evidence to support a 
conviction, the court of appeals must affirm so long as the 
evidence, viewed in the light most favorable to the prose- 
cution, could lead a rational trier of fact to find guilt be- 
yond a reasonable doubt. See, e.g., Glasser v. United 
States, 315 U.S. 60, 80 (1942); Curley v. United States, 
160 F.2d 229, 232-33 (D.C. Cir.), cert. denied sub nom. 
Smith v. United States, 331 U.S. 837 (1947). Jackson 
v. Virgina, 443 U.S. 307, 318-19 (1978), effectively 
raised the Glasser/Curley standard to constitutional status, 
in ruling that Jn re Winship, 397 U.S. 358 (1970), re- 
quired federal courts to review state-court convictions for 
sufficiency of the evidence on habeas corpus. Unlike de 
novo appellate review, which requires that the court weigh 
the evidence for itself, however, “this [Jackson v. Virginia 
sufficiency of the evidence] inquiry does not require a 
court to ‘ask itself whether if believes that the evidence
