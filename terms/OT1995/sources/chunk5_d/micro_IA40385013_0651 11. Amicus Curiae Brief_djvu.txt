INTEREST OF AMICI CURIAE 


Because the Age Discrimination in Employment Act 
(ADEA) defines "employer" to include "a state or political 
subdivision of a state,” 29 U.S.C. § 630(b), a state agency 
that violates the ADEA is liable for both legal and equitable 
relief. 29 U.S.C. § 626(b) & (c). Institutional employers 
such as the States, like their private sector counterparts, 
have an acute interest in knowing the rules that govern 
ADEA litigants. The States, therefore, have a strong 
- interest in this case because it requires this Court to define 
the nature of proof needed to establish a prima facie case in 
causes of action instituted pursuant to the ADEA. 


STATEMENT OF THE CASE 


James O'Connor initiated the present action pursuant to 
the ADEA following his discharge from employment with 
Consolidated Coin Caterers Corporation (Consolidated). 
O'Connor v. Consolidated Coin Caterers Corp., 56 F.3d 
542, 543 (4th Cir. 1995). O'Connor was 56 years of age at 
the time of his discharge; he was replaced by someone who 
was 40 years of age. /d. at 544. The United States District 
Court for the Western District of North Carolina granted 
summary judgment in favor of Consolidated, ruling that 
O'Connor had failed to establish, under the proof scheme 
enunciated in McDonnell Douglas Corp. v. Green, 411 U.S. 
792 (1973), a prima facie case that Consolidated had done 
anything other than treat age neutrally in deciding to 
terminate him. 56 F.3d at 545. The district court went on 
to hold that O'Connor had likewise failed to establish by 
traditional proof methods that his discharge was age-based. 
Id. 


On appeal, the United States Court of Appeals for the 
Fourth Circuit affirmed the judgment. The court held that 
O'Connor could not survive summary judgment under the 


3 


McDonnell Douglas rubric because the employee who 
replaced him was outside of the class of persons protected 
by the ADEA. /d. at 547. The court held that O'Connor 
fared no better in his efforts to offer direct proof that age 
was the determining factor in Consolidated's decision to 
discharge him. /d. at 549-50. The court found no 
discriminatory intent was evinced by the age-related 
remarks that Consolidated allegedly made. /d. 


Judge Butzner filed a separate opinion concurring in part 
and dissenting in part. /d. at 550-51. 


SUMMARY OF ARGUMENT 


This Court should adopt the requirement that the court 
of appeals imposed in this case. Just as discriminatory 
intent is not manifested when a member of a particular race, 
national origin, or gender is replaced by a member of the 
same classification, such intent does not arise under the 
ADEA when one member of the protected classification 
replaces another. The rule that the court of appeals used 
should be affirmed because it parallels the line drawing that 
Congress engaged in when it enacted the ADEA, and it 
ensures consistent results when determining whether a party 
has made out a prima facie case under the ADEA. 


ARGUMENT 


A PLAINTIFF ALLEGING AN UNLAWFUL 
VIOLATION OF THE ADEA MUST SHOW, TO 
ESTABLISH A PRIMA FACIE CASE UNDER 
THE MCDONNELL DOUGLAS CRITERIA, 
THAT HE WAS REPLACED BY SOMEONE 
OUTSIDE OF THE PROTECTED AGE 
GROUP. 


The decision of the court of appeals -- that in ADEA 
cases the plaintiff must focus on the personal characteristics 
of the person chosen to replace him and show that he or she 


4 


was someone outside of the class of persons protected 
against age discrimination -- should be affirmed for the 
principal reason that it is consistent with Congress’ decision 
to craft bright lines in limiting the ADEA's protection to 
those falling within a defined age group.’ In setting forth 
that the ADEA is "to promote employment of older persons 
based on their ability rather than age [and] to prohibit 
arbitrary age discrimination in employment,” 29 U.S.C. § 
621(b), Congress deliberately chose to have the statute's 
prohibitions apply only to those individuals at least 40 years 
of age and less than 65 years of age. 29 U.S.C. § 631(a). 
This choice sharply distinguishes the ADEA from virtually 
any other civil rights statute, such as Title VII, for example, 
which extends its protections to a// members of a particular 
race, national origin, gender, or other statutorily defined 
class. 


The test that the court of appeals applied results in no 
more of an injustice than does that which derives from 
Congress’ decision to delimit the class of plaintiffs eligible 
for protection under the ADEA. In fact, a bright line rule, 
requiring a plaintiff to show as an essential part of his prima 
facie case that he was replaced by someone outside of the 
class of persons protected by the ADEA, is fully consonant 
with Congress’ drawing of a clear line in limiting the 
protections of the ADEA to a defined age group. 


The court of appeals’ requirement also provides an 


' The Fourth Circuit is not alone in requiring an ADEA 
plaintiff to show that he or she was replaced by someone outside 
of the protected class. The Sixth Circuit adheres to a like view, 
see LaPointe v. United Autoworkers Local 600, 8 F.3d 376, 379 
(6th Cir. 1993), though it did not always do so as evidenced by 
a case predating LaPointe. See Barnes v. Gencorp, Inc., 896 
F.2d 1457, 1466 (6th Cir. 1990). 


5 


unambiguous, workable rule that, to the exclusion of any 
other rule, best befits the age characteristic. Requiring that 
the age of the person chosen to replace the ADEA claimant 
be outside of the ADEA's protected class, as an essential 
element of the McDonnell Douglas framework, ensures the 
precise type of objective evidence that provides the basis for 
a permitted inference of discriminatory intent. The first and 
third prongs of the McDonnell Douglas scheme are no more 
than standing requirements; the discharge constitutes the 
cognizable injury, and being a member of the ADEA's 
protected ciass satisfies the requirement of being within the 
ambit of interests protected by the statute. McCorstin v. 
United States Steel Corp., 621 F.2d 749, 753 (Sth Cir. 
1980). The second prong relating to the plaintiff's 
qualifications for the job, at most, operates to negate a 
possible rebuttal to the claim of discrimination that has been 
deemed reasonable to place within the plaintiffs burden of 
proof. /d. A showing that the plaintiff was replaced by 
someone outside the protected class is evidence truly 
probative of unequal treatment and is, therefore, a relevant 
consideration. 

This type of showing provides the courts with the most 
effective and trustworthy method by which to deal with age- 
based discrimination cases predicated on circumstantial 
evidence. As Petitioner concedes, unlike race or gender, 
age is not an immutable characteristic. Brief of Petitioner 
at 17. Age is a matter of degree. It is a continuum. A 
disparity in age can result from a differential spanning many 
or a few years, or even only months. It is this quality which 
compels the bright-line rule employed by the court below. 

Amici recognize that other circuits have not required a 
plaintiff to show as part of his or her prima facie case proof 
that the replacement employee is outside the protected age 
group. See Roper v. Peabody Coal Co., 47 F.3d 925, 927 


6 


(7th Cir. 1995); Rinehart v. City of Independence, Mo., 35 
F.3d 1263, 1266 (8th Cir. 1994); Freeman v. Package 
Machinery Co., 865 F.2d 1331, 1335 n.2 (1st Cir. 1988); 
Bienkowski v. American Airlines, Inc., 851 F.2d 1503, 1505 
(Sth Cir. 1988); Cooper v. Asplundh Tree Expert Company, 
836 F.2d 1544, 1547 (10th Cir. 1988); Maxfield v. Sinclair 
International, 766 F.2d 788, 792 (3d Cir. 1985); Haskell v. 
Kaman Corp., 743 F.2d 113, 122 (2d Cir. 1984); Douglas 
v. Anderson, 656 F.2d 528, 532-33 (9th Cir. 1981); Cuddy 
v. Carmen, 694 F.2d 853, 857 (D.C. Cir. 1982). But the 
tests that have been substituted in place of this requirement 
are unworkable, inconsistent with one another, and 
incompatible with the essentially arbitrary line that 
Congress drew in enacting the ADEA. 


For example, both the District of Columbia and the 
Tenth Circuit Courts of Appeals allow an ADEA claimant 
to show that he or she was replaced by a "younger" person. 
Cuddy v. Carmen, 694 F.2d at 857; Cooper, 836 F.2d at 
1547. Under this paradigm, a discharged employee of 49 
years of age could establish a prima facie case of age-based 
discrimination in any case where the person replacing him 
or her is "younger," even if the age disparity is a year or 
even less. Stated differently, under the "younger" person 
approach, an inference of age discrimination would be 
permitted even where the probative value of such an 
inference would be virtually nonexistent. 


In apparent recognition of the difficulty created by the 
“younger” person formulation, other circuits have qualified 
that test somewhat. The Third, Fifth, Seventh, and Eighth 
Circuit Courts of Appeals require that the employee who 
replaced the plaintiff be "sufficiently" younger than the 
plaintiff. See Maxfield, 766 F.2d at 792; Bienkowski, 851 
F.2d at 1505; Roper, 47 F.3d at 927; Rinehart, 35 F.3d at 
1266. The Second and Ninth Circuit Courts of Appeals 


7 


require that the plaintiff show that the employee who 
replaced him or her was “substantially” younger than he or 
she. See Haskell, 743 F.2d at 122; Douglas v. Anderson, 
656 F.2d at 532-33. 


Far from eliminating the difficulty engendered by the 
"younger" person showing, the “sufficiently” and 
"substantially" younger requirements only add to the 
problem. What constitutes a "sufficient" or "substantial" 
age disparity is an open question. The vagueness inherent 
in both terms will only inspire irreconcilable results. 


In contrast, requiring that a plaintiff show that the person 
who replaced him or her was someone outside of the 
protected class provides the sort of meaningful guidance 
needed when grappling with age-based discrimination 
claims under the McDonnell Douglas rubric. It promotes 
ease of administration, and leads to a result that renders 
application of the McDonnell Douglas scheme neither 
mechanistic nor ritualistic. Importantly, it ensures that an 
inference of discriminatory intent will be drawn in those 
circumstances warranting such an inference, and eliminates 
the inconsistent judicial line drawing that courts have 
engaged in when determining whether a prima facie case 
has been made out. 


The United States and the American Association of 
Retired Persons, in their respective briefs as amicus curiae, 
contend that the rule applied by the court below runs 
counter to the proposition stated by this Court in 
Connecticut v. Teal, 457 U.S. 440, 454 (1982), that an 
employer cannot escape liability for a specific instance of 
racial discrimination by treating other members of the 
affected party's racial group favorably. See Brief of United 
States at 15-16; Brief of American Association of Retired 
Persons at 21. This argument confuses unlawful 


discrimination with discriminatory intent. Teal was a 
disparate impact case. In such cases, the focus is on 
whether the challenged practice has a discriminatory effect. 


Here, the focus is on the entirely separate matter of 
discriminatory intent. As this Court pointed out in 7eal, 
"[p]roof that a work force was racially balanced or that it 
contained a disproportionately high percentage of minority 
employees is not wholly irrelevant on the issue of intent 
when that issue is yet to be decided." 457 U.S. at 454. 
Likewise, proof that the employee who replaced the ADEA 
claimant is someone outside of the protected class is 
relevant insofar as it permits the inference of age-based 
discrimination to be drawn. 


Put simply, Teal does not stand for the proposition that 
unlawful discriminatory intent is evinced when a female 
employee is repiaced with another female, or when an 
African-American employee is replaced with another 
African-American. It is equally nonsensical to hold in this 
case that replacing a discharged employee with someone 
within the protected age group constitutes impermissible 
discrimination under the ADEA.

CONCLUSION 


For the reasons stated, the judgment of the court of 
appeals should be affirmed.
