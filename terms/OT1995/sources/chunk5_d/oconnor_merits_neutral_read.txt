Statute Involved 

The Age Discrimination In Employment Act (ADEA), 
29 U.S.C. § 621 et seq., provides in pertinent part: 

§ 623. Prohibition of age discrimination. 

(a) Employer Practices. It shall be 
unlawful for an employer: 

(1) to fail or refuse to hire or dis- 
charge any individual or 
otherwise discriminate against 
any individual with respect to 
his compensation, terms, con- 
ditions, or privileges of 
employment because of such 
individual's age. 

§ 631. Age Limits. 

(a) Individuals at least 40 years of age. 
The prohibitions in this Act shall 
be limited to individuals who are at 
least 40 years of age.

Summary of Argument 

Few employers admit to the discriminatory state of 
mind which is the ultimate issue in an employment dis- 
crimination case. The Court has therefore created a prima 
facie case which raises a presumption of employment 
discrimination, because it eliminates the most common 
reasons for adverse employer action. McDonnell Douglas 
v. Green, 411 U.S. 792 (1973) (Title VII). This inference of 
discrimination serves to call forth from the employer an 
articulation of its reasons for the adverse action. Some 
courts have, despite McDonnell Douglas, required in Title 
VII firing-and-replacement cases that employees show as 
part of the prima facie case that they were replaced by 
someone outside the protected class. The Fourth and 
Sixth Circuits, contrary to every other circuit, require a 
showing in ADEA cases that the replacement is outside 
the protected class, i.e., under age 40. These develop- 
ments are unwarranted. McDonnell Douglas did not 

require any showing of characteristics of the employee 
hired instead of the plaintiff, and an ADEA firing-and- 
replacement case is not distinguishable in terms of most 
common reasons for adverse employer action. 

The establishment of the prima facie case should 
logically not include a requirement —- discrimination in 
favor of a person too young to be protected by the ADEA 
- that would effectively create a new exception to the Act. 
The establishment of a prima facie case is only the first 
step in proving a violation of a particular statute. The 
ADEA prohibits discrimination “because of . . . age,” 29 
U.S.C. § 623(a)(1), not because of membership in a class 
of persons over 40 years of age. Thus a violation of the 
ADEA does not, unlike a violation of Title VII, turn on 
proof of a binary characteristic such as race or gender, but 
on age, which is a matter of degree. Replacement by a 
person either inside or outside the protected age group 
may therefore create an inference of age discrimination. 
The legislative history and the policy of the ADEA to 
protect all employees over age 40 also support this inter- 
pretation, while the Fourth Circuit rule is illogical: it 
allows a prima facie case to be made where a 40 year old 
is replaced by a 39 year old, but not when a 69 year old is 
replaced by that same 40 year old. 

Petitioner easily meets the prima face case require- 
ments of McDonnell Douglas. Adding any requirement of 
a showing of characteristics of his replacement will only 
encourage the sort of analysis undertaken by the Fourth 
Circuit, which intensively discussed the characteristics of 
Petitioner’s replacement and even the Respondent’s artic- 
ulated reasons for Petitioner’s discharge as part of its 
prima facie case analysis. This course of proceeding is at

be possible and often plausible. Only the very 
best workers are completely satisfactory, and 
they are not likely to be discriminated against - 
the cost of discrimination is too great. The law

For this reason, the task of the fact-finder in most age 
discrimination cases, as in other kinds of discrimination

evidence, including generalized expressions of animus (if 
the plaintiff is fortunate enough to be able to discover 
them), relative qualifications of the fired and the unfired, 
statistical evidence and the like, from which discrimina- 
tory motive may be inferred.

the inquiry into the question of intentional discrimina-

employer need do no more than produce “some evi-

for its decision; this is only a burden of production, not a 
burden of proof. Watson, 487 U.S. at 986. And, most 
important, the burden of proving discrimination rests at 
all times on the plaintiff, id.; once the employer's burden 
of production has been met, the presumption furnished 
by the prima facie case vanishes, and the question is 
whether, considering all the evidence, the plaintiff has

A prima facie case is a presumption, a subset of 
circumstantial evidence which alters, in favor of the 
plaintiff, the normal requirements of circumstantial proof. 
This is done for reasons of probability, Furnco, 438 U.S. at 
577, and from considerations of relative access to proof 
and the need to effectively implement the national anti- 
discrimination policy, see, McCormick on Evidence, § 343, 
vol. 2 at 454-455 (4th ed. 1992). These reasons are suffi- 
ciently similar in a replacement case and a failure to 
rehire case such as McDonnell Douglas that the same 
prima facie showing should obtain in each case. Any 
requirement that characteristics of the replacement be 
shown as part of the prima facie case will in effect abolish 
the presumptive character of the prima facie case, and 
leave discrimination complainants with the very burden 
of making out a traditional circumstantial evidence case 
that it was the purpose of the prima facie case to remove. 

It is not at all surprising that a prima facie case 
would not include any reference to the protected status of 
the replacement employee. After all, an employer may rid 
itself of a member of a class protected by law against 
discrimination, for reasons of age animus, only to dis- 
cover that, for various reasons, it cannot get a satisfactory 
replacement for the time being from outside the protected 
class; the discharged individual nevertheless has a cause 
of action for discrimination. To be sure, the fact that a 
new employee belongs to the same class as the eliminated 
employee would be probative in consideration of the 
employer’s contention that it lacked invidious motiva- 
tion. But the function of the prima facie case is not to 

17 

make the final determination, but simply to move the 
case to the next stage of analysis, where the employer 
must put forward its legitimate reasons for action. 

But even if the replacement employee’s nonmember- 
ship in the protected class were ordinarily a proper part 
of the McDonnell Douglas prima facie case in cases of race 
or gender discrimination, it would make no sense what- 
soever to import the same consideration into age discrim- 
ination cases. After all, age, unlike race or gender, is not a 
binary characteristic. If a plaintiff alleges that she was 
fired because she is female, her replacement must either 
be female or not female. If the allegation is of discrimina- 
tion against blacks, the replacement is either black or not. 
The same is not, however, true with respect to age dis- 
crimination, because age is a matter of degree. A 68 year 
old manager may be replaced by a 25 year old, a 39 year 
old, or a 45 year old - in each case the manager has been 
replaced with someone substantially younger. Assuming 
the two employees have similar qualifications, an infer- 
ence is created that the explanation for the different treat- 
ment is age. 

Of course, if the statute specified a binary charac- 
teristic, by forbidding discrimination on the basis of 
membership in the class of employees who are between 
40 and 70 years old, then such evidence of age discrimi- 
nation might not be sufficient to create a prima facie case. 
The language of the statute, however, does not permit 
such a construction. Section 4 of the ADEA, 29 U.S.C. 
§ 623(a), provides that

It shall be unlawful for an employer 

(1) to... discharge any individual . . . because 
of such individual’s age 

Section 12 of the ADEA, 29 U.S.C. § 631, entitled “Age 
limits,” provides 

(a) Individuals at least 40 years of age. 

The prohibitions in this chapter shall be limited 
to individuals who are at least 40 years of age. 

This language precludes the rule adopted by the 
courts below. What Section 623 makes unlawful is 
adverse treatment, including discharge, “because of such 
individual's age.” Under Section 623, such discrimination 
is prohibited regardless of the age of the person who 
benefits from the discrimination. The statute does make it 
relevant whether the individual claiming discrimination is 
over age 40, because under Section 631 only persons over 
age 40 may claim discrimination. But even Section 631 
does not purport to authorize discrimination in favor of 
persons at the younger end of the protected range of 
ages. 

The language of the ADEA supports Petitioner's 
position in yet another way. Section 4(f) of the statute, 29 
U.S.C. § 623(f), creates a number of exceptions to the 
general rule against discrimination on the basis of age. 
For example, an employer does not violate the ADEA by 
following a bona fide seniority system, 29 U.S.C. 
§ 623(f)(2)(A), or by making certain kinds of payments 
under an employee benefit or early retirement incentive 
plan that meets various specific requirements. 29 U.S.C. 
§§ 623(f)(2)(B), 623(i), 623(1). And the employer is 
expressly authorized by 29 U.S.C. § 631(c) to compel

retirement by employees over 65 who hold bona fide 
executive or high policy-making positions, so long as 
their retirement benefits have a minimum value. The very 
fact that Congress, when it saw a need for exceptions to 
the ADEA’s rule against discrimination, was perfectly 
capable of enacting statutory language in quite detailed 
terms, strongly suggests that it did not intend to have the 
courts craft an additional exception allowing discrimina- 
tion whenever the favored employee is within the same 
protected class as the discrimination complainant.

SUMMARY OF THE ARGUMENT 

The circuit court below held that in a discharge 
replacement case under the ADEA, the McDonnell Douglas 
model required the Petitioner to show that he was replaced 
by a person outside of the protected age group in order to 
raise a reliable inference of illegal age discrimination. This 
application of the McDonnell Douglas test was appropriate 
given the context of this case. A reality of life is that as 
older workers leave the work force and are replaced, the 
replacements almost always will be younger. Thus, no 
inference of age discrimination can be drawn from a mere 
chronological comparison between the ages of a plaintiff and 
a replacement. The circuit court therefore properly applied 
an objective test for determining when a difference in age 
alone can raise a minimal inference of age discrimination 
using the McDonnell Douglas model for a prima facie case.

Petitioner to this single proof scheme. Rather, it adopted a

C. Summary. 

The Fourth Circuit properly employed a flexible 
analytical model that required Petitioner’s prima facie case 
to contain at least some evidence from which one could infer 
that Respondent discriminated against him because of his 
age. That minimal evidence could have included evidence 
that Respondent retained an employee less than 40 years old 
in the job in question or any other circumstantial or direct 
evidence that Petitioner's age motivated the decision to 
terminate his employment. The mere fact, as Petitioner 
argued below, that someone younger was retained or, as 
Petitioner now argues, that Respondent continued to have a 
need for a position similar to the position Petitioner 
previously held is too nebulous to have created that sort of 
minimal inference required under this Court’s precedents.

"[T]here is no disparate treatment under the ADEA 
when the factor motivating the employer is some feature 
other than the employee’s age." Hazen Paper, 507 U.S. at 
___, 113 S. Ct. at 1705. Therefore, in order to establish a 
prima facie case under the ADEA, a claimant must introduce 
some evidence that raises an inference of a causal nexus

role any court must fulfill when evaluating whether an age 
discrimination claimant has minimally satisfied a prima facie 
case. It fully examined whether Petitioner had developed 
sufficient facts to sustain an inference of a causal connection 
between the claimant’s age and the adverse employment 

decision. The court determined that Petitioner's evidence
