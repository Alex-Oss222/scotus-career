INTERESTS OF AMICI CURIAE 


The American Association of Retire.’ Persons (AARP) 
is a nonprofit membership organization of persons age 5O and 
older that is dedicated to addressing the needs and interests of 
older Americans. AARP seeks through education, advocacy, 
and service to enhance the quality of life for all by promoting 
independence, dignity, and purpose. 


2 


More than one-third of AARP's thirty-two million 
members are employed individuals, most of whom are 
protected by the Age Discrimination in Employment Act, 29 
U.S.C. § 621 et seq., (ADEA). 

One of AARP's primary objectives is to strive to 
achieve dignity and equality in the work place through 
positive attitudes, practices, and policies regarding work and 
retirement. Through its research, publications, and training 
programs, AARP seeks to eliminate ageist stereotypes, to 
encourage employers to hire and retain older workers, and to 
help older workers overcome the obstacles they face because 
of age. 

AARP seeks to insure that older persons are judged on 
their ability, not age. To this end, AARP created a Worker 
Equity Initiative in 1985 to study and address issues affecting 
the employment of older persons. AARP has developed 
educational and advocacy programs to encourage and promote 
the employment of older persons. As part of its advocacy 
efforts, AARP has filed more than 100 amicus curiae briefs 
in the United States Supreme Court, federal appellate and 
district courts, and state courts regarding the proper 
interpretation and application of the federal and state 
discrimination laws to insure that the rights of older workers 
are fully protected. 

The National Employment Lawyers Association 
(NELA) is a national bar association of over 2,000 lawyers 
who regularly represent employees in employment-related 
disputes. NELA members comprise a large segment of the 
leaders of the bar specializing in employment law on behalf 
of employees. NELA members regularly handle age 
discrimination cases. 


3 


In light of its interest in the application of employment 
law, NELA has previously filed amicus curiae briefs with the 
United States Supreme Court, as well as the federal Circuit 
Courts of Appeals, and various state Supreme Courts. 

Because of its practical experience with the issues at 
bar, NELA is well-suited to brief this Court on the 
importance of the issues and the practical effects of the 
Court's decision beyond the immediate concerns of the 
parties. 

Both amici are vitally interested the proper 
formulation and application of the requirements for a prima 
facie case of age discrimination. 


SUMMARY OF ARGUMENT 


In the typical employment discrimination suit, the plaintiff 
is able to present circumstantial, but not direct evidence of 
discrimination. In the absence of direct evidence, the prima 
facie case functions as a framework for establishing a 
rebuttable presumption of discrimination, thus compelling a 
response from defendant. 

Because no single formulation of the prima facie showing 
can possibly be expected to capture the many guises in which 
such discrimination can occur, and because plaintiff's burden 
is light, this Court has repeatedly emphasized that one 
acceptable framework, its McDonnell Douglas model, is, of 
necessity, a flexible test, adaptable to different factual 
situations. Ignoring this principle, the court below rigidly 
applied an adaptation of McDonnell Douglas for ADEA 
discharge cases that requires as its fourth element a showing 
that the discharged employee was replaced by someone 
outside the protected age group. The court held that because 


4 


the 56 year old petitioner's replacement was 40 years old, just 
within the age group protected by the ADEA, petitioner could 
not, aS a matter of law, make the required prima facie 
showing and granted summary judgment for defendant. 

Finding that petitioner's evidence failed to satisfy even its 
relaxed prima facie standard applicable to reduction-in-force 
cases, the court below improperly relied on evidence relevant 
to respondent's rebuttal, while essentially ignoring the 
probative value of plaintiff's circumstantial evidence. 
Finally, because of its too rigid adherence to the formulaic 
approach, the court below essentially ignored petitioner's 
direct evidence of discriminatory intent.

A. Purpose Of The Prima Facie Case. 


Since in the typical employment discrimination case direct 
evidence may be unobtainable, this Court developed the 
McDonnell Douglas prima facie framework for evaluation of 
the available circumstantial evidence. The prima facie case 


is based on the notion that, by ruling out the more 
obvious job-related reasons for [the employer's 
decision, an employment discrimination] complainant 
can create an inference of some tainted reason, i.e., 
some discriminatory reason, sufficient to require the 


5 


employer to articulate a legitimate reason for [the 

employment decision. }" 

Loeb v. Textron, Inc., 600 F.2d 1003, 1013 (1st Cir. 1979). 
It is, therefore, the first step in "sharpen[ing] the inquiry into 
the elusive factual question of intentional discrimination." 
Texas Dept of Community Affairs v. Burdine, 450 U.S. 248, 
255 n. 8. (1981). 

In an ADEA case alleging discriminatory discharge, 
plaintiff's ultimate burden is to prove by a preponderance of 
the evidence that age was a motivating factor in the 
employer's decision to discharge him. Hazen Paper Co. v. 
Biggins, _ U.S. __, 113 S. Ct. 1701, 1706 (1993). 
Plaintiff's proof may consist of direct or circumstantial 
evidence. United States Postal Service Bd. of Governors v. 
Aikens, 460 U.S. 711, 714 n. 3 (1981). Direct evidence is 
not required because "it is often unavailable or difficult to 
find." Chipollini v. Spencer Gifts, Inc., 814 F.2d 893, 897 
(3d Cir.), cert. dismissed, 483 U.S. 1052 (1987). 
"Employers are rarely so cooperative as to include a notation 
in the personnel file, “fired due to age’; or to inform a 
dismissed employee candidly that he is too old for the job." 
Thornbrough v. Columbus and Greenville Railroad Co. , 760 
F.2d 633, 638 (Sth Cir. 1985). Therefore, most employment 
discrimination plaintiffs must rely on circumstantial evidence 
because direct evidence is unobtainable. “Even an employer 
who knowingly discriminates on the basis of age may leave 
no written records and may communicate it orally to no one." 
LaMontagne v. American Convenience Products, Inc., 750 
f.2d 1405, 1410 (7th Cir. 1984). Because "[t}here will seldom 
be ‘eyewitness’ testimony as to the employer's mental

“devised a framework that would allow both plaintiffs and the

Il. TO REQUIRE ADEA DISCHARGE

A. In Enacting The ADEA, Congress' Purpose 
Was To Prohibit Employers From Using 
Age As A _ Determining Factor In 
Employment Decisions. 


The purposes of the ADEA are set forth in the first section 
of the Act: 


to promote employment of older persons based on their 
ability rather than age; to prohibit arbitrary age 
discrimination in employment; to help employers and 
workers find ways of meeting problems arising from 
the impact of age on employment. 


29 U.S.C.. § 621(b). The statutory language adopted by 
Congress to implement this purpose is that work place 
discrimination against "any individual...because of such 
individual's age" is unlawful. 29 U.S.C. § 623. The statute 
says nothing about the age of others. Rather, in the 
circumstances of this case, it commands an examination of the 
employer's actions in order to determine if the individual 
employee's age was a motivating factor in the decision to 
discharge him. 

Although the text of the ADEA does not address the 
narrow question presented by this case, the legislative history 
of the Act speaks to it directly and forcefully. Senator Javits, 
one of the sponsors of the ADEA, stated: 


17 


Section 4 of the Bill [29 U.S.C. § 623] specifically 
prohibits discrimination against any "individual" 
because of his age. It does not say that the 
discrimination must be in favor of someone younger 
than age 40. In other words, if two individuals ages 
52 and 42 apply for the same job and the employer 
selected the man age 42 . . . because he is younger 
than the man 52, then he will have violated the act . . 


113 Cong. Rec. 31,255 (1967). Thus, it is clear that 
Congress intended to include within the coverage of the 
ADEA claims based on the relative ages of members of the 
protected class. "[T]he ADEA protects older employees from 
discrimination on the basis of age, not just discrimination in 
favor of persons under the age of forty." Roper v. Peabody 
Coal Co., 47 F.3d 925, 927 (7th Cir. 1995). 

A regulation promulgated by the Equal Employment 
Opportunity Commission expressly states that: 


It is unlawful in situations where this Act applies, for 
an employer to discriminate in hiring or in any other 
way by giving preference because of age between 
individuals 40 and over. Thus, if two people apply for 
the same position, and one is 42 and the other 52, the 
employer may not lawfully turn down either one on the 
basis of age, but must make such a decision on the 
basis of some other factor. 


29 C.F.R. § 1625.2(a). Because the EEOC is the primary 
agency charged with implementing the ADEA, its 
interpretation is entitled to great deference. Chevron, U.S.A., 


18 


Inc. v. Natural Resources Defense Council, 467 U.S. 837, 
844 (1984). 

There is not a scintilla of evidence in either the statute or 
its legislative history evincing an intention that an employer 
should be "insulated from liability for age discrimination 
when he chooses among people in the protected class." 
Mayall v. Peabody Coal Co., 7 F.3d 570, 572 (7th Cir. 
1993); see Kralman v. Illinois Dept. of Veterans’ Affairs, 23 
F.3d 150, 155 (7th Cir. 1994). The O'Connor decision, 
however, subverts the congressional purpose by turning the 
ADEA's shield against age discrimination for every older 
worker into a sword in the hands of employers to be used 
against many older workers who are discharged because of 
their age, through the simple artifice of hiring a replacement 
who is at least age 40. 


B. Requiring Plaintiffs To Prove Replacement By 
Someone Under Age 40 Essentially Eviscerates 
The Law's Protections For Workers In The 
Upper Ages Of The Protected Class. 


The practical consequences of dismissing age 
discrimination claims when a replacement is within the 
protected class cannot be ignored. "If no intra-age group 
protection were provided by the ADEA, it would be of 
virtually no use to persons at the upper ages of the protected 
class whose jobs require experience since even an employer 
with clear anti-age animus would rarely replace them with 
someone under 40." Maxfield v. Sinclair Intern., 766 F.2d 
788, 792 (3d Cir. 1985) Moreover, "a requirement that the 
replacement be from a nonprotected group fails to take the

ieality of the working place into account." Carter v. City of 
Miami, 870 F.2d 578, 583 (11th Cir. 1989). That reality is: 


Seldom will a sixty-year-old be replaced by a person 

in the twenties. Rather the sixty-year-old will be 

replaced by a fifty-five-year-old, who, in turn, is 
succeeded by a person in the forties, who will be 
replaced by a younger person. Eventually a person 
outside the class will be elevated but rarely to the 
position of the one fired. This is especially true in 
management and technical fields where knowledge and 
experience, the product of years, are necessary 
prerequisites to appointment of persons on high rungs 
of the corporate ladder. 

McCorstin v. United States Steel Corp. , 621 F.2d 749, 754 

(Sth Cir. 1980). 

In the work place, the most common type of age 
discrimination claim is one brought by a 57-year old who is 
terminated.” Indeed, more than seventy-five percent of all 
age discrimination claims are discharge claims.“ The 
requirement of showing replacement by one outside the 
protected class would, therefore, effectively deny a remedy to 
those most frequently victimized by age discrimination. 
Further, as the population continues to age, the need to

Although age is a “continuum along which the distinctions

The civil rights laws protect the right of the individual 
employee to be free of invidious employment discrimination. 
These statutes do not permit employers to compromise this 
individual right by the non-discriminatory treatment of other 
employees in the protected class. Connecticut v. Teal, 457 
U.S. 440 (1982). In the case of the ADEA, "Congress never 
intended favorable treatment of some members of a plaintiffs’ 
age group to excuse discrimination against others." Airline 
Pilots Ass'n v. Trans World Airlines, 713 F.2d 940, 953 (2d 
Cir. 1983), aff'd on other grounds sub. nom. Trans World 
Airlines, Inc. v. Thurston, 474 U.S. 946 (1985). Thus, the 
employer's replacement of a terminated 56 year old employee 
with another employee who is 40 years old, as the Fourth 
Circuit found was done in this case, does not preclude the 
possibility that the termination of the 56 year old was based 
on age. The Fourth Circuit's contrary holding smacks of the 
“bottom line" analysis emphatically rejected by this Court in 
Teal, 457 U.S. at 454.
