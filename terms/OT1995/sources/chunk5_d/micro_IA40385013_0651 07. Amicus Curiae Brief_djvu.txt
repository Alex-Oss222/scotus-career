INTERESTS OF AMICUS CURIAE 


The National Retail Federation (NRF) is the world’s 
largest trade association. Its membership includes the 
leading department, specialty, discount, mass merchan- 
dise, and independent stores, as well as 32 national and 
50 state associations. The NRF’s members represent an 
industry that encompasses over 1.4 million retail estab- 
lishments in the United States, employs nearly 20 million 
people (one in five American workers), and registered 
sales of more than $2.2 trillion in 1994. 


The parties have consented to the filing of this brief. 


Issues arising under the Age Discrimination in 
Employment Act, particularly when a reorganization, 
consolidation, or elimination of positions is involved, are 
of keen interest to the employing retailers because of the 
changes taking place within the retail industry. During 
the last several years various major retail and department 
store chains have reorganized, merged with one another, 
and eliminated duplicate or unprofitable facilities. The 
retailers’ elimination of duplicate management structure, 
redundant staff, and facilities have led to complicated 
decisions involving personnel and employment actions. 
These changes and reorganizations have prompted and 
will continue to prompt, as the case below, claims of 
discrimination under the ADEA. 


Additional concerns arise when it is asserted, as here 
by Petitioner, that an automatic presumption of illegal 
age discrimination arises simply because a “qualified” 
protected age worker is terminated and afterwards the 


work is performed by a younger worker. It is the experi- 
ence of many retailers, when hiring to fill vacancies in 


full-time sales and first level managerial positions in 
most areas of the country, that the applicant pool will be 
heavily populated by younger workers. Further, in 
assessing the qualifications and abilities of either 
employees or applicants, it is the experience of many 
retailers in today’s environment that the younger person, 
due to educational and cultural differences, is much more 
likely to be literate and experienced in using computers 
to retrieve information. Such a worker gains quick access 
to extensive information systems data on inventory 
levels, pricing, cost, availability of merchandise, ship- 
ment dates, re-order information, etc., which enhances 
efficient and effective job performance in the retail indus- 


try. 


SUMMARY OF ARGUMENT 


In determining whether an employer was entitled to 
summary judgment in an ADEA disparate treatment dis- 
charge case, the Fourth Circuit properly used the tradi- 
tional standards of proof applied in civil cases and 
focused upon the issue of unlawful motivation. In an 
ADEA case, as this Court has held, plaintiff's claim 
depends on whether age actually motivated the 
employer's decision. 


Petitioner claims he established a prima facie case 
under this Court’s McDonnell Douglas formula and that 
ADEA claimants in establishing a prima facie case should 
be relieved of the burden of making out a traditional 
circumstantial evidence case. However, strict use of the 
McDonnell Douglas formula in ADEA discharge cases is 


inappropriate. A prima facie case of age discrimination is 
not established if plaintiff only shows that he was within 
the protected age group, that he had been performing his 
job for some time, and that his employment was termi- 
nated. 


If the McDonnell Douglas framework is to be used, it 
must be appropriately modified to focus on whether 
plaintiff possesses sufficient evidence of disparate treat- 
ment of similarly-situated individuals on the basis of age 
to give rise to an inference of unlawful motivation or to 
show that the employer did not treat age neutrally in 
making personnel decisions. By appropriate modification 
to two parts of the original McDonnell Douglas frame- 
work, assessment of the discharged ADEA plaintiff's 
prima facie case can be squared with traditional analysis of 
proof in civil cases generally.

ARGUMENT 


Petitioner (“O’Connor”) brought suit claiming he was 
terminated by Respondent (“Consolidated”) in violation 
of the Age Discrimination in Employment Act (ADEA), 
and requested a jury trial. (App. to Petition, p. 23). Con- 
solidated moved for summary judgment because no gen- 
uine issues of fact existed and Consolidated was entitled 
to judgment as a matter of law. Granting Consolidated’s 
motion, the District Court and Fourth Circuit found that 
O’Connor “failed to present any evidence that Consoli- 
dated did not treat age neutrally,” and “O’Connor failed 
to establish . . . by direct and/or circumstantial evidence 
that he was terminated because of his age.” O'Connor v. 


Consolidated Coin Caterers Corp., 56 F.3d 542, 545, 546-49 
(4th Cir. 1995). 


In reviewing the decisions below, this Court has an 
important opportunity to clarify (1) the applicability of 
the standards of proof traditionally applied in civil cases 
to cases brought under the ADEA and (2) the appli- 
cability of the McDonnell Douglas framework to employ- 
ment termination cases brought under the ADEA. Such 
clarification is critically important to employers in all 
industries, including the retail industry. 


First, this Court should recognize that regardless of 
how the evidence in an ADEA case may be marshalled, 
an ADEA plaintiff, to survive summary judgment, must 
always come forward with sufficient evidence that a rea- 
sonable jury could infer that plaintiff's age actually moti- 
vated the employer's decision. As more fully explained 
below, this requirement is consistent with the traditional 
standards of proof in civil cases and should be applicable 
in all civil cases, including ADEA cases. 


Second, this Court should clarify (1) whether the 
McDonnell Douglas framework applies at all in an ADEA 
discharge case, and (2) if it applies, how two key parts 
should be formulated. The NRF respectfully suggests that 
the McDonnell Douglas framework, even as modified by 
some courts, should not apply to ADEA discharge cases 
as they are significantly different from other employment 
discrimination cases. On the other hand, if this Court 
decides that the framework should apply, then the two 
key parts must be revised for ADEA discharge cases. 


Finally, this Court should affirm the Fourth Circuit's 
decision because O’Connor failed to come forward with 


evidence from which a reasonable jury could conclude 
age was a motivating factor in Consolidated’s decision to 
terminate him. 


I. IN ASSESSING WHETHER PLAINTIFF ESTAB- 
LISHED A PRIMA FACIE CASE OF AGE DISCRIM- 
INATION, THE FOURTH CIRCUIT PROPERLY 
FOCUSED ON THE ISSUE OF UNLAWFUL 
MOTIVATION, USING TRADITIONAL STAN- 
DARDS OF PROOF IN CIVIL CASES. 


To survive summary judgment, an ADEA plaintiff 
must present sufficient evidence that a reasonable jury 
could infer that age actually motivated the employer's 
decision. This requirement is consistent with the tradi- 
tional standards of proof in civil cases and should be 
applicable in ADEA cases. 


The application of this principal to ADEA cases is not 
new. In this case, the Fourth Circuit observed that courts 
must apply the “ordinary standards of proof used in civil 
cases” to assess whether a plaintiff is entitled to submit 
his case to the jury. 56 F.3d at 548. Other circuits also 
recognize that “a court considering summary judgment 
must decide whether plaintiff's facts, if believed, would 
prove that, more likely than not, the employer fired the 
employee because of his age.” Bodenheimer v. PPG Indus., 
Inc., 5 F.3d 955, 959 n.8 (5th Cir. 1993). Indeed, “[t}he 
standard for summary judgment mirrors that for directed 
verdict, so that if on any part of the prima facie case there 
would be insufficient evidence to require submission of 
the case to a jury, [the court] must affirm the grant of 
summary judgment.” Barnes v. Southwest Forest Indus., 
Inc., 814 F.2d 607, 609 (11th Cir. 1987); see Leichihman v. 


Pickwick Intern., 814 F.2d 1263, 1269 (8th Cir. 1987), cert. 
denied, 484 U.S. 858 (1987) (“In choosing an appropriate 
framework for a prima facie case in a particular factual 
context, it must be remembered that the ultimate issue in 
a disparate treatment case is whether the employer inten- 
tionally discriminated against the plaintiff”); LaGrant v. 
Gulf & W. Mfg. Co., 748 F.2d 1087, 1090 (6th Cir. 1984) (“A 
prima facie case is one where there is sufficient evidence to 
withstand a motion for summary judgment or for a 
directed verdict”); Hedrick v. Hercules, Inc., 658 F.2d 1088, 
1094 (5th Cir. 1981) (“plaintiff in a reduction-in-force 
ADEA case could establish a prima facie case by . . . pro- 
ducing evidence, circumstantial or direct, from which a 
fact-finder might reasonably conclude that the employer 
intended to discriminate”); see also Carter v. Duncan- 
Huggins, Ltd., 727 F.2d 1225, 1245 (D.C. Cir. 1984) (Scalia, 
J., dissenting) (“[I]n order to get to the jury the plaintiff 
would still have to introduce some evidence that would 
enable a reasonable person to conclude that the bas s for 
this discriminatory treatment was race.”). 


As this Court has recognized, liability in a disparate 
treatment case “depends on whether the protected trait 
(under the ADEA, age) actually motivated the employer's 
decision.” Hazen Paper Co. v. Biggins, 113 S. Ct. 1701, 1706 
(1993). Thus, the critical, essential element of plaintiff's 
ADEA case is illegal motivation, and “there is no dispa- 
rate treatment when the factor motivating the employer is 
some feature other than the employee's age.” Id. at 1705. 


While a plaintiff's necessary proof may be based on 
direct evidence of discriminatory motive, plaintiffs in 
employment discrimination cases generally rely on - 


indirect evidence from which an inference of 
discriminatory motive may be drawn, namely, 
comparative evidence demonstrating that the 
treatment of the plaintiff differs from that 
accorded to otherwise ‘similarly situated’ indi- 
viduals who are not within the plaintiff's pro- 
tected group. 


D. Shlei & P. Grossman, EMPLOYMENT DISCRIMINA- 
TION LAW, Chap. 36, “PROOF,” p. 1291 (2d ed. 1983). 
Accordingly, when assessing whether the ADEA plaintiff 
established a prima facie case of discriminatory motive by 
relying on circumstantial evidence, the courts must focus 
on the relative ages of the individuals who are favored 
and adversely affected by the personnel decisions as well 
as the demonstrated qualifications and job performance 
of the individuals involved. 


The Fourth Circuit, in this case. properly recognized 
that “[i]n order to establish his claim, O’Connor may rely 
on the normal methods of succeeding on a civil suit by 
establishing by a preponderance of the evidence that but 
for Consolidated’s motive to discriminate against him 
because of his age, he would not have been discharged.” 
56 F.3d at 545. In finding against O'Connor and entering 
summary judgment, the Fourth Circuit focused on all the 
various facts, including the relative ages of the individ- 
uals involved and the comparative background, experi- 
ence, and job performance of O’Connor and those who 
remained at work. After properly assessing the evidence 
under ordinary standards of proof (as set forth above), 
the Fourth Circuit properly affirmed the District Court's 
entry of summary judgment in favor of Consolidated.

Il. THE MCDONNELL DOUGLAS FORMULA, EVEN 
AS MODIFIED BY CERTAIN COURTS, DOES NOT 
PROVIDE AN ACCEPTABLE ALTERNATIVE 
PRIMA FACIE CASE TEST IN ADEA DISCHARGE 
CASES. 


O’Connor claims he established a prima facie case 
under the framework which this Court used in McDonnell 
Douglas v. Green, 411 U.S. 792, 802 (1973), as modified by 
the courts in an ADEA discharge case. O’Connor claims 
ADEA complainants should be relieved from the “burden 
of making out a traditional circumstantial evidence case.” 
(Pet. Br., p. 6). Thus, O’Connor raises questions of first 
impression concerning the role that McDonnell Douglas 
plays in assessing whether or when a plaintiff makes a 
prima facie ADEA case and whether this Court intended 
that the McDonnell Douglas framework impose a lesser 
standard in ADEA jury cases when compared to the 
traditional standards of proof in civil jury cases. The NRF 
respectfully submits that the original McDonnell Douglas 
framework should not be applied to ADEA discharge 
cases; instead, the traditional standards of proof for civil 
jury cases should apply. 


When this Court first described the burden-shifting 
formula in McDonnell Douglas, it cautioned: “The facts 
necessarily will vary in Title VII cases, and the specifica- 
tion above of the prima facie proof requirement . . . is not 
necessarily applicable in every respect to differing factual 
situations.” 411 U.S. at 802 n.13. Four years later, this 
Court emphasized that: 


The importance of McDonnell Douglas lies, not in 
its specification of the discrete elements of proof 


there required, but in its recognition of the gen- 
eral principles that any Title VII plaintiff must 
carry the initial burden of offering evidence ade- 
quate to create an inference that an employment 
decision was based upon a discriminatory crite- 
ria illegal under the Act. 


International Bro. of Teamsters v. United States, 431 U.S. 324, 
358 (1977). 


NRF respectfully submits that the McDonnell Douglas 
formula was not initially intended to determine whether 
plaintiff made a prima facie case for a jury; nor was it 
intended to shift the focus, when applying standards 
used generally in assessing proof in civil cases, away 
from the essential elements necessary to establish dis- 
criminatory motivation. From the beginning, the Sixth 
Circuit recognized that age discrimination jury cases 
were different and it would be inappropriate to simply 
borrow and automatically apply the guidelines estab- 
lished in McDonnell Douglas. Laugesen v. Anaconda Co., 510 
F.2d 307, 312-13 (6th Cir. 1975).! The Eighth Circuit also

As we show below, modification of the McDonnell 
Douglas framework in ADEA discharge cases has caused 
much confusion and conflict in the circuits. Whether the 
modified McDonnell Douglas framework offers ADEA 
plaintiffs a lesser burden than traditional circumstantial 
evidence analysis depends on whether the modification 
focuses on comparative treatment evidence. 


The problem of applying the McDonnell Douglas 
framework to ADEA cases is best demonstrated by the 
confusing and conflicting results in the same types of 
case in different circuits and sometimes even within the 
same circuit. The Seventh Circuit has observed this very 
problem within its circuit:

somewhat confusing variations of the elements

Finally, and most important, in some cases, the

was a determining factor in the employer’s 
motivation.” 


Oxman, 846 F.2d at 453 (emphasis added). 


This confusion is readily apparent in ADEA employ- 
ment termination cases. The McDonnell Douglas frame- 
work was designed to require a job applicant, as a part of 
his prima facie case to “demonstrate at least that his rejec- 
tion did not result from the two most common legitimate 
reasons on which an employer might rely to reject a job 
applicant: an absolute or relative lack of qualifications or 
the absence of a vacancy in the job sought.” Teamsters, 431 
U.S. at 358 n.44. In the employment termination case 
crafting a revised McDonnell Douglas framework is much 
more complicated. An employee may be terminated, for 
example, because of unsatisfactory job performance, vio- 
lation of a work rule regulating conduct, the elimination 
of his job, a more general reduction in force affecting a 
number of the same or similar jobs or the consolidation of 
jobs. Such complexities have caused the various modifica- 
tions of McDonnell Douglas to move away from the focus 
on the most likely reason for termination to a focus on 
comparative treatment of similarly-situated individuals. 
However, this more refined approach, which moves 
closer to the traditional analysis, has resulted in different 
formulations of the two elements that are frequently 
contested: (1) whether the protected age plaintiff was

A. Application of the Third Part of the McDonnell 
Douglas Framework to ADEA Cases Has 
Resulted in Conflicting and Confusing Opin-

element, most circuits have different requirements for 
ADEA discharge cases. The Fourth, Eighth, Ninth, and 
Tenth Circuits require the ADEA plaintiff to show that at 
the time of discharge he was performing his job at a level 
that met his employer's legitimate expectations or at least 
that he was doing satisfactory work. E.g., Mitchell v. Data 
General Corp., 12 F.3d 1310, 1315 (4th Cir. 1993); Leichih- 
man, 814 F.2d at 1268; Wallis v. ]. R. Simplot Co., 26 F.3d 
885, 891 (9th Cir. 1994); Lucas, 857 F.2d at 1400. The 
Fourth Circuit below, as detailed above, and the Ninth

prong similar to the original McDonnell Douglas fourth 
prong:

age discrimination under the disparate treat- 
ment theory by showing he: (1) was a member 
of the protected class . . . ; (2) was performing 
his job in a satisfactory manner; (3) was dis-

emphasizes that even though the employee performed 
satisfactorily in the past, the employee must show that he 
was meeting the expectations of the employer at the time 
of termination. Karazanas v. Navistar Intern., 948 F.2d 332, 
336 (7th Cir. 1991). The First Circuit employs the term 
“qualified,” but in the sense that plaintiff “was doing his 
job well enough to rule out the possibility he was fired 
for inadequate job performance, absolutely or relative.” 
Loeb v. Textron, Inc., 600 F.2d 1003, 1013 (Ist Cir. 1979). 


On the other hand, in the Second, Third, Fifth, and 
Sixth Circuits, the courts have strictly adhered to the 
McDonnell Douglas formula and require only that the 
discharged plaintiff show he was “qualified for the posi- 
tion.” E.g., Pena v. Brattleboro Retreat, 702 F.2d 322, 324 
(2nd Cir. 1983); Chipollini v. Spencer Gifts, Inc., 814 F.2d 
893, 897 (3rd Cir.), cert. dismissed, 483 U.S. 1052 (1987); 
Moore v. Eli Lilly & Co., 990 F.2d 812, 815 (5th Cir. 1993); 
Ackerman v. Diamond Shamrock Corp., 670 F.2d 66, 69 (6th 
Cir. 1982).

pletely eliminates the requirement that plaintiff meet 
even the limited “qualified” prong when the plaintiff has 
been in the position from which he was discharged for 
some time. See Pace v. Southern Ry. System, 701 F.2d 1383, 
1386 (11th Cir.), cert. denied, 464 U.S. 1018 (1983); Rosen- 
field v. Wellington Leisure Prods., Inc., 827 F.2d 1493, 1495

n.2 (11th Cir. 1987) (“The reason for this modification is

significant period of time, qualification for that position 
sufficient to satisfy the test of a prima facie case can be 
inferred.”). Seemingly, not much at all is required if this 
modification is used. 


Because the McDonnell Douglas formula is not easily 
adapted to the ADEA discharge cases, it should not be 
applied in those cases to displace traditional standards of 
proof. Assuming, however, that the McDonnell Douglas 
framework is applicable to ADEA discharge cases, this 
Court's resolution of the conflict over the formulation of 
the “qualified” or “satisfactory performance” element is 
critical in assessing the result below and proof in future 
ADEA cases. It is difficult to imagine that the focus of a 
court's inquiry regarding this third element would be on 
anything but the plaintiff's “performance” at the time of 
the adverse personnel action. The NRF urges this Court 
to adopt that focus. 


Applying that focus to this case, the Fourth Circuit 
assessed that “O’Connor was not performing well in 
August of 1990” when he was terminated even though he 
had been in the job for some time. 56 F.3d at 547. Thus, 
the Fourth Circuit’s assessment of the evidence under its 
modified McDonnell Douglas ADEA formula produces the 
same result as the Fourth Circuit’s use of standard 
methods of assessing proof of illegal motivation. The 
NRF respectfully suggests that this Court clarify whether 
the McDonnell Douglas framework applies to ADEA cases, 
and if it does, the NRF urges this Court to focus part of 
the framework on plaintiff's performance at the time the 
discharge decision is made.

B. A Rigid Application of the Fourth Part of the 
McDonnell Douglas Framework (“Still Search- 
ing”) to ADEA Discharge Cases Has Been 
Largely Rejected. 

The Circuit Courts in ADEA discharge cases have 
generally rejected the “continues to search” fourth prong 
of McDonnell Douglas in favor of comparative treatment 
analysis. While such a step moves the modified McDon- 
nell Douglas framework closer to the traditional analysis 
of proof of motivation, there is confusion regarding the 
weight that should be given to the relative ages of 
allegedly similarly-situated individuals.* 


Although not argued below, both Petitioner and 
Amicus Equal Employment Opportunity Commission 
argue that there should be no focus upon the comparative 
characteristics of the plaintiff and other individuals 
selected by the employer to perform the work previously 
performed by plaintiff. (Pet. Br., p. 16). However, if a 
“real” prima facie case is to result, then comparative treat- 
ment of similarly-situated individuals is critical. For this 
reason all of the circuits have properly rejected such a 
limited formula because in effect, in the ADEA discharge 
case, it merely requires the plaintiff to show that he or 
she was in the protected age group and was then termi- 
nated. 


% The modified “fourth prong” has also been used in Title 
VII race discrimination discharge cases by the lower courts. See 
St. Mary's Honor Ctr. v. Hicks, 113 S. Ct. 2742, 2747 (1993) (citing 
the lower court decision there which characterized the fourth 
prong: “that the position remained open and was ultimately 
filled by a white man”).

Focusing on the comparative ages of the involved 
individuals has also raised questions. The Sixth Circuit 
has observed: 


The processing of age is a universal human pro- 
cess. In the very nature of the problem, it is 
apparent that in the usual case, absent any dis- 
criminatory intent, discharged employees will 
more often than not be replaced by those youn- 
ger than they, for older employees are con- 
stantly moving out of the labor market, while 
younger ones move in. This factor of progres- 
sion replacement is not necessarily involved in 
cases involving the immutable characteristics of 
race, sex and national origin. 


510 F.2d at 312, n.4. The Eighth Circuit has rejected an 
“automatic presumption” of discrimination when a youn- 
ger worker replaces an older worker. Holley v. Sanyo Mfg., 
Inc., 771 F.2d 1161, 1167 (8th Cir. 1985) (“the fact alone 
that Holley’s duties were assumed by a younger person - 
the fact of an age differential — itself is insufficient addi- 
tional evidence to establish a prima facie case”); Leichih- 
man, 814 F.2d at 1270 (“In Holley, we agreed with the Sixth 
Circuit that the ADEA does not create “automatic pre- 
sumption” against an employer whenever an older 
worker is replaced by a younger one.”).4 


* Where there is a delay in selecting a replacement, the fact 
the replacement is younger may not create a prima facie case and

The Seventh Circuit has recognized that: 


It is not a violation of the ADEA to replace an 
employee in the protected class with a younger 
person, as long as the protected employee is not 
replaced because of his age. Because younger 
people often succeed to the jobs of other people 
for perfectly legitimate reasons, the mere fact 
that an older employee is replaced by a younger 
one does not permit an inference that the 
replacement was motivated by age discrimina- 
tion. 


La Montagne v. American Convenience Prods., Inc., 750 F.2d 
1405, 1413 (7th Cir. 1984). Similarly, the Fifth Circuit 
looked to cases from other circuits and concluded: 


We agree with the tenor of these cases in so far 
as they establish that replacement by one within 
the protected category will not preclude proof of 
a prima facie case. However, in no case does the 
court hold that as a matter of law a prima facie 
case is established if a plaintiff simply shows he 
is in the protected group, was adversely affected 
by an employment decision, was qualified, and 
was replaced by one younger than himself. In 
each of these cases not only was the plaintiff 
replaced by someone younger but there also was 
other evidence of discrimination based on age. 


Pace v. Southern Ry. System, 701 F.2d 1383, 1390 (5th Cir. 
1983). 


In reduction-in-force cases where there is no replace- 
ment (as plaintiff's job is simply eliminated), the Courts 
have required plaintiffs to satisfy the fourth prong by 
other evidence from which an inference of discriminatory 
intent can be drawn. E.g., Bashara v. Black Hills Corp., 26

F.3d 820, 823 (8th Cir. 1994) (to establish a prima facie 
case in a reduction-of-force situation, the plaintiff must 
meet the first three requirements and “ ‘must come for- 
ward with additional . . . evidence that age was a factor in 
his termination.’ ”); LeBlanc v. Great American Ins. Co., 6 
F.3d 836, 842 (1st Cir. 1993) (in a reduction in force case to 
satisfy element (4), the plaintiff may demonstrate either 
that the employer did not treat age neutrally or that 
younger persons were retained in the same position); 
Branson v. Price River Coal Co., 853 F.2d 768, 771 (10th Cir. 
1988) (in reduction-in-force cases, courts have modified 
the fourth prima facie element by requiring the plaintiff to 
produce evidence, circumstantial or direct, from which a 
fact-finder might reasonably conclude that the employer 
intended to discriminate in reaching the decision at 
issue); EEOC v. Western Electric Co., 713 F.2d 1011, 1014 
(4th Cir. 1983) (the fourth element in a reduction-in-force 
modified formula is usually characterized by stating that 
a plaintiff must produce “some other evidence that the 
employer did not treat age neutrally”). 


If some modified McDonnell Douglas framework is 
applicable to ADEA cases, this Court's clarification of the 
showing an ADEA plaintiff must make under the fourth 
part of the test is crucial to the result below and future 
ADEA cases. Simply requiring an ADEA plaintiff to dem- 
onstrate that he was replaced by someone younger (per- 
haps only six months younger) hardly demonstrates age 
animus on the part of an employer. On the other hand, 
requiring plaintiff to show that he was replaced by an 
individual outside the protected class creates a more 
direct tie to the possibility that age may have been a 
determining factor in the employment decision.

At least, any modified McDonnell Douglas fourth 
prong should focus in all ADEA discharge cases upon a 
formulation borrowed from the reduction-in-force cases 
where comparative treatment evidence was necessarily 
limited. Accordingly, an ADEA protected group plaintiff 
could meet this fourth prong by producing other evi- 
dence from which it can be reasonably inferred that the 
employer intended to discriminate against the plaintiff 
because of his age or did not treat age neutrally in its 
decision-making process. This general requirement is nec- 
essary because, as the Eighth Circuit has observed: 


A prima facie case, although a minimal showing, 
at least requires the plaintiff to show that the 
employer's actions were more likely than not 
based on a consideration of impermissible fac- 
tors. 


Leichihman, 814 F.2d at 1270. Such a modification of the 
fourth prong squares the McDonnell Douglas framework 
with traditional analysis of proof in civil cases. If this 
fourth prong test is adopted, the Fourth Circuit's decision 
should be affirmed, as the courts below found that 
O’Connor failed to supply the necessary evidence.
