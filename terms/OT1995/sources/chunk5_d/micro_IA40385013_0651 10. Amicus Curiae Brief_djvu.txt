STATEMENT OF THE CASE 


The plaintiff, James O’Connor, brought this law- 
suit pursuant to the Age Discrimination in Employ- 
ment Act of 1967 (ADEA), 29 U.S.C. 58 621-634. 
He contends that he was terminated because of his 
age by his employer, Consolidated Coin Caterers 
Corp., (Consolidated), which is a division of Canteen 
Corporation (Canteen). The district court granted 


4 


summary judgment in favor of Consolidated because 
O'Connor had failed to establish a prima facie case. 
The district court’s ruling was affirmed by the 
Fourth Circuit. 


Consolidated operates cafeterias and vending ma- 
chines for industrial plants, businesses, schools and 
health care facilities. (Pet. App. at 2-3).' O'Connor 
was the supervisor of one of Consolidated’s regional 
operations. In 1990, Canteen restructured its opera- 
tions and changed the makeup of its operating terri- 
tories by combining them into larger territories. 
(Pet. App. at 3-6). 


As a result of this reorganization, two supervisors 
were adversely affected. O’Connor (age 56) was dis- 
charged; and Allen Hunter (age 57) was reassigned. 
Canteen chose Ted Finnel (age 40) and Mike Kiser 
(age 35) to manage the newly-expanded districts. 
(Pet. App. at 5). According to Canteen, Finnel and 
Kiser were selected to manage the new districts be- 
cause there were substantially greater management 
responsibilities than had been required under the 
previous organizations and the new districts were 
substantially larger in geographic area.” (Pet. 
App. 5). 


Finnel and Kiser were selected because their 
former direct supervisor “had first-hand knowledge 
of both men’s work and abilities, and considered them 
competent to handle the greater responsibilities and 
larger geographic territory.“ (Id.) Consolidated did 
not select plaintiff O’Connor because he was slow in 
responding to problem accounts in his old territory, 
and the new territories “served a greater number of 


„Pet. App.” references are to the appendix to O’Connor’s 
Petition for Writ of Certiorari. 


5 


customers, entailed more accounts and covered a sub- 
stantially larger geographic territory.” (Id. at 5-6). 
“Given that O’Connor was slow with his already re- 
duced territory [i.e., from six to three geographic 
units], Consolidated concluded that he could not 
handle an even larger district.” (Jd. at 6). 


O'Connor asserted instead that he was discharged 
because of his age. The district court, however, 
granted Consolidated’s motion for summary judg- 
ment, and that ruling was affirmed by the Fourth 
Circuit. The Fourth Circuit stated that in order to 
succeed, the plaintiff has to prove by a preponderance 
of the evidence that “but for Consolidated’s motive 
to discriminate against him because of his age, he 
would not have been discharged.” (Pet. App. at 8). 


Because O’Connor lacked direct proof of age dis- 
crimination, he attempted to use the indirect method 
to establish a prima facie case. In this regard, the 
Fourth Circuit used the following test patterned 
after McDonnell Douglas Corp. v. Green, 411 U.S. 
792 (1973), but modified for age discrimination 
cases. Under this test, the plaintiff can establish a 
prima facie case if he can prove: 


(1) the employee was protected by the ADEA; 
(2) he was selected for discharge from a larger 
group of candidates; (3) he was performing at 
a level substantially equivalent to the lowest level 
of those of the group retained; and (4) the proc- 
ess of selection produced a residual workforce 
containing some unprotected persons wio were 
performing at a level lower than that at which 
he was performing. 


(Pet. App. at 9-11 (emphasis added) ). 


Although O’Connor met the first two standards, 
the Fourth Circuit held that he did not meet the 


6 


requirements of the third prong applicable to age 
discrimination termination/replacement and reduc- 
tion-in-force cases: 


The third element requires that O’Connor per- 
form at a level substantially equivalent to the 
lowest level of those of the group retained. Es- 
sentially, this element demands that O’Connor 
perform his job satisfactorily, thereby meeting 
Consolidated’s legitimate expectations. Accord- 
ing to Consolidated, O’Connor cannot satisfy this 
element because even prior to the reorganization 
that gave rise to the new district manager posi- 
tions, O’Connor was slow in dealing with prob- 
lem accounts, his territorial responsibilities had 
already been greatly reduced, and he experienced 
a problem with food delivery in an unrefrig- 
erated truck. 


(Pet. App. at 11-12). 


The court stated that O’Connor had failed to prove 
that Consolidated did not treat age neutrally in dis- 
charging him, especially because O’Connor was not 
comparable to Kiser or Finnel who had performed 
their jobs as expected, whereas O’Connor’s territory 
was reduced prior to his termination due to his slow 
response to problem accounts. (Pet. App. at 13-14). 
Thus, the decision to discharge O’Connor was not 
based on age discrimination, “but rather on the age- 
neutral reason that O’Connor’s performance did not 
recommend him for the job, while Finnel and Kiser’s 
did.” (Pet. App. at 14). 


The Fourth Circuit then stated that the fourth 
prong of the prima facie test required O’Connor to 
prove that “persons outside the protected class [i.e., 
persons age-39 or less] were retained in the same 
position.” (Pet. App. at 13). This ruling has been 


7 


seized upon by the plaintiffs in framing the question 
to be decided by the Court. But, the Fourth Circuit 
also noted that O’Connor could not meet the third 
prong of the requirements for a prima facie case, i.e., 
he did not show that “he was performing a level 
substantially equivalent to the lowest level of those 
of the group retained.” (Pet. App. at 11). There- 
fore, the presence or absence of a comparator em- 
ployee outside the age group protected by the ADEA 
is not as important a factor as O’Connor argues here, 
since he lacked the relative qualifications to allow 
him to establish a prima facie case in any event. 


Thus, the amicus curiae will address the Fourth 
Circuit’s ruling that in establishing a prima facie 
case, the plaintiff must meet the burden of proving 
that his performance level was substantially equiva- 
lent to that of the employees who were retained. 


SUMMARY OF ARGUMENT 


The plaintiff argues to this Court that he should 
be relieved of the prima facie burden of proving that 
he was performing at a level substantially equivalent 
to those who were retained during a corporate re- 
structuring. The Fourth Circuit clearly was correct 
in rejecting that position. 

The central purpose of the McDonnell Douglas 
standards is to eliminate one of the most common 
nondiscriminatory reasons for the discharge of an 
individual in a protected class: “the absolute or rel- 
ative lack of qualifications” for the job. Int’l Bhd. 
of Teamsters v. United States, 431 U.S. 324, 358 
n.44 (1977). To establish a prima facie case, the 
plaintiff, at minimum, should have to prove that he 
“was performing to his employer’s legitimate expec- 
tations.” Collier v. Budd Co., 66 F.3d 886, 889 (7th 


Cir. 1995). Also, the plaintiff should not be able to 
establish a prima facie case unless he can establish 
that those who were retained in the position that he 
wanted were performing at a lower level than the 
plaintiff. Mitchell v. Data General Corp., 12 F.3d 
1310, 1315 (4th Cir. 1993). Because O’Connor 
failed to establish his qualifications, the Fourth Cir- 
cuit properly dismissed his suit. 


In reading the qualifications requirement out of 
the McDonnell Douglas standards, the plaintiff is 
attempting to prove his initial case on the basis that 
he was terminated and younger workers were re- 
tained. A mere age difference, however, is not suffi- 
cient to fulfill the plaintiff’s burden. 


But it simply cannot be enough in a termination 
case that the employer merely retain[s] younger 
employees. If that were enough, practically any 
terminated employee in a protected class could 
make out a prima facie case of age discrimina- 
tion, since most companies will retain at least 
some employees under age 40. 


Gadsby v. Norwalk Furniture Corp., No. 95-1507, 
1995 U.S. App. LEXIS 35229, at *26 (7th Cir. Dec. 
13, 1995). 


Finally, without a qualification requirement, plain- 
tiffs would be encouraged to rely only on age- 
differential statistics in an attempt either to prove 
class-wide discrimination or to bolster an argument 
that an individual plaintiff was a victim of age dis- 
criminaton. In other words, plaintiffs would be en- 
couraged to use “disparate impact” statistics to prove 
age discrimination. This Court has never authorized 
that theory under the ADEA, and several justices 
and lower courts have opposed its use. See Hazen 
Paper Co. v. Biggins, 113 S.Ct, 1701, 1706, 1710 


9 


(1993); Ellis v. United Airlines, Inc., No. 94-1351 
and 95-1034, 1996 U.S. App. Lexis 120, at *23-34 
(10th Cir. 1996) (Rejecting disparate impact proof 
under the ADEA). 


Because the plaintiff failed to demonstrate that he 
possessed qualifications sufficiently equivalent to the 
retained employees, he did not sustain the burden 
necessary to prove a prima facie case. 


ARGUMENT 


I. TO ESTABLISH A PRIMA FACIE CASE OF AGE 
DISCRIMINATION IN THE CONTEXT OF A COR- 
PORATE REORGANIZATION, THE PLAINTIFF 
MUST ESTABLISH THAT S/HE WAS PERFORM- 
ING AT A LEVEL SUBSTANTIALLY EQUIVALENT 
TO THOSE RETAINED. 


A. Contrary to the Numerous Cases Applying McDon- 
nell Douglas v. Green, the Plaintiff Here Is Attempt- 
ing to Let an Age Difference Establish a Prima 
Facie Case and to Eliminate the Longstanding 
Prima Facie Requirement That the Plaintiff Estab- 
lish Performance Credentials That Are Equivalent 
or Superior to the Comparators Who Were Retained. 


The ostensible issue that the plaintiff presented for 
review by this Court is whether a plaintiff in an age 
discrimination lawsuit must establish: “that he was 
replaced by a younger person or that a younger per- 
son was retained in a similar position, or must show 
that he was replaced by someone outside the protected 
class.” (Pet. at i). Indeed, the plaintiff and support- 
ing amici curiae spend a great deal of effort arguing 
various extreme hypotheticals posing whether a 65- 
year old discharged plaintiff could be the victim of 
age discrimination when replaced either by a 39-year 
old (i.e., outside the protected group), or a 41-year 
old (i.e., inside the protected age group). 


10 


Actually, the plaintiff is attempting to have this 
Court adopt an improper prima facie test in which 
an age difference alone would suffice to establish a 
prima facie case, and any consideration of relative 
qualifications would be dropped from the plaintiff’s 
burden of proof. The question presented by the peti- 
tioner presumes that a prima facie case could be 
shown by age differences alone. Also, the petitioner 
argues that: 


Any requirement that characteristics of the re- 
placement be shown as part of the prima facie 
case will in effect abolish the presumptive char- 
acter of the prima facie case, and leave discrimi- 
nation complainants with the very burden of 
making out a traditional circumstantial case 
that it was the purpose of the prima facie case 
to remove. 


(Br. for Pet’r at 16, 21). 


Thus, in arguing the issue presented, the petitioner 
subtly has tried to shift the inquiry in such a way 
as to avoid one of the principal findings underpin- 
ning the ADEA, which is to prevent the setting of 
arbitrary age limits “regardless of potential for job 
performance.” 29 U.S.C. §621(a)(2). Also, the 
petitioner’s position is contrary to the central pur- 
pose of the McDonnell Douglas standard, that is, to 
eliminate one of most common nondiscriminatory rea- 
sons for the discharge of an individual in a protected 
class: the absolute or relative lack of qualifications” 
for the job. Int'l Bhd. of Teamsters v. United States, 
431 U.S. 324, 358 n. 44 (1977). 


This standard requires the plaintiff to prove “that 
the employer, after having rejected the plaintiff’s ap- 
plication for a job or promotion, continued to seek 
applicants with qualifications similar to the plain- 


11 


tiff’s.” Watson v. Ft. Worth Bank & Trust, 487 U.S. 
977, 987 (1988). A fortiori, if the persons retained 
were more qualified than the plaintiff, then the 
plaintiff’s initial burden cannot be met. , 


Of course, if this traditional route is followed in 
this case, the plaintiff will lose. As both the district 
court and Fourth Circuit pointed out, the evidence 
shows that O’Connor cannot prove that he was as 
qualified as those employees who were retained. 
Indeed, his past performance supervising a signifi- 
cantly less complex territory indicated that his 
qualifications were substantially inferior to those of 
the employees who were retained and given expanded 
responsibilities. 

In essence, the plaintiff wants this Court to let 
him establish a prima facie case without any con- 
sideration of how his qualifications stack up against 
those who were retained, even though this is the 
only meaningful way to apply the McDonnell Douglas 
test. Under O’Connor’s position, the only burden the 
plaintiff should be required to meet is to show that 
the employees who were retained were sufficiently 
younger than he was. As we show below, however, 
the Court should reject this attempt to rewrite the 
McDonnell Douglas test and should clarify that an 
age difference alone is not sufficient to allow the 
plaintiff to proceed in court. 


B. To Establish an ADEA Prima Facie Case, the Plain- 
tiff Bears the Burden of Proving Qualifications for 
the Job in Question. 


Title VII of the Civil Rights Act of 1964, 42 U.S.C. 
§ 2000e et seqg., prohibits discrimination in employ- 
ment on the basis of race, color, religion, sex or na- 
tional origin. Similarly, the Age Discrimination in 


12 


Employment Act of 1967, 29 U.S.C. § 621 et seq., 
prohibits discrimination on the basis of age. 29 
U.S.C. § 623. Neither statute, however, authorizes 
recovery merely because an employer’s legitimate 
exercise of managerial discretion adversely affects a 
member of a protected group.* Both Title VII and 
the ADEA provide a remedy only when the action 
complained of was taken “because of” the illegal 
criterion.* Thus, 


Whatever the employer’s decisionmaking process, 
a disparate treatment claim cannot succeed un- 
less the employee’s protected trait actually played 
a role in that process and had a determinative 
influence on the outcome. 


Hazen Paper Co. v. Biggins, 113 S. Ct. 1701, 1706 
(1993). 


In a series of decisions beginning with McDonnell 
Douglas Corporation v. Green, 411 U.S. 792 (1973), 
and continuing through St. Mary's Honor Ctr. v. 
Hicks, 113 S. Ct. 2742 (1993), this Court has devel- 
oped a method to allow a plaintiff in appropriate 
cases to demonstrate disparate treatment and viola- 
tion of employment discrimination laws despite the 


2 The ADEA is not a vehicle for reviewing the propriety 
of business decisions. . [t]his court will not second guess 
business decisions made by employers, in the absence of some 
evidence of impermissible motives.” Faulkner v. Super Valu 
Stores, Inc., 3 F.8d 1419, 1427 (10th Cir. 1993). 


Under § 703 (a) of Title VII, it is an unlawful employment 
practice for an employer to discriminate against an employee 
“because of such individual’s race, color, religion, sex or na- 
tional origin.” 42 U.S.C. § 2000e-2 (emphasis added). The 
ADEA prohibits discrimination against any individual be- 
cause of such individual’s age.” 29 U.S.C. § 623 (a) (emphasis 
added). 


13 


unavailability of direct evidence.” Trans-World Air- 
lines, Inc. v. Thurston, 469 U.S. 111, 121 (1985) 
(quoting Loeb v. Textron, Inc., 600 F.2d 1008, 1014 
(Ist Cir. 1979)). Recognizing that “[t]here will 
seldom be ‘eyewitness’ testimony as to the employer’s 
mental processes,” U.S. Postal Serv. Bd. of Governors 
v. Aikens, 460 U.S. 711, 716 (1983), so that direct, 
“smoking gun” evidence is often unavailable, this 
Court has instead developed “a sensible, orderly way 
to evaluate the evidence in light of «ommon experi- 
ence as it bears on the critical question of discrimina- 
tion.” Furnco Constr. Corp. v. Waters, 438 U.S. 567, 
577 (1978). 


McDonnell Douglas established the basic order of 
proof. As this Court explained in Burdine, the plain- 
tiff first must establish a prima facie case; the em- 
ployer then is given an opportunity to “articulate” 
some legitimate, nondiscriminatory reasons for its 
actions; and, if that is done, the plaintiff then must 
prove that the employer’s stated reasons were pre- 
textual.” Burdine, 450 U.S. at 252-53. The “ultimate 
burden” of persuading the trier of fact that the de- 
fendant intentionally discriminated against the plain- 
tiff “remains at all times with the plaintiff.” 450 
U.S. at 253. 


The McDonnell Douglas decision enumerated the 
generic factors that make up the plaintiff’s prima 
facie case of discrimination. Thus the plaintiff must 
prove: 


(i) that he belongs to a racial minority; (ii) 
that he applied and was qualified for a job for 
which the employer was seeking applicants; (iii) 
that, despite his fications, he was rejected; 
and that, after his rejection, the position re- 
mained open and the employer continued to seek 


14 


applicants from persons of complainant’s qualifi- 
cations. 

411 U.S. at 802 (emphasis added). The specific re- 

quirements, however, can vary from case to case, 


depending upon the employment action and statute 
at issue. McDonnell Douglas, 411 U.S. at 802 n.13. 


The plaintiff, however, must satisfy one basic ele- 
ment, no matter the context. This method of estab- 
lishing a prima facie case is intended to eliminate 
“the most common non-discriminatory reasons for 
the plaintiff's rejection,” Burdine, 450 U.S. at 254, 
such as the plaintiff's lack of qualifications or the 
unavailability of the position. Here the plaintiff's 
“lack of qualifications” is crucial to the case. 


In applying the McDonnell Douglas standard to 
ADEA cases involving reductions in force of reor- 
ganizations, the courts have recognized that the pri- 
mary issue is the “relative performance” of the 
plaintiff and retained employees, since many of the 
employees may have been meeting existing perform- 
ance standards before the employer decided to elimi- 
nate positions and choose among many previously 
qualified candidates. Mitchell v. Data General Corp., 
12 F.3d 1310, 1315 (4th Cir. 1993). 


In such a situation, the plaintiff, at minimum, 
should have to prove that he “was performing to his 
employer’s legitimate expectations.” Collier v. Budd

C. An Age Differential Between the Plaintiff and the 
Retained Employees Is Not Sufficient to Establish a 
Prima Facie Case of Age Discrimination. 


As previously explained, if relative qualifications 
were not part of the plaintiff’s prima facie burden, 
then the plaintiff would find it much easier to argue 
that an age differential alone is sufficient to establish 
an initial case of age discrimination. O’Connor ulti- 
mately is relying on the fact that he was terminated 
and that younger supervisors were not. 


But it simply cannot be enough in a termination 
case that the employer merely retain[s] younger 
employees. If that were enough, practically any 
terminated employee in a protected class could 
make out a prima facie case of age discrimina- 
tion, since most companies will retain at least 
some employees under age 40. Accordingly, [the 
plaintiff] has failed to satisfy even the more 
permissive formulation of the fourth factor of 
the prima facie case.
