This case presents a fundamental issue concerning what 
is required of a replaced plaintiff in a suit under the Age 
Discrimination in Employment Act to establish a prima 
facie case pursuant to the proof scheme established by 
this Court in McDonnell-Douglas v. Green, 411 U.S. 792 
(1973). Specifically, this Court must determine whether 
a replaced plaintiff will be entitled to the judicially- 
created inference of unlawful employer motive without 
making a showing regarding the replacement’s age. The 
Court of Appeals held that the status of the replacement 
employee was vital to the plaintiff's prima facie case under 
McDonnell-Douglas in an ADEA suit. 


In such litigation, the framing of plaintiff's burden 
under McDonnell-Douglas defines the circumstances in 
which employers are required to defend against claims of 
illegal age discrimination when there has been no affirma- 
tive showing, at that point, of unlawful employer intent. 
Thus, this issue is crucial to employers who must expend 
significant amounts of time and money defending against 
age discrimination lawsuits, a great number of which are 
baseless. The resolution of this question will have a 
direct and recurring impact on the employer companies 
and associations who are members of the Chamber. Ac- 
cordingly, the Chamber believes its perspective on this 
issue may be of help to the Court in its consideration of 
the question here presented.

SUMMARY OF THE CASE 


Respondent, Consolidated Coin Caterers Corp. (“Con- 
solidated”), is a vending and food service company. 
Although separately incorporated, Consolidated operates 
as a division of its parent company, Canteen Corp. (Pet. 
App. 26). 


Petitioner, James O'Connor, began his employment 
with Consolidated in 1978. In December, 1989, Peti- 
tioner became the District Manager of the Consolidated 
region known as “4Cs South.” In July, 1990, Petition- 
er’s immediate supervisor, Ed Williams, reallocated a 
segment of Petitioner’s region to another manager. On 
August 9, 1990, Consolidated terminated Petitioner as 
part of a reorganization. At that time, Petitioner was 56 
years old. (Pet. App. 26). 


After the reorganization, Consolidated operated only 
two districts, instead of the four it previously had oper- 
ated. Petitioner’s old region was combined into a new 
district also called “4Cs South.”, Consolidated retained 
a 40-year old employee, Ted Finnell, to manage the new 
“4Cs South” region. (Pet. App. 26). 


Petitioner brought this suit alleging that his termina- 
tion violated the Age Discrimination in Employment Act, 
29 U.S.C. § 623. After substantial discovery, Consoli- 
dated moved for and the disrtict court granted sum- 
mary judgment. The district court held that Petitioner 
had not established a prima facie case under McDonnell- 
Douglas v. Green, 411 U.S. 792 (1973), because he not 
only failed to show that his replacement was younger 
than 40 and thus outside the protected class, but also 
failed to present any other evidence demonstrating that 
Consolidated had not treated age neutrally in terminating 
Petitioner. (Pet. App. 29-30). The district court further 
found that Petitioner likewise failed to make a prima 
facie case using traditional principles of proof. (Pet. App. 
39-34). The court discounted Petitioner's direct evidence, 
statements made by his immediate supervisor, because

there was no relationship between the statements and the 
decision to discharge Petitioner. (Pet. App. 31). Addi- 
tionally, the district court found unpersuasive Petitioner's 
indirect evidence comparing his job performance with that 
of his replacement. (Pet. App. 33-34). 


On appeal, the Fourth Circuit upheld the grant of sum- 
mary judgment stating that “applying the McDonnell- 
Douglas paradigm we conclude that O’Connor cannot 
establish a prima facie case because he fails to satisfy the 
fourth element, i.e., he was not replaced by someone out-

the district court, the court of appeals also concluded that 
Petitioner had not proven a prima facie case of age dis- 
crimination under ordinary standards of proof using direct 
or circumstantial evidence. (Pet. App. 15-19). 


SUMMARY OF THE ARGUMENT 


In a disparate treatment case under the Age Discrimi- 
nation in Employment Act, a plaintiff is required to 
prove that his employer discriminated against him because 
of his age. Plaintiff has the initial burden of establishing 
a prima facie case demonstrating the employer's unlawful 
intent. A plaintiff may establish a prima facie case 
through one of two ways. First, he may do so through 
traditional methods of direct, indirect, or circumstantial 
proof. When such traditional evidence is unavailable, 
however, the plaintiff may resort to the inference of dis- 
criminatory employer intent created by this Court in 
McDonnell-Douglas v. Green, 411 U.S. 401 (1973). 


In McDonnell-Douglas, this Court provided that a 
plaintiff in a discrimination suit is entitled to an inference 
of illegal employer animus, despite the absence of an 
affirmative showing to that effect, if a four-part formula- 
tion is satisfied. The Court reasoned that the facts estab- 
lished pursuant to this four-part formula would eliminate 
the two most obvious reasons for an adverse employment 
action, poor job performance and lack of a position. This 


5 


would leave room for an inference of the critical unlawful 
motive. 


The instant case presents the question of how the - 
fourth prong of the original McDonnell-Douglas frame- 
work—whether the employer continued to seek to fill the 
open position—should be applied when the plaintiff 
actually has been “replaced. The Chamber urges modifi- 
cation of the fourth prong to require that, in the replace- 
ment context, the plaintiff in an ADEA suit must establish 
that the replacement was outside the protected class. In 
the alternative, the Chamber suggests that the plaintiff be 
required to demonstrate that the replacement was “sub- 
stantially younger.” The Chamber also recommends that 
this “substantially younger” standard be interpreted to 
require at least a ten-year age disparity between the plain- 
tiff and the replacement. 


A showing that plaintiff's replacement was outside the 
protected class or, in the alternative, “substantially 
younger” is necessary to justify the McDonnell-Douglas 
inference of discriminatory animus. No such inference 
can be justified by common sense or experience when the 
plaintiff, and the replacement, both are within the class 
protected by the ADEA or when the relative ages of the 
two employees are comparable or only slightly different. 


Neither of the suggested requirements would unduly 
burden plaintiffs who have legitimate claims of discrimina- 
tion because proof of age is readily available and easily 
established. A plaintiff always has the option of estab- 
lishing the prima facie case by utilizing traditional evi- 
dence. Further, the Chamber’s proposed definite standard 
will discourage meritless discrimination claims that un- 
justifiably burden employers and clog the legal system. 


Moreover, the decision in this case undoubtedly will 
have wide-ranging effects in Title VII litigation and will 
provide guidelines for determining whether the charac- 
teristics of a replacement have relevance with respect to 
the McDonnell-Douglas formulation under that statute.

plicable in both ADEA and Title VII suits, wherein the 
plaintiff initially has the burden of establishing a prima 
facie case demonstrating the employer’s unlawful motive.*

B. Appropriate Modification of McDonnell-Douglas for 
ADEA Cases 


It is the Chamber’s position that, in ADEA cases, this 
Court should draw a bright line between the plaintiff's 
age and the replacement’s age. 


The lower courts often have noted the difficulty they 
have experienced in applying McDonnell-Douglas in 
ADEA cases. The Seventh Circuit recently noted that it 
has developed no fewer than four different statements of 
the McDonnell-Douglas prima facie case requirements in 
ADEA cases involving discharges. See Kralman v. Illinois 
Dep't of Veterans’ Affairs, 23 F.3d 150, 153 n.2 (7th 
Cir.), cert. denied, 115 §. Ct. 1372 (1994). Even Amicus 
AARP acknowledges that the lower courts have struggled 
in adapting the fourth element of McDonnell-Douglas to 
ADEA cases. (AARP Br. 9 n.6). This struggle has re- 
sulted in inconsistent decisions both between and within 
the circuit courts and a total lack of uniformity in admin- 
istering the federal statute. A bright line standard will 
provide clear guidance for both courts and litigants. 


Furthermore, a bright line is necessary because age is 
not a discrete, immutable characteristic, like race or 
gender, which separates members within the protected 
group indelibly from persons outside the protected group. 


12 


Carter v. City of Miami, 870 F.2d 578, 583 (11th Cir. 
1980). Instead, it has shades and gradations. Absent 
a clear, albeit somewhat arbitrary, line, age distinctions 
are ephemeral.” 


1. Replacement Is Outside the Protected Class 


This Court, consistent with the Fourth Circuit's holding 
below, should adopt a bright line requirement that a plain- 
tiff must show that the replacement employee is outside 
the protected class in order to be entitled to the benefits of 
a McDonnell-Douglas inference of illegal motive. 


By its terms, the ADEA prohibits discrimination only 
against older employees, and it defines that group as per- 
sons 40 years and older. 29 U.S.C. Section 631(a). To 
justify an inference of unlawful employer motive under 
McDonnell-Douglas, therefore, a_ plaintiff necessarily 
should show that someone lacking the protected charac- 
teristic, i.e., someone younger than 40 years old, was 
treated more favorably. 


Furthermore, Congress's intent in passing the ADEA 
was to eliminate practices which operated to disadvantage 
the employment opportunities of the class of workers pro- 
tected by the Act.” “[{T}he primary purpose of the 
[ADEA] was to facilitate the hiring of older workers.”

negligible, certainly “not onerous.” See Burdine, 450 U.S.
