## Questions, relief and preserved scope

**A, p.i:** “Whether Section16(a) of the Public Telecommunications Act of1992, whether standing alone or as limited by the Court of Appeals, violates the First Amendment.” A seeks certiorari, contests the existing uniform6a.m.–10p.m. prohibition, and challenges both the substantive restrictions and the lower court's remedy extending the10p.m. opening to all broadcasters. A pp.20–21 argues the court should not rewrite the statute. Its separate pp.26–29 vagueness argument is genuinely present in the petition; describing the petition as raising no definition issue would be inaccurate. The approved simulated review scope still controls whether that independent question may be adjudicated; at minimum, the definition's breadth and contextual uncertainty matter to the channeling burden.

**P, p.i:** three questions concern (1) the18-hour6a.m.–midnight ban and the absence of a meaningful safe harbor for constitutionally protected speech of serious merit, particularly under the assertedly broad and vague indecency definition; (2) whether *Pacifica* authorizes censorship regardless of merit, infringing the audience's rights; and (3) whether indecent speech may be suppressed on the assertedly unsupported theory of harm to all minors under18 and ineffective parental supervision. These are the petitioners' characterizations, not neutral findings. P p.10 n.5 distinguishes the lower court's favorable **midnight rationale** from its actual decree opening the safe harbor at10p.m.

**G, pp.i, 8–9:** the government's operative question is the constitutionality of the **6a.m.–10p.m. prohibition**. It expressly states that **the government did not petition for review** of the lower court's invalidation of the10p.m.–midnight restraint. It argues the Court reviews judgments, not statements in opinions, and cannot use petitioners' case to decide a midnight restriction in a different statute. Thus, ordinary review of this record must not restore the harsher midnight opening simply because the lower opinion discussed its abstract permissibility. Restoration would enlarge respondents' rights beyond the unchallenged part of the decree; no government petition authorizing that relief is supplied.

**R, pp.1–2 n.3:** Pacifica expressly rejects the government's contention that it challenged only the18-hour ban. Its alternatives—separate treatment of public radio, station/channel-specific restrictions and television blocking technology—are asserted to defeat the16-hour ban as well. Do not treat Pacifica as conceding the uniform10p.m. scheme or abandoning review of the actual decree.

G pp.18–20 asks the Court to hold these petitions for consideration in light of the related cable-indecency litigation, identifying the similar indecency definition and claimed child-protection interests. R pp.8–10 asks for concurrent consideration instead. Neither request is a Court action, a governing cable holding, or a dependency that overrides the supplied March4 merits event. A and P ask for certiorari and removal of the challenged speech restrictions; no damages, finding of liability for a particular broadcast, or adjudication of the separate forfeiture-procedure case is sought here.

## Actual regulation, qualifications and record treatment

F ¶¶1–3 and its amendatory text distinguish **obscenity prohibited at all times** from **indecency**. The enacted scheme barred indecency6a.m.–10p.m. on public stations within47U.S.C.§397(6) that sign off at or before midnight, and6a.m.–midnight on every other covered radio or television station. The classification is not simply public versus commercial: a public station remaining on air after midnight falls within the later opening. The lower decree supplies the present uniform10p.m.–6a.m. harbor.

F ¶4 n.10 defines indecency as language/material that **in context** depicts or describes sexual/excretory activities or organs in terms patently offensive measured by contemporary community standards for the broadcast medium. F ¶¶29–32 does not create automatic exemptions for news, public affairs or serious merit, but neither does it deem all sexual discussion or every particular word indecent. Context, reasonable licensee judgment and earlier applications matter; F ¶31 cites dismissed clinical/instructional and bona fide news complaints. A p.3 n.1 expressly acknowledges that its listed examples were **not all found indecent**. P pp.13–14 asserts chilling effects, including altered or withheld literary programs, with citations to its submitted comments; those are party-supported record assertions, not new judicial findings that every listed work was prohibited.

**Retained administrative defense, F ¶¶37–38, P App.127a–128a:** the FCC says it would consider case-specific evidence of “no actual risk” of children in the broadcast audience **in the station's market at the time**. It **tentatively** continues that defense alongside the midnight safe harbor. “Market-wide data demonstrating that there is no appreciable child audience during the relevant time period would raise a viable defense” outside the safe harbor. It regards ordinary program/station-specific ratings as inadequate **until** convincing factual evidence demonstrates relevance despite rapid channel switching (“grazing”). This is neither a categorical license for stations with low historic child ratings nor an absolute refusal ever to consider station-specific proof. No particular broadcaster has been adjudged entitled to the defense here.

F ¶33 explains the favored sign-off-station classification as an economic accommodation for public stations with limited budgets that otherwise lack any broadcast time within the standard safe harbor. That is the agency's stated justification, not a finding that their child audiences present a different risk. The lower court's classification ruling and precise decree are supplied by the independently verified lower opinion.

## Audience and access: use the right denominator

* **Child access to sets:** F ¶¶21,24 and G p.12 cite over63% of households with more than one television,25–50% of children with a set for personal use, and a study with bedroom radios for74% of kindergarten–sixth graders and88% of seventh–twelfth graders. Those data support opportunity for unsupervised access; they do not measure actual exposure to indecent programs or prove parental inability in every home.
* **Television:** F ¶22 and G p.13 cite projected nationwide teen viewers of about4.3million at11–11:30p.m. and3.1million at11:30p.m.–1a.m.; G cites fewer than1million at1:45–2a.m. F ¶23 reports market-specific morning and late-evening data, with separate weekday/Saturday figures. Do not flatten these estimates into identical all-day or seven-day audience findings.
* **Radio:** F ¶25 distinguishes an **average quarter-hour** teen audience of2,437,000 from6a.m.–midnight and716,000 after midnight from **one-day cumulative reach** of9,244,000 teens listening at least five minutes during7p.m.–midnight and3,533,000 after midnight. Cumulative reach is not simultaneous audience. F ¶25 n.57 acknowledges little quantitative radio-listening information for children under12.
* **Adult opportunity:** P p.16 n.9 cites47.0million adults watching network-affiliated stations at10–11p.m., compared with16.9million atmidnight–1a.m.; radio figures are11.7million and7.4million adults for those periods, respectively, based on quarter-hour audience. G pp.14–15 relies on meaningful remaining late-night audiences, taping for later use, staying up later and other media. Neither party's account establishes universal practical adult access or universal exclusion. G expressly says nonbroadcast alternatives are **not in themselves determinative**.
* **Supervision:** A pp.6,19–20 and P p.27 n.15 cite submitted survey evidence that at least98% of under18s are with adults, at school or asleep during8p.m.–6a.m.; A p.20 n.19 gives more than99% for6–10a.m. P says over93% were under parental supervision and over99% under adult supervision during10p.m.–6a.m. These categories must not become proof that98% are actively co-viewing, effectively controlled, or actually watching broadcasts. The government disputes the inference from adult presence to effective supervision, not the proposition that no children are ever with adults.
* **Public radio:** P pp.29–30 n.17 relies on a0.2% share of **teen listening** for public radio and a weekly teen public-radio cumulative reach below5%. Those measures are not zero child audience or a finding that the whole broadcast-radio audience is negligible. F ¶27 n.67 answers that format preferences do not disprove substantial children in the total audience or prevent changing stations.

## Strongest supported opposing positions

**Petitioners:** nonobscene, sometimes seriously meritorious expression remains protected; a sixteen- or eighteen-hour exclusion can impair meaningful adult and older-minor access even without a24-hour ban. *Pacifica* sustained a specific contextual broadcast ruling and expressly left other contexts open; *Sable* rejected elimination of adult access to protect children and does not demand foolproof alternatives. Petitioners demand evidence connecting the actual breadth, times, age group and medium to the asserted interests. Their harm objection concerns the absence of a shown causal link for the protected indecency category; they distinguish *Ginsberg*'s variable-obscenity statute. They challenge reliance on whole-market audiences without evidence of actual indecent-program exposure; question treating all under18s alike; emphasize diverse parental choices; and contest replacing contemporaneous news or live discussion with later recordings or other media. A pp.12–25; P pp.11–30; R pp.2–8.

**Government:** accessible over-the-air programming differs from media requiring purchase, subscription or affirmative acquisition. Its dual interests concern child welfare and parents' practical ability to exercise authority. It invokes common sense and *Ginsberg*/*Pacifica*, arguing that causal social-science proof is not a constitutional prerequisite. It accepts the general compelling-interest/least-restrictive-means formulation and argues this record meets it without requiring resolution of a lower standard for broadcasting. It relies on general audience data, rapid tuning, multiple household receivers, the concentration of supervision into fewer late-night hours and retained adult opportunities. G pp.9–16. Its responses to maturing-minor distinctions, G p.15 n.6, invoke the recognized under18 line and older teens' ability to use late hours; those are arguments, not evidence that all ages have identical vulnerabilities.

**Definition dispute:** A pp.26–29 argues that *Pacifica* adjudicated the specific Carlin broadcast, not a facial challenge to the generic definition; it invokes chilling effects and inconsistent fact-specific application. G pp.16–18 argues *Pacifica* and *Sable* implicitly reject the facial vagueness attack and distinguishes other-media definitions. These are competing readings. Do not turn a Court's silence into an independently verified holding or assume every cited post-divergence historical Supreme Court proposition governs this simulation.

**Limits on petitioners' evidentiary rhetoric:** their phrases “no evidence,” “uncontroverted,” and “admittedly absent” remain advocacy. F contains data, explanations and case-law-based harm premises; the live dispute is the sufficiency and fit of those materials. Conversely, the government's characterizations of a meaningful harbor, harmful material, effective supervision and legislative balancing are not independent judicial findings. General audience evidence is not itself an experiment establishing harm from indecency.

## Alternatives and the February1996 enactment

A pp.23–25 and P/R discuss parental rules, limiting sets in bedrooms, installing television blocking devices, co-viewing/co-listening, different evening hours, radio-specific treatment, station/program evidence and television blocking technology. F ¶¶30–36 discusses broadcaster discretion, alternative media and practical supervision; ¶43 asserts that other alternatives cannot effectively further the interest without an itemized comparison. Avoid saying the record contains no consideration of any alternative. The competing question is whether the consideration adequately supports this content-based scheme.

P p.30 n.17 and R p.7 present a V-chip as an asserted less restrictive television alternative. They do **not** establish an operational nationwide ratings/blocking system, an equivalent radio technology, universal receiver replacement or current reliable blocking of all relevant content.

Section551 was enacted February8,1996 and adds date-eligible statutory material. Its congressional findings characterize timely program information and parental blocking tools as nonintrusive and narrowly tailored; those are legislative findings, not judicial proof in this administrative record. The statute does not repeal§16(a) here or automatically resolve the pending constitutional challenge. The conditional rating amendment becomes effective one year after enactment **only upon the specified FCC determination that distributors have not established acceptable voluntary rating rules and agreed voluntarily to broadcast rating signals**. The FCC must set the manufacturing requirement's applicability **no earlier than two years after enactment**. Covered sets have screens at least13inches diagonally; carriers merely transporting without trading are excepted. Alternative blocking technology must identify programs without ratings, be comparably priced and allow broad multichannel blocking as effectively and easily; the FCC determination triggers the requirement to allow either technology. No subsequent FCC determination, implemented rating system or new-set penetration may be assumed as of March4. The text's rating authority also excludes rating by political or religious content.
