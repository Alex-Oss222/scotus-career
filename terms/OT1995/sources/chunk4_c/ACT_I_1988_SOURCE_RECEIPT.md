## What Ginsburg's court opinion actually decides

1. **Reviewable general agency action, pp. 1336–38 and notes 5–7.** Although styled informal adjudications, the three rulings, public notice, and reconsideration order announced a standard of general applicability. The FCC may select adjudication rather than rulemaking under *Chenery*, but cannot thereby insulate a general standard from judicial review. The court does not decide whether the public notice was independently reviewable; the reconsideration order suffices.

2. **Reasoned change in the definition, p. 1338.** The FCC adequately explained why it abandoned a practical restriction to repeated use of Carlin's seven words: an otherwise equally offensive description of sexual or excretory organs or activities should not escape regulation simply by avoiding those words. The earlier policy could produce anomalous or arbitrary outcomes, and no reasonable tighter formulation was offered in this proceeding. The approval is of the explanation for this definitional change; it does not supply the missing justification for changed broadcast hours.

3. **Vagueness and the lower court's obligation, pp. 1338–39 and notes 8–10.** Ginsburg expressly treats the Supreme Court's *Pacifica* disposition as stopping an inferior court from deciding the vagueness challenge on the merits. The virtually identical generic definition had underlain the approved Carlin adjudication; the court infers implicit acceptance that “indecent” was not impermissibly vague, while acknowledging *Pacifica* did not specifically adjudicate facial vagueness. Note 8 distinguishes the nature of material from whether its broadcast is actionable at a given time: a violation still requires indecent material and broadcast when children reasonably may be in the audience. This is not a finding that the definition is perfectly clear or that timing is irrelevant.

4. **Serious merit, pp. 1339–40 and notes 10–14.** The court reaches the overbreadth challenge because it reads only two members of *Pacifica*'s five-member majority as having addressed overbreadth on the merits. Indecent but nonobscene expression remains protected whether or not it possesses serious merit. Merit is relevant to patent offensiveness but does not automatically immunize the material from channeling: socially valuable words or descriptions may have the same impact on children whose parents wish to limit exposure. The definition is sustained for the stated purpose of channeling to protect unsupervised children, with sensitivity to broadcasters, adults, and parents. Note 14 relies on the FCC's assurance that reasonable licensee judgments will receive weight when sanctions are considered, tempering chilling effects through restrained enforcement. It promises neither absolute deference to licensees nor an exemption for all meritorious works.

5. **Broadcast medium and constitutional scrutiny, pp. 1340–44 and notes 11–12, 18–21.** Broadcasting cannot segregate children's and adults' material as an adults-only bookstore section can; that makes sensitivity to all protected interests particularly necessary. Note 18 expressly rejects characterizing indecency channeling as content-neutral time, place, and manner regulation. Channeling is content-based; such restrictions ordinarily require a precisely drawn means of serving a compelling interest. Protecting minors' physical and psychological well-being is compelling, but speech restrictions must be carefully tailored. Here the requisite precision requires a reasonable safe harbor. The government interest actually advanced is facilitating parental supervision of unsupervised children, not overriding parents who permit access. Note 20 identifies home-privacy protection from adult intrusion as a *Pacifica* rationale on which these FCC rulings did not rely. Note 21 calls cogent the argument that permitting programming outside common workday hours may improve parental supervision; it does not establish a particular mandatory hour.

6. **Evidence and hours, pp. 1341–43 and notes 16–18.** The post-10 p.m. rulings lacked a rational connection between the evidence and selected restriction. The figures concerned all radio audiences rather than the stations at issue. The FCC used ages 12–17 without explaining a shift from its earlier concern with under-12s or providing an explained extrapolation. Los Angeles figures lacked a comparison denominator. Santa Barbara figures showed only 3.7–4.3 percent of the age group listening, with no explanation why that was a reasonable risk sufficient for these restrictions. These are defects in the reviewed record, not categorical holdings that four percent can never matter or that any age above twelve is constitutionally excluded. Reasons offered for midnight could also fit an earlier hour. Undefined case-by-case enforcement is not an adequate substitute: definitional imprecision combined with no safe harbor risks suppressing protected programming altogether. Broadcasters require clear notice of reasonably determined permissible hours.

7. **Distinct merits and remedies, pp. 1334–35, 1341, 1343–44 and note 15.** The court affirms the *Infinity* declaratory warning for the 6–10 a.m. show, finding no rationally proposed basis to distinguish it from the *Pacifica* afternoon broadcast under the parent-child rationale. No sanction had been imposed, in recognition of reliance on prior FCC policy. It vacates the two post-10 p.m. actionable-broadcast rulings (*Pacifica Foundation* and *Regents of U.C.*), vacates the reconsideration order in part, and remands the channeling question for fresh decision after a full and fair hearing on a full record. The remand extends beyond merely reconsidering the two individual adjudications. The court encourages reconsideration of daytime prescriptions too, but does not vacate *Infinity*. It cannot require the agency to use rulemaking. It chooses no conclusive constitutionally valid hour, and does not invalidate all channeling or the generic definition. The unchallenged requirement of appropriate advance warnings remains unaffected.
