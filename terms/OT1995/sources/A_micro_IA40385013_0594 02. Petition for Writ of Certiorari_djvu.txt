STATEMENT OF THE CASE 


On November 3, 1988, the City and State of New 
York, the State of California, and several other cities, public 
interest groups, and individuals brought this suit to compel 
the Department of Commerce and the Bureau of the Census 
to make statistical adjustments to the 1990 United States 
census in order to reduce the undercount of the population, 
particularly minorities, anticipated by the plaintiffs. 
Jurisdiction was based on 28 U.S.C. §§ 1331, 1337, and 
1361, as well as 5 U.S.C. § 702. The suit survived an early 
dismissal motion. See City of New York v. U.S. Dept. of 
Commerce, 713 F. Supp. 48 (E.D.N.Y. 1989). 


In 1991, then-Secretary of Commerce Robert A. 
Mosbacher issued his decision not to make statistically-based 
adjustments to the 1990 United States census. 56 Fed. Reg. 
33,582. In reaching that conclusion, the Secretary applied 
guidelines that he had published (55 Fed. Reg. 9,838) 
following a court-approved stipulation between the parties to 
this litigation. See City of New York v. U.S. Dept. of 
Commerce, 739 F. Supp. 761, 769 (E.D.N.Y. 1990). The 
Secretary's decision not to adjust the census enumeration was 
upheld by the district court on the basis of the administrative 
record and the additional evidence presented at a lengthy 
trial. See City of New York v. U.S. Dept. of Commerce, 822 
F. Supp. 906 (E.D.N.Y. 1993). 


6 


The district court, applying an “arbitrary and 
capricious" standard under the Administrative Procedures 
Act, 5 U.S.C. § 706(2)(A), determined that the Secretary's 
decision was reasonable and supported by the record. The 
court relied, in particular, on the reasonableness of the 
Secretary’s approach of focusing on distributive rather than 
numeric accuracy -- that is, while statistical adjustment 
would probably result in greater numeric accuracy for the 
nation as a whole, adjustment would not assure greater 
accuracy, and would probably result in greater inaccuracy, 
at the city or block level, the level at which the greatest 
effects of adjustment would be felt for intra-state redistricting 
and allocation of federal funds. 822 F. Supp. at 921-925. 


The court of appeals reversed and remanded for 
further proceedings. 34 F.3d 1114 (2d Cir. 1994). The court 
determined that the arbitrary-and-capricious standard of 
review was inappropriate. Instead, the court looked to the 
reapportionment cases such as Karcher v. Daggett, 462 U.S. 
725 (1983), to find a constitutional standard of review. 
Concluding that the equal protection and due process 
principles of “one person/one vote” required the Department 
of Commerce to make a good-faith effort to achieve census 
accuracy “as nearly as practicable," 34 F.3d at 1129, 1131, 
the court held that the Secretary’s decision was subject to 
heightened scrutiny: Did the decision not to adjust further a 
legitimate governmental objective and was it essential to the 
achievement of that objective? 34 F.3d at 1116, 1131. 


Judge Timbers dissented from the opinion of the 
court of appeals. 34 F.3d at 1131-1132. He concluded that 
the panel decision conflicted with two other circuit decisions, 


7 


City of Detroit v. Franklin, 4 F.3d 1367 (6th Cir. 1993), 
cert. denied, 114 S.Ct. 1217 (1994), and Tucker v. U.S. 
Dept. of Commerce, 958.F.2d 1411 (7th Cir.), cert. denied, 
113 S.Ct. 407 (1992), which had earlier rejected challenges 
to the Secretary’s decision. 


REASONS FOR GRANTING THE WRIT . 


The court of appeals adopted a standard of review 
inconsistent with the principles enunciated in Franklin v. 
Massachusetts, 112 S.Ct. 2767 (1992), and U.S. Depr. of 
Commerce v. Montana, 503 U.S. 442 (1992). 


The judgment of the court of appeals conflicts with 
the decisions of two other circuits, City of Detroit v. 
Franklin, 4 F.3d 1367 (6th Cir. 1993), cert. denied, 114 
S.Ct. 1217 (1994); and Tucker v. Dept. of Commerce, 958 
F.2d 1411 (7th Cir.), cert. denied, 113 S.Ct. 407 (1992). 


This case is important to the Nation as a whole. 
Finality of the 1990 United States census enumeration is 
undeniably important for a number of reasons. If the 
judgment of the court of appeals is not reviewed, and the 
matter is remanded for further proceedings, it will likely be 
near the end of the decade before the validity of the 1990 
census results will be finally determined. 


I. The Court of Appeals Adopted a Standard of 
Review Inconsistent with Precedent from This 
Court 


The court of appeals determined that the decision of 
the Secretary of Commerce not to adjust the 1990 census 
results was subject to heightened equal protection scrutiny. 
The court said that “the federal government, no less than the 
States, is required to make a good-faith effort to achieve the 
Constitution's plain objective of equal representation for 
equal numbers of people." 34 F.3d at 1129. Accordingly, 
the court held that the Secretary had the burden to show that 
his decision not to adjust furthered a legitimate governmental 
objective and was essential to achieve that objective. 


This heightened standard of review was derived from 
the apportionment cases such as Karcher v. Daggett, 462 
U.S. 725 (1983); Kirkpatrick vy. Preisler, 394 U.S. 526 
(1969); Reynolds v. Sims, 377 U.S. 533 (1964); and 
Wesberry v. Sanders, 376 U.S. 1 (1964), in which this Court 
held that “as nearly as practicable" congressional and state 
legislative districts had to be mathematically equal in size. 
The court of appeals viewed the Secretary's decision not to 
adjust the census statistically to be equivalent to a denial of 
equal protection with respect to the fundamental right of 
equality of voting power, for which "[jJudicial standards [of 
review] are well developed and familiar." 34 F.3d at 1128 
(citing Baker v. Carr, 369 U.S. 186, 226 (1962)). 


The standard of review adopted by the court of 
appeals imposes a greater burden on the Secretary than 
required by the Constitution, which provides that the census 


9 


be conducted “in such manner as [Congress] shall by Law 
direct.” U.S. const. art. I, § 2, cl. 3. The standard which 
has been used by this Court in other census cases, and which 
should have been used by the court of appeals, is whether 
the Secretary's decision "is consistent with the constitutional 
language.” Franklin v. Massachusetts, 112 S.Ct. at 2777 
(citing U.S. Dept. of Commerce v. Montana, 112 S.Ct. at 
1426).’

decision “is consistent with the constitutional language,” id. 
at 2777 (citing U.S. Dept. of Commerce v. Montana, 112 
S.Ct. at 1426). 


Rather than determine whether the Secretary's 
decision was consistent with the constitutional language, the 
court of appeals imposed on the Secretary a heightened 
standard of equal protection review. This is a greater burden 
on the Secretary than this Court’s decisions require, and the 
judgment of the court of appeals therefore warrants review.

Il. The Judgment of the Court of Appeals Is in 
Conflict With Decisions from Other Circuits 


Judge Timbers observed in dissent from the panel 
decision of the court of appeals that the opinion of the court 
conflicted with decisions in two other circuits, City of 
Detroit v. Franklin, 4 F.3d 1367 (6th Cir. 1993), cert. 
denied, _U.S.__, 114 S.Ct. 1217 (1994), and Tucker v. 
U.S. Dept. of Commerce, 958 F.2d 1411 (7th Cir.), cert. 
denied, _ U.S. __, 113 S.C. 407 (1992). The judgment 
of the court of appeals should be reviewed to resolve this 
conflict. 


In Tucker, the court of appeals held that neither the 
census statutes nor the Constitution provide a judicially 
administrable standard by which the decision to adjust or not 
to adjust the census headcount could be measured. Affirming 
the dismissal of a complaint to compel federal officials to 
adjust the census statistically, the court concluded that even 
a demonstrated undercount or differential underdount in the 
actual enumeration did not invoke a judicial remedy, not 
because the issue was nonjusticiable, or because the political 
question doctrine barred the suit, or because the plaintiffs did 
not have standing, but because "Article 1, section 2, clause 
3 does not authorize lawsuits founded on disagreement with 
the Census Bureau's statistical methodology." 958 F.2d at 
1418.’

methodology of the census did not give rise to a 
constitutional claim, are in conflict with the decision of the 
court of appeals in this case, that disagreement with the 
methodology of the census gives rise to an equal protection 
claim requiring heightened scrutiny of the administrative 
decision not to adjust the headcount. The judgment of the 
court of appeals should be reviewed to resolve the conflict.
