## Article III: what the primary authorities establish

**Gordon and Hayburn require careful writing-status distinctions.** The actual March 9, 1865 Gordon disposition, 69 U.S. (2 Wall.) 561, dismisses for want of appellate jurisdiction; Miller and Field dissent, and reasons are deferred. The later appendix, 117 U.S. 697–706, prints Taney's previously prepared paper, considered after his death. Its reporter expressly says the contemplated Court opinion was never prepared. It does not establish a delivered and later withdrawn majority opinion. The paper's broad execution language at 702–703 must retain this status. The reported Hayburn proceeding, 2 U.S. (2 Dall.) 409–414, likewise contains no Supreme Court merits decision. The familiar objections to executive suspension and legislative revision are the circuit judges' communications printed in its note. These sources still form part of the inherited history, expressly discussed in later Court opinions; their status cannot be flattened into two fully reasoned Supreme Court holdings.

**Ferreira, 54 U.S. (13 How.) 40, 45–52 (1851), supplies a reasoned Court decision.** The judge operated as a claims commissioner under a procedure lacking the ordinary suit, process, party structure and court record. The award created no debt without Treasury approval; the Secretary could revise it within that nonjudicial arrangement. The Supreme Court therefore lacked appellate jurisdiction. Judicial-seeming discretion alone did not make the function Article III adjudication. The Chief Justice's appended Yale Todd account, 52–53, acknowledges the absence of recorded grounds and must retain the note's inferential status. Neither Ferreira's reserved appointments question nor this historical account establishes an IGRA mediator-appointment defect.

**Brimson, 154 U.S. 447, 475–490 (1894), validates a genuinely judicial action assisting administration.** Under §12 of the Interstate Commerce Act, a commission sought a court order compelling witness testimony and documents; the witnesses contested the commission's authority and demands. The court determined a disputed legal duty through an ordinary adversarial proceeding. Its judgment was conclusive and enforceable by judicial process. Contempt followed disobedience of the court's order, not merely the agency's request. The Court rejected the premise that aiding an administrative inquiry necessarily deprives an adjudication of judicial character, while leaving particular relevance, authority and privilege questions for remand.

The Court discusses Hayburn, Ferreira, Gordon and Sanborn at 481–485. Its explanation at 487 expressly includes that the judgment “may be enforced by process.” The Tribe's substituted-party paraphrase cannot silently remove that feature or turn the case into a holding about a judgment whose only legal effect depends on later discretionary relief. Conversely, the case does not demand that judicial assistance itself resolve every underlying regulatory question. Brewer's separate dissent, 155 U.S. 3–10, argues that the proceeding improperly makes the court a compulsory investigative instrument of the commission, despite its adversarial form. That objection is published opposition, not the controlling rule.

**Nashville, 288 U.S. 249, 259–265 (1933), and Aetna, 300 U.S. 227, 239–244 (1937), preclude an unqualified execution requirement.** Nashville sustained appellate review of an actual, adverse and conclusive state declaratory adjudication of a tax dispute. It expressly treats coercive process or execution as nonessential to every judicial judgment and asks what the determination conclusively accomplishes. Aetna holds the federal declaratory statute procedural: it does not create a constitutional controversy where none exists. A real dispute over presently asserted insurance rights could receive specific, conclusive relief through a declaration without damages or an injunction. Future changes in the claimant's condition did not make the determination of present rights advisory.

These authorities therefore distinguish a binding adjudication from an abstract opinion or a recommendation awaiting another branch's approval. They do not make the declaratory label sufficient. Further-relief authority appeared in the statutes before those Courts; the Governors are entitled to their argument that IGRA's special remedial arrangement limits that route. Nor do these cases decide a State's immunity or create an IGRA cause of action, waiver, coercive remedy or exception to the enacted sequence.

## Genuine Article III alternatives that remain open

The strongest **objection** is broader than whether the Secretary can alter the words of a bad-faith finding. The Governors argue that the judgment itself does not supply judicial relief for either asserted interest: it does not enforce good-faith bargaining, and it does not produce a compact or authority to game. Binding component findings would be insufficient, on their account, if actual relief still depends on a nonministerial executive determination. Brimson's enforceable order and Ferreira's distinction between adjudication and a commission require an answer to that description. Any rejection of the objection must identify the actual legal relief the judgment accomplishes, rather than assert finality without content.

The strongest **answer** is that a legal right to good-faith negotiations and access to the conditional statutory alternative can receive binding, partial judicial protection before every ultimate gaming term is fixed. Under §2710(d)(7)(B), a bad-faith finding starts specified periods and duties; nonagreement triggers mediation; the mediator must select the proposal best comporting with governing law and the court's findings and order; nonconsent in the following period triggers a Secretary who “shall prescribe” constrained procedures. A conclusive adjudication could thus affect legal relations and legally available next steps, although the State retains choices and nonjudicial actors retain implementation responsibilities. Aetna and Nashville require examination of that legal effect, rather than inference of nonjudicial character from absence of contempt alone.

Neither account is adopted here. Later analysis must determine whether those effects constitute actual partial relief and whether later discretion implements that relief or makes its legal effectiveness contingent. It must consider the Governors' objection that changing the remedial description would evade IGRA's selected compromise. A §2201 declaration requires a lawful procedural and substantive basis; §2202 is not an unlimited source of relief contrary to the governing statute. Conditional duty and a no-contempt construction likewise require statutory support. Constitutional avoidance cannot supply a missing remedy.

Keep the statutory constraints exact. The direct command to respect findings and order appears in (B)(iv); (B)(vii) instead refers to consistency with the selected proposal, IGRA and relevant state law. The latter's mandatory prescription duty is not unfettered discretion simply to do nothing. The text does not automatically select the Tribe's proposal if the State submits none. A court-dependent process cannot be replaced by an independent Secretary bypass after a jurisdictional dismissal without a separate lawful source. Section 2721 preserves remaining provisions but does not itself satisfy omitted triggers.

No favorable merits determination presently exists on this record: the separate September 1993 judgment for Florida and the Governor and its stayed appeal remain intact. No automatic preclusion, entitlement to particular games, binding effect against every future party, compact, license or new bad-faith finding follows from the sources. The Article III objection's asserted jurisdictional character may warrant consideration despite late presentation, but does not establish the separate Tenth Amendment question's preservation or authorize an independent mediator-appointment ruling. Actual Morgan Stanley/Plaut protects the completed, unappealed private securities judgment described in the addendum; its historical broader quotations cannot be imported as actual simulated law.

## Clear statement: independent narrower alternative

The complete Governors' pp19–22 add a genuine threshold alternative even though both lower courts found Congress's intent clear. Their argument is not merely that IGRA lacks the phrase “Eleventh Amendment.” They contend that State references could contemplate official defendants under Young, especially for prospective relief, leaving direct-State abrogation unnecessary; they also assert legislative silence about immunity. This is advocacy about possible statutory meaning and congressional assumptions, not a sourced finding of what Congress actually thought.

**Dellmuth, 491 U.S. 223, 227–232 (1989), requires unequivocal statutory text.** General federal jurisdiction, repeated State references, substantial state duties and the inference that States are logical defendants were insufficient in the EHA provisions then before the Court. Legislative history could neither replace missing textual clarity nor improve an already clear declaration. The Court assumed Congress's conceded §5 power; it did not decide Article I power. Its judgment concerned tuition reimbursement against the Commonwealth. Its name and official defendant do not transform the case into a ruling denying every prospective Young action.

**Scalia's concurrence, 491 U.S. at 233, rejects a magic-words reading.** He joined on the understanding that clear statutory subjection of States to monetary-damages suits could eliminate immunity without expressly mentioning sovereign immunity or the Eleventh Amendment. Brennan's dissent, joined by Stevens, would infer abrogation from text and structure and use history as confirmation. Blackmun separately finds the statute sufficient even under Atascadero. Stevens separately identifies the immunity involved as judicially created rather than the textual Eleventh Amendment's restriction and adheres to his Union Gas account.

For IGRA, a rigorous contrary argument must examine the **combined** express jurisdiction over an action arising from a State's negotiating failure, the evidentiary burden allocated to the State, and the court's commanded order to the State and Tribe. It cannot dismiss the text merely as scattered mentions of States. The statutory action, burdens and orders may give the references a role different from EHA's general review provision and program obligations. Conversely, calling those references clear does not answer the proposed official-capacity reading without analysis. The same-day historical Union Gas clear-statement judgment and its actual five-Justice statutory coalition, including Scalia, remain part of the comparison; its distinct constitutional coalition and plurality reasoning must not be conflated. No choice between these constructions is made in this note.

**Current-force qualification:** The education statute itself changed before divergence. Section 1403, added October 30, 1990, expressly removes State immunity for chapter violations occurring in whole or part after that date and makes legal and equitable remedies available as against other public entities. Therefore Dellmuth does not establish current 1996 immunity for those later IDEA violations. Its interpretation of the earlier text and its general clear-statement method remain useful. This qualification is separate from the 1986 §1415 preservation/exhaustion and fee amendments that limit use of Smith v. Robinson, already documented in the initial coverage note.
