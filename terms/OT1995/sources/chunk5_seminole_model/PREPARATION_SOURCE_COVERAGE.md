## Enforcement-route distinctions

**Sea Clammers, 453 U.S. 1, 13–21 (1981).** The Court declined an additional implied private action under FWPCA/MPRSA, then independently found their comprehensive enforcement mechanisms displaced §1983 enforcement. Its analysis examines actual citizen suits, notice, governmental actions, review routes, relief and savings language. It does not decide Young. The particular state agencies and directors were dismissed earlier and were not petitioners (n7); the separate tort-immunity questions were outside review (n12). Its §1983 reasoning does not place the burden on a plaintiff to prove preservation (n31).

Stevens accepts that a comprehensive scheme can evidence exclusivity, but insists that this is an aid to discerning actual legislative intent, not an end in itself, 453 U.S. at 27–31. He reads the savings language to preserve §1983 and distinguishes an express preexisting remedy from an action implied under the new statute. He nevertheless agrees no new private damages action is implied under these general-public environmental statutes. His position is not a claim that courts may invent every useful remedy.

**Smith v. Robinson, 468 U.S. 992, 1009–1021 (1984).** Its EHA-specific holding foreclosed bypass of the enacted education process through the overlapping equal-protection/§1983 and §504 routes, with distinct treatment of due process and fee-relatedness. Footnote15 preserves the assumed power to remedy constitutional deprivations; footnote17 does not equate a challenge to defective procedures with bypassing their substantive operation. O'Connor joined the Court. Stevens joined Brennan's dissent, which accepted exhaustion to preserve the EHA process but would preserve compatible remedies, including fees.

**Current-force qualification:** Do not state Smith's EHA exclusion or no-fee result as current 1996 EHA law. Congress's predivergence 1986 amendment added §1415(f), preserving constitutional, Rehabilitation Act and other federal disability-protection rights, procedures and remedies while requiring exhaustion for actions seeking relief also available under the EHA, to the same extent as an EHA action. It also added the separate fee provision. Smith's method remains discussed in Wilder; the decision and its dissent also provide published historical positions. Neither establishes a current general Young exclusion.

**Wilder, 496 U.S. 498, 508–523 (1990).** The Court separates an enforceable federal right from displacement of the enacted §1983 remedy and from a directly implied cause of action (n9). It finds a binding substantive Medicaid reimbursement right despite state discretion within the standard. On foreclosure, generalized federal funding oversight and limited state administrative review do not equal the comprehensive mechanisms of Sea Clammers or Smith. It requires express provision or other specific statutory evidence to show intended withdrawal of §1983. Stevens joined. O'Connor, Scalia and Kennedy joined the dissent's provision-specific concern that courts not replace the designated rate-setting process with their own; the dissent allows that an action might enforce the statutory process itself. Wilder's n4 reports the lower court's prospective-officer answer to immunity; the Court's presented holding is §1983 enforceability, not a separate universal Young holding.

**Young, 209 U.S. 123, 151–161 (1908).** There must be an actual connection between the officer and enforcement; that connection need not appear in the challenged enactment. Discretion cannot authorize unlawful enforcement, while directing discretionary affirmative action and enforcing a State's contract present distinct limits. Its application to a federal statutory good-faith negotiating duty needs additional analysis. The opinion does not resolve IGRA, a gaming entitlement, or statutory remedy displacement.

**Blatchford, 501 U.S. 775, 779–788 (1991).** No inherent tribal exception or mutual convention surrender; §1362 neither delegates the United States' sovereign exemption nor unmistakably abrogates. The Court does not decide Congress's Article I power, and leaves possible officer relief for remand. Scalia's opinion is joined by O'Connor, Kennedy and Souter among others. Stevens joins Blackmun's dissent disputing Hans/clear statement and emphasizing federal Indian-affairs authority. The majority's consent and clear-statement rulings cannot be transformed into a decision about unmistakable IGRA language and congressional power.
