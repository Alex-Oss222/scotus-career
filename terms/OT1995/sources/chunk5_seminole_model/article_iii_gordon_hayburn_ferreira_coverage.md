## Status and exact scope

- **Gordon v. United States, 69 U.S. (2 Wall.) 561 (1865):** The contemporaneous report records dismissal for want of appellate jurisdiction under the then Court of Claims arrangement, with Miller and Field dissenting. Reasons might be announced later; no reasoned opinion appears there. The later reporter note gives March 9, 1865 as the judgment date. Cornell reproduces this same short report.
- **Gordon, 117 U.S. 697–706 (appendix, published 1886):** At 697 the reporter explains that Taney prepared this paper before his death; it was delivered to the other Justices afterward and considered when the later judgment was reached. A proposed Court opinion was never prepared. The original paper was unavailable; an executor's examined copy was recovered and printed with surviving members' assent. This is not proof of a delivered, withdrawn majority opinion. At 698–699 the paper describes Secretary estimation and congressional appropriation as conditions of payment; at 702–703 it argues for a conclusive judicial judgment and execution rather than an opinion made operative only through another branch's future approval. At 706 the reporter records the 1866 repeal of section 14 and later appellate practice. The OCR's “2 Wall. 661” and “3865” are not separate citations/dates; use the independently verified 69 U.S. 561 report and the reported March 9, 1865 event.
- **Hayburn's Case, 2 U.S. (2 Dall.) 409–414 (1792):** The scan places the case heading at 409, after the end of another case on that same page. The report at 409–410 says the Attorney General's ex-officio motion was not allowed because the Court was divided; his renewed party-backed request was held under advisement and no merits decision was pronounced after legislative replacement of the procedure. The familiar separation-of-powers statements are circuit judges' communications printed in the reporter's note: New York 410; Pennsylvania 411–412; North Carolina 412–414. They object to the Secretary's suspension and legislative revision of purported judicial decisions. New York accepted a commissioners construction; Pennsylvania declined to act; North Carolina reserved whether individual commissioners service was authorized. The CAP body includes a short adjacent “Rule” entry between the disposition and its long note; it is preserved for source completeness and supplies no Hayburn holding. Long-s OCR, the printed date strings, and the adjacent entry should not be used for exact quotations without checking the scan.
- **United States v. Ferreira, 54 U.S. (13 How.) 40, 45–52 (1851):** Complete Court opinion plus the Chief Justice's note inserted by order of the Court at 52–53 were read. The designated judge acted as a claims commissioner, not an Article III court. Statutory procedure supplied no ordinary suit, parties, process or court record, and no debt arose on the judge's award; Treasury approval controlled liability (46–47). The Secretary's revision was valid within that nonjudicial scheme; the Supreme Court lacked appellate jurisdiction (47–48, 51–52). Exercise of judgment and discretion is not by itself constitutional judicial power (48). Ferreira expressly says Hayburn had no Supreme Court decision on the merits (49–50). It reserves the Appointments Clause question (51), so it cannot independently establish a mediator-appointment defect.
- **Yale Todd reporting in Ferreira, 54 U.S. at 52–53:** This is the Chief Justice's later account, inserted by Court order, of the 1794 recovery of pension payments. It expressly says no grounds opinion or dissent was recorded; its unanimity/reasoning descriptions are inferences. It also says the assumed original-jurisdiction basis had since been rejected. Do not turn this note into an unqualified 1794 reasoned Article III holding.

## Neutral relevance and limits

These sources substantiate the released executive-review/advisory-function branch; they add no independently different neutral ground. They require attention to what the statutory adjudication legally accomplishes, whether another actor can revise or withhold legal effect from it, and whether the actor is exercising a court's judicial authority or a separate commission. They do not alone decide whether binding partial relief followed by constrained administrative implementation is advisory. In particular, Taney's broad execution language must retain its writing status and statutory setting; it does not by itself settle the separately researched declaratory-judgment route. No historical post-divergence Plaut proposition was imported. Fresh public law through March 26 remains a parent-controlled prerequisite to modeling.
