QUESTIONS PRESENTED FOR REVIEW 


1. Whether the Due Process Clause of the United 
States Constitution permits res judicata (claim preclusion) 
or collateral estoppel (issue preclusion) to bar the re- 
litigation of taxpayers’ constitutional claims that were, or 
could have been, raised in prior litigation when the same 
claims are raised in subsequent litigation by taxpayers 
who, although not parties to the prior litigation, share an 
identity of interest with the taxpayers in the prior litiga- 
tion, attempt to prosecute the same cause of action, and 
have no discernible private right involved in that cause of 
action. 


2. Whether the Equal Protection Clause of the 
United States Constitution permits the Alabama Legisla- 
ture to authorize Jefferson County to impose an occupa- 
tional or license tax on those businesses, professions and 
occupations, and only those businesses, professions and 
occupations, not already required to pay such a tax un- 
der state law.

STATEMENT 


In 1988 the County Commission (the “County Com- 
mission”) of Jefferson County (the “County”) imposed a 
general license tax (the “Occupational Tax”) on natural 
persons pursuing any occupation within the County (sub- 
ject to certain exemptions at issue in this case) in an 
amount equal to 4% of each taxpayer's employment 
compensation. The Occupational Tax was levied by Ordi- 
nance No. 1120 (“Ordinance No. 1120”) (J.A. 34) 
adopted by the County Commission on September 29, 
1987, and was to take effect on January 1, 1988. In impos- 
ing the Occupational Tax, the County Commission relied 
on the authority of Act No. 406 (“Act No. 406”) (J.A. 33), 
enacted by the Legislature in 1967, and limited the tax in 


2 


accordance with the exemptions mandated by the act. 
This case arises from an action commenced by Petition- 
ers Jason Richards (“Richards”) and Fannie Hill (“Hill”), 

on behalf of themselves and other taxpayers similarly 
situated, contending that the exemptions from the Occu- 
pational Tax violated the equal protection clause of the 
United States Constitution. At the root of their claims are 
erroneous contentions (i) that there is no rational basis 
for the exemption of state-regulated professionals from 
the Occupational Tax and (ii) that a vast number of 
other people are exempt from the tax when, in fact, they 
are legally subject to the tax and have been paying it 
since its inception. 

A. The Nature of the Challenged Exemptions. It is 
regrettable that both the Petition for Writ of Certiorari 
and the Brief on the Merits submitted by the Petitioners 
portray the exemptions mandated by Act No. 406 and 
implemented by Ordinance No. 1120 as a “cockamamie” 
and “bizarre” (Pet. for Cert. 7) “crazy salad exemption 
scheme” (Pet. Merits Brief, Quest. I, p.i) that is design- 
edly devoid of all rationality. There are sound reasons, 
deeply embedded in both the historical and constitu- 
tional fabric of Alabama lawmaking, which explain and 
justify the exemptions challenged in this case. Unfortu- 
nately, these reasons were not adequately developed in 
the proceedings below because of the County's reliance 
on the preclusive effect of a prior judgment of the Ala- 
bama Supreme Court upholding the Occupational Tax, 
Bedingfield v. Jefferson County, 527 So. 2d 1270 (Ala. 1988); 
nor, perhaps, were they sufficiently brought to the atten- 
tion of this Court prior to its grant of the Petition for a 
Writ of Certiorari. Nevertheless, this Court has taken ju- 
risdiction of the Petitioners’ equal protection claims, and 
the record of the case, together with the applicable laws, 
ordinances and public records of Alabama and the 
County, provide a sufficient basis for the Court's disposi- 


3 


tion of those claims. In considering the claims, however, 
it is desirable for this Court to understand the relevant 
legislative background of Alabama’s business license 
taxes and Act No. 406. 


The class of taxpayers exempted by Act No. 406 from 
any county tax authorized by the act was precisely the 
class of taxpayers upon which the state had imposed li- 
cense taxes since 1927. Any taxpayer at any time sub- 
jected to a county tax pursuant to Act No. 406 was a 
taxpayer who had theretofore been fortunate enough to 
be exempt from any state or county business or occupa- 
tional license taxes in Alabama. The “exemption scheme” 
attacked by the Petitioners represents in large measure 
the categories of businesses and professions selected for 
taxation by the Legislature of Alabama when, in 1927, it 
enacted Act No. 163 (named after its sponsor, the 
“Tunstall Act”; Ala. Acts, 1927, p.139), which was the pre- 
cursor of Alabama's present business and professional 
license code. 

B. State Policies Reflected in the Tunstall Act and 
Successor License Acts. Included in the Appendix filed 
with this Brief is an excerpt from A History of Educational 
Finance in Alabama, 1819-1986, by Dr. Ira W. Harvey, Pro- 
fessor of Education at the University of Alabama in 
Birmingham (Auburn University, 1989). Resp. Appndx. 
A-194. As a distinguished academic and former Assistant 
Director of Finance and Administration for the Alabama 
Department of Education, Dr. Harvey is a recognized ex- 
pert on educational finance in Alabama and the related 
tax laws. Since the Journals of the Alabama Legislature 
record only actions taken without debate, and since there 
are no other official sources of legislative history, Dr. 
Harvey's research provides the only comprehensive 
record now available of how and why the Tunstall Act 
and the successor state license code—which constitute 
the legislative platform of Act No. 406—were enacted. 


4 


Alabama's financing of public education, compared 
with its sister states, is characterized by two anomalies: 
first, because of very low local property taxes, it finances 
its public schools with a relatively high percentage of 
state generated funds and, second, it safeguards those 
state funds for education by an elaborate system of con- 
stitutional and statutory earmarking that prevents their 
diversion for other governmental purposes. See Childree 
v. Hubbert, 524 So. 2d 336 (Ala. 1988). The main engine 
for the legal sequestration of educational taxes is the 
Alabama Special Educational Trust Fund (“ASETF”), to 
which all state taxes earmarked for public education are 
committed and from which all annual educational ap- 
propriations are made. The largest use by far of state tax 
revenues in Alabama is for public education, with the 
appropriations from the ASETF in the current fiscal 
year amounting to $3,331,000,000 (Ala. Acts, 1995, Act 
No. 95-748, p.1700), as compared with $862,000,000 
from the general fund for the other obligations of state 
government, including Medicaid, social welfare, prisons 
and law enforcement (Ala. Acts, 1995, Act No. 95-740, 
p. 1588). 

In addition to providing the precursor of the state's 
business and professional license code, the Tunstall Act 
in 1927 created the ASETF and the earmarking of educa- 
tional taxes therefor. The creation of the ASETF as an 
attempted solution to the then chronic underfunding of 
public education in Alabama is chronicled by Dr. Harvey. 
Resp. Appndx. A-194-202. As the result of public pressure 
and Governor Bibb Grave's adroit legislative strategy, the 
Tunstall Act was passed as a combination of (i) major 
business license taxes committed to the ASETF for public 
education and (ii) various minor business and profes- 
sional license taxes for the support of general govern- 
ment. Jd. A-200. The major business license taxes levied 
and earmarked for education included: taxes of 2.5% on 


5 


the gross receipts of intrastate business of railroads and 
the gross receipts of telegraph companies, telephone 
companies and express companies; taxes on the genera- 
tion of hydroelectric power; production taxes on coal 
mining, iron ore mining and other mineral extraction; 
and a 15% tax on the wholesale price of tobacco prod- 
ucts, Ala. Acts, 1927, pp. 139-148. The ASETF earmarking 
of such of these educational license taxes as remain in 
effect has been carried forward into present law. Ala. 
Code 1975, § 40-1-31. Dr. Harvey explains the combined 
purpose of the Tunstall Act as follows: 


In addition, this Act placed privilege and li- 
cense taxes upon insurance companies, accoun- 
tants, insurance adjusters, architects, attorneys, 
retail sales from railway cars, dentists, doctors, 
engineers, motor trucks, auto dealers and as- 
sorted other professional and business pursuits, 
including a tax of two cents per gallon on lubri- 
cating oil. The total revenue from this bill was 
estimated to yield about $4 million in its first 
year. Practically all of this revenue would be 
generated from those taxes earmarked to the 
Alabama Special Educational Trust Fund. 


Id. A-202. With hyperbole that can be understood only in 
light of Alabama's historically sullen resistance to taxa- 
tion, Dr. Harvey reports (ibid.) that Governor Graves cel- 
ebrated the passage of what, in retrospect, was an ex- 
tremely modest first step by proclaiming: 


This is Alabama's great day, her indepen- 
dence day. Today she is free and I thank God 
for this legislature that has done more for the 
rehabilitation of the state than any other in her 
history. (Montgomery Advertiser, July 15, 1927.) 


As detailed in Dr. Harvey's account of interrelated tax 
and educational legislation for the legislative quad- 
rennium beginning in 1935 (Resp. Appndx. A-245-260), 


6 


the ASETF and the license taxes levied by the Tunstall 
Act were modified and continued as part of a number of 
significant developments. A comprehensive revenue act 
was passed by the Legislature in 1935 (the “1935 Revenue 
Act”), which among other things (i) expanded the gen- 
eral revenue potential of the Tunstall Act by imposing 
state license taxes on a number of additional business 
and professional activities, (ii) levied personal and corpo- 
rate income taxes pursuant to Amendment No. 25, which 
had been ratified as part of the Alabama Constitution in 
1933, and (iii) adopted state taxation measures for banks 
and insurance companies that preempted their further 
taxation by counties and municipalities. Ala. Acts, 1935, 
No. 194, pp. 256-574. Driving the need for new revenues 
was the adoption in 1935 of long-needed laws for the 
improvement of public education, including a school 
minimum program, state provided textbooks, and 
supplemental appropriations for unpaid teachers’ sala- 
ries. Resp. Appndx. A-253-260. With relatively minor revi- 
sions since 1935, the business and professional license 
provisions of the 1935 Revenue Act have been continued 
in effect and constitute the present state license provi- 
sions that, as mandated exemptions from taxes levied 
pursuant to Act No. 406, give rise to the present case. 

Act No. 406, which was enacted when Alabama's Code 
of 1940 was in effect, authorizes the County “to levy a 
license or privilege tax upon any person for engaging in 
any business for which he is not required by law to pay 
any license or privilege tax to either the State of Alabama 
or the county by any of the following Article 1, Chapter 
20, Title 51; Sections 176, 177, 178, 180, 182, 183, 184, 
186, 429 and 826 in Title 51 of the Code of Alabama of 
1940 as amended”. ].A. 33. Article 1, Chapter 20, Title 51 
of the 1940 Code is now codified as Article 2 of Ala. Code 
1975, Tit. 40, Chap. 12, and for convenience has been 
reproduced in the Appendix to this Brief. Resp. Appndx. 


7 


A-17. The general provisions of the state license code 
relating to the levy and collection of the license taxes 
imposed by said Article 2, including the levy of “piggy- 
back” taxes for counties, have also been included in the 
Appendix. Ala. Code 1975, Tit. 40, Chap. 12, Art. 1; Resp. 
Appndx. A-l. The separate state license taxes levied by 
the aforesaid Article 2—which constitute the bulk of the 
license taxes listed by the Petitioners to create the im- 
pression of the “crazy salad exemption scheme”"—gener- 
ally correspond to those taxes originally levied for gen-

bama., It is significant that the Legislature has persisted 
for more than sixty years in denying to counties the 
power to levy license taxes on professions regulated by 
the state. Under Alabama's constitutional structure, 
counties are regarded as instrumentalities of the state 
subject to direct legislative control, while municipalities, 
by contrast, are to a great degree constitutionally insu- 
lated from legislative intrusion. The primary instrument 
for legislative control of the counties is the enactment of 
local laws, or population bracket general laws of local 
application, that in either case apply to a single county. 
This structure is a reflection of times, prior to the mod- 
ern state, when the counties were the only local agencies 
through which state government could act.‘ 


‘The political jurisdiction of a county includes all municipalities 
located within its geographical territory.

On the other hand, with respect to legislative control 
of municipalities, the Alabama Constitution contains two 
powerful limitations on the use of local, special or private 
laws: first, Section 104 absolutely prohibits the Legisla- 
ture from passing local, special or private laws relating to 
a number of subjects, among which are the incorpora- 
tion of any municipality (Ala. Const., § 104(5)) and the 
alteration of the government of any municipality (/d. 
§ 104(18)); second, Section 105 prohibits the Legislature 
from passing any local, special or private law in any case 
that is provided for by a general law. As a consequence of 
historical legislative rules and practices, laws applying to 
a single county or other political subdivision are easier to 
pass than general laws. Thus, the independence of mu- 
nicipalities from the Legislature is protected by the rela- 
tive difficulty of trying to govern their affairs through the 
passage of general laws. 

Because of this constitutional structure and legislative 
appreach, counties in Alabama are governed by local 
laws to a much greater degree than are municipalities. 
Municipal governments are controlled by general laws, 
while county governments can be customized to legisla- 
tive taste by local laws. See Baldwin County v. Jenkins, 494 
So. 2d 584 (Ala. 1986). The same dichotomy of munici- 
pal independence versus county dependence is pre- 
sented by Alabama’s laws with respect to local taxation. 
For example, all municipalities in the state are empow- 
ered to levy a general sales tax, parallel to the state sales 
tax, for such purposes and at such rate as may be deter- 
mined by the municipal governing body. Ala. Code 1975, 
§ 11-51-200 et seg. By contrast, all counties depend upon 
local laws (or population bracket general laws of local 
application) for their authority to levy sales taxes, and, in 
the case of the County's sales tax, both the rate of the tax 
and the purposes for which the proceeds may be used

SUMMARY OF ARGUMENT 


It is well established that a prior judgment obtained by 
private citizens should be given preclusive effect with re- 
spect to subsequent litigation by different private citizens 
if the prior litigation does not involve any private inter- 
ests, the right sought to be enforced is a public right, and 
the people are regarded as the real party in interest. 
Privity between the prior litigants and those precluded 
from litigation of the same public issue is created by their 
identity of interest in the public issue. The equal protec- 
tion claims raised by the Bedingfield plaintiffs concerning 
the exemptions from the Occupational Tax caused such 
claims to be decided in Bedingfield, even though the trial 
court and the Alabama Supreme Court failed to discuss 
the claims in their opinions. The Petitioners cannot 
claim any private right with respect to their equal protec- 
tion claims that differentiate them from the Bedingfield 
plaintiffs or any other taxpayers, and this identify of in- 
terest creates the necessary privity to preclude further 
litigation of their equal protection claims in this case. 

Legislative bodies such as the County hold broad 
power — to be exercised in their discretion — to make 
classifications for tax purposes. Such is the deference due 
that exercise of legislative discretion that a tax-related 
classification will not be disturbed by this Court if there is 
any conceivable basis on which to sustain it. That rule is 
well-established, has often been applied by this Court, 
and is controlling in this case. 


Although the taxpayer bears the heavy burden of elimi- 
nating every conceivable basis that may lawfully have ani- 
mated the challenged legislation, the Petitioners have of- 
fered the Court only inapposite judicial authority and 
conclusory assertions in pursuit of that burden. Richards 
has not identified any act of invidious discrimination, nor 
has he offered relevant authority or analysis, to support 
and explain his assertions. 


26 


Finally, there are a number of conceivable bases legiti- 
mizing the classification decisions attacked. The estab- 
lishment of the Occupational Tax involved, essentially, 
only a judgment to tax all natural persons not already so 
taxed. The Legislature acted with reference to an exist 
ing set of circumstances, and they acted reasonably in 
light of those circumstances. Additionally, the business 
and professional licensing tax law developed reasonably, 
with a plausible purpose behind every aspect of it. Fi- 
nally, the distinction made between state-regulated pro- 
fessionals and other employees in Jefferson County, in 
addition to being a classical exercise of the discretionary 
classification power, can be justified from several conceiv- 
able perspectives, including the decision of the state to 
retain exclusive regulatory control over the learned pro- 
fessions, as well as other professions historically regulated 
by the state.

The broad, discretionary power of legislatures to make 
classifications applies in all areas of social and economic 
policy, but “in taxation, even more than in other fields,

legislatures possess the greatest freedom in classifica-

Complementing the broad latitude accorded the state 
in establishing taxation classifications is the very limited, 
very deferential, standard of judicial review. “[E}very pre- 
sumption should be indulged in favor of the constitution- 
ality of the legislation.” Bradley v. City of Richmond, 227 
U.S. 477, 485 (1913). The presumption must be honored 
“if any state of facts reasonably can be conceived that 
would sustain it, and the existence of that state of facts at 
the time the law was enacted must be assumed.” Rast v. 
Van Deman & Lewis Co., 240 U.S. 342, 357 (1916). “It is 
not the function of [the] Court in cases like the present 
to consider the propriety or justness of the tax, to seek 
for the motives, or to criticize the public policy which 
prompted the adoption of the legislation.” State Board of 
Tax Commissioners v. Jackson, 283 U.S. 527, 537 (1931). 
Instead, as stated in Madden v. Kentucky, 309 U.S. 83, 88 
(1940), “the presumption of constitutionality can be 
overcome only by the most explicit demonstration that a 
classification is a hostile and oppressive discrimination 
against particular persons and classes.”

foregoing principles. For example, the Court has upheld:

(1885); a law requiring a license fee for grain warehouses 
and elevators situated on railroad right-of-ways, but not 
for grain elevators and warehouses differently situated, 
W.W. Cargill Co. v. Minnesota, 180 U.S. 452 (1901); a law 
imposing a tax on the business of hiring persons to labor

B. The decisions upon which the Petitioners rely turn on 
factors not present in this case. 

Having failed to find a single favorable decision of this 
Court that is materially on point, the Petitioners have 
been forced to rely on case law that is inapposite because 
the outcomes were governed by considerations not 
present in this case. In each of those cases, for any of 
several reasons, the Court engaged in more sweeping 
scrutiny—albeit under what was stated to be a rational- 
basis test—than is appropriate in cases, such as this, in- 
volving local occupational tax legislation. 

The equal protection clause does not exist in a 
vacuum, its protection against discrimination varies sub- 
stantially in force and effect depending on the circum- 
stances underlying the challenged discrimination, the na- 
ture of the benefit or burden that is the subject of dis- 
crimination, the manner in which the discrimination is 
created and applied, and the rationale underlying the 
discrimination. The existence of differing degrees of 
scrutiny under the equal protection clause—strict, inter- 
mediate, and rational-basis—confirms the Court's recog- 
nition that the rights protected are considered to be of 
varying importance. Strict and intermediate scrutiny have 
relatively limited application to certain suspect classifica- 
tions or to fundamental rights of great importance in a 
free society. On the other hand, the degree of scrutiny 
applied under the rubric of “rational-basis” may vary dis- 
cernibly according to the interests at stake and the na- 
ture of the classification. “|T]|he standard of rationality as 
we have so often defined it must find some footing in the 
realities in the subject addressed by the legislation.” 
Heller, 113 S. Ct. at 2643. Thus, the “rational-basis” in- 
quiry will differ in scope—and result—according to the 


41 


context; rational reasons for classifications based on oc- 
cupation may be considered irrational, or illegitimate, 
for classifications based on state of residence. 


The concept of a fluid rational-basis standard is exem- 
plified by the Court’s decision in Metropolitan Life Ins. Co. 
v. Ward, 470 U.S. 869 (1985), on which the Petitioners 
rely. Ward involved a challenge to Alabama's practice of 
levying a higher gross premiums tax on out-of-state insur- 
ance companies than that levied on domestic insurance 
companies. The trial court, affirmed by the Alabama Su- 
preme Court, upheld the taxation scheme on the basis, 
inter alia, that the disparity of classification was intended 
to further the legitimate purpose of encouraging the for- 
mation of new insurance companies in Alabama. On re- 
view in this Court, a majority reversed and remanded the 
Alabama Supreme Court's decision. The Court held 
“that promotion of domestic business within a State,” if 
accomplished by discriminating against foreign corpora- 
tions, “is not a legitimate state purpose.” 470 U.S. at 880. 
The Court reached that conclusion while at the same 
time agreeing “with the holding in Allied Stores (358 U.S. 
528] that “a State’s goal of bringing in new business is 
legitimate and often admirable.” 470 U.S. at 879. 

Despite the occasional disclaimer to the contrary, this 
Court's jurisprudence clearly recognizes and applies vary- 
ing levels of rational-basis scrutiny—depending on the 
right implicated or the classification made. When the 
constitutionally-protected and fundamental “right to 
travel” is involved for example, the Court's “cases leave 
no doubt that it will tigger intensified equal protection 
scrutiny.” Zobel v. Williams, 457 U.S. 55, 66 n.1 (1982) 
(Brennan, J., concurring). At the other end of this spec- 
trum, there are local economic regulations. “When local 
economic regulation is challenged solely as violating the 
Equal Protection Clause, this Court consistently defers to 
legislative determinations as to the desirability of particu- 


42 


lar statutory discriminations.” Zobel, 457 U.S. at 81 
(Rehnquist, J., dissenting). 

Each of the cases upon which the Petitioners rely in- 
volved factors—either the nature of the classification 
made or the constitutional significance of the rights im- 
plicated—that required closer rational-basis scrutiny than 
would be applicable to run-of-the-mill occupational taxes 
such as the one involved here. Those cases do not repre- 
sent an application of the rational-basis standard as lim- 
ited as that which this Court has consistently applied in 
cases addressing social and economic matters implicating 
no constitutional interest or policy other than the equal 
protection clause—the right to be treated the same as 
others in the applicable class. As we have seen, the result 
in Ward was directly attributable to the discrimination 
against residents of other states, an abrogation of consti- 
tutional policy not alleged in this case. 

The decision in WHYY, Inc. v. Borough of Glassboro, 393 
U.S. 117 (1968), is equally inapposite, and for the same 
reason. In that case, the Vourt condemned, on equal 
protection grounds, a refusal to grant an out-of-state,

though addressed under the rubric of rational-basis 
equal protection, the case turned on the different and 
less favorable treatment of the out-of-state, nonprofit cor- 
poration “solely because of [its] foreign incorporation.” 
Id. at 120.

C. The classification determining the natural persons who 
would be subject to the Occupational Tax does not violate 
equal protection. 

The equal protection claims in this case appear to be 
based only on assertions that the Occupational Tax and 
its exemptions are “cockamamie.” At bottom, this claim 
seems to be nothing more than a bald assertion that 
Richards ought not to be required to pay occupational 
tax if anyone else is exempt therefrom. This Court re- 
solved that issue over a hundred years ago in Bell's Gap. 


This Court has repeatedly held that only “clear and 
hostile discriminations against particular persons and 
classes” are forbidden by the equal protection clause. 
E.g., Heisler v. Thomas Colliery Co., 260 U.S, 245, 255 
(1922); Lehnhausen v. Lake Shore Auto Parts Co., 410 U.S. 
356 (1973). The equal protection clause forbids only dis- 
tinctions or discriminations that are “palpably arbitrary” 
or “insidious.” The County enjoys a presumption of con- 
stitutionality that “can be overcome only by the most ex- 
plicit demonstration that a classification is a hostile and 
oppressive discrimination against particular persons and 
classes.” Madden, 309 U.S. at 88. 

Because Richards has failed to suggest that any action- 
able disparity between similarly situated people results 
from the County’s classification, the equal protection 
analysis need go no further. However, there are plausible 
reasons for the configuration of the Occupational Tax. 

As explained in the Statement, the County’s taxing au- 
thority is derived from Act No. 406 of the Alabama Legis- 
lature. The most fundamental reason for the classifica- 
tions is that the Legislature, acting well within its discre-

tion, concluded that the natural candidates for county 
business and occupation taxes were businesses and natu- 
ral persons not already subject to an occupation or busi- 
ness tax assessed by the state or the County. The broad 
group of employees that the County could ultimately tax 
already existed as a classification—the class of employees 
then exempted from existing state and county taxes. Un- 
der those circumstances, a more logical and reasonable 
classification can scarcely be conceived. The Court, in 
fact, has on several occasions noted with approval that a 
challenged classification was predicated on a classifica- 
lion already in existence. A “[vJalid reason” for an excise 
tax statute establishing two classificetions of utilities for 
tax purposes “appears from the fact that the [chal-

established” under New York law. New York Rajid Transit 
Comp. v. City of New York, 303 U.S. 573, 580 (1938). To like 
effect is FCC v. Beach Communications, 113 $.Ct. at 2103 
(“Congress borrowed [the challenged classification | 
from pre-Cable Act regulations, and although the exist-

necessary to the rationality of the statute, it is plausible 
that Congress also adopted the FCC's earlier rationale.”). 
The Court addressed circumstances quite like those of
