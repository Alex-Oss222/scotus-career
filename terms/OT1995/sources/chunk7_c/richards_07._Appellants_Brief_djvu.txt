QUESTIONS PRESENTED FOR REVIEW 


Does the convoluted exemption scheme described in 
Alabama Justice Maddox’ dissenting opinion — attacked 
for the first time ever in the case at bar — warrant reversal 
of the Alabama Supreme Court's decision in this case on 
federal equal protection grounds? 

We contend that it does, because neither the State of 
Alabama nor Jefferson County can advance any rational 
interest served by the crazy salad exemption scheme built 
into the Jefferson County occupational tax. 


Can Alabama, through interpretation of its state case 
law pertaining to res judicata, deny plaintiffs’ never before 
presented, and never before heard, federal constitutional 
claims, thus denying plaintiffs due process of law? 


We contend that it cannot, and that the Alabama Su- 
preme Court's reversal of a denial of summary judgment 
below — unless reversed by this Honorable Court — will 
effectively deny these plaintiffs any opportunity to ever 
be heard upon meritorious federal constitutional claims. 
We contend that because no party to a prior proceeding, 
Beddingfield v. Jefferson County, 527 So.2d 1270 (Ala. 1988), 
which went to judgment before the effective date of the 
tax, had ever paid it, no one in Bedingfield, supra, could 
have launched the exemption scheme attack made here.

STATEMENT OF GROUNDS OF JURISDICTION 


The jurisdiction of this Honorable Court is invoked 
pursuant to 28 U.S.C. § 1254 because this Petition is from 
a decision of the Supreme Court of Alabama in a civil case. 
(i) Date of the decision: March 31, 1995 
(ii) Application for Rehearing Overruled: June 9, 1995 
(iii) No cross petition has been filed. 

(iv) Jurisdiction is based on 28 U.S.C. § 1254(1) and Rule 
10.1(c) of the Rules of the Supreme Court. 
(v) Writ of Certiorari granted November 22, 1995. 


STATEMENT OF THE CASE 


(1) Nature of Case, Course of Proceedings 
and Disposition 

On May 20, 1988 the Alabama Supreme Court decided 
Bedingfield v. Jefferson County, 527 So.2d 1270 (Ala. 1988), 
rejecting three separate challenges to the county occupa- 
tional tax here in issue on state law grounds. (R. 231) The 
same three state law issues had been decided adversely to 
the Bedingfield challengers in a state trial court, prompting 
that appeal. (R. 265) Bedingfield, supra, was nota class action, 
and did not decide any federal question in the trial or 
appellate court. A final judgment in the Bedingfield case was 
entered three weeks before the tax became effective. (R. 
340) 

Neither the plaintiffs nor the defendant in the Bedingfield 
case ever sought to add any additional parties or to make 
their case a class action, though Alabama’s class action 
practice is well established. Rule 23, Alabama Rules of Civil 
Procedure. 

Bedingfield , supra, was decided on these three esoteric 
state law issues: (1) Whether Jefferson County's occupa- 


tional tax violated the intent of its own enabling act; (2) 
Whether the Alabama Constitution was violated by the 


2 


enabling act's “bracket bill” status (in Alabama, a “bracket 
bill” is one which addresses counties by population “brack- 
ets” rather than by name), or by the enabling act’s similarity 
to “special, private or local laws” forbidden by the state 
constitution, Constitution of Alabama, 1901, §§ 105, 106, 110, 
Amendments 375, 389; and (3) Whether the enabling act 
violated § 104(15) of the Constitution of Alabama, 1901, 
which prohibits local laws from regulating the assessment 
or collection of taxes. (R. 231) The Circuit Court for 
Jefferson County decided all three issues in Jefferson 
County’s favor on December 11, 1987— three weeks before 
the January 1, 1988 effective date for collection of the tax. 
(R. 265) Both Bedingfield decisions are before this Court in 
the joint appendix. They reveal a sweeping victory for 
Jefferson County on all three narrow state law issues, both 
in the trial court and before the Alabama Supreme Court. 
After the Bedingfield appeal, Jefferson County began collect- 
ing its occupational tax. 

On April 21, 1992 Jason Richards and Fannie Hill filed a 
class action complaint in state court, attacking the exemp- 
tion scheme built into the same county occupational tax. 
Hill and Richards claimed that the exemption scheme built 
into the tax denied them equal protection of the laws under 
the Fourteenth Amendment to the Constitution of the 
United States. 


A motion for summary judgment, based upon a defense 
of res judicata, claimed to have resulted from the Bedingfield 
decision, was filed by Jefferson County in the trial court on 
June 4, 1992. (R. 218) The trial court considered what the 
Bedingfield case was, and was not, in ruling on Jefferson 
County’s motion. (R. 414) Bedingfield was not an attack on 
the exemption scheme built into the occupational tax 
(indeed, until the county began collecting its occupational 
tax, no one had standing to complain about exemptions 
from taxation). No federal constitutional claims were de- 
cided by either court in Bedingfield , and Bedingfield did not 


3 


involve attempts by any party to join or effect service of 
process upon any individuals other than the parties plaintiff 
and defendant. (R. 414) Neither Jason Richards nor Fannie 
Hill nof any member of the class they represent were given 
notice of any kind — actual or constructive — that any claim 
they might wish to pursue under the Constitution of the 
United States, after collection of the tax began, could or 
would be in any way impaired by the Bedingfield case. (R. 
265) 

In the instant case, on April 8, 1993, Jefferson County was 
granted summary judgment upon all state law claims, but 
summary judgment was denied as to petitioners’ federal 
constitutional claims. (R. 414) Jefferson County and an 
intervenor tax recipient, Birmingham-Jefferson Civic Cen- 
ter Authority, then appealed the trial court’s denial of 
summary judgmentas to these federal constitutional claims 
to the Alabama Supreme Court. (R. 418) 


The Alabama Supreme Court reversed the trial court on 
March 31, 1995 and overruled an application for rehearing 
on June 9, 1995. The dissenting opinion in the Alabama 
Supreme Court made it clear that no claim remotely similar 
to these was presented to, heard by, or decided by either the 
trial court or the Alabama Supreme Court in Bedingfield , 
supra. But five justices of the Alabama Supreme Court 
interpreted this Court's decision in Heiser v. Woodruff, 327 
U.S. 726 (1946), to compel preclusion of the federal claims 
made by Richards and Hill. 


Richards and Hill filed a timely Petition for Writ of 
Certiorari in this Court, and on November 22, 1995 the writ 
was granted.

well as the record on appeal, the Alabama Supreme Court

ARGUMENT 


I. JEFFERSON COUNTY’S TAX EXEMPTION SCHEME 
VIOLATES THE EQUAL PROTECTION CLAUSE 
GUARANTEES OF THE FOURTEENTH AMEND- 
MENT BECAUSE IT IS NOT EVEN MINIMALLY RA- 
TIONAL. 


Ten years ago, this Court held that when a tax exemption 
scheme distributes benefits conferred by a state unequally, 
the distinctions made therein are subject to scrutiny under 


9 


the Equal Protection Clause of the Fourteenth Amend- 
ment. Hooper v. Bernalillo County Assessor, 472 U.S. 612 
(1985). New Mexico, by statute, had exempted from its state 
property tax $2,000 of the tax value of property owned by 
honorably discharged veterans who served on active duty 
during the Vietnam war, but had limited application of this 
exemption to veterans who were New Mexico residents 
before May 8, 1976. Hooper qualified for the exemption in 
all other respects, but failed to establish a residence in New 
Mexico until 1981. He applied for the $2,000 exemption, 
nevertheless, and was denied it based upon his newcomer 
status. After losing in his efforts to invalidate this discrimi- 
natory exemption feature on equal protection grounds 
before the Bernalillo County Assessor, the County Valua- 
tion Board and the New Mexico Court of Appeals, Hooper 
was granted certiorari here. This Court wasted no time 
reversing all three New Mexico decisions upholding the 
exemption scheme because there was simply no rational 
state interest served by its clear relegation of Vietnam 
veterans arriving after May 8, 1976 to a status of “second 
class citizens.” 472 U.S. at 623. New Mexico's contention 
that its exemption scheme rewarded “its own” citizens with 
gratitude for their honorable military service was rejected 
here, and this Court held that if a statutory tax exemption 
scheme cannot pass the minimum rationality test set forth 
in Zobely. Williams, 457 U.S. 55 (1982), equal protection is 
violated and its inquiry ends. 472 U.S. at 623. 

Zobel, supra, involved a statutory scheme for the distribu- 
tion of state mineral deposit income, in the form of divi- 
dends, to all adult residents of Alaska. Mr. and Mrs. Zobel 
objected to the distribution scheme, a dividend unit plan, 
because it allowed adult residents one “unit” for each year 
of residency subsequent to 1959 — the first year of Alaskan 
statehood. Because they moved to Alaska in 1978, the 
Zobels insisted that the dividend distribution plan was 
unfairly discriminatory, and violated the guarantees of the 


10 


Equal Protection Clause of the Fourteenth Amendment. 
They claimed it could not pass this Court's minimum 
rationality test. 457 U.S. at 61. After an exhaustive analysis 
of its minimum rationality precedents, a majority of this 
Court tersely held: 


In our view Alaska has shown no valid state 
interests which are rationally served by the distinc- 
tion it makes between citizens who established 
residence before 1959 and those who have be- 
come residents since then. We hold that the Alaska 
dividend distribution plan violates the guarantees 
of the Fourteenth Amendment. 


—457 U.S. at 65 


In so doing, this Honorable Court made it clear that 
Alaska’s only apparent justification for the retrospective 
aspect of its dividend unit program, “favoring established 
residents over new residents,” was constitutionally unac- 
ceptable, citing Viandis v. Kline, 412 U.S. 441 (1973). See 
also, Shapiro v. Thompson, 394 U.S. 618 (1989). 

Tax assessments, rather than exemptions, were the sub- 
ject of a similar analysis in this Court in Allegheny Pittsburgh 
Coal Company. Webster County, 488 U.S. 336 (1989). There, 
West Virginia citizens experiencing recent sales of property 
were assessed in a manner determined by this Court to 
involve “gross disparities” when compared to their neigh- 
bors whose properties had not recently sold. A trial court in 
West Virginia found that the Webster County Assessor 
systematically and intentionally discriminated against re- 
cent sale property owners by imposing a tax rate often 
thirty-five times higher than the rate imposed upon their 
similarly situated neighbors without any other justification 
for that distinction. It therefore concluded that the scheme 
was in violation of the Fourteenth Amendment's Equal 
Protection Clause. The Supreme Court of West Virginia 
reversed. This Court granted certiorari and again reversed, 


11 
based upon the Court of Appeals’ failure to recognize the 
comparative aspects of Allegheny Pittsburgh's complaint. 
488 U.S. at 342. Citing Hillsborough v. Cromwell, 326 U.S. 620, 
623 (1946), this Court held that the Equal Protection 
Clause protects an individual from state action which se- 
lects him out for discriminatory treatment by subjecting 


him to taxes not imposed on others of the same class. 488 
U.S. at 345. 

Applying similar logic, this Court examined a “special 
use” permit requirement within a zoning scheme in Cleburne 
v. Cleburne Living Center, Inc., 473 U.S. 432 (1985). There, a 
group home determined by the city to be a “hospital for the 
feeble minded” was attacked by a group home owner as 
violative of equal protection. A federal district judge in 
Texas upheld the city’s right to discriminate against the 
home and its proposed inhabitants because it found that 
mental retardation conferred neither suspect nor quasi- 
suspect status upon them. 473 U.S. at 437. The Fifth Circuit 
reversed, based upon its conclusion that mental retarda- 
tion warranted at least quasi-suspect classification and, 
therefore, higher scrutiny in the district court. This Court 
granted certiorari because it disagreed with the Fifth Circuit's 
classification analysis. Although mental retardation was 
held not to confer quasi-suspect status upon the Cleburne 
Living Center's residents, this Court nevertheless held 
classification status was of no consequence because the City 
of Cleburne’s zoning scheme could not pass the minimum 
rationality test applied in Hooper and Zobel, supra. U.S. 
citizenship alone was held to be sufficient, under the Four- 
teenth Amendment, to invoke minimum rationality protec- 
tion, as follows: 


The State may not rely on a classification whose 
relationship to an asserted goal is so attenuated as 
to render the distinction arbitrary or irrational.

We have no quarrel with Alabama's definition of the four 
necessary elements for a claim preclusion, or res judicata, 
defense: 


(1) a prior judgment on the merits, (2) ren- 
dered by a court of competent jurisdiction, (3) 
with substantial identity of the parties, and (4) 
with the same cause of action presented in both 
suits. 


or with its leap, if and when all four elements are met, to this 
Honorable Court's conclusion in Heiserv. Woodruff, 327 U.S. 
726, at 735, that “[i]n general a judgment is res judicata not 
only as to all matters litigated and decided by it, but as to all 
relevant issues which could have been but were not raised 
and litigated in the suit”. We do, however, disagree sharply 
with its reliance upon Heiser v. Woodruff, supra, for the 
proposition that these wholly different parties, prosecuting 
a wholly different cause of action (which could not have 
been litigated in Bedingfield), are bound thereby. Heiser, 
supra, involved an attempt to re-litigate in bankruptcy court 
a fraud claim twice litigated — once by a bankrupt and once 
by his trustee. It did not address claims by strangers to a

previous action (like Richards and Hill) which could not

Our quarrel is with the Alabama Supreme Court’s failure

consider that the Richards and Hill attack on the exemp-
