COMPLAINT 
INTRODUCTION 

1. The matter in controversy arises under the Indian Gaming 
Regulatory Act, Pub. L. No. 100-497, 25 U.S.C. §§ 2701 to 2721. 

2. This action arises from the failure of the State of Florida 
to negotiate in good faith to conclude a tribal-state gaming 
compact as required by the Indian Gaming Regulatory Act, 25 
U.S.C. § 2710(d)(3). 

JURISDICTION 

3. The plaintiff Seminole Tribe of Florida (hereinafter the 
“Tribe”) invokes the jurisdiction of this court pursuant to Section 

13a 
Complaint 

1l(d)(7)(A) of the Indian Gaming Regulatory Act, 25 U.S.C. 
§ 2710(d)(7)(A). The jurisdiction of this court is also invoked 
pursuant to 28 U.S.C. §§ 1331 and 1362. Plaintiff also seeks 
declaratory relief pursuant to 28 U.S.C. § 2201. 

PARTIES 

4. The plaintiff Seminole Tribe of Florida is a federally 
recognized Indian tribe with a constitution, governing body, and 
by-laws, duly organized under Section 16 of the Indian 
Reorganization Act, 25 U.S.C. § 476, 48 Stat. 984. Seminole 
Tribal headquarters are in Broward County, Florida. The 
Seminole Tribe of Florida occupies and is recognized as 
possessing powers of self-government over its tribal Indian 
lands. The Tribe conducts gaming operations under the Indian 
Gaming Regulatory Act on such lands, which were acquired in 
trust by the United States prior to October 17, 1988 as required by 
25 U.S.C. § 2719 to qualify as the situs of tribally conducted 
gaming. 

5. The defendant State of Florida, pursuant to the Indian 
Gaming Regulatory Act, is the entity which must negotiate in 
good faith with the Seminole Tribe to conclude a compact 
governing Class III gaming. 

6. The defendant, Lawton Chiles, is Governor of the State of 
Florida and, by authority of that office, is responsible for 
negotiations with the plaintiff Tribe pursuant to the Indian 
Gaming Regulatory Act. 

BASIS OF THE CLAM 

7. The Indian Gaming Regulatory Act was enacted in 1988. 

l4a 

Complaint 

The Act’s express purpose was to provide a federal statutory basis 
for operation of gaming by Indian tribes on Indian lands for the 
purpose of promoting economic development and strong tribal 
government The Indian Gaming Regulatory Act provides a 
comprehensive federal regulatory scheme to determine whether a 
specific type of gaming will be permissible on Indian lands. Class 
I games (social games or traditional forms of Indian gaming) are 
not subject to regulation. Class II games (Bingo and similar to 
bingo games as well as certain card games) are subject only to 
tribal regulation under the oversight of the National Indian 
Gaming Commission. Class III games (all other games) are 
permitted only if conducted “in a state that permits such gaming 
for any purpose by any person, organization, or entity,” in 
conformance with a tribal-state compact. 25 U.S.C. §§ 2703, 
2710(d). 

8. The Indian Gaming Regulatory Act requires that, upon a 
tribe’s request, a state must enter into good faith compact 
negotiations for the conduct of Class III gaming by the tribe. 25 
U.S.C. § 2710(d)(3)(A). 

9. The Indian Gaming Regulatory Act makes tribally 
proposed Class III gaming the mandatory subject of a compact 
negotiation between a tribe and that state if the state permits such 
gaming “for any purpose by any organization or entity.” 25 
U.S.C. § 2710(d)(1)(B). 

10. The State of Florida has evolved a broad public policy 
favoring the conduct of Class III gaming in a variety of 
circumstances. The State, by statute, expressly authorizes play of 
the following games of chance which fall within the category of 
games designated by Indian Gaming Regulatory Act as Class III 
games: (1) parimutuel betting on horse racing; (2) parimutuel 

15a 
Complaint 

betting on dog racing; (3) parimutuel betting on Jai Alai; (4) off 
track betting at parimutuel site, (5) poker and (6) a state-operated, 
state wide lottery, utilizing on-line computer gambling devices, 
which may be operated in an unlimited variety of forms selected 
by the State Lottery Department. 

11. The State also knowingly permits charities to operate 
Class III games of chance including craps, blackjack, roulette, 
and raffles at charitable fund raising events, variously called 
“Casino Nights,” “Monte Carlo Nights,” “Las Vegas Nights” or 
other similar terms. 

12. The State also permits the use of computers and other 
electronic gambling devices in the sale of lottery tickets and the 
selection of numbers played by the public in the lottery, in the 
operation of Jai Alai and in the playing of bingo at state 
authorized bingo halls. 

13. On January 29, 1991, James E. Billie, Chairman of the 
Seminole Tribe, wrote to defendant Governor Lawton Chiles 
requesting commencement of negotiations between the Tribe and 
the State of Florida for entry into a tribal-state compact providing 
for the conduct by the Tribe of Class III gaming activities on 
Seminole tribal Indian lands pursuant to the Indian Gaming 
Regulatory Act, Pub.L. 100-497, 25 U.S.C. §§ 2701 to 2721. 
(Exhibit A) 

14. In early February, 1991, Jim Shore, General Counsel to 
the Tribe, received a telephone call from J. Hardin Peterson, Jr., 
the Governor’s General Counsel, saying that Governor Chiles 
had authorized him to represent the Governor’s Office in 
negotiating a compact and inviting the Tribe to submit a proposed 

- compact for review. This was confirmed by Mr. Peterson’s letter 

16a 
Complaint 

dated February 15, 1991. (Exhibit B) In both the telephone 
conversation and the letter, Mr. Peterson stressed that the 
compact negotiations could be concluded very simply and 
without difficulty. 

15. On March 4, 1991, Mr. Shore, representing Chairman 
James Billie, submitted a specific form of proposed compact 
providing for tribal operation of poker, as well as any and all 
video, electronic and computer aided games which duplicate 
poker, bingo, pull-tabs, lotto, punchboards, tip jars, instant bingo 
or other games similar to bingo. In his accompanying letter, Mr. 
Shore indicated that the Tribe was making a “conservative” 
request in order to “make it possible for the compact to be 
favorably concluded in a short time.” (Exhibit C) 

16. The State did not respond to the Tribe’s March 4 letter 
until nearly three months later when, by letter dated May 24, 
1991, the Governor’s representative, Mr. Peterson, rejected all 
but one of the Tribe’s compact requests and advised the Tribe that 
the only game proposed by the Tribe which could be the subject of 
a compact under Florida law was the card game of poker. (Exhibit 
D) 

17. On June 18, 1991, Chairman Billie wrote to Governor 
Lawton Chiles to express the Tribe’s dissatisfaction with the 
content of the Peterson May 24 letter and to request the personal 
involvement of the Governor in the compact negotiations. That 
letter also responded to the State’s position by submitting 
additional games for consideration in the negotiations that 
appeared to meet some of the objections raised in the May 24 
Peterson letter. At the same time, the Tribe requested that 
negotiations be expanded to include casino gaming. (Exhibit E)

18. On June 25, 1991, the Tribe submitted a ten-page legal 
memorandum providing specific support for the compact 
provisions it had submitted to Mr. Peterson and Governor Chiles. 

19. On July 25, 1991, Mr. Peterson wrote to Chairman Billie 
on behalf of Governor Chiles (Exhibit F) and explained his May 
24 letter as follows: 

I regret that you were disappointed at the 
content of my letter of May 24, 1991, but it 
was simply my intent to advise the Seminole 
Tribe of my understanding of the 
requirements of the Indian Gaming 
Regulatory Act and to provide the Tribe with 
some views expressed to me by the Office of 
the Attorney General. 

20. On August 22, 1991, representatives of the Seminole 
Tribe met with State representatives to discuss the Tribe’s 
pending compact request. At this meeting, the State expressed 
willingness to negotiate concerning poker and other card games, 
raffles and parimutuel wagering on dog and horse racing and jai 
alai. Mr. Peterson, however, repeated his earlier written assertion 
that the State would not negotiate for any form of gaming that was 
not expressly allowed by Florida state law and announced that the 
State refused to negotiate compact provisions for any machine 
gaming (video, electronic or computer aided games) or for any 
form of casino gaming. 

21. At a final meeting with Tribal representatives on 
September 17, 1991, representatives of the State of Florida again 
reasserted the State's position that machine gaming and casino 
gaming are not subject to the Indian Gaming Regulatory Act 
compact negotiation requirement. 

18a 
Complaint 

22. The Indian Gaming Regulatory Act required the State 
and its Governor to respond in good faith to the Tribe’s request to 
negotiate within 180 days. 25 U.S.C. § 27 10(d)(7)(B)(i). 

23. More than 180 days have expired since the Tribe's 
January 29, 1991 request that the State enter into negotiations for 
a tribal-state gaming compact. 

24. The State and its Governor have failed to respond in 
good faith to the Tribe’s request for compact negotiations and 
have not conducted those negotiations in good faith. Specifically, 
they have failed to give good faith consideration to the Tribe’s 
request for inclusion of various forms of machine gaming and 
casino gaming in the compact negotiation. They have maintained 
that position even though the proposed tribal games are 1) 
consistent with the State’s broad public policy favoring the 
conduct of Class III gaming in a variety of circumstances, and 2) 
are specifically comparable to specific forms of gaming that the 
State itself either expressly authorizes by law or allows to 
operate. Despite the Tribe’s stated readiness to negotiate for the 
purpose of entering into a compact, the State and its Governor 
have refused to enter into any negotiation for inclusion of such 
gaming in a tribal-state compact. They have thus violated the 
Indian Gaming Regulatory Act requirement of good faith 
negotiation. 

PRAYER FOR RELIEF 
WHEREFORE, plaintiff prays this honorable court to: 
1. Order the State, acting through the Governor, and the 

Tribe pursuant to 25 U.S.C. § 2710(d)(7)(B){iii) to conclude a 
tribal-state compact governing the conduct of gaming activities 

19a 
Complaint 

on Seminole Indian lands within a sixty-day period from entry of 
the Order, and appoint a mediator to resolve any impasse in 
accordance with 25 U.S.C. § 2710(d)(7)(B)(iv). 

2. Enter a declaratory judgment that the Indian Gaming 
Regulatory Act, 25 U.S.C. §§ 2701 to 2721, by reason of the State 
of Florida’s permission for the operation “for any purpose by any 
person, organization or entity” of games described in paragraphs 
10-12 above, obligates the State of Florida to negotiate in good 
faith with the Tribe regarding the conduct of any or all Class III 
games of chance on the Seminole Indian lands, including use of 
machines in the operation of those games, and including casino 
gaming. 

3. Award plaintiff the costs of this action. 
4. Order such other and further relief as the court deems just.

With regard to other games referenced in the proposed compact, 
we believe that § 2710(d) (1) (B) would prevent negotiation of a 
compact for the operation of banking card games. Although 
§ 849.085, Florida Statutes, does permit the paying of poker,

gambling devices found in § 849.231, Florida Statutes, would 
prevent the playing of banking card games traditionally using a 
blackjack table or a “shoe or box” for the dealing of cards. 

Our conclusion is that the only game which Florida law allows 
and therefore which can be the subject of compact negotiations is 
poker. This letter is, therefore, the State’s response, pursuant to 
25 U.S.C. § 2710(d)(3)(A), to the Tribe’s request for a negotiated 
compact for poker or any other game permitted by § 849.085, 
Florida Statutes. 

Upon review of the proposed compact, the proposed State 
authority to inspect and regulate the permitted games and the 
environment in which they are played is insufficient. Because of 
the case-intensive nature of these games, the possibility of 
criminal infiltration of the operation requires a substantial State 
presence. We suggest inclusion of provisions for: State licensing 
of employees, including criminal background investigations; 
State law enforcement and criminal court jurisdiction; regulation 
and background checks for providers to the Tribe; unlimited free 
access for State regulators and/or law enforcement officer; and 
rules for bet limits and rakeoffs. 

In addition to the aforementioned criminal enforcement 
concerns, the State would require provisions addressing the 
following issues: health and safety code applicability; regulation 
of alcoholic beverages; and tort remedies for patrons. All of the 
issues raised in this letter have been the subject of compacts we 
believe are appropriate for inclusion in this compact.
