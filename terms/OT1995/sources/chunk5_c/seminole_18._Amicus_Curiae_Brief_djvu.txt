I. Interest of Amici Curiae 


This brief of amici curiae National Indian Gaming Asso- 
ciation, Minnesota Indian Gaming Association and Califor- 
nia-Nevada Indian Gaming Association (hereinafter the 
“Associations”), is filed in support of petitioner Seminole 
Tribe of Florida pursuant to the written consent of all 
parties. The Associations are national and regional organiza- 
tions representing the interests of federaliy recognized tribal 
governments that depend on gaming under the Indian Gam- 
ing Regulatory Act, 25 U.S.C. sections 2701-2721 


2 


(“IGRA” or the “Act’’) to fund education, care for the 
elderly, housing, health care and other tribal governmental 
programs. Congress anticipated that in order to achieve the 
Act’s goals of tribal self-sufficiency and funding for impor- 
tant governmental projects, tribal gaming activities would be 
pursued in, and have an impact on, interstate commerce. 
Congress recognized the effects Indian gaming would have 
on interstate commerce and enacted IGRA, in part, to 
address those effects. Amici will also demonstrate that, just 
as Congress expected, Indian gaming in practice has had a 
significant impact on interstate commerce. 


The success of tribal governmental gaming depends in 
large part on the tribes’ ability to engage in what the Act 
classifies as “class III” gaming, which is clearly the most 
economically competitive form of gaming available.’ Con- 
gress provided, however, that in order to engage in class III 
gaming a tribe must either reach a Tribal-State compact or 
determine through court action that the state has chosen not 
to avail itself of the opportunity to regulate class III gaming. 
Amici share a concern that Congress’ carefully-crafted bal- 
ance of tribal and state interests, which conditions class III 
gaming on either achieving Tribal-State compacts — with

social or traditional games, and is solely regulated by Tribes. See 25 
U.S.C. §§ 2703(6), 2710(a) (1). Class I] gaming consists of bingo and

not be frustrated by the Eleventh Amendment barrier erro- 
neously erected by the Eleventh Circuit’s opinion in this 
case, reported at 11 F.3d. 1016 (11th Cir. 1994). The lower 
court held that Congress lacks authority to abrogate the 
states’ sovereign immunity to provide the judicial supervi-

In erroneously determining that Florida could invoke its 
sovereign immunity to upset the delicate balance wrought 
by Congress in IGRA, the Eleventh Circuit incorrectly held 
that IGRA was enacted exclusively under the Indian Com- 
merce Clause, and that Congress lacked the power under 
that clause to abrogate state immunity. While the Indian 
Commerce Clause doubtlessly supports IGRA, and amici 
agree with petitioner Seminole Tribe that the Indian Com- 
merce Clause alone empowers Congress to abrogate state 
sovereign immunity where necessary to carry out its consti- 
tutional duties regarding Indian affairs, the Act makes clear 
that Congress was also acting pursuant to its powers over 
interstate commerce. An analysis of that power provides 
further support to petitioner’s position, as well as supplying 
an independent basis for upholding the Act’s class III 
compacting process. 


Il. Summary of Argument 


Congress enacted IGRA, in part, pursuant to its powers 
under the Interstate Commerce Clause, and Tribes are in 
fact engaged in such commerce. Congress was explicitly 
concerned in IGRA with the tribes’, states’ and commercial 
gaming industries’ interstate economic interests and impact. 
The Act anticipates that tribes will use the mails, tele- 
phones, broadcasting and other technologies in furthering 
the Act’s goals of promoting tribal self-sufficiency. Indeed, 


4 


congressional hearings and other forums that have examined 
the impact of tribal governmental gaming under IGRA are 
replete with examples of the Act’s effect on interstate 
commerce. Those effects include: major purchases and sales 
of food and other goods which typically move in interstate 
commerce; dependency on transportation programs which 
utilize common carriers and highways; extensive employ- 
ment for persons who commute from far beyond reservation 
boundaries with corresponding reductions in reliance on 
state and federal welfare programs; and dramatic upswings 
in tourism and visitors crossing state lines to enjoy tribal 
facilities. IGRA also addresses the need and means for 
combating organized crime,’ which subject this Court has 
repeatedly held implicates interstate commerce. IGRA’s 
references to the gaming industry generally and the reputa- 
tion and integrity of those who participate in it, its focus on 
controlling organized crime, as well as the substantial im- 
pact on commerce which Indian gaming actually has, more 
than amply evidence congressional intent to legislate over 
matters affecting interstate commerce. 


For all of these reasons, the Associations submit that 
Congress enacted IGRA, in part, under the Interstate Com- 
merce Clause. Amici suggest that the Eleventh Circuit’s 
contrary holding was erroneous, and respectfully request 
that the Court reverse the Eleventh Circuit’s opinion and 
judgment.

Il. Argument 


A. Congress Enacted the Indian Gaming Regulatory Act, 
in Part, Pursuant to its Powers Under the Interstate 
Commerce Clause 


The Eleventh Circuit erroneously concluded that Con- 
gress did not enact IGRA pursuant to its plenary powers 
over interstate commerce. Specifically, the court found that 
“alleviating a supposed burden on interstate commerce”’ was 
not among Congress’ purposes in enacting IGRA. Seminole 
Tribe of Florida v. Florida, 11 F.3d 1016, 1025-26 (11th 
Cir. 1994). In reaching this conclusion, the lower court 
misapprehended the broad scope of congressional power 
under the Interstate Commerce Clause. Congress’ power 
over interstate commerce is not liinited to alleviating pre- 
existing burdens thereon, as the Eleventh Circuit assumed. 


The Interstate Commerce Clause gives Congress the 
power to legislate with respect to all “commerce which 
concerns more states than one.” Gibbons v. Ogden, 22 U.S. 
(9 Wheat.) 1, 194 (1824). The power generally allows 
Congress to reach at least three categories of problems: 


First, the use of channels of interstate or foreign com- 
merce which Congress deems are being misused, as, for 
example, the shipment of stolen goods (18 U.S.C. secs. 
2312-2315) or of persons who have been kidnaped (18 
U.S.C. sec. 1201). Second, protection of the instru- 
mentalities of interstate commerce, as for example, the 
destruction of an aircraft (18 U.S.C. sec. 32), or 
persons or things in commerce, as, for example, thefts 
from interstate shipments (18 U.S.C. sec. 659). Third, 
those activities affecting commerce. 


Perez v. United States, 403 U.S. 146, 150, 91 S.Ct. 1357, 
1359 (1971). 


6 


When Congress regulates under the Interstate Commerce 
Clause, it only needs a rational basis for finding that the 
regulated activity substantially affects interstate commerce, 
and the means it employs need only be reasonably adapted 
to the ends sought. Hodel v. Virginia Surface Min. & Recl. 
Ass'n, 452 U.S. 264, 101 S.Ct. 235 (1981). The “substantial 
economic effect” test is easily satisfied, even where the 
regulated activity is seemingly only local in nature. See 
Wickard v. Filburn, 317 U.S. 111, 63 S.Ct. 82 (1942) 
(regulation of wheat grown by a farmer wholly for home 
consumption was within Congress’ interstate commerce 
power because it supplied the grower’s needs which other- 
wise would have been satisfied by purchases in the open 
market). See also United States v. Ohio, 385 U.S. 9, 87 
S.Ct. 66 (1966); (per curiam) United States v. Haley, 358 
U.S. 644, 79 S.Ct. 537 (1959); (per curiam) Bender v. 
Wickard, 319 U.S. 731, 63 S.Ct. 1326 (1943) (per curiam); 
Beckman v. Mall, 317 U.S. 597, 63 S.Ct. 199 (1942) (per 
curiam).

primary motive when legislating under that power as is 
evident from the cases upholding the Civil Rights Act of 
1964.° In Heart of Atlanta Motel v. United States, 379 U.S.

depend on recitals of the power which it undertakes to exercise.” Woods

Employment Opportunity Commission v. Wyoming, 460 U.S. 226, 243 
n.18, 103 S.Ct. 1054, 1064 n.18 (1983); Fullilove v. Klutznick, 448 U.S. 
448, 476-78, 100 S.Ct. 2758, 2773-2774 (1980). In United States v. 
Darby, 312 U.S. 100, 61 S.Ct. 451 (1941), “a class of activities was held 
properly regulated by Congress without proof that the particular intra- 
state activity against which a sanction was laid had an effect on 
commerce.” Perez, 402 U.S. at 152, 91 S.Ct. at 1360. In Darby, the 
Court recognized that “Congress has sometimes left it to the courts to 
determine whether the intrastate activities have the prohibited effect on

Numerous aspects of IGRA reveal a sufficient nexus to 
commerce to bring the Act within Congress’ interstate 
commerce powers. First, Congress expressly contemplated 
interstate gaming activity in IGRA. Second, in enacting 
IGRA, Congress balanced the competing economic inter- 
ests of Indian Tribes, the states, and the commercial gaming 
industry. Third, IGRA regulates both Indian and non- 
Indian economic concerns. Fourth, Indian gaming has a 
substantial effect on interstate commerce, a fact that was 
well-known to Congress when it enacted IGRA. Finally, 
Congress enacted IGRA, in part, to assure that the nation-

gaming.

Activity in IGRA

take. Simultaneous games participation between and 
among reservations can be made practical by use of 
computers and telecommunications technology as long 
as the use of such technology does not change the 
fundamental characteristics of the bingo or lotto games

interstate commerce nature of IGRA, and the error com- 
mitted below in rejecting those factors.

with issues that arise under its Interstate Commerce Clause 
authority. The Eleventh Circuit Court of Appeal’s conclu- 
sion that respondent State of Florida is entitled to a judg- 
ment of dismissal because there was no exercise of that 
authority was in error and should be reversed, and the case

HISTORY OF US GAMING 


Origins of U.S. Gaming 


The United States is in what has been referred to as the 
“third wave” of legalized gaming, in that this is the third 
time that gaming, in one form or another, is available 
virtually everywhere in America. The first wave of gaming 
began in the colonial era and lasted through the Civil War. 
The second wave began after the Civil War and lasted 
through the early twen#eth century when most forms of 
gaming throughout the U. S. were banned. The third wave 
began in 1964, with the passage of legislation legalizing a 
lottery in New Hampshire. 


Lotteries dominated the first wave and were used to help 
finance the colonization of America. As such colonization 
took place, the popularity of the lotteries increased and they 
were used to finance projects too massive for traditional 
government or private financing sources. Institutes of higher 
learning such as Harvard and Princeton were financed in 
part from tax proceeds generated by lotteries. 


The nation’s first lotteries were considered respectable 
and not a form of gaming, but rather, a voluntary form of tax 
collection patronized by the most respectable citizens. By 
1831, lotteries were sponsored by cight states and wagers 
exceeded $66 million, five times the annual federal budget. 
By the time the Civil War started, scandals and swindles 
plagued the lotteries and a wave of morality swept the 
nation. This soon led to the abolition of lotteries, and the 
first wave of legalized gaming receded. 


The second wave of legalized gaming followed the Civil 
War. Lotteries made a comeback and other forms of gaming 
proliferated. This comeback was the strongest in the Recon- 
structed South as a means of dealing with the extreme 
financial difficulties that resulted from the war. Gaming also 


l3a 


thrived in the frontier cities of the West and in most major 
urban areas. 


During the 1840s and 1850s, the gold and silver strikes in 
California and Nevada caused an influx of miners and others 
into those territories. Gaming became an integral part of the 
boomtown atmosphere. Gaming also thrived in the cities of 
San Francisco, Kansas City and Denver, all of which were 
known as gaming capitals in the 1850s. During this period, 
anti-gaming legislation was passed but was not effective and 
only served to drive gaming underground where it was 
tolerated if not supported by law enforcement officials. 


However, the second wave did not persist, and by 1910, 
the year Nevada.banned casinos, legal gaming was virtually 
non-existent in the U.S. By the 1930’s casinos had returned 
to Nevada and horse race wagering had returned to twenty- 
one states. Lotteries were not legalized again until 1964 
when such activity was legalized in New Hampshire, and, 
according to some experts, this ushered in the third wave of 
legalized gaming in America. 


Despite a weak economy the gaming industry in the U.S. 
has exhibited an impressive growth rate. The morality argu- 
ment has lessened as governments and churches become big 
players. The industry has become a huge economic and 
social force. Players are demanding faster and easier games. 
This fundamental value shift is currently changing the form 
gaming takes across the country. Gaming is legal in one 
form or another in every state (including the District of 
Columbia) except Utah and Hawaii. Land based casino 
gaming (including casinos on Indian Reservations) is of- 
fered in 20 states. In addition, Indian tribes in Florida and 
New Mexico operate electronic games such as video poker 
without compacts because the states have refused to negoti- 
ate with the tribes. Excursion boat (riverboat gaming) is 
legal in Illinois, Indiana, Iowa, Louisiana, Missouri, and 
Mississippi. Every other state with a major waterway, or 


l4a 


lake is considering riverboat gaming. 38 states (including 
Washington D.C.) permit lotteries and remaining jurisdic- 
tions where gaming is legal offer various forms of pari- 
mutuel wagering.

HISTORICAL REVIEW OF INDIAN GAMING 


Introduction 


American Indian Nations embrace a distinct culture, 
language and spirit. Casino gaming has been referred to 
many as the “return of the buffalo”. The buffalo was used by 
the American Indian people for total subsistence, food, 
shelter, clothing, children’s games, and trading. 


Gaming has replaced the buffalo as the mechanism used 
by American Indian people for survival. Today, the proceeds 
from gaming are used by Indian Nations for subsistence, 
cultural preservation, and to replenish impoverished econo- 
mies. Not surprisingly, the spread of Indian Gaming has 
spurred controversy. As Indian casinos became more suc- 
cessful, competitors have lodged charges of unfair advan- 
tages and organized crime infiltration. During a 1992 Senate 
hearing on Indian Gaming, Paul Maloney, senior counsel! at 
the Department of Justice, testified that a special investiga- 
tion uncovered no evidence of organized crime infiltrating 
Indian gaming. In all cases of crime or robbery, Tribal 
governments have brought the crime to justice. Opponents 
accuse Indians of exploitation; that they are using gaming as 
a means to provide short-term financial growth and enor- 
mous benefits. Proponents refer to the intent of IGRA when 
allegations of exploitation are raised. 


The primary intent of IGRA is to “provide a statutory 
basis for the operation of gaming by Indian tribes as a means 
of promoting tribal economic development, self sufficiency, 
and strong tribal governments”. Under IGRA, all revenues 
from gaming operations are required to be used solely for 
governmental or charitable purposes. Gaming revenues are 
used to build infrastructure, schools, hospitals, roads, sewers, 
and water systems and to fund health care and education. 


Many reservations do not have sufficient resources and 
are located in undesirable regions that cannot support devel- 


23a 


opment. These tribes have become dependent on federal aid, 
as attempts at economic development have failed prior to 
the advent of Indian gaming. 


Government aid provided to Indians often result in bene- 
fiting local communities. This is due to the fact that there is 
a scarcity of Indian businesses and high unemployment, 
sometimes as high as 80%. A significant portion of the 
federal dollars are therefore spent outside the reservation. 


The lack of a vital economy on reservations makes it 
nearly impossible to improve tribal conditions. This hopeless 
environment and constant struggle help to explain the high 
incidence of alcoholism and suicide, and the lack of ade- 
quate health care.in many tribal communities. According to 
the 1990 Census, the poorest counties in the United States 
are located on Indian Reservations. There are also 3,200 
counties in the United States. Of the Poorest 1% (32 
counties), 7 are located on Sioux reservations in South and 
North Dakota. In addition, several other Indian Reserva- 
tions in various counties in the United States were also listed 
amongst the 32 poorest counties. 


More recently, gaming has enabled a few tribes to im- 
prove their situations considerably. The profits are used to 
build road and water systems and healthcare centers, pro- 
vide job training scholarships and both home and business 
loans to tribal members. More tribes will benefit from 
gaming with the proliferation of Indian gaming operations. 
Currently, there are 547 tribes of which only 79 (14.4%) 
have Class III gaming. 


Legislative and Regulatory Issues 


Indian Tribes are sovereign governments. They have ex- 
isted as sovereign governments long before Europeans set- 
tled in North America. Treaties signed with settlers in 
exchange for land guaranteed the tribes continued ability to 
be recognized and treated as sovereign entities. However, 


24a 


historically state governments have been hostile in recogniz- 
ing Indian Tribes as independent entities. 


Under the constitution of the United States, the federal 
government is obliged to protect tribes from states, who 
have often coveted the Indian’s lands and assets, and sought 
to impose power over Indian tribes and people. 


Several landmark court cases have confirmed this recog- 
nition of Indian sovereignty. These court cases include, 
Cherokee vs. Georgia, 1831, and California v. Cabazon, 
1987. The Indian Gaming Regulatory Act (IGRA), passed 
by Congress in 1988, affirms that tribes have the power to 
conduct gaming on Indian lands subject to tribal/state 
jurisdiction. 


Indian Gaming was conducted on many reservations 
throughout the US prior to the passage of Indian Gaming 
Regulatory Act (IGRA). This legislation was passed in 
respo%¢ to a 1987 federal court decision “California versus 
Cabazon” which stated that tribes could engage in forms of 
gambling that were not expressly prohibited by the state in 
which the tribe is located. The “California versus Cabazon” 
case and US supreme court finalized the distinction in 
which the court applied a criminal/prohibitory — 
civil/regulatory distinction. In other words, if a state regu- 
lates gaming, in any form, then gaming falls under civil law 
for which Indian nations cannot be prosecuted and the state 
is obligated under federal law to enter into compact negotia- 
tions with the tribe. If a form of gambling sought by a tribe 
is expressly prohibited by the state under criminal law, then 
the state can refuse negotiations for particular games on that 
basis. The Cabazon case solidified issues surrounding gam- 
ing on tribal lands which had previously been questioned. 


The purpose of IGRA was to create a balance between 
the tribe’s right to conduct gaming on their reservations and 
the public interests of the states in which the tribes are 


25a 


located. IGRA became effective on October 17, 1988. The 
goals of the act include tribal economic development, self- 
sufficiency and a strong tribal government. The act provides 
a framework by which the games are conducted to protect 
both the tribes and the state. The act created three classes of 
gaming and allocated regulatory jurisdiction over each class 
among tribal, federal, and state sovereigns. 


¢ Class I — includes social games solely for prizes of 
minimal value or traditional forms of Indian gaming 
engaged in by individuals and is subject to exclusive 
tribal regulatory jurisdiction. 


¢ Class II — includes all forms of bingo and other games 
similar to bingo, such as pull-tabs and punch-boards 
(electronic or non-electronic), provided these are 
played in the same location as bingo games. Class II 
also refers to non-banking games that either expressly 
allowed or not expressly prohibited by state law and 
banking card games in existence in the specified states 
of Michigan, North Dakota, South Dakota, Montana, 
and Washington State on or before May 1, 1988. 
Class II gaming is subject to tribal regulatory jurisdic- 
tion, with extensive oversight by the National Indian 
Gaming Commission (NIGC). 


¢ Class II] — includes all forms of gaming that are not 
Class I or II, including slots, casino games, banking 
card games, tiorse and dog racing, pari-mutuel wager- 
ing and jai-alai. Class III gaming is lawful on Indian 
lands only if the gaming has been authorized by a tribal 
ordinance approved by the chairperson of the NIGC 
and such gaming is permitted by the state for any 
purpose by any person, organization, or entity, and is 
conducted in conformance with a tribal-state compact 
entered into by the tribe and the state that is in effect. 
The tribal-state compact entered into by the tribe was 
proposed by the Nevada congressional delegation on 


26a 


behalf of the states. The jurisdiction over class III 
gaming is left to the tribal-state compact negotiation. 


The sovereignty of American Indian tribes was limited by 
IGRA which required a tribe to negotiate a compact with 
the state. Under IGRA, a tribe must request a state to enter 
into compact negotiations. Once a request is made, the state 
has an obligation to negotiate in “good faith” to enter into a 
compact. The compact must also be approved by the Secre- 
tary of the Interior. Jurisdiction is provided to the federal 
district court concerning the following: 


. Any cause of action by a tribe arising from a state’s 
failure to conduct negotiations in good faith. 


° An action by a state or a tribe to enjoin any class III 
activity conducted in violation of the compact. 


* Any cause of action initiated by the Secretary of the 
Interior to enforce the Secretary’s authority under 
IGRA to enforce mediation procedures. 


Today, there have been law suits because some states 
have failed to live up to IGRA and negotiate in “good faith” 
with tribes. There have been a number of landmark cases 
prompted by the proliferation of Indian Gaming. These 
cases have clarified gaming issues that IGRA addressed. 
Some of the major cases are summarized below: 


Mashantucket Pequot Tribe versus 
State of Connecticut, 199] 


This decision involved the first interpretation of IGRA. 
The tribe filed suit in accordance with IGRA requiring the 
state of Connecticut to negotiate terms for operating games 
of chance on its reservation. The state agreed that “its 
limited authorization of the conduct of Las Vegas nights by 
nonprofit organizations does not amount to a general allow- 
ance of such (casino-style) gaming as the tribe would 
institute, and further that such gaming activity is contrary to 


27a 


the State’s public policy.” The court determined that the 
issue was whether the form of gaming sought by the tribe 
violated the State’s public policy. 


The court reiterated what was stated in the Cabazon case. 
“If the intent of state law is generally to prohibit certain 
conduct, it falls within the areas of criminal jurisdiction, but 
if the state law generally permits the conduct at issue, 
subject to regulation, it must be classified as 
civil/ regulatory.” 


The court reasoned that since the state permits certain 
casino style games at charity events, the Mashantuckets use 
was within the meaning of IGRA, and not against public 
policy of the state. The court held that the state was 
required to enter into a compact with the Pequot tribe and 
that the games conducted in Las Vegas nights were subject 
to negotiations as were the rules of conduct for these games. 


Lac Du Flambeau Band of Lake Superior Chippewa 
Indians Versus Wisconsin, 1991 


In this case the tribe filed suit against the state under 
IGRA because the state failed to negotiate in good faith for 
a gaming compact. The court concluded that “the state is 
required to negotiate with the plaintiffs over the inclusion in 
a tribal state compact of any activity that includes the 
clement of prize, chance, and consideration and that is not 
prohibited expressly by the Wisconsin Constitution. The 
State is required to negotiate casino games such as video, 
slot machines, and blackjack with the tribes because the 
activities are permitted under Wisconsin law within the 
meaning of IGRA.” 


The Cabazon case finalized the legal distinction between 
criminal/prohibitory and civil/regulatory. In this case it was 
determined that Wisconsin’s gaming structure was regula- 
tory in nature. The central issue in this case was whether the 
state “expressly permits” the type of gaming which the tribe 


28a 


was secking to conduct. The Federal court said that the 
“inquiry” is whether Wisconsin prohibits those particular 
gaming activities. It does not.” 


Seminole Tribe of Florida vs. Lawton Chiles, Governor of 
the State of Florida, 1994 


This case was first filed in the Southern District of Florida 
on September 19, 1991. The Seminole Tribe brought suit 
against Lawton Chiles, Governor of Florida, to remediate 
the alleged failure of the State of Florida to conduct good 
faith negotiations for the tribe to establish gaming opera- 
tions on their land. The State of Florida dismissed the 
claims on two grounds, 1) Gaming activities proposed by 
the Seminole Tribe are not conducted in the State of 
Florida. 2) Pursuant to the Eleventh Amendment, Congress 
does not have the constitutional power to enforce “good 
faith” requirement of the compact process by explicitly 
providing the Tribe a judicial remedy against the state. 


On June 18, 1992, the final ruling sided with the Seminole 
tribe. The Florida Federal Judge ruled that the Eleventh 
Amendment was not a justifiable defense by the states. The 
Tribe’s central argument against Florida’s rebuttal was that 
IGRA abrogated the State’s immunity when it enacted 
IGRA. This ruling was important in that it found that 
Congress has the power to abrogate State immunity. Subse- 
quently, interlocutory appeal ensued. The appeal resulted in 
a finding that Congress did not have the power to abrogate 
state immunity, but the tribes should have remedy in “bad 
faith” cases. Therefore, the Secretary of the Interior was 
directed to implement procedures for the tribes in lieu of a 
compact with the states. 


The State's Use of the 10th and 11th Amendments 


Congress included a provision in IGRA that the states 
and tribes negotiate a compact for those games being 


29a 


considered by the tribe. The states have responded to IGRA 
by using the 10th and 11th amendments to block the 
establishment of Indian Gaming Facilities. The state’s basic 
Strategy is to refuse to negotiate all or certain kinds of 
Class III games (such as slot machines, card games, pari- 
mutuel racing etc.). If a tribe feels the state has failed to 
negotiate in good faith, IGRA says that the tribe can sue the 
state for failure to negotiate. 


The states have been using the 10th amendment as a 
defense because it reads the powers not delegated to the 
federal government by the constitution are reserved for 
individual state governments. The state therefore cannot be 
compelled by the federal government to act on anything that 
is not stated in the constitution. This is disputed by the 
tribes. 


States have been using the 11th amendment as a defense 
when tribes file suit against them for allegedly negotiating in 
bad faith. The 11th amendment says that a state cannot be 
sued by individuals based on federal legislation, that a state 
has immunity from such lawsuits unless congress makes it 
clear that immunity does not apply. One response according 
to Jerry Strauss, an attorney representative for several tribes 
is “Congress has the power to abrogate state immunity and 
nobody disputes that Congress intended to do that when 
writing IGRA.” 


When the tribe seeks an appeal by taking the state to 
court the state dismisses the suit on the grounds of sovereign 
immunity as defined in the 11th amendment. The amend- 
ment bars suit by individuals against a state to enforce rights 
conferred by federal legislation, in the absence of clear 
congressional intent to authorize such suits.

REVIEW OF ECONOMIC IMPACT STUDIES 


Introduction 


There have been several studies which show the positive 
effects of Indian Gaming on reservations and the economy. 
The Indian Casinos stimulate economic growth due to direct 
and indirect impacts. For example, direct impacts are the 
wages paid to employees as well as the purchases from 
suppliers. For purposes of illustration, let us review the 
economic impact of the wages paid to employees of the 
casino. The casino employees have the means to consume 
and they will therefore spend a portion of their payroll on 
goods and services both on and off the reservation. The 
producers of these goods now have extra incomes and they 
will in turn spend a portion of their income. This process will 
continue. Thus an endless chain of secondary consumption 
and respending is set in motion by the primary investment 
(in this example the casino employees’ wages.) This is what 
is referred to as the multiplier effect. Spending stimulates 
additional or induced spending in addition to employment. 
There are other positive economic effects such as decrease 
in unemployment and public assistance expenditures. 


Although different methodologies have been used there 
are a number of common findings which are revealed in a 
majority of the studies: 


* Increases in tribal revenues to support Indian govern- 
ment services, economic development, and self- 
sufficiency 

* Increases in employment (Indian and non-Indian) 

* Reduction in public assistance expenditures 

* Creation of induced spending and induced employment 


¢ Increases in the number of out-of-state visitors which 
stimulates visitor spending 


¢ Increases in tax revenues

only benefit the Indian community but the surrounding 
regions as well. As will be explained in several of the studies, 


more often than not the casinos employ a higher percentage

gaming employees are non-Indians.

employment created by the opening of the Foxwoods High

creating jobs in all of Connecticut.

figures tend to drop.

PUBLIC ASSISTANCE TRENDS| 


Job Creation and Public Assistance Relationships 


A common economic development strategy is to develop 
industries and jobs in areas considered depressed. The 
rationale for this strategy is that creating a job in a depressed 
area will have a relatively greater benefit than creating a job 
in an economically progressed area. Depressed areas of a 
state or county will traditionally rely on and absorb compar- 
atively more public assistance than a metropolitan area 
relative to their individual contribution te the state’s 
economy. 


The significance of the Tribal gaming industry in Minne- 
sota is that a majority of the facilities, by virtue of being 
located on Indian reservations, are located in rural counties 
and distributed throughout the state. Consequently, the 
introduction of Tribal gaming has not only led to the 
creation of many jobs, but jobs which have a substantially 
greater economic benefit than would be the case in the 
urban areas. 


Thus, when analyzing the benefits of the Tribal gaming 
industry, changes in demand for public assistance were 
considered. 


Analysis Approach 


Data pertaining to public assistance spending within the 
state was obtained from the State Department of Human 
Services. After considering the major public assistance pro- 
grams, the Aid to Families with Dependent Children 
(AFDC) program was selected due to its significance in the 
overall public assistance environment. In the state of Min- 
nesota, the AFDC program currently serves 63% of the 
Indians receiving public assistance, and 45% of all recipients 
receiving public assistance (making AFDC the largest sin- 


72a 


gle human service program in the state in terms of recipient 
counts). 


AFDC recipient counts is selected counties covering the 
period from 1987 to 1992 were analyzed. Because of the 
relative newness of Tribal gaming, identifiable trends would 
most likely be evident in that time period. 


Only those non-urban counties with Tribal gaming facili- 
ties were considered. These parameters were used because, 
in such counties, the gaming facilities have emerged as one 
of the major employers. Any fluctuations in AFDC demands 
would more strongly correlate to the presence of Tribal 
gaming than it would in an urban area. A wider range and 
number of economic factors would influence a change in 
public assistance patterns in an urban area. Given the 
criteria, Cass County containing the Northern Lights and 
Palace Bingo facilities, Cook County containing Grand 
Portage Casino, Goodhue County containing the Treasure 
Island facility, Mahnomen County containing the Shooting 
Star Casino, Mille Lacs County containing the Grand Ca- 
sino, Pine County containing the Grand Casino Hinckley, 
Redwood County containing the Jackpot Junction facility, 
and Roseau County containing the Lake of the Woods 
Casino were studied. 


Recipient count trends in each selected county were then 
compared to the trends in the state as a whole and to the 
largest urban counties, Hennepin and Ramsey. 


Recipient Trends 


The results of this analysis suggest that the employment 
generated by the Tribal gaming industry is reducing the 
demand for AFDC services and expenditures, and is there- 
fore fulfilling one of the main goals of the State/Tribal 
compact — economic development.

PRELIMINARY ESTIMATES OF ECONOMIC 
IMPACTS RELATED TO TRIBAL GAMING 
IN SOUTH DAKOTA 


Submitted by: Dr. Michael K. Madden 
Professor of Economics, University of South Dakota 


Tribal gaming commenced in South Dakota in October 
of 1990 with the opening of the Royal River Casino in 
Flandreau. Since then four additional casinos have begun 
operations with the latest occurring in the spring of 1993. 
(Refer to the accompanying state map for their respective 
geographical locations. ) 


The Travel Industry 


During the last nine years, I have been responsible for 
measuring the economic impacts of the vacation travel 
industry throughout the state and have accordingly tracked 
the economic impacts of tribal casinos as they have devel- 
oped since 1990. The added travel spending in each of the 
counties in which tribal casinos are located has been sub- 
stantial. Prior to tribal gaming, aggregate travel spending in 
these five counties totaled about $8 million with Codington 
County (Watertown) accounting for the bulk of this 
amount. In 1993, travel spending in these five counties 
amounted to about $31 million, an increase of 281%. To- 
gether, these counties now account for nearly 8% of tourism 
spending in the entire state compared to about 2% prior to 
tribal gaming. 


Economic Impacts 


Visitor spending translates into economic impacts such as 
jobs and earnings. Although no formal economic impact 
study has been undertaken to date, preliminary discussions 
have begun concerning the completion of such a study in the 
near future. Upon the completion of this research, more 
precise economic estimates will be possible. The following 


economic impact highlights are based on an informal survey 
of the five operating tribal casinos. 


Direct employment in these casinos today amounts to 
more than 1,700. Assuming indirect employment im- 
pacts are similar to the South Dakota travel industry in 
general, an additional 570 jobs have been generated in 
the state through the multiplier effect. The total impact 
on employment is therefore estimated to be 2,270. 


It is estimated that 70 to 75 percent of the direct 
employment positions are held by Native Americans. 
This translates into an increase of between 1,200 and 
1,300 new jobs held by Native Americans in South 
Dakota. The remaining 25 to 30 percent of the jobs 
represent new employment opportunities for South 
Dakotans living in communities near the casino 
operations. 


New wages and salaries in the regions surrounding 
the tribal casinos are estimated to be more than 
$25 million. Because most regions surrounding the 
tribal casinos are of low population, these added earn- 
ings have produced significant relative impacts on per- 
sonal income within communities. 


Non labor operating expenditures amounts to at least 
$22 million for the five operating casinos. These ex- 
penditures are related to the required business 
purchases of goods and services from local and non 
local vendors. Examples include food and beverages, 
utilities and various other miscellaneous purchases. 


In addition, an undetermined amount of business activ- 
ity occurs through spending by casino employees. 


It is estimated that tribal profits derived from gaming 
operations amounted to nearly $9 million in 1993. 


89a 


+ The use of these profits vary from tribe to tribe. Some 
of the resources have been allocated to infrastructure 
improvements relating to the casinos themselves. Ex- 
amples include lodging and camping facilities as well as 
additions to casino capacity. Other economic develop- 
ment programs have also been pursued. Training and 
educational programs for tribal members have been 
instituted or expanded within a number of the tribes. 


¢ At least $750,000 in contributions have collectively 
been made to tribal and other social programs. Exam- 
ples include day care facilities, senior citizen programs, 
law enforcement and treatment centers. 


The Future 


There are at least three additional tribal casinos at some 
stage of planning in South Dakota. (See attached map.) If 
economic growth occurs in proportion to what has been 
demonstrated since 1990, a number of inferences can be 
drawn. Vacation travel spending related to tribal casinos will 
grow from $31 million today to $52 million. Total employ- 
ment will increase from 2,270 to 3,770 and earnings are 
expected to grow from $22 million to $37 million. 


Conclusion 


From an economic standpoint tribal gaming has proven to 
be an effective rural economic development tool. The added 
economic activity has been distributed in the communities 
surrounding the casinos producing economic vitality which 
obviously would not have occurred in the absence of tribal 
gaming enterprises.

The Wisconsin Winnebago Nation has two nationally 
recognized firms that assist in management and oversight. 
The Accounting firm Arthur Anderson conducts all gaming 
audits and financial reports required under tribal, state and 
federal law. 


Attorneys Process & Investigation Services Inc. (API), a 
nationally respected private investigative consulting agency, 
conducts the background investigations and regulatory en- 
forcement required by tribal ordinance, tribal/state compact 
and IGRA. With API, we have a top notch private investi- 
gation firm of 35 people with an estimated 500 years of law 
enforcement experience at our disposal. 


API assisted us by providing information that was not 
available from state or federal sources. As a result, serious 
suitability issues were identified and the Winnebago Nation 
took appropriate regulatory actions. This information helped 
our operations keep their integrity and protect our Nation. 
Again this information was not available to us from state or 
federal sources. 


All prosecutions of state criminal law are provided by the 
State of Wisconsin under the tribal/state compact entered 
into by the Nation and the State in July of 1992. 


Many people believe that Indian country cannot possibly 
have the level of security and control that Nevada and New 
Jersey have over their gaming operations. That is not true. 
The Wisconsin Winnebago have state-of-the-art security, 
surveillance, on-line accounting, and audit controls. The 
Winnebago employ nearly 1700 people at 3 casinos. More 
than 300 of these employees are working in the security, 
surveillance and accounting areas to insure the integrity of 
the games, to protect our customers and to protect the 
Winnebago Nation. 


We have these controls because it our people’s money 
that is at stake. We know what can happen. We want to do

it is our children’s future that is at stake. We challenge 
anyone to come to the lands of the Winnebago Nation to 
review our personnel, procedures and equipment. If any 
governmental body, local, state or federal is doing more to 
protect the integrity of its games, its customers, and its 
citizens we want to know about it. 


The Winnebago Nation’s gaming operations are very 
successful in large part because the Nation has a strong and 
effective gaming ordinance; experienced legal, accounting 
and investigative personnel; and a dedicated Gaming Com- 
mission with all the regulatory and enforcement authority 
necessary for the maintenance of successful gaming. 


Benefits of Indian Gaming 


Indian gaming is not about gambling. It is about survival 
and bringing my people out of the third world conditions 
that they have lived in for generations. It is about sover- 
eignty and the right of one government to operate free from 
the interference of another government. Finally, it is about 
an agreement and compromise worked out among sovereign 
governments. 


When Congress established the National Indian Gaming 
Regulatory Act it said the act had three purposes: Promote 
tribal economic development, self sufficiency, and strong 
tribal government. Indian gaming in Wisconsin is doing all 
of these things and more. 


I have heard the testimony given at the recent hearing 
regarding the positive impact that Indian gaming has had on 
not only tribal, but on local economies at well. The situation 
is not any different in Wisconsin, but the facts bear 
repeating. 


The Winnebago Nation employs more than 2000 people. 
Nearly 80% of these people are employed at our gaming

enterprises. Employment has quadrupled in the last two 
years. More than 60% of these employees are non-Indian. 
More than 300 of our gaming employees came from the 
welfare rolls. Statewide, Indian gaming jobs took 800 people 
off welfare and 1400 more from the unemployment rolls. 
Casino income is the sole support for the families of nearly 
80% of the casino employees. According to Health & Social 
Service Secretary Gerald Whitburn, state aid to Indian 
families has dropped. He says that Indian gaming is a major 
reason for this drop. 


Indian gaming has also been a great benefit to the larger 
community. For example, in Sauk County where one of our 
casinos is located, unemployment in 1991 was 30% above 
the state average, now it is only 3% above the state average. 


In Wisconsin alone, Indian gaming has created 7000 jobs. 
Is there someone that has 7000 jobs that will employ these 
Wisconsin people? According to a Dept. Of Commerce 
study, when government creates jobs it costs $20,000 per 
job. Does someone have the $140 million replace the 7000 
jobs threatened by attacks on Indian gaming? 


The Winnebago Nation has no natural resources and we 
do not have a significant land base, yet we are expected to 
provide the same governmental services that the Navajo or 
the Yakima tribes provide their members. We cannot de- 
pend on the federal government to give us the money to 
provide these services. We cannot depend on the state 
government to give us the money to provide these services. 
And we don’t want to. We want to provide for our own. 


To do this, we need a stable tribal economy. This econ- 
omy must provide jobs for our people, both skilled and 
unskilled; this economy must return money back into our 
community and the pockets of our people; and finally, this 
economy must allow us to diversify so we can sustain our 
economy into the future. We know that diversification is the

TESTIMONY 
OF 
BERNARD BOUSCHOR, CHAIRMAN

APRIL 27, 1994 
Dear Chairman Inouye and Members of the Committee: 


I. Introduction 


On behalf of the Sault Ste. Marie Tribe of Chippewa 
Indians, I wish to express our appreciation for extending to 
us this opportunity to discuss the implementation of the 
Indian Gaming Regulatory Act as it relates to Indian 
gaming operations in the State of Michigan. | am pleased 
and honored to address you with representatives of the 
National Indian Gaming Association and Tribal govern- 
ments from across the Country. 


The Sault Ste. Marie Tribe of Chippewa Indians wishes 
to express to the Committee our concern over recent calls to 
amend the Indian Gaming Regulatory Act to increase 
Federal or State regulation of Indian gaming. We believe 
the success of Tribal gaming operations in Michigan is proof 
of the fact that Tribal governments are capable of and are 
more successful when they have the primary role in regulat- 
ing their own gaming operations. 


Il. Background 


The Sault Ste. Marie Tribe of Chippewa Indians is the 
largest federally recognized Tribe in Michigan, with over 
20,000 members. Located in Michigan’s Upper Peninsula, 
the Tribe provides direct services to members in seven 
counties (Chippewa, Mackinac, Luce, Alger, Schoolcraft, 


102a 


Delta and Marquette). Gaming revenues are essential to the 
provision of services to this population. 


Although the Tribe has been conducting a Bingo opera- 
tion for several years, a small casino was opened in 1985. In 
1985 the Tribe began with a 10,000 square foot facility 
offering blackje: X and poker and employing about 89 em- 
ployees. Today the Tribe operates a 95,000 square foot 
gaming facility in Sault Ste. Marie, Michigan and another in 
St. Ignace, Michigan. These casinos offer a wide variety of 
games including slot machines. The gaming operations in- 
cludes hotels, a restaurant and gift shops. 


The Tribe’s gaming operations now employ over 1600 
people and produced over 100 million dollars in gross 
revenue last year. The Tribe is the sole owner and operator 


of all its’ gaming operations. 
After over five years of negotiation and litigation, a 


compact between each of the Michigan Tribes and the State 
of Michigan was finally signed in August, 1993. 


III. Gaming Regulation 


The compact between the Tribes and the State stands as a 
recognition of the Tribes’ right to regulate their own gaming 
operations as sovereign governments, as well as their success 
at self regulation for over eight years. Under the compact 
with the State of Michigan, the Tribes maintain the primary 
responsibility for regulating their own gaming activities. The 
State’s role is limited to monitoring Tribal regulation. The 
compact does, however, require that certain minimum stan- 
dards be followed. For instance, the compact specifies that 
certain Michigan laws pertaining to employment be fol- 
lowed, the Tribes comply with the Bank Secrecy Act and 
that minimum standards be followed with regard to back- 
ground checks. (See attachment #1, Compact between the 
Sault Ste. Marie Tribe and the State of Michigan). 


103a 


Indian gaming operations in Michigan are operated free 
of any infiltration by organized crime. These casinos have 
not generated an increase in criminal activity. Indeed the 
Sault Ste. Marie tribe of Chippewa Indians works closely 
with the Federal Bureau of Investigation, criminal investiga- 
tors with the Bureau of Indian Affairs, Michigan State 
Police and local police agencies. We have received the 
support of the Chief of Police of the City of Sault Ste. 
Marie, officials of the Michigan State Police and other law 
enforcement officials. (See the attached correspondence 
from Sault Ste. Marie City Police Chief Fitzgerald and the 
Chippewa County Prosector Pat Shannon). We consistently 
work with Canadian police authorities as we are located very 
near the U.S.-Canadian border. The Tribe has its’ own 
public safety department comprised of thirteen officers. 
(See attachments; #2, Letter from the Detroit Police Of- 
ficers Association, #3 Letter to Ted Gatzaros from the 
Wayne County Sheriff's Department, #4 Letter to Ted 
Gatzaros from the Wayne County Sherifi's Local 502, #5 
Letter from Chief of Police of the City of Sault Ste. Marie 
to Governor John Engler, #6 Letter from Chippewa County 
Prosecutor Patrick Shannon to Governor Engler). 


The biggest obstacie to effective law enforcement we 
currently face in Michigan is not inadequate regulation, bui 
inadequate funding and jurisdictional barriers. Indeed, the 
Sault Ste. Marie Tribe is currently working with local 
government officials to fund law enforcement services to 
expand the financial resources available to local law enforce- 
ment agencies and thereby improve the availability of police 
services to local communities as well as the Tribal commu- 
nity. In addition, Michigan Tribes have been working to pass 
cross-deputization legislation in Michigan to help alleviate 
the problems imposed by jurisdictional barriers to prosecu- 
tion of criminal activity. 


104a 


Effective regulation of their own gaming operations is 
certainly one of the principal reasons for the success of the 
Tribe’s gaming operations in Michigan. The Sault Ste. 
Marie Tribe has developed a system of regulation modeled 
after the systems utilized by the State of Nevada. The 
minimum internal control standards set up by the Nevada 
Gaming Control Board have been adapted for use in the 
Tribe’s gaming operations. 


In addition to these internal controls, the tribe conducts 
external, independent audits. The company conduction the 
audit, Anderson, Tackman & Company has been licensed by 
the State of Wisconsin for work in casinos. These audits 
have been approved by the office of the Inspector General. 


The Tribe has spent a great deal of time and money to 
employ the best possible security measues to insure the 
integrity of its’ gaming activities. We have included a state 
of the art camera security system utilizing over 364 cameras 
in one facility alone. The entire operation of the facility is 
captured on video tape. The tapes themselves are retained 
until the accounting paperwork is balanced out. We have 
purchased a player tracking system and a slot information 
system that allows us to compare the theoretical hold 
against the actual. Any discrepancy is investigated. Comput- 
erized accounting records are checked daily, weekly, quar- 
terly, and finally yearly. Background checks are performed 
on every employee. This list in not meant to be exhaustive, 
but representative of the complexity of the regulatory mea- 
sures we have taken. 


The Tribe currently complies with the Bank Secrecy Act. 
W2-G forms for gambling winnings are required to be filled 
out and filed. 1042-S forms for non-resident aliens are filed. 
Currency Transaction Reports are filled out and filed. 


In addition to these measures the Tribe has instituted an 
internal system of checks and balances. The management of 


10Sa 


the gaming operations have been separated from tribal 
governmental operations with the creation of an Economic 
Development Commission. The Economic Development 
Commission must report to the Tribal Board of Directors as 
to the efficiency and integrity of the gaming operations. The 
casino comptroller and assistant comptroller are in fact 
Tribal employees, not casino employees. Therefore, they are 
wholly independent of casino management and report di- 
rectly to the Tribal Chief Financial Officer. The legal 
representation of the Casino is provided by the Tribal 
Attorney’s office. These officials are responsible to the Board 
of Directors. They in effect function as watchdogs for the 
Tribe. 


Furthermore, the Tribe has adopted an extensive gaming 
ordinance which establishes a Tribal Gaming Commission 
independent of both the Tribe Board of Directors and the 
Economic Development commission. The gaming Commis- 
sion will license all management officials and gaming em- 
ployees as well as all gaming vendors. The Commission will 
possess independent audit powers (See attachment #7; 
Tribal Gaming Ordinance). 


The Sault Ste. Marie Tribe is proud of its’ success as well 
as the integrity of its’ gaming operations. After all it is the 
Tribes that have the most to lose from badly run gaming 
operations. It stands to reason that the Tribes themselves are 
in the best position to regulate their own gaming operations, 
as it is they who have the most to lose. We have established 
ourselves as successful ...

Dear Governor Engler: 


Those of us in Sault Ste. Marie who feel responsible in 
some measure for maintaining a viable economy, shudder a 
bit when we hear of any potential threat to tribal gaming 
operations. Therefore, the Board of Directors of the Sault 
Ste. Marie Chamber of Commerce strongly supports an 
agreement between the State of Michigan and Native 
American Tribes which will assure that the tribes can 
continue present gaming operations on reservation lands. 


There is no doubt that during the early years of the 
development of the Kewadin Casino in Sault Ste. Marie, 
many area business people were apprehensive and uncom- 
fortable with the expansion of gambling. We predicted that 
there would be a great deal of negative impact. We could 
see the possibility of an increase in crime, even some 
involvement of organized crime, unfair competition which 
might destroy certain types of businesses and an unhealthy 
situation arising between the local community and an auton- 
omous Indian society. 


None of these predictions have come true. The Sault 
Tribe of Chippewa Indians are very good neighbors. The 
profits from their operations have made possible a dramatic 
improvement in the standard of living, not only for tribal 


109a 


members, but for the entire community. Hundreds of jobs, 
good jobs, are available. Social programs, good housing, 
auxiliary businesses and new opportunities exist at a level we 
never anticipated. After recovering from the economic dol- 
drums following the closure of the local air force base in the 
1970’s, our entire area is enjoying a revival. Tourism is 
growing and developing into a year-round industry, in part, 
because of the attraction of the casino. Visitors who come to 
gamble, see our beautiful peninsula, and return with the 
families to enjoy the sights. 


It would be tragic to have anything happen which might 
reduce the benefits derived from tribal businesses. They, and 
we, need your support.

Nations and Tribes to be self-sufficient, self-governing and

Congress enacted the Indian Gaming Regulatory Act in 
1988 “to promote tribal economic development, tribal self- 
sufficiency, and strong tribal government.” The Act is doing 
just that. No useful purpose would be served by replacing 
the Indian Gaming Regulatory Act with a new more com- 
plex statutory scheme and a larger federal bureaucracy to 
usurp the regulatory roles now negotiated, agreed upon and 
played by Indian Nations and States. 


I will refer to the casino enterprise of the Oneida Nation 
to illustrate the current success of the Indian Gaming 
Regulatory Act.
