**Actual statutory setting:** Massachusetts conditioned medical registration on a promise not to collect more than Medicare's reasonable charge from covered beneficiaries. Medicare permitted payment through an itemized bill or assignment accepting the reasonable charge as the full charge. The court distinguished Congress's failure to disturb preexisting balance-billing practice from creation of an affirmative right protected against significant state interference. Neither the enacted payment alternatives, the cited original legislative statements, a later description of the legislative compromise, nor Congress's rejection of a federal ban established the latter intent. The opinion also rejected the evidence that the state ban would frustrate Medicare access, discussing the actual local billing and participation evidence rather than declaring price restrictions categorically permissible. Part II rejected the distinct substantive-due-process challenge to the license condition.

**Justice-specific preemption position:** Breyer expressly recognized express, field, impossibility, and demonstrated obstacle preemption. He did not adopt a physical-impossibility-only rule or require the word “right” in every federal statute. In Part I-C he distinguished affirmative federally protected options in Lawrence County, Michigan Canners, de la Cuesta, Franklin National Bank, and the other cited cases. Franklin was described as construing the Federal Reserve Act to confer a national bank's right to act as a savings bank. The decisive inquiry was what Congress actually created, measured by the statute's text, history, subject, and purposes; federal silence alone did not insulate existing practice from state regulation.
