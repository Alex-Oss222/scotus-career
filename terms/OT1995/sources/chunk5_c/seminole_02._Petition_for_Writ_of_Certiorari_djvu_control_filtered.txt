Petitioner, the Seminole Tribe of Florida, is a federally 
recognized Indian Tribe. The Seminole Tribe seeks cer- 
tiorari to review the opinion and judgment of the United 
States Court of Appeals for the Eleventh Circuit which 
held that Congress lacked the power to abrogate state 
Eleventh Amendment immunity under the Indian Com- 
merce Clause.

STATEMENT OF THE CASE 

The Seminole Tribe of Florida sued the State of Florida 
and its Governor on September 19, 1991, asserting juris- 
diction primarily under 25 U.S.C. § 2710(d)(7)(A) (i); 
(App. 97a). The essence of the suit was that the State 
and the Governor had “failed to respond in good faith to 
the Tribe’s request for compact negotiations and have not 
conducted those negotiations in good faith.” Compl. at 
q 24. 

The Indian Gaming Regulatory Act (“IGRA”), Pub. 
L. No. 100-497, 102 Stat. 2467 (1988) (codified at 25 
U.S.C. §§ 2701-2721; 18 U.S.C. §$§ 1166-1168), requires 
Indian Tribes to compact with a state in order to conduct 
class III gaming—gaming that is neither social or tradi- 
tional class I gaming, nor within the various bingo, pull 
tab and card games which are authorized as class II gam- 
ing under the statute. Class II] gaming on Indian lands 

4 

is lawful if authorized by a tribe located in a state that 
permits such gaming, and “conducted in conformance with 
a Tribal-State compact entered into by the Indian tribe 
and the State... .” §2710(d)(1); (App. 93a). Con- 
gress provided a remedy if the Tribal-State compact nego- 
tiations fail to achieve an accord: 

(A) The United States district courts shall have ju- 
risdiction over— 

(i) any cause of action initiated by an Indian 
tribe arising from the failure of a State to enter 
into negotiations with the Indian tribe for the 
purpose of entering into a Tribal-State compact 
under paragraph (3) or to conduct such nego- 
tiations in good faith. . . . 

(B)(i) An Indian tribe may initiate a cause of ac- 
tion described in subparagraph (A)(i) only after the 
close of the 180-day period beginning on the date on 
which the Indian tribe requested the State to enter 
into negotiations under paragraph (3)(A). 

§ 2710(d) (7) (A) (i) & (B) (i); (App. 97a). 

The Seminole Tribe sued the State of Florida and its 
Governor pursuant to Congress’ jurisdictional mandate 
and 28 U.S.C. § 1331 (federal question jurisdiction). 
The State and the Governor moved to dismiss on Elev- 
enth Amendment sovereign immunity grounds. The dis- 
trict court denied their motions, Seminole Tribe of Florida 
v. Florida, 801 F. Supp. 655 (S.D. Fla. 1992); (App. 
26a-42a), stating: 

Given Congress’ plenary authority over Indian rela- 
tions, explicitly noted in the text of the Constitution 
at Article I, § 8, cl. 3, and the uniquely federal issues 
raised when such authority is exercised, considered 
in conjunction with the principles enunciated by the 
Supreme Court in Pennsylvania v. Union Gas Co., 
491 US. 1, 109 S.Ct. 2273, 105 L.Ed.2d 1 (1989), 
we conclude that Congress, when acting pursuant to 

5 

the Indian Commerce Clause, has the power to abro- 
gate the States’ immunity. 

860i F. Supp. at 658; (App. 32a). 

The State and the Governor appealed to the Eleventh 
Circuit.’ The Seminole case was consolidated with Poarch 
Creek Indians v. Alabama, No. 92-6244. In that case, 
the district court had granted the State’s and the Gover- 
nor’s Eleventh Amendment motions to dismiss. The Elev- 
enth Circuit reversed the Seminole decision and affirmed 
the Poarch Creek decision, concluding that the Indian 
Commerce Clause did not provide Congress with the 
power to abrogate the State’s Eleventh Amendment im- 
munity. Seminole Tribe of Florida v. Florida, 11 F.3d 
1016, 1028-29 (1!th Cir. 1994); (App. 22a, 25a). 

The Eleventh Circuit found that Congress clearly 
“manifested its intent to abrogate the states’ immunity” 
under IGRA. 11 F.3d at 1024; (App. 14a). The court 
held “that Congress enacted IGRA solely under the 
Indian Commerce Clause,” but then concluded that the 
Indian Commerce Clause does not permit Eleventh 
Amendment abrogation. 11 F.3d at 1026; (App. 18a). 
The court’s rationale rejected the relevance of Pennsyl- 
vania v. Union Gas Co., 401 U.S. 1 (1989), questioning 
its viability and drawing a distinction between Congress’ 
powers under the Interstate Commerce Clause (Union 
Gas) and the Indian Commerce Clause. 11 F.3d at 
1026-28; (App. 18a-22a). 

The court of appeals noted that its conclusion regard- 
ing Congress’ Eleventh Amendment abrogation power con-

I. THE DECISION BELOW EXPRESSLY CONFLICTS 
WITH THE DECISION OF THE EIGHTH CIRCUIT 
IN CHEYENNE RIVER SIOUX TRIBE v. SOUTH 
DAKOTA, 3 F.3d 273 (8th Cir. 1993), AND PRESENTS 
THE IMPORTANT AND UNANSWERED QUESTION 
OF CONGRESS’ POWER TO ABROGATE THE 
STATES’ ELEVENTH AMENDMENT IMMUNITY 
UNDER THE INDIAN COMMERCE CLAUSE 

The Eleventh Circuit was unequivocal: 

We hold that, although decisions of the Supreme 
Court demonstrate that Congress does possess the 
power to abrogate the states’ Eleventh Amendment 
sovereign immunity in certain cases, Congress did 
not possess that power when enacting IGRA under 
the Indian Commerce Clause, U.S. Const. art. I, § 8, 
cl. 3. 

11 F.3d at 1019; (App. 2a). 

Having determined that Congress enacted IGRA 
solely under the Indian Commerce Clause, we now 
must determine whether the Indian Commerce Clause 
permits Congress to abrogate the states’ Eleventh 
Amendment immunity. We conclude that it does not. 

11 F.3d at 1026; (App. 18a). 

The Eleventh Circuit acknowledged there was no doubt 
Congress intended to abrogate the State’s immunity, but 
concluded: 

That intent cannot be given effect, however, as Con- 
gress did not possess the power to abrogate the states’ 
immunity when it enacted IGRA. 

11 F.3d 1024; (App. 14a). 

The court of appeals acknowledged that this Court has 
not yet addressed the abrogation power in the context of 
legislation enacted pursuant to the Indian Commerce 
Clause. It recognized that section 5 of the Fourteenth 
Amendment and the plenary power over Interstate Com- 

8 

merce, article I, section 8, clause 3, provide a basis for 
abrogation, citing Fitzpatrick v. Bitzer, 427 U.S. 445 
(1976), and Pennsylvania v. Union Gas Co., 491 US. 
1 (1989), 

[but] [t]he Court has not yet found that Congress 
possesses the power to abrogate the states’ sovereign 
immunity when legislating under any other provi- 
sions of the Constitution. 

11 F.3d at 1023; (App. 13a). 

This case presents the Court, under the Constitution’s 
Indian Commerce Clause, with that important, but un- 
answered question. The importance of the question is 
underscored by the historically sensitive nature of the rela- 
tionship between the federal government and Indian tribes, 
and by Congress’ attempts to address conflicts between 
states and tribes pursuant to its plenary power to regulate 
commerce with the Indian tribes under article I, section 8, 
clause 3 of the Constitution. The Indian Gaming Regula- 
tory Act is a quintessential example of Congress’ delicate 
balancing to accommodate the rights and needs of tribes 
and states. The Act was prompted by California v. 
Cabazon Band of Mission Indians, 480 U.S. 202 (1987), 
which left Indian gaming unregulated: 

In an attempt to supply some much-needed regula- 
tion, and after contentious debate concerning the ap- 
propriate state role in the regulation of Indian gam- 
ing, Congress enacted the Indian Gaming Regula- 
tory Act. 

11 F.3d at 1019; (App. 3a). 

Survival of the Act’s central promise to the tribes——a 
remedy for a state’s failure to engage in good faith nego- 
tiations—will resolve constitutional, statutory, and con- 
flicting decisional doubts. Compare Kickapoo Tribe of 
Indians v. Kansas, 818 F. Supp. 1423 (D. Kan. 1993) 
(IGRA abrogates Eleventh Amendment immunity), with 
Sault Ste. Marie Tribe of Chippewa Indians v. Michigan, 
800 F. Supp. 1484 (W.D. Mich. 1992); Ponca Tribe of 

9 

Oklahoma v. Oklahoma, 834 F. Supp. 1341 (W.D. Okla. 
1992); Spokane Tribe of Indians v. Washington, 790 
F. Supp. 1057 (E.D. Wash. 1991) (no Eleventh Amend- 
ment abrogation under Indian Commerce Clause). The 
Eleventh Circuit expressly acknowledged its conflict with 
the Eighth Circuit on the IGRA abrogation issue: 

One sister circuit, in determining that the Eleventh 
Amendment did not bar suits under IGRA, men- 
tioned that the state had “actively engaged in ne- 
gotiating tribal-State compacts and ha[d] reaped the 
benefits from these negotiations.” See Cheyenne 
River Sioux Tribe v. State of South Dakota, 3 F.3d 
273, 281 (8th Cir. 1993) (“Cheyenne II”), aff’g 
830 F. Supp. 523 (D.S.D. 1993) (“Cheyenne I”). 
As the Eighth Circuit Court of Appeals primarily 
rested its decision on the fact that Congress had 
abrogated the state’s Eleventh Amendment immunity, 
an argument we discuss (and reject) in part III.B., 
below, it is not clear how much weight the court gave 
to the state’s participation in negotiations. To the 
extent that the Eighth Circuit relied on South Da- 
kota’s negotiations as a basis for finding a waiver of 
sovereign immunity, we respectfully disagree.

II. THE DECISION BELOW PRESENTS A CONFLICT 
IN PRINCIPLE WITH PENNSYLVANIA v. UNION 
GAS CO., 491 U.S. 1 (1989) 

The decision below found Pennsylvania v. Union Gas 
Co., 491 U.S. 1 (1989), to be the case “most relevant” 
to the issue of whether the Indian Commerce Clause “per- 
mits Congress to abrogate the states’ Eleventh Amend- 
ment immunity.” 11 F.3d at 1026; (App. 18a). The 
Union Gas plurality concluded that since the Commerce 
Clause: 

withholds power from the States at the same time as 
it confers it on Congress, and because the congres- 
sional power thus conferred would be incomplete 
without the authority to render States liable in dam- 
ages, it must be that, to the extent that the States 
gave Congress the authority to regulate commerce, 
they also relinquished their immunity where Congress 
found it necessary, in exercising this authority, to 
render them liable. The States held liable under 
such a congressional enactment are thus not “uncon- 
senting”; they gave their consent all at once, in rati- 
fying the Constitution containing the Commerce 
Clause, rather than on a case-by-case basis. 

491 US. at 19-20. 

The decision below rejected Union Gas, distinguishing 
it as only applicable to the Interstate Commerce Clause, 
not the Indian Commerce Clause. Neither Union Gas 
nor the language of the Commerce Clause suggests that 
the ratification analysis permits interstate and Indian com- 
merce to be treated differently for Eleventh Amendment 
abrogation purposes. Although the clauses “have very 
different applications,” Cotton Petroleum Corp. v. New 
Mexico, 490 U.S. 163, 192 (1989), the difference favors 
abrogation under the Indian Commerce Clause. 

The Interstate Commerce Clause allows Congress to 
limit state action to maintain free trade among the states. 
Id. “{T]he central function of the Indian Commerce

Ill. THE DECISION BELOW CONFLICTS WITH EX 
PARTE YOUNG, 209 U.S. 123 (1908), OR PRESENTS 
AN IMPORTANT UNANSWERED QUESTION UN- 
DER THAT DOCTRINE 

The Ex parte Young fiction permits an injunction 
against a state officer to force compliance with federal 
law. The Seminole Tribe’s suit against the Governor of 
Florida—the Siate official deemed responsible for nego- 
tiating an Indian Gaming Regulatory Act compact—in- 
vokes the doctrine. 

The Act creates a cause of action and federal court 
jurisdicuon for “failure of a State to enter into negotia- 
tions . . . or to conduct such negotiations in good faith.” 
25 U.S.C. § 2710(d)(7)(A) (i); (App. 97a). Once the 
Governor admits he is “the State” for compact negotiation 
purposes, he, like Attorney General Young, becomes a 
defendant whose presence vitiates Eleventh Amendment 

2 The court of appeals’ doubts about the continued viability of 
Union Gas permeated its opinion. We submit that these doubts 
need not be resolved under the construct we posit, because the 
Indian Commerce Clause provides surer footing for abrogation 
than does the Interstate Commerce Clause. 

12 

problems. Therefore, the court may order him to cease 
illegal actions—i.e., his continuing refusal to negotiate in 
good faith. See 209 U.S. at 155-56. 

The court below held the Ex parte Young doctrine 
inapplicable because it incorrectly believed that how and 
whether to negotiate is sub’cct to gubernatorial discretion. 
11 F.3d at 1028-29; (Arp. 23a-24a). But IGRA 
allows the Governor no such discretion. The Act pro- 
vides that upon receiving a compact request, “the State 
shall negotiate with the Indian tribe in good faith... .” 
25 U.S.C. §2710(d)(3)(A) (emphasis added). The 
court was not asked to compel the Governor to exercise 
any discretion, but only to determine, in light of the 
present factual record, whether his behavior complied with 
IGRA requirements. 

The lower court’s analysis thus fails to address the re- 
lief authorized by IGRA and sought below: a ruling 
that the State has not negotiated in good faith and an 
order that it do so. 25 U.S.C. § 2710(d)(7)(B) (iii); 
(App. 98a). If a governor still refuses to participate in 
good faith negotiations, then IGRA remedies can pro- 
ceed under the authority granted to the Secretary of the 
Interior. The process requires no further participation by 
either a state or its governor. 25 U.S.C. § 2710(d)(7) 
(B) (iv)-(vii); (App. 98a-99a). 

Moreover, contrary to the Eleventh Circuit’s conclusion 
(11 F.3d at 1029; App. 24a), the Governor is the proper 
defendant. The Florida Governor’s authority, as the 
State’s chief executive officer, to conduct compact nego- 
tiations with the Seminole Tribe is unquestioned. 

Congress’ plenary constitutional power to compel states 
to cooperate with tribes imposes a duty upon a state and 
its officials that arises from the states’ duty to conform 
to the Constitution. Ex parte Young provides the basis 
for enforcing that duty by suing the responsible state offi- 
cial in federal court. 209 U.S. at 159. It similarly pro- 
vides the basis for enforcing a state’s duty to abide by 

13 

Congress’ mandate under the Indian Commerce Clause of 
the Constitution. If Ex parte Young does so provide, the 
decision below conflicts with that doctrine, and the lower 
court should have provided the relief contemplated by 
IGRA. If the application of Ex parte Young in this con- 
text is not clear, this case presents an important unsettled 
question of the ability of the federal courts to provide the 
relief contemplated by the Indian Gaming Regulatory 
Act.
