INTEREST OF AMICI CURIAE 


The Tohono O’odham Nation, White Mountain 
Apache Tribe, and Pascua Yaqui Tribe (the “Tribes”) are 
federally recognized Indian tribes occupying and govern- 
ing Indian lands within the State of Arizona.’ The Pascua 
Yaqui Tribe and the Tohono O’odham Nation have been 
operating high stakes gaming on their lands near the City 
of Tucson, Arizona since the mid-1980s. After Congress 
adopted the Indian Gaming Regulatory Act in 1988, cod- 
ified at 25 U.S.C. § 2701 et seq., (“IGRA”) the amici Tribes 
requested that the Governor of Arizona enter into class III 
tribal-state gaming compacts. The Tribes and the Gover- 
nor could not agree on compact terms, and so in 1992 the 
Tribes brought an action in federal court for the District 
of Arizona to invoke the remedial provisions of IGRA 
and obtain compacts.? 


The amici Tribes and nearly 100 other tribes through- 
out the country have successfully followed the pro- 
cedures under IGRA and now have compacts with states.3

> IGRA has been widely successful in resolving disputes 
over Indian gaming. As of February 1995, 98 tribes in 22 states 
had class III compacts approved by the Secretary of Interior 
under IGRA. Bureau of Indian Affairs, Tribal-State Compact List 
(Jan. 26, 1995) (unpublished list on file with the Bureau of 
Indian Affairs). 


For the amici Tribes, gaming under their approved com- 
pacts is now providing substantial funds to subsidize 
badly needed governmental services and build significant 
capital infrastructure for future development. Their com- 
pacts will not be directly affected if the Court affirms the 
Court of Appeals’ decision below. However, the amici 
Tribes have already faced one collateral constitutional 
challenge to their compacts by third parties. If IGRA is 
found to be unconstitutional in this case, even in part, all 
settled compacts will be subject to the risk of further 
litigation and the success of class III gaming will again be 
frustrated. 


The experience of the amici Tribes and the State of 
Arizona under IGRA demonstrates that the Act’s reme- 
dial provisions actually work and, if pursued in Seminole 
Tribe of Florida v. Florida, 11 F.3d 1016 (11th Cir. 1994), 
would not violate the Eleventh Amendment rights of the 
State of Florida or its Governor. As happened in Arizona, 
government officials in Florida will be able to choose 
whether they want to participate in each stage of the 
IGRA process. Should the State choose not to participate, 
no Florida official will be subjected to any coercive order 
from the district court. Florida’s Eleventh Amendment 
concerns are completely theoretical and should be 
rejected. 


Opposition to gaming on Indian reservations, for 
whatever reason, does not make out an Eleventh Amend- 
ment claim. After several years of public debate and 
much input from the states, IGRA was carefully crafted 
by Congress to fully protect the states’ Eleventh Amend- 
ment rights and, at the same time, encourage states and 
tribes to enter into compacts. The IGRA remedial process 


was adopted to permit states to regulate gaming, if they 
so desired, but it was not intended to allow states to veto 
a tribe’s right to conduct class III gaming.

The District Court’s Rulings

and types of gaming conducted in Arizona, the district 
court granted summary judgment in favor of the Tribes 
and, pursuant to 25 U.S.C. § 2710(d)(7)(B)(iii), declared a 
60-day time limit for the parties to conclude negotiations 
for class III compacts. The court refused to specify what 
types of gaming must be the subject of negotiation. 
Yavepai-Prescott Indian Tribe v. Arizona, 796 F. Supp. at 
1296. The legislative history of IGRA persuaded the court 
that Congress wanted to offer states the “unique oppor-

on Indian lands,” and the court hoped that mutual self- 
interest would motivate the State and the Tribes to nego- 
tiate. Id. at 1296-97. The court stated that “IGRA’s terms 
do not force the State to enter into a compact, it only 
demands good faith negotiation. ...” Id. at 1297. The 
court did not order the Governor to agree to a compact,

ARGUMENT 


The Ex parte Young Doctrine Applies and Permits the 
Suit Against the Governor of Florida to Invoke the 
IGRA Remedial Provisions 


The Eleventh Amendment was adopted to prevent 
federal courts from entering judgments that must be paid 
out of state treasuries. As this Court recently held, 


[CJourts of appeals have recognized the vul- 
nerability of the state’s purse as the most salient 
factor in Eleventh Amendment determinations 
... In sum... the vast majority of circuits 
... have concluded that the state treasury factor 
is the most important factor to be considered 

. and, in practice, have generally accorded 
this factor dispositive weight. 


Hess v. Port Auth. Trans-Hudson Corp., __ U.S. __, 115 
S.Ct. 394, 404 (1994). 


If the Court concludes that Congress cannot waive a 
state’s immunity through its adoption of IGRA, the Semi- 
nole Tribe can still obtain complete relief if the Governor 


13 


remains a defendant under Ex parte Young, 209 U.S. 123 
(1908). The Eleventh Amendment does not prevent fed- 
eral courts from prospectively compelling state officials 
to obey federal law, nor does it disable federal courts or 
Congress from fashioning prospective remedies to protect 
the federal statutory rights of a plaintiff. Green v. Man- 
sour, 474 U.S. 64, 68 (1985). 


The IGRA remedial provisions pose no threat to Flor- 
ida’s treasury and the State does not need to be a defen- 
dant in Seminole. The Eleventh Circuit misapplied Ex parte 
Young and ignored the limited role Congress gave to the 
federal courts under IGRA to set the clock for negotia- 
tions and to select a mediator, if necessary. First, the court 
below concluded that Ex parte Young was inapplicable 
because the conduct affected by IGRA involved official 
government duties. 11 F.3d at 1029. In addition, the court 
concluded, incorrectly, that Ex parte Young was inapplica- 
ble because under IGRA a state official is allowed to 
exercise discretion, namely, determining whether or not 
to negotiate a compact, negotiating a compact, and 
enforcing a compact. Id. at 1028. 


The court was wrong on both counts. The fact is, all 
Ex parte Young cases involve official government duties. 
There is certainly no limitation on using the Ex parte 
Young doctrine to compel official conduct. Ex parte 


23 The Ex parte Young fiction that allows suits against state 
officers despite the Eleventh Amendment is not concerned with 
whether a state officer’s conduct is “official,” but whether the 
relief ordered by the court is in fact only prospective. Pennhurst 
State School & Hospital v. Halderman, 465 U.S. 89, 106 (1984); 
Edelman v. Jordan, 415 U.S. 651, 664-65, 667 (1974).

Young has allowed the enforcement of sweeping federal 
court injunctive relief requiring state officers to prospec- 
tively conform their official conduct to the legitimate 
mandates of federal law.?4 


The court of appeals also misapplied an Ex parte 
Young exception for discretionary acts.?° The discretion 
available to a state under IGRA survives even if the 
remedial procedures of IGRA are invoked. The Act does 
not authorize the district court to require that the State of 
Florida agree to a compact. The Eleventh Circuit seemed 
to believe that the Governor or the State of Florida might 
be compelled by the district court to negotiate, sign, and 
perform under a compact. 11 F.3d at 1028. IGRA’s 
remedies were carefully written to avoid this type of 
judicial interference with state affairs. See Yavapai-Prescott 
Indian Tribe v. Arizona, 796 F.Supp. at 1297. The amici 
Tribes’ experience in Arizona certainly demonstrates that 
a state’s discretion to sign or not sign a compact — to get 
involved in the regulation of Indian gaming or not - 
remains with the state throughout the IGRA remedial 
process.

25 The law of injunction and mandamus is concerned with 
discretion in general. A court in equity will consider the scope 
of an officer’s discretion to determine whether an applicant is 
entitled to an equitable remedy and to set the terms of the 
remedy. Puerto Rico v. Branstad, 483 U.S. 219, 227 (1987). An 
equity court’s concern over discretionary duties is inapplicable 
when the court is administering a narrow, carefully prescribed, 
remedial process crafted by Congress.

While mandating “good faith negotiations”, IGRA 
offers states an opportunity to become involved in tribal 
gaming. Provided they negotiate in good faith, states are 
given the opportunity to negotiate over both the scope and 
regulation of gaming in Indian country. This is precisely 
what the states sought when Congress was considering 
IGRA.7¢ If a state chooses to opt out of the IGRA process, 
regulation of class III gaming will be resolved by the 
Secretary of Interior without state participation. IGRA 
gives that choice to state officials and the Act's judicial 
remedies do not empower the courts to take the state’s 
options away. The court supervised remedy under IGRA 
is procedural only. IGRA provides a procedure to allow 
tribes to ultimately obtain a compact when class III gam- 
ing is authorized under federal law, either with a state or 
the Secretary. The fact that Congress has given states a 
voice in the regulation of Indian gaming - if they want it 
- does not violate the state’s Eleventh Amendment rights. 


The Eleventh Amendment analysis must look to the 
specific relief which a district court may grant. Under 
IGRA, the district court will not need to compel any state 
official to do anything. If the district court determines that 
a state has failed to negotiate or has negotiated in bad 
faith, the court merely announces a sixty-day period for

Preface 


Pursuant to this court’s order dated December 16, 
1992, the undersigned undertook his obligations as medi- 
ator pursuant to 25 U.S.C.S. § 2710, one of the provisions 
of an act of Congress popularly known as the Indian 
Gaming Regulatory Act, appearing generally as 25 
U.S.C.S. § 2701 et seq. In obedience to the same order of 
this court, the White Mountain Apache Tribe, the Pascua 
Yaqui Tribe and the Tohono O’Odham Nation each on 
December 18, 1992, presented to the mediator, its last best 
offer for a gaming compact with the state of Arizona, and 
the state of Arizona simultaneously and on the same date 


App. 12 


submitted to the mediator its last best offers for gaming 
compacts with each of said Indian tribes, copies of each 
having been filed with this court. Since that time, the 
parties have submitted a great deal of information to the 
mediator in the forms of reports, affidavits, briefs, mem- 
oranda, and excerpts from some pleadings filed in this 
matter. In addition, the mediator spent five days in on- 
site visits to the reservations of the plaintiff/intervenors 
viewing portions of the reservations, meeting with tribal 
officials, and with city, county and state officials from 
communities in or near the reservations in order to obtain 
information concerning the impact of each of the pro- 
posed gaming compacts. In addition, I interviewed many 
witnesses, expert and otherwise, offered by both sides, 
concerning gaming in Arizona, both tribal and non- 
Indian, as it existed in the past, as it exists presently, and 
their predictions as to what the effects would be in each 
of the scenarios represented by the offers of compacts of 
each party. The parties have also submitted to me infor- 
mation concerning tribal gambling in states other than 
Arizona where such gambling has been in effect since 
Congress adopted the Indian Gaming Regulatory Act. In 
addition, the parties have cited me to several federal 
court decisions interpreting the meaning and scope of 
that Act. 


The role of the mediator, in general, under the Indian 
Gaming Regulatory Act is made difficult by the fact that 
the Act itself is both vague and incomplete in many 
respects regarding the types and scope of class III gaming 
required to be negotiated between the state and the 
tribes. Further, under the Act, true mediation is not con- 
templated: the mediator is forced to choose one of two 


App. 13 


competing compacts in its entirety. He may not pick and 
choose the more acceptable portions of each and meld 
them together into a compact more acceptable to each 
party or to suggest concessions to be made. Further, 
apparently Judge Rosenblatt, in an abundance of caution 
to avoid violation of the state of Arizona’s rights under 
the tenth amendment to the United States Constitution, 
purposely avoided determining the specific types of class 
III gaming required to be negotiated between the state 
and the tribes. Therefore, the mediator finds himself in 
the position of being required to do just that in making 
his choice of the compacts submitted. 


Selection of Compacts 


After having considered all of the matter submitted 
by the parties, in my role as mediator under the Indian 
Gaming Regulatory Act, I hereby select the following 
compacts from those submitted to me: 


1. As between the last best offer compacts of the 
state of Arizona and the White Mountain Apache Tribe, I 
select the compact proposed by the White Mountain 
Apache Tribe as being that which best comports with the 
terms of the Indian Gaming Regulatory Act, other appli- 
cable federal law and the findings and order of the court. 
As a consequence, I reject the proposed compact of the 
state of Arizona. 


2. As between the last best offer compacts of the 
state of Arizona and the Pascua Yaqui Indian Tribe, | 
select the compact proposed by the Pascua Yaqui Indian 
Tribe as being that which best comports with the terms of 


App. 14 


the Indian Gaming Regulatory Act, other applicable fed- 
eral law and the findings and order of the court. As a 
consequence, I reject the proposed compact of the state of 
Arizona. 


3. As between the last best offer compacts of the 
state of Arizona and the Tohono O’Odham Nation, | 
select the compact proposed by the Tohono O’Odham 
Nation as being that which best comports with the terms 
of the Indian Gaming Regulatory Act, other applicable 
federal law and the findings and order of the court. As a 
consequence, I reject the proposed compact of the state of 
Arizona. 


Reasoning Underlying Selections 


Although it is not required under the Act, I feel it 
important for the parties, the court, and the public to be 
aware of the reasons I selected the compacts submitted by 
the Indian tribes rather than those of the state. Perhaps 
those reasons will be of interest to the Secretary of the 
Interior in the event the state refuses to accept the 
selected compacts. 


My first concern as mediator was to discover why 
Congress passed the Indian Gaming Regulatory Act in 
the first place. Its reasons are succinctly stated at the very 
beginning of the Act: 


#2702 Declaration of Policy 
The purpose of this Act is - 


(1) to provide a statutory basis for the 
operation of gaming by Indian tribes as a means 


App. 15 


of promoting tribal economic development, self- 
sufficiency, and strong tribal governments; 


(2) to provide a statutory basis for the reg- 
ulation of gaming by an Indian tribe adequate to 
shield it from organized crime and other cor- 
rupting influence, to ensure that the Indian tribe 
is the primary beneficiary of the gaming opera- 
tion, and to assure that gaming is conducted 
fairly and honestly by both the operator and the 
players; and 


(3) to declare that the establishment of 
independent Federal regulatory authority for 
gaming on Indian lands, and the establishment 
of a National Indian Gaming Commission are 
necessary to meet congressional concerns 
regarding gaming and to protect such gaming as 
a means of generating tribal revenue. 


Oct. 17, 1988, P.L. 100-497 #3, 102 Stat. 2467. 


It is obvious that this Act was intended to give Amer- 
ica’s Indian tribes a source of revenue not previously 
available to them to somehow compensate for the genera- 
tions of neglect that both state and federal governments 
have allowed to occur to reservation Indians. It seems to 
me that those who drafted the Act’s declaration of policy 
must have had in mind the difficult situation in which 
many Indian tribes find themselves today, especially the 
tribes involved in this case. For whatever reasons, neither 
the state nor the federal government has furnished ade- 
quate revenue to provide for the needs of these tribes in 
areas of health, education, economic development, or 
governmental infrastructure. Also, the tribes, though 
varying in degree between them, lack tax bases and natu- 
ral resources to generate returns sufficient to provide 


a 


App. 16 


those necessities. Each of the tribes involved has unem- 
ployment rates several times that of Arizona as a whole. 
Indians on these reservations have exceptionally high 
rates of alcoholism, drug abuse, school dropouts, teen 
suicides and pregnancies and adult suicides. The rate of 
functional illiteracy among tribal members in each of the 
tribes involved greatly exceeds the rate in the rest of 
Arizona. In all three tribes, depression and feelings of 
hopelessness prevail. Their leaders see no light at the end 
of the tunnel: no hope that things will change and get 
better. None of the tribes would favor gambling as its 
primary resource if other sources of revenue were avail- 
able. Yet the tribes see the opportunities under the Indian 
Gaming Regulatory Act as being the only ones left to 
them which will provide a “jump start,” to give them the 
money: to provide a higher level of quality education to 
their young and old so they can compete in the Anglo 
economic world; to preserve their cultures; and to fulfil 
the most basic needs of housing and health care to their 
people. 


Although I have resided in Arizona all but six 
months of my life, and have seen living conditions on 
several Indian reservations in Arizona, I was shocked to 
see the substandard housing, medical and educational 
conditions that existed on the Tohono O’Odham Reserva- 
tion. The elderly, sick and disabled on that reservation 
live in conditions not unlike many third world countries 
in Africa today. To a lesser degree, many of the conditions 
I observed on the Tohono O’Odham Reservation exist on 
the other two reservations. The White Mountain Apache 
tribal courts cannot function because of lack of courtroom 
space. That tribe also has no juvenile detention facility, 


App. 17 


and it must house juvenile offenders in the same facility 
with adults. These conditions would not be allowed to 
exist anywhere else in Arizona. Even counsel for the state 
in this matter did not dispute the dire economic needs of 
the tribes involved. 


The tribes’ economic needs no longer being in doubt, 
my main task as mediator was to decide which of the 
submitted compacts, the state’s or the tribe’s, “best com- 
ports with the terms of this Act and any other applicable 
Federal law and with the findings and order of the court” 
as required by the Act. 


By the terms of 25 U.S.C.S. [sic] § 2710(d)(1), class III 
gaming activities shall be lawful on Indian lands only if 
such activities are: 


(B) located in a State that permits such 
gaming for any purpose by any person, organi- 
zation or entity, and 


(C) conducted in conformance with Tribal- 
State compact entered into by the Indian tribe 
and the State under paragraph (3) that is in 
effect. 


Paraphrasing section 2703 of the Indian Gaming Reg- 
ulatory Act, class I gaming is defined as meaning social 
or traditional games usually engaged in by Indians for 
prizes of minimal value and in connection with ceremo- 
nies. Class II gaming includes bingo, pull tabs, lotto, 
punch boards, tip jars, instant bingo, and card games that 
are allowed by the state. Banking card games, including 
baccarat, chemin de fer, blackjack, electronic facsimiles of 
any game of chance, and slot machines are specifically 


App. 18 


excluded from class II gaming. Class III gaming, the 
primary one in contention in this case, is defined as “all 
forms of gaming that are not Class I gaming or Class II 
gaming.” 


By inference all banking card games and the 
machines in question here are included within class III, as 
well as a lottery, and parimutuel wagering on dog or 
horse races. 


The main differences between the compact offers of 
the state and the tribes is in the number of the machines 
to be allowed, and whether dice and card table games 
will be permitted. 


The state of Arizona takes the position that the 
Indian tribes in Arizona should not be allowed to operate 
any type of gaming operation on their reservations that it 
has not permitted to be operated off the reservations. 
Although willing to allow a limited number of video 
gaming machines, Arizona would not allow the Indians 
to operate table games such as dice, poker or blackjack 
operated by live dealers. The tribes seek not only to have 
a larger amount of machines, but would have table games 
as well. 


It is undisputed that several types of gaming defined 
as class III gaming under the Indian Gaming Regulatory 
Act are being operated in Arizona. The state of Arizona 
itself has raised over 1.5 billion dollars in revenue 
through its statewide lottery and related instant games 
since the lottery was adopted in Arizona in 1981. Cur- 
rently several lottery drawings occur each week. The 
state of Arizona spends in excess of eight million dollars 
a year to advertise its lottery games and to encourage the 


App. 19 


public to buy tickets. Television, radio and newspapers in 
Arizona are replete with advertisements to induce Arizo- 
nans and visitors to gamble in the state’s games. Some of 
the state’s instant games provided by the state are scratch 
cards which pay the player if he or she ends up with 
winning combinations of symbols identical with those 
used in mechanicai or electronic slot machines. Other 
instant games provide likenesses of playing cards, which 
pay the player if combinations are obtained which would 
be winning hands at poker or blackjack. Lotteries and slot 
machines, whether mechanical or electronic, are class III 
gaming devices under the Act. 


Arizona allows parimutuel wagering in both horse 
and dog tracks in the state, and has recently authorized 
off-track betting in sports bars and other establishments 
statewide. The state regulates the industry and receives 
substantial revenue from taxes generated from its opera- 
tion. 


Social, as opposed to commercial gambling, is per- 
mitted in Arizona, without regulation. Also, the manufac- 
ture, sale and possession of slot machines are permitted 
in Arizona, without regulation. 


Charity “Las Vegas Nights” or “Casino Nights” are 
permitted in Arizona, without regulation. In such events 
non-profit organizations can raise funds for charitable 
purposes by providing full-scale casino type gambling to 
its patrons, including blackjack, poker, dice, roulette, and 
slot machines. Although reliable statistics were not avail- 
able on short notice at the request of the mediator, the 
evidence presented showed that the occurrence of such 
charitable casino nights was frequent and statewide.

A description of the state of gambling in Arizona is 
given in this case by Professor William M. Thompson, of 
Las Vegas, Nevada, an expert in the field of gambling: 


Gambling in Arizona is permitted and consis- 
tent with the public policy of the State of Ari- 
zona. A wide range of gaming activities occur 
within the state. Arizona permits more forms of 
gaming than any other state in the southwest, 
with the exception of Nevada. Arizona permits 
charity casino nights, bingo, pull tabs, instant 
lottery, lotto; dog racing, harness racing, quarter 
horse racing, thoroughbred racing, both inter- 
state and intrastate betting, off-track betting, 
tele-theatre betting, as well as the retail sale of 
slot and video machines. Clearly, Arizona is a 
gambling state. In Arizona gambling activity - 
including casino games - is permitted. The per- 
mitted forms of gambling activity in Arizona are 
almost identical to those permitted in California 
with these exceptions: Arizona has dog race bet- 
ting which California does not, and California 
has commercial card rooms which Arizona does 
not. California permits the sale of antique 
machines only, whereas modern video and slot 
type machines are readily available and widely 
sold in Arizona; and California does not permit 
charity casino nights. In the famous Cabazon 
case, the United States Supreme Court deter- 
mined that the gaming policy of California was 
civil and permissive in nature and not criminal 
or prohibitory in nature. Virtually all of the 
forms of gambling permitted in California are 
also played in Arizona. Like California, the State 
of Arizona receives considerable tax revenue 
from gaming activity conducted in the state. 
Arizona state lottery revenues rank 16th highest

in the United States and state income (from) 
gambling exceeds one-third of the amount the 
State of Nevada realizes from taxation of gam- 
biing operations. 


Arizona has long since passed the time when it pro- 
hibited gambling. It now not only allows it, but encour- 
ages it, in a great many forms. Arizona relies on gambling 
to a great extent in raising revenue to avoid higher taxa- 
tion on its residents. In my opinion none of the types of 
class III gambling being sought by the tribes now violates 
public policy in Arizona. The types of gaming sought in 
the tribes’ compact should be included within those 
negotiated in gaming compacts with Indian tribes in Ari- 
zona. It seems cruel to allow non-Indian charities in 
Arizona to use casino gambling to raise substantial sums 
of money for such purposes as: providing scholarships 
for educational needs; raising money for medical services 
and supplies for the sick, the elderly and the disabled, 
and yet to deny the Indian tribes in Arizona that same 
opportunity for the very same purposes. 


I do not base my opinion that class III gaming 
already is the public policy of Arizona merely on the 
allowance of charity casino nights alone, however. Per- 
mitting horse and dog racing and parimutuel betting on 
those events took Arizona on the first step into class III 
gaming. Then the biggest step was taken when the voters 
of Arizona decided that Arizona should run a state-spon- 
sored lottery. It was made clear then that the citizens of 
Arizona wanted class III gambling. I also feel that there is 
no principled difference between the state’s own instant 
type of blackjack played on Arizona’s scratch game and 
the situation where a person sits across the table and 


App. 22 


receives cards from a live dealer. The same logic applies 
to video machines which display symbols of playing 
cards. To me, both are the same as the live dealer table 
games, except perhaps in speed of play. All of the above 
are class III gaming under the Indian Gaming Regulatory 
Act, and are permitted in Arizona. Therefore, comparable 
games should be negotiable within a proposed gaming 
compact with Indian tribes. 


As to the question of the great discrepancy between 
the offered compacts in the number of machines, the 
state’s offers of 250 video machines to each of the tribes 
herein displays an apparent lack of regard for the signifi- 
cant differences in the various tribes’ population, demog- 
raphy, geography, and economic and social needs. For the 
small tribes with which the state has already compacted, 
this limitation may be justified, as the income thereunder 
may indeed be satisfactory to them. However the needs 
of the tribes involved in this action are so much greater 
that it makes the state’s “one-size-fits-all” offer seem 
arbitrary. 


I am unimpressed with the state’s argument that the 
greater number of machines requested by the tribes 
herein, phased in over the periods requested, would 
overwhelm the state’s ability to investigate and certify 
the persons and machines involved. This argument is 
belied by the fact that the tribes compacts offer to pay all 
reasonable costs for the state’s assistance. If Arizona does 
not have enough investigative personnel to handle the 
increase in work, the tribes’ offers would cover the costs 
of the additional hires. Further, as prescribed in the

Indian Gaming Regulatory Act, the primary respon- 
sibility for regulation of the Indian gaming and enforce- 
ment of the laws is directed to fall on the tribes, the U.S. 
Justice Department, the National Indian Gaming Com- 
mission and the Interior Department. Although the 
National Indian Gaming Commission seems to be slow to 
gear up to its responsibilities, it is to be assumed that all 
these departments of the U.S. government will indeed 
fulfill their legal obligations under the Act. 


In conclusion, I would state that my selection of 
compacts in this case is based on the state and federal law 
as it exists today. Things might be different if Arizona 
would hereafter legislatively abolish all class III gaming, 
or if Congress were to amend the Indian Gaming Regula- 
tory Act to preclude the tribes offers under the Arizona 
circumstances. However, speculating as to what would 
happen if those things or either of them occurred is not 
within my responsibilities as mediator. Those are matters 
of public policy, best arrived at by either the Arizona 
Legislature or the U.S. Congress. However, I hope that 
any such legislative changes will not be sought in a spirit 
of retaliation against the tribes, or without first providing 
Indian tribes with adequate means to address the serious 
problems I have referred to above.
