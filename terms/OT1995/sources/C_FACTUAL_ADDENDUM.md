## A. St. P. C. v. B. C., No. 94-7810

### Lower judgment and statutory text

The complete October 17, 1994 rehearing opinion, including additional reasons and all footnotes, is preserved in `C_ac_0743-01.json` and `.txt`: [Harvard Caselaw Access Project copy of 643 So.2d 743](https://static.case.law/so2d/643/cases/0743-01.json). The January 27, 1994 original opinion is preserved in `C_ac_0719-01.json` and `.txt`: [643 So.2d 719](https://static.case.law/so2d/643/cases/0719-01.json). These are the texts of the Louisiana Supreme Court, not later commentary. The rehearing caption identifies Louisiana No. 93-CA-1125. The Court took a direct appeal because the juvenile court had declared state statutes unconstitutional under Louisiana Constitution art. V, §5(D).

The rehearing decree states: **“Accordingly, the judgment of the trial court declaring La.Rev.Stat. 9:364 D unconstitutional is affirmed.”** It does not itself impose a new visitation schedule or determine that abuse has been proved under a higher burden. Its opinion expressly confines the issue decided to the proof standard at the factfinding stage. A decree directing a hearing under a newly specified standard should not be described as a quotation of this existing lower decree.

Section 9:364(D), reproduced in full in rehearing footnote 2 and the original opinion, provided:

> If any court finds that a parent has sexually abused his or her child or children, the court shall prohibit all visitation and contact between the abusive parent and the children, until such time, following a contradictory hearing, that the court finds that the abusive parent has successfully completed a treatment program designed for such sexual abusers, and that supervised visitation is in the children’s best interest. Any testimony by a licensed mental health professional with training, experience, and expertise in treating sexual abuse victims, who is the therapist for the abused child, shall be given greater weight by the court than other testimony on issues of visitation.

The text requires both successful treatment and a court finding of the children's best interests after the contradictory hearing. It says supervised visitation, not a return to unrestricted contact. It does not itself expressly state the evidentiary burden. Rehearing footnote 7 reproduces §9:362(6): a treatment program is **“a course of evaluation and psychotherapy designed specifically for perpetrators of family violence, and conducted by licensed mental health professionals.”** These quotations are from the date-eligible court's reproduction of the statute; a separately authenticated 1992 session-law scan was not obtained.

### Established procedural and factual record

- The child was born September 1988. The parents separated two months later. A 1989 divorce continued joint custody with the mother domiciliary parent and increased father visitation.
- After a May 1992 report, OCS videotaped a child interview and obtained a medical examination reported as normal. The district attorney brought a child-in-need-of-care (CINC) petition; visitation was discontinued pending the proceeding.
- On November 10, 1992, the juvenile court found that the evidence tipped toward child protection and adjudicated the child in need of care as to the father. The court later described the proof as a bare preponderance. Its December disposition authorized weekly supervised visitation for up to one hour at OCS, gave OCS discretion to shorten visits in light of the child's reaction, and directed a shift to a neutral relative's supervision as soon as possible.
- In January 1993, the mother sought the no-contact consequence of newly enacted §9:364(D), relying on the prior CINC finding. She separately sought costs under §9:367. The father asserted due-process and indigency objections; the attorney general was served and participated.
- Rehearing footnote 4 expressly states that the father had **no notice at the CINC factfinding hearing** that its outcome could subject him to §9:364(D) sanctions, and rejects the mother's assertion that the CINC finding binds him conclusively because he did not seek review. This is a positive lower-court statement, not a conclusion from missing records.
- Rehearing footnote 8 reports an OCS case-plan goal that the father admit abuse and its extent, and an expert's conclusion that continuing denial prevented benefit from treatment at that time. The father's argument that many programs required admission is identified as his argument; it is not statutory text or an adjudication that every program does so.
- The lower court's remedial discussion states that Children’s Code authority permits extremely limited supervised visitation, temporary suspension, and other protection even if the §9:364(D) abuse finding is not made. The existing lower disposition used supervised contact. Neither proposition gives a parent an entitlement to unsupervised contact.

### Distinct therapist component and source limits

The original opinion separately treated the therapist-weight sentence under Louisiana Constitution art. II, §2. It held the command unconstitutional insofar as it prescribed mandatory evidentiary weight rather than being merely advisory. Rehearing footnote 1 expressly acknowledges that this portion had been struck. The original opinion also construed the statute to leave treatment completion and best-interests decisions with the court, rather than granting a therapist veto over access to court. This state-law ruling is a separate component; a federal proof-burden disposition does not silently reinstate mandatory therapist weighting. The present neutral assignment does not establish a separate federal challenge to that state-law ruling or to §9:367's fee issue.

## Prior public due-process and child-liberty materials

It establishes an at-least-clear-and-convincing threshold for complete, irrevocable parental-rights termination, using Mathews and allocation of error risk. Stevens joined the majority. ## Field v. Mans, No. 94-967

**Objective dates/route:** Argument October 2, 1995; supplied event November 28, 1995. The First Circuit judgment, 36 F.3d 1089 (1994), affirmed the district court and bankruptcy court rejection of nondischargeability. The underlying Chapter 11 filing was December 10, 1990.

**Primary records:** Internet Archive item [micro_IA40385013_0573](https://archive.org/details/micro_IA40385013_0573), separate petition, both merits briefs and JA PDFs and OCR in `C_records/field_*`; exact download URLs in `C_records/download_manifest.json`. [Official 1994 Code §523](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/USCODE-1994-title11/pdf/USCODE-1994-title11-chap5-subchapII-sec523.pdf) is saved as `C_USCODE1994_title11_chap5-subchapII-sec523.pdf` and `.txt`.

The debt arose from a June 1987 transaction, a $187,500 note secured by a second mortgage, and Mans's personal guarantee. The mortgage called for consent to transfer and acceleration if the property was conveyed without consent. The deed to the partnership was executed October 8, 1987, before the October 9 consent letter, and recorded October 19. The October letters did not disclose the accomplished transfer. The Fields' lawyer located it in February 1991.

**Direct transcript verification:** JA printed pp.35–45 contains the bankruptcy court's oral findings; pp.36–45 were examined here. The court found the letters implicitly represented that no transfer had occurred, expressly found actual subjective reliance and detriment, and said the Fields in effect extended credit by not accelerating. It separately required reasonable reliance, expressed as an objective prudent-person inquiry. It discussed the 1988 report that De Felice claimed ownership, Field's project visits and discussion with the banker, and failure to ask who held title or inspect the registry. That record must be assessed in its own terms; neither substituting a reliance label nor treating the court as having made no actual-reliance finding fairly states it. The transcript contains an isolated sentence at JA44 saying “nondischargeable,” surrounded by its stated ruling for defendant and repeated treatment as dischargeable; do not use that apparent verbal/transcription slip to invert the judgment.

Respondent's merits brief pp.32–33 affirmatively advances the separate statutory contention that the extension of credit was not obtained by the alleged fraud. That is a party argument; it does not decide causation. The direct record shows the bankruptcy court's statement about an effective extension, but this source work does not certify that every preservation or debt-nexus question was adjudicated. A change in reliance rule alone cannot be reported as a fresh finding that the full debt was obtained by fraud.

Section 523(a)(2) covers debts for money, property, services, or an extension, renewal, or refinancing of credit **to the extent obtained by** the specified conduct. Subsection (A) says “false pretenses, a false representation, or actual fraud, other than a statement respecting the debtor’s or an insider’s financial condition.” Subsection (B) separately requires use of a written, materially false statement respecting that financial condition, reasonable creditor reliance, and intent to deceive. The official text confirms those separate terms. The 1994 publication does not itself establish retrospective application of its unrelated amendments to a 1990 case; the operative (A)/(B) wording is also reproduced in the case's filed records.

## NLRB v. Town & Country Electric, No.94-947

**Objective dates/route:** Argument October 10, 1995; supplied event November 28, 1995. The Eighth Circuit, 34 F.3d 625 (August 31, 1994; Nos.92-3911 and 93-1218), denied enforcement on its employee-definition ground. Respondents included Town & Country Electric and Ameristaff Personnel Contractors. The Board decision is 309 N.L.R.B.1250 (1992).

**Primary records:** Archive item [micro_IA40385013_0572](https://archive.org/details/micro_IA40385013_0572), petition, JA, petitioner brief, and two separately catalogued respondent briefs downloaded to `C_records/town_country_*`. [Official 1994 Code §152](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/USCODE-1994-title29/pdf/USCODE-1994-title29-chap7-subchapII-sec152.pdf) saved under `C_USCODE1994_title29_chap7-subchapII-sec152.*`. [Eighth Circuit text](https://openjurist.org/34/f3d/625) cross-checks the identity and below-ground; read only its actual opinion, not its later-citation metadata.

The record concerns 11 union-affiliated applicants: two professional union officials and nine other union members. Ameristaff hired Malcolm Hansen for temporary referral; he worked briefly at the Minnesota project and was dismissed. Ten applicants were not interviewed. The Board found discrimination in interviewing and retaining workers; the employer contested those fact-based charges as well as statutory status. The Eighth Circuit resolved status as its threshold ground. Thus employee status and whether particular acts were unfair labor practices remain analytically distinct; the status question by itself supplies no amount of backpay, reinstatement schedule, or complete enforcement decree.

Section 152(3)'s full statutory definition, including every exclusion, is reproduced in the saved official Code. It includes “any employee,” is not limited to employees of a particular employer unless the subchapter expressly says so, and includes specified workers whose work ceased because of a current labor dispute or unfair labor practice who have not obtained substantially equivalent regular work. It excludes agricultural laborers, domestic servants in a home, persons employed by a parent or spouse, independent contractors, supervisors, employees of Railway Labor Act employers, and employees of persons not statutory employers. No union-pay exclusion is written in that list; whether the common-law relationship itself excludes the workers is the legal dispute, not an additional quoted exception.

There was a contemporaneous circuit conflict, identified in the Eighth Circuit text, involving H.B. Zachry, 886 F.2d70 (4th Cir.1989), and Willmar Electric, 968 F.2d1327 (D.C.Cir.1992), with earlier Henlopen, 599 F.2d26 (2d Cir.1979), among the contrary authorities. This positively corrects the supplied “no material conflict identified” statement without importing a Supreme Court result.

## Thompson v. Keohane, No.94-6615

**Objective dates/route:** Argument October 11, 1995; supplied event November 29, 1995. State judgment: Thompson v. State, 768 P.2d127 (Alaska App.1989); federal judgment: 34 F.3d1073 (9th Cir.1994), unpublished disposition. The Supreme Court issue supplied is the review characterization of Miranda custody, not the independent voluntariness claim. The record identifies unsuccessful voluntariness litigation below, but that claim is not presented in the petition under review.

**Primary records:** Archive item [micro_IA40385013_0614](https://archive.org/details/micro_IA40385013_0614), separate petition, both merits briefs and JA in `C_records/thompson_*`. These PDFs include the lower orders and questioning transcript. The record has a September 15, 1986 station interview, about two hours, with two unarmed plainclothes troopers in a small room. Thompson drove there after a request ostensibly to identify belongings and immediately identified them. Officers repeatedly said he could leave and repeatedly said they knew he killed his former wife. They disclosed searches of his home and truck. He confessed, was permitted to leave, accepted a ride after the truck was impounded, and was arrested about two hours later.

The state trial court decided suppression on submitted papers without an evidentiary hearing, identified voluntary arrival, unarmed questioners, repeated freedom-to-leave statements and departure without arrest, and described the custody issue as very close. The jury heard the tape and convicted him of murder and evidence tampering. Both federal courts treated the state custody determination as presumptively correct under then-§2254(d).

The pre-AEDPA statute conditions the presumption on a factual determination after merits hearing by a competent state court, in proceedings between the applicant and State or state officer, evidenced by written findings, opinion or other reliable adequate written indicia. It provides eight exceptions: unresolved factual merits; inadequate factfinding procedure; inadequate development of material facts; lack of jurisdiction; unconstitutional failure to appoint counsel for an indigent applicant; lack of full/fair/adequate hearing; other due-process denial; and lack of fair record support when the relevant record is produced. Unless one of those conditions applies, the applicant bears convincing-evidence proof of error at a federal evidentiary hearing. The complete statutory quotation is in petitioner?s merits brief Appendix A, printed pp.A-1?A-2 (directly read); no AEDPA provision effective in 1996 applies at this event. An attempted official 1994 §2254 PDF download returned non-PDF content, so that attempted file is not authenticated statutory evidence.

A circuit division was expressly described in the date-eligible source record: compare Feltrop v. Delo, 46 F.3d766,773 (8th Cir.1995), and Jacobs v. Singletary, 952 F.2d1282,1291 (11th Cir.1992). Do not retain a categorical “no material conflict” assertion. The current entering slice should include simulated Wright v. West in addition to Stansbury and Withrow. Miller v. Fenton, 474 U.S.104 (1985), Cuyler, Strickland, Patton and Wainwright v. Witt are the predivergence fact/law authorities requiring their own exact scopes.

The preceding pp.41?43 include the petitioner?s characterization of the interrogation and postdeparture surveillance; these remain party assertions unless the underlying transcript or finding establishes them.
