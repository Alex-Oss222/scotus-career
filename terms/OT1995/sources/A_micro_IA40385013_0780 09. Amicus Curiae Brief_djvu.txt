INTEREST OF AMICI CURIAE 


Americans for Effective Law Enforcement, Inc. (AELE), 
as a national not-for-profit citizens organization, is interested in 
establishing a body of law making the police effort more 
effective, in a constitutional manner. It seeks to improve the 
operation of the police function to protect our citizens in their 
life, liberties, and property, within the framework of the various 
State and Federal Constitutions 


AELE has previously appeared as amicus curiae over one 
hundred times in the Supreme Court of the United States and 
over thirty-five times in other courts, including the Federal 
District Courts, the Circuit Courts of Appeal and various state 
courts, such as the Supreme Courts of California, Illinois, 
Ohio, and Missouri. 


The National District Attorneys Association, Inc. 
(NDAA), is a nonprofit corporation and the sole national 
organization representing local prosecuting attorneys in 
America Since its founding in 1950, NDAA’s programs of 
education, training, publications, and amicus curiae activity 
have carried out its guiding purpose of reforming the criminal 
justice system for the benefit of all our citizens. 


Amici are national professional associations representing the 
interests of law enforcement and prosecutorial agencies at the 
state and local levels Our members include (1) law enforce- 
ment officers and law enforcement administrators who are 
charged with the responsibility of overseeing the process of stop 
and frisk, arrest and search and seizure within the bounds of the 
law, as well as training and the formulation of administrative 
guidance on this subject, and (2) prosecutors and police legal 
advisors who, in their criminal jurisdiction capacity, are called 
upon to advise law enforcement officers and administrators in 
connection with such matters, and to prosecute crimes and

represent the states on appellate review of cases involving 
evidence obtained as a result thereof 


Because of the relationship with our members, and the 
composition of our membership and directors—including active 
law enforcement administrators and counsel—we possess direct 
knowledge of the impact of the ruling of the court below, and 
we wish to impart that knowledge to this Court. 


ARGUMENT 


IN NON-WARRANT CASES, APPELLATE COURTS 
SHOULD NOT CONDUCT A DE NOVO STANDARD OF 
REVIEW CONCERNING REASONABLE SUSPICION TO 
STOP AND PROBABLE CAUSE TO SEARCH. A UNI- 
FORM STANDARD OF NO DE NOVO REVIEW SHOULD 
APPLY TO BOTH WARRANT AND NON-WARRANT 
CASES. 


In this case Milwaukee, Wisconsin, police suspected that 
two people in a car with California license plates were drug 
dealers. They followed them into a motel and obtained one of 
their names from the registry and another from automobile 
records. They then ran both names through “NADDIS,” the 
Narcotic and Dangerous Drug Information System operated by 
the Drug Enforcement Administration (DEA) DEA came back 
with two “hits”, the names corresponded “more or less to names 
of suspected drug dealers in the NADDIS database ” 


; Although the magistrate judge and trial court were given no 
information on the reliability of the NADDIS system, the 
Seventh Circuit Court of Appeals, in United States v. Ornelas- 
Ledesma, \6 F 34714 (7th Cir 1994), concluded that there was 
reasonable suspicion for the police to make an investigative stop 
of the pair as they were leaving the motel 


The defendants filed for review with this Court. requesting 


4 


a consideration of two questions, 1.¢.: 

1 In non-warrant cases, should appellate courts conduct a de 
novo standard of review concerning reasonable suspicion to 
stop and probable cause to search? 

Il. Was it a violation of defendants’ due process rights for the 
trial court to refuse access to the raw data comprising the 
NADDIS check, which was later used to justify both the 
stop and the search? 


In granting review, this Court confined itself to question I, 
supra. Amici submit that the issue is a very important one for 
search and seizure law. The Seventh Circuit had previously 
adopted a “clear error” standard of review in search warrant 
cases, i.e, deferring to the findings below and not conducting 
a de novo review on appeal on the issue of probable cause 
unless there were “clear error.” United States v. Spears, 965 
F 2d 262 (7th Cir. 1992). 


If the Court adopts the same rule for warrantless stops, 
frisks, and searches, it would clarify the procedural law with 
respect to appellate review of search and seizure probable cause 
issues, including the rules with respect to investigative stops 
under Jerry v. Ohio, 392 US. 1 (1968). It would also be of 
great assistance to law enforcement officers who need help, and 
not hindrance, in their daily work. It would provide clear 
guidance to the police through a uniform system of Fourth 
Amendment jurisprudence and carry forward the Court’s 
emphasis upon “bright line” rules for police, an emphasis which 
was initiated in New York v. Belton, 453 U.S. 454 (1981), as 
urged by amici in several cases before this Court in the last 15 
years 


Amici submit that the cype of question involved in this case 
is essentially a question of fact. As such, using the “totality of 
the circumstances” approach established by this Court in //linois 
v. Gates, 462 U.S. 213 (1983), the suppression level and trial 
courts are in the best position to resolve these factual issues

As noted in Spears, supra, 

The question .. isthe same did probable cause exist? The 
[United States Supreme] Court tells us that “[p]robable 
cause exists where ‘the facts and circumstances within [the 
officers’] knowledge and of which they had reasonably 
trustworthy information [are] sufficient in themselves to 
warrant a man of reasonable caution in the belief that’ an 
offense has been or is being committed.” Srinegar v. 
United States, 338 U.S. 160, 175-76, 69 S Ct. 1302, 1311, 
93 L.Ed. 1879 (1949) (quoting Carroll v. United States, 267 
U.S. 132, 162, 45 S.Ct. 280, 288, 69 L Ed 543 (1925)). It 
is a legal conclusion, dependent solely on a factual analysis: 
the facts are what the police knew and did, the finding of 
probable cause is a conclusion that those facts meet the legal 
criteria for probable cause. So the question for us is whether 
this finding should be reviewed deferentially, as ordinary 
findings of fact are, or de novo, as ordinary legal rulings are. 


_. . All we are doing today is announcing one standard of 
review for all reasonable searches. This is not as big a step 
as it appears at first blush We already review exigent 
circumstances cases for clear error. See, e.g., United States 
v. Sewell, 942 F.2d 1209, 1213 (7th Cir. 1991). The same 
is true of consent cases. See, e.g., United States v. Berk, 
930 F.2d 1219, 1221 (7th Cir), cert. denied, US _, 
112 S$ Ct. 269, 116 L.Ed.2d 221 (1991). By our action 
today we simply bring probable cause-based non-warrant 
cases in line with the rest of our Fourth Amendment reason- 
able search jurisprudence. And in line with three of our 
sister circuits, who likewise review probable cause determi- 
nations for clear error. See United States v. Fox, 902 F.2d 
1508, 1513 (10th Cir), cert. denied US. _, Ill 
S$ Ct. 199, 112 LEd2d 161 (1990), United States v. 
Williams, 897 F.2d 1430, 1435 (8th Cir 1990), United 
States v. Santana, 895 F 2d 850, 852 (ist Cir. 1990) 
United States v. Spears, supra, at 270-271

Amici note that, as with the determination of the issues 
surrounding the prophylactic rule of Miranda v. Arizona, 384 
U.S. 436 (1966), independent review by appellate courts at both 
state and federal levels of questions of fact is not only unnec- 
essary on the basis of Fourth Amendment and Miranda rule 
jurisprudence, but also unwise from a judicial policy standpoint. 
The appellate courts simply do not have the resources to re- 
examine questions of fact in Fourth Amendment cases. Opening 
the door to de novo review of such issues will open a chapter of 
judicial involvement in the minutiae of fact issues similar to that 
plaguing our courts in Miranda cases, as chronicled in the study 
on the impact of Miranda's prophylactic rule upon the appellate 
courts in “Miranda v. Arizona—lIs It Worth the Cost?,” 24 
Calif. Western Law Rev. 185-200 (1987-1988). As amici 
pointed out in our brief filed with this Court in United States v. 
Green, 113 S.Ct. 1835 (1993) (petition for certiorari dismissed 
upon death of respondent), the appellate courts are not only 
unequipped to deal with such questions of fact, but frequently 
their chorus of uncoordinated voices leaves the police with 
virtually no “bright line” guidance on factual issues. 


The problem is the same in Fourth Amendment cases as in 
Miranda prophylactic rule cases. If the Court sanctions de 
novo review in this case, it will open a large Pandora’s Box of 
inconsistent appellate rulings in Fourth Amendment cases and 
leave the police with little or no guidance at the level of the 
courts in which their work is largely involved, i.e, the sup- 
pression and trial level courts. It would do disservice to the 
principle articulated by this Court in Gates, supra, 462 U.S. at 
232, 103 S.Ct. at 2329, that “probable cause is a fluid concept 
—turning on the assessment of probabilities in particular factual 
contexts—not readily, or even usefully, reduced to a set of legal 
rules.” The Court would introduce into Fourth Amendment 
jurisprudence the multiplicity of levels of review of essentially 
fact issues that now exists in Miranda cases. Thompson v. 
Keohane, 116 S.Ct. 457 (1995).

As concluded wisely by the court below: 

In both cases [warrant and warrantless], if there is probable 
cause, the search is reasonable and does not violate the 
Fourth Amendment. If there is no probable cause, the 
factfinder at trial will not consider the evidence, either 
because the warrant did not issue, or the exclusionary rule 
operates to keep it out. It is the front-line judicial officer in 
both cases who conducts the initial inquiry, who concludes 
there is or is not probable cause, and an appellate panel 
should not substitute its judgment in either case. 

United States v. Spears, supra, at 271. 


Amici submit on behalf of law enforcement officers, 
administrators, and counsel, that affirmance of the decision 
below is consonant with established case law, sound judicial 
policy, and the need to provide law enforcement with bright line 
rules that can be effectively applied at the street level. To rule 
otherwise will be to create a new era of confusion and inconsis- 
tency in Fourth Amendment jurisprudence at the state and 
federal appellate levels similar to that which presently exists in 
the area of Miranda's prophylactic rule case law. We respect- 
fully ask the Court not to launch the nation’s appellate courts 
upon this fruitless endeavor. 


CONCLUSION 


Amici urge this Court to affirm the decision of the court 
below on the basis of the precedents of this Court and sound 


judicial policy.
