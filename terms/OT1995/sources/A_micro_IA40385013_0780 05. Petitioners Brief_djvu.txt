STATEMENT OF THE CASE 


The petitioners were charged in a one-count indict- 
ment with possession with intent to distribute approx- 
imately 2,000 grams of cocaine in violation of 21 U.S.C. 
§ 841(a)(1) and 18 U.S.C. § 2. (R.4.) Petitioners challenged 
the stop, whether there was consent to search, the scope 
of the search, the arrests and the statements. (R. 12) 
(R.[14]) (Brackeved numbers refer to docket numbers for 
Saul Ornelas). Evidentiary hearings were held over the 
course of four days. 


The matter before the Court emanates from a sup- 
pression motion. Milwaukee County Sheriff’s agents 
seized cocaine after obtaining consent to “look through” 
the vehicle, and after effectuating a more intrusive, par- 
tial vehicle dismantlement. (Tr. at 151) The reasonable 
suspicion for the stop consisted of the articulated facts as 
developed through the course of the suppression hearing 
below. 


As Milwaukee County Sheriff's Detective Michael 
Pautz conducted drug interdiction activities at 8:30 a.m. 
in the morning of December 11, 1992, he observed a two- 
door 1981 Oldsmobile bearing California plates parked in 
front of Room 4 of the Suburban Motel, 4600 S. 27th St., 
Milwaukee, Wisconsin. (J.A. 9) 


a 


Pautz testified that he believed that older two door 
GM vehicles were often used to transport drugs, and 
viewed California, along with Arizona, Florida, Texas and 
New York, as source states for controlled substances. (Tr. 
at 62) (J.A. 9) Pautz also believed other older two door 
cars to be suspect. He further noted that even newer 


model Chryslers and Fords were deemed suspect. (Tr. at 
63-64) 


Pautz contacted his dispatcher to run a check on the 
plates. The dispatcher advised that the vehicle was listed 
to Miguel Ornelas of 830 North 16th Street, San Jose, 
California with an additional name of “Ledesma.” (J.A. 9) 
At the time of the evidentiary hearing, Pautz was unsure 
whether the dispatcher indicated that the name 
“Ledesma” came before or after the name “Ornelas.” (J.A. 
10) At the time of the stop, however, he believed the last 
name to be “Ornelas.” (Tr. at 105) 


After speaking with the dispatcher, Pautz then spoke 
with the motel clerk who disclosed that at 4:00 a.m. that 
morning, an individual named Ismael Ornelas had 
checked into room 4 with another individual, without 
prior reservations, and was due to check out at 11:00 a.m. 
(J.A. 10) 


Approximately 20 minutes after receiving the infor- 
mation from the dispatcher, Pautz’s partner, Detective 
Don Hurrle, arrived. Pautz called the Milwaukee DEA 
office and asked for a Narcotics and Dangerous Drug 
Information Systems, (hereinafter referred to as 
NADDIS), check on the names “Ismael Ornelas and 
Miguel Ledesma Ornelas.” (J.A. 10) The check was made 


by car phone with all the information being relayed ver- 
bally. 


A short time later, Pautz was advised that both 
names appeared on NADDIS and was given the following 
information: “Miguel Ledesma Ornelas, also known as 
Miguel Ledesma-Lemus, with a date of birth of Septem- 
ber 29, 1945, 5'6", 150 pounds, Hispanic male was a heroin 
dealer from El Centro, California; Ismael Ornelas, Jr., 
from Tucson, Arizona was a 1,000 kilogram a month 
cocaine dealer.” (Tr. at 35) (Emphasis added) Pautz was 
not familiar with the geography of California and did not 
know that San Jose and El Centro, California are 600 
miles apart. (J.A. 10) 


There were no wants or warrants for either name 
listed in the NADDIS. (J.A. 58) Pautz stated, “And these 
were just names that we had received. Not necessarily the 
people that could have been in the room.” (Tr. at 35) 
Pautz was asked if he checked the DOB of the registered 
owner of the car to determine whether it matched the 
DOB which had been received from NADDIS. He 
responded, “I didn’t have the date of birth. Or I would 
have done it.” (Tr. at 57) He was then asked if he 
attempted through his dispatcher to get the DOB of the 
registered owner of the car. He responded, “All 50 states 
are different, and I didn’t call, have them call California 
because I didn’t think that they could get that informa- 
tion from California, and I don’t know that they could 
today.” (Tr. at 58) 


Detective Hurrle was similarly asked what he did to 
determine if the registered owner of the car and the 
NADDIS hit were the same person, in that one person 


was from San Jose and the other from El Centro. He 


responded, “Nothing. We didn’t know his date of birth at 
the time.” (Tr. at 327) 


Sergeant Robert Wiggins, of the Milwaukee County 
Sheriff’s Department, who was in charge of the communi- 
cations bureau, second shift, testified that his bureau 
routinely runs Department of Motor Vehicle computer 
checks using state license plate numbers to obtain the 
owner's name, residence, and the make of car to which 
the license is listed. 


In response to a request by the defense in preparation 
for the suppression hearings, he ran the license plate on 
the 1981 Oldsmobile in question and found it registered 
to Miguel Ornelas, also known as “Ledesma Miguel 
Ornelas.” He also received the date of birth and other 
identifying information for the registered owner of the 
Oldsmobile by running a second “administrative” request 
(J.A. 16) The registered owner had a date of birth of 
September 19, 1963. (Tr. at 362) 


At the hearing, Wiggins affirmed that “All the police 
officers on the street know about the system and know 
how it’s used, and use it on a regular basis.” (Tr. at 358) 


Deputy Sheriff Gil Pagan testified that the vast 
majority of persons of Spanish descent who use the last 
names of their parents, use the father’s name first, fol- 
lowed by the mother’s maiden name. Pagan confirmed 
that the sheriff's department “never made any attempts 


to train their officers as to this potential problem.” (Tr. at 
291) 


Of further interest was the fact that the officers 
recovered the certificate of title from the car. The name on 
the certificate was “Ornelas, followed by the word 
Miguel, followed by the word Ledesma.” (Tr. at 291) 
According to Pagan, the person’s name would be Miguel 
Ornelas Ledesma. (Tr. at 292) 


Shortly after obtaining the information from 
NADDIS, Detective Lyle Luedke and his drug sniffing 
dog were summoned. Pautz testified, “It was not neces- 
sarily our intent to use the dog. We merely wanted to 
have that dog available.” The officers saw nothing 
unusual during the surveillance. (Tr. at 330) 


At about the expected time of departure, Luedke and 
Hurrle observed two people exit room 4. One individual 
loaded the trunk with luggage. (Tr. at 305) A few minutes 
later both men got into the car. The officers then moved 
their own vehicles to either side of the suspect vehicle. 
(Tr. at 306) 


Hurrle identified himself and requested identifica- 
tion of both individuals. Saul Ornelas produced a Califor- 
nia driver’s license and Ismael Ornelas-Ledesma gave a 
license showing an address of 3979 Alhamra Avenue, 
Martinez, California, 94553. Ismael Ornelas-Ledesma said 
they had just gotten in from California. (J.A. 12). 


Hurrle retained possession of the licenses of both 
individuals. (J.A. 12) Hurrle then asked if they were in 
possession of any contraband or drugs. Both “responded 
almost simultaneously, they had nothing illegal or any- 
thing like that.” (J.A. 12) Hurrle asked if he “could look 
through the vehicle,” and the driver said “sure.” (Tr. at 
151) 


While Luedke was searching the interior, Saul 
Ornelas “started to walk away, 15, 20 feet towards the 
mote! office and I [Hurrle] asked him where he was 
going, and would he please come back. And he complied, 
came back to the car.” (Tr. at 310) 


Hurrle searched the trunk area and luggage with 
negative results. Luedke then searched the car’s interior. 
While searching the interior rear passenger side armrest 
area, Luedcke testified: 


I discovered that a portion of that panel 
approximately ten inches by six inches appeared 
to be or didn’t appear but physically felt like it 
was somewhat loose. . . . | considered it was a 
possibility that panel had been removed and 
there could be something concealed in that area. 


I then went to the vent area of the right rear 
quarter panel. That would be the area just out- 
side of the passenger area where there’s a small 
plastic vent cover. I then looked at the vent 
cover. .. . I noticed a chrome colored Phillips 
screw, and a closer examination of that screw 
indicated that there was rust in the area of the 
Phillips head of the screw. . . . It indicated to me 
that that screw had been taken out at some 
time... . 


I then used a small pocket type knife to 
remove the Phillips screw from the plastic cover, 
I then looked into the area of the right rear 
quarter panel... . 


(Tr. at 155-56) 


Luedke claimed to have seen the top portion of a bag. 
Reaching in, he felt a hard block substance later found to 


be cocaine. Upon examining the bag’s contents, peti- 
tioners were handcuffed and arrested. (Tr. at 158) 


Luedke never asked permission of either petitioner to 
remove the screw. (Tr. at 172) 


At the hearing, Petitioners requested the government 
to produce the screw. It was brought to court and exam- 
ined by the magistrate judge. The findings were as fol- 
lows: “Let me say the Court also has examined the screw, 
and the Court could see no evidence of rust in there.” (Tr. 
at 424) 


The magistrate judge’s recommendations included 
the findings that there was reasonable suspicion to stop 
and that the petitioners were seized within the meaning 
of the Fourth Amendment. However, the magistrate 
judge found that the consent to search, given by the 
Petitioners, did not allow the officers to dismantle parts 
of the car to search for drugs, citing United States v. 
Garcia, 897 F.2d 1413, 1419-20 (7th Cir. 1990). (J.A. 20, 21) 


The magistrate judge opined that the: “record dis- 
closes no evidence from which Luedke could conclude 
that a hidden panel contained contraband.” (J.A. 22) His 
ultimate conclusion was that there was “not probable 
cause to dismantle the panel.” (J.A. 22) Nonetheless, the 
court denied the motion to suppress, noting that the 
drugs would have been inevitably discovered, citing Nix 
v. Williams, 467 U.S. 431 (1984). (J.A. 22, 25) 


The district court, although not relying on the inevi- 
table discovery theory, found there was reasonable suspi- 
cion to stop and that probable cause existed to search the 
car’s body cavity. (J.A. 33-34) The court noted that the 


9 


NADDIS check was for a person whose two last names 
were reversed from the proper last name of the car’s 
owner. The court reasoned that those “discrepancies as a 
basis for defeating probable cause introduces require- 
ments of certainty and absolute correctness into the anal- 
ysis that were expressly proscribed by the Supreme Court 
on Brown v. Texas.” (J.A. 34, n.3) 


The trial court also denied petitioners’ request to 
have a joint inspection of the car at the courthouse in 
order that an examination of the alleged loose panel 
could be conducted. (J.A. 43) Petitioners entered guilty 
pleas reserving their right to appeal under Fed. R. Crim. 
P. 11(2). 


The United States Court of Appeals for the Seventh 
Circuit rejected petitioners’ contention that the correct 
standard of review for determinations concerning the 
stop and search was de novo. “Although the question is 
close, we conclude that the district court did not commit 
clear error in holding that there was a lawful stop. And 
clear error is the test; ... “ (J.A. 65) 


The court also held the clear error standard of review 
should apply to probable cause determinations. (J.A. 68) 


It characterized the courier profile as “only a little 
better than a dragnet for Hispanics.” (J.A. 61) It reversed, 
because it found that the trial court could not have prop- 
erly believed that the panel was loose without having 
either heard the testimony, inspected the car, or asked the 
magistrate for a specific credibility determination. 


The court remanded for a specific determination 
whether the panel was loose. (J.A. 72-73) It held that if 


10 


the testimony of Luedke was found not to be credible, the 
motions to suppress should be granted. The court speci- 
fically noted “that the interior of a 1981 Olds unlike that 
of a Rolls Royce is apt to be rather worn and battered 
after eleven years.” If credible, the trial court would have 
to determine whether the somewhat loose panel on an 
eleven-year-old car could transform reasonable suspicion 
into probable cause. (J.A. 69-70) 


Upon remand, the trial court referred the remanded 
issue to the magistrate judge who determined that 
“except for the rule of Falsus In Uno Falsus In Omnibus, | 
have no reason not to believe the panel was loose.” (J.A. 
79) The magistrate again concluded that there was not 
probable cause (J.A. 80) on the basis of the somewhat 
loose panel, coupled with the “questionable drug courier 
profile,” (J.A. 79) and the “convoluted and mismatched 
NADDIS reports”. (J.A. 80) 


On appeal by the Government, the district court dis- 
agreed with the magistrate and denied the motions to 
suppress. (J.A. 85-90) Again Petitioners instituted a sec- 
ond appeal arguing that de novo was the correct standard 
of review. (Main brief, filed 11-17-94, at pp. 2-6). The 
court of appeals’ two-page order reaffirmed that its 
review was deferential. “The district court's finding of 
probable cause may be set aside only if clearly erroneous. 
We do not find clear error .. . ” (J.A. 92)

SUMMARY OF THE ARGUMENT 


1. Appellate courts apply varying standards of 
review which are dependent upon whether the issue des- 
ignated as one of fact, a mixed question of fact and law, 
or of law. Courts frequently quote Brown v. Allen, 344 U.S. 
443, 506 (1953) for its definition of issues of fact as being 
“basic, primary, or historical fact: facts in the sense of a 
recital of external events and the credibility of their nar- 
rators.” In Townsend v. Sain, 372 U.S. 293, 306 n.6 (1963), 
the Court stated, “So-called mixed questions of fact and 
law, which require the application of a legal standard to 
the historical-fact determinations, are not facts in this 
sense.” 


Once a designation of the issue as being one of fact is 
made, the clearly erroneous standard of review is 
employed. If the designation is one of law, a de novo 
review will be afforded. Mixed questions of fact and law 
have been generally reviewed de novo. The exceptions 
typically involve cases where the trial court is uniquely 
situated to evaluate credibility, demeanor, and local con- 
cerns. As such, it is said the trial court is in a better 
position than the appellate court to correctly determine 
the issue. Questions of intent to discriminate, competency 
to stand trial, and juror impartiality are some areas where 
this Court has found that deference to the trial court is 
warranted. 


2. Historically, in the area of search and seizure, this 
Court has consistently applied the de novo standard of 
review. This Court has examined de novo appeals on 
direct review of state court decisions, review of lower 
federal courts, and habeas review of state court decisions. 


12 


Such de novo reviews are generally conducted with an 
independent assessment of the basic facts, while giving 
appropriate deference to credibility determinations. 


3. This Court recently, in Thompson v. Keohane, 64 
US.L.W. 4027 (U.S. 11/28/95), had occasion to review 
whether, for Miranda purposes, a person was “in cus- 
tody.” The lower federal courts held that the state court's 
determination that the person was not in custody was a 
question of fact, which merited the presumption of cor- 
rectness under 28 U.S.C. § 2254(d). This Court held that 
the person was “in custody”. An “in custody” determina- 
tion was one which required the application of a set of 
historical facts to an objective legal standard. The ulti- 
mate question which necessitated a weighing of the 
“totality of the circumstances”, was a question for inde- 


pendent review. 


To determine if a person is “in custody”, the court 
must evaluate whether a reasonable man would believe 
he was in custody. When trial judges engage in this type 
of decisionmaking process, they do so with a view to 
identifying recurrent patterns and advancing uniform

Plenary review fosters the orderly development of 
the law, by reducing conflicting decisions of lower courts. 
It promotes consistency and uniformity in law develop- 
ment. Since this Court hears only a small fraction of the 
cases which are heard by federal courts of appeal, the 
lower appellate tribunals are relegated the primary

important that lower appellate courts have direction from 
this Court in exercising their collective judgment in defin-

a corollary, it is essential that lower appellate courts have 
the independence to exercise their appropriate appellate 
responsibilities. 


The development of the law can be best served when 
the focus of appellate review is upon the reasonableness 
of the particular conduct which gives rise to the search or 
seizure. It is far less productive for the development of 
consistent and predictable applications of the law, to have 
the appellate analysis emphasis a means by which to 
establish some basis for sustaining the decision below.

Deferential review of legal issues, especially in the 
search and seizure context, is contrary to one of the

should be subject to full and meaningful checks and 
balances. If it is not, it is akin to a tyranny of mediocrity. 


The constitutional core values, implicit in the concept 
of probable cause, require the protections which plenary 
review fosters. 


Ill. THE CLEARLY ERRONEOUS STANDARD IN 
NON-WARRANT CASES IS CONTRARY TO THE 
POLICY OBJECTIVE ENUNCIATED IN GATES. 


Illinois v. Gates, 462 U.S. 213 (1983) abandoned the 
two-pronged test concerning an informant’s veracity and 
reliability and reaffirmed the totality of the circumstances 
test as the basis for the issuance of a warrant. Gates noted 
that warrants are often drafted by non-lawyers in the 
midst and haste of a criminal investigation. Such persons 
do not keep abreast of each judicial refinement of the 
nature of probable cause. The Court stated that “probable 
cause is a fluid concept - turning on the assessment of 
probabilities in particular factual contexts — not readily, 
or even usefully, reduced to a neat set of legal rules.” 
Gates, 462 U.S. at 232. 


37 


The rule which the Gates’ decision pronounces was 
that: “after-the-fact scrutiny by courts of the sufficiency 
of the affidavit should not take the form of de novo 
review.” Gates, 462 U.S. at 236. The Gates Court reaf- 
firmed, at 212, that there should not be “A grudging or 
negative attitude by reviewing courts toward warrants,” 
citing Ventresca, 380 U.S., at 108. Failure to give deference 
is inconsistent with the Fourth Amendment's strong pref- 
erence for searches conducted pursuant to a warrant.” 
Gates, 462 U.S. at 236. 


The Gates Court gave further evidence of its intent to 
give deference to the magistrate’s decision when the offi- 
cer makes a determination to apply for a warrant. 
“Reflecting this strong preference for the warrant process, 
the traditional standard of review of an issuing magis- 
trate’s probable cause determination has been that so 
long as the magistrate had a substantial basis for 
... conclud[ing] that a search would uncover evidence of 
wrongdoing, the Fourth Amendment requires no more.” 
Gates at 236. This standard “serves the purpose of encour- 


aging recourse to the warrant procedure .. . ” Gates, 462 
U.S. at 237. 


In United States v. Spears, 965 F.2d 262 (7th Cir. 1992), 
the United States Court of Appeals for the Seventh Cir- 
cuit announced that it would apply the clearly erroneous 
standard of review to both warrant and non-warrant 
cases. Spears was cited as the basis for applying the 
clearly erroneous standard in this case. (J.A. 68) The 
rationale in Spears, at 270-71, was as follows: 


It would be anomalous to require a more search- 
ing review of a district judge’s probable cause 


38 


determination, made after a full adversary pro- 
ceeding . . . Because there is little to distinguish 
a magistrate’s probable cause determination in a 
warrant case and a district judge’s probable 
cause determination in a nonwarrant case, 
appellate review should be the same for both. In 
warrant cases, the magistrate is the front-line 
judicial officer making the initial determination. 
In non-warrant cases, it is the district judge who 
makes the initial determination, who asks the 
same question the magistrate asks in a warrant 
case: is there probable cause .. . If there is 
probable cause, the scope of the search without 
a warrant is no greater than one with a warrant 
... It is the frontline officer in both cases who 
conducts the initial inquiry, who concludes there 
is or there is not probable cause, and an appel- 
late panel should not substitute its judgment in 
either case. 


In announcing that the standard of review in warrant 
and non-warrant should be the same, the Spears court 
ignored the essential rationale in Gates, which was to 
promote the warrant process. The Spears panel justified 
this decision, in part, by asserting that the scope of the 
search with or without the warrant would be the same. 
This analysis ignores a practical premise historically 
espoused by this Court, that the magistrate is a gate- 
keeper, to prevent unwarranted Fourth Amendment 
_ intrusions. More particularly, the magistrate serves to 
screen applications for warrants which fail to articulate 
probable cause; limit the scope of the proposed search; 
and prescribe the terms and conditions upon which such 
search shall be carried out. 


39 


Those two factors reflect this Court's long stated 
belief that the magistrate should stand between the police 
officer’s zeal in law enforcement and the privacy interests 
of the individual to be free from arbitrary police conduct. 
Gates rewards the officer who took the time to secure the 
warrant by reaffirming, “resolution of doubtful or margi- 
nal cases in this area should be largely determined by the 
preference to be accorded to warrants,” citing United 
States v. Ventresca, 380 U.S. 102, 109 (1965). 


Further, this Court has recognized that once a war- 
rant is procured, intrusion upon interests protected by 
the Fourth Amendment are less severe. Gates, 462 U.S. at 
237 n.10. By making the standard of review the same for 
warrant and non-warrant cases, the Spears court seriously 
erodes the incentive to procure the warrant by police and 
prosecutors. 


The Spears court also justified its decision by listing 
other areas in which it has applied the clearly erroneous 
standard. The list included exigent circumstances, citing 
to a 1991 Seventh Circuit case. Spears, 965 F.2d at 271. This 
reliance is similarly misplaced. In Minnesota v. Olson, 495 
U.S. 91, 100 (1990), the Court conducted a de novo review 
involving a “fact-specific application of the proper legal 
standard” to the question whether exigent circumstances 
existed. 


There was no deference in Olson to the lower court's 
legal finding despite that independent review resulted in 
affirmance of the decision below. The Spears decision, 
which places emphasis on the district court’s position as a 
front line adjudicator during the conduct of a probable

The Spears court also ignored the rationale in Stone v.

which the Court alluded to in Powell.
