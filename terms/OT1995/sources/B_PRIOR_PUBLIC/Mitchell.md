### Wisconsin v. Mitchell, No. 92-515

Merits decision, 1993-06-11

#### Chronology and Posture

Argued 1993-04-21; decided 1993-06-11. Review of the Wisconsin Supreme Court, 169 Wis.2d153, 485 N.W.2d807 (1992), which held the penalty enhancement unconstitutional.

Question: Whether the First Amendment forbids enhancement of the penalty for an independently criminal battery because the defendant intentionally selected the victim on account of race, and whether evidentiary use of speech makes the provision substantially overbroad.

A jury convicted Mitchell of aggravated battery after he directed a group attack on a white youth. The jury also found intentional racial selection under the applicable Wisconsin statute. The ordinary two-year maximum became seven years, and Mitchell received four years. Section939.645 applies when a person commits a covered crime and intentionally selects the victim, or property because of the owner’s or occupant’s status, on account of race, religion, color, disability, sexual orientation, national origin or ancestry. It requires a special verdict on those predicates and does not apply where proof of such status is already required for conviction of the underlying crime. The felony maximum may increase by up to five years and the fine by up to $5,000; the separate misdemeanor tiers are not the sentence at issue. Amendments enacted in 1992 are outside this case. The state court characterized the practical effect as punishment of thought; it did not supply a narrowing definition that eliminates the intentional-selection predicate.

Chief Justice Stone-Zsela and Justices White, Blackmun, Stevens, O’Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter and Thomas participated in argument and decision.

#### Judgment

Reversed and remanded, 9–0.

| Component | Disposition and named votes | Remedy and limits |
|---|---|---|
| First Amendment validity of the intentional-selection enhancement | Reverse: Stone-Zsela, White, Blackmun, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas (9); no dissent | The State may enhance punishment for this independently criminal battery on the proved selection predicate; abstract belief remains protected. |

#### Controlling Propositions and Authority

##### Federal constitutional effect is not settled by a state court’s characterization

**Controlling proposition:** The Court accepts the state court’s authoritative construction of statutory terms, but independently determines the federal constitutional effect of the law so construed. Wisconsin’s characterization of the intentional-selection enhancement as punishment of thought therefore does not resolve the First Amendment question or remove the statutory requirement of an independently committed crime.

**Authority:** White’s Parts I and II; Stone-Zsela, White, Blackmun, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas (9 Justices). This proposition has direct majority support.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that respect for state-law construction does not surrender the federal constitutional inquiry. Ferber and Terminiello require acceptance of authoritative state definitions that determine what a law prohibits. The Wisconsin court here did not redefine a statutory term to eliminate the underlying crime or intentional victim selection. It instead characterized the practical effect of considering discriminatory motive as punishment of thought. Whether that effect violates the First Amendment is the question this Court must decide. The State’s reply that the statute concerns conduct is not, by itself, a complete answer either: the enhanced penalty does depend on a particular motive. The Court must therefore examine whether that motive is constitutionally relevant to the criminal act rather than presume validity from a label. This method preserves binding state-law meanings while allowing independent federal review. It creates no license to rewrite the selection requirement or decide unrelated state-law sentencing questions.

**Precedent treatment:**

- New York v. Ferber, 458 U.S.747: authoritative state construction accepted.
- Terminiello v. Chicago, 337 U.S.1: binding construction of operative state-law terms distinguished from the federal characterization at issue here.

##### Intentional racial selection can aggravate an independently criminal battery

**Controlling proposition:** The First Amendment permits an enhanced penalty for an independently criminal battery when the defendant intentionally selects the victim because of race under the applicable statutory predicate. The State may prove a relevant motive through lawful evidence, but may not punish abstract racist belief, expression or association without its legally relevant connection to the offense or another legitimate issue.

**Authority:** White’s Parts I and II; Stone-Zsela, White, Blackmun, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas (9 Justices). This proposition has direct majority support.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that the selection requirement identifies a feature of the criminal attack, not an independent offense of offensive belief. Barclay permits consideration of racial motivation connected to the murder there; the Dawson decision preserves that distinction while prohibiting irrelevant proof of abstract belief. A legislature may account for the distinct individual and community harms it reasonably attributes to deliberately discriminatory attacks. Hishon and Roberts likewise show that considering discriminatory purpose does not automatically convert regulation of conduct into punishment of protected expression. The R.A.V. decision invalidated substantial reach into protected speech and reserved the broader question of distinctions within unprotected speech categories. This statute instead requires an independently criminal act and intentional selection of its target. Nothing in that reserved question requires invalidation here. The State must prove the actual statutory nexus, and speech evidence remains subject to relevance and other evidentiary limits. No punishment for belief alone or automatic admission of offensive statements is authorized.

**Precedent treatment:**

- Dawson v. Delaware, March 9, 1992: entered connection-to-legitimate-issue limit applied; abstract belief remains protected.
- Barclay v. Florida, 463 U.S.939: racial motivation connected to the criminal act distinguished from abstract association evidence.
- R.A.V. v. City of St. Paul, June 22, 1992: entered substantial-overbreadth holding distinguished; noncontrolling content-selection reasoning is not imported.
- Hishon v. King & Spalding, 467 U.S.69, and Roberts v. United States Jaycees, 468 U.S.609: regulation of discriminatory conduct distinguished from suppression of a protected message.
- Runyon v. McCrary, 427 U.S.160: protection for belief does not confer a right to engage in unlawful discrimination.

##### Speculative future evidentiary use does not establish substantial overbreadth

**Controlling proposition:** The possibility that a person might suppress protected views for fear that those views could later help prove intentional selection in a future crime does not establish substantial overbreadth of this enhancement. The First Amendment permits relevant statements to prove criminal intent, subject to evidentiary safeguards and Dawson’s prohibition on punishment for abstract belief.

**Authority:** White’s Part III; White, Blackmun, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas (8 Justices). This proposition has direct majority support.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that the proposed chilling chain is too speculative to invalidate the statute on its face. It assumes that a person will withhold protected expression because the person might later commit a covered crime and the expression might then prove the required discriminatory selection. The statute does not prohibit the present expression itself. Haupt establishes that earlier statements may be relevant evidence of criminal intent even though the statements could lawfully have been made. Dawson supplies the necessary boundary: irrelevant belief or association is not a legitimate basis for punishment. Ordinary rules of relevance, reliability and admissibility continue to operate, so the decision does not admit every offensive remark a prosecutor offers. The contrary argument would turn legitimate evidentiary use into a general constitutional obstacle to proving motive. That attenuated concern is unlike a law directly reaching a substantial range of protected speech. It therefore does not sustain this facial challenge to the intentional-selection enhancement.

**Precedent treatment:**

- Haupt v. United States, 330 U.S.631: relevant earlier statements may establish criminal intent subject to careful evidentiary scrutiny.
- Dawson, March 9, 1992: the limit on irrelevant abstract-belief evidence preserved.
- R.A.V., June 22, 1992: direct substantial reach into protected expression distinguished from speculative evidentiary chilling.

#### Current-Law Effect

Before this decision: R.A.V. supplied substantial-overbreadth law and Dawson required a legitimate connection for belief evidence. After this decision: all nine sustain enhancement for this intentional-selection battery; eight reject the distinct speculative-chill ground in a controlling opinion. The reserved within-unprotected-speech question remains open. No later jury-allocation rule, independent speech crime or blanket admissibility rule enters.

#### Mandate, Remedy, and Stage

The Wisconsin Supreme Court judgment is reversed and the case remanded for proceedings consistent with the enhancement’s First Amendment validity. The Court removes the constitutional obstacle but does not issue a new sentencing order or decide equal-protection and vagueness claims outside the granted question. The intermediate court found the former waived and rejected the latter; this Court does not alter those unreviewed determinations or erase ordinary evidentiary objections.

Supreme Court review is complete and the Wisconsin proceedings resume on remand. The First Amendment obstacle is removed; no new sentence, acquittal, decision on the unreviewed Fourteenth Amendment claims or subsequent evidentiary ruling is inferred.
