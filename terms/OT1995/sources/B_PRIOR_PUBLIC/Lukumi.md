### Church of the Lukumi Babalu Aye, Inc. v. City of Hialeah, No. 91-948

Merits decision, 1993-06-11

#### Chronology and Posture

Argued 1992-11-04; decided 1993-06-11. Review of the Eleventh Circuit’s affirmance, 936 F.2d 586 (1991), of the district court’s judgment at 723 F.Supp.1467 (S.D.Fla.1989).

Question: Whether Hialeah’s four interlocking animal-sacrifice ordinances violate the Free Exercise Clause through religious targeting and unequal pursuit of the asserted secular interests.

After the church announced its planned Santeria center, the city adopted resolutions opposing ritual sacrifice and four substantive ordinances. The district court upheld them after trial; the Eleventh Circuit affirmed without relying on the district court’s child-welfare interest. The separate individual defendants had received legislative immunity, and other rejected claims are not within this review. Sincerity and religious status are not disputed. Ordinance 87-40 incorporates Florida’s animal-cruelty chapter except its penalties; the city’s necessity construction treats religious sacrifice as unnecessary while permitting food slaughter, pest control, euthanasia and other secular killings. Ordinance 87-52 prohibits owning, keeping, possessing, sacrificing or slaughtering enumerated animals or any other animal with intent to use it for food, and limits application to killing in any ritual regardless of whether flesh or blood will actually be consumed. Its exception permits licensed establishments to slaughter animals specifically raised for food where properly zoned or permitted under state/local law and Agriculture Department rules. Ordinance 87-71 defines sacrifice as unnecessary killing, tormenting, torturing or mutilating an animal in a public or private ritual or ceremony not primarily for food consumption and prohibits such sacrifice citywide. Ordinance 87-72 defines slaughter as killing for food and requires a properly zoned slaughterhouse meeting health, safety and sanitation codes; it expressly exempts persons or organizations slaughtering or processing for sale small numbers of hogs or cattle per week in accordance with the state-law exemption. Each ordinance permits a fine up to $500, imprisonment up to sixty days, or both. These are distinct exemptions and operative provisions, not one interchangeable exception.

Chief Justice Stone-Zsela and Justices White, Blackmun, Stevens, O’Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter and Thomas participated in argument and decision.

#### Judgment

Reversed and remanded, 9–0 in the judgment; all four challenged ordinances are invalid as the scheme presented.

#### Controlling Propositions and Authority

##### Objective religious targeting defeats neutrality

**Controlling proposition:** A law burdening religious practice is not neutral when its text, definitions, exemptions and operation identify religious conduct for distinctive restriction, even if it avoids naming the religion. Hialeah’s interlocking scheme targets Santeria sacrifice on that objective showing; adverse impact alone and the words “ritual” or “sacrifice” alone do not establish the rule’s trigger.

**Authority:** Kennedy’s Parts II-A-1 and II-A-3; Stone-Zsela, Stevens, Scalia, Kennedy, Thomas (5 Justices). This proposition has direct majority support.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that the city’s design, not a label of secular purpose, determines neutrality here. Fowler forbids discriminatory treatment of religious exercise, and Smith does not shield a law whose object is to suppress it. Ordinance 87-71 excludes killings primarily for food while capturing offerings to the orishas. Ordinance 87-52 reaches ritual killing with a food intent but spares the licensed food establishments described in its own exception. Ordinance 87-40’s necessity judgment treats religious reasons as insufficient while accepting comparable secular reasons. Those provisions leave almost only the Santeria practice prohibited. The city therefore cannot prevail merely by avoiding an express denominational name. Ordinance 87-72 reaches more secular conduct, but operates with the same scheme; its independent validity in another setting is reserved. The words ritual and sacrifice can have secular uses, and unequal impact alone is insufficient. The demonstrated definitions and operation supply the targeting conclusion without making subjective legislative motive an indispensable condition.

**Precedent treatment:**

- Fowler v. Rhode Island, 345 U.S.67: discrimination in the treatment of religious exercise prohibited.
- Employment Division v. Smith, 494 U.S.872: targeting falls outside the neutral-law rule; that rule is not reconsidered here.
- McDaniel v. Paty, 435 U.S.618: special religious disabilities distinguished from neutral regulation.
- Bowen v. Roy, 476 U.S.693: the individualized-exemption discussion supports requiring a compelling reason for denying religious hardship treatment where comparable secular reasons are assessed.

##### Substantial exemptions for comparable secular harm defeat general applicability

**Controlling proposition:** A law is not generally applicable where it substantially leaves unregulated comparable secular conduct threatening the interests invoked to burden religious exercise. Each challenged ordinance fails that minimum requirement here, including 87-72’s express state-law exemption for small-number hog or cattle operations; the Court does not define every boundary of general applicability.

**Authority:** Kennedy’s Part II-B; Stone-Zsela, White, Stevens, Scalia, Kennedy, Thomas (6 Justices). This proposition has direct majority support.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that a city cannot pursue these asserted harms only when they arise from religious conduct. Smith’s general-applicability boundary and Fowler’s equality principle require examination of the comparable activities left outside the prohibitions. Food slaughter, hunting, fishing, pest eradication and permitted euthanasia undermine the claim that the first three ordinances comprehensively prevent unnecessary animal killing. The public-health distinction is equally deficient: disposal of carcasses and consumption of uninspected meat create the same risks regardless of religious motivation, yet the scheme leaves comparable secular conduct alone. Ordinance 87-72 has its own defect, an express exception for small-number hog and cattle operations qualifying under state law. The city does not explain why those operations escape the asserted health and cruelty concerns. This is substantial underinclusion, not an inconsequential imperfection in a comprehensive law. The Court need not define a universal general-applicability test or hold that every exemption invalidates every regulation. The unequal pursuit of the identified harms resolves these ordinances.

**Precedent treatment:**

- Smith, 494 U.S.872: general-applicability requirement applied, without reopening its neutral-law holding.
- Fowler, 345 U.S.67: unequal treatment of religious exercise applied.
- Hobbie v. Unemployment Appeals Commission, 480 U.S.136: protection against unequal treatment of religious observers preserved.

##### Narrow tailoring independently fails for every ordinance

**Controlling proposition:** A restriction of religious practice that is nonneutral or not generally applicable must satisfy compelling-interest scrutiny and narrow tailoring. These ordinances fail narrow tailoring independently of whether the asserted ends could be compelling because comparable harms are left unregulated and neutral, narrower controls on disposal, care, killing methods and location could address the city’s concerns.

**Authority:** Kennedy’s Part III, narrow-tailoring ground; Stone-Zsela, White, Stevens, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas (7 Justices). This proposition has direct majority support.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that the city’s chosen means are not narrowly drawn to its asserted ends. Yoder and McDaniel require scrutiny of the actual burden, not acceptance of an abstractly important objective. General disposal rules could address carcasses left in public places; care requirements could address the conditions in which animals are held; and rules about killing methods could address cruelty without classifying the religious reason as unnecessary. The district court’s view that secrecy makes narrower rules unenforceable does not explain why a ban on private sacrifice would work better than rules against publicly observable disposal. A slaughterhouse label likewise supplies no justification by itself, and the first three ordinances apply regardless of location. The exemption in 87-72 further defeats the fit asserted for its own location rule. Under Arkansas Writers’ Project, failure of narrow tailoring suffices to invalidate the challenged restriction. The Court does not decide entitlement to an exemption from a genuinely evenhanded anti-cruelty law.

**Precedent treatment:**

- Wisconsin v. Yoder, 406 U.S.205: rigorous justification of restrictions on religious exercise applied.
- McDaniel, 435 U.S.618: demanding scrutiny of religious disabilities applied.
- Arkansas Writers’ Project, Inc. v. Ragland, 481 U.S.221: lack of narrow tailoring independently suffices under strict scrutiny.
- Schneider v. State, 308 U.S.147: collateral public harms cannot justify unnecessary suppression when direct regulation is available.

##### The selectively pursued interests are not compelling in this application

**Controlling proposition:** Where the government burdens protected religious conduct but leaves appreciable comparable harm unregulated despite feasible controls, it has not established that its asserted interest justifies the selective restriction at the highest constitutional level. Hialeah’s substantial underinclusion independently defeats its compelling-interest showing for this scheme; the decision does not declare animal welfare or public health inherently unimportant.

**Authority:** Kennedy’s Part III, compelling-interest ground; Stone-Zsela, White, Stevens, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas (7 Justices). This proposition has direct majority support.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court also holds that the city has not established a compelling justification for the discrimination actually imposed. Strict scrutiny examines the interest in applying the particular restriction, not merely the importance of public health or humane treatment in general. The city permits many secular killings and fails to address comparable disposal and inspection risks while prohibiting the religious practice. Those choices leave appreciable harm of the same asserted kind outside the scheme. Florida Star illustrates why an interest cannot sustain an exceptional speech restriction when substantial equivalent harm remains unaddressed, and the same defect is decisive here. The city’s assertion that secular food use or pest control is obviously necessary does not explain why religious reasons alone must be discounted. This ground is independent of the narrower-means failure. It does not resolve the constitutionality of a comprehensive anti-cruelty law or deny the seriousness of the interests identified. It holds only that the selective scheme does not establish the required justification.

**Precedent treatment:**

- Florida Star v. B.J.F., 491 U.S.524: substantial unregulated comparable harm undermines the asserted exceptional justification; no broader rule is imported from a separate writing.
- Yoder, 406 U.S.205: the asserted interest must justify the particular restriction at the required level.

#### Current-Law Effect

Before this decision: Smith, Fowler and the governing strict-scrutiny principles governed religious targeting. After this decision: five directly adopt the objective-neutrality ground, six the minimum general-applicability application and seven each independent strict-scrutiny failure. All four ordinances are invalid as presented. The three-Justice enactment-history discussion is noncontrolling. No Marks synthesis is necessary, no free-exercise exemption regime replaces Smith, and no majority decides an exemption from a genuinely general anti-cruelty law. The Court’s June 7 private-access holding remains distinct.

#### Separate Positions Relevant to the Decision

Kennedy’s Part II-A-2, joined by Stone-Zsela and Stevens, treats the sequence of enactment and contemporaneous official statements as corroboration of the objectively established discriminatory object under Arlington Heights and Feeney. Public hostility is not converted into a finding that every speaker’s intent belonged to the council. That discussion has three joins and supplies no controlling motive test. Scalia concurs in part and in the judgment. He regards neutrality and general applicability as substantially overlapping and rejects the attempt to identify a single subjective motive in a collective legislature, consistent with his Edwards dissent. A law objectively targeting religious practice fails even if its sponsors profess only humane concerns; he joins all of Kennedy’s opinion except II-A-2. Souter concurs in part and in the judgment, joining I, III and IV. He distinguishes the settled prohibition on persecution from Smith’s broader claim that genuinely neutral and general laws need no free-exercise justification. In his view Smith’s relation to Sherbert, Yoder and the unemployment cases, its briefing history and its textual foundation warrant reconsideration in a case that requires it. He neither overrules Smith here nor claims novelty alone permits abandoning precedent; the Casey approach to stare decisis remains a separate, contextual discipline. Blackmun, joined by O’Connor, concurs in the judgment. They would require compelling justification and the least restrictive available means for substantial burdens on religious exercise even from general laws. They regard discrimination of this kind as necessarily failing that standard, while preserving the seriousness of animal-welfare concerns and reserving whether a sincere general anti-cruelty law must provide an exemption. These additional approaches remain noncontrolling.

#### Mandate, Remedy, and Stage

The Eleventh Circuit judgment is reversed and the case remanded for declaratory and appropriate injunctive relief preventing enforcement of the four challenged ordinances as the discriminatory scheme presented. The Court does not invalidate Florida’s entire animal-cruelty chapter, decide damages, reopen the individual defendants’ immunity or grant immunity from neutral animal-welfare laws. Any severability determination must identify an independently lawful operative provision; it cannot preserve the same religious discrimination under a different label. Whether Ordinance 87-72 could survive as an independently enacted, differently situated law is not decided.

Supreme Court review is complete. The lower courts implement the express relief against the discriminatory scheme without treating every Florida cruelty provision as invalid or inferring a damages award. The private-access holding in Lamb’s Chapel and the religious-targeting holding here do not resolve Zobrest’s distinct question.
