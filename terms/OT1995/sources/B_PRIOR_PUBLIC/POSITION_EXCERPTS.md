| Kennedy | Thomas | Concurrence in part and in judgment: actual statutory forfeiture covered; reserve whether all historical in rem forfeitures punished owners. | Reverse and remand; does not join Scalia’s exclusive test |

#### Separate Positions Relevant to the Decision

Scalia agrees that taking lawful property under these provisions is partly punitive and constitutes a fine. He rejects the need to establish owner blameworthiness across all historical forfeitures and would measure excessiveness through the property’s traditional closeness to the offense, treating value as irrelevant to that particular instrumentality inquiry. His exclusive test is not the Court’s rule. Kennedy, joined by Thomas, accepts the actual statutory conclusion but cautions against anachronistically imposing one personal-punishment theory on every historical forfeiture. He reserves whether such forfeitures invariably punish their owners and the constitutional question involving a truly innocent owner; he does not adopt Scalia’s exclusive remand test. Stone accepts coverage from the actual culpability-sensitive and deterrent statutory operation. He would examine the interest and supported value taken, seriousness and extent of wrongdoing, relevant owner culpability and the property’s actual role as nonexclusive considerations. He adopts no mandatory ratio, exhaustive checklist or later gross-disproportionality formula. The common remand leaves that choice of test open rather than converting Stone’s proposals into law.



## BFP.md

Source full copy: terms/OT1995/sources/B_PRIOR_PUBLIC/BFP.md

#### Opinion Topology

| Writing | Author | Joined by | Relationship to judgment | Scope |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Opinion of the Court | Souter | Stone-Zsela, Blackmun, Stevens, Ginsburg | Reverse and remand both routes | Entire opinion; both controlling propositions and all limits. |
| Dissent | Scalia | O'Connor, Kennedy, Thomas | Would affirm both routes | Conclusive value for regular noncollusive real-property mortgage foreclosure only. |


#### Separate Positions Relevant to the Decision

Scalia, joined by O'Connor, Kennedy and Thomas, dissents. They read reasonably equivalent value in the legal market Congress actually included: a compelled mortgage foreclosure conducted under the State's governing requirements. Ordinary market value assumes a voluntary sale, and a judicially constructed reasonable foreclosure price would replace the legal conditions that define this transaction. The dissent's rule would be conclusive only for a noncollusive real-property mortgage foreclosure complying with governing law. An irregularity permitting state-law invalidation would remove that conclusive treatment and require the otherwise applicable federal inquiry; insolvency, timing, other avoidance grounds and recovery would remain separate. The dissent relies on the property-law setting recognized by Butner and the concrete harm that uncertain titles and avoidance risk can cause lenders, purchasers and bids. It would affirm both lower-court routes on the value element, not on the rejected theory that BFP lacked a property interest. It does not extend its rule to tax sales, personal property, collusion or defective procedure.



## Bray.md

Source full copy: terms/OT1995/sources/B_PRIOR_PUBLIC/Bray.md

#### Opinion Topology

| Writing | Author | Joined by | Judgment relationship | Scope |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Court opinion, Parts I, II, IV and V | Scalia | Stone-Zsela, White, Kennedy, Thomas | Court mandate | I: posture; II: targeted private right; IV: jurisdiction, fees, state remand; V: hindrance nonreach. |
| Plurality, Part III | Scalia | White, Kennedy, Thomas | Additional proposed grounds | No class-based animus here; broader exclusion of equal intrastate obstruction. Four supporters only. |
| Concurrence in part and in judgment | Stone-Zsela | No other Justice | Joins I, II, IV, V and mandate | Reserves class question and categorical travel extension. |
| Concurrence | Kennedy | No other Justice | Joins Scalia throughout | Federal statutory limits coexist with state enforcement responsibility. |
| Concurrence in judgment in part/dissent in part | Souter | No other Justice | Rejects deprivation/fee basis; disputes hindrance remand | Accepts settled Griffin/Scott constraints; no formal Court-opinion join. |
| Dissent | Stevens | Blackmun | Would affirm; alternatively hindrance consideration | Sex-specific coercion, travel access and distinct hindrance theory. |
| Dissent | O’Connor | Blackmun | Would affirm; alternatively hindrance consideration | Class-based denial of equal protection through organized obstruction. |


#### Separate Positions Relevant to the Decision

**Scalia Part III, joined by White, Kennedy and Thomas; plurality only.** These four would additionally find no class-based invidious animus here. A group defined merely by the activity the alleged tortfeasor opposes is insufficient; opposition to abortion differs from targeting women because of sex. They leave open whether women can be a qualifying class. Their purpose analysis uses Griffin, Scott and Feeney’s distinction between action because of a group’s disadvantage and action despite its effect. They also favor a broader exclusion of purely local obstruction applied without discrimination against interstate travelers. Neither additional ground has five votes.

**Stone-Zsela, concurring in part and in the judgment, no joins.** The independent private-right defect suffices. Opposition to abortion alone neither proves nor disproves sex discrimination; Griffin’s full class boundary need not be decided. He joins jurisdiction, fees, state remand and hindrance nonreach, but does not endorse the plurality’s categorical local-obstruction formulation. Those reservations prevent attribution of the additional grounds to the Court.

**Kennedy, concurring, no joins.** Kennedy joins Scalia throughout. Limited federal coverage does not remove state responsibility to enforce ordinary trespass and obstruction laws. Respect for separate federal and state authority cannot justify tolerating serious unlawful conduct. This observation adds no enforcement order or remand direction.

**Souter, concurring in the judgment in part and dissenting in part, no joins.** Souter accepts Griffin/Scott as settled statutory constructions and rejects the present deprivation theory and its fee basis. He would consider hindrance separately. A purpose to impede constituted state authorities supplies an additional limiting element and, in his view, need not carry the deprivation clause’s entire class-animus and privately protected-right restrictions. Overwhelmed police warrant further examination, not automatic liability. He disputes exclusion of that opportunity from affirmative remand directions. His view establishes no purpose finding and no successful federal claim.

**Stevens, joined by Blackmun, dissenting.** They would affirm federal relief. Deliberate exclusion from abortion services targets a choice available only to women, and organized blockades impair actual access by interstate patients. Griffin and travel protection reach this purposeful deprivation; equal obstruction of local patients is not a complete answer. Stevens also reads hindrance to reach organized interference with state protection. They would uphold relief or at least allow development of that theory. Neither their interpretation nor the asserted inference of purpose controls.

**O’Connor, joined by Blackmun, dissenting.** They regard organized blockades as deliberate denial of equal legal protection to women seeking lawful medical care. O’Connor’s position, consistent with her Scott dissent join, treats collective coercion preventing a protected class from enjoying its rights as the statute’s concern. They reject characterization of the exclusion as incidental disadvantage and would uphold federal relief; the distinct hindrance provision reinforces their position. This does not convert Casey’s governmental-regulation framework into a freestanding constitutional prohibition on private acts.



## Celotex.md

Source full copy: terms/OT1995/sources/B_PRIOR_PUBLIC/Celotex.md

#### Opinion Topology

| Writing | Author | Joined by | Relationship to judgment | Scope joined |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Opinion of the Court: June-coverage and execution/review-remedy portions | Thomas | Stone-Zsela, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Breyer | Reverse and remand | Exact operative order scope and conforming remedy with qualified relief/review routes; seven direct joins in these portions. |
| Opinion of the Court: actual-relatedness, sufficient interim §157 authority, and complete collateral-review portions | Thomas | O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Breyer | Reverse and remand | Six direct full-rationale votes on all three portions; Stone-Zsela does not join them. |
| Concurrence in part and in the judgment | Stone-Zsela | No other Justice | Reverse and remand; joins the coverage and remedy portions | Colorable jurisdiction and an order not transparently void require orderly challenge; does not decide actual §1334(b) relatedness or affirmatively resolve §157 interim authority. |
| Dissent | Stevens | Ginsburg | Would affirm enforcement authorization | Accepts June coverage but rejects use of relatedness and an interim label as sufficient authority under §157 for this non-Article III judge to restrain the Article III bond court. |


#### Separate Positions Relevant to the Decision

Chief Justice Stone-Zsela concurs in part and in the judgment. He joins the June-coverage and execution/review-remedy portions. The injunction had at least a colorable basis in the relationship between the bond, surety arrangements, and reorganization, and was not transparently void. Parties subject to such an operative order must challenge it in the issuing proceeding and through authorized review; they may not obtain contrary execution from another court. He therefore reverses and would set aside the execution authorization while leaving proper relief available. He does not join the Court's affirmative actual-relatedness or sufficient interim §157 determinations, or their complete collateral-review rationale. His ground neither protects an injunction lacking even an arguable jurisdictional basis nor decides ultimate statutory power to restrain every nondebtor surety. It supplies no contempt finding, perpetual restraint, or automatic appeal; Celotex's continuation burden and the qualified review routes remain.

Justice Stevens, joined by Justice Ginsburg, dissents. They accept that the June 13 order expressly reaches the bond proceeding and that immediate execution is actually related to the Chapter 11 case under §1334(b)'s nonexclusive district-court jurisdiction. Their objection concerns the allocation of adjudicative power to the bankruptcy judge under §157 and Article III. Northbrook's undertaking matured independently of Celotex's tort debt. A related controversy and the issuing judge's core label do not, in their view, empower that non-Article III judge to prevent the Article III bond court from deciding the undertaking when §157 assigns final determination to the district court. Calling the restraint interim does not answer that structural objection.

They accept that ordinary legal error does not generally authorize disobedience and do not reject orderly review of orders issued with the requisite authority. They regard that antecedent condition as unsatisfied here and would affirm enforcement of Northbrook's bond. They do not rely on the original October wording alone, deny all related jurisdiction, presume consent or a proved refusal, find exact collateral depletion, adopt a blanket ban on every nondebtor injunction, or assert the injunction's indefinite validity. Their ground concerns this statutory allocation and this restraint. It supplies no controlling rule and changes no amount of the undertaking.



## Clearwater.md

Source full copy: terms/OT1995/sources/B_PRIOR_PUBLIC/Clearwater.md

#### Opinion Topology

| Writing | Author | Joins | Judgment relationship |
|---|---|---|---|
| Opinion of the Court | O'Connor | Stevens, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg, Breyer | Eight votes for partial vacatur and bounded statutory remand; all holdings in this opinion. |
| Opinion concurring in part and dissenting in part | Stone-Zsela | No other Justice | Agrees with preservation of favorable relief and existing remand; would affirm the adverse identifying and written-policy determinations, subject to purpose and severability. |


#### Separate Positions Relevant to the Decision

Chief Justice Stone-Zsela concurs in leaving the Church's favorable relief and the purpose-and-severability remand undisturbed, but dissents from reopening the surviving identifying and written-policy requirements. He would affirm the Court of Appeals' judgment on the grounds stated in his opinion. RFRA governs future municipal enforcement even though the ordinance predates it, and the Church's inability to invoke an unenacted statute below does not forfeit its protection. The Church must establish a substantial burden; the City then bears production and persuasion on a compelling interest and the least restrictive means.

He regards the existing record as sufficient to invalidate the broad internal financial, operational, organizational, and recordkeeping demands under RFRA. The private-statement option still requires annual sworn information and supporting records, and criminal sanctions reinforce the burden. The sixty-day refund requirement, triggered by a voluntary refund promise, a written request, and willful nonpayment, also displaces the Church's religiously connected refund process. Fraud prevention is compelling, but targeted fraud law, limited identification, transaction-specific investigation, and accurate statements of promised refund terms address the shown secular harms more narrowly. He adds no constitutional holding against those provisions because RFRA suffices.

In his view, the limited identifying requirements establish no substantial religious burden on this record and perform ordinary civil identification without theological licensing or interruption of solicitation pending judicial review. The contemporaneous written-policy requirement, including its good-faith proviso, survives even assuming a substantial burden: concrete evidence of misleading refund representations supports requiring accurate disclosure of the policy voluntarily chosen, without dictating that policy or deciding spiritual entitlement. He also rejects the independent First Amendment challenges to those bounded surviving requirements, subject to purpose and severability.

He would leave the disputed sectarian-purpose issue for the district court because evidence of official hostility or denominational targeting precludes summary judgment for the City without establishing the final factual answer. Florida severability follows that determination. Materially different future applications require their own RFRA assessment. His position supplies no immunity from ordinary fraud, contract, restitution, tax, reporting, or criminal law, no bar to a targeted subpoena or audit grounded in a concrete secular enforcement need and lawful process, and no authority to decide religious truth. Private donations do not become public financing through truthful-solicitation regulation. These application and constitutional positions are his alone; the Court does not adopt them.

## Harris.md

Source full copy: terms/OT1995/sources/B_PRIOR_PUBLIC/Harris.md

#### Opinion Topology

| Writing | Author | Joined by | Relationship to judgment | Scope joined |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Opinion of the Court | O’Connor | Stevens, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg, Breyer throughout; Stone-Zsela in the precise fault/notice propositions and cumulative-punishment holdings identified below | Affirms all three components | Commerce economic-class ground: eight. Complete fault/intentional-assistance construction: eight, with Stone-Zsela additionally joining knowing participation, fair notice, and rejection of general legal knowledge. Statutory cumulative authorization and narrow Hunter application: nine. |
| Opinion concurring in part and in the judgment | Stone-Zsela | No other Justice | Affirms each component and joins the specified portions of the Court's reasoning | Distinct instrumentality ground; additional no-interstate-history-knowledge and no-permanent-deprivation conclusions; preserves the proof and remedy limits stated in his writing |
| Concurring opinion | Stevens | No other Justice | Joins the Court's bounded opinion and unanimous affirmance | Applies controlling Hunter while preserving his earlier constitutional objection to multiplication of convictions; no new dissenting judgment vote |

The eight-Justice commerce opinion controls directly. Stone-Zsela's distinct commerce rationale is not an alternative holding of the Court and is not combined with it under Marks. Every controlling proposition has its own direct majority.


#### Separate Positions Relevant to the Decision

Chief Justice Stone-Zsela concurs in part and in the judgment. On commerce, he does not join the Court's economic-class rationale. He would sustain this prosecution because Congress may protect an interstate transportation instrumentality from a direct intrastate attack when the offense requires proof of that particular instrumentality's interstate connection. An automobile can carry persons and property through interstate channels; these defendants forcibly seized possession and control of the Buick itself. Its Michigan manufacture and Arkansas and Mississippi titling satisfied the enacted connection. The direct taking and vehicle-specific element together perform constitutional work, rather than attaching remote history to an unrelated local offense. He rejects the Fifth Circuit's general chain through travel, insurance, violent-crime costs, and the national economy. His ground neither reclassifies violent crime as economic activity nor makes every offense in or near a vehicle federal. Past movement alone is not a universal source of authority. This instrumentality application has no additional joins.

On fault and notice, the Chief Justice joins the knowing-participation and fair-notice propositions, requiring proof of the firearm and interstate-vehicle facts as enacted. He additionally concludes that this original statute requires neither knowledge that the vehicle traveled interstate nor a separate intent permanently to deprive. Ratzlaf's distinct willfulness language supplies no such requirements, and Staples's concern with apparently innocent possession turning on an unknown physical characteristic differs from pointing a shotgun at occupants and ordering them out. He finds the jury evidence sufficient to establish knowing participation beyond a reasonable doubt. That explanation does not convert the Court's bounded statute-level ruling into a separate majority adjudication of evidentiary sufficiency or of an unknown instruction. He does not eliminate fault from a fact Congress makes part of the wrongful conduct. His additional nexus-knowledge and deprivation-duration conclusions are noncontrolling. He joins the statutory authorization and bounded Hunter application sustaining the consecutive punishment and reserves a different pair lacking clear authorization.

Justice Stevens concurs. He joins the Court's statutory construction and its limited application of Hunter as governing precedent. He does not withdraw his Hunter dissenting position: multiple convictions can create stigma, risks, and collateral consequences distinct from the total authorized prison term, and his earlier view would give the same-offense protection content independent of legislative authorization. The present opinion neither denies those consequences nor announces unlimited legislative power to multiply convictions. Its application of existing precedent to this clear cumulative command permits his join without deciding again the constitutional foundation on which he previously disagreed. He also joins the Court's economic vehicle-taking ground without withdrawing his broader Lopez dissenting position. His preserved constitutional objection remains noncontrolling; he supports affirmance of both convictions and the consecutive terms here.

## Lopez.md

Source full copy: terms/OT1995/sources/B_PRIOR_PUBLIC/Lopez.md

#### Opinion Topology

| Writing | Author | Joined by | Relationship to judgment | Scope joined |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Opinion of the Court | Kennedy | Stone-Zsela, O’Connor, Scalia, Thomas | Affirm dismissal | The original stand-alone possession offense lacks the required commerce connection on the offered downstream-effects theory; no commerce element is judicially inserted. The holdings preserve existing commercial precedents and the stated nonreach limits. |
| Concurrence | Stone-Zsela | No other Justice | Joins the Court’s two identified holdings and affirmance | States the complete six routes of commerce authority and their genuine-connection requirement; does not replace the limited Court ground with a categorical noneconomic exclusion. |
| Dissent | Breyer | Stevens, Souter, Ginsburg | Would reverse and remand | Rational substantial class-effects ground sustaining the original offense without an added commerce element; unresolved sentencing objections remain for appellate consideration. |


#### Separate Positions Relevant to the Decision

Chief Justice Stone-Zsela joins the Court’s limited holdings and concurs. Federal commerce authority is broad but enumerated. His complete account permits regulation of: actual interstate transactions; channels of commerce; instrumentalities, persons and things in interstate commerce; economic classes substantially affecting an actual interstate market; intrastate conduct whose inclusion is necessary to prevent evasion or material impairment of a valid interstate regime; and materially established interstate externalities that individual States cannot internalize. Each route requires its relevant connection to be established. A jurisdictional phrase must perform genuine constitutional work in the individual case. No other Justice joins this complete formulation.

He rejects the chain from guns near schools through violence or fear, impaired education, reduced productivity and the national economy because it supplies no judicially enforceable stopping point. National importance and generalized downstream social costs do not establish the missing enumerated-power connection. The defect is not the word noneconomic: a valid interstate regime or genuinely demonstrated interstate spillovers can support regulation of intrastate conduct under his account. Wickard and Perez remain at their actual economic-class scope. He would not insert a missing element, adjudicate an amended law, or foreclose a materially different enactment supported by its own constitutional authority. Commerce authority and individual rights are distinct inquiries; ordinary state police authority does not settle compliance with an independently applicable right. His concurrence establishes no Second Amendment classification and preserves the Elections Clause and Reconstruction Amendments’ own texts and standards. The additional framework is noncontrolling.

Justice Breyer, joined by Justices Stevens, Souter and Ginsburg, dissents. Congress could rationally find that the class of school gun possession substantially affects national commerce through disrupted education, workforce preparation and travel. Wickard and Perez require attention to the class rather than treating one defendant’s effect as independently substantial. The dissent would retain judicial review, a substantial class relationship and reasonable means, while deferring to the legislative judgment presented here. It rejects a universal findings prerequisite and does not equate every imaginable consequence with a substantial commercial effect. In its view, the Court discounts the practical national connection by treating the local and noncommercial features of possession as insufficiently connected to the class-wide effects. Safe schools’ desirability alone and the gun’s remote interstate manufacture are not its grounds. The dissent reads the original offense without a new nexus element; on that reading the indictment omits no required commerce allegation. It would reverse the constitutional dismissal and remand for the unresolved supervised-release and special-assessment objections, not approve the entire sentence. Its application is noncontrolling.

#### Separate Positions Relevant to the Decision

Kennedy’s Part II-A-2, joined by Stone-Zsela and Stevens, treats the sequence of enactment and contemporaneous official statements as corroboration of the objectively established discriminatory object under Arlington Heights and Feeney. Public hostility is not converted into a finding that every speaker’s intent belonged to the council. That discussion has three joins and supplies no controlling motive test. Souter concurs in part and in the judgment, joining I, III and IV. In his view Smith’s relation to Sherbert, Yoder and the unemployment cases, its briefing history and its textual foundation warrant reconsideration in a case that requires it. He neither overrules Smith here nor claims novelty alone permits abandoning precedent; the Casey approach to stare decisis remains a separate, contextual discipline. Blackmun, joined by O’Connor, concurs in the judgment. They would require compelling justification and the least restrictive available means for substantial burdens on religious exercise even from general laws. They regard discrimination of this kind as necessarily failing that standard, while preserving the seriousness of animal-welfare concerns and reserving whether a sincere general anti-cruelty law must provide an exemption. These additional approaches remain noncontrolling.



## Mezzanatto.md

Source full copy: terms/OT1995/sources/B_PRIOR_PUBLIC/Mezzanatto.md

#### Opinion Topology

| Writing | Author | Joined by | Relationship to judgment | Scope joined |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Opinion of the Court | Thomas | O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Ginsburg, Breyer | Reversal | Entire limited impeachment-waiver opinion, including its waiver presumption and individualized challenge limits. |
| Concurrence | Ginsburg | O'Connor, Breyer | Joins the Court in full | Emphasizes the reservation of case-in-chief and other broader uses. |
| Concurrence in the judgment | Stone-Zsela | No other Justice | Reversal and remand | Government must prove knowing and voluntary acceptance of the personal-inconsistent-testimony use; no join in the six-Justice presumption. |
| Dissent | Souter | Stevens | Would affirm the new-trial judgment | Particular Rules protect institutional candor against advance bargaining-entry waivers. |


#### Separate Positions Relevant to the Decision

Justice Ginsburg, joined by Justices O'Connor and Breyer, joins the Court and emphasizes the limited issue before it. Impeachment follows the defendant's own decision to give inconsistent trial testimony. The Government did not seek to use the proffer as proof in its case in chief, a use that could affect the incentive for candid negotiations differently. Agreement with the limited waiver therefore supplies no answer about that different agreement or other broader uses. The concurrence preserves a boundary of the Court's holding, without imposing a separate public-interest burden or requiring another hearing on an unraised individual validity issue.

Chief Justice Stone-Zsela concurs in reversal and would remand under a distinct allocation of proof. A represented defendant may knowingly and voluntarily agree before the proffer that the Government may use his statements solely to impeach his own inconsistent trial testimony. The Government invokes that waiver and controls the proposed condition, so it must prove knowing and voluntary acceptance. Representation and competence do not settle whether the actual term was understood and freely accepted; clarity, prosecutorial threats or deception, and the circumstances of the demand matter to that inquiry. He does not presume actual coercion from bargaining or require a new hearing when the existing record permits the necessary determination. Enforcement remains subject to ordinary constitutional and evidentiary safeguards.

His ground does not approve case-in-chief or sentencing use, or a waiver effectively eliminating the right to trial. Defense counsel's cross-examination, independent defense evidence, and argument of another theory do not ordinarily trigger this personal-testimony waiver. Any broader trigger would require a separate explicit agreement and a distinct validity analysis, neither of which is decided. Existing rule-based perjury, false-statement, and comparable-abuse exceptions retain their own prerequisites. He therefore joins the judgment rejecting categorical nonwaivability while declining the Court's affirmative-challenge presumption. His Government-proof requirement is not the Court's controlling allocation.

Justice Souter, joined by Justice Stevens, would affirm the Ninth Circuit's new-trial judgment. General waiver doctrine does not resolve whether Congress protected plea discussions partly for the judicial system's benefit. Brooklyn Savings demonstrates that private consent cannot always defeat a public statutory policy, and these Rules deliberately encourage candid bargaining through enumerated, limited exceptions. Allowing an advance waiver to become the price of entering discussions can remove the protection before its function begins. Peretz's consent to allocation of a trial function and ordinary evidentiary waiver do not decide that distinct statutory design. The dissent neither denies all criminal waivers nor finds Mezzanatto personally coerced. It would sustain the preserved objection, and the lower court already found the admission nonharmless; its result is a new trial rather than acquittal or a rule of automatic reversal for every violation.

#### Separate Positions Relevant to the Decision

Blackmun concurs, joined by no other Justice, emphasizing the substantial deference due Congress's predictive judgments. Only portions of the legislative record were submitted to the District Court. That material may suffice to defeat the challengers' summary-judgment request while leaving unresolved material issues that prevent summary judgment for the defenders. He neither treats legislative findings as conclusive nor requires each broadcaster to establish impending bankruptcy. His separate emphasis accompanies his complete join in Kennedy's analysis.

Stevens concurs in part and in the judgment, joined by no other Justice. He agrees that the general scheme is content neutral and subject to intermediate review, but finds the existing record sufficient to reject the facial challenge. Congress may act before broadcasters collapse, given cable's audience, distribution control and incentives. Serious injury from exclusion need not await further demonstration in the form the plurality seeks. Congress need not establish that every broadcaster is vulnerable; the separate retransmission-consent option may leave compulsory carriage especially useful to vulnerable stations, a prediction rather than a finding about actual elections. Forecasts of displaced cable programming may themselves be speculative and affected by voluntary conduct. He expressly accepts review for reasonable inferences from substantial evidence, provided Congress need not make a formal judicial or agency-style record. Although he would affirm, he concurs in vacatur and remand so that a disposition commands a majority, following the disposition-accommodation approach discussed in Justice Rutledge's concurrence in the result in Screws v. United States, 325 U.S. 91, 134. His agreement with the judgment does not adopt the plurality's demand for further proof.

Stone-Zsela concurs, joined by no other Justice. Corporate cable operators exercise a genuine editorial function and retain its full protection; control of an important private communications route does not itself constitute state action. Tornillo and the relevant Pacific Gas & Electric reasoning present a serious compelled-publication concern. The permitted distinction is bounded: Congress addresses a physical distribution bottleneck and preservation of independently available broadcast service, and separately identified signals need not be attributed to the operator. Commercial and noncommercial categories, localism statements and actual administration still require attention for message preference. Evidence that some stations were dropped does not alone establish systemic jeopardy, while spare capacity does not eliminate the burden on each affected operator. Supported forecasts can justify prevention without scientific certainty or completed failures. Ladue's practical attention to usable expression supports examining displaced programming and realistic alternatives; MCI's different delegation question supplies little guidance on Congress's enacted choice. He joins remand without final validation, a judicially substituted quota or automatic Act-wide injunction. This concurrence adds no separate Court holding and no general power to compel editorial balance in newspapers or other media.

O'Connor concurs in part and dissents in part, joined by Scalia and Ginsburg throughout and by Thomas only in Parts I and III. In Parts I and III, all four regard the compulsory preference as content based: Congress assigns finite editorial space to favored broadcast expression because of local, informational and educational programming. The absence of viewpoint hostility does not eliminate that selection. They regard localism and viewpoint diversity as insufficiently compelling to justify it; even assuming educational and public-affairs objectives compelling, the breadth fails strict tailoring. That assumption is not a holding that those objectives can never be compelling. The physical bottleneck is real, but does not authorize Congress to select favored speech on private editorial capacity. Neutral competition measures, capacity-access arrangements or common-carriage structures may present different constitutional questions; their mention approves no particular law or taking. Relief would address the challenged carriage regime, not the whole Act.

O'Connor's Part II supplies an independently sufficient alternative, joined only by Scalia and Ginsburg. Even assuming content neutrality, the requirements reach broadcasters and situations in which the asserted threat or anticompetitive danger does not justify the burden. More evidence about how often such dangers occur would not cure that breadth. Congress could direct protection to threatened broadcast service or anticompetitive conduct, but the present extensive preference goes beyond those needs. These three would invalidate under intermediate scrutiny as well as strict scrutiny; they do not join a remand to discover evidence that could cure the scheme. Thomas's nonjoin preserves the distinction between his strict ground and this alternative.

Ginsburg also dissents separately, joined by no other Justice. She emphasizes the lower dissent's account of content preference and the failure to substantiate danger to free television. She does not find viewpoint discrimination, and does not turn the absence of adequate proof into a proved fact that no station could ever suffer. Content-neutral and content-related purposes can be interwoven; the former do not erase the latter. If further proceedings occur and danger remains unsupported, she would require judgment for the appellants. That conditional observation does not constitute agreement with the present remand or its intermediate classification.

#### Separate Positions Relevant to the Decision

Chief Justice Stone-Zsela concurs in part and dissents in part. He joins the closer injunction-specific standard and the Court's component merits dispositions. He would require a replacement conduct-based post-refusal protection on remand, proposing eight feet subject to site-specific proof and a narrower supported implementation if that measure cannot be justified. This preferred mandatory remedy differs from the Court's permission to consider lawful relief. He agrees only in the disposition leaving the independent validity of the particular disputed terms and applications of paragraphs (7)–(9) undecided; that disposition-only agreement supplies no additional rationale.

Outside the fixed access zone, an enjoined person must remain free to make a peaceful initial approach, offer literature or request conversation. Protection begins only after words or clear conduct convey that further individualized contact is unwanted, a reasonable person would understand that refusal, and the enjoined speaker has notice. Silence, disagreement with the viewpoint and entry into the vicinity do not automatically trigger it. Once triggered, that speaker must stop following, shadowing, accompanying, repeated reapproaches, surrounding and close-range individualized speech, including coordinated substitution of another participant to continue the same pursuit. His proposed order would require at least eight feet of separation while the person proceeds toward or away from the facility within a finite, ascertainable, route-related clinic vicinity.

The refusal ends a particular unwanted encounter, not all exposure to a viewpoint. Lawful stationary signs, speech to the public, offers to willing listeners and communication from outside the separation remain protected. An unavoidable passing encounter, a patient voluntarily approaching a stationary protester and momentary inability to separate safely must not be punished. The speaker must stop targeted pursuit and take reasonable safe steps once able to comply; a listener cannot force a speaker from an otherwise lawful stationary location. Renewed voluntary engagement can reopen communication, but repeated unwanted approaches cannot be used to solicit that permission.

Carroll's precision requirement, Claiborne Hardware's protection of advocacy and association, Yamasaki's remedial limitation, Ward's content distinction and Frisby's focused protection support his proposed conduct-based response. The practical aim is to make the termination of a close encounter observable after conduct that defeated earlier protection. Yet a moving personal zone may burden leafleting, be difficult to measure on a narrow sidewalk, or turn aversion to speech into suppression. The stated safeguards answer those dangers without proving the number necessary. The state court would have to define the vicinity, support space and necessity with record-specific findings, and consider concrete less burdensome means. If eight feet cannot lawfully be supported, it must implement a supported narrower protection, rather than an unsupported numerical floor. Eight feet is a proposed operational measure, not a constitutional number or an already established rule.

Legally bound defendants and persons shown to act in concert with notice are the only subjects of that order. Touching, grabbing, intentional obstruction, threats, vehicle interference, surrounding and coordinated physical intimidation may independently be prohibited when the record and governing law support them, without waiting for refusal. These express conduct positions are not blanket approval of every disputed term in paragraphs (7)–(9). Casey protects choice against state interference and creates no direct private-liability right here; Bray preserves independently grounded state relief without weakening speech protection. Contempt requires notice and the process appropriate to the actual sanction. The separate writing decides no contempt-classification question and rests on no later personal-zone authority.

Justice Stevens concurs in part and dissents in part. He rejects categorical content discrimination and the association challenge, joins the residential reversal, and agrees with the Court's nonreach and limited remand. He declines separate geographic tailoring of the 36-foot zone and the noise and images merits. Under Izumi, later argument does not alone enlarge the petition. His earlier willingness to reach an indispensable intervention issue does not require resolving additional provisions unnecessary to these selected questions. Leaving those provisions intact on that limited review is not a merits endorsement.

His injunction standard is individualized equitable relief, not the Court's heightened speech-injunction formula or a universal adoption of ordinary Ward review. Yamasaki requires no more burden than necessary to give complete relief; Professional Engineers, Hudson and Paradise explain why a remedy for identified wrongdoers can reach beyond the precise past act when needed to prevent recurrence or eliminate its effects. The trial court's familiarity matters. Those principles do not insulate every remedy or presume that the entire decree is necessary.

He would sustain paragraph (5)'s physical-approach restraint for the bound actors in this established setting. It is not a prohibition on every unsolicited word or all advocacy within 300 feet. Distant expression, offered verbal or written counsel that does not become prohibited approach, and invited nonthreatening conversation remain distinct. The documented approaches and interference support this individualized constraint even though a similar general rule for all speakers would pose a different issue. He therefore disagrees with the reversal of the initial-invitation condition, but does not endorse a general right to suppress unwelcome ideas.

He joins the narrower Frisby-based conclusion that the residential radius exceeds supported protection. Advance invitation does not cure suppression of general neighborhood activity. His agreement there does not join the Court's distinct standard or the private-geography, noise and images merits. He would permit lawful consideration of further precise conduct relief on remand; he does not require a new post-refusal injunction or endorse a numerical substitute. His equitable theory and clinic-approach position remain noncontrolling.

Justice Souter concurs. He emphasizes that actual concert is a matter for individual determination, not a synonym for opposition to the clinic. Regal Knitwear allows an injunction to reach actual assistance to a bound party while preserving a stranger's right to deny that relation. The preliminary appearance exchanges included troubling references to abortion opposition, but also reserved nonassociation and concert defenses for later proceedings and distinguished arrest reports from formal charges. They cannot be treated as a final finding against every absent person.

His concurrence accepts the written order and Florida's authoritative limiting construction without declaring every later enforcement neutral. A person must have notice and must actually fall within the lawful bound category; receiving the order alone supplies neither concert nor guilt. His position differs from the dissent's operational inference, yet grants no immunity to an unconstitutional application. The named petitioners' categorical challenge and Cheffer's independent federal preliminary-relief litigation remain separate. He joins the provision-specific speech review and derivative relief; his additional explanation neither approves a new distance nor imposes a mandatory replacement decree.

Justice Scalia concurs in part and dissents in part, joined throughout by Justices Kennedy and Thomas. They would also reverse the public expressive exclusion and the noise restriction. Their first sufficient objection is that a judicial command directly forbidding selected persons' future protected advocacy demands the rigorous protection for prior restraints even if nominally content neutral. Carroll, Keefe, Vance and Claiborne Hardware support that distinction. Ordinary conduct orders and incidental consequences of neutral sanctions are different. Alexander's distinction between postconviction effects and a direct future-speech prohibition does not shelter this order.

They separately reject the decree's asserted neutral operation. Turner requires attention to what a rule does, not merely a benign description of its aims, while preserving the proposition that speaker identity alone is not content discrimination. Here the already reported preliminary exchanges connecting coverage to opposition to the clinic supply the additional operational concern. The three accept the abstract legality of actual concert with notice and the bar on ideological guilt; they do not treat that legal category as proof of neutral implementation. They determine no absent person's concert or contempt.

The public-zone objection also concerns the legal predicate for this expansion. They accept the facts found but dispute whether incidental slowing and momentary hesitation, without the necessary relation to obstruction forbidden by the predecessor, legally justify the whole exclusion of peaceful expression. This is a legal sufficiency objection, not a retrial through selected video. Precisely directed road, driveway or conduct controls may address genuine interference; none is found sufficient or ordered as a compulsory substitute. The crossing and shoulder permissions do not themselves establish the necessity of the full exclusion.

For noise, they accept that the qualified clause fits the Court's test if that test is assumed. That concession does not affirm it or join the governing standard. The separate objection is that medically desirable restraint is not an identified adequate prior or threatened violation of statute, common law or the predecessor justifying this personalized speech order. They do not find that Florida has no applicable noise law, deny medical harm or invent a decibel threshold. Strict direct-restraint review independently remains applicable. They concur in reversing private-side exclusion, images, initial-consent approach and residential territory under their stricter grounds, without a right to trespass, threaten, pursue a declining person or invade a home.

They join the confined review-scope ruling and decline independent paragraphs (7)–(9) merits despite actual briefing. Every predicate they would reverse must fall from dependent enforcement; at minimum they join removal of the predicates this judgment actually sets aside. Independently lawful obstruction, assault, threats and conduct remedies survive. Their position does not command a replacement personal zone, approve Stone-Zsela's proposed distance, decide an unreported contempt or dissolve the entire predecessor injunction.
