### United States v. Harris, No. 94-297

Merits decision, April 27, 1995

#### Chronology and Posture

On review of the United States Court of Appeals for the Fifth Circuit's June 29, 1994 judgment in United States v. Harris, 25 F.3d 1275, No. 93-7554. The questions concern Congress's commerce authority to enact the original armed-carjacking offense, its criminal-fault and fair-notice requirements, and cumulative convictions and consecutive punishment under §§2119 and 924(c). The defendants seek relief from their convictions and sentences; the Government seeks affirmance.

The offense occurred December 12, 1992. The borrowed 1976 Buick Electra was manufactured in Flint, Michigan and titled in Arkansas and Mississippi. The trial evidence recounted by the Fifth Circuit placed Harris with a sawed-off shotgun and Hoskins with an ax handle at the confrontation: the occupants were ordered out, Hoskins drove away, and Harris pursued the occupants. The defendants' retaliation/afterthought account and Harris's claimed ignorance were defenses, not accepted contrary findings. Each defendant was convicted of aiding and abetting carjacking and of using or carrying a firearm during a crime of violence, receiving forty-one months on Count One and sixty months on Count Two, consecutive.

The December 12, 1992 offense is governed by original §2119. A person possessing a firearm as defined in §921 who takes or attempts to take a motor vehicle previously transported, shipped, or received in interstate or foreign commerce from another's person or presence by force and violence or intimidation is subject to a fine, imprisonment up to fifteen years, or both. Resulting serious bodily injury, as defined in §1365, raises the maximum to twenty-five years; resulting death permits imprisonment for any number of years up to life, with the fine-or-both alternatives retained. Neither enhanced tier is decided. The later intent-to-cause-death-or-serious-injury language is not part of this offense. The enactment's encouragement of federal/state cooperation and appropriate prosecution consistent with prosecutorial discretion is neither an additional element nor an automatic constitutional limit.

Offense-date §924(c)(1) requires use or carrying of a firearm during and in relation to a crime of violence or drug-trafficking crime for which the person may be prosecuted in federal court. Its inclusive parenthetical reaches predicates with their own deadly-weapon enhancements. The ordinary term is five years in addition to the predicate punishment, consecutive to every other imprisonment term, expressly including the predicate term. Probation and suspended sentence are barred, and the then-effective provision denies parole during its term. A crime of violence under §924(c)(3) is a felony having as an element use, attempted use, or threatened use of physical force against another's person or property, or by its nature involving a substantial risk that such force may be used in committing it. No later amendment of that definition is adjudicated.

The then-effective separate firearm tiers prescribed ten years for a short-barreled rifle or shotgun; thirty years for a machinegun, destructive device, or firearm equipped with a silencer or muffler; twenty years for a second or subsequent conviction; and life without release for such a repeat conviction involving the listed machinegun, destructive-device, or silencer/muffler category. A sawed-off description in this record establishes neither statutory barrel length nor a basis for increasing the reviewed sentence. The later semiautomatic-assault-weapon tier and later deletion of the parole sentence are not applied to the 1992 conduct.

Chief Justice Alex-Lamar Stone-Zsela and Justices Stevens, O’Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg, and Breyer participated.

The Court affirms the Fifth Circuit's judgment unanimously on all three presented challenges. Both defendants' convictions and consecutive sentences remain in force. No new trial, resentencing, or further merits remand is ordered.

#### Controlling Propositions and Authority

##### Congress may protect the transportation instrumentality against this direct forcible taking

**Controlling proposition:** The Commerce Clause permits Congress to protect an instrumentality of interstate commerce against a direct intrastate forcible taking when the federal offense requires proof of the particular instrumentality’s interstate connection. Original §2119 is constitutional as applied to the forcible taking of the Buick proved here; the judgment is affirmed without adopting the Fifth Circuit’s aggregate-effects rationale.

**Authority:** Chief Justice Stone-Zsela’s opinion of the Court, joined by Justices Stevens, O’Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer: nine direct votes for the qualified instrumentality rule and its application. The four-Justice economic-acquisition concurrence joins this holding and does not limit those joins.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that Congress may protect the interstate transportation instrumentality itself against forcible seizure. Lopez recognizes authority over instrumentalities and explains that intrastate conduct may be reached when it directly attacks one. The required connection must exist before aggregate magnitude can matter; national crime costs do not establish it.

Here the vehicle’s transportation function, the direct attack and the enacted interstate element each perform a distinct task. An automobile is capable of carrying persons and property through interstate transportation channels. The prohibited taking attacks possession and control of that instrumentality itself. Section 2119 requires the Government to prove in every prosecution that the particular vehicle was transported, shipped or received in interstate or foreign commerce. The Buick’s Michigan manufacture and Arkansas and Mississippi titling establish that condition. The Court neither supplies a missing statutory element nor finds that the Buick was traveling interstate when taken.

The defendants’ reliance on the taking’s local character does not defeat this protective authority. Unlike an offense in which an interstate object appears incidentally, this offense directly seizes control of the protected transportation instrumentality. No profit motive or intended interstate resale is necessary to that connection. The Fifth Circuit’s chain through violence, travel, insurance and the national economy does not supply the governing ground.

The holding does not make every crime in or near a vehicle federal, and past interstate movement alone creates no universal jurisdictional rule. Allied-Bruce’s reservation about remote object history remains intact because the direct attack and transportation function do constitutional work independent of that history. Wickard and Perez remain effective within their economic-class scope; the Court does not need that separate route to decide this application. Both the carjacking conviction and the dependent firearm conviction survive the commerce challenge.

**Precedent treatment:**

- United States v. Lopez, April 26, 1995: applies its recognized authority to protect instrumentalities against direct attacks and its requirement of a constitutionally identifiable connection before aggregation. The direct taking and enacted vehicle connection distinguish the stand-alone school-zone possession offense; no missing element is inserted.
- Allied-Bruce Terminix Cos. v. Dobson, January 18, 1995: preserves its reservation that remote interstate object history alone is not constitutionally conclusive. Here the vehicle’s transportation function and the direct attack on its control supply independent constitutional work.
- Wickard v. Filburn, 317 U.S. 111 (1942): preserves its economic-class scope; no economic-class alternative holding is necessary here.
- Perez v. United States, 402 U.S. 146 (1971): preserves its economic-class rule allowing class regulation without an independently substantial effect from each instance; it does not supply this holding’s instrumentality ground.

##### Knowing participation and intentional assistance give the original offense definite content

**Controlling proposition:** Original §2119 requires knowing armed participation in taking or attempting to take the covered vehicle from another's person or presence by force and violence or intimidation; aiding-and-abetting liability requires intentional assistance in that taking, not mere presence, association, or awareness of another's plan. Read with that ordinary criminal fault, the statute gives fair notice of the charged conduct and defeats the presented lack-of-fault and vagueness challenges without adding a profit motive, interstate-resale intention, knowledge of federal law, or the later intent-to-kill-or-seriously-injure element.

**Authority:** Chief Justice Stone-Zsela’s opinion of the Court, joined by Stevens, O’Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg, and Breyer: nine direct votes for knowing participation, intentional assistance, fair notice, and rejection of a general legal-knowledge requirement. The Chief Justice’s additional conclusions about knowledge of interstate history and permanent deprivation appear only in his separate concurrence and have no additional joins.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that statutory silence does not make this serious forcible-taking offense a crime of unwitting involvement. Staples preserves ordinary factual culpability, and X-Citement Video requires attention to the whole statutory setting rather than automatic strict liability. The taking and force terms identify the wrongful conduct; participation in that taking must be knowing, and assistance must be intentional. Shabani's rejection of guilt by association supplies a consistent boundary, without converting aiding and abetting into conspiracy. Ratzlaf's known-illegality rule rested on distinct willfulness language and does not add a universal legal-knowledge element here. A claimed retaliatory motive does not negate participation in the taking as a matter of law. The lower record treats Harris's claimed ignorance as a defense, not an accepted finding. No supplied original charge or preservation record establishes an omitted-fault instruction. The Court rejects the presented statute and notice challenge without approving unknown instructions, finding harmless error, or ordering a new trial. Knowledge of interstate history and deprivation duration receive no majority resolution.

**Precedent treatment:**

- Staples v. United States, May 23, 1994: applies contextual factual culpability despite silence while retaining its distinct regulated-characteristic holding.
- United States v. X-Citement Video, Inc., November 29, 1994: applies contextual criminal-fault construction without transplanting its depiction elements or deciding every jurisdictional-knowledge question.
- United States v. Shabani, November 1, 1994: preserves the distinction between knowing participation and mere association; its conspiracy holding does not itself define this separate offense.
- Ratzlaf v. United States, January 11, 1994: distinguishes its specific willfulness and known-illegality language rather than converting it into a universal ignorance-of-law defense.
- Lopez: preserves its refusal to insert an absent commerce element; that limit does not eliminate ordinary criminal-fault construction of the enacted taking offense.

##### Section 924(c) expressly adds a consecutive term for this armed carjacking

**Controlling proposition:** The offense-date §924(c) authorizes its additional, consecutive punishment for the original §2119 carjacking predicate even though firearm possession is integral to that predicate's definition. The Government must still establish firearm use or carrying during and in relation to a qualifying federally prosecutable predicate; this cumulative-authorization holding does not equate possession with every §924(c) condition or determine universal element overlap.

**Authority:** Stone-Zsela's opinion, joined on this statutory holding by Stevens, O’Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg, and Breyer: nine direct votes. The holding sustains the reviewed forty-one-month carjacking terms and additional sixty-month firearm terms, not an unreviewed weapon enhancement.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that the enacted punishment directions encompass this pairing. Section 924(c) reaches any qualifying federal crime of violence, includes predicates with their own deadly-weapon enhancement, adds punishment to the predicate, and expressly forbids concurrent service. The defendants distinguish an offense defined by firearm possession from an otherwise complete offense carrying an enhancement. But the parenthetical's inclusion is nonexclusive; it does not narrow the general command to the examples it names. Original §2119's later enactment and silence therefore supply no exception for armed carjacking. Ordinary construction resolves this question before residual lenity, which Ratzlaf preserves for genuine remaining ambiguity. Smith keeps firearm use or carrying and relation to the predicate distinct; the Court does not deem incidental possession sufficient. Deal addresses a different question about additional convictions in one prosecution and does not independently decide this statutory pair. The existing five-year firearm terms stand against the presented challenge. No different weapon classification, higher sentence, merger, or concurrent conversion is ordered.

**Precedent treatment:**

- Ratzlaf: preserves residual lenity after ordinary construction; the inclusive, additional, and consecutive directions leave no residual ambiguity for this pairing.
- Smith v. United States, June 1, 1993: preserves the separate firearm-use and relation requirements; a predicate's possession element does not automatically establish all §924(c) elements.
- Deal v. United States, May 17, 1993: distinguishes its conviction-sequence question, which does not itself decide cumulative punishment under these two statutes.

##### Hunter permits the clearly authorized punishment in this single prosecution

**Controlling proposition:** Applying Missouri v. Hunter, the clearly authorized §2119 and §924(c) convictions and consecutive punishments imposed in this single prosecution do not violate the Double Jeopardy Clause even assuming overlap under Blockburger. This holding applies the governing same-trial precedent to the clear statutory direction and neither decides universal element overlap nor changes the rules for successive prosecutions.

**Authority:** Stone-Zsela's opinion, joined by Stevens, O’Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg, and Breyer: nine direct votes for this bounded application. Stevens joins the application of controlling Hunter while preserving his earlier objection to legislative multiplication of convictions; his reservation does not subtract a vote from this limited holding.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that the clear cumulative direction resolves the double-jeopardy objection under Hunter. Blockburger supplies a method for discerning whether two offenses carry distinct legislative punishment, and Whalen applies that construction principle when contrary authorization is absent. Hunter establishes the governing consequence when the legislature instead clearly authorizes both punishments at one trial. Section 924(c)'s additional and consecutive commands provide that authorization here. The Court therefore need not decide whether every possible original carjacking violation satisfies every condition of the firearm offense. The defendants' overlap argument cannot displace the clear direction in this same-trial setting. Dixon preserves Grady's distinct successive-prosecution rule; neither a common episode nor the present judgment silently changes it. This decision makes no independent claim that multiple convictions lack stigma or collateral consequences and does not endorse every possible multiplication of charges. It applies the existing precedent to this pairing. Both convictions and their supplied consecutive terms remain in force without resentencing.

**Precedent treatment:**

- Missouri v. Hunter, 459 U.S. 359 (1983): applies its governing same-trial rule to the clear cumulative authorization without reconsidering its constitutional foundation.
- Blockburger v. United States, 284 U.S. 299 (1932): applies its construction role rather than treating possible overlap as an absolute bar to this expressly authorized same-trial punishment.
- Whalen v. United States, 445 U.S. 684 (1980): preserves the presumption against cumulative punishment absent contrary legislative intent and distinguishes the clear direction here.
- United States v. Dixon, June 28, 1993: preserves its successive-prosecution holdings, including the continued operation of Grady.
- Grady v. Corbin, 495 U.S. 508 (1990): remains governing law for its essential-conduct successive-prosecution setting; this single prosecution does not invoke or displace that rule.

**Limits and questions not reached:** The Court decides no independent instruction, indictment, preservation, harmlessness, sufficiency, or intradistrict-venue question. It determines neither knowledge of the vehicle's interstate history nor an independent permanent-deprivation requirement by a majority. No later carjacking intent amendment, later firearm tier, new short-barrel classification, serious-injury or death enhancement, or different charging pair is adjudicated.

#### Precedent Treatment and Current-Law Effect

Lopez’s connection-before-magnitude architecture governs: its instrumentality category supports protection against the direct taking proved here, while generalized downstream crime costs do not supply the connection and no absent nexus element may be inserted. Allied-Bruce’s remote-history reservation remains intact. Wickard and Perez retain their economic-class scope without becoming an alternative holding in this case. Staples, X-Citement Video and Shabani support the stated culpability boundaries; Ratzlaf retains its distinct willfulness and residual-lenity rules. Smith keeps firearm use or carrying and relation to the predicate separate. Deal’s conviction-sequence holding does not decide this statutory pair. Hunter governs the clearly authorized one-trial cumulative punishment; Blockburger and Whalen do not override the express direction. Dixon and Grady retain their distinct successive-prosecution operation. No precedent is overruled.

Congress may protect an interstate transportation instrumentality against a direct intrastate forcible taking when the offense requires proof of that particular instrumentality’s interstate connection. That rule sustains the original §2119 prosecution here; it does not make past interstate movement alone sufficient, federalize every offense in or near a vehicle, or adopt the Fifth Circuit’s general crime-cost rationale. The original statute requires knowing participation and intentional assistance for aiding-and-abetting liability. Knowledge of the vehicle’s interstate history and a separate permanent-deprivation requirement receive no majority resolution. Clear §924(c) cumulative and consecutive authorization governs this §2119 pairing, and Hunter permits the resulting same-trial punishment. Existing successive-prosecution law remains unchanged. No later statutory amendment or unproved trial defect becomes law through this affirmance.

#### Separate Positions Relevant to the Decision

Chief Justice Stone-Zsela, who delivers the opinion of the Court, also writes separately on fault questions the Court does not reach. He concludes that this original statute requires neither knowledge that the vehicle traveled interstate nor a separate intent permanently to deprive. Ratzlaf's distinct willfulness language supplies no such requirements, and Staples's concern with apparently innocent possession turning on an unknown physical characteristic differs from pointing a shotgun at occupants and ordering them out. He finds the jury evidence sufficient to establish knowing participation beyond a reasonable doubt. That explanation does not convert the Court's bounded statute-level ruling into a separate majority adjudication of evidentiary sufficiency or of an unknown instruction. He does not eliminate fault from a fact Congress makes part of the wrongful conduct. His additional nexus-knowledge and deprivation-duration conclusions are noncontrolling.

Justice Stevens concurs. He joins the Court's statutory construction and its limited application of Hunter as governing precedent. He does not withdraw his Hunter dissenting position: multiple convictions can create stigma, risks, and collateral consequences distinct from the total authorized prison term, and his earlier view would give the same-offense protection content independent of legislative authorization. The present opinion neither denies those consequences nor announces unlimited legislative power to multiply convictions. Its application of existing precedent to this clear cumulative command permits his join without deciding again the constitutional foundation on which he previously disagreed. His preserved constitutional objection remains noncontrolling; he supports affirmance of both convictions and the consecutive terms here.

Justice Breyer concurs, joined by Justices Stevens, Souter and Ginsburg. They join the Court’s opinion, including its qualified instrumentality holding. The vehicle’s transportation function and the direct seizure of its possession and control supply a substantive connection independent of the vehicle-history element. The latter element limits the vehicles covered; it does not by itself establish constitutional sufficiency. That combination sustains this application without a finding of a current interstate trip or intended resale.

They also regard the economic vehicle-acquisition ground as independently sufficient. Forcibly acquiring a marketable vehicle belongs to the class of vehicle theft connected to the interstate vehicle market. Wickard and Perez permit regulation of a properly defined economic class without requiring each individual instance to have a substantial interstate effect. A retaliatory motive does not remove the taking from that class or create a profit-intent element. Allied-Bruce’s reservation against treating remote object history as conclusive remains intact because the regulated taking supplies the commercial relationship. The concurrence preserves their broader position in Lopez; joining this sufficient instrumentality ground does not withdraw that dissent. The concurrence’s economic alternative has four published joins and is not an additional holding of the Court.
