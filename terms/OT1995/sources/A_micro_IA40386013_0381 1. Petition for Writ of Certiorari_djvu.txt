STATEMENT OF THE CASE 


,.1On June 1, 1989, a superseding indictment 
was filed in the United States District Court 
for the Southern District of Alabama, charging 
24  codefendants, including Larry Feldman, 
a/k/a/ Larry Fleming (hereinafter referred to 
as: Petitioner) with violating various drug 
laws. Petitioner's trial was severed, and he 
was tried as a single Defendant. 


Petitioner was tried before the Hon. Alex 
Howard, U.S. District Court Judge for the 
Southern District of Alabama. On November 17, 
1989, a jury found Petitioner guilty of Count 
4, violating 21 U.S.c. §846, conspiracy to 
commit a drug trafficking offense. On 
February 1, 1990, the District Court sentenced 
Petitioner to 235 months imprisonment without 
parole, plus five years' Supervised release. 


Petitioner appealed his conviction to the 
Eleventh Circuit Court of Appeals which 
affirmed his conviction and sentence without 
opinion on August 1, 1991. 


Petitioner filed a motion pursuant to 28 
U.S.C. §2255, or, in the alternative, 18 
U.S.C. §3582(c) (2), before the District Court 
on October 27, 1995. Petitioner argued that 
subsequent amendments to the commentary to 
United States Sentencing Guidelines (U.S.S.G.) 
§§1B1.3 (relevant conduct) and 301.1 
(obstructing or impeding justice) enabled him 
to collaterally attack his sentence, and that 
he should have been given a downward reduction 
for playing a minor role, if any, in the 
Richard Lynn organization. The District Court 
denied Petitioner's §2255 motion without a 
hearing on April 3, 1996, holding that 


2 


Petitioner's conviction and sentence were not 
"otherwise subject to collateral review." 
Petitioner appealed the denial of his §2255 
petition to the Eleventh Circuit Court of 
Appeals, which affirmed without an opinion on 
December 6, 1996. 


Petitioner now seeks the review of this 
Court. 


ARGUMENT 


1. Certiorari should be granted to 
resolve a split among the circuits with regard 
to whether clarifying amendments to the United 
States Sentencing Guidelines (U.S.S.G.) should 
be applied retroactively ina 28 U.S.C. §2255 
habeas corpus petition. 


 ¥ Certiorari should be granted to 
resolve whether the holding of Stinson v. 
United States, 508 U.S. 36, 113 S.Ct. 1913, 


123 L.Ed.2d 598 (1993) is limited to direct 
appeals, or applicable to post-appellate 
proceeding such as 28 U.S.C. §2255 petitions. 


Because the two questions presented for 
this Court's review are interrelated, and must 
be considered in pari materia, Petitioner 
presents both questions as part of the same 
overarching argument. 


On direct appeal, Petitioner argued that 
the District Court abused its discretion in 
refusing to hold an evidentiary hearing. 
Petitioner contended that the affidavits of 
Jack Marshall, Kevin Sheehy, Robert Eyster, 
and Butch McKeown, all of whom were leaders 
and organizers in the Richard Lynn 


organization, conclusively proved that 
Petitioner was not a member of the conspiracy. 


Petitioner also argued that subsequent 
clarifying amendments to United States 
Sentencing Guideline (U.S.S.G.) Rules §3C1.1 
and §1B1.3 commentary established that 
Petitioner was entitled to be resentenced 
pursuant to the new comments, and established 
a means of collateral attack upon Petitioner's 
sentence. Petitioner also argued that he was 
improperly denied a four-step decrease in his 
sentence for playing a minor role (if any) in 
the criminal conspiracy. 


The District Court's ruling which was 
affirmed without opinion by the Eleventh 
Circuit Court of Appeals is in direct conflict 


with this Court's decision in Stinson v. 
United States, 508 U.S. 36, 113 S.Ct. 1913, 


123 L.Ed.2d 598 (1993), which held that 
clarifying amendments to the U.S.S.G. must be 
considered in correcting a defendant's 
sentence, even after the direct appeal is 
concluded. Further, the Eleventh Circuit's 
decision is at odds with the First Circuit 
Court in Isabel v. United States, 980 F.2d 60 
(lst Cir. 1992), and Tenth Circuit in 


United

1995).

C. Downward Adjustment for Minor Role. 


Petitioner, at sentencing, requested a 
downward adjustment for role based on his 
limited involvement (arguendo) in the offense. 
The Court denied this request. Subsequent to 
Petitioner's sentencing, the United States 
Sentencing Commission modified the sentencing 
guidelines to make more equitable the 
punishment for minor players engaged in large 
scale drug conspiracies. 


15 


a ne 


U.S.S.G. §2D1.1 Commentary Application 
notes suggests that there are certain 
instances in drug cases where the relevant 
conduct base offense level may over represent 
the seriousness of the Defendant's culpability 
in the criminal activity and as such warrant a 
departure from the guidelines. (See U.S.S.G. 
1994, §2D1.1 application note (16)). 


In the instant case, Petitioner's base 


offense level was set at 36. A two-level 
3Cl1.1 increase resulted in an aggregate base 
level of 38. The guidelines based on a 


criminal history score of I were 235 to 293 
months. Fetitioner received a sentence of 235 
months. 


Assuming, arguendo, that Petitioner is 
guilty of the offense charged by the 
Government, the evidence plainly indicates 
that his was, at best, a very minor role in 
the offense. Petitioner's conduct as alleged 
by the Government warrants a §3B1l.2 downward 
adjustment of four levels. 


Subsequent to Petitioner's sentencing the 
Ninth Circuit Court of Appeals in United 
States v. Benitez, 34 F.3d 1489 (9th Cir. 
1994), recognized that application of a minor 
role adjustment should be based on a relevant 
comparison of co-participants in the case at 
hand. The Court added that in order for the 
application to apply the defendant should be 
substantially less culpable than his co- 
participants. Using this analysis, it is 
clear that Petitioner should receive a four- 
level §3B1.2 downward adjustment. 


Petitioner's culpability, if any, was 
less than most other participants. United 


16 


States v. Brown, 29 F.3d 9543 (5th Cir. 1994). 


The Government's own evidence and witnesses 
showed conclusively that Petitioner's 
participation was less than the average 


participant. United States v. Mitchell, 31 
F.3d 271 (5th Cir. 1994). 


Petitioner concedes in this case that his 
base offense level, based on the quantity of 
drugs in the total conspiracy (over 50 kilos), 
called for a base level of 36. Application 
note 16 suggests that the offense level should 
be greater than 36 for the provision to apply. 
In the instant case, it is respectfully 
submitted that the base level of 36 overstates 
the seriousness of Petitioner's culpability 
and that a departure was warranted. 


The District Court held that subsequent 
case law may not be utilized for collateral 
attack of a sentence. Petitioner contends 
that the evidence as adduced by the Government 
plainly shows that his role was, at most, a 
minor one in the Richard Lynn organization. 
It would be a manifest injustice to deny 
Petitioner a four-step downward reduction for 
minor role in the offense. - 


CONCLUSION 


Based on this Court's clear holding in 
Stinson, supra, Petitioner contends that the 
District Court and the Eleventh Circuit Court 
of Appeal erred in refusing to retroactively 
apply the clarifying amendments to the 
U.S.S.G. in his case. The Eleventh Circuit 
Court of Appeals, by affirming per curiam, 
ratified the District Court's decision as its 
precedent. 


17 


This Court should grant certiorari to 
resolve the split among the circuits with 
regard to retroactive application of 
clarifying amendments to the U.S.S.G in 28 
U.S.C. §2255 petitions, and also to correct 
the District Court's and Eleventh Circuit's 
refusal to follow the binding precdent of 
Stinson.

B. Ground Two "Relevant Conduct" and 
Ground Three "Obstruction of Justice 

Grounds Two and Three of Givner's 2255 
Motion assert that Givner may collaterally 
attack his sentence because the comments to 
Guideline Sections 1B1.3 (Relevant Conduct) 
and 3Cl.1 (Obstructing or Impeding the 
Administration of Justice) have been amended 
by the Sentencing Commission to Clarify the 
meaning of such Sections. The Court rejects 
Givner's contention that changes in the 
commentary on a section of the Guidelines may 
serve as a ground to collaterally attack a 
sentence that has been affirmed on appeal. 

The Eleventh Circuit has held that 


amendments to the comments that merely clarify 


the relevant section "must be considered" on 


direct appeal. United States v. Gardiner, 955 


F.2d 1492, 1499 (llth Cir. 1992); see also 


United States v. Howard, 923 F.2d 1500, 1503- 


1505 (llth Cir. 1991). The Eleventh Circuit 


has not extended such holding to Section 2255 
Motions. However, the United States Court of 
Appeals for the First Circuit, relying on 


Howard, has extended the Howard holding beyond 


a final decision of the Court of Appeals to a 


Section 2255 proceeding. Isabel _v. United 
States, 980 F.2d 60 (ist Car. 20m) . In a 


review of a Section 2255 Motion the First 
Circuit held: 


It is true that a sentencing court 
applies the law in effect at the date of 
sentencing. Nevertheless, in interpreting an 
applicable guideline virtually all circuits 
have found or stated that it is appropriate to 
consider post-sentencing amendments’ that 
Clarify but do not substantively change the 
guideline. 


ins -2t. 62. This Court holds that the 
reasoning of the First Circuit is misguided 


10 


and insufficient to Support such a holding and 
this Court declines to follow the . First 
Circuit. 

The Eleventh Circuit rule applies. when 
the commentary is amended after sentencing, 
but before review on direct appeal. Such a 
rule is logical because, assuming a defendant 
exercises his right of: appeal, an action 
against a defendant is not final until review - 
by an appellate court. However, to extend 
such a holding to collateral attacks flys in 
the face of finality in criminal prosecutions. 
"Perpetual disrespect for the finality of 
convictions disparages the entire criminal 
justice system." McCleskey v. Zant, 499 U.S. 
467, 492 (1991). Revisiting sentences that 
have been affirmed on direct appeal each time 
the Sentencing Commission "clarifies" the 
commentary to the Guidelines challenges the 


finality of convictions. In effect, no 


11 


sentence would be final as commentary is 


constantly being rewritten and "clarified." 
In the action before this Court, Givner was 
sentenced over six years ago; the Eleventh 
Circuit affirmed such sentence nearly five 
years ago; and Givner now attacks such 
sentence based upon alleged clarifying 
commentary subsequently adopted by the 
sentencing Commission. If this inmate is 
successful in this collateral attack, then he 
and all others Similarly situated will be 
permitted to continue assaults on their 
sentences on every occasion the Sentencing 
Commission amends commentary in a fashion that 
is deemed "clarifying." There will never be 
finality in any federal criminal conviction as 
defendants will constantly attack their 
sentences when commentary language is changed. 
Defendants may file multiple habeas petitions 


spanning over many years and the United States 


12 


— 


will have to be prepared to defend sentences 
ad infinitum. "Federal collateral litigation 
places a heavy burden on scarce federal 
judicial resources, and threatens the capacity 
of the system to resolve primary disputes." 
McCleskey v. Zant, supra, 499 U.S. at 491 
(citation omitted). The holding of the First 
Circuit unduly increases the amount of such 
collateral litigation and destroys’ the 
public's comfort in the finality of sentences. 
The burden on federal judicial resources will 
be astonishing. This Court holds’ that 
collateral review based on commentary 
"clarification" is not permitted because 
amendments to the commentary are . ~»not 
sufficient to meet the "otherwise subject to 
collateral review" standard of Section 2255. 
Therefore, the Court holds that Givner is not 


able to collaterally attack his sentence on 


13 


the ground that the comments to Guideline 


- Section 1B1.3 and 3Cl1.1 have been "clarified." 


C. Ground Four "Minor Role" 

Givner argued at sentencing that he 
should receive a downward adjustment in his 
offense level under USSG Section 2D1.1 for a 
minor role in the offense. This Court denied 
Givner's request and Givner did not appeal 
such denial. In an effort to show cause for 
not appealing this Court's finding, Givner 
argues that recent case law has clarified 
Section 2D1.1 by holding that application of a 
minor role adjustment should be determined by 
comparing the conduct of the defendant with 
the conduct of the other co-conspirators. 
Givner asserts that this law changed after his 
appeal. Givner has not supplied the Court 
with legal support for the proposition that 
subsequent case law interpreting a section of 


the Guidelines may be asserted as a ground for 


14 


collateral attack of a sentence. In addition, 
none of the cases cited by Givner, which 
allegedly reinterpreted USSG Section 2D1.1, 
are cases from the Eleventh Circuit. 
Therefore, Givner's fourth ground for review 
is an insufficient ground to support a 
collateral attack of Givner's sentence. 
III. CONCLUSION 

The Court finds that Givner has failed to 
show that his conviction and sentence are 
"otherwise subject to collateral review" and, 


therefore, Givner has failed to establish a 


claim for relief under Section 2255. The 
Court DENIES Givner's 2255 Motion. [Doc. 
#48). 


As the Court's holding is based on legal 
conclusions supported conclusively by the 


record and the file, no evidentiary hearing is 


necessary. Rule 8 (a) Rules ---§2255 
Proceedings; Cross v. United States, 893 F.2d 


15 


1287. 1292 (11th Cir.), cert. denied, 498 U.S. 
849 (1990). Purely legal issues may be 
decided without an evidentiary hearing where 
the petitioner would suffer no prejudice as a 


result. Hinman _v. United States, 730 F.2d 


649, 652 (11th Cir. 1984). Where the record 
contains all of the evidence necessary for an 
adjudication of the petitioner's legal claims, 
there is no need for a hearing. Id. at 652- 
653. Even where allegations potentially raise 
issues of fact, a hearing is not required 
where the factual dispute would have no effect 
on the conviction, sentence or relevant 


proceeding. Id. at 653.
