SUMMARY OF ARGUMENT 


The district courts’ traditional equitable powers are indispens- 
able to the independence of the Judiciary and to the maintenance 
of our tripartite system of government. Although Congress may 
limit the district courts’ equitable powers, decisions of this Court 
make clear that once Congress invokes the district courts’ 
equitable jurisdiction, Congress may not limit that jurisdiction 
unless it unequivocally expresses its intent to do so. This Court 
requires Congress to state its intent to limit the district courts’ 
equitabie juri sdiction clearly and unmistakably to prevent the 
aggrandizement of Congress at the expense of the courts. Garbled 
whispers by Congress in a statute's legislative history definitely 
should not displace rules refined over centuries by a co-equal 
branch of government acting in its area of expertise. 


In enacting the citizen suit provision of RCRA , 42 U.S.C. § 
6972(a)(1)(B), Congress invoked the district courts’ equitable 
jurisdiction without clearly and unmistakably limiting the scope 
of that jurisdiction. As a result, RCRA’s citizen suit provision 
empowers the district courts to exercise all their traditional 
equitable powers. Those traditional equitable powers include 
the power to grant restitution. Granting a landowner restitution 
from the prior owner who caused the contamination of the 
property furthers two of Congress’ policy goals under RCRA, 
namely, promoting prompt remediation of contamination and 


— 


assuring that the party responsible for the contamination incurs 
the costs of remediation.’ 


> Although this amicus brief does not discuss how the “imminent and 
substantial endangerment” clause of RCRA’s citizen suit provision should be 
interpreted, Amicus Bi-State agrees with, and joins in, the arguments pre- 
sented in the Brief of Amici Curiae Massachusetts, Missouri, etc., that 
construing the “imminent and substantial endangerment” clause to preclude 
parties who have incurred costs of remediation prior to filing a RCRA citizen 
suit from pursuing restitution under RCRA’s citizen suit provision would be 
inconsistent with Congress’ invocation of the district courts’ equitable 
jurisdiction and would be inconsistent with Congress’ policy concern of 
promoting prompt remediation. Moreover, Amicus Bi-State believes that 
regardless of how this Court interprets the “imminent and substantial endan- 
germent” clause, this Court should decide the extent of the district courts’ 
equitable jurisdiction under RCRA's citizen suit provision so that parties 
faced with decisions regarding the appropriate means of addressing contami- 
nation that “may present an imminent and substantial endangerment” can 
conduct their affairs with a clear understanding of their rights under RCRA’s 
citizen suit provision.

ARGUMENT 


The District Courts Are Empowered To Award Restitu- 
tion Under RCRA’s Citizen Suit Provision Because 
Congress Did Not Clearly And Unmistakably Limit The 
Broad Equitable Jurisdiition Of The District Courts 
And The Remedy Of Restitution Promotes RCRA’s 


Statutory Purposes. 


A. The District Courts Are Empowered To Grant All 
Traditional Equitable Remedies, Including Restitution, 
Because RCRA’s Citizen Suit Provision Invokes The 
Broad Equitable Jurisdiction Of The District Courts 


Without Clearly And Unmistakably Limiting That Ju- 
risdiction. 


1. Our government is one of separated nowers, with an 
independent Judiciary to check the excesses of the other branches 
and to protect individual rights. See, e.g, Commodity Futures 
Trading Comm'n v. Schor, 478 U.S. 833, 848 (1986). From the 
very inception of our Republic, the drafters of the Constitution 
viewed equitable jurisdiction as playing an indispensable role in 
securing the independence of the Judiciary and, consequently, in 
maintaining separation of governmental powers. Thus, our 
Constitution extends “[t)}he judicial Power ... to all Cases, in Law 
and Equity, arising under [the] Constitution, the Laws of the 
United States, and Treaties made, or which shall be made, under 
their Authority ....” U.S. Const. art. Ill, § 2, cl. 1 (emphasis 
added). Alexander Hamilton thought equitable jurisdiction of 
such importance to the Judiciary that he expounded on its 
necessity: 


It has also been asked, what need of the word “equity”? 
What equitable causes can grow out of the constitution and 
laws of the United States? There is hardly a subject of 
litigation between individuals, which may not involve 
those ingredients of fraud, accident, trust or hardship, 


which would render the matter an object of equitable, rather 
than of legal jurisdiction, as the distinction is known and 
established in several of the states. 


The Federalist No. 80 at 406-07 (Alexander Hamilton) (Gary 
Wills ed. 1982). 


The early decisions of this Court also recognize the role equity 
plays in maintaining the separation of powers and in securing an 
independent Judiciary. Acentury and a half ago, Justice Johnson 
spoke of the indispensability of that jurisdiction to the exercise 
of judicial power: 


For, as an essential branch or exercise of judicial power, 
[equitable jurisdiction] is acknowledged to exist every- 
where; nor is it possible for any one acquainted with its 
nature and character, and the remedies it affords for the 
assertion of rights or the punishment of wrongs, to doubt 
that the power to exercise it, and the means of exercising it, 
must exist somewhere; or the administration of justice will 
be embarrassed, if not incomplete. - 


Lessee of Livingston v. Moore, 32 U.S. (7 Pet.) 469, 547 (1833). 


Recognizing judicial power is incomplete without equitable 
jurisdiction, this Court concluded that, in sovereignties in which 
the judiciary lacks the full range of equitable jurisdiction, the 
separation of governmental powers is less than perfect: 


The natural distribution and the necessary distribution to 
individual security, is into legislative, executive and judi- 
cial; but it is obvious, that every community may make a 
perfect or imperfect separation and distribution of these 
powers, at its will. It has pleased Pennsylvania, in her 
constitution, to make what most jurists would pronounce an 
imperfect separation of those powers; she has not thought 
it necessary to make any imperative provision for incorpo- 
rating the equity jurisdiction, in its full latitude, into her 


jurisprudence... . 


Id. at 546-47. 


2. Equity, though vital to the Judiciary and separation of 
powers, is not unchecked. As is appropriate in a democracy, our 
Constitution has relegated to Congress the ultimate say in what 
remedies, including equitable ones, the lower federal courts may 
grant. U.S. Const. art. III, § 1; Glidden Co. v. Zdanok, 370 U.S. 
530, 557 (1962) (holding it consistent with Article III for 
Congress to limit the remedial powers of a federal court to the 
award of money damages); Lauf v. E.G. Shinner & Co., 303 U.S. 
323, 329 (1938) (holding that Congress has the power to pre- 
clude the district courts from issuing injunctions in the absence 
of specific findings). 

Congress’ ultimate control over the lower federal courts’ 
equitable powers, of course, does not make those equitable 
powers, or the separation-of-powers structure that they promote, 
unworthy of protection. It just makes those powers and that 
structure hard to protect. This Court, however, has been vigilant 
in protecting such fundamental structures of our Constitution in 
the face of Congress’ express constitutional authority to alter 
them. 


A vital weapon in this Court's arsenal for safeguarding hard- 
to-protect constitutional structures has been the clear (or “plain’’) 
statement rule.’ Thus, to protect “the fundamental constitutional 
balance between the Federal Government and the States,” in the 
face of Congress’ express constitutional power to abrogate the

States’ Eleventh Amendment immunity, this Court held “that 
Congress may abrogate the States’ constitutionally secured 
immunity from suit in federal court only by making its intention 
unmistakably clear in the language of the statute.” Afascadero 
State Hosp. v. Scanlon, 473 U.S. 234, 238, 242 (1985). And, 
again, in Delmuth v. Muth, 491 U.S. 223, 227-28 (1989), this 
Court applied that “simple but stringent” clear statement test 
“[t]o temper congress’ acknowledged powers of abrogation with 
due concern for the Eleventh Amendment's role as an essential 
component of our constitutional structure.” Similarly, in Gre- 
gory v. Ashcroft, 501 U.S. 452, 470 (1991), this Court applied a 
clear (“plain”) statement rule to protect the internal operations of 
a State government from Congress’ constitutional authority to 
enact laws affecting those internal operations. Significantly, this 
Court stated in Ashcroft that the structure of state sovereignty in 
a federal system and separation of powers are of comparable 
value to our Republic. /d. at 458. 


To protect separation of powers, this Court has enunciated a 
similar clear statement rule with respect to limitations on the 
traditional range of the district courts’ equitable powers. In 
Porter v. Warner Holding Co., 328 U.S. 395, 398 (1946), this 
Court addressed the remedies available to district courts under 
the Emergency Price Control Act of 1942, ch. 26, § 205(a), 56 
Stat. 33 (the “Price Control Act”) and, in doing so, created aclear 
statement rule for determining the scope of relief afforded by 
statutes that allow resort to the district courts’ equitable jurisdic- 
tion. 


The Price Control Act’ s enforcement provision, Section 205(a), 
like RCRA’s citizen suit provision, explicitly empowered the 
district courts to enforce compliance with and to enjoin past and 
future violations of the Act. /d. at 397. Like RCRA's citizen suit 
provision, Section 205(a) also empowered the district courts to 
issue “other orders” but did not specify that the term “other 
orders” encompassed restitution. /d. Nevertheless, this Court 


—_ we 


held that the Price Control Act empowered the district courts to 
grant restitution. /d. at 402. 

Acknowledging the district courts’ jurisdiction under the 
Price Control Act “to enjoin” and “to enforce” is an equitable 
jurisdiction, this Court expounded on the usual scope of equi- 
table jurisdiction. 

Power is thereby resident in the District Court, in exercising 
this jurisdiction, “to do equity and to mould each decree to 
the necessities of the particular case.” Hecht Co. v. Bowles, 
321 U.S. 321, 329. It may act so as to adjust and reconcile 
competing claims and so as to accord full justice to all the 
real parties in interest; if necessary, persons not originally 
connected with the litigation may be brought before the 
court so that their rights in the subject matter may be 
determined and enforced. In addition, the court may go 
beyond the matters immediately underlying its equitable 
jurisdiction and decide whatever other issues and give 
whatever other relief may be necessary under the circum- 
stances. Only in that way can equity do complete rather 
than truncated justice. 


Porter, 328 U.S. at 398. The Court then addressed Congress’ 

power to limit the scope of the district courts’ equitable jurisdic- 

tion: 
[T]he comprehensiveness of this equitable jurisdiction is 
not to be denied or limited in the absence of a clear and valid 
legislative command. Unless a statute in so many words, or 
by a necessary and inescapable inference, restricts the 
court’s jurisdiction in equity, the full scope of that jurisdic- 
tion is to be recognized and applied. 


Id. 


This Court has reaffirmed this clear statement rule on eight 
subsequent occasions. See California v. American Stores Co.., 


495 U.S. 271,295 (1990); Amoco Prod. Co. v. Gambell, 480 U.S. 
531, 542-46 (1987); United States v. Rodgers, 461 U.S. 677, 708 
(1983); Weinberger v. Romero-Barcelo, 456 U.S. 305, 313 
(1982); Califano v. Yamasaki, 442 U.S. 682, 705 (1979); Rene- 
gotiation Bd. v. Bannercraft Clothing Co., 415 U.S. 1, 19-20 
(1974); Mitchell v. Robert DeMario Jewelry, Inc., 361 U.S. 288, 
290-91 (1960); United States v. Moore, 340 U.S. 616, 619 
(1951). See also Hecht Co. v. Bowles, 321 U.S. 321, 330 (1944) 
(holding “if Congress desired to make ... an abrupt departure 
from traditional equity practice ... it would have made its desire 
plain”). 


Notably, on two of those occasions, this Court specifically 
held that even when Congress simply granted the district courts 
power to issue injunctions, the statute under consideration also 
empowered the district courts to grant restitution. Moore, 340 
U.S. at 619-20; Mitchell, 361 U.S. at 296. 


This Court and the lower federal courts have applied the 
Porter clear statement rule in numerous regulatory settings. See, 
e.g., American Stores, 495 U.S. 271 (interpreting Clayton Act); 
Amoco, 480 U.S. 531 (interpreting Alaska National Interest 
Lands Conservation Act); Weinberger, 456 U.S. 305 (interpret- 
ing Federal Water Pollution Control Act); Yamasaki, 442 U.S. 
682 (interpreting Social Security Act); Bannercraft Clothing, 
415 U.S. | (interpreting Freedom of Information Act); Mitchell, 
361 U.S. 288 (interpreting Fair Labor Standards Act of 1938); 
Moore, 340 U.S. 616 (interpreting Housing and Rent Act of 
1947); Porter, 328 U.S. 395 (interpreting Emergency Price 
Control Act of 1942); Reebok Int'l, Lid. v. Marnatech Enters, 
970 F.2d 552, 561-62 (9th Cir. 1992) (interpreting Lanham Act); 
Pierce v. Amaral, 928 F.2d 94, 96 (8th Cir. 1991) (interpreting 
Interstate Land Sales Full Disclosure Act); SEC v. First City Fin. 
Corp., 890 F.2d 1215, 1230 (D.C. Cir. 1989) (interpreting 
Securities Exchange Act of 1934); FTC v. Southwest Sunsites, 
Inc., 665 F.2d 711, 718-19 (Sth Cir.) (interpreting Federal Trade 
Commission Act), cert. denied, 456 U.S. 973 (1982).

The Porter clear statement rule thus has become an integral! 
fibre in the cloth of our regulatory law. 


3. In enacting RCRA’s citizen suit provision, Congress 
specifically invoked the district courts’ equitable jurisdiction 
when it granted them the power to “restrain”.* Congress did not 
stop there, however; Congress also provided that the district 
courts may “order such person to take such other action as may 
be necessary.” This Court twice has stated that Congress is well 
aware of the existence of the principles encapsulated in the 
Porter rule. American Stores, 495 U.S. at 295; Weinberger, 456 
U.S. at 313. In RCRA’s citizen suit provision, however, Con- 
gress did not make clear any intent it may have had to limit the 
district courts’ equitable jurisdiction. RCRA'’s citizen suit 
provision is devoid of preclusive language. In light of Porter,

Congress’ inclusion in RCRA's citizen suit provision of lan- 
guage invoking the district courts’ equitable jurisdiction without 
expressly limiting the scope of that jurisdiction evinces an 
unambiguous intent to make the traditional equitable remedies, 
including restitution, available to district courts addressing RCRA 
citizen suits. 

This Court may find isolated statements in RCRA’s legisla- 
tive history suggesting that the district courts somehow are 
precluded from granting relief other than an injunction. Such 
isolated statements are “beside the point.” De/muth, 491 U.S. at 
230. As this Court held in Delmuth, to satisfy a clear statement 
rule “evidence of congressional intent must be both unequivocal 
and textual.” Id. (emphasis added). “If Congress’ intention is 
‘unmistakably clear in the language of the statute,’ recourse to 
legislative history wiil be unnecessary; if Congress’ intention is 
not unmistakably clear, recourse to legislative history will be 
futile, because by definition [a clear statement rule] will not be 
met.” /d. 


B. The District Courts Properly May Grant Restitution 
Because That Remedy Furthers RCRA’s Dual Statu- 
tory Purposes Of Promoting Prompt Remediation Of 
Contamination And Assuring The Party Responsible 
For The Contamination Incurs The Costs Of 
Remediation. 


1. As discussed above, by failing to state clearly and 
unmistakably in RCRA’s citizen suit provision that the district 
courts are precluded from granting restitution, Congress left the 
Judiciary free to exercise its traditional equitable power to grant 
such relief. Before the district courts can grant restitution under 
a regulatory statute, however, this Court also requires that 
restitution further the purposes of the statute. See Mitchell, 361 
U.S. at 291-92 (“When Congress entrusts to an equity court the 
enforcement of prohibitions contained in a regulatory enact- 


— 


ment, it must be taken to have acted cognizant of the historic 
power of equity to provide complete relief in light of the statutory 
purposes); Porter, 328 U.S. at 400 (holding that, in fashioning 
equitable remedies under the Emergency Price Control Act, the 
district courts “must act primarily to effectuate the policy of the 
Emergency Price Control Act”); Hecht, 321 U.S. at 331 (holding 
that the district court’s equitable discretion to withhold or grant 
injunctive relief under a regulatory statute must be exercised in 
light of the large objectives of that statute). 

Restitution does further RCRA’s purposes. Indeed, restitu- 
tion presents the best option of furthering RCRA’s purposes 
when, as in this case, the government seeks to have a subsequent 
property owner remediate the contamination caused by others. 


2. Congress enacted the Resource Conservation and Re- 
covery Act of 1976 (RCRA), Pub. L. No. 94-580, 90 Stat. 2795 
(1976), as an amendment to the Solid Waste Disposal Act. 42 
U.S.C. §§ 6901 -6992k (1988). RCRA initially had a prospective 
focus. Congress designed RCRA to encourage parties to mini- 
mize waste production and to require parties handling solid and 
hazardous wastes to observe appropriate management practices 
so that environmental contamination resulting from waste han- 
dling and disposal would be minimized. 42 U.S.C. § 6902 
(1988). 


Congress first focused on the need to address retroactively the 
problems of facilities contaminated as a result of past treatment 
or disposal of hazardous substances in 1980, when Congress 
enacted the Comprehensive Environmental Response Compen- 
sation and Liability Act (CERCLA), Pub. L. No. 96-510, 94 Stat. 
2767 (i980), creating a retroactive, strict liability regime de- 
signed to promote prompt remediation and to assure that the 
“polluter pays.” S.Rep. No. 848, 96th Cong., 2d Sess. 33 (1980). 


By the time Congress enacted the Hazardous and Solid Waste 
Amendments of 1984, Pub. L. No. 98-616, 98 Stat. 3224 (1984) 


ny 


(HSWA), however, Congress apparently had realized that 
CERCLA would not succeed in promoting the prompt remediation 
of present and historical contamination problems associated 
with (1) all facilities engaged in the treatment, storage or disposal 
of hazardous waste, and (2) the improper management of petro- 
leum underground storage tanks systems, as Congress specifi- 
cally addressed these two problems in HSWA. With respect to 
contamination problems at treatment, storage and disposal fa- 
cilities, Congress included in HSWA provisions authorizing the 
EPA to require “corrective action” at such facilities. Pub. L. No. 
98-616, Tit. II, § 206, 98 Stat. 3239 (1984) (codified at 42 U.S.C. 
§ 6924(u)(1988)) (requiring as a permit condition “corrective 
action for all releases of hazardous waste or constituents from 
any solid waste management unit at a treatment, storage or 
disposal facility”) and Pub. L. No. 98-616, Tit. II, § 233, 98 Stat. 
257-58 (1984) (codified at 42 U.S.C. § 6923(h)( 1988)) (autho- 
rizing issuance of corrective action orders when “there is or has 
been a release of hazardous waste into the environment” from an 
interim status facility). With respect to preventing and remediating 
contamination problems associated with leaking petroleum un- 
derground storage tank systems, Congress included in HSWA an 
entirely new subchapter outlining management and remediation 
procedures associated with petroleum underground storage tank 
systems. Pub. L. No. 98-616, Tit. VI, § 601, 98 Stat. 3277-3288 
(1984) (codified as amended at 42 U.S.C. §§ 6991-699 1(i)( 1988)). 


At the same time that it enacted these new provisions designed 
to assure that parties responsible for environmental contamina- 
tion promptly clean up such contamination, Congress also 
amended RCRA's citizen suit provision, by adding section 
6972(a)(1)(B). Pub. L. No. 98-616, Tit. IV, § 401, 98 Stat. 3268 
(1984)(codified at 42 U.S.C. § 6972(a) (1988)). 


Through these provisions, Congress added to RCRA’s pro- 
spective management focus a retroactive remedial focus that 
encompasses CERCLA’s environmental policy goals of pro- 
moting prompt-remediation of environmental contamination 


and of assuring that the parties responsible for the contamination 
incur the costs associated with remediating the contamination. 


3. When a state seeks to compel an innocent current 
property owner to remediate contamination resulting from the 
activities of a former property owner, Congress can be sure that 
both of its policy goals - the prompt cleanup of contamination 
and the shifting of costs to the culpable party - will be advanced 
only if district courts have the power to grant restitution to the 
current property owner who promptly cleans up the contamina- 
tion. To understand why the availability of restitution plays a 
key role in facilitating Congress’ dual policy goals, one simply 
needs to evaluate the options available to an innocent current 
property owner faced with a state cleanup order. 


If restitution is not available under RCRA’s citizen suit 
provision, when a state orders an innocent current property 
owner to remedy contamination caused by a former property 
owner, the current property owner has almost no incentive to 
remediate the contamination promptly because it has no guaran- 
tee that it can recover its costs. Rather, the current property 
owner has every incentive to contravene one of Congress’ gaals 
- prompt remediation - to facilitate Congress’ other goal - the 
imposition of costs on culpable parties. Instead of promptly 
remediating the property and incurring the associated costs of 
remediation, the current property owner has every incentive, 
including the availability of attorneys fees, to delay remediation 
while pursuing a citizen suit action under RCRA directed toward 
obtaining an order that will force the former owner and/or 
operator to remediate the contamination and thereby incur the 
costs of remediation directly. The existence of possible indepen- 
dent state common law claims for damages, such as misrepresen- 
tation and breach of warranty, are unlikely to provide any 
significant incentive for the current property owner promptly to 
incur the costs of remediation given the uncertainty of recovery 
under such fact-dependent causes of action, particularly given 


~~ = 


potential statute of limitations problems, and because the current 
owner will have to absorb all transaction costs associated with 
the litigation 

Accordingly, if district courts do not have the authority to 
grant restitution under RCRA’s citizen suit provision, current 
property owners nonetheless will make use of RCRA’s citizen 
suit provision, but will do so in a way that promotes only one of 
Congress’ policy goals, shifting the cost of remediation to 
culpable parties, at the expense of Congress’ other policy goal, 
assuring prompt remediation. By contrast, if restitution is 
available under RCRA’s citizen suit provision, when a state 
orders an innocent current property owner to remediate the 
property, the innocent current property owner has an incentive to 
remediate promptly because it can be confident that it can shift 
the costs of remediation to the culpable party and seek the 
recovery of attorneys’ fees. Consequently, the availability of 
restitution is essential to satisfying Congress’ policy goals of 
prompt remediation and assuring that the parties responsible for 
the contamination incur the costs of remediation..
