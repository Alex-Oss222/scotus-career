## 2. Judgment below and material corrections to the supplied capsule

The Second Circuit **vacated and remanded** the district judgment. It did not order adoption of adjusted figures. Kearse wrote for the panel majority; Leval joined; Timbers dissented by adopting the district-court analysis. A4, A23–40.

The majority rejected both the challengers' demand for de novo adjudication and the use of ordinary APA arbitrary-and-capricious review for the constitutional claim as it understood this record. It required a showing that the Secretary's refusal to make the adjustment was **essential to achievement of a legitimate governmental objective**. The burden shifted after the majority concluded that challengers had shown the Secretary failed to make the required good-faith effort toward equality as nearly as practicable. Its use of the familiar strict-scrutiny vocabulary earlier in the opinion must not obscure the precise formulation actually sent back on remand. A23, A33–40.

The majority relied on the voting/apportionment line—Baker, Wesberry, Reynolds, Kirkpatrick and Karcher—and reasoned that constitutional apportionment claims did not require the intentional-discrimination showing applicable to the ordinary disparate-impact claims in Washington v. Davis and Arlington Heights. It treated foreseeable minority undercounts, feasible improvement, and the Secretary's priority choices as supporting the good-faith inference. These are the lower court's legal characterizations, not independently established Supreme Court holdings in this simulation. A26–40.

The appellate majority separately rejected the States' argument that §195 categorically barred the proposed statistical adjustment. It read §195 together with §141(a) and the 1976 amendments/legislative history as permitting and encouraging statistical supplementation of an actual enumeration. A23–25. This is a reviewed lower ruling, not a resolved simulation rule.

The capsule's “no material conflict” statement needs correction. **Timbers expressly identified conflicting Sixth- and Seventh-Circuit treatment:** City of Detroit v. Franklin, 4 F.3d 1367, and Tucker v. U.S. Department of Commerce, 958 F.2d 1411. A40. Their full opinions have not been read by this investigator; the exact extent of the conflict should be verified rather than reduced to an assumed identical holding on every issue.

The district court found that adjusted counts would be better “for most purposes,” and that challengers had made a powerful case for adjustment; it nevertheless upheld nonadjustment under the APA because the Secretary's reasons were not arbitrary or capricious. Its expressed view that it probably would order adjustment on de novo review is not an order and does not establish that every geographical measure improved. A59, A77–91, A94–95.

The district court separately vacated a protective order over already-produced adjusted block-level tapes, allowing their use and public disclosure. A91–94. The appellate opinion under review concerns the adjustment challenge. Do not silently extend the present merits review or any proposed remedy to the tape-disclosure ruling without petition/brief support.

## 3. Facts and administrative process verified from primary text

The 1990 initial enumeration used an address list, mailed questionnaires, repeat mailings, personal nonresponse follow-up and coverage-improvement programs. The latter added approximately 5.4 million people. The reported enumeration was 249,632,692. A14–17, A47–49. Broad follow-up efforts included directed minority-language outreach, vacant-unit rechecks, a “Were you counted?” campaign, parole/probation checks, housing recanvasses and local review. These efforts are relevant to the claim of a deliberate abandonment of counting efforts; they do not erase the undisputed differential undercount.

The PES used about 170,000 households and roughly 5,000 blocks (the Secretary gives the more precise 171,390 housing units/group quarters and 5,290 block clusters). It used matching, dual-system estimation, 1,392 mutually exclusive poststrata and smoothing to generate adjustment factors, applied down to blocks and then aggregated. A7–8, A16–17, A51–58, A157. The Secretary's detailed account refers to 377,381 surveyed persons; the district/appellate descriptions round to approximately 400,000. Preserve the difference between a precise administrative count and a rounded judicial description.

The contemporaneous proposed PES adjustment estimated a national undercount around 2.1% (5,269,917), with higher rates among Hispanics (5.2%), American Indians (5.0%), Black persons (4.8%) and Asian-Pacific Islanders (3.1%); non-Hispanic whites were estimated at 1.2%. A17–18, A50, A58. The district court's footnote 12 reports subsequent computer/statistical revisions reducing the estimate to about 1.6%; that later observation is not the information set on which the July 1991 decision was initially made. A58. Secretary A180 also distinguished production DSE's 2.1% figure from the roughly 1.4% bias-corrected estimate in the total-error analysis. These are distinct measures and times, not interchangeable “true population” findings.

The proposed principal adjustment would transfer House seats to California and Arizona from Wisconsin and Pennsylvania under the existing formula. A17, A87, A220. One alternate smoothing method would instead transfer only Wisconsin's seat to California. A220. The controversy is not simply whether adding people always improves representation; it includes the geographic distribution of estimated persons and the effects of alternative assumptions.

The 1989 stipulation vacated the 1987 nonadjustment decision, required de novo consideration by the new Secretary with an open mind, a PES of at least 150,000 households suitable for possible correction, published technical and policy guidelines, expert advice, a detailed explanation if adjustment were declined, and a decision by July 15, 1991. It expressly retained the Secretary's lawful supervisory and decision authority. A9–13, A54–55. The stipulation's “de novo” reconsideration describes the agency obligation, not a requirement that reviewing courts decide census methodology de novo.

The eight-person Special Advisory Panel divided four to four. The Census Bureau's Undercount Steering Committee favored adjustment seven to two; Director Bryant favored adjustment while acknowledging serious professional disagreement. The Under Secretary/Administrator opposed it. A19–20, A59–60. The district court rejected the asserted showing of improper partisan influence on this record. A62–63 n.16. Do not describe the Secretary's 1991 decision as judicially found to be a partisan sham.

## 4. All eight guidelines, actual findings and qualifications

These are administrative guidelines adopted under the stipulation, **not the operative language of §§141 or 195**. Their content and legality must remain separate from any statutory or constitutional rule.

1. **Accuracy and supplementation.** The enumeration was presumptively the most accurate count at national, state and local levels unless the adjusted count was shown more accurate, using accepted statistical practice and professional judgment. Statistical/inferential procedures could supplement, not substitute for, the census. The Secretary prioritized **distributive accuracy**—correct geographic population shares—over numerical totals alone. He accepted likely national numerical improvement yet found insufficient convincing improvement in geographical shares, including uncertainty at small-area levels. A12, A146–147, A151–201.
2. **Consistency, completeness and usability.** Adjusted counts had to be consistent/complete at national, state, local and block levels and of sufficient quality/detail for reapportionment, redistricting and all published uses. The Secretary acknowledged that the proposed figures met consistency and detail requirements; his objection concerned quality, including residual heterogeneity within poststrata. He did not find that block-level figures could not be produced. A148, A202–213.
3. **Prespecification and robustness.** Proposed adjustments had to arise from prespecified procedures and be robust to reasonable alternatives in production and statistical models. The Secretary acknowledged both the impossibility of specifying every step beforehand and broad compliance/professionalism in the 1990 effort. He expressly found **no evidence of unprofessional or political manipulation of this PES**. Concerns included alternative smoothing choices with different seat consequences and future susceptibility to political influence. Aggregate national robustness did not eliminate those concerns. A148–149, A213–228.
4. **Future census efforts.** Both adjustment and nonadjustment effects on public participation and institutional effort had to be considered. The NORC survey showed lower expressed participation following either outcome; the Secretary found no compelling evidence either way as to individual motivation. He nevertheless predicted greater harm from adjustment to local outreach, congressional funding, enumerator incentives and professional independence. These are reasoned predictions claimed by the Secretary, not empirical findings that adjustment necessarily causes those harms. A149–150, A228–238.
5. **Constitutional and statutory compatibility.** Adjustment could not violate the Constitution or federal law. The Secretary discussed legal concerns but expressly stated that **legal considerations did not provide the basis for his decision**, because his statistical/substantive grounds already led him to decline adjustment. A150, A238–241. A categorical §195 prohibition cannot simply be attributed to the actual agency rationale.
6. **Sufficient timely data.** If sufficient data and analysis were unavailable to publish adjusted counts by July 15, 1991, nonadjustment would follow. The Secretary found sufficient evidence to decide **against adjustment**, while emphasizing incomplete research, late corrections and limited professional scrutiny. A150–151, A241–248. The district court noted that adjusted counts were ready for publication by the deadline; nonproduction was not the dispositive rationale. A86 n.26. Do not recast the result as an automatic missed-deadline decision.
7. **Political-transfer disruption.** Both courses' disruption had to be assessed. The Secretary considered ongoing state redistricting, possible House reapportionment changes, litigation and state deadlines, ultimately finding adjustment more disruptive. He recognized that nonadjustment could also generate litigation. Challengers disputed reliance on already-released figures because notices had warned users that adjustment remained possible. A87–88, A249–256. The district court also identified an inconsistency between the decision's references to important funding shares and its claim that relatively little federal money would move; it held that inconsistency insufficient to invalidate this guideline analysis. A88.
8. **Explanation and documentation.** The general rationale had to be clearly stated; technical documentation had to meet professional standards. The Secretary found this satisfied and **neutral between outcomes**, because adjustment too could have been explained. Statistical complexity/public incomprehension was not an independent veto. A151, A256–258.

The district court summarized guidelines 1, 2, 3, 4 and 7 as supporting nonadjustment, with 5, 6 and 8 not tipping the balance. A71. Keep that summary distinct from each guideline's actual command and the Secretary's own stated reasoning.

Important contested statistical details: the Bureau's corrected loss-function estimates favored improved state shares in 29 States versus 21 worsened at the measured variance; allowing a variance factor of 2.0 within the committee's suggested 1.7–3.0 range changed the estimate to 28 or 29 worsened. A190–193. The dispute included what loss function/value weighting to use, whether “target population” was another estimator rather than truth, whether total-error estimates included all material uncertainty, and whether group-level estimates justified conclusions at smaller geographic levels. A181–201. The Secretary's worked equal-undercount example at A183 shows how absolute counts can improve while proportional shares deteriorate; it is an illustration, not a finding that the actual 1990 undercount was uniform.

## 5. Operative statutory terms and necessary separations

**13 U.S.C. §141(a)** commands a decennial population census as of April 1, in form/content determined by the Secretary, “including the use of sampling procedures and special surveys”; it also authorizes obtaining other necessary census information. **Section 141(b)** requires state totals for congressional apportionment within nine months, reported to the President. **Section 141(c)** separately provides for State-requested geographic tabulation plans, nonpartisan criteria, consultation over defects and the Secretary's final geographic-format authority, with State tabulations/basic counts transmitted no later than one year. The census discretion and these timing/reporting duties are separate.

**Sections 141(d) and (e)** concern the mid-decade census and its uses. Subsection (d) authorizes the specified 1985-and-every-ten-years mid-decade census using sampling/surveys. Subsection (e)(1) directs use of the most recent comparable census data for the specified federal-program benefit determinations; **(e)(2) independently forbids using mid-decade information for congressional apportionment or prescribing congressional districts**. That express mid-decade restriction is not the text of the distinct §195 exception.

**Section 141(f)** requires reports to the relevant congressional committees on proposed subjects/types of information at least three years before the census, proposed questions at least two years before, and necessary modifications after those submissions but before the census. **Section 141(g)** defines “census of population” to include housing and related matters. None alone imposes a duty to use this PES.

**13 U.S.C. §195**, complete operative text:

> Except for the determination of population for purposes of apportionment of Representatives in Congress among the several States, the Secretary shall, if he considers it feasible, authorize the use of the statistical method known as “sampling” in carrying out the provisions of this title.

Preserve “shall,” “if he considers it feasible,” and the full exception. Whether the exception prohibits apportionment sampling or excepts that use from the mandatory-feasibility direction is a statutory dispute, not settled by replacing the words with “the Secretary may always sample.” The lower majority's reconciliation with §141(a) is summarized above.

**2 U.S.C. §2a(a)–(b)** requires the President's prescribed census/apportionment statement and the Clerk's certifications using equal proportions and at least one Representative per State. It does not itself answer all questions about later correction, coercive relief against the President or a new allocation. The Secretary's discussion of possible congressional action at A250–251 is the agency's legal view, not a holding that courts lack any remedy.

The district court quoted **State Farm's APA §706(2)(A) formulation**: forbidden factors, total failure to consider an important aspect, explanations contrary to the record, or explanations too implausible to reflect expertise/different judgment. A69. Its later colloquial “beyond the pale” language should not erase those elements. A89–91. A separate official full-text §706 check was not undertaken here.

## 6. Entering-law points already verified before the boundary incident

The fresh validator should use the actual simulation authorities, not the lower opinions' historical descriptions of post-divergence Supreme Court law. In particular, the district court's discussion at A63–65 of historical Franklin's finality result **does not state this simulation's law**.

Exact relevant Standards titles in the entering-law slice are:

- **“Constitutional review of interstate equal-proportions apportionment.”** Montana preserves constitutional review and allows the particular relative-equality method amid indivisible seats, state boundaries and minimum representation. It neither validates every counting rule nor imports every intrastate district-equality requirement into the national census.
- **“Counting temporarily overseas federal personnel with enduring state ties.”** Franklin allows the specified residence-connected inclusion, not any dataset or any census methodology.
- **“Reviewability of the Secretary's completed census determination.”** Franklin holds this kind of completed census determination final under §704 despite later presidential transmission; the statutes supply law to apply and do not entirely commit counting to unreviewable discretion. It leaves the President's own APA status/coercive-relief question open.
- **“An agency interpretation necessarily embodied in its decision.”** Boston & Maine permits a reasonably discernible actual agency rationale, but not new findings or a substitute appellate policy.
- **“Limited modification authority and mandatory tariff filing.”** MCI enforces the actual statutory delegation before deference. It establishes neither a universal major-questions rule nor a census-specific prohibition on sampling.

The fuller Franklin holdings in the entering-law slice also require a contemporaneous, reasonably discernible explanation connecting information and the agency's objective to the chosen method, applying State Farm/Bowman. Its record-specific approval of home-of-record information does not predetermine the 1990 PES dispute.

The sources thus preserve distinct litigated routes for the clean validator to assess: the Second Circuit's constitutional burden; ordinary APA review of the actual decision under simulated Franklin; the States' statutory-bar position and its relation to the §141/§195 texts; and the relief appropriate to whichever defect, if any, is established. This note selects none. Lower-court findings that adjustment improves many measures, reviewability under Franklin, and authority to use a method are separate from entitlement to an injunction compelling that method.
