STATEMENT OF INTEREST OF AMICI CURIAE 


Kaufman and Broad-South Bay, Inc. is a real estate 
development company that developed for residential use 
approximately one hundred acres of real property in Milpitas, 
California. After discovering that hundreds of barrels of toxic 
waste had been dumped and buried on the property years before by 
others, Kaufman and Broad-South Bay spent over $26 million to 
remedy the dangerous conditions at the site. Kaufman and Broad- 
South Bay, Inc. is a wholly owned subsidiary of Kaufman and 
Broad Home Corporation. Subsidiaries of Kaufman and Broad 
Home Corporation develop homes throughout the State of 
California. In October, 1992, Kaufman and Broad-South Bay filed 
an action in the United States District Court for the Northern 
District of California, captioned Kaufman and Broad-South Bay. 
Inc. vy. Unisys Corporation, et al., No. C-92-20677 EFL, which 
remains pending in that court. On May 7, 1993, in an order 
published at 822 F. Supp. 1468 (N.D. Cal. 1993), the district court 
dismissed that portion of Kaufman and Broad-South Bay, Inc.'s 
complaint that seeks restitution of response costs under 
section 7002(a)(1)(B) of RCRA, 42 U.S.C. section 6972(a)(1)(B). 


Petitioners here seek review of a decision of the United 
States Court of Appeals for the Ninth Circuit that expressly 
“disapprove(s} of the reasoning used by district courts that have 
dismissed complaints brought under section 6972(a)(1)(B) to the 
extent the plaintiffs sought reimbursement of clean-up costs.” KFC 
Western. Inc. vy. Meghrig, 49 F.3d 518, 523 (9th Cir. 1995). In 
the ruling challenged here, the Ninth Circuit specifically identifies

it criticized. Accordingly, the Ninth Circuit's decision, which is 
binding on the United States District Court for the Northern

Kaufman and Broad-South Bay, Inc.'s claim for equitable 
restitution of its response costs cannot stand.

reversed in this case, Kaufman and Broad-South Bay, Inc. will lose 
an important means of recovering from the responsible parties the 
millions of dollars it has spent to remedy dangerous environmental 
contamination that it did not create.

A. The Broad Grant of Authority in RCRA Section 
7002(a)(1)(B) Allows a Court to Order a Party to Make 


Equitable Restitution. 


Section 7002(a) of RCRA, 42 U.S.C. section 6972(a), 
expressly confers on the district courts the power to order persons 
who have caused or contributed to contamination “to take such 
other action as may be necessary." This broad equitable 
jurisdiction includes the authority to grant restitution and injunctive 
relief to private parties faced with contamination that may present 
an endangerment to the environment or human health.’ Unless 
specifically limited, Congress’ grant of equitable jurisdiction to the 
courts is not restricted but is presumed to include all of the 
traditional equitable powers that the courts have historically 
exercised. As this Court has stated:

Nothing in section 7002(a)'s broad grant of jurisdiction, by 
words or inference, limits the district courts’ jurisdiction to certain 
kinds of equitable relief — for example, as Petitioners suggest, to 
orders compelling “contamination cleanup” or to actions “required 
to stabilize and/or eliminate the imminent and substantial

had intended such a limitation, it would have been a simple matter

to abatement, it expressly says so. Cf. Petition, at 18. In enacting 
the RCRA citizen suit provision, Congress simply did not impose 
the limitation that Petitioners now seek to engraft onto the statute. 
Instead, it conferred on district courts the full power to grant any 
appropriate equitable relief in light of the necessities of the case.

B. Analysis of RCRA Section 7002(a) in Relation to RCRA 
Section 7003 Confirms That Private Parties Are Entitled to 
Restitution of Their Response Costs. 


In concluding that RCRA section 7002(a) authorizes 
restitution orders in cases brought by private parties, the Ninth 
Circuit relied on cases interpreting RCRA section 7003, codified 
at 42 U.S.C. section 6973, which authorizes suits by the 
Administrator of the United States Environmental Protection 
Agency (EPA). The Ninth Circuit's analysis in this regard is so 
compelling that Petitioners do not even attempt to rebut it. 


As the Ninth Circuit pv .ited out, the language permitting 
citizen suits in RCRA section 7002 is “substantially identical” or 
“virtually identical” to the language in RCRA section 7003 
authorizing the EPA to bring suit. KFC Western, Inc, v. Meghrig, 
49 F.3d 518, 522-23 (9th Cir. 1995). In particular, both sections 
expressly permit the district courts to order responsible persons “to 
take such other action as may be necessary." 42 U.S.C. 
sections 6972 and 6973. The congruence of statutory language is 
no accident: in amending the citizen suit provision in 1984 to 
authorize district courts “to take such other action as may be 
necessary,” Congress intended that the new version of section 7002 


confer upon private parties the right to bring actions “pursuant to 
the standards of liability established under section 7003." H.R. 
Rep. No. 198, 98th Cong., 2d Sess., Pt. 1, 53 (1983), reprinted in 
1984 U.S.C.C.A.N. 5576, 5612. The Senate report contains 


similar language: 


These amendments are intended to allow citizens 
exactly the same broad substantive and procedural 
claim for relief which is already available to the 
United State [sic] under section 7003 [citation 
omitted]. Any differences in language between 
these amendments and 7003 are not intended to 
reflect a difference in the claims, but merely 
clarify that citizens will have the same claim 
presently available to the United States. 


S. Rep. No. 284, 98th Cong., Ist Sess., at 56-57 (1983); cf. 1984 
U.S.C.C.A.N. 5576, 5612.’ Accordingly, as the Ninth Circuit 
recognized, there is “no principled reason to distinguish between 
the relief available to the Administrator and that available to private 
plaintiffs under the virtually identical provisions.” KFC Western, 
Inc. v. Meghrig, 49 F.3d 518, 523 (9th Cir. 1995). See Middlesex 
County Bd. of Chosen Freeholders v. N.J., 645 F. Supp. 715, 721 
(D.N.J. 1986). Petitioners do not and cannot argue to the 
contrary. Indeed, even the dissenting opinion below recognized 
that section 7002(a) and section 7003 should be interpreted 
consistently. Jd. at 524 n.1 (Brunetti, J., dissenting). 


As the Ninth Circuit recognized, both the Third and Eighth 
Circuits have held that the EPA is entitled to restitution of its 
response costs when it brings an action under section 7003. United

Act (CWA), formerly known as the Federal Water Pollution

First, unlike the CWA/FWPCA provision, the RCRA 
citizen suit provision expressly allows suits concerning past 
conduct. . Indeed, Gwaltney itself specifically cited section 
7002(a)(1)(b) of RCRA for the proposition that “Congress has 
demonstrated in yet other statutory provisions that it knows how to 
avoid this prospective implication by using language that explicitly 
targets wholly past violations.” Gwaltney v. Chesapeake Bay

action as may be necessary.” Accordingly, Gwaltney, like Sea

that language. Contrary to Petitioners’ assertions, the statutory 
language actually used in RCRA does matter, and the significant 
differences between this language and the provisions of other 
environmental statutes precludes an attempt to fabricate an 
overarching “history” or “purpose” of citizen suits (Petition at 13- 
14, 28) that overrides the express statutory text.

the “imminent and substantial endangerment” language limits the

such an action should be filed. KFC Western, Inc. v. Meghrig, 49 
F.3d 518, 521 (9th Cir. 1995); United States v. Aceto Agric.

addressed before suit is filed. Because there is no dispute here that

do nothing, or to litigate rather than remediate, will have that 
remedy preserved. See Bayless Investment & Trading Co. v. 
Chevron U.S.A., 39 Env't Rep. Cas. (BNA) 1428, 1432 (D. Ariz. 
1994). In short, Petitioners "would foreclose a RCRA remedy for 
the innocent buyers who clean up contaminated property.” KFC 
Western, Inc, v. Meghrig, 49 F.3d 518, 524 (9th Cir. 1995). 
They would also eliminate vital incentives for parties to engage in 
cleanup activity. Recognizing a cause of action for restitution 
following cleanup is the only way of implementing Congress's 
intent “to confer upon the courts the authority to grant affirmative 
equitable relief to the extent necessary to eliminate any risks poses 
by toxic wastes.” United States v. Price, 688 F.2d 204, 214 (3d 
Cir. 1982). 


15 
CONCLUSION 


For all these reasons, the petition for a writ of certiorari to 
review the judgment and opinion of the United States Court of 
Appeals for the Ninth Circuit in this case should be denied.
