### ERISA equitable categories and nonfiduciary compensation

**Governing question:** What instructions govern ERISA equitable categories and nonfiduciary compensation?

**Current rule:** Section 502(a)(3)'s appropriate equitable relief means traditionally equitable categories, not compensatory damages merely because equity could award them in trust litigation. Compensation from an admitted nonfiduciary for plan losses is unavailable under that provision. The rule does not prohibit every equitable monetary remedy or decide the expressly disclaimed antecedent cause-of-action question; restitution is not reopened.

Hewitt Associates — The reviewed compensatory award is not appropriate equitable relief](https://github.com/Alex-Oss222/scotus-career/blob/main/terms/OT1992/records/mertens_merits_1993-06-01.md), 1993-06-01. Scalia’s Opinion of the Court; Blackmun, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas (5 Justices). Direct majority support for this exact proposition; no Marks synthesis.

**Present operation effective:** 1993-06-01; Mertens v. Hewitt Associates, for the components identified above.

### ERISA guaranteed-benefit exclusions and insurer fiduciary functions

**Governing question:** Which insurer assets are excluded from plan-asset status by ERISA §401(b)(2).

**Current rule:** A guaranteed-benefit policy itself is a plan asset; the insurer’s underlying assets are not deemed plan assets solely because it issued the policy only to the extent the policy genuinely guarantees the amount of benefits. A nonguaranteed component does not gain the exclusion from general-account placement. Plan-asset status subjects the insurer to §3(21)(A) fiduciary duties to the extent it exercises the specified authority or control over management or disposition; breach, causation, loss and remedy remain separate. McCarran-Ferguson does not displace ERISA’s express insurance-related duties. Compatible state insurance regulation survives the saving clause, but a concrete conflicting obligation cannot control over ERISA itself. An agency interpretation cannot erase the exclusion’s component limitation.

### Corporate procedures for ERISA plan amendments

**Governing question:** Sufficiency and implementation of a welfare plan’s reservation of amendment power to a corporation.

**Current rule:** A written reservation of amendment to the Company can satisfy ERISA §402(b)(3)’s procedure and authorized-person requirements because §3(9) includes corporations and applicable corporate law identifies who can bind them. A list of named officers is not invariably required. Any additional procedure the plan actually adopts remains binding. Sufficiency of the reservation does not prove that an authorized actor adopted an amendment: actual express or implied authority, approval and any legally effective ratification, with its timing and consequences, require separate determination.

**Authority by component:**

- The company-amendment clause satisfies §402(b)(3): [The company-amendment clause satisfies §402(b)(3)](https://github.com/Alex-Oss222/scotus-career/blob/main/terms/OT1994/records/Curtiss_Wright_Corp_v_Schoonejongen_merits_1995-03-06.md#public-projection), 1995-03-06. Souter’s unanimous opinion, joined by Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O’Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Thomas, Ginsburg, and Breyer; all nine adopt the statutory construction.
- A sufficient procedure still requires authorized corporate action: [A sufficient procedure still requires authorized corporate action](https://github.com/Alex-Oss222/scotus-career/blob/main/terms/OT1994/records/Curtiss_Wright_Corp_v_Schoonejongen_merits_1995-03-06.md#public-projection), 1995-03-06. Souter’s unanimous opinion, joined by Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O’Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Thomas, Ginsburg, and Breyer; all nine adopt this required remand and its limits.

**Present operation effective:** 1995-03-06; the component authorities identified above.

**Exceptions and limits:** A summary plan description is not proof of authorized amendment. Sponsor amendment power remains distinct from fiduciary duties, written-instrument and disclosure requirements. No universal procedural-violation remedy, vesting rule or amendment immunity follows.
