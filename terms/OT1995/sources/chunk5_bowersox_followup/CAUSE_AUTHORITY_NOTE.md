## Exact controlling propositions

1. **Abuse is not confined to intentional abandonment.** At 489–490 the Court explains that a petitioner may abuse the writ through inexcusable neglect, whether or not the earlier omission was deliberate. At 493 it adopts cause and prejudice to determine abuse through inexcusable neglect. A showing of good faith alone is not the governing excuse.

2. **Cause requires an objective external impediment and actual causal effect.** At 493–494 the Court states that the petitioner must show “some objective factor external to the defense impeded counsel's efforts.” Examples include official interference making compliance impracticable and a factual or legal basis not reasonably available to counsel. Constitutionally ineffective assistance can constitute cause, but ordinary attorney error does not; at 495 the Court expressly disclaims any constitutional right to counsel in federal habeas.

   The application at 497–498 is more precise: “For cause to exist, the external impediment, whether it be government interference or the reasonable unavailability of the factual basis for the claim, must have prevented petitioner from raising the claim.” At 498: “Omission of the claim will not be excused merely because evidence discovered later might also have supported or strengthened the claim.” The existence of difficult external circumstances and a prisoner's subjective response are not automatically equivalent to the legally required impediment.

3. **The State pleads first; the prisoner then bears the responsive burden.** At 494: “The government satisfies this burden if, with clarity and particularity, it notes petitioner's prior writ history, identifies the claims that appear for the first time, and alleges that petitioner has abused the writ.” Only then does the burden to disprove abuse become the petitioner's. This requirement is not established or defeated merely by the research collection's lack of the State's paper.

4. **Cause and actual prejudice are separate requirements.** At 494, after cause is established, the prisoner must show “‘actual prejudice’ resulting from the errors of which he complains.” At 502: “As McCleskey lacks cause for failing to raise the Massiah claim in the first federal petition, we need not consider whether he would be prejudiced by his inability to raise the alleged Massiah violation at this late date.” Thus an independently sufficient no-cause determination can avoid resolving the underlying constitutional prejudice question. Conversely, an arguable error about cause does not itself establish prejudice or negate another adequate ground for denial.

5. **No automatic evidentiary hearing follows.** At 494: “The petitioner's opportunity to meet the burden of cause and prejudice will not include an evidentiary hearing if the district court determines as a matter of law that petitioner cannot satisfy the standard.” A properly supported need to resolve a material factual issue remains different from legal insufficiency even assuming the asserted facts.

6. **The miscarriage exception remains distinct.** At 494–495 and 502 the Court retains a narrow miscarriage-of-justice exception despite lack of cause. For the current event, the later effective simulated *Schlup* and *Sawyer* rules supply their distinct crime-innocence and eligibility-innocence thresholds. Neither the exception nor its failure is inferred from a missing source. The Court's particular innocence determination in *McCleskey* is no fact about Williams.

7. **Both statutory conditions and other barriers remain material.** At 486–487 the Court quotes then-effective §2244(b) completely, including the prior merits-hearing alternatives and both “unless” clauses. When both exception conditions are satisfied and other barriers, including nonexhaustion and procedural default, are absent, the Court says the petition must be considered. That conditional duty is not a categorical prohibition on discretionary consideration whenever one condition is unmet; the following paragraph expressly addresses the statute's silence concerning the limits of discretion. At 487 it quotes Rule 9(b), including the prior-merits-determination condition for repeated grounds. These passages corroborate the current public entering-law qualifications; no modern AEDPA provision is substituted.

## Earlier appellate opportunity: what this case establishes

At 498 the Court requires a reasonable and diligent investigation aimed at including relevant grounds in the first federal petition. At 500–501 it emphasizes prompt pursuit through available discovery and hearing procedures. It does **not** decide that a prisoner whose alleged impediment ceases only after district judgment invariably must seek remand during the earlier appeal, amend the judgment, or petition for rehearing before presenting a later claim. No automatic appellate-remand-request rule may be attributed to this source.

The reported 1990 opportunity remains relevant to Williams's diligence and continued omission. But the known sequence matters: the second district denial occurred in 1988; the reported permission change followed transfer in 1989; the en banc proceeding occurred in 1990. Failure to request remand is not by itself a finding that the claim could freely have been added at that stage, or a complete legal answer to cause for the earlier omission. Conversely, this chronology does not establish a right to omit the available matter throughout the continuing earlier proceeding. The legal significance of that opportunity must be reasoned, not presumed.

## Effect on the proposed penalty-IAC procedural path

The new reported facts identify an actual cause controversy, but **the disagreement alone does not establish a substantial appellate ground** under the *Barefoot–Stokes* rule. The report supplies evidence of withholding permission until transfer, opposing conduct and representations, and a district no-causation finding. It does not reproduce the experts' actual opinions or identify incapacity, coercion preventing presentation, an incorrect legal test, or a procedural failure in adjudicating cause. Those matters must not be invented. The existence of testimony or a certificate does not itself show error under the objective-impediment rule.

A legally material, supported appellate procedural error can in principle supply a ground upon which relief might be granted, including a remand rather than an immediate writ. But the error must be identifiable and correcting it must be capable of affecting the result. Here the specific penalty-phase attorney omission and resulting prejudice remain unknown; the reported Hays classifications are unallocated and do not establish whether an independent merits ground applies to that claim. A finding that the cause question alone supports denying vacatur would therefore need a supported explanation both of its substantiality under *McCleskey* and of its possible remedial significance. It cannot presume incapacity, actual prejudice, a hearing entitlement, or absence of another sufficient ground.

This validates a narrower conclusion than the validator's preliminary suggestion: **the reported cause dispute is an analytical candidate, not by itself a certified sufficient terminal-denial ground.** The earlier suggestion that its mere identification removed every material obstacle to that particular path is withdrawn. The absence of an original paper is not the obstacle; the missing propositions are the legally significant impediment/error and the possible consequence of correcting it. This note does not decide whether an independently supported first-degree, second-degree, amendment, or other authorized path permits resolution without those propositions.

For a threshold no-cause route, *McCleskey* confirms that underlying IAC prejudice need not be decided if lack of cause is independently sufficient and any material exceptional gateway is addressed. It does not turn an unavailable expert report into a failure of proof or make a district finding immune from review. Nor does it alone establish that every other ground before the court fails.

## Separate-writing limit

Marshall's dissent disputes the governing standard, its retroactive application, the treatment of the record and refusal to remand. Those arguments are date-eligible separate reasoning only. The controlling objective cause rule is not replaced by the dissent's good-faith account of *Sanders*, and the dissent's advocated remand supplies no automatic remand or stay entitlement for Williams.

## Previously adjudicated same grounds: precise limits of this authority

*McCleskey*'s actual dispute concerns a new federal ground omitted from the first federal petition. Its treatment of same-ground successiveness must not be enlarged into an unannounced categorical rule.

At 487, after discussing §2244(b)'s conditional duty to consider a new, nonabusive ground, the Court states:

> It does not state whether a district court may overlook a deliberately withheld or otherwise abusive claim to entertain the petition in any event. That is, it does not state the limits on the district court's discretion to entertain abusive petitions.

The Court immediately explains that Congress did not foreclose the “court-announced principles defining and limiting a district court's discretion,” citing *Delo*'s abuse ruling about a newly raised fourth-petition claim. The statute's discretionary language therefore supplies neither unrestricted permission nor a self-executing mandatory-dismissal rule.

At 485 the Court recounts that *Sanders* established that courts must reach the merits of an abusive petition if “the ends of justice demand.” At 495 it states:

> In addition, the exception to cause for fundamental miscarriages of justice gives meaningful content to the otherwise unexplained “ends of justice” inquiry mandated by Sanders.

That discussion then expressly attributes to **a plurality** in *Kuhlmann*, 477 U.S. at 454, the formulation requiring consideration of successive petitions supplemented by a “colorable showing of factual innocence.” It cannot be silently promoted into a majority holding of *Kuhlmann*. Nor does *McCleskey*'s application of cause and prejudice to a newly omitted ground independently decide every question concerning discretionary reconsideration of the same ground previously adjudicated on its merits.

Consequently, this complete reading supplies no holding that an old constitutional-merits debate can **never** support a successive stay without newly proffered innocence evidence. It also supplies no holding that the existence of a prior dissent or an earlier favorable panel disposition **itself** authorizes reopening. An affirmative same-ground reconsideration path must identify the governing *Sanders/Kuhlmann* rule and demonstrate why the established circumstances satisfy it, while respecting any subsequent effective simulated law. Existing trial evidence and the earlier dissent remain existing evidence and prior disagreement, not new evidence or a newly passed innocence gateway. This source note does not independently certify that distinct path.
