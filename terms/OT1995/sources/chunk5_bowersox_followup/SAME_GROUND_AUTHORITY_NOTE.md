## What Sanders actually establishes

At 15 the Court treats §2255 and federal habeas alike for this purpose, then separates **same grounds previously heard and determined** from **abuse by omission or withholding**. Giving controlling weight to a prior denial requires the same ground, prior merits determination, and a conclusion that redetermination would not serve the ends of justice.

At 16 a “ground” is “a sufficient legal basis for granting the relief sought”; different factual allegations, legal arguments or phrasing need not create a different ground. Prior merits consideration requires that raised factual issues were resolved through a hearing or conclusively by the files and records. Doubt concerning whether grounds are identical is resolved in the applicant's favor. These are the stated *Sanders* formulations, not new Williams findings.

At 16–17 the applicant may show that redetermination serves the ends of justice. A prior evidentiary hearing that was not full and fair is one example. For purely legal issues, an intervening change of law or another justification for not previously raising a crucial point or argument may suffice. The Court adds:

> First, the foregoing enumeration is not intended to be exhaustive; the test is “the ends of justice” and it cannot be too finely particularized. Second, the burden is on the applicant to show that, although the ground of the new application was determined against him on the merits on a prior application, the ends of justice would be served by a redetermination of the ground.

At 18–19 the Court expressly places the principles within sound judicial discretion and states that the federal judge has “the power—and, if the ends of justice demand, the duty—to reach the merits.” The distinction between **permission** and **required reconsideration** must be retained. At 15 it separately permits summary denial when the application, files and records conclusively show lack of merit. Its actual remand involved a prior pleading dismissal that was not a merits adjudication, not simply a desire to retry a fully decided legal issue.

## Effect of later law supplied for this event

*Sanders* construed the 1948 statutes. Its quotation of old §2244, including express “ends of justice” language, is not the operative 1996 text. The 1966 §2244(b), quoted in *McCleskey* and current simulated *Schlup*, uses “need not be entertained” and two “unless” conditions. It does not turn repeated presentation into a jurisdictional prohibition or a command to dismiss every repeated ground. *McCleskey*, 486–487, leaves the limits of discretion to governing judicial principles; its actual holding subjects abusive new grounds to objective cause and prejudice, with the distinct miscarriage exception. Consequently, *Sanders*'s older good-faith treatment cannot be used to evade *McCleskey* on an omitted new claim.

The *Kuhlmann* factual-innocence discussion identified in the authorized sources is a plurality. Neither that status nor *McCleskey*'s discussion at 495 supports silently announcing an exclusive majority rule that every same-ground reconsideration requires new innocence evidence. Conversely, the plurality cannot simply be ignored as reasoning relevant to discretionary finality.

Current simulated *Schlup* controls when crime innocence is invoked as the gateway; it requires reliable new evidence and its demanding probability showing. It preserves independent barriers and holds that §2244(b)'s qualified refusal permission does not itself sustain a refusal premised on the legally wrong gateway standard. It does not establish automatic review of every repeated ground or allow the same trial evidence to be relabeled new innocence evidence. *Sawyer* remains distinct. *Barefoot–Stokes* still require substantial grounds upon which relief might be granted to support the successive-petition stay.

## Bounded first-degree application

The reported earlier first-degree instruction presentation and merits consideration support the **same-ground** inquiry. A vacated favorable panel result, an en banc dissent, disputed kidnapping evidence and disagreement concerning an accomplice's credibility are not themselves newly discovered evidence, an intervening change in law or a newly satisfied innocence gateway. Their existence alone neither requires reopening nor proves that the earlier hearing was not full and fair.

Nevertheless, the supplied law supports a genuine **discretionary reconsideration argument**, distinct from an automatic new-ground or innocence exception. That argument must explain why the concrete constitutional and trial-record issue is substantial and why redetermination is a sound use of the remaining discretion despite prior full consideration and finality. An identified sufficiently serious prior legal error or other supported ends-of-justice justification must do the work; the number of earlier dissenters cannot substitute for it. Whether the known instruction issue meets that standard remains an evaluative question for neutral modeling, not a fact established by this note.

Accordingly, neither categorical statement is justified by this source review: “the claim was previously adjudicated, therefore relief is legally impossible,” or “the earlier judges disagreed, therefore the stay must stand.” A supported exercise of reconsideration discretion can potentially make relief available without inventing new evidence.
