## Instruction rule and its application limits

*Hopper*, 610–612, states that *Beck* requires a lesser-included-offense instruction when the evidence warrants it, and **only** when the evidence warrants it. The Court quotes the rational-jury rule: the evidence must permit conviction of the lesser offense and acquittal of the greater. The particular Alabama rule allowing an instruction on any reasonable theory supported by the evidence satisfied federal standards. The Court rejected an automatic new-trial rule from the mere existence of an invalid preclusion clause. At 612–614 its result rested on the defendant's own repeated admissions negating the asserted lack of intent. The footnote recognizes that a different defendant might plausibly identify different tactics or evidence but for a preclusion clause; that possibility was contradicted by the actual admissions there. Brennan and Marshall joined reversal of the conviction relief but opposed reinstating death on their separate categorical punishment ground.

*Beck* was not separately reopened. The operative interpretation here is supplied by the complete *Hopper* Court opinion, not an incomplete reading of *Beck*. *Hopper* neither decides Missouri's offense elements nor establishes that Williams admitted the intent that Evans admitted.

The prior Williams report supplies two genuine arguments under this rule. The State can rely on the prior finding that the abduction merely implemented a planned murder and did not constitute the independent felony required under the court's reading of Missouri law. Williams can point to the reported transport and confinement, efforts to obtain seclusion, and impeached accomplice account as a basis for a rational alternative finding, while challenging the distinction between an incidental kidnapping and incidental movement. These remain arguments about reported evidence; no witness's credibility is resolved by this note. Both trial parties requested the first-degree instruction. The independent successive-ground inquiry remains necessary even if the merits question is substantial. The second-degree claim retains its separate offense and presentation questions.

## Proportionality and a state-created liberty interest are different questions

*Pulley*, 41–42, rejects habeas relief based solely on a perceived state-law error. It assumes for discussion that a sufficiently egregious state-law error could violate equal protection or due process, but finds no established departure from state precedent there. A possible later evolution of state law was for the state courts, not a basis for the federal writ on the showing in that case.

At 44–51 *Pulley* rejects an invariable federal constitutional requirement of comparative proportionality review in every capital case. At 51–54 it sustains the particular California system in light of its other safeguards, while assuming that a system sufficiently lacking other checks might require a different assessment. It does not announce that every conceivable state review defect is immune from federal scrutiny. Stevens joined the result and all but Part III; his separate writing emphasized meaningful appellate review while agreeing that comparative review is not invariably necessary. Brennan's dissent, joined by Marshall, advocated mandatory comparative review; that position is not the Court's rule. No later simulated Harris action is supplied or imported by this earlier primary source.

*Hicks*, 345–347, protects the specific state-created liberty interest in the sentencing jury's exercise of its statutory discretion against arbitrary deprivation. Oklahoma had concededly denied that discretion under an invalid mandatory provision, and the reviewing court merely conjectured that the same sentence might result. It did not itself resentence. The majority did not decide the procedure required for appellate resentencing. Rehnquist's dissent disputed whether the asserted deprivation and underlying invalidity were established; it supplies no controlling replacement for the majority's rule.

The proposed Williams amendment is reported as a **due-process** claim concerning approximately two hundred absent capital-case files. It should not be recast as only the universal Eighth Amendment entitlement rejected in *Pulley*. The strongest State response is that incomplete comparative files do not themselves establish arbitrary deprivation of a protected liberty interest or a federally invalid sentence. The strongest contrary response is that an actual state-created sentencing safeguard cannot be arbitrarily withheld merely because the Federal Constitution did not require the State to create that particular safeguard. *Hicks* supplies that distinction, not a finding that Missouri withheld the protected opportunity here.

On this source base, the exact Missouri statutory entitlement and the alleged omissions' effect remain unestablished. Missing case files are Williams's allegation, not an admitted defect. This note does not identify comparators, decide materiality, construe an unquoted Missouri statute, or assume that every file omission is either harmless or a due-process violation. A particular Justice's reliance on a more specific state-law duty, material comparator or actual deprivation needs support for that proposition. Conversely, an asserted legal insufficiency that persists even taking the reported allegation as true can be evaluated without finding the allegation false.

The reported late amendment stage supports a separate discretionary argument, but neither *Pulley* nor *Hicks* supplies the district court's actual reason for denying leave or a categorical amendment deadline. This review did not retrieve a separate amendment-law source; a disposition materially adopting such a new procedural rule needs its own verified authority. No unsupported amendment rule is supplied to complete a vacatur rationale.
