## Questions, posture, and preserved challenge

The petitioner's questions concern (1) whether Rule 11(f)'s factual-basis inquiry reaches criminal forfeiture charged in the indictment/information and included in a plea; and (2) whether a guilty plea waives the Rule 31(e) special verdict and any Sixth Amendment forfeiture-jury right without specific advice and an express waiver. Petitioner's Br. i; JA313–329. The Tenth Circuit's October 19, 1994 opinion, **38 F.3d 523**, affirmed the forfeiture order. Its holdings and statements are evidence of the judgment under review, not entering Supreme Court law.

An eleven-count superseding indictment included CCE, drug, firearms, and money-laundering charges. After a week of trial Libretti entered the October 5, 1992 agreement (filed October 6), pleading guilty to CCE under §848. The government agreed to recommend the twenty-year minimum and dismiss the other charges. The bargain removed additional potential imprisonment, including a disputed firearms charge carrying a thirty-year consecutive term. These are bargain terms and exposure, not convictions on the dismissed counts. JA79–82, 313–316; both briefs' statements.

The sentence included twenty years' imprisonment, five years' supervised release, a $5,000 fine, $50 assessment, 500 hours of community service, and forfeiture. At the conclusion of sentencing Libretti personally objected that there was no factual basis for the **whole forfeiture**; the judge noted the objection and referred to sufficient trial evidence. JA154, 316. This is a recorded objection, not silent acquiescence. His challenge identified accounts containing legitimate wages, an employee savings plan, and IRA assets. JA319. Rule 32 presentence-report objections were addressed separately by the circuit in footnote 5; it focused on Rule 11 because the district court had not based the forfeiture order on the alleged presentence inaccuracies.

The December 23, 1992 forfeiture order was appealed. The Tenth Circuit treated forfeiture as a final part of the sentence: post-appeal proceedings could continue for §853(n) third-party claims, but the notice of appeal removed district-court jurisdiction over the defendant's own challenge. His later modification request did not qualify as a timely, narrow Rule 35(c) correction. The court rejected government's mootness motion and found a limited-remand request moot. JA317–319, 329. Do not conflate ongoing third-party proceedings with absence of appellate jurisdiction.

The Eighth Amendment excessive-fine argument was rejected below, JA328–329. The government's merits brief p.13 n.6 identifies that issue as **not pressed here**. Neither this addendum nor the neutral packet should silently transform the Rule 11/jury case into a fresh excessive-fines adjudication.

## Exact agreement and colloquy limits

Agreement ¶2 describes forfeiture of all known assets **as prescribed in §853**, plus later-discovered assets up to $1.5 million. Paragraph 3 contains a general jury-trial waiver. Paragraph 9 requires identification of facilitating assets and financial disclosure. Paragraph 10 transfers **his right, title, and interest** in all his assets to Wyoming's Division of Criminal Investigation, expressly listing real estate, personal property and guns/computer, property in federal possession, accounts, investments, retirement accounts, cash, and checks/funds. Paragraph 11 says the court is not bound. JA79–82. The agreement is broad, but its words do not themselves resolve statutory authority over every asset or third-party ownership.

During the full colloquy the judge advised about trial, jury, guilt, and the trial ending upon the plea. The judge described the indictment's forfeiture property. Libretti asked whether future lawful salary or inheritance during imprisonment could be taken; judge and counsel distinguished future legitimate property from concealed proceeds of the drug enterprise. The judge used drug-transaction limitations in describing forfeiture (JA88–89, 100), while Libretti acknowledged reading and understanding the agreement. He disputed several offense details before entering the CCE plea; the judge ultimately found a voluntary plea and adequate factual basis for guilt. JA83–121. The **complete colloquy contains no specific advice about the separate Rule 31(e) forfeiture verdict**; this bounded observation does not assert what counsel privately explained.

The circuit characterized the written agreement as unambiguous and treated narrower colloquy statements as extraneous to that agreement. It found a knowing and voluntary waiver without specific Rule 31(e) advice, and expressly avoided deciding whether the Sixth Amendment independently protected a forfeiture jury. JA325–328. The petitioner disputes that approach: forfeiture is a significant direct consequence, a separate statutory jury issue may survive a guilty plea to guilt, and general jury advice about guilt does not necessarily convey the forfeiture jury right. Petitioner's Br.10–14. The government contends voluntary stipulation resolves the contested property issues and general advice plus the agreement suffices, alternatively even if constitutional jury protection exists. Government Br.34–50. Those are live opposing paths, not resolved facts.

## Property: keep distinct statutory branches and owners

The December order's list covers a Wyoming lot, two jointly held Colorado condominium interests, vehicles, cash, a diamond ring, mobile home, firearms, an IRA, bank accounts (including two childhood/parent-joint accounts), municipal bonds, GNMA instruments, and cashier's/traveler's checks. It also specifically directs reissue of an **$8,416.23 General Chemical 401(k) distribution check** as **substitute assets for assets dissipated or otherwise expended by Libretti**. JA155–164, item NN. Some remaining firearm-related property is treated as abandoned separately. This is not a finding that every item is an original drug proceed, and the item-NN description is not a blanket statutory finding for the entire list.

The appellate caveat in footnote 8 limits the plea to property over which Libretti had power of disposition; it gives inalienable pension/profit-sharing or valid spendthrift-trust interests as examples outside that power, like third-party property. JA326. **That legal caveat is not a factual finding that the listed IRA or distribution check actually enjoyed such inalienability.** Nor does describing a source as employment income settle §853(p).

Post-order proceedings involved third-party claims. As described by both briefs and the April 2 order, the court ordered return of a diamond ring to the recipient and childhood-account passbooks/funds to the parents; further factual proceedings concerned the lot, disputed funds, and the defendant's assertions. The April 2 order directed a magistrate hearing allowing Libretti to show by a preponderance that his property was not forfeitable, required maintenance of the status quo, and addressed other claimant-specific requests. JA298–312 (conclusion directly read at310–312). Its defendant-specific reconsideration was later stayed and was jurisdictionally ineffective under the circuit's finality ruling. Do not adjudicate the third parties' claims in the defendant's Rule 11 appeal or treat the entire post-order proceeding as a completed factual exoneration of all property.

The government's assertion of more than $1 million in drug proceeds versus roughly $410,000 in assets is a **litigation argument supporting substitute forfeiture**, not this researcher's finding that the §853(p) predicates were proved for every item. Government Br. factual statement and argument; JA and petitioner's discussion contest application. A broad 'the plea permits forfeiture of any property without statutory nexus or substitute conditions' proposition lacks source support.

## Statute and rules that must be kept separate

Section 853(a) applies upon qualifying conviction and requires forfeiture of (1) property constituting or derived from proceeds obtained directly or indirectly as a result of the violation; (2) the person's property used or intended to be used in any manner or part to commit or facilitate it; and, for CCE, (3) interests, claims, or property/contractual rights affording control over the enterprise. The court orders described property forfeited when imposing sentence. The government's sentence-characterization argument rests on this language and §848(a); the petitioner's rule-specific argument does not require that forfeiture be an offense element for every possible legal purpose.

Section 853(p) is separate. A qualifying §853(a) asset must, **as a result of an act or omission of the defendant**, (1) be unlocatable despite due diligence, (2) have been transferred/sold/deposited with a third party, (3) have been placed beyond the court's jurisdiction, (4) have substantially diminished in value, or (5) have been commingled with other property not divisible without difficulty. Then the court orders forfeiture of other property **of the defendant, only up to the value of that unavailable qualifying property**. Lawfully sourced property is not categorically immune to this branch, and agreement alone must not be restated as a freestanding statutory forfeiture power.

Section 853(d)'s rebuttable trial presumption requires government proof by a preponderance of both acquisition during or reasonably after the offense and no likely source other than the offense. It is not automatic from a broad guilty plea. Petitioner's Br.20 n.12 expressly leaves the general trial burden issue open while pressing Rule 11's factual inquiry.

Section 853(n) allows **persons other than the defendant** to petition after notice within the stated thirty-day period. Court, without jury, determines whether a preponderance establishes a preexisting superior/vested legal interest at the offense or bona fide purchase for value without reasonable cause to believe the property forfeitable; the order must be amended accordingly. This statutory third-party remedy is separate from the defendant's Rule 31(e) claim and does not supply that defendant a postjudgment (n) hearing. Subsection (k)'s bar on third-party intervention is subject to (n).

Rule 11(f) tells the court, despite acceptance of a guilty plea, not to enter judgment without an inquiry satisfying it of a factual basis for the plea. Rule 7(c)(2) bars forfeiture judgment unless indictment/information alleges the extent of the interest/property subject to forfeiture. Rule 31(e) requires a special verdict on the extent of forfeitable interest/property, if any, when indictment/information alleges criminal forfeiture. The then-sentencing Rule 32(b)(2), renumbered Rule32(d)(2) in 1994, addresses forfeiture judgment/seizure. Do not retroject later Rule32.2. Government appendix; Petitioner's Br.15–21 with historical Advisory Committee Notes. Rule23(a)'s written waiver/consents are argued by petitioner to govern the forfeiture jury; government disputes applicability to this guilty-plea disposition.

## Competing doctrinal authorities; reading sufficiency and exact limits

The circuit split is concrete: Roberts (Seventh) and Reckmeyer (Fourth) require an independent Rule11 factual inquiry for stipulated forfeiture; Bachynsky (Fifth) examines the overall record without requiring all basis at the plea hearing; Boatner (Eleventh), adopted below, treats the forfeiture sentence agreement as outside Rule11(f). JA319–325. The parties dispute how the 1972 forfeiture rules and Advisory Notes interact with the 1966 Rule11 inquiry, McCarthy/Alford, and the court's sentencing authority. The government's brief discusses the judge's discretion to inquire when a stipulation appears unreliable, and §6B1.4(d); 'Rule11(f) does not apply' and 'a judge must accept every stipulated sentence' are distinct propositions.

On the jury branch, petitioner invokes common-law forfeiture practice and Rule31(e)'s constitutional foundation; government invokes sentencing decisions including McMillan, Cabana, and Spaziano and disputes the historical analogy. Government distinguishes ordinary procedural waivers from the limited rights requiring personal, informed waiver and discusses Broce, Olano, Boykin, Brady, and Santobello. These source descriptions identify live paths, not recommended votes or holdings. The entering-law slice's Gaudin, Godinez, and Mezzanatto remain operative simulated constraints. Austin/Alexander concern punishment/Eighth Amendment characterization; their label alone cannot decide either present procedural question.

**Remaining source limits:** no historical Supreme Court merits writing has been opened for this matter. The digest supports framing the two granted procedural issues with the actual plea and property qualifications. It does not support an asset-by-asset finding that all §853(a)/(p) predicates were proved, an assertion that counsel never advised Libretti privately, or a conclusion about unread trial testimony. If a contemplated disposition turns on such a factual finding rather than the procedural rule and existing judgment, read the remaining JA and petitioner's full argument/reply before deciding; there is a located primary record, not a demonstrated missing-record blocker. Exact retirement-plan restrictions, tracing, values, and substitute predicates require that further evidence. No absence claim is drawn from unread pages.
