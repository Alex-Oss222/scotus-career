### Kyles v. Whitley, No. 93-7927

Merits decision, April 19, 1995

#### Chronology and Posture

Argued November 7, 1994; decided April 19, 1995. On writ of certiorari to the United States Court of Appeals for the Fifth Circuit, reviewing 5 F.3d 806 (October 14, 1993), which affirmed denial of federal habeas relief. The question is whether the established suppressed favorable evidence, viewed collectively, is material to Kyles’s conviction under Brady and Bagley.

Dolores Dye was killed and her automobile taken on September 20, 1984. Kyles’s first trial ended in a deadlock; the second produced a conviction and death sentence. No reason for the deadlock beyond disagreement is established. The prosecution used eyewitness identifications, the murder weapon found behind a stove at the apartment where Kyles stayed, ammunition and a holster, the victim’s purse and papers found in garbage, and pet food of brands she had bought. A print linked Kyles to a receipt in the car, but processing destroyed its readable date. The defense presented a theory that Joseph “Beanie” Wallace was implicated and framed Kyles. Adverse credibility findings and evidence supporting guilt remain part of the record.

The established suppression concerns six contemporaneous eyewitness statements, Beanie’s recorded September 22 and written September 23 accounts, prosecutor Strider’s interview notes, a garbage-retrieval memorandum, and the parking-lot plate list. Smallwood’s earlier sequence differed from his trial observation account; Williams’s early description differed from Kyles’s height and build. Beanie’s accounts bear on corroboration, the police investigation, his possible access, and the provenance of physical evidence. Some lawful uses arise through police examination and preparation without calling Beanie or admitting every statement for its truth. The seventeen-plate list omitted Kyles’s plate, but its completeness is disputed. The Court finds neither Beanie’s guilt nor planting, witness perjury, or a complete list.

Chief Justice Alex-Lamar Stone-Zsela and Justices Stevens, O’Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg, and Breyer participated.

#### Judgment

The Court reverses the denial of relief on the conviction-level Brady claim, 6–3, and remands for a conditional writ. Louisiana must retry Kyles within a reasonable time fixed by the district court or release him under the writ.

| Judgment component | Disposition and vote | Supporting Justices | Opposing Justices | Remedy or remand |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Conviction-level Brady claim | Reverse and remand, 6–3 | Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O’Connor, Souter, Ginsburg, Breyer | Scalia, Kennedy, Thomas | Conditional writ requiring a new trial within a reasonable period fixed by the district court, or release under the writ. |

The Court does not order acquittal, unconditional immediate release, a fixed number of days, or penalty-only relief. It does not independently adjudicate other claims merely appearing in the briefs.

#### Controlling Propositions and Authority

##### Collective suppression undermines confidence in this conviction

**Controlling proposition:** Suppressed favorable evidence is material when its collective lawful uses, considered in the whole record and in the defense’s preparation and presentation, create a reasonable probability of a different result sufficient to undermine confidence in the verdict. No item must independently satisfy that standard, and the defendant need not show acquittal more likely than conviction or that the remaining evidence is legally insufficient; the established suppression here meets the standard.

**Authority:** Justice Souter’s opinion of the Court, joined by Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O’Connor, Ginsburg and Breyer: six direct votes for the rule, application and conviction-level relief. The dissent accepts collective assessment but disputes materiality on this record.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that the undisclosed evidence changes the relationship among the prosecution’s apparently reinforcing proofs. Brady requires disclosure of material favorable evidence, and Bagley supplies a probability sufficient to undermine confidence rather than a demand for probable acquittal. Earlier identification accounts permit substantial impeachment; Beanie’s differing accounts support corroboration and examination of the investigation; and the garbage material bears on the provenance of tangible evidence. These uses reinforce one another. They do not all depend on calling Beanie or accepting every statement as true. The remaining identifications, ammunition, holster, pet food and credibility objections remain significant. But counting that evidence as sufficient does not answer how the defense could have challenged its apparent independence after disclosure. The plate list contributes only within its disputed completeness. The Court finds no planting, perjury or factual innocence. It resolves the established cumulative suppression and leaves later evidence and independently unadmitted constitutional claims outside this decision.

**Precedent treatment:**

- Brady v. Maryland, 373 U.S. 83: applies the duty to disclose material favorable evidence to the established suppression.
- United States v. Bagley, 473 U.S. 667: applies reasonable-probability materiality to the combined defense counterfactual, including lawful impeachment and preparation uses.
- Schlup v. Delo, January 23, 1995: distinguishes its gateway showing; it supplies no additional innocence prerequisite for this Brady claim.

##### The prosecutor’s responsibility includes the investigating team

**Controlling proposition:** A prosecutor must learn of favorable evidence known to police acting on the government’s behalf in the case and disclose it when Brady requires disclosure; personal ignorance does not remove that evidence from the materiality inquiry. This case-specific responsibility does not impute all knowledge of every government office or establish unrestricted investigative or open-file duties.

**Authority:** Souter’s opinion, joined by Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O’Connor, Ginsburg and Breyer: six direct votes. The dissent’s consideration of the police-held material does not supply additional votes for an affirmative organizational rule.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that the prosecution cannot avoid its disclosure responsibility by separating the trial lawyer from police who worked on the case. Brady protects the fairness of the trial, and Bagley assesses the material effect of nondisclosure; that protection would fail if the same favorable information counted only when personally recalled by the prosecutor. Here the eyewitness statements, recorded accounts, plate list and investigative memorandum arose within the investigation used to prosecute Kyles. Their lawful defense uses therefore enter the same collective inquiry. The rule does not require every agency to search all government files, and it does not convert every nondisclosure into a completed constitutional violation. Materiality and the evidence’s favorable character still matter. United States v. Williams addresses a distinct federal grand-jury duty and its specified remedy conditions; its personal-knowledge limitation does not narrow trial Brady responsibility. The Court creates no open-file entitlement and excuses no lack of diligence concerning evidence already known to the defense.

**Precedent treatment:**

- Brady and Bagley: apply their trial-disclosure protection to information held by police acting for this prosecution.
- United States v. Williams, May 4, 1992: distinguishes its confined grand-jury disclosure and dismissal rules; those limits remain effective within their own setting.

##### A completed material Brady violation requires no second duplicative harmlessness showing

**Controlling proposition:** Once this suppression is found material under Brady and Bagley, a second prejudice or harmlessness test may not defeat relief merely by reassessing whether the same nondisclosure affected the verdict. This holding rests on the prejudice built into the completed Brady violation and establishes no universal harmlessness rule for other habeas claims.

**Authority:** Souter’s opinion, joined by Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O’Connor, Ginsburg and Breyer: six direct votes for this claim-specific ground. The dissent finds no material violation and adopts no independent harmlessness ground.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that a second duplicative prejudice inquiry cannot rescue a conviction after material suppression has already undermined confidence in its verdict. Bagley makes the requisite effect part of the constitutional violation itself. The Court has found that effect through the combined disclosure counterfactual, not merely identified an error whose effect remains unexamined. Brecht retains Chapman for its specified preserved protected-silence claim, and O’Neal extends that treatment to its specified personal-intent category; neither decision supplies a general habeas rule displacing Brady’s own materiality requirement. Nor does this decision replace Bagley with Chapman. The State’s surviving evidence has already been considered, and it does not restore confidence after the lawful combined uses are taken into account. Ordinary habeas gates and independently presented claims retain their own rules. For this established violation, the appropriate relief is a conditional new-trial opportunity, not an additional weaker review of the same prejudice or a declaration of innocence.

**Precedent treatment:**

- Bagley: treats materiality as a defining element of the suppression violation, not an initial screen before a second duplicative prejudice test.
- Brecht v. Abrahamson, April 21, 1993, and O’Neal v. McAninch, February 21, 1995: preserve their actual category-specific holdings and distinguish them from claim-defining Brady materiality.

#### Precedent Treatment and Current-Law Effect

Brady and Bagley govern collective materiality and the trial prosecution’s responsibility. Williams remains confined to its distinct grand-jury setting. Schlup remains a gateway rule. Brecht and O’Neal retain their category-specific holdings; this decision neither adopts a general habeas Kotteakos rule nor extends Chapman universally. No precedent is overruled.

Courts must assess the established favorable suppression collectively, including legitimate effects on preparation, corroboration, impeachment and examination of the investigation. Prosecutors bear case-specific responsibility for investigating-police information. A completed material Brady violation carries no second duplicative harmlessness hurdle. The holdings neither require open-file discovery nor treat every nondisclosure as structural error, and they do not excuse defense lack of diligence about known evidence.

#### Separate Positions Relevant to the Decision

Justice Scalia, joined by Justices Kennedy and Thomas, dissents. The three accept cumulative reasonable-probability review and consider the police-held material. They find that the supported additional uses do not sufficiently weaken the surviving identifications, physical evidence and adverse credibility findings to undermine confidence in this verdict. They treat the jury’s knowledge of a framing theory as relevant but not conclusive, and Beanie’s absence as a limitation on some uses rather than a bar to every investigative use. Their disagreement concerns the combined application: they reject the majority’s assessment that the new corroboration and provenance challenges materially change the evidentiary picture. They do not demand proof of innocence, impose a materiality threshold on each item, or hold that mere legal sufficiency defeats Brady. Having found no material violation, they would affirm denial of this claim without adopting a second harmlessness ground. Their application is noncontrolling.

Justice Stevens joins the Court’s application of Bagley without withdrawing his earlier request-sensitive criticism of that standard. The Court does not reject that criticism or adopt a new restrictive pretrial-disclosure theory; no additional writing is filed here.

#### Mandate, Remedy, and Stage

The case returns through the Fifth Circuit for the district court to issue a conditional writ. The district court fixes a reasonable period for Louisiana to retry Kyles; failure to retry within that period requires release under the writ. No immediate unconditional release or acquittal is ordered, and the Court does not predict the result of any new trial.
