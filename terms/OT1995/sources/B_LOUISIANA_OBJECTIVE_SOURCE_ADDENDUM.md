## Objective event and record support

R pp.1–2 identifies a roughly seven-mile stretch near Lake Providence and approximately 2,000 acres. Mississippi and the Houston Group propose the Austin Smith line to the west; Louisiana seeks a boundary toward the east. R pp.9–11 says the parties agreed to submit the earlier trial record supplemented by additional evidence; the supplemental hearing ran September 12–16, 1994. The report describes boat and automobile views at different river stages. These are source-grounded Master-process facts, not new Court orders.

R p.11 identifies two requested determinations: interstate boundary location, and whether Louisiana may cancel the Houston Group's title. R pp.11, 28, 32–33 recommends the Austin Smith line and denial of cancellation. The report is a recommendation, not Court law.

R pp.7 n.7 and 15 records Louisiana's closing position as the live thalweg and the withdrawal of the Hatley Harrison line. **L p.29 nevertheless asks first for the Harrison coordinates in R Appendix B and alternatively for the present live thalweg.** M pp.11–13 objects that this changes Louisiana's position after its concessions at the hearing. A clean packet must preserve both the actual prayer and the preservation objection; it should not erase the Harrison alternative as if no longer requested.

## Each exception and each material source response

**A — Boundary channel's continued movement.** L pp.5, 11–14 excepts to a supposedly frozen boundary remaining ambulatory and being pushed west by accretion. It distinguishes Arkansas v. Tennessee, 246 U.S. 158 (1918), and 397 U.S. 88 (1970), as actual avulsions into a new bed, unlike a navigation course moving inside the river's existing banks. R p.12 states that the island exception keeps a boundary on the same side of an island after navigation shifts to the other side; the secondary boundary channel may still move through accretion/erosion until water ceases to flow, at which point it becomes fixed. R p.25 says the island did not cross an active channel: western accretions pressed the boundary channel westward while the navigation channel remained east. M pp.6–7, 15–17 supports that distinction and quotes Arkansas's flowing-versus-stagnant channel language. These are distinct legal positions; neither the required rule nor its application follows merely from calling the channel frozen.

**B — Alleged island disappearance.** L pp.6, 16–27 claims disappearance before statehood, in 1883, and in 1948. R pp.13–15 and M pp.3–4, 18 explain that, even accepting an early disappearance, Louisiana's experts recognized that the island surveyed in 1881 formed on Mississippi's side; they therefore regard the navigation channel's precise location in 1881 and the pre-statehood theory as nondispositive. L retains a competing account of landform identity. Do not treat that account as a Master finding.

For **1883**, L pp.21–22 n.5 relies on LA-SE-159-ORIG, an 1881 base map with asserted 1883 hydrographic additions, alleges a 1,500-foot steamboat channel over the old island and cites supplemental transcript testimony about depths and authenticity. R pp.19–21 questions the exhibit's provenance and interpretation, notes an island navigation light, contrasts published P-SE-23, cites Blackwell and two others' May 5, 1885 affidavits saying the family continuously lived there from April 2, 1882 and cultivated 20 acres, and quotes the November 1883 River Commission report about work building a bar to the island's head. M pp.19–23 says Louisiana assumes a desired channel width and converts possible partial erosion to total disappearance; it cites Winkley cross-examination favoring P-SE-23's reliability and denying that total destruction necessarily follows. These sources conflict about what LA-SE-159 proves; this researcher has not independently evaluated that map.

For **1948**, L pp.24–26 n.6 cites LA-SE-20A–D as showing 25–30 feet of water above the highest bed point, disputes the report's tree evidence as confusion with the 1881 survey, and questions the gauge used to interpret a 1949 high point. R p.21 instead cites P-PT-1 p.4 and Burkhardt deposition p.32 for trees in the 1994 northern ridge occupying the 1948 island location, and Mayeux cross-examination for a 1949 point five feet above ordinary high water. M pp.18–19 says Louisiana confuses the island's old 1881 location with its later location; its date formulation differs from R, describing the elevation concession as 1948. Keep that source discrepancy visible: R attributes the five-foot observation to 1949, M to 1948. Do not invent a reconciled date.

**C — Definition and permanence of an island.** L pp.6, 17–24 invokes a landform above and surrounded by water at mean high water, alternatively the engineers' top-bank criterion, relying on surveying material, 43 U.S.C. §1301, Borax, Scott v. Lattig, Texas v. Louisiana, and Rutz/Carrick. It objects that a bar or moving alluvial deposit cannot continuously be an island merely because successive outlines overlap. R pp.22–24 expressly assumes the mean-high-water test for analyzing the evidence, finds no convincing evidence of its failure for a significant period, and separately proposes a practical permanence inquiry considering vegetation and recurring dryness. M pp.23–30 disputes both the proposed definition's legal relevance and Louisiana's factual application; its pp.25 n.23 and 30 give identified maps/elevations and overlap comparisons. M distinguishes tidelands/public-land survey decisions from interstate island-boundary law, and Rutz's private title setting from this sovereignty question. A record should not collapse the report's assumed-test alternative into an outright refusal to consider elevation.

**D — Support for the Austin Smith line.** L pp.3, 7, 28–29 specifically objects that the line lacks technical foundation and crosses historic Louisiana farmland. It describes Smith's distance from the navigation thalweg as a 7,000–8,000-foot error and cites 5 Sup. Tr. 100–128, 111–120, 143, 146. R pp.25–28 separately explains (1) why southern confluence sandbars, as found, lay east of the boundary channel and joined the island before the channel dried; and (2) why Smith's placement of the dead channel is accepted despite his earlier different survey line. R p.28 says Smith had used another surveyor's location in the older Gypsum litigation but made his own determination for Houston v. Thomas. M pp.13–16 responds that the distance argument compares the wrong channels, that the alleged early farmland had eroded, and that 1948/1950/1952 hydrographic exhibits support the line and silting process. M p.16 n.17 acknowledges no hydrographic retrieval for 1954 because the western channel was no longer used for navigation. These are responsive evidence citations, not independent remeasurement by this researcher.

**E — Misapplication of law, expert treatment and new hearing.** L pp.7, 14–28 objects to substituting lay impressions and map names for professional geomorphology, hydrology, elevation evidence, and the difference between bars and islands. It also challenges extending island accretion to the southern formations. L pp.4–5 asks a new supplemental hearing for asserted misconstruction and inadequate evidentiary treatment, and pp.28–29 invokes Rule 52(a)'s clear-error formula and requests a changed boundary. M pp.8–11 answers that R independently evaluated both sides rather than adopting the old district decision; M p.46 says no reason for a new trial has been shown, and n.42 disputes importing Rule 52 into original-jurisdiction review. The merits disposition must account for the new-hearing request rather than silently omitting it.

## Alternative ground, private-title scope, remedy

M pp.35–46 independently presses **prescription and acquiescence**, citing assertions of sovereignty, patents, tax records, proceedings, surveys, officials' conduct, private use, and lengthy inaction. It cites Georgia v. South Carolina, 497 U.S. 376 (1990), and Illinois v. Kentucky, 500 U.S. 380 (1991), among older decisions. It expressly says R made no specific acquiescence findings/recommendation. L pp.26–27 disputes Mississippi sovereignty/occupation and cites Louisiana use and official conduct. This remains a argued alternative, not an unopposed established fact or a recommendation adopted by the Master. No private person's title judgment alone binds either State's sovereignty.

R pp.28–32 addresses Louisiana's prayer to cancel private title. Besides rejecting the disappearance premise, it finds Louisiana a stranger to title and unable to assert unidentified third parties' superior claims; it then supplies alternative patent-presumption/laches and deed-description grounds. **M p.31 expressly states Louisiana filed no exception to the title findings or recommendations; L's enumerated exceptions and final prayer concern the boundary.** Do not convert this posture into an affirmative decree adjudicating title among absent private owners. Existing simulated Mississippi v. Louisiana controls the jurisdiction/title distinction.

R Appendix E is only the Master's proposed decree. M p.47 asks its entry; L p.29 asks the Harrison boundary or live thalweg. The October 31 decision remains to be modeled; December 4 implementation depends on the actual resulting judgment.
