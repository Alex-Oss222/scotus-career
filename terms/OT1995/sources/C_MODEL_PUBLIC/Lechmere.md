### Lechmere, Inc. v. NLRB, No. 90-970

Merits decision, January 27, 1992

*Lechmere, Inc. v. NLRB*, 502 U.S. 527 (1992), No. 90-970. Argued November 12, 1991; decided January 27, 1992. The Court granted review of the First Circuit's judgment, 914 F.2d 313, which enforced the National Labor Relations Board's order requiring access for nonemployee union organizers. Lechmere challenges the Board's application of Jean Country, 291 N.L.R.B. 11, to its nondiscriminatory exclusion policy. The questions are the governing statutory access rule and its application to this record.

All nine Justices participated at argument and at decision.

The First Circuit judgment is reversed and enforcement of the Board's nonemployee-access order is denied, 6-3. Stone, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter and Thomas support reversal and denial of enforcement; White, Blackmun and Stevens would affirm enforcement.

Stone delivers the opinion of the Court, joined in full by O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter and Thomas. White dissents, joined by Blackmun. Stevens dissents separately, adopting White's first two accommodation-and-precedent reasons but not his criticism of Babcock as wrongly decided.

For a nondiscriminatory exclusion policy, nonemployee organizers must show that employees are practically beyond reasonable offsite communication before the Board may balance a need for entry against the employer's property rights; greater convenience or organizing success does not satisfy that threshold. This workforce's community residence, public approaches and substantial direct contacts defeat the asserted exception, while discriminatory exclusion, employees' own solicitation and genuinely inaccessible workforces remain governed by their distinct rules.

Stone's opinion, joined by O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter and Thomas, controls both the statutory rule and denial of enforcement through six Justices.

The Court holds that the Board cannot replace Babcock's threshold with general balancing. Section 7 protects employee self-organization; access for outside organizers serves that right derivatively and is limited. Babcock distinguishes employees' solicitation from nonemployee entry, and Central Hardware requires the need for entry to be shown before accommodation. Hudgens's flexible language does not erase that distinction, while Sears confirms the demanding burden to establish that reasonable alternatives are unavailable. The Board's expertise remains relevant to actual access obstacles, but an established statutory construction fixes the legal inquiry. These employees live in the surrounding community. Organizers used public approaches and obtained substantial direct contacts; the small number of signed cards does not establish inability to communicate. Jean Country improperly treats alternatives as merely one factor in balancing rather than an antecedent requirement here. The Court demands neither organizational success nor exhaustion of every imaginable technique. It preserves the separate rules for discriminatory exclusion, employee solicitation and workers isolated by their living or working location.

*NLRB v. Babcock & Wilcox Co.*, 351 U.S. 105, is applied and reaffirmed: employee and nonemployee activity differ, and demonstrated inaccessibility precedes balancing under a nondiscriminatory rule.

*Republic Aviation Corp. v. NLRB*, 324 U.S. 793, preserves employees' distinct organizing rights, which are not narrowed here.

*Central Hardware Co. v. NLRB*, 407 U.S. 539, supplies the requirement of a prior showing that nonemployee access is needed.

*Hudgens v. NLRB*, 424 U.S. 507, retains its accommodation language within Babcock's nonemployee threshold; it does not authorize balancing in every case.

*Sears, Roebuck & Co. v. Carpenters*, 436 U.S. 180, supplies the heavy burden concerning alternative access and preserves the separate route based on discrimination.

*Chevron U.S.A. Inc. v. Natural Resources Defense Council, Inc.*, 467 U.S. 837, and *Maislin Industries, U.S., Inc. v. Primary Steel, Inc.*, 497 U.S. 116, preserve general administrative deference while requiring a later agency approach to respect a governing judicial statutory construction.

*Jean Country*, 291 N.L.R.B. 11, is rejected insofar as its universal balancing replaces the prior inaccessibility inquiry for nondiscriminatory exclusion of nonemployee organizers; other contexts are not adjudicated.

Babcock's nonemployee threshold controls: demonstrated practical inaccessibility precedes balancing, and a more productive organizing location does not establish necessity. The Board may assess actual obstacles within that rule; it may not replace the rule through Jean Country. Discriminatory exclusion, employee activity, public-property advocacy and genuinely isolated workforces retain distinct treatment. No new general deference or constitutional-entry rule is adopted.

White, joined by Blackmun, would uphold the Board's accommodation and its assessment of effective alternatives. He reads Babcock, Central Hardware and Hudgens as leaving broader room to balance and argues that modern administrative deference also supports Jean Country; he criticizes Babcock's original approach. Stevens agrees only with the accommodation and subsequent-precedent reasons. He defends Babcock's employee/nonemployee distinction and declines to join the claim that it was wrongly decided. All three would affirm enforcement.

The Board's order is set aside insofar as it requires the nonemployee organizers' access on these facts and cannot be enforced on this record. The judgment permits no interference with employees' distinct organizing rights or lawful activity on public property. Supreme Court merits proceedings end. No remand for a new balancing exercise or retained supervision is ordered.
