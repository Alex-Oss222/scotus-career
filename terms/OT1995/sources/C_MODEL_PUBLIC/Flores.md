### Reno v. Flores, No. 91-905

Merits decision, March 23, 1993

#### Chronology and Posture

Argued October 13, 1992; decided March 23, 1993. On writ of certiorari to the Ninth Circuit's en banc judgment of August 9, 1991, 942 F.2d 1352, affirming the district court's May 25, 1988 relief against the juvenile-release regulation. The questions concern statutory authority, substantive liberty, review procedures and related equal-protection objections. The government seeks reversal of the mandatory responsible-adult release and automatic-hearing requirements.

Chief Justice Stone-Zsela and Justices White, Blackmun, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter and Thomas participated at argument and decision.

#### Judgment

The Court reverses and remands, seven to two. It sets aside facial invalidation and the resulting mandatory addition of every responsible adult as a release category and universal automatic-hearing requirement. The 1987 Juvenile Care Agreement remains in force.

| Judgment component | Disposition and vote | Supporting Justices | Opposing Justices | Remedy or remand |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Statutory and substantive-due-process facial invalidation | Reverse, 7–2 | Stone-Zsela, White, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas | Blackmun, Stevens | Set aside mandatory responsible-adult release category; remand. |
| Universal automatic immigration-judge hearing | Reverse, 7–2 | Stone-Zsela, White, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas | Blackmun, Stevens | Set aside the facially grounded injunction; preserve properly supported implementation litigation. |
| Facial equal-protection objection | Rejected, 7–2 | Stone-Zsela, White, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas | Blackmun and Stevens adhere to their dissent from the judgment | No independent basis to sustain facial relief. |

The disposition makes no finding that every detention, notice or delay is lawful. It leaves the conditions agreement intact and directs no particular child's detention or release.

#### Controlling Propositions and Authority

##### 1. Statutory authority for the juvenile release regulation

**Controlling proposition:** Section 1252(a)(1) permits the Attorney General to use the family-custodian and discretionary-release arrangements in 8 C.F.R. § 242.24 for temporary juvenile custody pending deportation proceedings; the regulation is not facially beyond that authority because it does not mandate release to every willing responsible adult. Custody remains subject to the statute's reasonable-dispatch limitation, its habeas remedy upon the requisite conclusive showing of unnecessary delay, the regulation's release avenues and the existing conditions agreement.

**Authority:** Scalia's opinion for the Court, joined by White, O'Connor, Kennedy, Souter and Thomas: six votes for this rule. Stone-Zsela concurs in the judgment on a narrower statutory and facial-review ground.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that Congress left room for reasonable arrangements governing detention and release while deportability is determined. Carlson v. Landon recognizes discretion within the immigration statute and requires a reasonable foundation for its exercise. The National Center for Immigrants' Rights decision permits general release conditions serving the immigration scheme, with individual safeguards, and does not confine every condition to physical attendance alone. Caring for a juvenile for whom an approved custodian is unavailable fits that statutory responsibility. The regulation permits parental designation and exceptional release, so it does not establish an absolute family-only barrier. The contrary reading would turn a discretionary custody statute into a command to entrust every otherwise releasable child to any willing adult. Neither the text nor the facial record requires that command. The Court does not decide the legality of particular prolonged custody, eliminate habeas review for unnecessary delay, or excuse breach of the conditions agreement.

**Precedent treatment:**

- Carlson v. Landon, 342 U.S. 524 (1952): applies discretion bounded by the immigration statute; it does not authorize indefinite custody.
- National Center for Immigrants' Rights, December 16, 1991, decision: applies general immigration-release authority with preserved individual safeguards; does not enlarge that decision into universal detention authority.
- United States v. Salerno, 481 U.S. 739 (1987): applies facial-review limits to the whole-regulation remedy.

##### 2. The facial substantive-due-process challenge fails

**Controlling proposition:** The Constitution does not give a juvenile without an available parent, guardian or close adult relative a fundamental right to placement with a willing unrelated adult instead of temporary, nonpunitive government care under this regulation and the binding conditions agreement. On this facial record, the family preferences and other release avenues reasonably serve care and attendance interests; individual unconstitutional confinement and enforcement of the agreement remain open.

**Authority:** Scalia's Court opinion, joined by White, O'Connor, Kennedy, Souter and Thomas: six. O'Connor and Souter also explain the substantial liberty interest in avoiding institutional confinement in a separate concurrence; Stone-Zsela does not join this formulation.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that the particular placement entitlement asserted here is not a fundamental constitutional right. Schall v. Martin recognizes that juveniles are subject to protective custody and that their liberty interests operate within the need for responsible adult care. Parham v. J. R. likewise rejects an assumption that a minor can simply choose complete freedom from adult custody. The challenged rule selects custodians and provides alternatives; it does not prescribe punishment. The conditions agreement supplies the protective setting against which this facial case must be judged, without establishing that every facility in fact complies. Youngberg v. Romeo preserves constitutional limits on institutional treatment, and Foucha requires a lawful ground for continued confinement after an authorizing predicate ends. Neither establishes a general right to unrelated-adult placement during lawful temporary immigration custody. The Court rejects the claim that the regulation must fall in all applications. Particular unlawful restraints and violations of the agreement remain subject to appropriate proceedings.

**Precedent treatment:**

- Schall v. Martin, 467 U.S. 253 (1984): applies the distinction between protective juvenile custody and punishment.
- Parham v. J. R., 442 U.S. 584 (1979): applies the special circumstances of juvenile care; does not extinguish a child's liberty interest.
- Youngberg v. Romeo, 457 U.S. 307 (1982): preserves constitutional limits on institutional care.
- Foucha v. Louisiana, May 18, 1992, decision: distinguishes psychiatric confinement after its predicate ends; no universal custody test is inferred.

##### 3. Request-based review is not facially inadequate

**Controlling proposition:** The described examination, notice and opportunity to obtain immigration-judge review on request do not facially require replacement with an automatic hearing for every detained juvenile. That result depends on the available procedures and the absence of a record establishing general inability to invoke them; it does not approve ineffective notice, denial of requested review, improper delay or unlawful custody in a particular case.

**Authority:** Scalia's Court opinion, joined by White, O'Connor, Kennedy, Souter and Thomas: six. Stone-Zsela agrees with setting aside the universal automatic-hearing remedy but reserves a distinct Mathews-based implementation inquiry.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that the facial record does not justify an automatic immigration-judge hearing in every case. For a juvenile not seeking voluntary departure, the examination and custody decision occur within twenty-four hours; a different officer examines unless no qualified alternative is available. The notice form provides English and Spanish notice of review, and a waiver can be withdrawn by requesting a hearing. Immigration-judge review, followed by administrative and judicial review, supplies a means to contest custody. Fare v. Michael C. recognizes that juveniles can knowingly waive even criminal protections; age alone therefore does not prove universal inability to request this review. The Japanese Immigrant Case supplies the underlying entitlement to due process in deportation proceedings. A proposed individualized comparison with every willing adult restates the rejected placement entitlement. Respondents have not established universal ignorance of the review option or inevitable excessive delay. The Court does not decide a supported claim that notice, assistance or delay makes review ineffective for a child or class.

**Precedent treatment:**

- Fare v. Michael C., 442 U.S. 707 (1979): applies the recognition that juveniles can make a knowing waiver; no universal finding of comprehension follows.
- The Japanese Immigrant Case, 189 U.S. 86 (1903): applies aliens' entitlement to due process in deportation proceedings.

##### 4. The challenged classifications do not establish facial unequal treatment

**Controlling proposition:** The regulation's distinction between juveniles with available family custodians and those without them survives the facial equal-protection objection because family availability bears a rational relation to safe placement and attendance. The separate federal juvenile-delinquency release scheme does not require identical release rules for immigration custody, and this ruling does not resolve unequal administration in a particular case.

**Authority:** Scalia's Court opinion, joined by White, O'Connor, Kennedy, Souter and Thomas: six. Stone-Zsela concurs in the judgment on this component and adds no ground.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that the classifications challenged here do not make the regulation facially unconstitutional. Family members and unrelated applicants for custody stand differently in relation to the child's continuing care, and the government may use that difference when arranging temporary custody. The regulation does not make family status an absolute bar to other placement: it provides parental designation and unusual-and-compelling-circumstances discretion. The objection therefore cannot proceed as though every child without an available relative must remain confined regardless of the regulation's alternatives. Schall v. Martin supplies the relevant recognition that protective juvenile arrangements may account for responsibility and care. The existence of different release provisions for federal juvenile-delinquency proceedings does not erase differences between that statutory setting and deportation proceedings. The Court does not hold that all children in the former system are citizens, that aliens lack equal-protection safeguards, or that discriminatory administration would be permissible. Its conclusion concerns the challenged classifications on this facial record.

**Precedent treatment:**

- Schall v. Martin, 467 U.S. 253 (1984): applies the legitimate relevance of protective care in juvenile custody; no general exemption from equal protection follows.

#### Precedent Treatment and Current-Law Effect

No precedent is overruled. National Center for Immigrants' Rights' individual-status and discretionary-relief safeguards remain controlling in their own channel. Foucha's psychiatric-custody holdings remain unchanged; the decision neither adopts a universal preventive-detention standard nor treats the conditions agreement as a finding of actual compliance.

Section 242.24 survives this facial attack. Subsection (b)(1) requires release in order of preference to a nondetained parent, legal guardian, or adult brother, sister, aunt, uncle or grandparent unless detention is required to secure timely appearance before the Service or immigration court, or ensure the juvenile's safety or that of others. If the only listed custodian is detained, (b)(2) provides discretionary case-by-case consideration of simultaneous release. Under (b)(3), a parent or guardian detained or outside the United States may designate another capable and willing person by sworn affidavit before an immigration or consular officer; that person must agree to care for the child and ensure presence at all future proceedings. Subsection (b)(4) permits other agreeing adult custodians only in unusual and compelling circumstances, at the district director's or chief patrol agent's discretion. These are distinct release avenues, not one undifferentiated exception.

Requested custody review and the enforceable 1987 conditions agreement remain. Section 1252(a)(1) makes the custody determination reviewable by habeas upon a conclusive showing that the Attorney General is not proceeding with the reasonable dispatch warranted by the particular facts and circumstances to determine deportability. No thirty-day legal cap is created. The twenty-four-hour examination and custody process described in the holding concerns a juvenile not seeking voluntary departure; the separate voluntary-departure safeguards are not adjudicated as universally sufficient here. No right to release to every willing unrelated adult and no automatic hearing for every detained juvenile is created. The Court does not decide particular excessive detention, defective notice, ineffective access to review or properly supported systemic implementation claims. Stone-Zsela's proposed means of remedying those defects, including necessary assistance or automatic review, remain noncontrolling.

#### Separate Positions Relevant to the Decision

O'Connor, joined by Souter, emphasizes that institutional custody seriously restrains a child's liberty even when no parent is available. She accepts the Court's judgment because the governing agreement calls for care comparable to licensed juvenile facilities, with education, recreation and reunification efforts, rather than punitive imprisonment. The concurrence does not approve arbitrary or punitive confinement and rests on the difference between lawful protective care and institutional punishment. Both Justices join the entire Court opinion.

Stone-Zsela concurs in the judgment on the limited facial showing. He recognizes freedom from institutional custody as a core liberty interest and reads § 1252(a)(1), Carlson and Chevron to permit temporary care directed to attendance and safe placement. Lawful initial custody does not answer a properly presented narrower challenge to a mandatory barrier causing unlawful continued detention. Administrative convenience cannot authorize unnecessary or indefinite custody. Under Mathews, age, language, assistance, notice and actual ability to seek review matter. A supported individual or systemic defect can require effective assistance, automatic review where necessary, or another remedy fitted to that defect. The record does not establish universal inability to request review, justify prescribing a hearing schedule, or require placement with every willing adult. He leaves the 1987 agreement intact.

Stevens, joined by Blackmun, would affirm. The dissent treats freedom from needless institutional custody as the right at stake and argues that a willing, responsible adult can protect care and attendance interests without detention. Carlson, Witkovich and National Center for Immigrants' Rights constrain immigration discretion to legitimate statutory ends; administrative convenience does not itself supply those ends. Schall's demonstrated preventive need does not validate a categorical refusal to consider suitable nonrelatives. The dissent also regards a child's inability to invoke review reliably as a reason for the neutral hearing the lower court required. Its statutory and constitutional objections do not command a majority.

Supreme Court merits review is complete; the case is remanded through the Ninth Circuit for proceedings consistent with reversal. The facial injunction falls to the extent stated. The conditions agreement remains enforceable in the district court. No particular later hearing date, mandate-issuance date, release order or settlement is specified.
