#### Separate Positions Relevant to the Decision

White, joined by Blackmun, Stevens and Souter, concludes in Part III that Louisiana also denies equal protection by withholding ordinary commitment safeguards from a sane acquittee while not permitting comparable indefinite dangerousness confinement for other persons whose original custody basis has ended. The four would require a particularly convincing justification for that disparity; this is a plurality position, not law.

O'Connor emphasizes that the holding concerns this indefinite psychiatric regime. She leaves open detention carefully limited in nature and duration to substantial safety concerns and questions extended confinement beyond what a conviction could have permitted, without deciding that question. No constitutional requirement that a State provide an insanity defense is adopted.

Stone-Zsela agrees that the psychiatric channel's ended predicate requires relief. He reserves independent equal protection, a new general scrutiny tier, universal proof rules and narrowly defined alternative regimes. Implementation cannot allow indefinite continuation while the State looks for a new justification; separate custody requires its own established authority and procedures.

Kennedy would affirm because the insanity adjudication follows proof of criminal conduct and belongs to a distinct legal channel. He views state definitions of criminal insanity and incapacitation as constitutionally relevant and rejects automatic transfer of ordinary civil-commitment rules to this setting.

Thomas, joined by Scalia, would affirm because acquittees differ from ordinary civil committees, the State may attach continued safety-based custody to that status, and periodic counseled review is adequate here. They reject an expansive new substantive right against this confinement and distinguish its lack of a fixed endpoint from a refusal ever to provide release review. Neither dissent controls.



## Heller

#### Opinion Topology

| Souter | Blackmun, Stevens | Dissent on family-party equal protection: information can be obtained without adding an adverse party with appeal rights; due process not reached. | Would affirm participation invalidation |

#### Separate Positions Relevant to the Decision

Kennedy, joined by White, Scalia and Thomas, would sustain the proof distinction. They regard easier diagnosis, a longer behavioral history supporting prediction, and generally less intrusive treatment as independently sufficient rational grounds. Addington’s minimum is met, and ordinary review permits imperfect generalizations. Souter, joined by Blackmun and Stevens, would also invalidate family-party status: useful information can be obtained through witnesses, while party status adds an adversary able to call and cross-examine witnesses and appeal a refusal to confine. They find no rational connection between that additional burden and the different condition and therefore do not reach due process. Blackmun separately preserves his view that disability classifications and substantial restraints on liberty warrant heightened review, while accepting that the proof distinction fails the ordinary standard used here.



## Flores

#### Opinion Topology

#### Separate Positions Relevant to the Decision

O'Connor, joined by Souter, emphasizes that institutional custody seriously restrains a child's liberty even when no parent is available. She accepts the Court's judgment because the governing agreement calls for care comparable to licensed juvenile facilities, with education, recreation and reunification efforts, rather than punitive imprisonment. The concurrence does not approve arbitrary or punitive confinement and rests on the difference between lawful protective care and institutional punishment. Both Justices join the entire Court opinion.

Stone-Zsela concurs in the judgment on the limited facial showing. He recognizes freedom from institutional custody as a core liberty interest and reads § 1252(a)(1), Carlson and Chevron to permit temporary care directed to attendance and safe placement. Lawful initial custody does not answer a properly presented narrower challenge to a mandatory barrier causing unlawful continued detention. Administrative convenience cannot authorize unnecessary or indefinite custody. Under Mathews, age, language, assistance, notice and actual ability to seek review matter. A supported individual or systemic defect can require effective assistance, automatic review where necessary, or another remedy fitted to that defect. The record does not establish universal inability to request review, justify prescribing a hearing schedule, or require placement with every willing adult. He leaves the 1987 agreement intact.

Stevens, joined by Blackmun, would affirm. The dissent treats freedom from needless institutional custody as the right at stake and argues that a willing, responsible adult can protect care and attendance interests without detention. Carlson, Witkovich and National Center for Immigrants' Rights constrain immigration discretion to legitimate statutory ends; administrative convenience does not itself supply those ends. Schall's demonstrated preventive need does not validate a categorical refusal to consider suitable nonrelatives. The dissent also regards a child's inability to invoke review reliably as a reason for the neutral hearing the lower court required. Its statutory and constitutional objections do not command a majority.

No complete opinion commands a majority. The narrow sufficiency proposition below has eight express supporters through their respective writings; that agreement does not make those Justices formal joiners of a single opinion.


#### Separate Positions Relevant to the Decision

Thomas, joined by Scalia, finds the trial evidence sufficient whatever the answer to the proposed broader review question and leaves that choice open. White endorses that application of Jackson. O’Connor, joined by Blackmun and Stevens, and Kennedy separately explain that Teague’s inquiry into the retroactive use of new rules does not itself replace independent application of existing federal law. Kennedy reserves whether this particular Jackson claim would require a new rule because it already fails on the merits.

Souter would stop at Teague. In his view, the later restriction imposed on the established inference from falsely explained recent possession was not dictated when the conviction became final. Stone-Zsela applies Jackson independently, confines disbelief to its proper role alongside affirmative evidence, preserves the State’s burden and rejects use of the prior felony as proof of a propensity to commit this theft. He finds no occasion for the proposed general change in the review framework.

#### Separate Positions Relevant to the Decision

O’Connor concurs in the notice reversal and dissents on Miranda review. Following her Duckworth concurrence, she would extend Powell to a claim involving voluntary but unwarned statements after a full and fair state opportunity. She regards involuntariness review as the appropriate means to remedy genuine coercion and the added deterrent value of later Miranda review as insufficient to outweigh finality and retrial costs. This does not reject collateral review of actual compelled statements.

Scalia, joined by Thomas, also joins the notice ruling but would remand for a determination whether unusual equitable circumstances justify renewed Miranda litigation after the opportunities already afforded. He treats prior full and fair litigation as a powerful equitable consideration under sections 2241 and 2243, subject to considerations concerning fairness or accuracy, rather than a jurisdictional defect. The separate writing urges a broader approach beyond this case; the Court does not adopt it. Herrera’s record-specific holding supplies no categorical innocence bar.



## Health_Care

#### Opinion Topology

| Writing | Author | Joins | Scope |
|---|---|---|---|
| Opinion of the Court | Ginsburg | Stone-Zsela, Blackmun, Stevens, Souter | Permissible functional construction and its supported application; five direct joins. |
| Concurrence | Stone-Zsela | — | Would also require the limiting statutory distinction; joins the Court's narrower rule. |
| Dissent | Kennedy | O'Connor, Scalia, Thomas | Would affirm the employer-interest ruling, reserving other unpresented grounds. |


#### Separate Positions Relevant to the Decision

Stone-Zsela concurs, joining the entire permissible-construction opinion. He would also hold that the statutory scheme requires the distinction between professional or technical direction and actual delegated supervisory authority. The inquiry begins with a listed function, identifies the decisions the employer authorized, examines their practical scope, and asks whether exercising that authority requires independent rather than routine or clerical judgment. Those considerations implement the statutory conditions; they do not require hiring, firing, policy control, a fixed percentage of supervisory work, or a particular personal sanction. A charge nurse's reallocation of aides may involve both clinical and supervisory judgment, so the actual delegation matters. Professional judgment can satisfy independent judgment when genuine supervisory authority exists; it cannot establish that authority by itself. The four employees are licensed practical nurses, and their protection does not depend on classifying them as registered professionals. The agency's actual limited-role findings support its result. If it had rested exclusively on the assertion that patient care never benefits the employer, Chenery would require remand rather than judicial repair. His mandatory construction has no other joins and does not bind later Boards; the Court's adopted rule permits the Board's present interpretation.

Kennedy dissents, joined by O'Connor, Scalia and Thomas. Providing patient care is the nursing home's business, and authority exercised to provide it serves the employer's interest even when professional norms also guide the nurse. The statute separately requires a listed supervisory function and independent judgment, which can preserve employee coverage without an additional exclusion from employer interest. They would affirm the Sixth Circuit's refusal of enforcement on the legal ground presented. They do not hold that every nurse is a supervisor or decide an alternative classification ground the Board did not present. In their view, the statutory objection cannot be answered by describing the same direction as incidental professional work when it otherwise satisfies the enacted definition.



## MCI

#### Opinion Topology

| Writing | Author | Joined by | Relationship to judgment | Scope joined |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Opinion of the Court | Chief Justice Stone-Zsela | Scalia, Kennedy, Thomas, Ginsburg | Affirm both consolidated dockets | Structural modification ground, statutory qualifications, deference analysis and remedy; all join fully. |
| Concurrence | Scalia | Kennedy, Thomas, Ginsburg | Affirm; all four also join the Court | Additional independently sufficient special-condition objection; four votes, noncontrolling. |
| Dissent | Stevens | Blackmun, Souter | Would reverse and remand | The modification power reasonably permits the rule; both asserted statutory barriers are rejected. |


#### Separate Positions Relevant to the Decision

Justice Scalia concurs, joined by Kennedy, Thomas and Ginsburg, while joining the Court's opinion in full. They would also hold that the general order independently fails §203(b)(2)'s special-circumstances-or-conditions requirement. Even assuming the modification power could reach the change, a substitute filing regime covering every long-distance carrier except AT&T and approximately forty percent of customers has the ordinary reach of sector regulation. Calling the category nondominant does not, in their view, establish the limiting condition for this order. They do not adopt a numerical cutoff or find that each nondominant carrier has market power; breadth is evidence of the order's regulatory character. They preserve general orders addressing genuine qualifying conditions and do not determine whether a smaller or differently conditioned waiver would qualify. The four Justices regard this statutory objection as sufficient by itself. It is not an alternative holding of the Court.

Justice Stevens dissents, joined by Blackmun and Souter. They would reverse the statutory-authority judgment and remand because the express power to modify any requirement, including by a general order for special conditions, reasonably accommodates the difference between dominant and competitive carriers. Nondominance can be special by character rather than by numerical rarity. The agency's actual competition rationale matters: mandatory advance public filing may burden competition without delivering the same protection against market power. The rule preserves voluntary filing, just-and-reasonable rates, nondiscrimination, complaint enforcement and the possibility of restoring filing obligations if conditions change. These features persuade the dissent that the Commission adjusted the statutory system rather than abandoned it. The 120-day ceiling limits an increased notice burden; it does not confine every relaxation to an insignificant detail. Boston & Maine supports contextual delegation, and the permissible constructions sustained in Health Care & Retirement and PUD No. 1 show why operative text and safeguards matter. Lechmere still requires respect for established statutory limits, but it does not resolve the scope of this express modification clause. The dissent rejects both the Court's modification ground and the concurrence's independent special-condition objection. Good cause, actual qualifying conditions and the agency's own reasons remain necessary; the dissent would not authorize elimination of §§201–202, sustain compulsory nonfiling, or adjudicate the separate complaint's monetary remedy.

## Sandin

#### Opinion Topology

| Writing | Author | Joined by | Relationship and scope |
|---|---|---|---|
| Opinion of the Court | Breyer | Stone-Zsela, Stevens, Souter, Ginsburg | Sufficient nonexclusive rule; no ultimate application holding; bounded remand |
| Concurrence | Ginsburg | Stevens | Full common rule/remand join; would already find interest and retains direct liberty |
| Concurrence | Breyer | Souter | Full common rule/remand join; would already find interest under whole Hawaii scheme |
| Dissent | O’Connor | Scalia, Kennedy, Thomas | Would reverse reviewed liberty predicate under replacement hardship framework |


#### Separate Positions Relevant to the Decision

Ginsburg, joined by Stevens, concurs. They would recognize protected liberty already on the actual punitive confinement record and also preserve an independent constitutional retained-liberty ground. Severe imposed isolation and its punitive character matter without depending on how a State drafts its code; administrative similarity and later expungement do not retroactively remove the need for process. They join the Court’s sufficient state-entitlement rule because it does not make codification necessary or replace intrinsic liberty with an exclusive hardship prerequisite. Breyer, joined by Souter, also concurs. The full defined-misconduct, impartial-decision and valid-evidence scheme constrains serious punishment, and the thirty-day isolation materially restricts ordinary activity. They would find the protected interest already established, while accepting the Court’s narrower remand and leaving actual procedural adequacy open. O’Connor, joined by Scalia, Kennedy and Thomas, dissents. They would replace the mandatory-regulation methodology with a rule ordinarily limiting state-created interests to atypical and significant hardship relative to ordinary prison life, while preserving direct unexpected-restraint and inevitable sentence-duration interests. They find the reviewed thirty-day confinement insufficient under that rule in light of comparable administrative and protective confinement and discretionary parole. Their proposed framework receives four votes and does not control. The concurrences’ affirmative application likewise has four votes; no Marks aggregation makes it the Court’s application holding.
