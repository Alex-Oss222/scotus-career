## Findings for accurate attribution

### Nunn v. State, 1 Ga. 243, 249–251 (1846)

Lumpkin's opinion expressly described a right of the whole people, “and not militia only,” and connected it to preparation of a well-regulated militia. The attribution supporting individual possession is accurate. Its actual distinction matters: it sustained suppression of concealed carrying while invalidating the prohibition of open bearing and quashing Nunn's conviction, which did not charge concealment. Nunn applied the federal Amendment directly to state legislation; it was not a Fourteenth Amendment incorporation decision. It does not supply a settled 1995 Supreme Court incorporation holding, an exclusively domestic-use rule, or Stone's particular disqualification framework. [Original opinion transcription](https://guncite.com/court/state/1ga243.html); [independent original-text transcription](https://law.counselstack.com/opinion/nunn-v-state-ga-1846).

### Andrews v. State, 50 Tenn. (3 Heisk.) 165 (1871)

**Historical label correction:** Andrews is post-Civil War, decided in 1871. The supplement's introductory description grouping all three state cases as pre-Civil War is inaccurate. Its date-eligibility for 1995 remains intact.

Freeman's Court opinion distinguished a citizen's private right to keep arms, with necessary incidents, from bearing arms for common defense (pp. 182–184). It expressly included purchase, suitable ammunition, repair, practice and transportation to and from home (pp. 178–179). Those propositions are genuinely in the Court opinion, not merely counsel's similar argument on p. 170. [Original opinion, including separate opinion and notes](https://guncite.com/court/state/50tn165.html).

The material limits are substantial. The court first held the federal Amendment inapplicable to state legislation (pp. 171–175), then construed Tennessee's common-defense guarantee and its express power to regulate wearing arms to prevent crime. Protected arms were tied to ordinary military equipment and citizen training; the opinion permitted regulation of public use and restrictions at churches and public assemblies. It sustained prohibitions covering dirks, sword-canes, stilettos and belt or pocket pistols; protection of a revolver depended on evidence that it was the military-type weapon described. It rejected a general self-defense excuse for forbidden weapons and reserved a distinct imminent-danger question concerning constitutionally protected weapons. Thus it supports individual keeping and acquisition incidents, but does not itself announce Stone's general common-handgun/home-defense rule. Nelson's broader separate reasoning, joined by Turney, must not be substituted for Freeman's Court opinion. [Independent original-text transcription, especially pp. 175–192](https://law.counselstack.com/opinion/andrews-v-state-tenn-1871).

### State v. Chandler, 5 La. Ann. 489, 490–491 (1850)

Preston's opinion connected open carrying to defense of oneself and one's country. The supplement's personal-defense attribution is supported. The opinion simultaneously upheld Louisiana's 1813 prohibition of concealed deadly weapons because open carrying remained available. It did not invalidate that prohibition, decide handgun purchase/home licensing, or conduct a Fourteenth Amendment inquiry. The ultimate reversal and remand for trial for manslaughter concerned the erroneous rejection of a self-defense instruction, with its applicability dependent on the actual attack evidence. [Original opinion transcription](https://guncite.com/court/state/5la489.html); [independent original-text transcription](https://law.counselstack.com/opinion/state-v-chandler-la-1850).

### Benton v. Maryland, 395 U.S. 784, 793–796 (1969)

The incorporation holding concerns the Fifth Amendment's Double Jeopardy Clause. Marshall's opinion applies the fundamental-to-the-American-scheme-of-justice inquiry, rejects a diluted state version after a guarantee is incorporated, and overrules Palko insofar as inconsistent with that holding. The supplement accurately describes that doctrinal method and equal-standard proposition. Benton does **not** itself incorporate every Bill of Rights provision, decide the Second Amendment's fundamental status, or overrule Cruikshank or Presser. Using Benton's method for arms is Stone's proposed doctrinal application, not Benton's historical holding. [Official U.S. Reports PDF](https://tile.loc.gov/storage-services/service/ll/usrep/usrep395/usrep395784/usrep395784.pdf); [Cornell opinion text](https://www.law.cornell.edu/supremecourt/text/395/784).

### Freedmen's Bureau Act of July 16, 1866, ch. 200, §14, 14 Stat. 173, 176–177

Section 14 expressly includes the “constitutional right to bear arms” in equal legal benefits concerning liberty, security and property. Its protected class is all citizens of the qualifying State or district, irrespective of race, color or prior slavery. Rights protection covers places with rebellion-interrupted judicial proceedings until restored, and places with rebellion-discontinued constitutional relations until restored and represented in Congress. The separate enforcement sentence requires military protection and jurisdiction through Bureau officers, under presidential/War Department rules, where judicial proceedings were interrupted, pending judicial restoration, restored constitutional relations and congressional representation. It prohibits greater race-based punishment than white persons face for the same offense. Bureau jurisdiction never exists in a State whose proceedings were not interrupted; it ceases when state and federal courts operate peaceably, constitutional relations are restored, and the State is represented in Congress. Section 1 separately continues the Bureau for two years. This is Reconstruction legislation with express limits, not an unqualified nationwide permanent arms code or a judicial incorporation ruling. [Official complete Act, especially pp. 176–177](https://www.govinfo.gov/link/statute/14/173).

### Senator Jacob Howard's May 23, 1866 introduction

The Congressional Globe expressly lists keeping and bearing arms among personal rights secured by the first eight amendments (p. 2765). Howard then explains the proposed amendment's purpose of restraining state infringements and its congressional enforcement provision (p. 2766). The supplement's attribution of those statements is accurate. The enumeration occurs in his **privileges-or-immunities** discussion; he separately addresses due process and equal protection. It is therefore evidence for his account of the proposed amendment's protection of rights, not an announcement of the later selective-incorporation method through Due Process. Do not attribute Stone's chosen doctrinal vehicle to Howard. This is an 1866 introduction, preceding 1868 ratification, and one identified speaker's explanation; the text alone does not establish every ratifier's understanding or Stone's precise limits on disarmament. [Original Congressional Globe, p. 2765](https://digital.library.unt.edu/ark:/67531/metadc30866/m1/847/), [p. 2766](https://digital.library.unt.edu/ark:/67531/metadc30866/m1/848/); [introduction/date, p. 2764](https://digital.library.unt.edu/ark:/67531/metadc30866/m1/846/).
