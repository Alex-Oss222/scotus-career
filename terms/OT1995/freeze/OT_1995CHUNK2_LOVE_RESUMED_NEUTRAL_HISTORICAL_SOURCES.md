## 1. Howard's explanation of the proposed Fourteenth Amendment

**Primary source:** Congressional Globe, 39th Congress, 1st Session, May 23, 1866, pp. [2764](https://digital.library.unt.edu/ark:/67531/metadc30866/m1/846/), [2765](https://digital.library.unt.edu/ark:/67531/metadc30866/m1/847/), and [2766](https://digital.library.unt.edu/ark:/67531/metadc30866/m1/848/). The original page images were read, including the transition between pp. 2765–2766; the OCR's broken words do not control the transcription below.

The Senate is considering H.R. No. 127 proposing a constitutional amendment. Howard opens because of Fessenden's illness and says he will present the committee's views and motives as he understands them. The printed proposal on p. 2764 does not yet contain the eventual opening citizenship sentence. Its first section contains the proposed state prohibitions concerning privileges or immunities of United States citizens, deprivation of any person's life, liberty, or property without due process, and denial of equal protection.

On p. 2765 Howard distinguishes the first clause's protection of citizens from the other clauses' protection of persons. After discussing Article IV and quoting *Corfield*, he says that the personal rights secured by the first eight amendments should be added to the privileges and immunities under discussion. His list expressly includes **“the right to keep and to bear arms.”** He then observes that prevailing doctrine does not make those federal guarantees restraints on state legislation. Continuing onto p. 2766, he explains that the proposed first section would restrain States and compel respect for the fundamental guarantees, while the fifth section would give Congress enforcement power. He separately discusses the due-process/equal-protection clauses and racial equality; he does not treat all constitutional rights as identical or include suffrage within the first section.

**Support and limit.** This is direct evidence that an amendment proponent expressly counted the arms guarantee among personal constitutional rights and explained the proposal as protecting those guarantees against States. It is stronger evidence than a modern scholar's report of Reconstruction intentions. The arms list has no formal militia-enrollment condition. But it is one speaker's explanation, framed most directly around **privileges or immunities**, not a judicial holding that the Due Process Clause incorporates the Second Amendment. The pages do not establish every legislator's or ratifying State's understanding, define permissible weapons or regulations, or resolve the later doctrinal treatment of the clauses. An argument using it under the received selective-incorporation method must explain that connection rather than silently attributing a due-process holding to Howard.

## 2. Freedmen's Bureau Act of July 16, 1866

**Primary source:** Act of July 16, 1866, ch. 200, §§ 1, 14, 14 Stat. 173, 176–177, [official Statutes at Large scan](https://www.govinfo.gov/link/statute/14/173). Pages 176–177 were visually checked across the section's page break. Page 177 records both Houses passing the returned bill over presidential objections on July 16, 1866. Section 1 continues the earlier Bureau act for two years; section 14 contains its own territorial, restoration, and jurisdictional conditions.

Section 14, in full (line-break hyphenation removed):

> SEC. 14. And be it further enacted, That in every State or district where the ordinary course of judicial proceedings has been interrupted by the rebellion, and until the same shall be fully restored, and in every State or district whose constitutional relations to the government have been practically discontinued by the rebellion, and until such State shall have been restored in such relations, and shall be duly represented in the Congress of the United States, the right to make and enforce contracts, to sue, be parties, and give evidence, to inherit, purchase, lease, sell, hold, and convey real and personal property, and to have full and equal benefit of all laws and proceedings concerning personal liberty, personal security, and the acquisition, enjoyment, and disposition of estate, real and personal, including the constitutional right to bear arms, shall be secured to and enjoyed by all the citizens of such State or district without respect to race or color, or previous condition of slavery. And whenever in either of said States or districts the ordinary course of judicial proceedings has been interrupted by the rebellion, and until the same shall be fully restored, and until such State shall have been restored in its constitutional relations to the government, and shall be duly represented in the Congress of the United States, the President shall, through the commissioner and the officers of the bureau, and under such rules and regulations as the President, through the Secretary of War, shall prescribe, extend military protection and have military jurisdiction over all cases and questions concerning the free enjoyment of such immunities and rights, and no penalty or punishment for any violation of law shall be imposed or permitted because of race or color, or previous condition of slavery, other or greater than the penalty or punishment to which white persons may be liable by law for the like offence. But the jurisdiction conferred by this section upon the officers of the bureau shall not exist in any State where the ordinary course of judicial proceedings has not been interrupted by the rebellion, and shall cease in every State when the courts of the State and the United States are not disturbed in the peaceable course of justice, and after such State shall be fully restored in its constitutional relations to the government, and shall be duly represented in the Congress of the United States.

**Support and limit.** Congress expressly identifies a constitutional arms right within a provision securing citizens' civil rights, personal liberty, personal security, and property-related protections without racial distinction. The beneficiaries of this section are all citizens of the covered State or district, not solely militia members; its text imposes no enrollment or military-service prerequisite. It supplies contemporaneous evidence for a personal-right understanding and federal protection against discriminatory deprivation. Its inclusion of property acquisition is relevant context, but the provision does not separately adjudicate an unlimited right to buy any particular firearm.

The first sentence's two geographic/restoration descriptions, the military-enforcement sentence, and the concluding exclusion/termination of Bureau jurisdiction must remain distinct. This was a reconstruction statute with specified reach, enacted before ratification of the Fourteenth Amendment. It is not itself a perpetual nationwide incorporation holding, a modern handgun-licensing standard, or a damages rule for the present defendants. Its equality language also leaves room to distinguish equal protection in possession and enjoyment from immunity against every nondiscriminatory arms regulation. The statutory evidence supports consideration of the constitutional issue; it does not decide that issue by itself.

## 3. Three state judicial treatments of personal arms

These are the actual judicial texts reproduced at the supplied URLs, read through their dispositions and, in *Andrews*, through the separate writing and reporter notes. They are historical state decisions, not Supreme Court holdings or later descriptions of those decisions. Reporter headnotes, editorial footnotes, and counsel's arguments are not attributed to the courts. The supplied reproductions retain reporter pagination; an independent original reporter scan was not obtained for these three decisions in this bounded check.

### Nunn v. State, 1 Ga. 243 (1846)

[Full opinion](https://guncite.com/court/state/1ga243.html), especially 249–251. Lumpkin's opinion reads the federal arms guarantee as protecting the people broadly, including persons beyond the militia and arms beyond those used by the militia, while connecting the guarantee to maintaining an effective militia. It treats the guarantee as restricting both state and federal legislation. The operative distinction permits suppressing concealed carrying but invalidates the 1837 act insofar as it prohibits open bearing. Because the charge concerning a non-horseman's pistol did not allege concealment, the judgment is reversed and the proceeding quashed.

**Use and limit.** This is direct antebellum judicial support for personal invocation without formal enrollment and for an open-arms right beyond a narrow military-weapons category. It is not an unrestricted-carry holding: the opinion sustains the concealed-carry restriction. Its direct application of the federal guarantee to a State predates the Fourteenth Amendment and conflicts with the later Supreme Court nonapplication rule preserved in *Presser*. It can supply historical evidence; it cannot silently override that controlling federal precedent.

### Andrews v. State, 50 Tenn. (3 Heisk.) 165 (1871)

[Full opinions](https://guncite.com/court/state/50tn165.html), especially 173–180, 185–193, 193–201. Freeman's majority opinion **rejects application of the federal Second Amendment to the State**, following *Barron*, and proceeds under Tennessee's own constitution. That constitution protects citizens' keeping and bearing arms for their common defense and expressly permits legislation regulating the wearing of arms to prevent crime.

The majority nevertheless identifies keeping arms as the individual's right as a citizen, not merely a right held while serving as a soldier. It explains that protected keeping entails purchase, ammunition, practice, repair, and the ordinary movement necessary to those activities, including taking a weapon to and from a gunsmith. Disappearance of the existing militia organization would not eliminate that right. Thus, it offers particularly direct historical support for possession and acquisition without formal enrollment.

Its protected weapons are materially limited by suitability for ordinary military equipment or efficiency as citizen soldiers. It sustains prohibitions on the listed dirks, sword canes, Spanish stilettos, belt or pocket pistols. Whether the statutory “revolver” belongs to the protected soldier's-weapon category requires evidence: some revolvers do, some do not. An absolute prohibition reaching protected keeping at home and incidental use or transport is too broad, while regulation of public carrying and use for public safety remains permissible. The indictments specify only a pistol and are too indefinite; the cases are reversed, the indictments quashed, and proceedings remanded. General appeals to self-defense do not permit choosing a weapon the law validly prohibits. The majority discusses actual imminent defense with a lawful weapon sympathetically but expressly leaves the future regulatory question for a proper case.

Nelson, joined by Turney, takes a broader separate position protecting arms for private defense and rejecting the majority's weapon limitation; that broader position is **not the majority's holding**. Sneed's objection runs in the opposite direction, favoring broader legislative prohibition. The state opinion therefore supplies both personal-right evidence and substantial regulatory/military-suitability limits, together with explicit federal nonapplication. It does not establish a federal constitutional entitlement to every handgun.

### State v. Chandler, 5 La. Ann. 489 (1850)

[Full opinion](https://guncite.com/court/state/5la489.html), 489–491. Preston's opinion rejects the argument that the arms guarantee invalidates Louisiana's 1813 concealed-weapons law. It reasons that the statute leaves open carrying available and links that openly exercised right under the federal Constitution to defense of oneself and the country, without covert advantages facilitating assassination. No formal militia enrollment appears as a condition in this discussion.

**Use and limit.** This is antebellum state judicial evidence connecting the arms right to personal defense and distinguishing open from concealed carry. Its arms ruling sustains regulation; it does not invalidate all restrictions. The actual reversal and remand concern an erroneous statement about the law of self-defense in the homicide instructions. Retrial is confined to manslaughter because the verdict had substantially acquitted the defendant of murder. This is neither a Fourteenth Amendment incorporation holding nor an acquisition or licensing case.

## 4. Benton and the received incorporation method

**Primary source:** *Benton v. Maryland*, 395 U.S. 784 (1969), [official United States Reports scan](https://tile.loc.gov/storage-services/service/ll/usrep/usrep395/usrep395784/usrep395784.pdf). The complete opinion and separate writings, including footnotes, were read. The material incorporation discussion is at 793–796.

The Court applies the Fifth Amendment double-jeopardy guarantee to States through the Fourteenth Amendment and expressly overrules *Palko* insofar as inconsistent. Relying on *Duncan* and *Malloy*, it rejects a diluted, case-specific fundamental-fairness substitute once a particular guarantee is found fundamental to the American scheme of justice. Its inquiry draws on the guarantee's English and American historical roots, widespread state recognition, and its concrete protection of individuals against repeated prosecution. It then applies the federal standard. Thus an existing adverse state-application precedent is not categorically immune from reconsideration through that method.

**Limit:** *Benton* decides double jeopardy, not arms; identification of an enumerated personal right alone is not the complete selective-incorporation analysis. Its historical reasoning concerns the particular guarantee before it. Harlan's dissent, joined by Stewart with a stated qualification concerning his earlier joins, objects both to reaching the constitutional question and to incorporation; it would nevertheless find the coerced retrial inconsistent with traditional due process if the merits were reached. Those are separate grounds, not limits adopted by the majority. No sitting Justice's firearm position follows from this earlier lineup.

## 5. Consequences for the questions left open in the neutral packet

The affirmative historical argument now has primary support: judicial recognition of personal keeping and defense, *Andrews*' express purchase incident, congressional inclusion of arms among citizens' civil protections, and Howard's explicit personal-right/state-restraint explanation. Formal enrollment is not stated as a necessary condition in any of those affirmative passages. The militia's public purpose and military suitability of weapons are distinct issues from whether only enrolled persons may invoke a right. The already supplied full *Miller* and *Presser* texts must continue to be considered on their own terms.

The opposing argument retains concrete sources and limits: the controlling Supreme Court nonapplication rule is not displaced by a state court, a statute, or a legislative speech; *Andrews* itself accepts federal nonapplication and limits weapon coverage; both *Nunn* and *Chandler* sustain concealed-carry regulation; section 14 has a reconstruction/equality setting and bounded operation; Howard's explanation is clause-specific; *Benton* requires an actual guarantee-specific analysis. These limitations cannot be replaced by saying that the primary record contains no evidence of personal arms rights or state protection, just as the affirmative evidence cannot be treated as already deciding modern incorporation or the validity of this arrest-based administrative refusal.

Nothing added establishes the individual defendants' liability, the clearly established law at the September 1990 acts, the procedural availability of their unraised immunity defense, or the remedy. Those remain separate questions under the existing neutral packet and entering law. In particular, historical evidence pertinent to the merits is not itself a finding that every relevant officer had clear constitutional notice of the precise asserted right.
