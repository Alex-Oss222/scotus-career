## Section 2243: commands, exceptions, and limits

The complete operative text contains eight paragraphs. Its opening command is:

> A court, justice or judge entertaining an application for a writ of habeas corpus shall forthwith award the writ or issue an order directing the respondent to show cause why the writ should not be granted, unless it appears from the application that the applicant or person detained is not entitled thereto.

Its final paragraph provides:

> The court shall summarily hear and determine the facts, and dispose of the matter as law and justice require.

The intervening paragraphs matter to a faithful description of the statutory procedure:

- The writ or show-cause order is directed to the custodian. Return is required within three days unless, for good cause, additional time “not exceeding twenty days” is allowed. The historical note confirms that this replaced the earlier distance-dependent return periods.
- The return must certify the true cause of detention. On its return, a hearing day is to be set not more than five days later, unless additional time is allowed for good cause. The text does not attach the return paragraph's twenty-day language to this separate hearing provision. The note explains that the hearing extension is no longer limited to the petitioner's request for more time.
- Production of the detained person's body at the hearing is required unless the application and return present only issues of law. The note links this provision to Walker v. Johnston, 312 U.S. 275 (1941); that opinion was not independently reopened for this addendum.
- The applicant or detained person may under oath deny facts in the return or allege other material facts. The return and suggestions against it may be amended by leave of court before or after filing.

The source credit is June 25, 1948, ch. 646, 62 Stat. 965. The notes identify consolidation of former 28 U.S.C. §§ 455–461, explain the timing and production changes, and record changes in phraseology. They do not add a separate equitable dismissal rule, a limitations period, or an automatic execution stay.

The source reinforces the need to analyze the statute and the habeas Rules together. “Law and justice” is operative statutory language that the parties' competing arguments must account for; reading that phrase alone does not resolve whether an independent equitable ground can support dismissal of this first personal federal petition. The opening nonentitlement exception, the alternative of a show-cause order, and the law-only production exception also prevent an inference that every filed petition necessarily receives a merits grant or an evidentiary hearing. This source recovery does not decide the relationship between the statutory timing language and the governing Rules or impose a newly calculated case deadline on an incomplete record.

## Section 2251: complete operative text and limits

The complete 1994 operative section reads:

> A justice or judge of the United States before whom a habeas corpus proceeding is pending, may, before final judgment or after final judgment of discharge, or pending appeal, stay any proceeding against the person detained in any State court or by or under the authority of any State for any matter involved in the habeas corpus proceeding.
>
> After the granting of such a stay, any such proceeding in any State court or by or under the authority of any State shall be void. If no stay is granted, any such proceeding shall be as valid as if no habeas corpus proceedings or appeal were pending.

The statute confers discretionary authority (“may”) on a justice or judge before whom a habeas proceeding is pending. Its exact temporal wording includes **after final judgment of discharge**, rather than after every kind of final judgment. Its subject is a proceeding against the detained person for a matter involved in the habeas proceeding. The consequences for State proceedings refer to “such” a stay and “any such proceeding”; they do not invalidate unrelated State action.

The second paragraph directly confirms that filing or pendency alone does not suspend the covered State proceeding. It supplies no automatic stay on the issuance of a certificate of probable cause and no automatic stay for either a first or a subsequent petition. Conversely, the text itself does not establish that an identified petition lacks substantial grounds, that a particular delay is abusive, or that a particular temporary stay is unnecessary. Those remain questions requiring the governing law and pertinent record.

The source credit is June 25, 1948, ch. 646, 62 Stat. 966. The notes identify former § 465 and its legislative sources, delete an obsolete provision for proceedings pending in 1934, and explain that an earlier three-month appeal provision was omitted as covered by §§ 2101 and 2107. That historical three-month provision is not part of § 2251's operative text. The recovered 1994 section contains no later counsel-related statutory subsections or special statutory stay duration. The existing packet's reliance on public McFarland law for qualifying preparation proceedings remains an interpretation of the governing statute, not a newly recovered additional paragraph of that statute.
