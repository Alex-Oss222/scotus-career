## Common premises and law entering the event

The petition and merits presentations include both original ERISA authority and ancillary enforcement authority. The latter is not the sole question. Peacock was a defendant in the first suit, but the judgment imposed no personal liability on him; he is a proposed new judgment debtor, not a person absent from that litigation. The first adjudication of nonfiduciary status is binding within its actual scope. Most conduct now relied upon postdates the underlying 1981–1985 pension wrongs; some alleged transfers precede the November 28, 1988 judgment. No commitment assumes that all transfers were postjudgment, that every disputed transfer had its face value, or that Peacock received assets equal to the whole debt.

The present judgment makes Peacock answer for the entire $187,628.93 Tru-Tech debt, with associated interest and fees, through veil piercing. It is not a severable award returning particular diverted property or limited to its value. The appellate court upheld the findings, rejected the claim-preclusion objection, affirmed fee entitlement against Peacock, and vacated the $205,182.01 **attorneys' fees and expenses** amount for recalculation. The earlier Tru-Tech judgment itself is not reopened. Finegold was not held liable and the other theories were rejected; those dispositions cannot silently become asset-recovery judgments on this review.

The operative Standards and Tests provisions are **Jurisdiction to enforce a settlement after dismissal**, present operation effective *Kokkonen*, May 16, 1994, and **ERISA equitable categories and nonfiduciary compensation**, present operation effective *Mertens*, June 1, 1993. Their entering-law wording is the current wording used here. Kokkonen separates factually interdependent claims from authority to manage proceedings and effectuate decrees; it does not make all enforcement disputes ancillary. Mertens limits §502(a)(3) to traditional equitable categories and rejects the particular compensatory nonfiduciary award, while reserving the expressly disclaimed antecedent cause of action and other equitable monetary remedies. Neither case already decides this entire controversy.

*O'Melveny* (June 13, 1994) requires a particular significant conflict before displacing the state law governing the acquired claims there; general federal financial interest or uniformity does not suffice. Exceptional federal fields survive. *Travelers* (April 26, 1995) distinguishes ordinary state-law effects from rules that actually govern plan benefits, administration, or an alternative ERISA enforcement remedy; it creates no categorical rule for all generally applicable law. The actual *Central Bank* rule permits its confined received securities action and cannot be replaced by the post-divergence historical no-aiding-and-abetting rule. Its different statutory and remedial history does not itself create an ERISA collection action.

The applicable enactments include §§1331 and 1332; ERISA §§409(a)–(b), 502(a)(2), (a)(3), (e)(1)–(2), and (g); Rule 69; and §1367 with its December 1, 1990 effective boundary. The 1987 action predates §1367. Filing the later suit in 1991 does not create an original federal claim to which supplemental jurisdiction can attach. No associate treats §1367 or Rule 69 as an independent original-jurisdiction grant.

## Issue-specific alternative-path comparison

**1. Original ERISA claim versus ordinary collection.** The strongest statutory argument treats veil piercing as identification of the existing fiduciary's legal identity, not a new Mertens compensation claim; federal alter-ego rules in contribution cases and equity's historical remedies give that characterization substance. The decisive contrary point is the action actually brought: the first court fixed Peacock's lack of fiduciary liability; the later suit imposes the whole debt from significantly later collection conduct without establishing a fresh ERISA or plan violation. Sections 409(b) and 502 cannot be bypassed by relabeling the defendant. Seven associates reject the asserted federal basis at that limited level. They do not hold that every losing federal remedy argument defeats §1331 jurisdiction, that Mertens already rejected all nonfiduciary causes, or that veil piercing can never identify an original ERISA obligor. Stevens finds it unnecessary to decide the alternative statutory ground because he sustains ancillary authority; his affirmative treatment of equitable enforcement is not a join in a new ERISA cause.

**2. New full liability versus decree enforcement.** For jurisdiction, the strongest argument is Kokkonen's independent decree-effectuation branch, together with Riggs, Labette County, creditor's bills, and the United States' argument that the debtor's controlling actor may not deliberately frustrate a federal judgment. A factual-common-nucleus requirement cannot exhaust that branch. Stevens accepts the branch for the supported alter-ego/obstruction theory. The seven accept the branch's existence but find that the present judgment goes beyond it: no existing command obliges Peacock personally to pay; the later suit adjudicates substantially different conduct and makes him liable for the entire debt. Beecher's limit is not avoided merely by proof of intentional frustration. They need not hold that every ancillary proceeding involving a third person is forbidden. Stevens's strongest response is that an alter ego is being identified rather than a genuinely independent debt created; the seven reject that characterization as sufficient by itself to confer jurisdiction over this judgment. This is a genuine legal disagreement, not a factual finding that the upheld transfers were innocent.

**3. Property recovery versus full-debt substitution.** Traditional execution, garnishment, attachment and recovery of transferred debtor assets present a materially narrower enforcement path. That path explains why restricting this judgment does not deprive the original court of every means to make its judgment effective. But the record supplies no separable asset-recovery award for this Court simply to affirm; consideration and value remain disputed, some transfers preceded judgment, and the court did not find that Peacock received the entire debt's value. All associates preserve the legal distinction. None orders automatic return of the enumerated notes, a new valuation, or reinstatement of claims the lower court rejected. A properly invoked property-recovery proceeding must establish its own legal and procedural predicates.

**4. Enforcement jurisdiction without a national substantive rule.** The United States' state-law/Rule 69 route is stronger than an all-or-nothing claim that ancillary jurisdiction necessarily establishes federal veil-piercing law. O'Melveny does not abolish exceptional federal rules; Starrett confirms a distinct federal contribution setting. Nevertheless, general uniformity and beneficiary protection do not demonstrate the necessary conflict for this postjudgment action. The Fourth Circuit's statement that South Carolina law is equivalent does not resolve the preserved dispute about its substantive application or amount. Stevens would remand for the actual governing rule and its application, without affirming liability on an unexplored state-law ground. The seven do not reach that merits choice after rejecting jurisdiction, but do not endorse the Fourth Circuit's categorical preemption premise.

**5. Personal jurisdiction and statutory service.** The nationwide-service rationale under §502(e)(2) is separate from subject-matter jurisdiction and was preserved. The government's scope objection and possible alter-ego/privity contacts supply competing arguments, not an adjudication of waiver or sufficient contacts. Stevens's ancillary route requires remand for a lawful service/personal-jurisdiction basis before renewed personal liability. The seven leave that question unresolved because their subject-matter disposition ends this action on its existing basis. No associate treats ancillary power as nationwide-service authorization, approves a Pennsylvania defendant's contacts by inference, or holds the objection forfeited.

**6. Preclusion and timing.** Neither alternative may make the original nonfiduciary finding disappear. Conversely, subsequent conduct is not automatically barred just because Peacock was a defendant earlier. The seven do not need to decide the preserved preclusion merits, and their jurisdictional disposition does not reverse the finding on a theory of identical historical conduct. Stevens may retain a later-conduct enforcement proceeding without reopening the old fiduciary-status question. No commitment uses §1367's later enactment to retroactively attach a supplemental claim to the 1987 action or manufacture original jurisdiction in the 1991 action.

## Exact remedial commitments and proposition compatibility

**O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer:** Reverse the Fourth Circuit insofar as it sustains federal jurisdiction and imposes the Tru-Tech debt on Peacock, and remand for the district court's jurisdictionally appropriate dismissal of this later action on the grounds asserted. The later personal-liability judgment and the dependent personal assessments of fees, expenses and interest cannot survive that disposition; the appellate fee-recalculation instruction cannot remain a direction to assess those sums against Peacock under the invalidated theory. This does not reinstate the already vacated $205,182.01 amount. It does not disturb the original $187,628.93 judgment against Tru-Tech or adjudicate away lawful fees or enforcement rights against that debtor. Dismissal is jurisdictional, not a merits declaration extinguishing available state-law rights or a freestanding prohibition on a properly founded collection proceeding. No state action, transfer, new pleading, or later lower-court event is invented.

**Stevens:** Sustain ancillary subject-matter jurisdiction, but vacate the present unqualified appellate affirmance of personal liability and derivative fee entitlement, and remand for resolution of the governing enforcement law and the preserved personal-jurisdiction/service ground. The existing findings retain their lawful appellate status; he does not order a fresh trial on every fact, find South Carolina law satisfied, impose the full debt again, or order immediate recovery of particular assets. Any renewed liability and monetary scope require the applicable rule and personal-jurisdiction predicates. Fees and expenses then require an independently lawful basis and the already identified objections must be addressed; ancillary jurisdiction alone creates no fee-shifting entitlement. The original Tru-Tech judgment is preserved. He opposes a mandate requiring dismissal for want of subject-matter jurisdiction.

The seven join the same limited original-basis, ancillary-power and remedial propositions. They are not joined by Stevens on those controlling grounds. All eight can support preserving the original debtor judgment, distinguishing actual asset recovery, rejecting automatic nationwide service, and accurately describing the fee/expense posture, but these shared limits do not turn the opposing remedial mandates into a unanimous judgment or a unified rationale. No opinion author or assignment is selected.
