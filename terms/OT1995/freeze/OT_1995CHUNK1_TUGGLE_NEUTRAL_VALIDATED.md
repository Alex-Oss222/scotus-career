## 1. Posture, submitted issues, participation, and relief

The judgment under review is *Tuggle v. Thompson*, 57 F.3d 1356 (4th Cir. June 29, 1995), which reversed the District Court's June 8, 1994 grant of habeas relief and directed dismissal. The relevant question is whether the Court of Appeals could sustain this capital sentence through the surviving vileness aggravator when the prosecution presented psychiatric future-dangerousness evidence and the state court had found denial of defense psychiatric assistance erroneous under *Ake v. Oklahoma*, 470 U.S. 68 (1985). Whether a cognizable, properly available *Ake* violation occurred remains contested in the current filings; neither a current concession nor this Court's final determination of that issue may be assumed.

The petition's first question includes *Ake* assistance and *Estelle v. Smith* examination-use claims in challenging the surviving-aggravator rationale. This packet principally develops *Ake* and sentence review. The petition's other questions concerning vileness instructions, voir dire, and rape sufficiency are not automatically admitted, decided, or absorbed into this issue. An exact quoted grant or broader plenary review is not supplied.

Tuggle seeks relief from the adverse habeas judgment and death sentence. The Commonwealth seeks preservation of the judgment and advances both the surviving-aggravator rationale and antecedent or alternative defenses. Review of the challenged rationale can be bounded without reinstating every ground on which the District Court granted relief. The conviction, other petition questions, any independent habeas ground, and the sentence-review component must remain distinct.

Chief Justice Stone-Zsela and Justices Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg, and Breyer are seated on the event date. No case-specific nonparticipation is established. ## 2. Trial record and state proceedings

### Documentary chronology established by the bounded appendix

The bounded respondent appendix covers printed appendix pages 13–34, represented by source PDF pages 23–33 in a double-page scan. Its OCR can interleave columns; the following describes substance rather than purporting to reproduce an exact transcript.

1. An October 13, 1983 motion requested evaluations under Virginia Code §§19.2-169.1 and 19.2-169.5 of Tuggle's present competency and mental condition at the time of the offense. It cited prior prison evaluations, a 1971 hospital evaluation, and possible mental illness or disorder. At the November 18 hearing, the court granted these examinations.
2. A separate November petition sought expenses for independent examination of scientific and physical evidence. The specified specialties were pathology, serology, ballistics, and odontology. The court permitted access to the Commonwealth's evidence but denied independent-expert authorization; counsel excepted. This particular list is not a request for a sentencing psychiatrist. Counsel's later statements that an earlier psychiatric-funding request had been made are recorded claims, not disproved merely by the contents of this separate physical-evidence petition.
3. Tuggle entered Central State Hospital on November 22. The December 1 report, signed by forensic psychiatrist Miller Ryans and clinical psychologist Arthur Centor, reported competency to stand trial and offense-time sanity. It stated that a future-dangerousness opinion had been formed but was not reported because the court had not requested it. The report also asserted that Tuggle had received Miranda warnings concerning use at a capital hearing and had signed a waiver. Those are the report's assertions. They do not establish this Court's determination of the warning's content or sufficiency, a valid waiver, counsel's advance notice, or the scope of the constitutional permission to use the examination.
4. A December 27 renewed motion sought transportation for examination by Dr. C. Robert Showalter and his group. The written motion described offense-time condition, present capacity to understand proceedings, and assistance in defending the trial. It asserted that counsel had previously requested funds and had now arranged an independent examination. At the January 3, 1984 hearing, counsel referred to prior psychiatric records, the capital charge, the original independent-evaluation request, and arrangements made after funding had been denied. The Commonwealth answered that the supplied psychiatrist and psychologist evaluations met its obligation and that further examination was unnecessary. The judge treated the earlier evaluation as complete and sufficient and denied further evaluation, expressly including the pending request.

These pages establish the requests they contain and the denial they record. They do not exhaust every motion, hearing, objection, consultation, or expert service in the complete trial record. They also do not decide whether the requests and the State's later use of psychiatric future-danger evidence together satisfied *Ake*. A personally preferred second evaluator and meaningful assistance with constitutionally relevant defense functions are separate propositions. Two examiners do not, by that number alone, establish adequate defense assistance; absence of the phrase “future dangerousness” from a request does not, by itself, decide the constitutional application.

### State-court history and the existing Ake determination

At the penalty stage Dr. Centor testified for the Commonwealth that Tuggle presented a high probability of future dangerousness. The jury found both future dangerousness and vileness and selected death. A valid aggravator permitted death; it did not require the jury to choose death rather than life. The distinction between eligibility and actual sentence selection is therefore material.

On direct appeal, *Tuggle v. Commonwealth*, 228 Va. 493, 503 (1984), affirmed. It noted that a need to rebut future dangerousness had not been advanced in the written motion or oral argument, cited Rule 5:21, and treated a further evaluation as a matter of trial-court discretion. In 1985, this Court vacated and remanded for consideration in light of *Ake*. *Tuggle v. Virginia*, 471 U.S. 1096. These are pre-divergence events.

On the 1985 reconsideration, the Attorney General stated in briefing and oral argument that the trial court had erred at the penalty stage and that resentencing was required. The Virginia Supreme Court held that Tuggle had not made *Ake*'s threshold showing for guilt-stage assistance. It nevertheless affirmatively held that *Ake* required the conclusion that denying him an independent psychiatrist was error once the Commonwealth presented psychiatric evidence of future dangerousness. Its footnotes noted that the request concerned sanity and competency rather than rebuttal of future danger and that the objection to Dr. Centor's testimony asserted the absence of counsel during the examination rather than denial of an expert. Rejecting the proposed resentencing remedy, it held that the error did not impair or invalidate the distinct vileness finding and reaffirmed the conviction and death sentence under its reading of *Zant v. Stephens*. *Tuggle v. Commonwealth*, 230 Va. 99 (1985).

Thus, the existing state history is not a history in which every court left the *Ake* issue wholly undecided. The 1985 state court made the identified affirmative constitutional ruling despite its stated request limitations. That ruling and its procedural significance must be confronted. The effect of that ruling upon the Commonwealth's present arguments is a legal question; this packet neither erases it nor declares every alternative defense conclusively foreclosed.

## 3. Federal judgments and present disputes

The District Court granted habeas relief on June 8, 1994. On the component developed here, it found denial of psychiatric rebuttal assistance and prejudicial spillover from the future-dangerousness evidence; it rejected the view that the surviving vileness finding alone resolved the sentence's validity.

The Fourth Circuit reversed on June 29, 1995 and directed dismissal. Its discussion at 57 F.3d 1361–1364 treated Virginia as a nonweighing State, relied on the separate vileness finding, and rejected the District Court's contrary *Zant* analysis. It followed *Smith v. Procunier*, 769 F.2d 170 (4th Cir. 1985), for rejection of the asserted psychiatric-evidence spillover where independent vileness evidence supported death. Its quoted *Smith* account concerns Smith's crime; none of those crime details is a factual finding about Tuggle. The appellate court did not revisit the state court's *Ake* determination and did not make a separate whole-record constitutional harmlessness assessment of lost examination, preparation, cross-examination, rebuttal, or mitigation functions. Its treatment of future dangerousness as removed from the legal case does not mean that the sentencing jury never heard that evidence.

The lower opinion quotes historical post-divergence Supreme Court language, including *Stringer*. ### Concession withdrawal and properly available defenses

### Controlling simulated holdings and limits

**Brecht — “Chapman governs this preserved Doyle claim on habeas” (April 21, 1993).** For preserved *Doyle* trial error properly available on habeas, the State must establish beyond a reasonable doubt, on the whole record, that it did not contribute to the verdict. Collateral posture alone does not replace that burden with *Kotteakos*. White's Parts II–III control through direct joins by Stone-Zsela, Blackmun, O'Connor, and Souter. The unanimous Part I identifies constitutional trial error, distinct from structural error. Other habeas gates remain independent. No general constitutional-habeas *Kotteakos* rule or general *Chapman* rule for every reserved category was adopted.

**O'Neal — “Chapman governs the preserved personal-intent instructional claim” and “The proper standard requires renewed review, not an automatic writ” (February 21, 1995).** For a properly available preserved constitutional trial violation in which the combined charge and prosecution argument permit conviction without proof of the defendant's own required intent, the State bears *Chapman*'s whole-record burden beyond a reasonable doubt. O'Connor's Parts II and IV, joined by Stone-Zsela, Souter, Ginsburg, and Breyer, control. The Court corrected the appellate standard without finding the assumed violation or actual harmlessness and without automatically reinstating the earlier writ. An available violation and failure of the State's required showing are conditions of eventual relief. O'Connor's broader Part III, joined by Stone-Zsela and Souter, proposes a general extension but does not control. Ginsburg and Breyer reserved that extension. Neither a general *Chapman* rule for all constitutional trial errors nor a constitutional-habeas *Kotteakos* rule governs merely by aggregation of distinct positions. The standard for this *Ake* category requires a fresh decision or an express reservation.

**Capital weighing correction.** *Stringer*, March 9, 1992, “What correction a constitutionally invalid aggravator requires in a capital weighing system,” and *Richmond*, December 1, 1992, “An actual cure is necessary,” require an actual authorized cure when a capital sentencer weighs an unconstitutional factor. Remaining factors alone do not suffice; lawful reweighing and constitutional harmlessness are distinct routes. Kennedy authored the controlling *Stringer* opinion, joined by Stone-Zsela, White, Blackmun, Stevens, and O'Connor. O'Connor authored *Richmond*, joined by Stone-Zsela, White, Blackmun, Stevens, Kennedy, Souter, and Thomas. Virginia's nonweighing character is material. These holdings do not automatically establish the collateral standard for an independent assistance error in Virginia. They also do not convert eligibility into a finding about the effect of impaired rebuttal on sentence selection.

**Simmons — “Parole ineligibility must be available to answer the State's future-danger case” (June 17, 1994).** When the State raises future danger and the only lawful noncapital alternative is life without ordinary parole, due process requires an effective opportunity to inform the jury of that fact by accurate presentation or an adequate instruction. Souter's Part I has direct joins from Stone-Zsela, Blackmun, Stevens, O'Connor, Kennedy, and Ginsburg. Its no-fixed-script, ordinary-parole, and sentencing-alternative limits remain. The direct-review, record-specific *Chapman* and new-penalty-proceeding holding in Part II has Stone-Zsela, Blackmun, Stevens, and Ginsburg joining Souter; O'Connor and Kennedy reserve the further remedial issue. This opportunity-to-answer analogy does not establish automatic harmlessness, automatic resentencing, or the collateral standard for *Ake*.

**Romano — “Another jury's death decision cannot displace the present jury's responsibility” and “Earlier aggravator reweighing does not resolve the responsibility error” (June 13, 1994).** Souter's controlling portions, joined by Stone-Zsela, Blackmun, Stevens, and Ginsburg, distinguish an independently actionable distortion of the sentencing jury's responsibility from the invalid-aggravator correction previously performed. The contextual responsibility rule requires more than speculative influence and preserves independently admissible prior-conduct evidence. No majority selected between a mandatory new penalty hearing and an initial constitutional harmlessness inquiry. It supplies neither a general constitutional evidence code nor an already-decided *Ake* remedy.

**Presentation and nonretroactivity.** *Schiro*, January 19, 1994, “Late presentation of nonretroactivity,” makes *Teague* nonjurisdictional and permits declining a defense omitted below and in the opposition and first raised at merits briefing; it creates no automatic universal forfeiture. *Caspari*, February 23, 1994, “The preserved nonretroactivity defense is fairly included here,” permits the specifically preserved same-rule subsidiary defense, without making every independent omitted question reviewable. Any relevant *Teague* inquiry requires the actual finality date, presentation, and precise rule, with both contemporary exceptions retained. Neither an unverified finality date nor a generic mention of collateral review supplies a bar here.

## 5. Live decisional paths and opposing arguments

These are alternatives for independent modeling, not recommended dispositions.

**A. Bounded review of the Court of Appeals' rationale on an express assumption.** The Court could assume, solely for reviewing that rationale, a properly available *Ake* violation and decide whether the surviving-aggravator treatment suffices. *Zant*'s admissibility and rebuttal premises and *Ake*'s defense functions support distinguishing legally sufficient eligibility from the effect of evidence and impaired assistance on the choice of death. Any resulting remand must identify whether the Court adopts an error-effect standard or leaves it open and must preserve genuinely available defenses without wiping away the existing state ruling. The strongest objection is that an antecedent request, adequacy, or default problem may support the judgment independently, making summary intervention premature. The assumption addresses the narrow legal question; it does not answer that objection by finding the antecedent issue for either party.

**B. Resolve a properly available antecedent or alternative ground.** With sufficient legal and record support, a court could address whether constitutionally adequate assistance was supplied, whether the actual requests implicated *Ake*, or whether a valid procedural defense prevents relief. The Commonwealth's case stresses the existing examinations, request language, objection history, and limits on choosing an expert. The strongest counterargument is the 1985 court's affirmative penalty-stage *Ake* ruling, combined with the distinction between an evaluation for sanity or competency and assistance in confronting later future-danger testimony. This path needs the actual prior ruling's legal effect and complete relevant record; the bounded appendix alone does not establish a comprehensive no-request or default premise. An assumption-based ruling cannot silently become a final no-error holding.

**C. Decide the error-effect standard or reserve it.** If an available violation requires error-effect review, extension of *Chapman* can draw on the actual reasoning of *Brecht*, *O'Neal*, and the practical accuracy concern of *Ake*. The contrary argument emphasizes *O'Neal*'s deliberately category-specific holding, independent collateral interests, and the difference between lost assistance and the trial-error categories already decided. *Kotteakos* alternatives are supported by the relevant prior separate positions, not by an already-controlling universal collateral rule. An opinion choosing either standard must give a fresh reason, state its trigger and burden, and explain the category's scope. No path may treat *O'Neal* Part III as Court law.

**D. Petition management.** Denial or plenary consideration remains a procedurally available response to the petition and identified vehicle or record issues. A denial creates no merits holding, does not imply agreement with the Fourth Circuit's rationale, and discloses no unannounced poll. A justified summary merits disposition must explain its bounded legal ground and remedy. The packet does not establish the need for plenary review merely because the complete record would be necessary for a broader final factual determination.

Under any error-effect rule, legally valid aggravation and other relevant prosecution evidence remain part of the actual record; they may not be ignored. Neither may the inquiry reconstruct favorable psychiatric testimony without evidentiary support. The functions potentially lost include relevant examination, preparation, expert rebuttal, assistance in cross-examination, and mitigation where supported. Their relevance is not a factual finding that any hypothetical expert would have changed the sentence.
