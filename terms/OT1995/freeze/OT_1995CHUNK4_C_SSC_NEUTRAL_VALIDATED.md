## Material record and operative terms

Smithtown purchases residential collection and disposal services in its improvement districts. SSC held seven of ten district contracts beginning January 1992 and separately served commercial customers. The contracts required delivery to the designated incinerator. The improvement districts' existence and the Town's authority to contract were not challenged. The condition applies to the Town-paid residential service; its validity does not automatically decide the Town's compulsory rule for other waste.

The facility is owned by Ogden/CE Huntington Limited Partnership, a Delaware limited partnership, on Huntington-owned land. Smithtown and Huntington committed to long-term fees, obtained exclusive disposal rights, and sold disposal capacity. Public financing, guarantees, land ownership, and the Town's financial stake do not make Ogden's facility a government-owned plant. Do not substitute a later public-facility case or an assumed public ownership finding.

The reported contract price included disposal costs as well as collection; the Town's payment to the hauler included a tipping-fee allowance. The approximate 1994 annual price was $218 per household, comprising $126 collection and $92 disposal. The hauler bore the cost of excess tonnage and retained the benefit of a lower volume. The lower opinion identifies reasons for this arrangement, including discouraging inclusion of outside waste and encouraging recycling. It does not find that SSC committed diversion or breach. The Town withheld more than $750,000 after alleged diversion; counterclaim liability and the amount due remain for ordinary proceedings.

Code §177-17(B), reproduced at 66 F.3d 507, provides: “No person authorized to collect or transport acceptable waste within the Town of Smithtown shall dispose of acceptable waste generated within the Town of Smithtown except at a solid waste management facility designated by the Town Board pursuant to this section.” The ordinance includes commercial and other accepted waste beyond the Town's purchased residential service. The opinion reports that the definition excludes recyclable and unacceptable waste. Section 177-102 authorizes misdemeanor sanctions, a fine up to $5,000, and imprisonment up to 60 days. These are attributed quotations and descriptions of the code in the full lower opinion; a complete independent Town code edition has not been verified.

The bid specification, quoted in the lower opinion's footnote 14, requires each bid to include the estimated disposal fees calculated from expected volume and the identified tipping rate. Contract terms, as reported, require delivery to the designated facility. No complete signed contract or finding resolving a disputed particular clause is supplied. A decision turning on an additional clause, an omitted exception, actual breach, or a particular payment accounting requires that record before disposition.

## Competing positions and lawful paths

SSC's strongest contract position is that collection and disposal are distinct markets, and the destination condition uses public purchasing in one to restrict later activity in another. South-Central Timber's downstream limitation and the practical restraint on private processors support that claim. Integration with the ordinance, the private operator's benefit, and the Town's own financing commitment cannot be disregarded merely because the same direction also appears in a contract. The fact that a private buyer can make a contract is relevant but does not by itself settle the public actor's constitutional capacity.

The Town's strongest response is that it buys a continuing, combined collection-and-disposal service with its own money, pays the disposal component through the hauler, and retains an interest in completion of that purchased service. Under White, formal privity with every participant is not an absolute boundary; a genuine purchaser may specify the performance it buys. This is distinguishable from a seller controlling a new processing transaction after transfer of its commodity. The Town's CERCLA argument concerns a continuing interest in proper disposal; it does not establish actual CERCLA liability in this record.

On the ordinance, SSC invokes Carbone's rule against compulsory routing to a designated local private processor and available neutral financing methods. The Town invokes its proprietary stake and the ordinance's connection to its purchased disposal capacity. The sovereign command and criminal penalties reach beyond voluntary purchase contracts, and the lower court treated that reach as regulation. Any different treatment must explain the distinction between market participation and the power exercised over independently arranged waste. Health, sanitation, cost recovery, and financing labels do not independently satisfy the discrimination justification burden.

Supported paths include sustaining the contract as part of the service actually purchased while invalidating the broader ordinance; treating the contract as an impermissible downstream restriction as well as invalidating the ordinance; or a narrower remand if a material fact about the purchased service or enforceable reach is genuinely unresolved. Any path sustaining the ordinance must supply a supported distinction from controlling Carbone and address its compulsory reach. These are competing legal paths, not assigned votes. A contract holding does not determine breach, damages, antitrust immunity, every subcontract, or general immunity for government contracts. A constitutional judgment alone does not convert a disputed counterclaim into a money judgment.

This is an NLRA preemption holding about genuine project purchases; it does not automatically resolve the dormant Commerce Clause question.

Fulton, February 21, 1996, supplies the current-term application through the neutral projection. Neither authority creates a general financing exception to discrimination.

The full pre-divergence opinions, all writings and footnotes, were read in official United States Reports PDFs:

* White v. Massachusetts Council, 460 U.S. 204 (1983), especially 208–214 and footnote 7: paid city projects, the extent of the city's participation, and no inflexible privity limit. Stevens and O'Connor joined Rehnquist's Court opinion. The particular federal-grant authorization holding does not establish congressional authorization for Smithtown. Its reservation of a Privileges and Immunities claim cannot decide a claim absent here.
* South-Central Timber v. Wunnicke, 467 U.S. 82 (1984), especially 93–99: White's Parts III–IV concerning downstream processing have only Brennan, Marshall, and Stevens joining him. They are a plurality, not a five-Justice rule. Powell and Burger concurred in part and would remand the participation question. Rehnquist, joined by O'Connor, dissented and characterized the condition as an ordinary proprietary sale term akin to a subsidy and White. Parts I–II have six votes on unmistakable congressional authorization; do not transfer that majority to Parts III–IV.
* Reeves v. Stake, 447 U.S. 429 (1980), especially 436–447 and Powell's dissent 447–454 joined by Stevens: majority protection for the State's sale of its own manufactured product, with natural-resource and broader restrictions not decided; the dissent distinguishes procurement for government needs from protection of private in-state customers. Stevens's later White join is also material and must be addressed instead of treating the earlier dissent as his immutable position.
