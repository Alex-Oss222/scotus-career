The sentencing jury answered the statutory questions about deliberate conduct with a reasonable expectation that death would result, and probability of future criminal violence constituting a continuing threat to society. Both answers were affirmative. The actual full charge and full testimony have not been independently retrieved here; the published lower opinion documents the requested supplemental mitigation instruction.

The lower court's parole analysis distinguishes legal ineligibility from a prediction of eventual release and rejects both the due-process and related mitigating-evidence claims because Texas made ordinary parole available. Its footnote separately treats an extension to factual parole improbability as a new rule under Teague. It also describes future danger to inmates and treats a challenge to the word 'would' as another formulation of the parole claim. These lower-court propositions are review subjects; its description of post-divergence Supreme Court authority is not imported.

For the father's admitted testimony, the lower opinion expressly identifies alleged mental illness and abuse during an earlier imprisonment, describes the father as a nonexpert, and says the defense requested an instruction allowing mitigating effect beyond the special issues. It rejects that claim using a requirement that the crime be attributable to a uniquely severe disability; the opinion says the nonexpert hearsay testimony failed to show that linkage. This is the stated appellate ground, not an independent finding that no abuse occurred or that no mitigating evidence was offered. The significance of the lay evidence under actual entering law remains for adjudication.

The separate deterrence claim asserted that presenting medical evidence would encourage an affirmative future-danger answer. The lower court rejects a Penry instruction claim based on evidence that could have been but was not offered, quoting the no-evidence instruction principle. No omitted medical diagnosis is established as trial evidence. The supplied review remains limited to parole information and special-issue mitigation; Brady, lesser offenses and a new ineffective-assistance claim remain outside it.
