## Explicit new neutral branch

**Whether IGRA assigns Article III courts an impermissibly advisory or executive-dependent function is a materially briefed, distinct question.** The National Governors' Association and allied state/local organizations urge dismissal on that ground independently of immunity. The Tribe contests the premise and argues that the judgment conclusively establishes legally effective rights and duties even though additional statutory steps determine the eventual gaming arrangement. The new branch must be considered before a model treats abrogation or Young as sufficient to permit this action.

The disagreement is not resolved by observing either that there is no immediate judicially compelled compact or that the Secretary cannot simply change a bad-faith finding. The amici expressly anticipate binding component findings and contend that the **judgment granting relief** must itself vindicate the asserted interest. The Tribe answers that a binding judgment opening the statutory alternative process and protecting the adjudicated legal determinations supplies real, partial relief. Each side disputes the other's description of what the judicial decision accomplishes.

The actual legal effects of the particular judgment, the source and extent of further relief, and whether subsequent discretion implements or revises that judgment therefore require separate analysis. The present immunity appeal establishes neither a favorable merits judgment for the Tribe nor the preclusive effect of one.

## The Governors' strongest Article III position

The amici begin with the constitutional limitation on assigning nonjudicial tasks to Article III courts. They invoke Hayburn's Case and Muskrat and distinguish two related requirements: a court must be able to give a conclusive, judicially enforceable judgment vindicating the plaintiff's legal interest; and relief cannot depend on another branch's subsequent discretionary choice whether to honor the adjudication. They cite Gordon, Sanborn, Brimson, Ferreira, Chicago & Southern Air Lines, and other pre-divergence authorities. The Tidewater quotation is identified in their brief as a dissent, not a holding. Their reliance on historical Plaut receives separate treatment below. [Governors' Br. pp.7–10, safe lines 535–674.]

Their application addresses both possible descriptions of the Tribe's interest:

1. If the interest is good-faith negotiation, they argue that IGRA specifies an order to **conclude a compact**, rather than a separately enforceable bargaining injunction. Failure to conclude leads to mediation, not contempt. Thus, on their reading, the court cannot itself compel fulfillment of the claimed negotiating right.
2. If the interest is obtaining a compact or gaming authorization, the court cannot impose agreement. A mediator selects a proposal, the State may withhold consent, and the Secretary ultimately prescribes procedures. The amici describe that final action as substantially discretionary and maintain that the earlier court order does not itself deliver the claimed relief.

They expressly argue that the second defect exists **even if** the executive must follow judicial findings of fact and conclusions of law: in their view, binding findings are insufficient when the relief awarded by the judgment still depends on nonministerial executive action. A neutral account that answers only “the Secretary cannot reconsider bad faith” would omit this strongest version of their objection. Conversely, “the court plays no further role” is their construction of the immediate tribal action, not statutory text establishing that federal courts can never act again. [Pp.10–12, safe lines 665–788.]

The amici reject three proposed answers. First, they argue that IGRA's specific remedy occupies the remedial field and excludes use of 28 U.S.C. §2201 to obtain broader relief. They acknowledge that the complaint invokes §2201 but describe its omission from the earlier merits arguments; that account must not replace the government's actual proposal that a declaration may suffice. Second, they distinguish Aetna and Nashville because a qualifying declaratory judgment is conclusive, not administratively revisable, and may support further relief under §2202. They argue that Steffel does not authorize a declaration that the defendant can disregard without judicial consequence. Third, they distinguish labor-law bargaining orders because those injunctions are enforceable by contempt. [Pp.12–14, nn.7–8, safe lines 790–920.]

Finally, they oppose curing the asserted problem by expanding the court's remedies. They argue that Congress selected limited relief as part of IGRA's federal/state/tribal compromise, and that coercing an exercise of state legislative authority would raise the distinct New York problem the limited scheme sought to avoid. This is an adversarial statutory-construction and constitutional-avoidance argument, not an independently established legislative finding about Congress's exact constitutional purpose. [Pp.14–15, safe lines 883–945.]

## The Tribe's strongest response and its qualifications

The Tribe argues that a final judgment on permitted gaming and the State's failure to negotiate in good faith is itself meaningful relief. It would establish the legal foundation for the statutory alternative process and **could** prevent Florida from using the compacting process to block gaming. The Tribe invokes res judicata and collateral estoppel, citing Parklane, as protection against later attacks on adjudicated matters. It argues that the method through which ultimate gaming rights develop does not make the preceding final determination advisory. [Reply pp.13–14, safe lines 592–637.]

This is an argument about the legal effect of a future favorable judgment, not proof that the Tribe already has one. Its strongest support is the distinction between conclusively deciding the federal legal dispute and supplying every later regulatory term. It does not require pretending that the court immediately awards a compact, chooses the games, or licenses gaming.

The Tribe then distinguishes Hayburn, Ferreira, and Chicago & Southern Air Lines as arrangements leaving executive officers free to review or determine the effect of the judicial product. Under its reading of IGRA, the Secretary has a later statutory task but cannot revise the court's determination that the State failed to negotiate in good faith. The mediator's proposal and the statutory conditions constrain the Secretary's work. The reply expressly maintains that Congress may supply a **partial judicial remedy** aiding later governmental action if the judgment itself is not subject to executive alteration. [Reply pp.14–17, safe lines 638–754.]

The reply's extended Brimson passage is explicitly a **paraphrase with substituted parties and duties**, not a verbatim holding about Indian gaming. It invokes the proposition that an adjudication may provide a conclusive basis for action while assisting an agency's lawful work. The competing amici position—that the judgment must itself award relief, not merely settle component findings—must still be answered. Neither the paraphrase nor a citation to finality alone establishes the precise legal consequences of this IGRA judgment.

The following limits prevent the Tribe's argument from becoming an invented holding:

- No automatic preclusion is established. Any later preclusion inquiry requires a valid, sufficiently final judgment, the actual claim or issue adjudicated, the necessary party relationship, and applicable litigation, necessity, and fair-opportunity limits. A judgment on good faith does not automatically decide the validity of every future compact, every Secretarial procedure, or later gaming conduct.
- Binding the parties on issues actually adjudicated is distinct from treating the Secretary, a mediator, or every future claimant as a party bound on every issue. The statutory constraints provide their own support; party-based preclusion must not be assumed to supply all of it.
- The favorable Tribe judgment discussed in the reply is hypothetical. The sourced record instead includes a separate merits judgment for Florida and the Governor, with its appeal stayed. Removing an immunity bar would not itself reverse that judgment or create a bad-faith finding, gaming-scope ruling, or preclusion favorable to the Tribe.
- The State's loss of influence in the statutory process does not automatically establish authority for every game the Tribe proposes. The statute's gaming eligibility and remedial conditions continue to apply.

## Check the statutory effects before characterizing the judgment

The official text supplies concrete constraints relevant to both accounts:

These features support a concrete debate over legal effect. The mandatory progression and the mediator's obligation to respect the court's findings support the Tribe's account of a consequential judgment. The amici answer that the progression nevertheless does not itself furnish judicially enforceable fulfillment of the asserted right and leaves the ultimate arrangement to nonjudicial discretion. The existence of later administrative discretion is not, without analysis, executive revision of a judgment; the absence of power to compel compact agreement is not, without analysis, the absence of any binding legal relief. Decide neither proposition by vocabulary alone.

## What “disregard” and “no contempt” actually establish

The Governors attribute to both the Tribe and the United States agreement that the judiciary cannot award enforceable concrete relief. Direct checking narrows that claimed agreement. Petitioner's p.26 says the State can disregard an order to mediate **if it is willing to give up the limited regulatory role Congress affords through the compact process**. Its p.29 and n.12 maintain that an order directing submission to mediation is available under Young as prospective or ancillary relief. The same passages call good faith a mandatory duty and dispute the lower court's inference that alternative procedures make it discretionary. They do not concede that all judicial findings and legal consequences are advisory or nonbinding.

Petitioner's p.26 n.9 goes further than the United States by asserting that nonparticipation would cause the Tribe's proposal to be selected. That remains a **party construction**. The United States says “presumably”; the enacted selection criterion remains the law. Neither source establishes an automatic default victory or a dispensation from federal-law and state-law constraints.

The United States' p.25 says there is no indication that failure to agree or participate during the court-ordered sixty-day period exposes the State to a more intrusive order or contempt; it treats the statutory progression as the consequence. Its pp.15–19 nevertheless support prospective determination and relief through that sequence, including a declaration that might suffice. The no-contempt limit is not a concession of the Governors' Article III conclusion. Nor may that conclusion be avoided by silently adding coercive remedies the government disclaimed; the State-directed passage does not independently resolve every possible sanction against an officer. [See the immutable United States source addendum.]

Florida's **own** p.6 jurisdictional note conditionally treats the Governors' argument as deserving attention **if** the statutory judicial role is nonjudicial and unenforceable. Florida immediately states that it views IGRA as “far more coercive,” and describes the prospective force of a federal construction of gaming law on negotiations and the Secretary. The Tribe quotes the latter passage in reply n.7. Thus, the exact position is conditional openness to the amici objection coupled with a different view of IGRA's effects—not an unqualified Florida concession that the judgment is ineffectual, and not a complete refusal by Florida to entertain Article III concerns. Florida's prediction about what the Secretary would consider binding is advocacy, not an administrative finding.

## Additional completeness from the full Governors' submission

The full source also sharpens two existing immunity alternatives without resolving them. Its Young opposition has distinct grounds: the asserted absence of federal necessity for an unenforceable order, and the asserted exceptional intrusion of requiring affirmative state lawmaking. These are proposed limitations grounded by the amici in Young/Green/Pennhurst and their New York analogy, not a controlling free-form balance already adopted for IGRA. The Tribe and United States counter with the mandatory federal good-faith standard, limited statutory consequences, and distinction between negotiation duties and chosen compact terms.

The Governors also dispute the **clear-statement** premise, notwithstanding the lower court's agreement with the Tribe on intent. They acknowledge IGRA's repeated State references but argue that Congress could have assumed suits would proceed against officers under Young without considering direct-State abrogation; they invoke the asserted absence of legislative discussion of immunity. That is a separately briefed, narrower alternative to deciding constitutional power. The opposing argument is the combined enacted jurisdiction, burden, and order language directed to the State, as set out in the packet and United States addendum. Neither the amici's conjecture about Congress's assumptions nor its characterization of the legislative record is a finding. The whole-statute clear-statement inquiry remains necessary before adopting that branch. [Governors' Br. pp.19–22.]

Their further Indian/interstate mutuality distinction and request to overrule Union Gas reinforce alternatives already identified. Those positions do not make Union Gas's inherited constitutional conclusion disappear, and historical post-divergence citations to other cases cannot substitute for actual simulated holdings. No new government consent, general federal enforcement cause, or immunity waiver follows from the observation that the United States can in appropriate circumstances sue States.

## Alternatives and remedial consequences to preserve

- **Reject the Article III objection at the scheme's actual scope:** Explain the binding judgment and concrete legal consequences that supply partial relief, the statutory limits on later actors, and why later discretion is implementation rather than revision. Leave the separate immunity and underlying merits questions for their proper resolution. Do not confer blanket preclusion or immediate gaming rights.
- **Accept the objection as applied to this cause and proposed remedy:** Identify whether the defect is absence of a judicially effective judgment, impermissible executive dependence/revision, or both, and trace that conclusion to the actual statutory provisions and inherited Article III authority. State the lawful jurisdictional disposition and what remains of the statutory scheme. Do not silently invalidate all IGRA.
- **Adopt a narrower lawful construction or preserve an unready issue:** Any declaration, prospective order, or further proceeding needs a lawful source and compatibility with the enacted sequence. The opponents' best argument about remedial exclusivity and statutory compromise must be addressed. Constitutional avoidance supplies no power to create an omitted federal program or command agreement on particular terms.

Section 2721 expressly preserves remaining provisions if a provision is invalid. That does not decide whether the court-dependent triggers for mediation and Secretarial procedures can be satisfied after jurisdictional dismissal, authorize bypassing them, or adjudicate an unpresented administrative program. If the Article III branch forecloses this action, any claimed surviving route requires its own statutory and constitutional support. If it does not, the stayed separate merits appeal and the absence of any present bad-faith finding remain.
