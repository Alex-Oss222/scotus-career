## What the opinion actually decided

Massachusetts required a physician seeking or renewing registration to agree not to charge covered Medicare beneficiaries more than Medicare's reasonable charge. The physicians challenged the balance-billing ban under the Supremacy Clause and, separately, under substantive due process. The First Circuit affirmed rejection of both challenges.

The federal statute allowed payment on an itemized bill or on an assignment under which the reasonable charge was the full charge. The court considered the physicians' argument that this enacted choice preserved an option to balance bill against state regulation. It also considered original legislative statements permitting additional private charges, a later account of the Medicare compromise, and Congress's later rejection of a federal balance-billing ban. The court found these materials insufficient to establish protection against state regulation, rather than a decision merely to leave the existing practice undisturbed at the federal level. Its assessment of the failed later amendment was expressly cautious; this addendum does not convert that material into conclusive original legislative intent.

The opinion's central distinction is explicit:

> To prevail, MMS must show that Congress intended to create an “option” in the strong sense of that word: that Congress intended to create a legal right to balance bill, a right immune from significant state interference. MMS cannot win by showing only that Congress failed to disturb a preexisting legal status quo that happened to permit doctors to balance bill.

That distinction is not a general holding that optional federal authority can never preempt a prohibition. Nor is it a demand that Congress always use the word “right.” Part I-A looks for that word **or other language suggesting creation of a protected right**, and Part I-C recognizes several affirmative authorizations as protected options. The relevant question is what Congress actually established in the particular statutory scheme, not whether its grant can be characterized verbally as permission rather than command.

## Obstacle preemption and the strongest competing readings

The opening discussion recognizes express preemption, field preemption, physical-impossibility conflict, and state laws that obstruct Congress's purposes and objectives. It expressly identifies the actual dispute as the last category: the physicians did not claim conflicting legal obligations. The opinion therefore cannot accurately support a physical-impossibility-only position in Barnett.

Part I-C accepts that a State cannot defeat an affirmative option that federal law protects. It distinguishes Lawrence County, Michigan Canners, de la Cuesta, Franklin National Bank, and the other cited option cases on that basis. In particular, it describes Franklin as **reading** the Federal Reserve Act to confer “the right of a national bank” to act as a savings bank. Its treatment of de la Cuesta notes the federal regulator's express exclusivity and conflict language. These passages illustrate evidence of a protected option; they do not state that every future federal authorization must reproduce that regulator's language to preempt state law.

Part I-B also considers the broader practical-obstacle claim: even apart from a generally protected billing option, the Massachusetts restriction allegedly would frustrate Medicare's purpose of ensuring access to care. The court examined both the asserted nationwide affordability/access balance and the actual Massachusetts evidence. It discussed testimony predicting physician departures or refusals alongside high existing Blue Shield participation under a comparable restriction, widespread Medicare assignment billing, substantial existing beneficiary protection, and physician-supply evidence. It found the claimed obstruction unproved. This reasoning is case-specific; it neither declares all price restrictions valid nor requires every obstacle-preemption claim to identify a separately worded individual “right.”

The opinion also gives weight to the traditional state role in medical-fee regulation and Medicare's stated limitation of federal intrusion. Those are features of its statutory setting. The source cannot establish, without analysis of § 92 and the national-bank precedents, the precise presumption or result in the distinct Barnett dispute.
