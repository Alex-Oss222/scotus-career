## Reconciled common rule and explanation

O'Connor, Kennedy, Souter, Ginsburg and Breyer support the following complete ground and applications:

1. Determine whether Congress intended the proceedings under §§881(a)(6), 881(a)(7) and 981(a)(1)(A) to be civil. The incorporation of customs-law in-rem procedures, civil enforcement mechanisms, notice and claim procedures, and the statutory burden arrangements support that intent. Legislative labeling matters but does not end the inquiry.
2. The clearest proof that the statutory forfeiture scheme is so punitive in purpose or effect as to be criminal can overcome civil intent. Neither an in-rem label nor a property fiction supplies irrebuttable immunity. Relevant considerations must be assessed at their real significance; they are not individually conclusive or a newly mandatory exhaustive checklist.
3. These schemes and the grounds established in these cases do not satisfy that showing. Removal of property used to facilitate crime, discouragement of illegal use and disgorgement of actual unlawful proceeds supply substantial nonpunitive functions. Deterrence, connection to a criminal violation and owner defenses do not alone transform the proceeding into criminal punishment. An offense predicate establishing prohibited property use differs from requiring the Government to prove that a particular claimant personally committed the crime. The State's or Government's ability to seek forfeiture does not itself establish a specific property's nexus, provenance or final statutory liability.
4. Halper's exceptional accounting method concerns a fixed in-personam civil penalty tied to governmental loss. It is not the method for weighing the value of every forfeited asset against enforcement costs, because forfeiture's nonpunitive functions are not confined to compensation of governmental expense. Halper remains law within its scope; Kurth Ranch's combined-operation inquiry and the forfeiture clearest-proof exception remain available within theirs. No categorical holding that every civil exaction is lawful follows.
5. Austin's Excessive Fines coverage survives. A statutory forfeiture may require review under that Clause without constituting a second criminal punishment under the Double Jeopardy Clause. Austin's partly-punitive coverage inquiry therefore does not automatically answer this different constitutional question. Neither excessive-fines validity nor an exclusive excessiveness test is decided here.

The Court-ground commitment reverses the Sixth and Ninth Circuit double-jeopardy grounds and remands for their properly remaining appellate matters. Ursery's other criminal appellate objections were not decided below; Currency's probable-cause, excessive-fines and limited in-rem-jurisdiction objections likewise were left unresolved. The Supreme Court does not decide them, automatically transfer title, establish every item as proceeds or order a new sentence calculation.

The common opinion reserves attachment of jeopardy in a punitive civil proceeding, same-offense analysis under actual Blockburger/Grady, and the Government's coordinated-proceeding theory as unnecessary. It makes no settlement waiver finding. The Sixth Circuit's Rule12(f) treatment and the actual dates remain intact as record matters. The earlier filing of the civil complaint or indictment alone is not treated as attachment of criminal jeopardy.
