**Lujan**

**Current rule:** A plaintiff invoking federal jurisdiction must establish a concrete and particularized, actual or imminent injury; a causal connection fairly traceable to the defendant's challenged conduct rather than to independent third parties' own decisions; and a likelihood, not mere speculation, that the requested relief will redress the injury. Authorization for any person to sue does not supply standing for only the public's undifferentiated interest in lawful administration. A procedural right must protect the plaintiff's own threatened concrete interest; when that connection exists, the plaintiff need not prove that the required procedure will certainly alter the ultimate agency decision. Aesthetic, recreational or professional wildlife interests may qualify, but past visits and indefinite return intentions without concrete plans or another supported basis for imminent renewed exposure do not establish threatened interference with personal use. At summary judgment the plaintiff must support challenged standing elements with specific facts by affidavit or other evidence, with favorable reasonable inferences available; allegations cannot replace missing evidence.

**Authority by component:**
- Stage-specific evidentiary requirement and existing injury, traceability and redressability elements: [Lujan v. Defenders of Wildlife, “1. Article III elements must be supported at the litigation stage”](https://github.com/Alex-Oss222/scotus-career/blob/87e62b7612c15409039d524fd66f743d0ad75e2a/terms/OT1991/records/Lujan_v_Defenders_of_Wildlife_merits_1992-06-12.md), June 12, 1992; Scalia Part II, joined by White, Kennedy, Souter and Thomas (five).
- Prospective personal-use boundary: Lujan, “2. Indefinite return intentions and general species interests do not establish threatened personal injury here,” June 12, 1992; Scalia Part III-A, same five-Justice opinion; Stone separately supports the record-specific conclusion.
- Citizen-suit and procedural-right boundary: Lujan, “3. Citizen-suit authorization does not dispense with a threatened concrete interest,” June 12, 1992; Scalia Part IV, same five Justices.

**Exceptions and limits:** No purchased-ticket or particular-document requirement is adopted. The redressability application to absent agencies, binding force of the challenged regulation upon those agencies, effect of particular funding changes and ESA §7(a)(2)'s foreign reach are not controlling holdings.

**Madsen**

**Current rule:** A content-neutral injunction must burden no more speech than necessary to serve the significant governmental interest supporting the remedy. This inquiry is more demanding than the ordinary content-neutral legislative time, place and manner rule, without becoming a universal least-restrictive-means test. Each restriction needs a remedial connection to the established interest and conduct. Public access, medical quiet and residential privacy may support relief, but neither those interests nor prior misconduct justifies territorial restrictions or advance-permission conditions broader than necessary. Removing an invalid restriction also removes its operative predicate; it cannot be restored by relabeling the same conduct under another clause.

**Missouri v. Jenkins**

**Holding:** A desegregation court may fund instructional and noninstructional salary support reasonably necessary to deliver educational measures repairing established segregation-caused injury, without treating low salaries as a separate constitutional wrong. These challenged orders are sustained on their independently sufficient intradistrict educational-repair and personnel-necessity findings; incidental voluntary attraction does not negate that ground, and the broader magnet foundation is not reopened.

**Proposition-level authority:** Souter's opinion of the Court, joined by Stone-Zsela, Stevens, Ginsburg and Breyer; five Justices adopt this holding and affirm both reviewed salary judgments.

**Limits and questions not reached:** The independently sufficient educational-repair findings sustain instructional and critical support-staff costs. November salary findings were not challenged as clearly erroneous; distinct December labor-market and support-staff challenges received that review. No automatic future increase, general educational-improvement guarantee, unproved interdistrict remedy or reopening of the broader magnet foundation follows.

**Holding:** Partial withdrawal may occur only after the defendant seeking release proves full and satisfactory compliance and practicable elimination of the relevant vestiges, whole-decree good faith shown by conduct, and the necessary causal and remedial connections; retained control necessary or practicable for compliance elsewhere and nonobstruction of remaining relief must be assessed. Missouri’s proof of installing programs does not make that complete showing, so continued funding and denial of partial release are affirmed; low scores alone establish neither continuing constitutional injury nor completed compliance, and national parity is not an indispensable release condition.

**Proposition-level authority:** Souter's opinion of the Court, joined by Stone-Zsela, Stevens, Ginsburg and Breyer; five Justices adopt the complete existing Freeman rule and its present-record application.

**Limits and questions not reached:** Program installation and spending alone do not establish practicable elimination or whole-decree good faith. Maximum educational potential and every program goal are not release requirements. No consensus, reviewer endorsement or Rufo changed-circumstances condition is added; properly supported later release remains possible.

The State and district’s respective responsibilities must be assessed separately; one defendant’s conduct does not establish the other’s compliance. The defendant bears the release showing and the showing that imbalance for which it disclaims responsibility is not traceable to the violation. Genuinely independent demographic change imposes no perpetual balancing duty once the required work is completed. Racial imbalance alone proves neither liability nor completed remedy. Partial release may, rather than must, follow a satisfactory showing. The court must examine whether retaining control is necessary or practicable for compliance elsewhere, avoid obstructing remaining relief, and define released duties, remaining obligations and retained supervision. Existing factual findings receive clear-error review; findings on one issue do not decide unexamined connections. There is no all-components-at-once restriction or good-intentions substitute.

**Sandin v. Conner**

**Holding:** A State creates protected prison liberty when substantive law meaningfully restricts officials’ authority to impose a significant deprivation. Mandatory wording alone is insufficient: courts must examine the genuine constraint in the whole disciplinary scheme and the meaningful restraint, without making state codification necessary for directly protected constitutional liberty.

**Proposition-level authority:** Breyer’s Opinion of the Court, joined by Stone-Zsela, Stevens, Souter and Ginsburg; five Justices adopt this sufficient, nonexclusive state-entitlement rule.

**Limits and questions not reached:** The rule is sufficient and nonexclusive. Mandatory procedures, routine transfers and brief inconveniences do not automatically create entitlements; direct constitutional, unexpected-restraint and sentence-duration interests remain. No atypical-and-significant-hardship test controls.

**Holding:** The summary judgment rejecting the reviewed liberty-and-process claim cannot stand on a mechanical reading of Hawaii’s regulatory words or dismissal of genuine punitive constraints. The district court must determine whether the rules genuinely constrain authority, whether the deprivation is meaningful rather than routine, and what process is due; the Court makes no liability or ultimate protected-interest finding.

**Proposition-level authority:** Breyer’s Opinion of the Court and remedial disposition; Stone-Zsela, Stevens, Souter and Ginsburg join the bounded rule-and-remand ground. Four concurring Justices separately would find the interest already established.

**Limits and questions not reached:** Substantial-evidence language is not rewritten as an express acquittal command; the whole misconduct, impartial-adjudication, evidence and sanction scheme matters. Punitive labels alone do not establish liberty; similarity to separately authorized administrative or protective custody alone does not defeat a punitive constraint. Later expungement supplies no retroactive process and discretionary parole consequences are not inevitable sentence extension. Wolff permits refusal of witnesses for irrelevance, lack of necessity or safety or correctional hazards. Neither the four concurring votes finding an established interest nor the dissent's four-vote replacement framework controls under Marks.

## Lewis v. Casey, No. 94-1511


### Record and procedural scope

Twenty-two prisoners filed the §1983 action on January 12, 1990. The certified class covered all adults then or subsequently in Arizona Department of Corrections custody. The Department operated nine prison facilities; the reported January 22, 1992 population was 14,424 men and 922 women. The lower appeal concerns court access, rather than every claim litigated in the class action. Separate contact-visitation, HIV employment, mental-health, disability and sex-equality rulings are described in footnote 2 and do not become additional components of this appeal. Source: [43 F.3d 1261, 1265–1266 and n.2](https://static.case.law/f3d/43/html/1261-01.html).

The published district findings are [834 F.Supp.1553](https://static.case.law/f-supp/834/html/1553-01.html), captioned November 13, 1992. The Ninth Circuit describes the ruling as November 16, 1992. Preserve that reported date discrepancy if the exact entry date matters; neither date is the Supreme Court decision date. The permanent injunction was entered October 13, 1993. The Ninth Circuit decided the relevant appeal December 27, 1994. It affirmed most relief but made the specific changes below.

### Personal examples and the underlying findings

The district court found that inadequate assistance had caused slow-reading prisoners to lose cases with prejudice and had prevented other prisoners from filing actions. Its footnote 37 identifies Bartholic's December 17, 1991 testimony at transcript pages 198–201 and Exhibit 956; footnote 38 identifies Harris's December 18 testimony at page 218. These are lower-court findings and identified supporting sources. They do not establish a fixed number of legally sufficient injuries across the entire class. The legal sufficiency, prospective relevance, representation and permissible remedial implications of the examples remain questions to be adjudicated. Source: 834 F.Supp. at 1558 and nn.36–38.

The recovered Joint Appendix, printed pages 96–100, reproduces the Bartholic testimony at trial pages 198–204. He described seventh-grade special education, dyslexia diagnosed when he was twelve, slow reading, missed lines and reversed letters. He testified that he filed a federal action in 1987 with another prisoner's help while at Florence Central; the action concerned outside recreation and medical matters. After transfer to SMU, he said the exact-citation request requirement prevented him from locating the cases he needed and library personnel refused research help. He described being put in a small library room, attempts to attract clerks for help, early removal as a nuisance, later denial of access based on a book-damage accusation which he denied, absence of a resulting disciplinary charge, inability to meet filing deadlines, and dismissal with prejudice. These details are his testimony, with the district court's narrower causal finding separately stated above; do not convert every detail into an independent adjudicated fact. The Joint Appendix contains selected transcript excerpts, signaled by omissions. Source: [IA Joint Appendix](https://archive.org/download/micro_IA40385013_0589/micro_IA40385013_0589%2006.%20Joint%20Appendix.pdf), pp.96–100.

The same Joint Appendix, printed pages 112–115, reproduces Harris's testimony at trial pages 207–210, 215–216 and 218. He said he requested assistance and a legal assistant in lockdown at Douglas and during a sixteen-day lockdown at Perryville, without receiving them. He also described unsuccessful requests to library personnel at Perryville. At trial page 218 he identified medical concerns as the reason for his request. His statement that he had been told he read at a second-grade level is testimony; the offered testing document was withdrawn after questions about its meaning, so do not describe that document as admitted expert proof. The published district finding of an inability to file is distinct from the transcript's description of the unsuccessful requests. Source: same Joint Appendix, pp.112–115; 834 F.Supp. at 1558 n.38.

### Evidence of recurring barriers, with qualifications

- The court found routine physical-library exclusion of lockdown prisoners at most facilities, exact-citation requirements, delays of days or weeks, and restrictions on the number of books and possession time. The practices varied: lockdown prisoners at Rynning and the Florence women's unit could enter and browse. Staffing, logistics and separation of populations were the Department's stated reasons. Evidence of vandalism existed at Perryville; the court did not find that browsing caused the damage and considered staffing a possible explanation. Source: 834 F.Supp. at 1555–1558 and nn.1–33.
- A 1989 system-wide study put 35% of adults at seventh-grade reading level or lower. Among 3,253 reception-center prisoners tested in a six-month period in 1990–1991, 17.2% tested below sixth grade and 14.5% were non-English-speaking. These are different populations, periods and measures; do not treat the latter percentage as an independently established current whole-system census. Source: id. at 1558 nn.34–36.
- The district court found insufficient trained legal assistants, inconsistent or absent testing, no general effective training program outside specified exceptions, no assurance of Spanish-speaking assistants, and translation difficulties when prisoners relied on untrained peers. It identified a court-ordered Central Unit program and a Tucson program as exceptions rather than finding that no training existed anywhere. Source: id. at 1558–1561.
- The library-collection findings included missing or outdated materials and uneven self-help resources. The court also expressly recognized post-filing improvements: most facilities had the required collection, libraries had generally become complete, hours expanded and the monetary eligibility threshold rose. Thus an initial defect and a continuing remedial need are separate inquiries. Source: id. at 1561–1562, 1565, 1568.
- The $22 supply policy looked to whether the account had exceeded that amount during the previous thirty days; it did not assess actual necessary expenditures individually. The court found copying practices exposed confidential documents to staff or prisoners and found restrictive, sometimes monitored attorney-call practices. The particular rules and practices differed among units. Source: id. at 1562–1565.

### Relief actually under review

Appendix A to 43 F.3d1261 reproduces the complete injunction and explanatory commentary. It must be used with the appellate modifications, not treated as wholly affirmed.

- Library placement: fully equipped libraries for facilities with population or capacity of at least 150, subject to five specified existing unit exceptions: Aspen DWI and Flamenco at Phoenix; Papago DWI and Maricopa at Douglas; Picacho at Florence. Exempt facilities still had to provide the ordered access opportunities. Individual library access could be postponed for documented safety/security inability or a medical determination preventing use.
- Time: on request, at least ten hours of actual library use weekly, with additional time for a filing or legal deadline. Non-advance-scheduling facilities generally had a fifty-hour weekly opening requirement. Advance-scheduling facilities had sixty-, seventy- or eighty-hour requirements according to specified population/capacity bands, plus evening/weekend provisions. Thus “fifty hours everywhere” would be inaccurate.
- Assistance: the program required direct assistance for prisoners unable adequately to research and write because of language, lack of library access or another reason. In the absence of lawyers or paralegals, it required sufficient minimally trained prisoner assistants, with selection, training, competence-review and security qualifications. It did not require a lawyer or direct assistant for every prisoner. Law clerks, legal assistants and librarians had distinct functions. Librarians could qualify through a library-science, law or paralegal degree; no universal law-degree requirement existed.
- The decree also prescribed research materials and guides, paging and scheduling, copying and notary services, supplies, confidential communications, training and monitoring. Its telephone minimum was three twenty-minute weekly calls to an attorney, designated attorney representative or legal organization, with additional calls in an emergency. Uncompleted calls did not count; documented abuse remained a qualification. The prisoner could pay or call collect.
- The Ninth Circuit vacated the $46 eligibility provision for the absence of a specific finding adequate for this class, notwithstanding the district court's criticism of $22 and the separate Gluth findings. It vacated §III.C's typewriter provision because the prisoners did not contest the Department's objection. It remanded the proposed copying-charge change from five to eight cents for a justification determination. It also remanded to permit objections to the special master's fees. The Ninth Circuit rejected an argument that the Department had forfeited its legal-assistant-function objection merely by failing to object to the proposed order; the proposal was not a final judgment. Sources: 43 F.3d at 1268–1272 and complete Appendix A, 1272–1283.

The factual findings about resources, exclusion, staff, literacy and individual experience are distinct from the lower courts' legal “core Bounds” approach, their conclusions about injury and their selection of system-wide relief. The neutral model must examine the latter issues rather than silently importing a historical Supreme Court assessment.

‘‘(iii) in the case of an order issued on or before the
date of enactment of the Prison Litigation Reform Act,
2 years after such date of enactment.
‘‘(B) Nothing in this section shall prevent the parties from
agreeing to terminate or modify relief before the relief is termi-
nated under subparagraph (A).
‘‘(2) IMMEDIATE TERMINATION OF PROSPECTIVE RELIEF .—In
any civil action with respect to prison conditions, a defendant
or intervener shall be entitled to the immediate termination
of any prospective relief if the relief was approved or granted
in the absence of a finding by the court that the relief is
narrowly drawn, extends no further than necessary to correct
the violation of the Federal right, and is the least intrusive
means necessary to correct the violation of the Federal right.
‘‘(3) LIMITATION.—Prospective relief shall not terminate if
the court makes written findings based on the record that
prospective relief remains necessary to correct a current or
ongoing violation of the Federal right, extends no further than
necessary to correct the violation of the Federal right, and
that the prospective relief is narrowly drawn and the least
intrusive means to correct the violation.
‘‘(4) TERMINATION OR MODIFICATION OF RELIEF .—Nothing
in this section shall prevent any party or intervener from
seeking modification or termination before the relief is ter-
minable under paragraph (1) or (2), to the extent that modifica-
tion or termination would otherwise be legally permissible.
‘‘(c) SETTLEMENTS.—
‘‘(1) CONSENT DECREES .—In any civil action with respect
to prison conditions, the court shall not enter or approve a
consent decree unless it complies with the limitations on relief
set forth in subsection (a).
‘‘(2) PRIVATE SETTLEMENT AGREEMENTS .—(A) Nothing in
this section shall preclude parties from entering into a private
settlement agreement that does not comply with the limitations
on relief set forth in subsection (a), if the terms of that agree-
ment are not subject to court enforcement other than the
reinstatement of the civil proceeding that the agreement settled.
‘‘(B) Nothing in this section shall preclude any party claim-
ing that a private settlement agreement has been breached
from seeking in State court any remedy available under State
law.
‘‘(d) STATE LAW REMEDIES.—The limitations on remedies in
this section shall not apply to relief entered by a State court
based solely upon claims arising under State law.
‘‘(e) P ROCEDURE FOR MOTIONS AFFECTING PROSPECTIVE
RELIEF.—
‘‘(1) GENERALLY.—The court shall promptly rule on any
motion to modify or terminate prospective relief in a civil action
with respect to prison conditions.
‘‘(2) AUTOMATIC STAY .—Any prospective relief subject to
a pending motion shall be automatically stayed during the
period—

‘‘(f) SPECIAL MASTERS.—
‘‘(1) IN GENERAL .—(A) In any civil action in a Federal
court with respect to prison conditions, the court may appoint
a special master who shall be disinterested and objective and
who will give due regard to the public safety, to conduct hear-
ings on the record and prepare proposed findings of fact.
‘‘(B) The court shall appoint a special master under this
subsection during the remedial phase of the action only upon
a finding that the remedial phase will be sufficiently complex
to warrant the appointment.
‘‘(2) APPOINTMENT.—(A) If the court determines that the
appointment of a special master is necessary, the court shall
request that the defendant institution and the plaintiff each
submit a list of not more than 5 persons to serve as a special
master.
‘‘(B) Each party shall have the opportunity to remove up
to 3 persons from the opposing party’s list.
‘‘(C) The court shall select the master from the persons
remaining on the list after the operation of subparagraph (B).
‘‘(3) INTERLOCUTORY APPEAL .—Any party shall have the
right to an interlocutory appeal of the judge’s selection of the
special master under this subsection, on the ground of partial-
ity.
‘‘(4) COMPENSATION.—The compensation to be allowed to
a special master under this section shall be based on an hourly
rate not greater than the hourly rate established under section
3006A for payment of court-appointed counsel, plus costs
reasonably incurred by the special master. Such compensation
and costs shall be paid with funds appropriated to the Judiciary.
‘‘(5) REGULAR REVIEW OF APPOINTMENT .—In any civil action
with respect to prison conditions in which a special master
is appointed under this subsection, the court shall review the
appointment of the special master every 6 months to determine
whether the services of the special master continue to be
required under paragraph (1). In no event shall the appoint-
ment of a special master extend beyond the termination of
the relief.
‘‘(6) LIMITATIONS ON POWERS AND DUTIES .—A special master
appointed under this subsection—
‘‘(A) may be authorized by a court to conduct hearings
and prepare proposed findings of fact, which shall be made
on the record;
‘‘(B) shall not make any findings or communications
ex parte;
‘‘(C) may be authorized by a court to assist in the
development of remedial plans; and
‘‘(D) may be removed at any time, but shall be relieved
of the appointment upon the termination of relief.
‘‘(g) DEFINITIONS.—As used in this section—

‘‘(1) the term ‘consent decree’ means any relief entered
by the court that is based in whole or in part upon the consent
or acquiescence of the parties but does not include private
settlements;
‘‘(2) the term ‘civil action with respect to prison conditions’
means any civil proceeding arising under Federal law with
respect to the conditions of confinement or the effects of actions
by government officials on the lives of persons confined in
prison, but does not include habeas corpus proceedings challeng-
ing the fact or duration of confinement in prison;
‘‘(3) the term ‘prisoner’ means any person subject to incar-
ceration, detention, or admission to any facility who is accused
of, convicted of, sentenced for, or adjudicated delinquent for,
violations of criminal law or the terms and conditions of parole,
probation, pretrial release, or diversionary program;
‘‘(4) the term ‘prisoner release order’ includes any order,
including a temporary restraining order or preliminary injunc-
tive relief, that has the purpose or effect of reducing or limiting
the prison population, or that directs the release from or non-
admission of prisoners to a prison;
‘‘(5) the term ‘prison’ means any Federal, State, or local
facility that incarcerates or detains juveniles or adults accused
of, convicted of, sentenced for, or adjudicated delinquent for,
violations of criminal law;
‘‘(6) the term ‘private settlement agreement’ means an
agreement entered into among the parties that is not subject
to judicial enforcement other than the reinstatement of the
civil proceeding that the agreement settled;
‘‘(7) the term ‘prospective relief’ means all relief other than
compensatory monetary damages;
‘‘(8) the term ‘special master’ means any person appointed
by a Federal court pursuant to Rule 53 of the Federal Rules
of Civil Procedure or pursuant to any inherent power of the
court to exercise the powers of a master, regardless of the
title or description given by the court; and
‘‘(9) the term ‘relief’ means all relief in any form that
may be granted or approved by the court, and includes consent
decrees but does not include private settlement agreements.’’.
