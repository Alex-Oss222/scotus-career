## 1. IBM — 517 U.S. 843 (June 10, 1996)

**Verified judgment:** Affirmed, **6–2**, with **Stevens taking no part**. No. 95-591; argued March 18, 1996. The historical Chief Justice in the following tables is Rehnquist, not the simulated Chief Justice.

| Writing / component | Historical support | Exact status |
|---|---|---|
| Thomas, opinion of the Court, 845–862 | Thomas, Rehnquist, O’Connor, Scalia, Souter, Breyer | Six complete joins; no part-specific qualification is reported. |
| Kennedy, dissent, 863–881 | Kennedy, Ginsburg | Both dissent from affirmance; Kennedy is the author. This is the sole separate writing. |
| Participation | Stevens | Did not participate in consideration or decision. |

**Correction to the generic comparator.** Its description of a categorical bar for a service closely connected to exporting obscures the question actually resolved. The six-Justice opinion held that the Export Clause prohibits nondiscriminatory federal taxes on goods in export transit. It rejected the proposed inference that later dormant-Commerce-Clause and Import-Export-Clause developments had displaced that rule. It retained *Thames & Mersey Marine Insurance Co. v. United States* and applied it to the challenged insurance-premium tax. The opinion did **not** newly decide the full constitutional boundary between a tax on goods and a tax on a distinct export-related service.

The limitation matters to historical comparison. The majority understood the Government to have conceded that *Thames & Mersey* controlled if it remained good law, and understood the Government deliberately to have pressed the nondiscrimination ground rather than a separate goods/services challenge. It reserved fuller exploration of when a particular activity or service assessment is sufficiently connected with goods in export transit to be equivalent to a tax on the goods. Those presentation and precedent premises were part of the majority's reason for declining to reconsider *Thames & Mersey*'s classification on this record. They are the majority's historical legal treatment of the submissions, not newly stipulated neutral facts. See 854–856, 861–862.

**Historical controlling rationale, with limits.** The Export Clause's express federal prohibition differs from the implied dormant Commerce Clause rule and from the States' Import-Export Clause prohibition of imposts and duties. Modern decisions sustaining state taxes on goods after transit or on services did not compel alteration of the federal Export Clause rule for goods in transit. The opinion retained the distinction between impermissible taxation of exports and ordinary burdens before the export process or sufficiently tangential services. It relied on the text, the precedents' separate constitutional settings, and stare decisis; it did not treat the Export Clause as a blanket exemption from every cost associated with exporting. *Michelin Tire* concerned goods no longer in transit; *Washington Stevedoring* concerned services and had reserved direct taxation of goods in transit. See 846–862.

**Kennedy/Ginsburg, complete separate-writing account.** The dissent would have reversed because §4371 taxed insurance services rather than exported articles. A general insurance-premium tax covering domestic as well as export risks did not become a tax on articles merely because insurance accompanied exportation. The stipulated premium formula combined value with shipment-specific risk; insurance prices therefore were not simply a proxy for the goods' value. The dissent discussed early federal insurance stamp taxation, the obsolete insurance/commerce premise of *Paul v. Virginia*, later service-tax decisions, and the distinction between documents representing exported goods and independent insurance contracts. It would reconsider the relevant service-tax treatment in *Thames & Mersey* without deciding the broad nondiscrimination issue the majority decided.

The dissent also disputed the majority's procedural premise: it read the Government's briefs and oral statements as leaving the narrower services ground available, emphasized that the narrower issue was fairly included within the presented constitutional question, and rejected treating litigation emphasis as a binding concession or inadequate factual record. Its references to likely effects of taxes are arguments, not additional historical findings. See 863–881, especially 864–869 and 879–881.

**Historical precedential topology:** A six-Justice opinion supplies the rule; no *Marks* reconstruction is necessary. Neither Kennedy/Ginsburg's service-tax rationale nor a broader categorical rule for all export-associated services may be attributed to the six.

## 2. Spink — 517 U.S. 882 (June 10, 1996)

**Verified judgment:** Reversed and remanded. No. 95-809; argued April 22, 1996; all nine participated. A bare overall vote label loses the material partial-join distinction.

| Writing / component | Historical support | Exact status |
|---|---|---|
| Thomas opinion, all portions except III-B | Thomas, Rehnquist, Stevens, O’Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Ginsburg, Breyer | Nine joins. Includes the §406 fiduciary prerequisite, III-A's sponsor/settlor holding, and IV's OBRA temporal holding. |
| Thomas, III-B, 892–895 | Thomas, Rehnquist, Stevens, O’Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Ginsburg | Seven joins to the benefit-payment / implementation ground. |
| Breyer, concurring in part and dissenting in part, 898 | Breyer, Souter | Joins the Court's opinion except III-B; would leave that difficult, unnecessary question for further lower-court development. No separate Souter writing. |

**Corrections to the generic comparator.** Add the separate OBRA issue, the remand, and the two reserved joins. Breyer and Souter did **not** hold that the releases made benefit payments prohibited transactions, and their writing is not an affirmance of the Ninth Circuit's §406 conclusion. Their objection was to deciding III-B's broader legal question on this occasion. Conversely, they cannot be listed as joining that ground merely because they agreed with the rest of Thomas's opinion.

**Component A — §406 prerequisite and amendment capacity (nine).** A violation of §406(a)(1) requires a fiduciary to cause the plan to engage in the prohibited transaction. A party-in-interest allegation alone does not remove that prerequisite. Lockheed and its board did not act as fiduciaries when adopting the amendments: amendment of a pension plan, like amendment of the welfare plan in *Curtiss-Wright*, is a sponsor's settlor function. Pension vesting requirements do not transform amendment itself into fiduciary administration, though other ERISA substantive obligations remain applicable. The opinion did not decide a categorical rule of nonfiduciary immunity from every form of ERISA relief; note 3 distinguishes the predicate violation question from the potential relief question discussed in *Mertens*. See 887–891.

**Component B — implementation / benefit payments (seven).** III-B did not decide whether the Retirement Committee actually acted as a fiduciary, because it concluded that paying benefits pursuant to an otherwise lawful plan was not the kind of transaction §406(a)(1)(D) prohibited. The statutory context involved potentially harmful dealings with parties in interest. Payment of benefits to participants in exchange for a plan condition, including retirement and releases, did not acquire that character merely because the employer benefited. The seven rejected a distinction under which ordinary compensation was allowed but some other employee performances were not. See 892–895.

Retain these qualifications: Spink did not allege an independent participation, funding, or vesting violation (note 5); whether a particular waiver would be enforceable under other law, including the Older Workers Benefit Protection Act, was a different question (note 6); and a sham benefit transaction disguising an unlawful asset transfer or a kickback scheme might present a different case, which Spink had not alleged (note 8). The broad language at 895 cannot be detached from the otherwise-lawful-plan setting and those reservations. The opinion also does not decide the lower court's unaddressed §404/§403 alternatives or a particular fee amount merely by announcing the §406 rule.

**Breyer/Souter's entire separate ground.** Their one-paragraph opinion identifies III-B's conclusion as difficult, unnecessary here, and likely to benefit from further lower-court consideration and informed submissions in a technical field. They would follow the Solicitor General's request not to reach it. Their writing supplies a scope-of-decision/remand preference; it supplies no contrary definitive construction of §406(a)(1)(D). See 898.

**Component C — OBRA timing (nine).** Section IV held that §§9201 and 9202(a), respectively amending the ADEA and ERISA accrual provisions, did not require credit for Spink's pre-1988 service. Section 9204(a)(1) expressly applied those changes only to plan years beginning on or after January 1, 1988, and only to employees with one hour of service in a covered plan year. The Court distinguished reduced total benefits from a reduced accrual rate. The express effective-date provision resolved the question under *Landgraf* without judicial default presumptions. The separate participation change under §9203(a)(1), governed by §9204(b)'s plan-year and service limits, was discussed in note 9; it was not the same statutory text as the accrual provision. See 896–897. The first syllabus paragraph reverses the ERISA/ADEA order in its reference to §§9201/9202; the actual opinion's correct section mapping controls.

**Historical precedential topology:** III-B has seven direct joins; the other decided grounds have nine. There is no fractured rationale requiring *Marks*. The authoritative judgment is reversal and remand, with the foregoing component-specific disagreement, rather than a single undifferentiated poll importing agreement on every implementation proposition.

## 3. Myers — 46 F.3d 668 (7th Cir. February 1, 1995)

The June 11, 1996 Supreme Court event is assigned by the simulation; this report supplies no historical Supreme Court grant, merits docket, author, poll, or separate writing for that event.

**Verified panel / result:** Manion wrote for a panel comprising Cudahy, Eschbach, and Manion. The reported decision affirms the district court's denial of the motion to suppress. It reports no dissent or concurrence. The conditional guilty plea preserved that suppression appeal.

**Historical lower grounds:** The court followed the historical Eighth Circuit *Pinson* and Eleventh Circuit *Ford*. It first found no subjective expectation of privacy because Myers had taken no steps to contain or conceal heat and vented it through the roof. Independently, it concluded that an expectation of privacy in escaped waste heat was not objectively reasonable, analogizing to exposed garbage, smoke, and drug scent under *Greenwood*, *Western Alfalfa*, and *Place*. It treated this passive Agema 210 scan as not penetrating the home or threatening its protected private activities. Note 1 expressly leaves aside more advanced equipment that might penetrate walls or otherwise become unacceptably intrusive. Read the no-search language with that equipment/record limit.

The decision did not separately resolve an excised affidavit's sufficiency, a *Leon* claim, an independent source, inevitable discovery, or a warrant exception for a scan assumed to be a search. It affirmed because it found no search. ## 4. Vera — 517 U.S. 952 (June 13, 1996)

**Scope control:** Nos. 94-805, 94-806, and 94-988; argued December 5, 1995. Historical review concerned racial-districting merits. The supplied OT1995-077 event is expressly reconstructed **standing-only** review. All historical merits topology below is comparison evidence about the historical case, not a merits issue added to the reconstruction and not a ready-made poll on its different standing question.

**Verified historical judgment:** Affirmance of the invalidation of Districts 18, 29, and 30, **5–4**, all nine participating. Supporting judgment: Rehnquist, O’Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Thomas. Dissenting: Stevens, Souter, Ginsburg, Breyer.

Ginsburg and Breyer joined **both** dissents. Souter did not join Stevens's dissent; Stevens did not join Souter's. There is no separate Scalia, Ginsburg, or Breyer writing. Do not merge either pair of concurrences or the two dissents into one rationale.

**Historical standing discussion and its exact evidentiary ceiling.** Part I of the three-Justice lead opinion states that Chen, residing in District 25 without specific personal-classification facts, lacks standing; Blum and Powers in 18, Thomas and Vera in 29, and Orcutt in 30 have standing under historical *Hays* and *Miller*. The latter rests on residence in a racially gerrymandered district and the stated unequal-treatment injury from racial assignment. See 957–958. The report contains **no separately tabulated nine-Justice standing vote**. Thomas/Scalia concurred in the merits judgment without separately joining or explaining Part I. Neither dissent supplies a plaintiff-by-plaintiff Article III disposition that can honestly be relabeled a vote to dismiss the reconstructed action.

Stevens disputed the diffuse harm and the new cause of action, while offering an alternative application of the existing merits doctrine; see 1008–1011 and note 7. Souter criticized the absence of an identifiable injured class and the expressive-harm conception, referring to *Valley Forge* at 1054 note 6 and 1071. These are authentic concerns relevant to his historical rationale, but neither is an express adjudication of the distinct resident-assignment allegations under the reconstructed standing-only grant. The actual simulation's separate standing and design-only merits rules remain supplied entering-law premises. No resident may be silently dropped, no additional personal injury inferred from districtwide administrative evidence, and no historical merits affirmance imported through a decision recognizing standing.

**O’Connor lead opinion (three direct joins).** Race must predominate over traditional districting principles to trigger strict scrutiny; mere race consciousness is insufficient. The opinion's language also says intentional creation of a majority-minority district does not invariably trigger scrutiny, subject to Kennedy's express reservation below. It found racial predominance in these mixed-motive districts after considering incumbency protection, communities of interest, demographic software, block-level line drawing, and district-specific evidence. It distinguished political affiliation correlated with race from using race as a proxy for political affiliation. It did not treat irregular shape alone, intentional majority-minority creation alone, or accurate demographic technology alone as dispositive. See 958–976.

The lead opinion assumed without deciding that compliance with §2's results test could be compelling. It required a strong evidentiary basis for the *Gingles* conditions and tailoring reasonably necessary to address the threatened violation, while rejecting a least-irregular-shape or perpetual expert beauty-contest requirement. State latitude exceeded a remedial court's latitude. It found these districts too racially manipulated and noncompact to be justified by §2, rejected the separate past-discrimination defense because the current identified concern was the same vote-dilution problem, and rejected §5 as a justification for District 18's unsupported augmentation of minority population beyond nonretrogression. See 976–984. Part IV defended the cause of action and its stare decisis basis against both dissents, acknowledging difficulty and the absence of bright-line rules. See 984–986.

**O’Connor concurrence (one).** Unlike the lead opinion's assumption, she affirmatively regarded §2 compliance as compelling. The statute's constitutional presumption, congressional enforcement authority, federalism, and the States' need to comply with both regimes supported that position. She described a five-part reconciliation: ordinary consideration of race without subordination; §2 obligations where *Gingles* conditions obtain; a strong evidentiary basis without any mandatory evidentiary form; reasonable tailoring with latitude for other legitimate principles; and invalidity where predominantly racial manipulation substantially departs from a proper district. Her acceptance of a strong basis for creating a Dallas minority opportunity district did not accept the final District 30 lines. See 990–995.

**Kennedy concurrence (one; material qualification of lead join).** He expressly did not regard himself as committed by the lead opinion's dicta that intentional majority-minority creation need not trigger scrutiny. He reserved whether a State's foreordaining racial majorities itself establishes predominance and cautioned that the summary affirmance in *DeWitt* adopted its judgment, not the lower court's reasoning. He agreed that these lines were not reasonably necessary to satisfy the assumed §2 interest. He distinguished compactness of the minority population, relevant to the first *Gingles* condition, from compactness of the resulting district. He identified the lack of evidence for two reasonably compact Harris County districts and rejected a supposed second predominance inquiry in narrow tailoring. Noncompactness caused by legitimate race-neutral considerations was not itself forbidden. See 996–999. A claim that all three lead-opinion Justices definitively rejected an automatic intentional-creation trigger would therefore overstate the historical agreement.

**Thomas/Scalia concurrence in judgment (two).** They regarded intentional creation of majority-minority districts as a racial classification requiring strict scrutiny, going beyond mere awareness that neutral criteria would produce such a district. The State's admitted use of race was sufficient; shape evidence was additionally sufficient but unnecessary. They invoked *Adarand* and historical *Miller* and rejected reading *DeWitt*'s summary affirmance as adopting reasoning inconsistent with those principles. They assumed a compelling interest without deciding it and agreed that these efforts were not narrowly tailored. They did not join the lead opinion wholesale. See 999–1003, including both footnotes.

**Stevens/Ginsburg/Breyer dissent (three).** It offered alternative grounds. Applying the existing doctrine, it found the shape of District 30 driven predominantly by political/incumbency constraints and communities of interest; majority-white district comparisons and the distribution of the district's population undermined a racial-predominance inference. It contested the lower court's dismissal of incumbency evidence, reliance on submissions prepared for other legal proceedings, and equation of political use of reliable racial data with invidious classification. The Houston districts were a closer question: the dissent assumed strict scrutiny there for argument, and concluded those districts, as well as District 30 if scrutiny applied, were adequately tailored to §2 compliance while pursuing legitimate goals. It rejected treating the particular court-remedial compact district as the sole permissible legislative solution. More fundamentally, it would abandon the new *Shaw* approach, whose diffuse harm, incentives, state-sovereignty costs, and judicial entanglement it found unjustified. It preferred direct scrutiny of harmful political gerrymandering to this race-specific mechanism. See 1003–1041, all notes.

**Souter/Ginsburg/Breyer dissent (three).** It separately challenged *Shaw*'s conceptual and administrable foundations: no distinct harmed class comparable to malapportionment or vote dilution, an expressive-injury definition reaching lawful remedial activity, and no workable separation of race from traditional community and incumbency considerations. It differentiated political representation from jury neutrality rather than simply denying racial stereotyping's existence. It welcomed O’Connor's affirmative §2 position, but regarded the remaining guidance as insufficient and potentially damaging to voluntary compliance, minority incumbency, and state control of districting. It examined possible compactness/shape approaches as alternatives if *Shaw* were retained, expressly recognized the lead opinion's leeway rather than asserting an unqualified shape mandate, and ultimately urged complete abandonment of the cause of action. Stare decisis did not persuade it to retain an unworkable doctrine with uncertain reliance and high institutional costs. See 1045–1077, all notes.

**Historical precedential topology / *Marks* limit.** Five agreed to the judgment, to applying strict scrutiny on these facts, and to the failure of narrow tailoring. Three signed the lead rationale; two used the broader intentional-racial-classification trigger. That overlap does not produce five direct joins to every lead-opinion proposition. Nor does the existence of a narrower trigger automatically establish that every aspect of the three-Justice opinion is a logical subset of the two-Justice concurrence. This verification does not announce a blanket *Marks* holding or a synthesized majority standing rule. Any reconciliation using a particular historical proposition must identify the actual writing and level of agreement. The reconstructed event reaches no racial-predominance, §2, §5, or narrow-tailoring merits issue.
