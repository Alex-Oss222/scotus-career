## Planned Parenthood, Sioux Falls Clinic v. Miller, 63 F.3d 1452 (8th Cir. 1995)

Full published lower opinion and all eleven footnotes read from [the reporter transcription](https://static.case.law/f3d/63/html/1452-01.html), corroborated by [OpenJurist](https://openjurist.org/63/f3d/1452/planned-parenthood-sioux-falls-clinic-v-d-miller-w). Nos. 94-3326SD and 94-3398SD; submitted May 15, 1995; decided August 31, 1995. Richard S. Arnold writes for a unanimous panel with John R. Gibson and Fagg; no separate writing. The panel affirms the district judgment in all respects: notice and civil/criminal-penalty provisions invalid, mandatory-information provision upheld, remainder severable. This is the historical lower comparator, not a historical Supreme Court merits decision, grant or vote.

The notice ruling invokes historical Casey’s large-fraction approach rather than Salerno’s independent no-valid-applications test. It requires a confidential, expeditious Bellotti-type bypass for mature and best-interests minors; one-parent notice and the abuse-reporting exception do not cure the identified deficiency. It preserves the distinction between consent and notice, explains potential practical obstruction, and leaves the permissibility of a nonjudicial independent decisionmaker unresolved. The mandatory-information and penalty dispositions remain distinct and do not enlarge the present reconstructed grant. Its historical Casey/Fargo account does not replace actual simulated Casey and Fargo.

The operative statutory text in footnote 2 covers an unemancipated minor and a female subject to an incompetency guardianship; written personal notice or the specified certified-mail mechanism; forty-eight-hour notice interval; and the three exact exceptions (medical emergency with insufficient time, written acknowledgment, reported abuse/neglect with confidentiality duties). Review must preserve the actual granted population rather than silently adjudicate guardianship applications. The abuse provision’s 24-hour discussion refers to the independent information waiting period; the text does not add a freestanding 24-hour abuse-report rule. The opinion’s source characterizations and quoted declarations are not new appellate factfinding by the simulated Court.

## Cooper v. Oklahoma, 517 U.S. 348 (1996), No. 95-5207, April 16

Sources: [Cornell syllabus](https://www.law.cornell.edu/supct/html/95-5207.ZS.html); [Stevens Court opinion](https://www.law.cornell.edu/supct/html/95-5207.ZO.html); [official report](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/USREPORTS-517/pdf/USREPORTS-517-348.pdf).

**Exact historical topology and result:** Stevens for a unanimous nine-Justice Court: Rehnquist, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg, Breyer. No separate writing, partial join, or recusal. Oklahoma Court of Criminal Appeals judgment **reversed and remanded**, 889 P.2d 293. Generic comparator's “Reversed” omits remand.

**Material historical holding and limits:** Oklahoma's clear-and-convincing burden violates due process because it allows trial of a defendant who is more likely than not incompetent. This is not a finding by this Court that Cooper actually was incompetent, an acquittal, or an unconditional discharge. Historical Medina permits a presumption of competence and defendant's preponderance burden; the decisive difference is that Medina's allocation changes outcomes only in equipoise, whereas Oklahoma's rule exposes probably incompetent defendants to trial. History and near-uniform contemporary practice, together with the risk to fundamental trial rights, support the result; no reallocation of the permissible preponderance burden to the State was required.

The Court applies Dusky's present rational understanding/ability to consult counsel test. It distinguishes Patterson's statutory affirmative defense from procedures securing a constitutional right. Addington is consistent: its heightened burden protects liberty against civil commitment, while Oklahoma's heightened burden threatens the right not to be tried while incompetent; competency and civil-commitment standards are substantively different. The State may detain for the reasonable restoration-evaluation period recognized by Jackson v. Indiana, and may try a restored defendant or malingerer. Footnote 19 distinguishes Cruzan (surrogate decision and irreversible error) and Ohio v. Akron Center (largely because judicial bypass was ex parte). This is a material historical explanation, not a general rejection of heightened proof standards or a claim that all 46 other States invariably placed an identical burden on defendants.

## Markman v. Westview Instruments, Inc., 517 U.S. 370 (1996), No. 95-26, April 23

Sources: [Cornell syllabus](https://www.law.cornell.edu/supct/html/95-26.ZS.html); [Souter Court opinion](https://www.law.cornell.edu/supct/html/95-26.ZO.html); [official report](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/USREPORTS-517/pdf/USREPORTS-517-370.pdf).

**Exact historical topology and result:** Souter for the same unanimous nine-Justice Court; no separate writing or partial join. Federal Circuit judgment, 52 F.3d 967, **affirmed**.

**Material historical holding and limits:** Patent construction, including terms of art within the claim even when expert testimony is offered, belongs exclusively to the court. The particular word “inventory” was for the judge. The Court preserves the jury's role in the ultimate infringement inquiry in an action at law; it does **not** create a jury exception for factual disputes embedded in claim construction. The generic comparator's “juries decide ... disputed historical facts within the construed claims” must not be read as reserving claim-construction subsidiary facts to a jury or deciding the later appellate standard of review.

The historical test first classifies the action and then asks whether the subsidiary issue must be jury-decided to preserve the substance of the 1791 right. Because modern claims lack an exact historical counterpart, the Court considers specification construction, precedent, judge/jury capacities, and uniformity. Patent meaning is a “mongrel” practice with evidentiary underpinnings; expert credibility usually is subsumed in construing the instrument coherently, and even the conceivable pure credibility contest does not create a jury entitlement. Winans supplies construction-versus-infringement allocation; Bischoff and Tucker concern identification/identity of external inventions, not jury authority over documentary construction. Stare decisis promotes, but does not guarantee, uniformity; issue preclusion cannot be asserted against new independent infringement defendants.

Reservations: footnote 9 expressly leaves open whether the conclusion about patent terms of art extends to other kinds of cases. Footnote 10 leaves open how far the Seventh Amendment crystallized a law/fact distinction and whether post-1791 precedent calling an issue factual would independently trigger the guarantee without a more specific ground. Footnote 3 leaves aside potential divergence between English practice and American assumptions/practices. No separate Marks issue.

## Holly Farms Corp. v. NLRB, 517 U.S. 392 (1996), No. 95-210, April 23

Sources: [Cornell syllabus](https://www.law.cornell.edu/supct/html/95-210.ZS.html); [Ginsburg Court opinion](https://www.law.cornell.edu/supct/html/95-210.ZO.html); [O'Connor separate opinion (ZX)](https://www.law.cornell.edu/supct/html/95-210.ZX.html); [official report](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/USREPORTS-517/pdf/USREPORTS-517-392.pdf).

**Exact historical topology:** Ginsburg's Court opinion joined in full by Stevens, Kennedy, Souter, Breyer (five). O'Connor, joined by Rehnquist, Scalia, Thomas (four), **concurs in the judgment in part and dissents in part**, 517 U.S. 409–415. The four concur in the judgment **as to live-haul truckdrivers** because their work is neither “by a farmer” nor “on a farm”; they dissent **as to chicken catchers and forklift operators**. Thus nine support the truckdriver component; five support, four oppose, the catcher/forklift component. Fourth Circuit judgment, 48 F.3d 1360, **affirmed**. Calling this simply 5–4 without identifying the driver component obscures the actual disposition.

**Majority scope:** This is an NLRB reasonable-interpretation/deference holding under the NLRA agricultural-worker exclusion as informed by FLSA §3(f), **not** a conclusion that the employer's statutory reading is impossible. The Court calls it plausible but not inevitable. Bayside controls the “by a farmer” branch: outsourcing growing to independent contractors ends Holly Farms' farming status for these birds, and collection for processing does not restore it. Truckdrivers admittedly do not work on a farm. For catchers/forklift operators the Board reasonably finds work incident to processing, rather than the independent growers' farming, given the growers' completed contractual work, minimal overlap, plant reporting/time clocks and functional integration. Employer identity is relevant, not a standalone rule excluding all processor employees from secondary agriculture.

**Separate ground:** O'Connor treats the “or on a farm” branch as unambiguous, without an employment-relationship limitation. Work can be incident to farming and processing simultaneously; removing mature birds is incident to the raising cycle regardless of who pays the crew. She regards the Board's narrowing as rendering the “on a farm” clause redundant and fails Chevron at the first step.

**Required qualifications:** Majority footnote 10 itself accepts that an activity can be incidental to more than one thing; its issue is whether this work is incidental to farming. Footnote 8 preserves “on a farm” coverage of auxiliary businesses and rejects redundancy. Footnote 7 declines the belated argument that collection is **primary** agriculture as “harvesting,” because it was not advanced below or focused in the certiorari petition; the case resolves **secondary** agriculture only. Distinguish pullet catchers/egg haulers, who were aligned with hatchery/farming operations and are not these broiler crews. FLSA regulatory wording about preparation for market retains the “by a farmer or on a farm ... incident to” condition. No Marks problem: a five-Justice opinion controls the disputed components, with additional judgment-only support for the drivers.
