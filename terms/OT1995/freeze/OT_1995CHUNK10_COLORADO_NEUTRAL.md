## Material facts and their evidentiary status

The state committee was federally registered and unincorporated. In April 1986, before either party selected its Senate nominee, it spent $15,000 on the radio advertisement “Wirth Facts #1.” Wirth had registered for the Democratic nomination. The advertisement identified Republican Party sponsorship, attacked representations concerning Wirth's defense and balanced-budget record, and referred to his Senate candidacy. The lower courts accepted that it lacked the narrow express advocacy words discussed in Buckley and MCFL. The court of appeals nevertheless classified it as an electioneering message identifying a candidate.

The committee had assigned to the National Republican Senatorial Committee its authority to make all $103,248 of its permitted 1986 §441a(d)(3) expenditures for this Senate contest. Thus the additional $15,000 exceeded the available allowance if this advertisement fell within that subsection. $103,248 is the adjusted 1986 allowance, not the statutory base amount or a constitutional minimum. The committee reported the expenditure as voter-information advertising, not a §441a(d) disbursement. The FEC sued after administrative proceedings and unsuccessful settlement efforts.

The supplied case presents an independent-expenditure premise: the record does not establish candidate consultation, request, suggestion or coordinated control for this advertisement. That premise must be tested against the supported record rather than displaced by a party-status label. The lower courts' use of “coordinated” rested on accepting the FEC's categorical legal treatment of party spending; it is not an independent factual finding that a candidate actually participated in this transaction. The government's contrary legal inference remains an argument to assess.

Callaway's deposition, Joint Appendix pp.188–214, is available. The researcher inspected pp.199–205 concerning his approval of the script, possible internal reviewers, discussions leading to the advertisement, and failures of recollection about authorship, production and particular meetings. General descriptions of party work with candidates and national committees at pp.192–196 do not themselves establish particular-ad coordination. His discussion of federally permissible funds at pp.207–208 is testimony, as are his opinions concerning Wirth's record. Neither testimony explaining the attack nor the advertisement itself becomes a judicial finding that its accusations were true.

## Operative statutory provisions

The official 1994 Code and the Joint Appendix pp.216–223 corroborate the terms. The October 1986 amendment affected Internal Revenue Code references elsewhere, not the quoted §441a(d) language. The relevant challenged terms are:

**2 U.S.C. §441a(d)(1):** “Notwithstanding any other provision of law with respect to limitations on expenditures or limitations on contributions, the national committee of a political party and a State committee of a political party, including any subordinate committee of a State committee, may make expenditures in connection with the general election campaign of candidates for Federal office, subject to the limitations contained in paragraphs (2) and (3) of this subsection.”

**Section 441a(d)(3):** “The national committee of a political party, or a State committee of a political party, including any subordinate committee of a State committee, may not make any expenditure in connection with the general election campaign of a candidate for Federal office in a State who is affiliated with such party which exceeds—

“(A) in the case of a candidate for election to the office of Senator, or of Representative from a State which is entitled to only one Representative, the greater of—

“(i) 2 cents multiplied by the voting age population of the State (as certified under subsection (e) of this section); or

“(ii) $20,000; and

“(B) in the case of a candidate for election to the office of Representative, Delegate, or Resident Commissioner in any other State, $10,000.”

Section 441a(c) separately adjusts the subsection (b) and (d) limits by the certified percentage difference between the preceding calendar year's CPI and the 1974 base period, for the calendar year of application. Section 441a(e) supplies annual Commerce certification and defines voting-age population as resident population eighteen or older. The presidential national-party allowance in subsection (d)(2) is distinct: two cents multiplied by national voting-age population, additional to expenditures by a national committee acting as a presidential candidate's principal campaign committee. It is background, not the spending allowance applied to this Senate dispute.

**Section 441a(a)(7)(B)(i):** “expenditures made by any person in cooperation, consultation, or concert, with, or at the request or suggestion of, a candidate, his authorized political committees, or their agents, shall be considered to be a contribution to such candidate”. Subparagraph (ii) separately concerns financing dissemination, distribution or republication of candidate-prepared campaign materials. Subsection (a)(8) separately attributes earmarked or intermediary-directed contributions to their original source and requires identification of that source and intended recipient. Their distinct triggers must not be fused. Their existence supplies no finding that this transaction satisfied them.

The government additionally invokes §441a(a)(2)'s contribution restrictions and characterizes subsection (d) as supplementary spending permission. The source supplement identifies that submission, but does not reproduce the complete subsection (a)(2). Before making that separate restriction the dispositive basis, inspect its operative terms in the official Code and its preservation/application in the briefs; do not infer a complete contribution judgment from this summary.

## Lower-court grounds and strongest supported positions

The District Court used Buckley and MCFL to construe “expenditures in connection with” narrowly and avoid the constitutional dispute. The Tenth Circuit rejected importing the express-advocacy limitation into party spending it regarded as coordinated, deferred to FEC advisory opinions 1984-15 and 1985-14, and used a clearly identified candidate plus electioneering-message standard. It relied on corruption and its appearance, the structural party–candidate relationship, and congressional electoral experience. The appellate opinion expressly recognized that Buckley's treatment of the party-expenditure provision addressed a Fifth Amendment challenge, not the First Amendment issue presented here. That distinction is material.

The Party's strongest supported argument is that paying for its own political message restricts its expression and the association of its members directly. A functional distinction between genuinely independent speech and candidate resources is not answered by calling all party activity coordinated. Electioneering about a registered opponent before a nominee is selected can be party expression without actual consultation. Equalizing voices or reducing campaign costs cannot alone justify restricting independent political advocacy. The Party also presses constitutional avoidance, express advocacy and limits on administrative deference. These are recorded submissions; none is adopted here.

The FEC's strongest supported response is that the party's continuing organizational mission, spending and candidate support may make party expenditures equivalent to contributions, even where a particular communication lacks express consultation. Subsection (d) can be viewed as permission above ordinary contribution restrictions. Categorical administration may protect the candidate-contribution system from circumvention through party intermediaries and corruption or its appearance. A message unmistakably directed at a Senate race may fall within the text even without “vote for” wording. The Court must evaluate that functional and statutory argument; it cannot reject it by overlooking the structural evidence or accept it by declaring actual coordination established.

Required concessions are equally bounded. Independent-spending protection does not automatically immunize candidate-directed payments, earmarked donations, republication or a demonstrated conduit. Legitimate corruption and anti-circumvention interests do not establish that every party message is actually coordinated, that equalization is sufficient, or that every dollar limit is fitted. An avoidance construction must remain a plausible reading of this statute; avoidance is not authority to rewrite the provision. A refusal to reach broader coordinated spending preserves the question, not an affirmative holding sustaining it.

## Law entering the event

The First Amendment protects political speech and association. Buckley, 424 U.S. 1, distinguishes limits on contributions from limits on independent expenditures and distinguishes valid corruption interests from equalization alone. Its resolution of §441a(d)'s Fifth Amendment issue is not an adjudication of the present First Amendment question. MCFL, 479 U.S. 238, supplies independent-expenditure and narrowing-construction reasoning in its distinct nonprofit-corporation setting. NCPAC, 470 U.S. 480, protects independent political advocacy at its actual scope. California Medical, 453 U.S. 182, concerns contribution and conduit regulation and does not categorically decide all party speech; Citizens Against Rent Control, 454 U.S. 290, protects pooling for collective political advocacy. Austin, 494 U.S. 652, remains within its surviving corporate-wealth setting. These authorities do not eliminate the need to classify the transaction and identify the actual justification.

**Day v. Holahan, May 15, 1995:** Its responsive-financing judgment has five votes but no controlling unified rationale: Kennedy, O'Connor, Scalia and Thomas regard the adverse proportional trigger as an expressive burden; the Chief Justice's judgment concurrence is narrower and different. Neither framework may be promoted to a majority rule. Its separately controlling holdings include the unanimous invalidation of Minnesota's $100 annual intermediary-contribution limit because of the established pooling burden and insufficiently fitted corruption/circumvention rationale. That proposition preserves the contribution/expenditure distinction, valid regulation of realistic conduits, and meaningful collective association; it creates no permanent dollar floor. The covered recipient category expressly excluded political-party units and candidate principal campaign committees. Applying its reasoning here is an extension or distinction to be justified, not an already decided party-spending result.

Day's unanimous MCFL application protects MCCL on its actual minimal-business-income and insignificant-corporate-funding record despite falling outside the literal statutory safe harbor. It does not overrule Austin or decide ordinary commercial corporate spending, direct candidate contributions, or materially changed conduit facts. Day's five-Justice severability holding concerning the linked Minnesota affidavit requirement supplies no automatic severability answer for FECA.

**McIntyre, April 19, 1995:** Stevens's opinion joined by the Chief Justice, O'Connor, Kennedy, Souter, Ginsburg and Breyer requires exacting scrutiny—narrow tailoring to an overriding interest—for the individual political leaflet identification demand. It rejects the general voter-information rationale and the insufficiently tailored fraud rationale on the truthful independent leaflet record. Thomas concurs only in the judgment; Scalia dissents. The Court expressly leaves candidate finance, campaign committees, organizational spending and materially different disclosure settings for separate analysis. It is therefore relevant speech-protection law with an express boundary, not a controlling resolution of party expenditure limits.
