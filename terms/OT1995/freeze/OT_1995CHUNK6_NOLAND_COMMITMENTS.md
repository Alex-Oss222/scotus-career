## Reach, record and enacted framework

Granted merits review of the Sixth Circuit's affirmance supplies the review channel. All nine event-date Justices participated at argument and decision; no recusal, quorum defect, preservation impediment or independent threshold disposition is established. The eight Associate commitments below reach the statutory question.

First Truck's Chapter 11 case commenced April 10, 1986, and converted to Chapter 7 August 1, 1988. The disputed IRS claims are nonpecuniary penalties on postpetition taxes, allowed as administrative expenses under §503(b)(1)(C). Taxes and interest retain administrative priority. The estate cannot pay all creditors. No Government misconduct is found. The Sixth Circuit expressly disclaimed automatic subordination of every tax penalty and required equitable consideration. Its actual reasons nevertheless concern the noncompensatory penalty, protection of creditors who supplied value, preserved priority for the Government's tax and interest losses, and the failed enterprise's insufficient assets. The legal adequacy of those reasons, rather than a fictional automatic-all-penalties rule, is reviewed.

Case-era §507(a)(1) gives first priority to §503(b) administrative expenses and the specified title 28 fees and charges. Section 726(a) expressly makes distribution subject to §510; paragraph (4)'s lower treatment reaches penalties arising **before the earlier of the order for relief or appointment of a trustee**, and only insofar as they do not compensate actual pecuniary loss. Its temporal qualification does not reach every penalty. Section 348(d)'s conversion treatment excepts §503(b) claims. The administrative status here therefore is not erased by conversion. The case-era tax cross-reference is §507(a)(7); later renumbering to (8) changes no result.

Section 510(c), notwithstanding (a) and (b), provides that after notice and a hearing the court **may**, under principles of equitable subordination, subordinate for distribution all or part of an allowed claim to all or part of another allowed claim, or an allowed interest to another allowed interest, and may order a securing lien transferred to the estate. Its allowed-claim coverage includes priority claims. Neither a subordination agreement under (a), a securities claim under (b), nor a lien-transfer ground is established here. Susceptibility to subordination and a legally sufficient ground to exercise that authority remain distinct.

The surviving *Ahlers*, 485 U.S. 197, 206, requires equity within the Code; its §1129 holding does not independently construe §510(c). *Taylor v. Standard Gas*, *Pepper* and *Comstock* establish misconduct-based applications, not a necessary resolution of every possible no-misconduct case. *Simonson* concerns the former statutory penalty prohibition; it does not itself supply a §510(c) priority rule. *Boteler* and *Nicholas* preserve the distinct treatment of postpetition penalties and their compliance function without deciding this subordination question.

## Alternative paths and sufficient ground

**Affirmance is a substantial alternative:** §726 expressly subjects distribution to §510; §510(c) covers allowed claims without an express misconduct element. The Edwards/DeConcini statements mention penalty claims susceptible to subordination and continued judicial development. The lower courts professed individual balancing, and protection of actual-loss creditors in this liquidating estate supports that exercise. This argument defeats an asserted immunity for administrative claims and warrants preserving genuine statutory equity.

**The adopted sufficient ground:** Those propositions establish power to subordinate a qualifying claim, but do not authorize replacement of Congress's ordering of categories by a judicial preference for compensatory claims. The reasons actually used recur whenever this class of nonpecuniary postpetition tax penalties competes for insufficient assets with actual-loss creditors. The lower court's disclaimer and balancing terminology do not supply a different equitable circumstance. Congress expressly gave these penalties administrative treatment while assigning the temporally different §726(a)(4) class a lower rank. Section 510(c)'s reference to principles of equitable subordination preserves adjudication of claims; it does not delegate a new general ranking of this statutory class. This conclusion does not freeze every equitable principle as of 1978 or make statutory priority invulnerable to a proper claim-specific ground.

**Broader reversal on indispensable creditor misconduct:** The Government's historic-doctrine argument has support in the classic cases. Its strongest objection is that those cases address their own creditor relationships, while §510(c)'s text and legislative discussion permit a serious dispute over further development. This case can be resolved by the invalid reasons actually employed. Every commitment below leaves undecided whether creditor misconduct is necessary in all equitable-subordination cases; none affirmatively authorizes a particular no-misconduct application, establishes new elements, or finds hypothetical misconduct here.

## Remedy and authority limits

For each Associate, reverse the Sixth Circuit's judgment sustaining subordination on the stated grounds and remand for proceedings consistent with the statutory limitation. The subordinate ranking cannot remain justified by those same class-based reasons relabeled as individual balancing. The decision does not itself calculate or order a distribution, erase tax or interest priority, direct a lien transfer, disallow the penalty, or determine another claim's rights. Further proceedings depend on the actual record and ordinary preservation; the reservation of other possible grounds does not establish that one exists, reopen every factual issue, or require a new misconduct hearing.

*Carlisle* controls its integrated Rules 29/45 problem, not every exercise of equity. Its restriction is relevant by analogy, with §510(c)'s express grant kept distinct. *Lonchar* rejects an additional dismissal sanction while preserving genuine independent stay equity and the conditional protection of required process; it is not a general prohibition on equitable adjudication. *Sheets* adds no bankruptcy priority or equity rule. The refreshed AEDPA and PLRA admissions have their provision-specific reach; neither supplies a new construction of §510(c) or changes this case's stage.

The already-admitted PLRA §804(b) is specifically checked against the [dated official extract](../sources/chunk6_c/NOLAND_PLRA_804B_DATED_EXTRACT.md), committed at `6c944e9`. Its new §523(a)(17) concerns a debt “for a fee imposed by a court for the filing of a case, motion, complaint, or appeal, or for other costs and expenses assessed with respect to such filing, regardless of an assertion of poverty by the debtor under section 1915 (b) or (f) of title 28, or the debtor’s status as a prisoner, as defined in section 1915(h) of title 28.” This appeal concerns distribution priority for postpetition tax penalties, not discharge of a court filing fee or associated filing expense. That exact enactment supplies no ground to alter these commitments; no general prisoner-only construction or disputed temporal application is decided.
