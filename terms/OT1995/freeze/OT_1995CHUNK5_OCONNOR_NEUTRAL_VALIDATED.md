## Exact submitted scope and source corrections

Petitioner's merits question is:

> Is a prima facie claim of age discrimination precluded by the fact that the employee who secures the job that the claimant was denied is within the class of persons protected against age discrimination?

The runtime supplies that question as the authorized submitted review scope. A request in the petition for broader review is not itself an additional granted question. The cleared reply states that the petition also requested review of ordinary direct/circumstantial proof, but contends that issue and comparative qualification are outside the present case; it argues their sufficiency in the alternative. Respondent invokes those matters to preserve the judgment. Their procedural treatment must be addressed, rather than omitted or assumed to have been independently granted.

The runtime requires these corrections:

- Finnell was **40**, turning 41 on August 27, 1990, not “over forty,” at the discharge. He was within the protected group because §631(a) includes people **at least 40**. O'Connor was 56; the sixteen-year difference is material without fixing a minimum age differential as law.
- The lower judgment rests on several grounds besides replacement within the protected class. The discovery ruling and postjudgment ruling also survived the appeal, but no additional Supreme Court question concerning them is established.
- Both lower opinions expressly recognize contrary circuit approaches to the replacement-age requirement. “No material conflict” is inaccurate. The parties disagree over the strength and scope of particular circuit precedents; no exhaustive circuit census is necessary to establish that the conflict is real.
- The district opinion and party briefs use August 9, 1990 for the termination; the appellate narrative uses August 10. This difference is retained rather than silently resolved. It does not change the ages or the submitted legal issue. The district merits appendix heading prints case number C-C-91-227-P, whereas the postjudgment order and appeal identify C-C-91-277-P/CA-91-277-C-C-P; that transcription/source discrepancy supplies no separate case or merits inference.

## Judgment under review and distinct lower grounds

The Western District of North Carolina granted summary judgment July 27, 1993, dismissing the ADEA claim with prejudice. The Fourth Circuit, No. 94-1214, 56 F.3d 542, affirmed. Its judgment was dated April 21, 1995; the supplied lower appendix records rehearing denial May 31 and mandate June 8. The latter is a **mandate making the April judgment effective**, not a new Supreme Court action. Review proceeds under §1254(1) from that final appellate judgment.

| Lower component | Actual reasoning and consequence |
|---|---|
| Replacement formulation | The Fourth Circuit viewed this as unlike a typical mass reduction in force because O'Connor could identify Finnell as his effective replacement. Its prima facie formulation required protected status, adverse discharge/demotion, performance meeting legitimate expectations, and a comparably qualified replacement outside the protected group. Finnell's age defeated the fourth element. |
| Alternative reduction-in-force formulation | Even assuming that framework, the court found no evidence creating a genuine issue on satisfactory performance compared with retained managers, or selection that failed to treat age neutrally. It relied on the prior territory reduction, slow responses to problem accounts, unrefrigerated-truck issue, and ability to manage enlarged districts. It rejected the probative force of the earlier review, bonus, salary comparisons and partial improvement; it found insufficient comparable evidence about Hunter and treated the four-person sample as inadequate statistical proof. These are additional grounds, not merely restatements of the under-40 rule. |
| Ordinary direct/circumstantial proof | The court separately found insufficient evidence that age determined discharge. It treated the remarks as isolated or lacking a nexus to the decision and incorporated its rejection of the indirect performance/comparator evidence. This ground does not formally depend only on the categorical fourth element, although petitioner disputes the entire handling of the evidence. |
| Discovery | The court found no abuse of discretion in the challenged discovery rulings. The opinion provides little substantive detail; this validation does not invent the requests, withheld material, or a new discovery entitlement. |
| Postjudgment Dennis affidavit | The district court treated the “new trial” motion after summary judgment as one under Rule 60(b)(2), and found both insufficient diligence and insufficient likelihood of a different outcome. The Fourth Circuit upheld denial under either Rule 59 or Rule 60. The evidence was not part of the timely summary-judgment record merely because the merits brief later recites it. |

Judge Butzner followed binding circuit precedent on replacement age while rejecting its legal and policy justification. He dissented from the ordinary-proof conclusion and would have vacated for a full evidentiary hearing, regarding Williams's “too damn old” statement and Dennis's account as enough for a jury and the latter as not reasonably available earlier. That is a lower-court separate opinion, not a current Supreme Court position or an established finding about diligence.

## Material record and competing applications

Consolidated operated cafeteria and vending services as a Canteen division. O'Connor had managed 4Cs North, moved to the troubled 4Cs South around the end of 1989/start of 1990, and lost some territory to Kiser in July. Consolidation then combined North and South Carolina management into two larger districts. Finnell, 40, and Kiser, 35, led the new districts; O'Connor's employment ended. Hunter, 57, moved from management to sales. The lower opinions call that a demotion; respondent disputes the characterization. This record does not authorize a fresh finding resolving it.

The employer says the July territorial reduction followed slow response to problem accounts and failure to respond promptly to unrefrigerated food deliveries; that it preceded Williams's knowledge of the broader consolidation; and that the resulting larger districts called for capabilities Finnell and Kiser had demonstrated. It disputes treating Finnell as a literal one-for-one replacement because his new territory included his former South Carolina responsibilities and other territory as well as O'Connor's.

O'Connor relies on twelve years of management experience, a “commendable minus” 1989 review, a large performance bonus, superior comparative review/salary placement, some improvement in a troubled division, and the contemporaneous July memorandum describing the territorial redistribution as allowing a more even distribution of volume. He disputes the later performance explanation and argues that current satisfactory performance, comparative qualifications and pretext could not be resolved for the employer at summary judgment. He does not deny the need to satisfy the legitimate qualification/performance element; he contests its demanding application and the folding of the employer's explanation into his initial burden.

The reported Williams remarks are: a “young blood” statement two days before termination in response to another employee approaching 50; “O'Connor, you are too damn old for this kind of work” in the preceding weeks, for which O'Connor could not recall the broader conversation; and a golf-related comment. Williams disputed the work-related remark. Petitioner argues the decisionmaker, workplace setting, proximity, pattern and disputed performance explanations together support an inference, even if none is a literal admission of the discharge's cause. Respondent argues the statements were innocuous, ambiguous or unconnected to the employment decision. The evidence must be considered as evidence and reasonable inferences, not transformed into undisputed motive or a universal requirement for direct evidence.

Dennis's later affidavit reports Williams saying after discharge that they were all getting old, O'Connor was getting old, and other employees were nearing 50; it also attributes the discharge directive to Arts. The district court considered this insufficient to establish the decision's motive and independently found no diligence: Dennis was known, available in Charlotte, and not questioned because of litigation strategy, cost and concern that an employed witness might be reluctant. Whether that conclusion was correct is distinct from the submitted prima facie question. Respondent's merits note 7 expressly objects that review of the postjudgment ruling is not before the Court. Nothing here treats the affidavit as timely admitted or vacates its exclusion.

## Governing statute and proof rules

The party briefs reproduce **29 U.S.C. §623(a)(1)**:

> It shall be unlawful for an employer ... to fail or refuse to hire or to discharge any individual or otherwise discriminate against any individual with respect to his compensation, terms, conditions, or privileges of employment, because of such individual's age.

Section **631(a)** limits the prohibitions to individuals who are **at least 40 years of age**. It sets the claimant's protected minimum; its text does not expressly set a replacement-age requirement. This validation relies on the reproduced operative text, not a claim to independent complete 1994 Code verification. Petitioner's analogy to other detailed statutory exceptions does not place seniority, benefit plans, early retirement, or the bona fide executive retirement exception in issue. No such defense is found satisfied and no imprecise paraphrase of those distinct provisions is adopted as an operative rule.

The two directly material public Standards entries are:

| Exact Standards title | Present operation effective | Controlling operation and preserved limits |
|---|---|---|
| **Age motivation, pension tenure and willfulness** | Hazen Paper, April 20, 1993 | Disparate treatment requires age actually to motivate and have a determinative influence on the employment decision. A correlated feature such as service-based pension status is not automatically age; an actual age proxy or mixed age/pension motivation may matter. Age-triggered vesting and disparate impact remain unresolved. Its willfulness rule concerns knowing or reckless disregard, not a new prima facie element; it creates no automatic liquidated-damages award here. |
| **Rejected employment explanations and circumstantial discrimination proof** | St. Mary's Honor Center v. Hicks, June 25, 1993 | Once the employer produces admissible nondiscriminatory explanations, proof of a prima facie case and rejection of those explanations do not compel judgment for the employee. They may support intentional discrimination without a mandatory extra category of independent/direct proof. Burdine's ultimate persuasion allocation remains; Hicks adopts neither a replacement burden allocation nor universal Title VII/§1983 equivalence. |

McDonnell Douglas, 411 U.S. 792 (1973), Furnco, 438 U.S. 567 (1978), Burdine, 450 U.S. 248 (1981), Teamsters, 431 U.S. 324 (1977), and Aikens, 460 U.S. 711 (1983), supply the surviving distinction between an initial inference, an employer's production of a legitimate reason, and the employee's ultimate persuasion burden. Their application must fit the specific discrimination claim rather than mechanically transfer every fact in the original hiring formulation. Both parties proceed within a McDonnell Douglas framework applied to ADEA cases by the lower courts. The present question can be considered on that shared assumption without deciding every feature of its wholesale applicability under the ADEA.

At summary judgment, Anderson v. Liberty Lobby, 477 U.S. 242 (1986), and Celotex, 477 U.S. 317 (1986), require evidence supporting a genuine issue of material fact, with reasonable inferences for the nonmovant; neither speculation nor a merely colorable assertion suffices. This is not a license to choose between supported competing explanations or make fresh credibility findings. Failure of a formal prima facie formulation and failure of all ordinary evidence are distinct propositions.

## Strongest party positions and alternative paths

**Petitioner's focused position:** The Act protects an individual against discrimination because of age, not merely because of membership in an over-40 class. A person 56 replaced by someone 40 can suffer exactly the prohibited differential treatment; the difference between a 39-year-old and a 40-year-old replacement has no categorical relationship to that inference. Age is a matter of degree. The relative age of the employee and replacement, rather than the replacement's crossing the protected threshold, can support the initial inference. This reading accords with the purposes of an evidentiary presumption and the ordinary possibility of discrimination within the covered range.

**Petitioner's broader and narrower alternatives:** His primary formulation would require no characteristics of the replacement at all: protected status, qualification, discharge and continued need for the job should call for an explanation. Alternatively, any younger replacement should suffice without a fixed minimum or “substantially younger” element; he acknowledges that the inference weakens as the age gap narrows. He opposes the Chamber's ten-year line and a mandatory substantial-gap requirement because they may be underinclusive, invite evasion, and confuse initial proof with ultimate persuasion. These are party positions, not established law. The record's sixteen-year difference permits consideration of a narrower age-inference rule without resolving every small-gap case.

**Respondent's strongest defense of the rule:** A prima facie case must identify circumstances that, if unexplained, support discrimination; it is not merely a demand for an employer to justify every adverse action affecting a covered employee. Younger people commonly replace departing older workers for nondiscriminatory reasons. The protected/unprotected division supplies an objective threshold tied to the statute, while ordinary direct/circumstantial proof and the alternative reduction-in-force formulation remain available. Respondent therefore does not assert that all intra-group discrimination is lawful; it defends a particular route to an initial presumption. It distinguishes EEOC §1625.2(a)'s recognition of intra-group discrimination and legislative remarks about substantive coverage from a judicially created proof scheme.

**Counterarguments the frame preserves:** Mere administrative convenience does not show why crossing 40 is necessary to infer discrimination against a 56-year-old when the Act protects age rather than group membership. But rejecting the under-40 requirement does not itself establish petitioner's broader proposition that no age-related circumstance is ever needed. Nor does Hazen's ultimate motivation requirement compel a plaintiff to prove the ultimate case before receiving the McDonnell Douglas inference. A context-sensitive relative-age inquiry can reject the categorical barrier while retaining an inference of discrimination and the unchanged ultimate burden.

**Alternative grounds for affirmance:** Respondent argues that satisfactory current performance, comparability and ordinary proof fail independently of replacement age, including the third element even if the fourth is corrected. It says the later consolidation and contemporaneous performance problems made the earlier review/bonus uninformative. Petitioner objects to reaching these issues within the submitted scope and, alternatively, points to the contemporaneous volume memorandum, improvement, stronger prior review, qualifications and work-related age remark. The question is not only whether a formally independent paragraph exists below: the Court must consider whether any ground remains adequate under the corrected proof rule and proper summary-judgment treatment, or whether that assessment belongs on remand.

**Available bounded paths:** The categorical age requirement could be sustained within the particular proof formulation while leaving ordinary routes open; it could be rejected while the same judgment is sustained on a properly reachable independent ground; or its rejection could require reversal/vacatur and remand for the unresolved effect on the remaining grounds. A relative-age formulation, the no-replacement-characteristics formulation, and petitioner's any-younger formulation are distinct alternatives with different implications. No numeric age-gap rule is necessary merely to adjudicate the categorical protected-class issue. A court may leave the exact proof formulation or particular application open where the judgment and remand identify what must be reconsidered.

The party briefs describe an EEOC position favoring the McDonnell Douglas formulation without replacement characteristics and business-amici concerns about an inference tied to age, including a ten-year proposal. Those are descriptions in the read party papers; this validator has not independently read all amicus briefs and does not adopt their characterization as an exhaustive account.

## Remedy, matters not decided, and record limits

Petitioner seeks reversal; respondent seeks preservation of summary judgment. Correcting the under-40 requirement alone neither proves age discrimination nor requires entry of judgment, damages, reinstatement, backpay, a finding of willfulness, or an unconditional trial. A remand may direct application of the corrected rule while preserving the employer's legitimate explanation, petitioner's pretext showing, ordinary Rule 56 constraints, and any properly remaining independent grounds.

A holding must not silently reopen the discovery or Rule 59/60 rulings, place Dennis's affidavit in the original summary-judgment record, or decide postjudgment diligence.
