## 1. What the six sources establish together

The original materials contain affirmative evidence of an arms right enjoyed by individuals, including possession and acquisition, and affirmative Reconstruction evidence that constitutional arms protection was understood as a personal guarantee requiring protection against state interference. They therefore cannot fairly be described as an evidentiary void on those subjects. Their legal forms and limits nevertheless differ: two antebellum state decisions construed the federal guarantee directly; a Reconstruction state decision recognized private keeping and purchase under its state constitution while rejecting direct application of the federal amendment; Congress enacted a geographically and temporally bounded equality-and-protection statute; Howard explained a proposed constitutional amendment; and Benton supplied a later Supreme Court method for applying a particular Bill of Rights guarantee against the States. None is itself a Supreme Court holding incorporating the Second Amendment or adjudicating the Maryland purchase policy in this case.

The sources permit an argument connecting an individual right to keep arms, the practical incident of lawful acquisition, Reconstruction protection against state abridgment, and the incorporation inquiry recognized in Benton. They also require separate work on the protected class of arms, lawful regulation, the constitutional clause and governing precedents through which state applicability would operate, the pleaded claim and remedy, and the September 1990 notice inquiry. Neither a militia purpose nor personal ownership alone answers all of those questions.

## 2. Howard's May 23, 1866 explanation of the proposed Fourteenth Amendment

**Original source and reading:** Congressional Globe, 39th Congress, 1st Session, pp. 2764–2766. All three supplied page images were inspected; page 2765 contains the arms passage and page 2766 its operative continuation. The proposed resolution and beginning of Howard's remarks appear on page 2764. [Original page 2764](https://digital.library.unt.edu/ark:/67531/metadc30866/m1/846/), [page 2765](https://digital.library.unt.edu/ark:/67531/metadc30866/m1/847/), [page 2766](https://digital.library.unt.edu/ark:/67531/metadc30866/m1/848/).

Howard explains that Fessenden, chairman on the Senate's part of the Joint Committee of Fifteen, cannot open the discussion because of his health. Howard undertakes to state the committee's views and motives as he understands them. This supplies the speaker's institutional context without converting every sentence into a demonstrated agreement of Congress or of the ratifying States.

On page 2765 he distinguishes the proposed privileges-or-immunities prohibition concerning citizens from the due-process and equal-protection protections concerning all persons. After discussing Article IV and quoting Corfield's account of fundamental privileges, he adds the personal rights secured by the first eight amendments. His express examples include “the right to keep and to bear arms,” alongside speech, press, assembly, petition, protection against quartering, search and seizure, trial protections, bail, and punishment. This is direct evidence that he classed the arms guarantee among personal constitutional rights; it is not an inference drawn merely from his mention of militia organization.

He then acknowledges the existing doctrine that these constitutional guarantees restrain the federal government rather than state legislation. The continuation on page 2766 describes the first section's object as restraining state power and requiring States to respect the fundamental guarantees he has identified. He separately identifies section five as Congress's affirmative enforcement authority; section one imposes restrictions on States, rather than itself conferring legislative power on Congress. His subsequent discussion also treats equal protection and the prohibition of class legislation, and expressly excludes suffrage from the privileges or immunities protected by the proposed first section.

**Support and limit:** The speech supplies substantial, express Reconstruction support for protecting personal arms rights from state abridgment. Its immediate account of enumerated rights runs through the privileges-or-immunities discussion; it does not set out the later selective-incorporation doctrine under the Due Process Clause. The resolution then under discussion had not yet become the ratified amendment, and these three pages do not document every later amendment, every congressional understanding, dissemination to the public, or ratifiers' agreement. Nor do they specify protected weapon categories, purchase-license conditions, an adjudicative standard of review, or damages liability. Those limits qualify the source's weight; they do not erase what Howard expressly said.

## 3. Freedmen's Bureau Act of July 16, 1866

**Original source and reading:** Chapter 200, 14 Stat. 173–177, including every section and the July 16 override attestations. The section 14 text on printed pages 176–177 was checked against the page images, including the page break after “race.” The unrelated preceding statute's July 13 approval on page 173 is not this Act's enactment date. [Official Statutes at Large](https://www.govinfo.gov/link/statute/14/173).

Section one continued the earlier Bureau Act for two years. Section fourteen is the pertinent enacted text; its declaration of rights and its military-jurisdiction provisions have distinct wording and must remain distinct:

> SEC. 14. And be it further enacted, That in every State or district where the ordinary course of judicial proceedings has been interrupted by the rebellion, and until the same shall be fully restored, and in every State or district whose constitutional relations to the government have been practically discontinued by the rebellion, and until such State shall have been restored in such relations, and shall be duly represented in the Congress of the United States, the right to make and enforce contracts, to sue, be parties, and give evidence, to inherit, purchase, lease, sell, hold, and convey real and personal property, and to have full and equal benefit of all laws and proceedings concerning personal liberty, personal security, and the acquisition, enjoyment, and disposition of estate, real and personal, including the constitutional right to bear arms, shall be secured to and enjoyed by all the citizens of such State or district without respect to race or color, or previous condition of slavery. And whenever in either of said States or districts the ordinary course of judicial proceedings has been interrupted by the rebellion, and until the same shall be fully restored, and until such State shall have been restored in its constitutional relations to the government, and shall be duly represented in the Congress of the United States, the President shall, through the commissioner and the officers of the bureau, and under such rules and regulations as the President, through the Secretary of War, shall prescribe, extend military protection and have military jurisdiction over all cases and questions concerning the free enjoyment of such immunities and rights, and no penalty or punishment for any violation of law shall be imposed or permitted because of race or color, or previous condition of slavery, other or greater than the penalty or punishment to which white persons may be liable by law for the like offence. But the jurisdiction conferred by this section upon the officers of the bureau shall not exist in any State where the ordinary course of judicial proceedings has not been interrupted by the rebellion, and shall cease in every State when the courts of the State and the United States are not disturbed in the peaceable course of justice, and after such State shall be fully restored in its constitutional relations to the government, and shall be duly represented in the Congress of the United States.

**Support and limit:** Congress expressly situated a constitutional arms right among rights of citizens concerning personal liberty, personal security, and property, and required enjoyment without distinctions of race, color, or previous slavery. The provision is affirmative enacted evidence, not merely a speaker's assertion. Its citizens, equal-benefit language, territorial and restoration conditions, and Bureau military-jurisdiction limits cannot be omitted. It is neither an enacted nationwide, perpetual handgun-purchase code nor itself a judicial holding about the Fourteenth Amendment, which it predates. Equal enjoyment and recognition of a constitutional right are both present; describing the provision only as militia administration omits its words, while describing it as resolving every substantive restriction omits its scope and structure.

## 4. Nunn v. State, 1 Ga. 243 (1846)

**Original source and reading:** The full judicial text, including the statute, procedural objections, treatment of other state decisions, and final disposition, was read. The online transcription is an original-opinion reproduction, not a modern doctrinal summary. [Opinion text](https://guncite.com/court/state/1ga243.html).

The Georgia statute regulated sales, keeping, and carrying of designated weapons, with a horseman's-pistol exception and other provisos whose uneven wording the court discussed. Nunn's indictment and conviction concerned having and keeping a pistol other than a horseman's pistol; the charge did not allege concealment. The evidence identified a breast pistol in his hand. The court ultimately reversed the judgment and quashed the proceeding because the challenged prohibition reached open bearing, while sustaining the prohibition of concealed carrying.

The court expressly rejected limiting the right to militia members or militia weapons and described it as belonging to the whole people, including women and young persons. It also expressly connected that broad possession-and-bearing protection to preparation of a well-regulated militia. The personal holders of a right and a public militia purpose thus coexist in its reasoning. Its discussion of natural self-defense and its contrast between legitimate regulation and destruction of the right are material to its conclusion.

**Support and limit:** Nunn is strong original judicial evidence for a personally held right broader than militia enrollment and for protection of the pistol involved there. It applied its understanding of the federal constitutional guarantee to state law, acknowledging but rejecting the view that the amendment restrained only the national government. It is an 1846 state-court ruling, not a Supreme Court holding or a Fourteenth Amendment incorporation decision. Its broad statements must be read with its actual approval of concealed-carry restrictions. It did not hold that every weapon regulation or every manner of bearing was forbidden, and it did not adjudicate a licensing official's damages liability.

## 5. Andrews v. State, 50 Tenn. (3 Heisk.) 165 (1871)

**Original source and reading:** The complete supplied opinion text was read: arguments, Freeman's opinion, the stated concurrences and Sneed qualification, Nelson's separate opinion, Turney's express concurrence in that separate opinion, and the footnotes. The reproduction identifies Page v. State as a separate case removed to a different file; no proposition from that absent case is attributed to Andrews. [Opinion text](https://guncite.com/court/state/50tn165.html).

Freeman's opinion first applies Barron's federalism reasoning and holds that the federal arms amendment does not constrain the State. It then construes Tennessee's own guarantee of keeping and bearing arms for common defense, together with the express state power to regulate wearing arms to prevent crime. The state and federal clauses are discussed as protecting related interests against different governments; the operative state-law analysis does not amount to federal incorporation.

The opinion expressly calls keeping arms, with its necessary incidents, a private individual right of the citizen rather than of the soldier. It says keeping necessarily includes purchase, appropriate ammunition, repair, transport to and from home for those ends, practice, and ordinary use. Its militia-related rationale explains why citizens must become familiar with protected arms in peace; it does not confine possession to persons actually serving. See pp. 177–185.

Those protected arms, however, are identified by their suitability to the citizen's military preparation. The opinion includes rifles, shotguns, muskets, and repeaters; it does not equate all pistols or all instruments capable of defense. It sustains prohibition of dirks, sword canes, Spanish stilettos, belt pistols, and pocket pistols. Whether a weapon labeled a revolver falls within the protected class requires evidence: a military repeater and an unsuitable pistol can share that designation. The statutory prohibition is too broad insofar as it prevents keeping and ordinary use of a protected weapon even at home, for repair, or to protect a child from a rabid dog. Regulation of public carrying remains available. Restrictions on carrying to church or public assemblies illustrate permissible regulation, and the right is subject to others' rights and the community's lawful interests. See pp. 179–188.

The opinion rejects a claim that anticipated self-defense permits use of any forbidden weapon. In the case's evidentiary posture, the weapon's character was not shown and habitual carrying since the war did not establish imminent peril. It discusses, but leaves for a properly presented future case, the protection that an actual, imminent defensive need might give to wearing a constitutionally protected weapon under a regulatory law. That discussion is not an unlimited private-carry holding. The concluding disposition reverses, quashes the indictments, and remands because their generic allegations of carrying a pistol fail to specify its statutory character. See pp. 188–192.

Nicholson and Deaderick concur in the opinion's general views. The report states Sneed's disagreement with limiting legislative power to prohibit wearing arms or narrowing the Act. Nelson's separate opinion, joined by Turney, reads common defense more broadly to include private defense, treats the arms guarantee as extending beyond warfare weapons, and regards the Act as an impermissible prohibition rather than a permissible regulation. It accepts public rather than concealed wearing as a possible regulation and maintains a broad right of immediate self-defense. Those more expansive propositions belong to the separate opinion, not Freeman's controlling analysis. See pp. 193–201.

**Support and limit:** Andrews supplies particularly explicit, original support for individual keeping and its acquisition incident. Its military-related protected-weapon boundary, substantial regulatory latitude, nonapplication of the federal amendment, narrower actual disposition, and divergent separate writing must travel with that support. The opening headnote's general constitutional characterization is not an adequate substitute for these distinctions.

## 6. State v. Chandler, 5 La. Ann. 489 (1850)

**Original source and reading:** The entire opinion, including the homicide instructions and the actual remand, was read. [Opinion text](https://guncite.com/court/state/5la489.html).

Chandler rejected the requested proposition that Louisiana could not prohibit concealed or open weapons. It upheld the 1813 prohibition of concealed dirks, daggers, knives, pistols, and other deadly weapons because it left carrying in full open view available. Its discussion expressly identifies the federal constitutional guarantee with open carrying for defense of oneself and one's country. This is affirmative evidence of a personal defensive understanding, together with approval of regulation of carrying's manner.

The judgment was reversed for the separate error concerning the requested self-defense instruction. The court explained the distinction between an actual physical attack reasonably indicating deadly or felonious danger and an unprovoked attack on a passive victim; it could not determine from the record which factual account was true. It remanded for trial on manslaughter alone because the earlier verdict substantially acquitted Chandler of murder. The constitutional passage did not invalidate the concealed-weapons statute or cause that reversal.

**Support and limit:** Chandler is a pre-Fourteenth Amendment state court's interpretation of a federal guarantee, not a Supreme Court incorporation ruling. It neither confines the right to military service nor supports unrestricted concealed carrying. Its individual-defense discussion cannot be detached from the restriction it upheld or substituted for its actual ground of disposition.

## 7. Benton v. Maryland, 395 U.S. 784 (1969)

**Original source and reading:** The complete official United States Reports text, pp. 784–813, was read, including all Court-opinion footnotes, White's concurrence, and Harlan's dissent joined by Stewart with its footnote qualification about earlier positions. [Official opinion PDF](https://tile.loc.gov/storage-services/service/ll/usrep/usrep395/usrep395784/usrep395784.pdf).

The Court applies the Double Jeopardy Clause to States through the Fourteenth Amendment, expressly overruling Palko insofar as inconsistent. Its analysis asks whether the particular guarantee is fundamental to the American scheme of justice, uses historical roots and the States' recognition of the protection, and rejects a diluted version of an incorporated guarantee: once that decision is made, the same constitutional standards govern state and federal action. See pp. 793–796. The judgment vacates and remands; the larceny conviction cannot stand, and the state court is to consider whether evidence from the improper larceny retrial tainted the burglary conviction.

Benton separately holds that concurrent sentences do not create a jurisdictional bar, because a challenged conviction can carry collateral consequences. It does not abolish every discretionary use of the concurrent-sentence doctrine. It reaches the constitutional question in its circumstances, including the state appellate court's decision to address it and uncertainty concerning the companion conviction and sentence. Those features are part of its vehicle analysis, not a general command to decide every constitutional question in every damages case.

White agrees with incorporation and the absence of a jurisdictional bar, while favoring retention of discretionary concurrent-sentence review limits. Harlan and Stewart would avoid the constitutional issue and dismiss the writ under that doctrine. Harlan rejects selective incorporation in favor of the older due-process inquiry, but concludes that, if reached, this particular retrial would fail that inquiry as well. The dissent's disagreement with incorporation is not part of the Court's holding; its agreement about the injustice of this retrial does not erase the disagreement over method.

**Support and limit:** Benton provides controlling pre-divergence Supreme Court authority for a right-specific incorporation inquiry and for equal constitutional standards after incorporation. It is not an arms holding and does not itself overrule Cruikshank, Presser, or Miller. Using its method to address a different guarantee requires examining that guarantee and expressly handling the force of its particular inherited precedents. An argument that earlier nonapplication reasoning should be reconsidered is available; a statement that Benton already performed that reconsideration for the Second Amendment is not supported by its text.

## 8. Validation and remaining source limits

The six assigned sources have been independently read and the legally significant distinctions above checked against their actual operative text. The full official Benton PDF text and full Freedmen's Bureau Act text were read; the two statutory page images resolve OCR and page-break issues in section fourteen. Howard's supplied three original page images were inspected, not merely an isolated arms quotation. Nunn, Andrews, and Chandler were read as full opinion transcriptions with their procedural endings and the available separate writings. These originals are mutually relevant historical sources, not a claim that one online aggregator supplies the governing federal rule.

The state-opinion reproductions were not independently collated against a second printed-report scan. They display reporter page markers and complete endings, and no internal gap affecting the stated holdings was identified; the expressly omitted separate Page case was not used. The Howard pages establish his explanation but are not a complete ratification-history collection. No missing item is filled by modern commentary, later firearms doctrine, or inference about a Justice's current choice. These are limits on particular uses of the sources, not a basis for declaring the supplied primary history absent.

The neutral packet's distinct questions of current constitutional merits, inherited Supreme Court authority, claim preservation, defendant capacity, relief, and conduct-date immunity remain for the authorized modeling stage. This addendum supplies historical evidence and incorporation-method authority without resolving them.
