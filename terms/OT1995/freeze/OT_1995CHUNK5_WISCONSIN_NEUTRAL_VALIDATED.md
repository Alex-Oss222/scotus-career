## Event, issues and judgment under review

The submitted review concerns the lawfulness of Secretary Mosbacher's July 15, 1991 refusal to adjust the 1990 census using the proposed post-enumeration survey (PES), particularly the constitutional standard applied by the Second Circuit. It is not a general referendum on all sampling or a judicial selection of the statistically best conceivable census. The inventory establishes the merits stage; this freeze does not invent or admit an additional historical grant/order or settle every scope question from a brief's characterization.

The district court, **E.D.N.Y., 822 F. Supp. 906 (April 13, 1993)**, upheld nonadjustment under arbitrary-and-capricious review. It found adjustment would improve counts “for most purposes” and described a powerful case for adjustment, while declining to substitute its judgment for the Secretary's reasoned choice. Its statement that it probably would order adjustment under de novo review was not an adjustment order. It did not find that every geographical measure improved. Its separate release of adjusted block-level tapes and dissolution of a protective order, A91–94, must not be disturbed or expanded without support that this issue is within review. The appendix's “Western District” heading at A41 is anomalous; use E.D.N.Y.

The Second Circuit, **34 F.3d 1114 (August 8, 1994)**, **vacated and remanded**; it did not compel use of adjusted numbers. Kearse wrote for herself and Leval; Timbers dissented by adopting the district court's analysis. The majority rejected both judicial de novo determination and the ordinary APA standard for the constitutional claim as it understood this record. After finding that challengers showed failure to make the required good-faith effort toward equality as nearly as practicable, it required the Secretary to show that refusal to adjust **(a) furthered a legitimate governmental objective and (b) was essential to achieving that objective**. Its descriptions of strict scrutiny do not replace this exact remand formulation with a different test. A23, A33–40.

The panel separately rejected the States' §195 bar, construing it with §141(a) and the 1976 amendments as permitting statistical supplementation of an actual enumeration. That is a lower ruling under challenge, not settled Supreme Court law. A23–25.

The capsule's “no material conflict” statement is incomplete: Timbers specifically cited **City of Detroit v. Franklin, 4 F.3d 1367 (6th Cir. 1993), and Tucker v. Department of Commerce, 958 F.2d 1411 (7th Cir. 1992)** as conflicting treatment. Wisconsin's brief discusses their methodological-review, standing and political-question reasoning. Their complete opinions were not independently read here; do not assert that every holding or remedy is identical, or turn their historical petition treatment into an in-world Court action.

## Material facts and distinct information sets

1. The enumeration used mailed questionnaires, repeated contacts, personal follow-up and coverage-improvement programs, including outreach directed at hard-to-count populations. Those substantial efforts do not erase the acknowledged differential undercount. Wisconsin's brief distinguishes the resident count, **248,709,873**, from the apportionment count, **249,632,692**; do not label the latter simply the resident enumeration.
2. The PES involved roughly 170,000 households and 5,000 blocks; the Secretary gives 171,390 housing units/group quarters and 5,290 block clusters. Matching and dual-system estimation, 1,392 poststrata and smoothing generated factors applied to blocks and aggregated. Whether those group estimates sufficiently capture within-group geographic heterogeneity is contested; neither side's preferred estimator is stipulated truth.
3. The proposed 1991 adjustment estimated about a **2.1%** national undercount, with substantially higher undercount rates among several minority groups. The contemporaneous proposal would move seats from Wisconsin and Pennsylvania to California and Arizona. Alternative smoothing could move only Wisconsin's seat to California. The dispute includes geographic shares, uncertainty, error weights and effects of modeling choices, not merely whether adding estimated persons improves the national total.
4. The district court's footnote and federal brief report later corrections, including a processing error, reducing estimated undercount to about **1.6%**. The federal brief says corrected adjusted figures would transfer **one seat from Wisconsin to California**. Respondents acknowledge that later one-seat calculation while arguing all discussed alternatives identify the Wisconsin/California distortion. These later corrections matter to present relief/standing, but cannot be silently substituted for the evidence available to the Secretary on July 15, 1991. The roughly 1.4% bias-corrected estimate discussed in the Secretary's total-error analysis is another distinct measure.
5. The 1989 stipulation required new agency consideration with an open mind, a PES of at least 150,000 households suitable for possible correction, published guidelines, expert advice, detailed explanation for nonadjustment and a July 15, 1991 decision. It retained lawful supervisory/decision authority. Its requirement of **de novo agency reconsideration** is not a command for **de novo judicial choice**.
6. The eight-member Special Advisory Panel divided evenly. The Undercount Steering Committee favored adjustment seven to two; Director Bryant favored it while acknowledging professional disagreement. The Under Secretary/Administrator opposed adjustment. Neither a majority of advisers nor administrative hierarchy alone resolves statutory reasonableness. The district court rejected the asserted showing of improper partisan influence; the Secretary expressly found no unprofessional or political manipulation of the actual 1990 PES.

## The Secretary's eight guidelines and essential qualifications

These are administrative criteria adopted under the stipulation, not the words of §§141 or 195. The source receipt gives precise appendix references for each.

| Guideline | Actual operation and material qualification |
|---|---|
| 1. Accuracy and supplementation | Presumed the enumeration most accurate at national, state and local levels unless adjustment was shown more accurate under accepted statistical practice/professional judgment; statistical procedures could supplement, not substitute for, the census. The Secretary prioritized geographic/distributive accuracy, accepted likely national numerical improvement, and disputed sufficiently reliable improvement in geographic shares, particularly smaller areas. A presumption in his guidelines is not itself a constitutional presumption. |
| 2. Consistency, completeness and usability | Required useful, consistent and sufficiently detailed counts for reapportionment, redistricting and published uses. The Secretary accepted consistency/detail but disputed quality, including residual heterogeneity. He did not find that block counts could not be produced. |
| 3. Prespecification and robustness | Required prespecified procedures and robustness to reasonable alternative production/statistical models. Perfect prespecification was impossible for both enumeration and adjustment; national robustness did not resolve seat sensitivity and smoothing disputes. His concern about future manipulation coexists with his express finding of no actual manipulation of this PES. |
| 4. Future efforts | Considered both courses' effects on participation and institutional effort. The participation survey showed adverse expressed responses to either outcome; he found no compelling evidence on individual motivation. His predictions about local outreach, funding, enumerators and professional independence favored nonadjustment; they are disputed predictions, not proved inevitable effects. |
| 5. Legality | No adjustment could violate the Constitution or statutes. The Secretary discussed legal concerns but expressly said legal considerations **did not provide a basis for his decision**, given his statistical/substantive grounds. A categorical §195 bar is not his operative agency reason. |
| 6. Sufficient timely information | The guideline required nonadjustment if insufficient data/analysis were available to publish adjusted counts by July 15. He found enough evidence to decide against adjustment while expressing concern about late corrections and incomplete scrutiny. The district court noted adjusted figures were ready by the deadline; this was not an automatic missed-deadline ruling. |
| 7. Disruption | Required assessment of both adjustment and nonadjustment. He predicted adjustment would cause greater redistricting/apportionment disruption, acknowledging litigation under either course. Challengers stressed that users had been warned adjustment remained possible. The district court recognized inconsistent funding rhetoric but did not find it dispositive. |
| 8. Explanation/documentation | Required a clear rationale and professionally adequate documentation. The Secretary found compliance and treated this guideline as neutral: adjustment also could have been adequately explained. Public difficulty understanding statistics was not a separate veto. |

The district court summarized guidelines 1, 2, 3, 4 and 7 as supporting nonadjustment, with 5, 6 and 8 not tipping the balance. That judicial summary does not rewrite the criteria or the Secretary's reasons. Competing loss-function estimates, variance assumptions and small-area inferences require their actual technical support if decisive; neither expert disagreement alone nor numerical improvement alone establishes the governing legal conclusion.

## Party positions, preservation and standing

**Federal petitioners** request reversal and remand with directions to enter judgment for petitioners (printed p.51). They defend the breadth of constitutional/statutory methodological choice, distributive accuracy, the reasoned comparison of uncertain alternatives and avoidance of mechanically importing intrastate districting rules. They argue that discriminatory effect alone does not establish a race-discrimination equal-protection violation under Washington v. Davis and Arlington Heights. They also argue that even if a Karcher-type framework applies, the substantial enumeration efforts and competing evidence defeat a finding that challengers met its initial good-faith threshold. **They expressly agree that neither the Constitution nor the Census Act categorically bars statistical sampling in the decennial census** (pp.26–27). Do not attribute the States' contrary statutory theory to them.

**Wisconsin and Oklahoma** defend reversal through substantial methodological discretion and objection to the panel's burden. They separately argue that §195's apportionment exception bars sampling for the count used to apportion Representatives, and that §141(a)'s permission for sampling remains meaningful for other census subjects/uses. Oklahoma expressly offers a fallback: even if sampling is permitted, permission does not make nonadjustment unlawful. Their institutional/finality concerns do not alone establish a jurisdictional bar under controlling simulated Franklin/Montana.

**Respondents** ask to **affirm the Second Circuit judgment**, preserving a remand for justification, rather than asking this Court's conclusion to order immediate adoption of a particular dataset (printed p.75). They argue that correction of differential undercount improves distribution as well as national totals; the Secretary's selected loss function, presumptions and treatment of minority undercount obscured that improvement. They contest the inference that a stronger alternative adjustment defeats the superiority of the proposed adjustment over the unadjusted count. They defend the representation-based good-faith/justification framework and distinguish an ordinary racial-disparate-impact claim requiring discriminatory purpose. They also argue that the district court's acceptance of the choice under broad agency guidelines does not establish its constitutionality. Their allegations of bad faith remain arguments, not new findings.

**Presentation of §195 is disputed.** Respondents contend under Rule 14.1(a) that a categorical sampling bar was not set forth or fairly included in the States' petitions; they quote the federal petitioners' opposition to divided argument in support. They argue that a related issue is not necessarily a predicate to answering the constitutional question and that a statutory prohibition could raise an additional constitutional issue. They also defend statutory sampling authority on the merits. The bounded briefs establish a live presentation objection, not an admitted historical ruling sustaining or rejecting it. A merits ruling on the bar must explain its lawful reachability; disposition on a narrower constitutional ground can reserve it.

**APA preservation is distinct from APA reviewability.** Federal petitioners state repeatedly that respondents did not challenge on appeal the district court's determination that the decision survived ordinary arbitrary-and-capricious review (pp.20–25,51). Respondents' inspected passages press independent constitutional scrutiny and criticize the district court's limited examination; they do not establish a separately preserved APA reversal claim. Simulated Franklin's availability of review does not erase an actual failure to preserve a particular challenge. A model may discuss APA principles as the lawful review framework, but may not automatically order an APA remand without resolving this preservation limit from the relevant bounded appellate papers if necessary.

**Standing is claim- and remedy-specific.** Federal petitioners raise questions about municipalities' authority to challenge interstate apportionment, individuals proceeding when California did not appeal, and plaintiffs outside States gaining seats invoking others' voting rights. They recognize possible funding injury. Respondents rely on California voters, Arizona's participation, other funding injuries and unitary nationwide relief, arguing one proper plaintiff suffices for the same relief. Arizona's status under the original proposal must not conceal the different later one-seat calculation. These are party arguments; no general dismissal, universal plaintiff standing, automatic entitlement of a State to control its voters' claim, or proved modern allocation is established by them.

**Standing holdings:** Montana's continuing allocation injury and likely declaratory redress were record-specific; initial statutory transmission did not itself extinguish redressability. Franklin recognized the State and its voters' identified lost-seat injury and likely correction through the statutory sequence, without needing a presidential injunction or assuming universal obedience by nonparties. It did not prove that any alternative dataset restores a seat. These rules require application to the present plaintiffs and requested relief; mere general interest in a more accurate census is insufficient.

**Pre-divergence authorities:** Wesberry, Kirkpatrick and Karcher remain available for their actual intrastate equality, good-faith and justification requirements; extending their burden to census methodology is an issue, not an already adopted result. Baker supports constitutional adjudication within judicial competence. Washington v. Davis and Arlington Heights distinguish intentional discrimination from disparate impact in an ordinary equal-protection racial-discrimination claim; respondents' separate representation theory must be analyzed in its own terms. State Farm requires relevant factors and a rational explanation, and rejects forbidden factors, failure to consider an important aspect, record-contrary explanations and explanations implausible as expertise or differing judgment. Bowman and Chenery preserve the actual-agency-ground constraint. Deference is not an excuse to replace that inquiry with the district court's colloquial “beyond the pale” phrase.

**Constitution/statutes:** Article I, §2, cl.3 directs an actual enumeration every ten years in the manner Congress directs; Fourteenth Amendment §2 supplies the operative whole-number population apportionment command. Separate equality arguments arise under the Fifth Amendment. Do not treat obsolete original enumeration exclusions as current instructions.

## Supported alternative paths and remedy constraints

1. **Reject the heightened constitutional framework and uphold this nonadjustment:** distinguish census estimation from avoidable intrastate line drawing, assess substantial enumeration efforts and rational distributive-accuracy grounds, then explain why the preserved challenge fails. Reversal/remand for entry of judgment is the federal request. This need not announce that all statistical adjustment is unlawful or all census decisions unreviewable.
2. **Affirm the constitutional justification remand:** accept the panel's extension of good-faith equality analysis, find its trigger satisfied on the relevant record, and preserve the government's opportunity to show the exact legitimate-objective/essentiality justification. This does not itself prove that adjustment must be compelled.
3. **Require reconsideration under a different, properly stated constitutional standard:** identify the legal error and the unresolved record or application task. A court may reject both the panel's burden and an unexamined assumption that agency discretion resolves every constitutional objection. Remand must perform identified work, not merely offer a second opportunity for preferred results.
4. **APA-based resolution within actual preservation:** apply controlling Franklin and the actual-agency-ground requirement where lawfully reached. If a preserved material defect warrants remand, identify it and leave permissible methodological choice to the proper decisionmaker. If the ordinary APA holding is unchallenged, reviewability alone supplies no license to reopen it. The necessary bounded appellate record should be obtained before choosing a disposition dependent on disputed preservation.
5. **Statutory resolution if properly within review:** assess §§141/195 together and distinguish the States' categorical theory from the federal position and Secretary's stated reasons. Resolve the Rule 14 objection before a broad bar ruling; consider whether narrower grounds make it unnecessary. A holding that sampling is permitted does not require this adjustment; a statutory bar cannot be treated as an agency explanation he actually adopted. No need to decide the constitutionality of a hypothesized prohibition unless that question is lawfully reached and necessary.
6. **Threshold or relief-specific disposition:** apply affirmative standing/redressability requirements to the actual remaining plaintiffs and requested remedy. A defect in one plaintiff's theory does not defeat another properly supported plaintiff seeking the same relief. Completed transmission, California's alleged nonappeal, funding injury and changed seat estimates require their distinct treatment. Do not invent mootness, an absolute timing bar or a presidential injunction.

Any direction to adopt numbers, move a seat, reopen completed districting or change funding must rest on an established legal duty, identified applicable dataset, lawful scope and effective remedy.
