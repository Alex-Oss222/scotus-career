## Matsushita — release, record and scope

**Question and remedy.** The authorized question concerns §1738 recognition of a Delaware class-settlement judgment releasing securities claims within exclusive federal jurisdiction under Exchange Act §27. The petition's additional private-right and tender-timing questions do not expand supplied scope. Petitioners request reversal and remand with instructions to dismiss with prejudice complaints of non-opt-out plaintiffs. Respondents defend rejection of the release and advance alternative constitutional grounds. Rejecting the appellate legal premise with a remand for unresolved objections is distinct from directing immediate dismissal.

**Chronology and actual documents.** Delaware actions began September 1990; the federal Epstein action began **December 3, 1990**, before the December 14 state amended complaint and both proposed settlements. Federal claims were already being litigated, and their appeal was pending at approval of the second settlement. The runtime's suggestion of federal pursuit only after settlement must not govern. The state complaint alleged fiduciary, disclosure, preferential-treatment and corporate-waste theories. The federal complaint challenged the tender arrangements involving Wasserman's preferred-stock exchange and Sheinberg's $21 million payment. Both concern the acquisition; whether the predicate facts overlap in the legally relevant way remains disputed.

The first settlement, without opt-outs or cash to the class, was rejected April 22, 1991: Chancery regarded the state claims as weak, federal claims as arguably meritorious, and the poison-pill benefit as inadequate. Following federal dismissal, the October 22, 1992 second stipulation offered a **$2 million gross fund, reduced by fees**, with opt-outs. The January 21, 1993 hearing preceded approval and February 22 final judgment; Delaware affirmed September 21, 1993. The published approval opinion carries February 16, while the orders refer to February 12; this variation does not establish different substantive proceedings.

The stipulation, JA 172–197, and judgment, JA 204–206 / Pet. App. 73a–75a, expressly release state and federal claims, including the California federal actions, arising from specified acquisition transactions, agreements and settlement. The judgment says they are released 'with prejudice by virtue of the proceedings herein and this Order and Final Judgment.' It also bars/enjoins covered actions. That text matters to the argument about a judicial judgment versus a merely private release; quoting it does not establish its lawful reach or operation against pending federal proceedings.

The class covers holders during the stated September 25, 1990–January 3, 1991 period, subject to exclusions. Distribution is to holders whose shares were tendered or cashed out/converted; the final order awards $250,000 fees/expenses. Valid opt-outs are excluded, with an extension for some late requests. The opinion reports eighteen opt-outs at the hearing, later briefs/Ninth Circuit nineteen. These are counts at different stages, not nineteen affirmative settlement contracts. Petitioners identify Rockford and Minton as opt-outs outside this review. The affected respondents did not object to the second settlement, but other class members did. Any mandate must preserve opt-outs.

Notice identified federal litigation/appeal, the release, objection procedure and exclusion opportunity. It went to record holders with forwarding/copy arrangements and newspaper publication. The judgment expressly finds adequate representation and due process. Respondents challenge the process and support for those findings; notice and opt-out do not simply erase that question. The full printed 1993 hearing contains competing arguments about collusion, small recovery, beneficial-owner notice, federal claim value, discovery and settlement authority. Do not adopt the assertion that no relevant inquiry or objection existed merely because respondents contend that the legally required adequacy inquiry was missing. Conversely, the recital does not resolve the permissible scope of collateral constitutional review.

**Judgment below.** The Ninth Circuit, 50 F.3d 644, denied recognition because it concluded these federal claims depended on different underlying facts and could not have been extinguished by adjudication of the state claims. It rejected contractual consent as an adequate analogy for absent class members. It expressly reserved releases of exclusively federal claims sharing the same predicate facts. It did not decide the fact-specific alternative due-process grounds. Other securities rulings below do not automatically become questions presented here.

**Competing positions.** Petitioners invoke §1738's ordinary same-state-effect rule, Delaware settlement law, Marrese/Kremer, and Nottingham/Grimes: approval of a comprehensive release differs from adjudicating the exclusively federal claim; §27 supplies no express exception and implied repeal requires the appropriate statutory showing. They rely on the judgment, class authority, notice, opt-outs and finality of Delaware findings, and dispute collateral relitigation.

Respondents stress judicially conferred representative authority and fiduciary duties rather than individual consent. They dispute Delaware-law effect/incorporation, independently invoke §27's jurisdictional allocation, and distinguish individual/derivative releases. Hansberry, Shutts and Prezant support their separate adequate-representation objection, concerning representative interests/membership, ability to litigate, counsel's incentives/presentation, scrutiny, notice and the release's asserted protection of counsel. Petitioners dispute these assertions with the formal findings, discovery and adversarial proceedings. Respondents also invoke supremacy against interference with the pending federal case. These alternatives are neither automatically waived because the Ninth Circuit chose another ground nor necessarily fit for fresh appellate factfinding.

The canonical decision neither settles every tender-offer private remedy nor revives an unpresented claim; do not expand this review or use historical Central Bank as governing law.

## Hiles — performance duty and excluded evidence

**Posture.** On the single statutory count, the trial court directed liability, excluded nondefect evidence as irrelevant under its rule, and submitted damages; the jury awarded $492,500. Illinois affirmed, 268 Ill. App. 3d 561, 644 N.E.2d 508. The railroad requests reversal and remand. No separate ordinary-negligence claim was pleaded. Distinguish that fact from the SAA duty's employee remedial framework; the SAA does not itself supply a freestanding private damages cause of action merely because the complaint invokes it.

**Statute.** For the July 18, 1990 injury, then-applicable 45 U.S.C. §2 required 'couplers coupling automatically by impact, and which can be uncoupled without the necessity of men going between the ends of the cars.' The 1994 recodification, §20302(a)(1)(A), requires 'couplers coupling automatically by impact, and capable of being uncoupled, without the necessity of individuals going between the ends of the vehicles.' Official 1994 text was checked. Its opening references §20302(c) and §20303; no exception there is identified as a ground here. The combined-long-load provision concerns hand brakes. Do not impose a new substantive 1994 rule on the 1990 accident.

**Record.** Hiles pulled and Fauver pushed a long-shank coupler in preparation for coupling; the other car was four or five car lengths away, with no prior attempted impact. Drawbar lateral movement accommodates curves. That general fact does not prove why this drawbar was displaced. Fauver said it was slued when uncoupled; Hiles disputes the railroad's inference that uncoupling must have occurred on a curve. Counsel offered curve/slope proof. Inspection less than two hours later reportedly found no defect and AAR compliance: this is **excluded affidavit/proffer evidence**, JA 10–11,21, not a jury finding of statutory compliance. Difficulty moving the bar and what the later inspection proves remain material.

**Railroad's strongest argument.** The Act requires coupling equipment and its performance once properly set, not a categorical prohibition of every task between stationary cars. Ordinary nondefective lateral displacement can be preparation for coupling under Affolder's properly-set qualification. Reply p.8 expressly disclaims requiring identification of a mechanical defect in the employee's prima facie case; the railroad seeks permission to show ordinary nondefective misalignment as a defense. Do not turn that into a universal defense based on a later inspection or mere reasonable care.

**Hiles's strongest argument.** An absolute performance duty can be breached despite mechanically sound parts. Opening a knuckle from outside differs from drawbar alignment requiring entry between cars; ordinary operations and unadopted technology do not necessarily excuse the statutory failure. A later inspection does not prove performance at the injury time. Hiles agrees that a futile/damaging impact attempt need not precede realignment (merits p.27 n.13); the absence of an attempt cannot be made dispositive without confronting that argument and petitioner's related treatment.

**Authority and remedy.** Affolder, Carter, O'Donnell, Wagner and Parker supply the performance/proper-setting/drawbar arguments; verify their whole primary text before a contested extension. Lewis, 711 F.2d 233 (D.C. Cir. 1983), joined by then-Judge Ginsburg, distinguishes equipment from operating procedures in FRA review. Its footnotes 6 and 39 also preserve performance-based liability and distinguish preventive preparation from actual malfunction; footnote 39 discusses failed coupling from misaligned drawbars or closed couplers. Any Ginsburg-specific use must retain both sides of that qualification. Gottshall's actual negligent-emotional-injury holding does not determine the SAA issue.

Affirmance requires the asserted statutory duty on this record. Reversal can reject per se liability for ordinary alignment and permit relevant excluded proof without finding every fact for the railroad. Specify any setting, performance, causation and defense requirements. Neither route silently adds ordinary negligence as a defense to a proven statutory violation, decides an unpleaded negligence claim, or makes the railroad an insurer of all workplace injury.

## Chesapeake / Bell Atlantic — actual post-repeal dispute

**Record and relief.** The complaint sought declarations against former §533(b), an injunction and further just/equitable relief; it did not expressly demand damages. The SG characterizes the case as seeking no damages or other retrospective relief. Complaint ¶28 describes an enhanced existing Alexandria telephone network, hundreds of channels, on-demand libraries, BVS programming and common-carrier capacity for others; ¶40 identifies the existing network. These identify claimed injury, not February 1996 operation/certification findings.

The amended October 7, 1993 final order declared §533(b) unconstitutional facially/as applied and enjoined enforcement **against named plaintiffs**, superseding the original broader order. The Fourth Circuit affirmed, 42 F.3d 181. No continuing operation of the old injunction against a new statute is assumed.

**Actual filings replace the runtime's availability gap.** The SG's February 8 suggestion, respondents' February 8 response, and SG's February reply were read in full. The Government says immediate repeal ended the old provision's effects and seeks appellate vacatur followed by dismissal as moot; new acquisition restrictions are outside this case. Respondents say the same injury persists because their desired open-video/video-dialtone programming awaits FCC rules due within six months, distinguishing ordinary cable and invoking Northeastern Florida. Alternatively they oppose vacatur under Bancorp because the United States voluntarily procured repeal and propose retaining the injunction to seek Rule 60(b) clarification. No such clarification was granted in these sources. The SG replies that ordinary cable service is immediately available even over telephone wires, while open video is an elective regime with different benefits/safeguards; the successor dispute should not receive an initial constitutional adjudication here. These are competing positions, not concessions or findings.

**Enacted distinctions.** Section 302(b)(1) repeals Communications Act §613(b), 47 U.S.C. §533(b). Section 302(b)(3) ends the identified video-dialtone rules/policies at enactment but does not require terminating an already approved system. New Act §651 is **47 U.S.C. §571**; §653 is **47 U.S.C. §573**. Do not confuse Act and Code numbering.

Section 653(a)(1) provides:

> A local exchange carrier may provide cable service to its cable service subscribers in its telephone service area through an open video system that complies with this section. To the extent permitted by such regulations as the Commission may prescribe consistent with the public interest, convenience, and necessity, an operator of a cable system or any other person may provide video programming through an open video system that complies with this section. An operator of an open video system shall qualify for reduced regulatory burdens under subsection (c) of this section if the operator of such system certifies to the Commission that such carrier complies with the Commission's regulations under subsection (b) and the Commission approves such certification.

The Commission must publish receipt and approve/disapprove within ten days. Do not collapse those distinct sentences into respondents' paraphrase that all local-exchange-carrier service awaits regulations; nor does the quotation establish present compliance by this proposed system.

Section 651(a)(3) applies ordinary Title VI to nonradio/non-common-carriage programming unless supplied through an open video system with approved certification; approved systems receive the different part-V/§653(c) treatment. Paragraph (4)'s election from radio/common-carriage routes likewise requires certification. Section 651(c) removes the §214 certificate requirement for video-delivery systems.

Section 653(b)(1)'s six-month rulemaking deadline includes reconsideration. It requires nondiscriminatory, just/reasonable carriage with the §§611/614/615 exception; if demand exceeds capacity, limits operator/affiliate selection to one-third of activated capacity without limiting channels they may offer directly to subscribers; permits one-channel duplicate service only with ready/immediate access; extends sports/network/syndicated exclusivity; and imposes navigation/identification safeguards. The [complete official §302 extract](../sources/chunk4_b/SAFE_telecommunications_1996_section302.txt), including §653(c)–(d) and §652 buyout provisions, was read. Do not infer actual FCC action/certification, a six-month total prohibition, compulsory new physical cable construction or Rule 60(b) success from this text.

**Entering law.** Church of Scientology, November 16, 1992, unanimously recognizes meaningful partial relief as sufficient to prevent mootness without establishing entitlement to all proposed remedies. Northeastern Florida AGC, June 14, 1993, holds continuing exclusion under a successor ordinance preserves the controversy despite changed details, while a replacement actually ending that injury could differ. That portion was joined by Stone-Zsela, White, Stevens, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter and Thomas; it did not automatically reinstate an old injunction.

Bancorp, November 8, 1994, unanimously separates §2106 power from equitable entitlement: mootness bars underlying merits adjudication but permits a just disposition of the judgment. Voluntary settlement ordinarily fails to justify vacatur without a further sufficient equitable reason; a contractual vacatur term alone is insufficient. Exceptional cases remain possible; events outside the movant's choice, including unilateral prevailing-party action, remain distinct. Neither 'repeal' nor 'voluntary' automatically decides this legislative-change case. Anderson v. Green, February 22, 1995, unanimously vacates preliminary constitutional judgments where separate judicial removal of indispensable approval frustrates review and leaves a program contingent, preserving later challenge. Compare its actual facts and equitable ground.

**Threshold paths.** Determine whether the original injury and meaningful lawful relief persist or whether the successor creates a substantially different controversy. Mootness still requires a separate vacatur decision and precise treatment of appellate and trial judgments. Remand to assess the change or permit bounded amendment is distinct from adjudicating the new statute. Denied vacatur does not itself extend the old injunction. This preflight validates the statutory-change/threshold alternatives; direct constitutional adjudication requires further full merits briefing/record review. Actual Turner remains controlling at its content-neutrality/intermediate-scrutiny and evidentiary limits without deciding this different restriction.

### Corrected neutral posture and questions


The petition's printed p.i presents (1) whether the Boyle government-contractor tort defense precludes otherwise permissible Spearin contract claims, and (2) whether DPA-compelled production permits the Government to avoid contractual liability for damages incurred in third-party tort litigation. It introduces these questions through Judge Plager's dissent below. The merits brief restates a broader combined question; the United States states the question as whether these military supply contracts included an implied-in-fact reimbursement obligation. Preserve the actual source distinctions rather than calling any paraphrase the verbatim granted question.

The Federal Circuit's May 4, 1994 judgment, 24 F.3d 188, consolidated appeals from summary judgments dismissing separate Claims Court suits. The parties seek **affirmance**, or **reversal and remand for further proceedings and development of the contract claims**. Petitioners' merits conclusion p.42 and reply pp.20–21 request remand. The present record does not establish entitlement to a final money award, final causation findings, or the amount of recoverable damages.

The runtime's statement that the Federal Circuit simply rejected extension of the performance warranty to third-party costs omits its selected ground. The appellate court expressly recognized that a specifications warranty can reach problems arising after performance (App.17a), left several warranty/breach/damages questions unresolved, and selected **lack of causation because the government-contractor defense would have defeated tort liability** as its ground for rejecting settlement recovery (18a–23a). The Government's narrower account of the warranty's existence and scope is an alternative argument for affirmance, not a substitute description of the appellate holding.

Hercules learned of dioxin risks in 1965 and changed its processing so its product had no measurable dioxin from 1966–1970. Thompson's lack of knowledge of hazards to users is separately described.

Do not claim the full contracts or transcripts were inspected here.

### Lower-court grounds and scope

**Specifications warranty and settlement.** App.17a–23a sets out warranty, breach, and causation as distinct elements. The court discussed doubts about a warranty encompassing settlement losses and scientific proof of injury but expressly rested on the contractor defense/causation ground. It treated the 1983, 1985 and 1987 lower-court defense decisions as demonstrating that litigation to conclusion would have defeated liability and viewed settlement as the manufacturers' voluntary choice. Petitioners and Plager challenge both certainty at the 1984 settlement date and the claimed logical equivalence of the two doctrines.

**Defense fees are separate.** App.23a–25a relied on Kania, construed this warranty as not including defense against meritless tort claims, and found no allegations that such a fee undertaking was directly contemplated at formation. Petitioners distinguish Kania's prosecutorial/jurisdictional setting and invoke third-party-litigation consequential-damages principles (merits p.23 n.8; reply pp.7,16 n.7). Even a successful defense can leave fees; settlement causation alone does not dispose of that fork.

**Superior knowledge.** App.15a–16a rejected extension beyond withheld information affecting costs or duration of contract performance, even assuming foreseeability and superior government knowledge. Plager (41a–43a) treats this as unwarranted temporal restriction of a common contract principle. The Claims Court expressly declined to decide relative knowledge as fact-intensive (56a). Do not report that issue as already found against petitioners.

**Reverse warranty/good faith.** Hercules pleaded purchaser-use obligations and good faith. App.25a–27a separately discusses reverse warranty; it doubts an actual agreement but again rests on causation and separately rejects fees. Claims Court 58a–59a rejected good-faith cost claims as performance-related. The Government says these separate claims were not renewed in the petition; current merits briefs chiefly present warranty, superior knowledge and DPA indemnity. Do not revive the broader original complaints' theories without a reachability determination.

**DPA indemnity.** App.28a–33a states that **Thompson, not Hercules**, pressed this claim below. It construes §§101/707 together as protecting against third-party contract breaches caused by required priority and rejects reimbursement for subsequent product tort liability. App.29a n.15 expressly leaves open the district court's suggestion of protection for increased risks caused by accelerated production; do not turn the lower holding into a universal denial of all DPA tort protection. The United States' merits pp.38–40 separately urges that even broad statutory immunity does not promise reimbursement.

**Authority/appropriations.** Claims Court 78a–80a and 100a–103a supplies an additional ground: actual authorized contracting power is required for express and implied-in-fact promises; no sufficient appropriation or statutory exemption was shown for the posited indemnity. It cites Merrill, Sutton, the Anti-Deficiency Act and California-Pacific Utilities. The Federal Circuit did not select this as its warranty disposition. It declined the trial court's separately asserted wholesale contractor-defense policy ground (15a), while using that defense within particular contract claims.

**Dissent.** Plager would remand for trial on unresolved facts/law (40a), objects to hindsight application of Boyle, and questions importing a state-law displacement doctrine into the tort litigation's federal/national-consensus setting. He connects superior knowledge and specifications warranties, construes the DPA's words broadly, and treats compulsion and control as strengthening contractual responsibility. His Takings Clause analogy (45a–46a) is not a preserved independent Supreme Court takings question or a takings holding.

### Competing supported paths for independent modeling

1. **Affirm for absent promised coverage.** Read Spearin as assuring the ability to perform supplied specifications and produce the required deliverable, rather than a promise to bear third-party tort-defense/settlement losses. Actual contractual intention and authority, Hatzlachh's tort/contract distinction, and the asserted third-party-damages analogies still require examination; tort immunity alone is not the entire reason. Separately reject a DPA indemnity because its shield language does not establish a reimbursement promise, without deciding the full breadth of statutory immunity.
2. **Affirm on the appellate causation and fees grounds.** Accept the claimed equivalence between proof of the warranty theory and a complete government-contractor defense, and distinguish voluntarily assumed settlement and unpromised defense costs. This must answer the actual 1984 uncertainty, later opt-out posture, potential-versus-actual liability principles, and the independent fee claim; simply citing Boyle is incomplete.
3. **Reverse/vacate and remand for the supported contract theory.** Treat warranty scope and foreseeable consequential damages under Spearin/contract principles as capable of encompassing the pleaded losses; reject categorical hindsight reliance on a tort defense as sufficient to negate causation; require factual development of agreement, specifications, breach, knowledge, foreseeability, potential liability, reasonableness, and authorized commitment as material to the selected theory. Petitioners seek this opportunity, not an immediate judicial finding that all those elements are met. This path can be limited to the warranty theory and to properly presented petitioners/components.
4. **Resolve DPA contract inference separately.** The strongest petitioners' formulation in reply pp.16–20 is an implied-in-fact promise inferred from the whole contractual setting, including statutory protection, coercion, control, superior knowledge and alleged contractor innocence. They expressly disclaim a private statutory cause of action under Cort v. Ash and disclaim treating §707's words alone as affirmative indemnity. The strongest government answer is that an immunity defense is not an authorized promise to pay, while a court-created obligation resting only on fairness is implied in law. A conclusion about §707's scope should not silently decide actual contracting authority, and vice versa.
5. **Alternative no-breach/authority affirmance only on an adequate record.** The Government expressly presses no proof of defective specifications/injury causation (merits pp.31–33) and lack of authorized open-ended indemnity (pp.39–40 n.23). Petitioners contest both, distinguish contract economic causation, invoke the judgment obligation under a valid Spearin warranty, and claim further facts are needed. A record-supported legal alternative can preserve affirmance, but disputed scientific or contract facts cannot be turned into new appellate findings. No general proposition that every implied warranty violates the Anti-Deficiency Act is supported.

These are supported choices for modeling, not commitments or a demand for a particular split. Narrow component dispositions remain possible subject to the preserved claim and complete remedy.
