The allegation is that Midwest returned a $64,294.27 check unpaid without telling the receiving bank that the reason was insufficient funds. The district court found a Regulation CC standard-of-care breach and awarded $43,912.06. Those are the district court's determinations, not new findings by these commitments. The Seventh Circuit vacated the judgment and directed dismissal for lack of jurisdiction; it did not decide the underlying regulatory-liability appeal. The original packet's late-notice account is not used.

There is one judgment component: whether the statutory interbank action can proceed in federal court and the resulting treatment of the Seventh Circuit's jurisdictional judgment. Its supporting propositions are separately identified below to keep statutory authorization, agency construction and remedy from being conflated.

1. **Statutory route.** A depository institution may bring in federal district court an action enforcing interbank liability established by the Board within §4010(f)'s delegation. Section 4010(d)'s authorization of any action under the section includes that action. Section 4010(a)'s exclusion of another depository institution withholds that subsection's customer remedy; it does not remove the distinct interbank liability arrangement from the same section's forum provision.
2. **Source and limits of authority.** This conclusion rests on the enacted relationship of subsections (a), (d) and (f), with §4008(c)'s payment-system responsibility providing context. A regulation or the Board's preference cannot create jurisdiction withheld by Congress. No rule of judicial deference concerning jurisdiction is needed to decide this case, and no general rule approving or forbidding such deference is adopted.
3. **Consequences for the appeal.** Reverse the Seventh Circuit's jurisdictional judgment and remand for consideration of properly remaining challenges to the district judgment. Do not affirm the bank's substantive liability, award damages anew, direct an injunction, or order an administrative damages proceeding. Recognition of the action does not decide negligence, causation, loss, bad faith, timeliness, a defense, or an unexamined state cause of action.

The §4010(a) individual/class additional damages and successful-action costs and reasonable attorney fees do not follow merely from recognizing the subsection (f) action. Subsection (f) ordinarily limits liability to the check amount; additional damages require bad faith and proximate consequence of the relevant act or omission. Section 4010(d) states a one-year period from the occurrence of the violation. No timely-filing dispute is decided. Subsection (c)'s protection requires proof by a preponderance of an unintentional bona fide error despite reasonably adapted preventive procedures and excludes an error of legal judgment. Subsection (e)'s separate protection concerns good-faith conformity to a Board rule, regulation or interpretation despite later amendment, rescission or invalidation. No finding that either protection applies is made. No broader damages, fee or defense ruling is necessary to remove the forum barrier.

## Enhanced alternative-path comparison

### Statutory authorization and the customer exclusion

**Path A — enforce the separate interbank regime.** Subsection (f) expressly authorizes the Board to impose or allocate loss and liability among depository institutions and limits that liability. Subsection (d) supplies a judicial forum for any action under the section. Reading those provisions together gives effect to interbank liability without extending subsection (a)'s expressly different customer remedy. The strongest objection is that subsection (f) is phrased as a rulemaking authorization and contains no separate sentence saying that an injured bank may sue; subsection (a), where Congress directly imposes liability, expressly excludes interbank plaintiffs. Touche Ross therefore demands more than pointing to a jurisdictional clause or an agency rule.

The answer is the combined enacted allocation: subsection (f) concerns the very interbank loss and liability absent from subsection (a), and subsection (d) refers to the whole section rather than only subsection (a). The action enforces liability Congress expressly empowered the Board to establish, through a forum Congress expressly supplied for the section. This construction does not infer a damages right from a reporting requirement, statutory beneficence, or agency regulation alone. The customer exclusion retains full work in its own remedial arrangement.

**Path B — treat subsection (a) as the only privately enforceable liability.** The strongest form preserves the express interbank exclusion and treats subsection (f) as regulatory allocation for administrative enforcement or otherwise available state remedies. Its principal authority is Touche Ross's refusal to let jurisdictional language and regulation supply absent substantive authorization. Its principal obstacle is that it narrows subsection (d) to one subsection without a corresponding textual restriction and discounts subsection (f)'s express interbank liability language. Section 4009(c)(1)'s residual administrative enforcement command does not expressly establish exclusive adjudication of one bank's damages against another. The Board reported that no such administrative mechanism existed. That absence alone cannot create a remedy, but it also cannot substantiate the lower court's proposed exclusive administrative route. Transamerica supplies an additional boundary: legal consequences can be supplied by the actual statutory structure without an explicit private-action sentence, and an exclusive state-forum premise needs support.

All eight Associates adopt Path A at the stated level of generality for the Justice-specific reasons below. They reject Path B's construction of this statute, not the congressional-authorization requirement on which it relies. A hypothetical rejection of the private action would also require distinguishing failure to state a claim from subject-matter jurisdiction, as Transamerica's footnote 4 illustrates. Because none selects that rejection here, no additional §1331 ruling or unsupported alternative decree is made.

### Agency role and remedial reach

**Textually sufficient route.** The Court independently determines that the statutory arrangement permits judicial enforcement. The Board's account of its available processes explains why the proposed administrative route lacks the factual support asserted below; it is not jurisdictional law. NationsBank and Town & Country require independent statutory boundaries before permissible agency construction and remain intact.

**Additional deference route.** An argument for respecting the Board's reading within delegated payment-system authority is supported by those cases' treatment of genuine delegations. Its strongest objection is that Congress, not an agency, supplies the federal forum, and those precedents did not adjudicate agency power to expand jurisdiction. The independent textual ground resolves this case without answering that broader dispute. Each Associate accepts the textually sufficient route and leaves the general deference issue open; none conditions the result on deference.

**Bounded remand versus immediate merits disposition.** Recognition of the forum removes the only ground the Seventh Circuit decided. The strongest argument for reinstating the district award outright is that it already rests on summary judgment. That does not establish that all properly remaining appellate challenges fail; the reviewing court never reached them. Each Associate therefore supports remand to that court, without a new negligence finding or unconditional damages award. Zicherman reinforces respect for the particular remedial regime; it does not supply the EFAA cause of action or resolve the bank's liability.
