## Shared legal conclusion and bounded relief

Each Associate commits to the following minimum propositions and remedy, for the individual grounds and with the methodological limits recorded below:

1. **The higher defendant burden is unconstitutional.** Oklahoma may not require Cooper to prove present trial incompetence by clear and convincing evidence. Unlike Medina's permitted defendant-preponderance allocation, this quantum can authorize trial even when the factfinder regards incompetence as more likely than competence. That additional risk is incompatible with adequate protection of the fundamental prohibition against trial while incompetent. Repeated opportunities to apply the same excessive burden do not cure its allocation of error.
2. **Medina's actual holding survives.** The Standards entry **“Defendant's preponderance burden and equivalent competence presumption,” present operation effective June 22, 1992**, permits that allocation at an adequate hearing with counsel and psychiatric evidence. Its associated presumption supplies no independent psychiatric weight. The present decision rejects the higher quantum without requiring the prosecution to prove competence, overruling Medina, changing Dusky's functional standard or changing the sufficient-doubt trigger for inquiry. No exclusive due-process methodology is extracted from Medina's divided opinions.
3. **The constitutional defect was operative here.** The trial judge expressly found that Cooper failed to carry clear-and-convincing persuasion; the appellate court sustained both that legal rule and findings assessed under it. The conflicting expert and lay evidence does not establish what a factfinder would conclude under a permissible standard. Diagnosis is not legal incompetence; malingering can coexist with illness; apparent factual understanding does not alone establish rational assistance to counsel. The judge's remarks illustrate the disputed inquiry but need not become a separate, ungranted substantive-competence holding. Neither this Court nor the model declares Cooper actually incompetent, competent, more likely incompetent, or exactly at equipoise.
4. **Set aside the burden-dependent appellate determination and remand.** Reverse the reviewed legal ruling approving clear-and-convincing persuasion, vacate the appellate affirmance insofar as the conviction and death sentence depend on that competency adjudication, and remand for constitutionally adequate proceedings. Existing findings under the invalid quantum cannot by themselves preserve the judgment. The receiving court must first assess whether a reliable retrospective determination of competence at the relevant trial period can be made. If it can, a competent factfinder may conduct that determination using the proper functional standard and a constitutionally permissible allocation, which may require the defendant to establish incompetence by a preponderance. A reliable determination sustaining competence may permit preservation of the conviction and dependent sentence through lawful further proceedings. If reliable retrospective evaluation is unavailable, or a valid evaluation establishes incompetence at trial, the conviction and dependent sentence must be set aside; a new trial is permissible only when Cooper is competent. The State may elect lawful retrial rather than seek to preserve the old judgment through retrospective evaluation.

This remedy does not itself vacate the trial judgment unconditionally or order an immediate new trial. Nor does it affirm that judgment pending only a ministerial correction. It removes the appellate determination resting on the invalid burden and requires a valid competency basis before the challenged conviction and sentence may be sustained. No burden or numerical proof standard for the separate feasibility inquiry is invented. There is no automatic release, acquittal, commitment, current-competence finding, treatment command, stay, execution authorization, deadline or new Supreme Court proceeding. Unrelated claims retain their lawful procedural posture.

## Material alternatives and objections

**Sustain the complete Oklahoma procedure.** The State's strongest case combines Medina's permission to allocate persuasion, counsel and psychiatric assistance, multiple examinations and renewed inquiry, its interest in timely prosecution, and the danger of feigning. Erroneous incompetence can delay adjudication and lead to unwanted confinement or treatment under separate law. These interests and safeguards matter, but none explains why a defendant whom the greater weight of evidence shows unable rationally to participate may nevertheless be tried. Expert disagreement makes the allocation consequential rather than dispensable. The State's assertion that a genuinely incompetent defendant ordinarily can meet the higher standard is not an established error rate or a cure for the class the rule exposes. The narrow invalidation does not require the maximally defendant-favorable procedure.

**Reject the higher quantum.** The strongest objection is that the substantive prohibition on incompetent trial cannot eliminate every risk of mistaken factfinding and does not mechanically prescribe a burden. The conclusion rests on the magnitude and direction of this particular increment beyond Medina: it deliberately places more than the risk of equipoise on the person whose ability to assist in demonstrating incapacity is at issue. Timely prosecution remains possible after a valid competence determination, and ordinary examination and credibility assessment can address malingering. Leland and Patterson concern different allocations; Winship's offense-element rule is not extended to competence. Addington, Jones and A. St. P. C. protect against distinct deprivations with their own burden directions. Raising proof required to impose a deprivation does not justify raising the accused's proof required to avoid an incompetent trial. Akron's maturity-bypass burden neither decides criminal competence nor overrides Dusky, Pate and Drope.

**Remedy: immediate new trial versus possible retrospective evaluation.** Petitioner requests a new trial. Pate, Drope and Riggins warn against pretending that a past ability to participate can always be reconstructed. But this record contains contemporaneous examinations, testimony and hearings; it does not establish either reliable reconstruction or its impossibility. Godinez preserves the need to assess that question without making old findings conclusive. A limited feasibility remand therefore has legal work to do. Its strongest objection is that time, episodic illness and contested feigning may make reconstruction inadequate; the remand expressly requires setting aside the conviction if that concern proves decisive. The competing automatic-new-trial route would decide an unestablished remedial predicate. No Associate adopts a general harmless-error or structural-error classification; appellate sufficiency under the wrong burden alone supplies no harmlessness finding. Tuggle's distinct psychiatric-assistance remand and reserved error-effect question do not select a new standard here.

The historical and comparative survey in the filings is treated as reported argument. The common conclusion requires no invented numerical consensus, unverified ancient quotation, empirical malingering rate, or assumption that federal §4241(d) controls a State. The established constitutional protection, actual Medina limit and demonstrated operation of this burden are sufficient.
