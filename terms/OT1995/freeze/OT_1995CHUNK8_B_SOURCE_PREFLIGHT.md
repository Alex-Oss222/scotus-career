## Jaffee v. Redmond - source-to-fact supplement

Source: the published [Seventh Circuit opinion, 51 F.3d 1346](https://openjurist.org/51/f3d/1346/jaffee-v-redmond), especially 1348-1352, 1354-1358 and notes 3-9, 13, 17-19. Its relevant facts, privilege analysis and footnotes were inspected. The case involves a federal excessive-force claim and a pendent Illinois wrongful-death claim, not diversity litigation alone. The jury awarded $45,000 on the federal claim and $500,000 on the state claim. The Court of Appeals rejected the separate objection to the deadly-force instruction, but reversed the judgment and ordered a new trial because of the privilege ruling and associated instruction.

The lower opinion reports conflicting eyewitness testimony about whether Allen was armed. Those are competing accounts, not an established finding that Redmond's shooting was lawful. Redmond sought counseling from Karen Beyer, a licensed clinical social worker, beginning three or four days after the shooting, continuing two or three sessions a week through at least January 1992. Beyer worked for the Village. The court describes her master's degree and extensive psychotherapy experience; her professional role is more specific than an unlicensed counselor or casual confidant.

The district court denied the motion to quash and ordered disclosure. It originally barred Redmond's shooting testimony but vacated that sanction before trial. The sanction actually delivered was an adverse-inference instruction: the jury was entitled to presume the withheld notes unfavorable to Redmond and the Village. The court also told jurors that withholding them lacked legal justification. Do not describe this as an actual disclosure of all counseling communications or as a continuing exclusion of Redmond's trial testimony. Beyer answered general credential and scheduling questions and some questions about the incident, and produced three redacted pages; the record recitation does not by itself settle waiver of all other treatment communications.

The Seventh Circuit recognized a privilege for confidential treatment communications, including licensed social workers, but made protection depend on balancing evidentiary need against privacy in the interests of justice. It treated other eyewitness testimony as reducing need in this record. Recognition, professional coverage, the availability of case-specific balancing, and the consequence for the trial instruction are distinct review questions.

The contemporaneous Rule 501, enacted at 88 Stat. 1933-1934, states:

> Except as otherwise required by the Constitution of the United States or provided by Act of Congress or in rules prescribed by the Supreme Court pursuant to statutory authority, the privilege of a witness, person, government, State, or political subdivision thereof shall be governed by the principles of the common law as they may be interpreted by the courts of the United States in the light of reason and experience. However, in civil actions and proceedings, with respect to an element of a claim or defense as to which State law supplies the rule of decision, the privilege of a witness, person, government, State, or political subdivision thereof shall be determined in accordance with State law.

The [official enactment](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/STATUTE-88/pdf/STATUTE-88-Pg1926-2.pdf), PDF pages 8-9, supplies the text. The 2011 restyling is not the operative wording. The state-law sentence must not disappear, although the court below treated the common federal privilege question as governing the evidence relevant to both claims. The proposed specific privilege Rule 504 was not enacted by Congress; it is not an operative federal rule. The lower opinion's report that all States recognize some form of psychotherapist privilege supports the existence of widespread state recognition, not uniform scope, qualifications, exceptions or professional coverage. Its Illinois excerpts identify a patient/therapist privilege under 740 ILCS 110/10 and a definition including social workers under 110/2.

Source-supported issues for clean neutral authoring: Rule 501's permission for reason-and-experience development versus the cost of withholding probative evidence; whether the treatment relationship depends on reliable confidentiality; whether licensing and treatment function justify including clinical social workers; and whether individual balancing protects access to evidence or defeats advance assurance of confidentiality. These issues arise from Rule 501 and the lower opinion. They do not themselves decide recognition, scope or remedy. Daubert and Williamson regulate other evidence questions and do not decide privilege.

Limits: No complete collection of every State's operative privilege law has been verified. Do not assert a uniform fifty-state privilege or supply broad future exceptions from that count. No new waiver finding, dangerous-patient finding, litigation-use exception, or psychotherapist-patient evidentiary fact is assumed. If the proposed resolution depends on those additional questions rather than the presented privilege/instruction dispute, obtain the pertinent original submissions and record passages before adjudicating them. No such additional premise is needed merely to compare the submitted legal paths.

## Montana v. Egelhoff - source-to-fact supplement

Source: [State v. Egelhoff, 272 Mont. 114, 900 P.2d 260 (July 6, 1995)](https://app.midpage.ai/document/state-v-egelhoff-883627), with the [published text cross-check](https://case-law.vlex.com/vid/state-v-egelhoff-no-889392159). The body gives July 6, 1995; a wrapper's October 1994 label is not the decision date. The state opinion supplies the statute, actual instruction, preservation ruling, competing evidence and remedy. The neutral packet's reference to **§45-2-203(3)** should be corrected by the clean validator to **§45-2-203**: the quoted historical provision is one unnumbered paragraph.

The complete challenged statutory paragraph reproduced by the Montana court is:

> A person who is in an intoxicated condition is criminally responsible for his conduct and an intoxicated condition is not a defense to any offense and may not be taken into consideration in determining the existence of a mental state which is an element of the offense unless the defendant proves that he did not know that it was an intoxicating substance when he consumed, smoked, sniffed, injected, or otherwise ingested the substance causing the condition.

The unknown-intoxicant exception is part of the enactment, not an exception for every involuntary or excessive intoxication. Instruction 11 gave the same rule with the shortened phrase concerning consumption; the state opinion prints that instruction separately. The earlier 1985 statute permitted use of an intoxicated or drugged condition to determine an element and separately excused responsibility if involuntarily produced incapacity prevented appreciation of criminality or conformity with law. Those superseded terms must not be imported into the amended paragraph.

Section 45-5-102's submitted deliberate-homicide theory required purposely or knowingly causing death. The published objective charge recitation states that purpose meant a conscious object to engage in conduct of that nature or cause that result; knowledge meant awareness of one's conduct, awareness that under the circumstances the conduct constitutes a crime, or awareness of a high probability that the conduct will cause a specified result. This is a bounded extraction of actual charge language, reported at 518 U.S. 40-41 from petition appendix 28a-29a; it supplies no Supreme Court conclusion about the challenged rule.

The state court found the constitutional objection preserved through the instruction-settlement objection and renewed new-trial motion. Egelhoff challenged consideration of evidence on the mental-state element, not a constitutional entitlement to an affirmative voluntary-intoxication defense. His intoxication evidence was admitted and could be used to explain memory and physical-capacity claims; the disputed instruction barred considering it for the mental-state element. An unqualified statement that the jury heard no intoxication evidence would be false.

The record recitation includes very high blood-alcohol readings, Egelhoff's asserted blackout and fourth-person account, and a doctor's testimony that someone experiencing an alcohol blackout can walk, talk and function. The prosecution pointed to directed behavior, coordination, speech and other circumstances bearing on his ability to commit the killings. Blackout, physical incapacity, absence of memory, and absence of purpose or knowledge are not interchangeable findings. The Montana court treated the restriction as reducing the burden on the State, reversed for a new trial, and did not resolve the remaining assignments concerning lay opinion, evidentiary sufficiency and dangerous-offender classification. Reversal of that constitutional rationale would therefore require attention to the unresolved lower-court issues rather than automatic Supreme Court findings on all of them.

Source-supported issues for clean neutral authoring: legislative power to define culpability and treat voluntary intoxication as irrelevant, versus a restriction on proof of a mental element the State retains; historical acceptance of limiting intoxication evidence versus the right to contest every actual element; and the significance of the complete charge and other uses of the evidence. Winship, Patterson, Martin v. Ohio, Sandstrom, Chambers and Rock are distinct pre-divergence authorities for their actual rules. Actual Gaudin assigns enacted elements to the jury; Staples and Ratzlaf construe their particular federal statutes; Victor requires examination of the complete proof instruction. None alone proves that every crime must retain subjective mens rea or that this statute is an evidentiary rule rather than an offense definition.

Limits: No complete trial transcript or original merits papers were recovered or certified in this preflight. The published lower opinion establishes the stated preservation ruling and the evidence recitations; it does not authorize a new finding about exact degree of mental impairment or harmlessness. A path requiring a different preserved theory, transcript-specific concession, or first-instance resolution of an unaddressed issue needs the identified original passage. No historical record gap is treated as a litigant default.

## Koon and Powell - source-to-fact and enacted-law supplement

Source: [United States v. Koon, 34 F.3d 1416](https://openjurist.org/34/f3d/1416), sentencing section J and its material notes; it quotes the sentencing memorandum at 833 F. Supp. 769, 786-790. The Ninth Circuit sustained the convictions and the district court's two-level bodily-injury adjustment, but vacated the sentences for resentencing after rejecting both downward departures. Those separate conviction and injury-adjustment rulings are not automatically reopened by the submitted departure questions.

The district court used the 1992 Guidelines. This is independently identified as an objective sentencing fact in the official published record recitation at 518 U.S. 88-89; no later resolution is incorporated. The [Commission's archived 1992 Manual](https://www.ussc.gov/guidelines/archive/1992-federal-sentencing-guidelines-manual) supplies the actual text. Chapter 2H1.4 used the greater of 10 or six plus the underlying-offense level. The lower court used aggravated assault: 15 plus six, four for dangerous-weapon use and two for bodily injury, producing level 27, category I, range 70-87 months. The separate five-level and three-level downward departures produced level 19 and 30-month sentences. The five levels were for victim misconduct; the three combined other factors. The three-level combination was not supported by a finding that each factor independently justified the full three levels.

The sentencing judge found that King's resistance and danger had ended by the criminal portion of the force. He also relied on earlier misconduct, the initial lawful force and the rapid transition during the encounter. The appellate opinion disagreed with the legal adequacy of that rationale and with its fit to the applicable guideline, while quoting the actual findings. A conclusion that King was still posing a danger during the criminal force would contradict the identified sentencing finding. A conclusion that the judge relied exclusively on mere chronological but-for causation would omit the judge's quoted dynamic-arrest and heartland reasoning; that is the court of appeals' criticism, not a substitute for the sentencing explanation.

The other departure grounds require separate treatment: unusual prison vulnerability tied to police status and extraordinary publicity; administrative/professional consequences including loss of law-enforcement employment; very low likelihood of recidivism; and the burden of successive state and federal prosecutions. The appellate court treated these as impermissible grounds in this case. It acknowledged unusual publicity but rejected its legal relevance, and noted that the record did not establish unavoidable continuous solitary confinement. The district court recognized that dual-sovereignty doctrine permitted the second prosecution; its departure did not rest on a double-jeopardy violation.

The complete relevant [statutory and policy texts](../entering-law/OT_1995CHUNK8_B_STATUTE_EXTRACTS.md) preserve these distinctions:

- Section 3553(b) requires an applicable-range sentence unless a circumstance of a kind or degree not adequately considered by the Commission should result in a different sentence. The adequate-consideration inquiry looks only to Guidelines, policy statements and official commentary. Section 3553(a) separately supplies sentencing purposes, offender/offense circumstances, available sentences, disparities and restitution.
- Section 3742(e) separately prescribes review of illegality, incorrect application, unreasonable outside-range sentences, and plainly unreasonable sentences without a guideline. Its final sentence requires due regard for credibility, clear-error protection for factual findings, and due deference to application of the Guidelines to facts. Section 3742(f) states the distinct remand/affirmance consequences. It does not say every legal interpretation receives deference.
- Chapter 1, Part A, 4(b) explains the typical range of cases addressed by a guideline, permits unusual cases to be considered for departure, identifies excluded grounds, and anticipates infrequent departures. It does not make every unmentioned factor forbidden.
- Section 5K2.0 recognizes incompletely considered circumstances and unusual degree; a factor already reflected in the applicable guideline may justify departure only on the specified unusual-degree basis. It does not erase a prohibition elsewhere.
- Section 5K2.10 permits reduction when the victim's wrongful conduct significantly contributed to provoking offense behavior. Its five listed considerations govern the extent: comparative size/strength; persistence and the defendant's efforts to avoid confrontation; reasonably perceived danger including reputation; actual danger; and other victim conduct substantially contributing to danger. Its criminal-sexual-abuse and nonviolent-offense qualifications, including unusual nonviolent retaliation after extended provocation, remain in force. The later sixth factor is not part of the 1992 text.
- Section 4A1.3 expressly locates category I's lower bound at the first offender with the lowest risk of recidivism and disallows a below-range departure on adequacy of criminal history. Sections 5H1.5 and 5H1.11 describe employment and public-service/prior-good-works considerations as not ordinarily relevant, while §5H1.10 excludes race, sex, national origin, creed, religion and socioeconomic status. Discouragement, full consideration and prohibition are not synonyms.

Actual Williams, including its burden on the sentence defender after established invalid-factor reliance, and actual Stinson govern. Williams requires both the same-sentence showing without invalid factors and independently reasonable extent grounded in the sentencing court's actual valid reasons. It does not place the causal burden on the challenger, treat possible lawful reimposition as enough, or authorize appellate invention of replacement reasons. A disposition affecting the three-level combination need not automatically determine the independent five-level departure.

For date-eligible judicial writing, [United States v. Rivera, 994 F.2d 942, 946-952 (1st Cir. 1993)](https://openjurist.org/994/f2d/942/united-states-v-rivera), is Chief Judge Breyer's own published opinion. It distinguishes forbidden, discouraged, encouraged and unmentioned considerations; independently reviews legal permission and interpretation; and gives respect to the sentencing judge's assessment of unusual circumstances and degree. Its proposed sequence is expressly a suggestion, not mandatory steps. The opinion is lower-court writing evidence, not controlling simulated Supreme Court law; its discussion of Williams must be read subject to the actual simulated Williams rule. Cooter & Gell, Pierce and Burns are possible surviving pre-divergence review/sentencing authorities, each limited to its own issue. The clean modeler must use each Justice's actual eligible writing and joins rather than infer agreement from labels.

Limits: No new factual prediction about prison treatment, causation of every injury, or inevitable reimposition of exactly 30 months is supplied. The published appellate sentencing discussion gives the identified findings and reasons; it is sufficient for source-supported comparison of the submitted review/grounds paths. A remedy that depends on an additional sentencing assurance or record fact must obtain that passage first.

## Rise v. Oregon - source-to-fact and enacted-law supplement

Sources: [59 F.3d 1556](https://openjurist.org/59/f3d/1556/rise), including the complete majority, dissent and footnotes; official [1995 ORS chapter 137](https://archives.oregonlegislature.gov/ORS_Archives/1995-Chapter-137.htm), §137.076; and official [1995 ORS chapter 181](https://archives.oregonlegislature.gov/ORS_Archives/1995-Chapter-181.htm), §181.085. The section-history notes identify 1991 enactment and the intervening 1993 amendments. The supplied inventory authorizes assigned merits review of the identified Ninth Circuit matter; this preflight invents neither a Supreme Court docket nor an argument date or separate grant event.

The challenged scheme is not restricted to violent sex crimes. Section 137.076(1) includes the specified rape, sodomy, unlawful penetration, sexual-abuse, public-indecency, incest and child-display crimes; burglary with intent to commit a listed sexual offense; promoting or compelling prostitution; conspiracy or attempt to commit the listed felonies in (a)-(c); and murder or aggravated murder. Attempted murder is outside that list. Rise, Durham and Rhodes had predicate convictions before enactment. Milligan's attempted murder and unlawful-weapon-use convictions were not predicates.

Section 137.076(2) requires the person to provide blood on request of the designated agency whether or not the judgment separately orders it; the sentencing court must enter the stated order, with cost reimbursement unless the person lacks ability to pay and a blood-sample probation condition where applicable. The agency must collect as soon as practicable after conviction and before release from incarceration. State Police notice that a sample is inadequate requires another sample. Thus the lower opinion's description of one lifetime draw is qualified by the actual adequate-sample and repeat-collection terms; it is not an absolute one-draw ceiling.

Section 137.076(3) requires medically acceptable collection by the listed licensed or qualified personnel or a person under physician direction/control, and compliance with prescribed procedures. The limited civil-liability protection applies to medically acceptable authorized collection. A procedural deviation that does not affect reliability does not alone render a test result or opinion inadmissible. Under (4), no sample is required when State Police notifies the court or agency of an adequate previously received sample, or when the court determines that collection would create a substantial and unreasonable health risk. Subsection (5) applies (1)-(4) to the specified persons incarcerated on September 29, 1991 and requires collection before release. Do not replace these predicates with a categorical statement that every prior convict is subject to collection forever.

Section 181.085 authorizes retention of samples and derivative physical material, identity/genetic-profile analysis, an identification database, and anonymous population-frequency databases. It permits accurate and reliable identification techniques, with examples. Disclosure is limited to the specified criminal-investigation/proceeding recipients and purposes; independently required discovery; and lawful court/grand-jury process for evidence not otherwise privileged. Recipient public agencies remain bound by the same limits. Subject access to database records is allowed on request, subject to a reasonable-likelihood-of-prejudice exception for pending investigations; the Department need not allow testing of physical material. Accuracy challenges and preservation procedures must be accommodated as stated.

Following reversal of the predicate conviction, judgment or order, a written destruction request plus a certified reversal order generally requires destruction of the person's sample, derived physical evidence and identification records. The obligation yields to another qualifying obligation; evidence relating to another covered person need not be destroyed, and a set-aside under ORS 137.225 does not itself affect the material. Retention therefore has stated rules; it is not established to be universally perpetual or immediately terminable at release.

The lower opinion characterizes the scheme as forbidding analysis for genetic predispositions. The official quoted section authorizes defined purposes rather than containing those words as a freestanding explicit prohibition. Keep that judicial characterization distinct from enacted language. The anonymous population-frequency permission is an actual additional statutory use; a statement that identity comparison is the sole permissible activity would be incomplete. No sample misuse is found merely because such misuse is technically possible.

The district court relied on special needs and penal administration. The appellate majority expressly sustained the program even assuming ordinary law enforcement was its only purpose. It used reduced offender privacy, the bodily procedure, identification utility, conviction-defined coverage, medical safeguards and limited uses. The dissent separately disputed equating bodily extraction and retained genetic information with fingerprints, stressed the ordinary investigatory purpose and lack of particularized suspicion, and distinguished Schmerber's permission for medically reasonable means from its predicates for initiating the intrusion. Those competing lower-court rationales supply supported paths, not an adopted Supreme Court exception. Actual Vernonia remains limited to its documented athletic program and non-law-enforcement use; historical language quoted in the lower opinion must be replaced with actual simulated law.

The due-process claims remain separate. The general claim sought a precollection hearing; the lower court regarded statutory predicate conviction as leaving little to contest. Milligan's claim concerned noncoverage and ensuing discipline. He refused at least three mistaken collection demands, underwent two disciplinary hearings without raising statutory noncoverage, and received a fine and disciplinary segregation. Tuttle's uncontroverted affidavit attributed the recommendation to reading the attempt language as reaching murder. The remaining appeal was only against Knox and Pearce; the dismissals of DOC, State Police, Madsen and John Doe defendants were not challenged. Neither Tuttle nor the disciplinarians was sued. The district court relied on at-most negligence; the appellate court instead found no evidentiary causal link to personal acts of Knox or Pearce, expressly leaving claims against responsible actors undecided. Supervisory title is not such a link. The source does not establish that Milligan's blood was actually drawn.

The ex post facto question concerns whether the retrospective collection obligation is punishment, not whether its operation is burdensome or constitutional under another clause. Collins v. Youngblood and the existing punishment/retroactivity authorities retain their separate functions. Schmerber, Winston, Skinner, Von Raab, Bell, Mathews and Daniels supply potential pre-divergence authority for their actual distinct propositions; no rule from a later DNA case is supplied. Actual Sandin must be considered if the disciplinary-segregation theory is reached, but no length or conditions of segregation have been established here.

Limits: The published opinion does not supply a segregation duration/conditions finding, a preserved challenge to every hypothetical secondary genetic use, or evidence connecting Knox/Pearce personally to the error. No factual reconstruction supplies those missing propositions. A path turning on any one must identify it specifically and obtain the source or remain within a supported narrower scope.
