## Actual instructions and the federal instruction rule

*Williams v. Armontrout*, 679 F. Supp. 916, 923 (W.D. Mo. 1988), CAP `b991-5`, directly reports that Williams's jury received instructions on capital murder and the lesser included offenses of **second-degree murder and manslaughter**, citing Respondent's Exhibit A-3 at 93–95. Both counsel and the State offered the refused first-degree kidnapping-murder instruction. *State v. Williams*, 652 S.W.2d 102, 112 (Mo. 1983), Point X, independently confirms that refusal. These are Williams's own instructions, not a comparator's. The original instructions have not been recovered; ordinary second-degree murder as given must not be equated with the distinct **second-degree felony-murder theory** reportedly omitted.

*Schad v. Arizona*, 501 U.S. 624, 645–648 (1991), Part III, is a Court holding, unlike its separate plurality treatment of verdict specificity. An evidence-supported noncapital second-degree option satisfied *Beck* despite omission of a requested robbery option; *Beck* does not require every evidence-supported lesser offense. The Court reasoned that jurors unwilling to acquit someone they believed committed robbery had no rational basis to choose capital murder over the available second-degree option. It expressly declined to hold that just any nominal lesser option, even one unsupported by evidence, suffices. Its reliance on conceded evidentiary support in *Schad* is not a concession by Williams. The dissent's proposed requirement of a lesser option corresponding to each prosecution theory is not the Court's rule.

This materially qualifies the base packet's instruction alternatives. The fact that evidence could support the omitted first-degree offense, combined with prior judicial disagreement, does **not alone** establish a substantial federal instruction ground after *Schad*. A proposed affirmative instruction rationale must address the actually supplied noncapital options and the Court's rule. Conversely, the recovered menu alone supplies neither the full instruction texts nor a new finding resolving every evidentiary and legal condition. It must not become an automatic rejection of every distinct instruction theory.

The separately reported second-degree claim includes due process and equal protection. Neither the state nor the 1988 federal report specifies its precise felony predicate, requested wording, or complete presentation history. A merits ground genuinely common to the supported presentation alternatives may avoid choosing that history, but its reasoning must cover the actual constitutional component being resolved. *Schad*'s *Beck* holding does not, merely by being cited, resolve a distinct claim of unconstitutional unequal treatment under state instruction rules. Equally, inconsistent state-law treatment or a state-law error is not automatically a federal violation. The base packet does not establish that the first-degree equal-protection theory from the earlier proceeding was repeated as a separate current claim; this supplement adds no claim or preservation finding.

The first-degree ground's actual prior merits adjudication remains supported. *Sanders* reconsideration discretion, the applicable successive-ground limits, and any federal merits question remain distinct inquiries. *Schad* must be addressed before treating discretionary redetermination of the old instruction dispute as itself a substantial ground for maintaining the stay. This supplement selects neither the available exercise of that discretion nor a judgment.

## Amendment discretion and the proportionality allegation

*Foman v. Davis*, 371 U.S. 178, 182 (1962), preserves both liberal amendment and discretion: leave is to be freely given when justice requires, absent an **apparent or declared reason**, such as undue delay, bad faith or dilatory motive, repeated failure to cure deficiencies, undue prejudice, or futility. Its list is illustrative. Outright refusal without any justifying reason appearing is an abuse of discretion; an expressly stated reason is not its only possible justification. The then-effective habeas Rule 11 permits application of civil rules **when appropriate and to the extent not inconsistent with the habeas Rules**. No unqualified importation of every civil rule or unrecovered §2242 quotation is supplied.

The supported amendment timing—four years of proceedings, completed discovery, a prepared magistrate report, and a district court ready to rule—can be assessed for an apparent justification. These facts do not automatically establish **undue** delay, prejudice, motive, or the district court's actual reason. The missing denial order proves neither that no reason was stated nor that none appears from the supported record. A rationale adopting an apparent justification must identify that justification and why the supported circumstances satisfy it. A rationale rejecting such justification must likewise use affirmative legal and record grounds, rather than treating the research gap as an adjudicated procedural violation.

The state direct-appeal opinion, 652 S.W.2d at 113–114, Point XIV, reports actual proportionality review, compares *State v. Blair*, and states that it considered the crime and defendant as required by §565.014.3(3). At 117 it calls death-sentence review statutorily mandated. This supports the identified review duty and that review was undertaken; it does not establish the complete statutory comparison universe, files actually examined, or the truth and materiality of the alleged approximately two hundred missing cases.

The amendment is a reported **due-process** challenge, so *Pulley*'s rejection of an invariable federal comparative-review requirement does not alone answer it. *Hicks* supplies a possible state-created-liberty-interest distinction, not a finding that this particular review was arbitrarily withheld. That distinction is developed analysis, not a certified formulation or preservation fact from Williams's amendment. A legal assessment accepting the reported omission allegation may avoid deciding its truth, but must still explain why that allegation does or does not amount to arbitrary deprivation of the particular protected opportunity. The fact of stated review cannot silently establish that its performance satisfied every duty; alleged incomplete files cannot silently establish a constitutional deprivation.
