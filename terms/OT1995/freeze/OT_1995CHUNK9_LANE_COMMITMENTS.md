## Statutory question and supported paths

Section 504(a) expressly covers both a program receiving federal financial assistance and a program conducted by an Executive agency or the Postal Service. Section 505(a)(2), however, makes Title VI's remedies, procedures and rights available to a person aggrieved by an act or omission of a recipient of federal assistance or a federal provider of that assistance under §504. It does not repeat §504's distinct category of all agency-conducted programs. Section 505(a)(1) separately supplies the specified Title VII remedies for §501 federal employment complaints. Section 505(b) separately permits, rather than requires, a reasonable attorney's fee as costs for a prevailing party other than the United States. None of those distinct channels may be silently substituted for this academy damages claim.

The 1986 enactment, now 42 U.S.C. §2000d-7, expressly removes States' Eleventh Amendment immunity for the listed discrimination statutes and supplies remedies at law and in equity to the same extent available against a public or private entity other than a State. Its actual subject and comparative language, not an invented federal-waiver sentence, must do any work attributed to it. The facility and employment qualifications in §504(c) and (d) do not answer this academy waiver question.

**A — affirm the damages dismissal.** The enactments do not unequivocally authorize compensatory damages against the United States for discrimination in this federally conducted program. Section 504's substantive coverage does not itself state the monetary remedy; §505(a)(2)'s federal-provider language has the narrower assistance-related referent supplied by its words. The remaining provisions retain substantial operation without a general agency damages waiver. The 1986 State-remedies comparison does not affirmatively expose every non-State public entity to every remedy. Under actual Nordic Village, a plausible nonmonetary or narrower-remedy operation defeats the claimed unequivocal consent to the particular affirmative money judgment. Actual Department of Energy v. Ohio likewise requires attention to the particular remedy and incorporated coverage. Actual Meyer separates a waiver from a cause and recognizes that a broad sue-and-be-sued clause can waive immunity; no such clause supplies the missing consent here.

The strongest contrary argument combines the express prohibition on federal agencies, Congress's designation of federal providers in the remedies clause, Franklin's remedial rule, and the 1986 legal-remedies comparison. These provisions make a damages waiver a plausible account of the statutory plan. Path A answers that plausibility is insufficient under the governing federal-waiver rule: the distinctions among recipient, provider and operator remain enacted distinctions, and Franklin did not decide consent to a federal Treasury award. Injunctive enforcement and the express fee provision also prevent the false assertion that the no-damages reading erases every consequence of federal coverage.

**B — reverse the categorical damages bar and remand.** Reading the provisions together, Congress expressly placed federal agencies under the same substantive nondiscrimination obligation, included federal providers in the remedial provision, and subsequently recognized legal and equitable remedies through the State-remedies comparison. This reading treats the full statutory arrangement as sufficient consent, rather than requiring the same sovereign-and-remedy formula in a single sentence. Franklin supports compensation in the established discrimination action once that consent is established; it does not independently waive immunity. The limiting construction of agency coverage and federal-provider remedies is, on this view, an unwarranted severance of provisions enacted to create effective federal obligations.

The strongest objection is Nordic Village's controlling requirement that a plausible narrower reading defeats the claimed monetary waiver. Path B must meet that rule through the combined enacted text, not treat remedial purpose, legislative history, the violation itself or a judicial preference for compensation as a substitute. Its proponents regard the full statutory scheme as distinguishing this case; they do not purport to erase Nordic Village or Ohio for the Court. Their position would allow litigation of entitlement and amount, subject to all remaining lawful requirements, and would not establish a universal right to damages for every §504 violation.

## Disposition and reserved questions

The six A commitments affirm the judgment denying compensatory damages on sovereign-immunity grounds while leaving the unchallenged discrimination and reinstatement relief intact. The two B commitments reverse that categorical ground and remand for further proceedings on damages, without determining entitlement or amount. No row makes fees mandatory, disposes of a fee application not presented, extends the employment remedy to academy enrollment, or rules on a differently situated federal assistance provider.

The paths share the distinction among substantive duty, consent to a particular remedy, cause of action and ultimate recovery. The disagreement is whether these enactments express the required consent for this particular federal program.
