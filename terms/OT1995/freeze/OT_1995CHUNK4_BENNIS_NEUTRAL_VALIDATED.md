## Posture and lower-court authority

The trial court abated the jointly owned automobile as a nuisance. The Michigan Court of Appeals, 200 Mich. App. 670 (1993), reversed on knowledge, continuing-nuisance and proof-of-prostitution grounds. The Michigan Supreme Court reversed that judgment in State ex rel. Wayne County Prosecutor v. Bennis, 447 Mich. 719, 527 N.W.2d 483 (1994), leaving the trial abatement in place. The merits-review channel is review of that final state judgment under §1257.

The state supreme court's **judgment had four votes; the lead rationale had three**. Riley wrote, joined by Boyle and Mallett. Griffin concurred only in the result. Cavanagh dissented with Levin and Brickley; Levin also wrote separately. A four-vote judgment is reviewable, but the packet should not present every passage of Riley's reasoning as an opinion joined by a state-court majority.

The federal Court does not have a general commission to redetermine Michigan's statutory definition of nuisance or the trial judge's state-law equitable discretion. The state-law dispute identifies the statute's application and the property's connection to misconduct; a federal disposition must rest on the federal question actually reached or properly presented. No independent state ground identified in the safe opinion supplies an alternative affirmance that avoids the federal owner-interest dispute altogether.

## Record and statutory operation

On October 3, 1988, John Bennis drove the jointly owned 1977 Pontiac, picked up a woman whom police observed soliciting motorists, drove roughly a block, and engaged in a sexual act in the vehicle. Officers observed the act; they did not observe an exchange of money. John was convicted of gross indecency. The state brought this separate nuisance-abatement proceeding.

Riley's opinion finds the statutory requirement satisfied by lewdness associated with prostitution and, alternatively, by circumstantial proof of prostitution. It relies on the pickup, route, surrounding circumstances and neighborhood testimony. A guard also reported earlier encounters between John and women in the area. The dissent emphasizes that these encounters did not establish repeated prohibited use of **this car** and quotes the intermediate court's statement that the car allegedly had been purchased only three weeks before the seizure-triggering incident. Do not turn the earlier encounters into an undisputed finding that the seized vehicle repeatedly facilitated prostitution.

MCL 600.3801 designates specified premises and vehicles used for lewdness, assignation, prostitution, gambling and other listed activities as nuisances subject to abatement. Section 3815(1) permits reputation evidence about the identified building, vehicle or place. Section 3815(2) provides: **“Proof of knowledge of the existence of the nuisance on the part of the defendants or any of them, is not required.”** The lead opinion uses the surrounding area's continuing nuisance and this vehicle's contribution to it; its note expressly limits the theory to an area with such a nuisance condition. Its reasoning is not a rule that any isolated act in any automobile anywhere automatically creates an abatable nuisance.

The lead opinion **assumes arguendo** that Tina neither knew of nor consented to the misuse. It treats the vehicle as voluntarily entrusted to her co-owner husband, rather than stolen or initially driven without her knowledge. This is sufficient to expose the constitutional issue on that assumption. It is not a complete affirmative finding that she satisfied every conceivable innocent-owner test. The source does not show that the trial court measured her actions against every reasonably available preventive measure.

All relevant state writings recognize the equitable character of the proceeding. Riley says the trial judge considered alternatives on the record and permissibly abated the entire interest. Cavanagh argues that equitable power permits tailoring relief to protect an innocent co-owner, drawing on alternatives such as allocation of net proceeds; Levin emphasizes the statute's commercial-nuisance history and argues that removing a knowledge-proof requirement does not necessarily eliminate innocent-owner protection.

**Evidence hierarchy for the offset details:** The original neutral packet reports the judge's concern about sale costs and the family's other automobile. Those particulars are not reproduced in the safe lower opinion read here. ## Federal arguments and distinct paths

**Owner's constitutional theory, as reconstructed by the authorized packet.** Tina's title and lack of participation/knowledge distinguish her from the offender; ordinary spousal co-ownership need not make her interest constitutionally forfeitable for a concealed act. A rule permitting forfeiture for this car's incidental use in a neighborhood nuisance may reach beyond the historical justification for instrumentalities or property whose owner could prevent misuse. An equitable ability to return her share raises the question whether the State may constitutionally refuse protection, rather than proving that the Federal Constitution already mandates a particular remedy.

**State's theory.** Goldsmith, Van Oster and Calero-Toledo reject innocence alone as a universal constitutional defense where an owner entrusted property to another who used it unlawfully. In-rem abatement concerns the property's prohibited use; Tina's co-title and her husband's lawful access supply a connection different from a stranger's taking. The State can also invoke deterrence and prevention without treating each co-owner as personally convicted. On a valid forfeiture theory, acquisition is not automatically an eminent-domain taking requiring an additional compensation payment.

**What the reserved Calero-Toledo passage does and does not decide.** Calero-Toledo discusses an owner who was uninvolved and unaware and who did all that reasonably could be expected to prevent unlawful use; it does not establish the reserved constitutional exception as an already binding holding. Its separate discussion of property taken without privity or consent does not equate consent to lawful use with knowledge of the particular illegal use. Recognition or elaboration of a protection here would require stating its rule, burdens, and relationship to entrustment. This record cannot support a new appellate finding that Tina did, or failed to do, everything reasonably expected merely because she was unaware or married to John.

Legally distinguishable paths include:

- Apply the inherited entrustment precedents and reject the assertion that unknowing co-ownership alone establishes a constitutional bar. Any affirmance should identify its dependence on the case's entrustment and property-use facts rather than silently establish unlimited forfeiture power.
- Adopt a defined substantive owner protection based on the reserved Calero-Toledo circumstances, or another adequately grounded due-process limit, and determine whether the existing findings resolve its predicates. A remand for a legally material unmade finding differs from ordering immediate return on an assumed factual entitlement.
- Address the asserted distinction between an instrumentality and an incidental location, and between abating an existing nuisance and imposing punishment on an uninvolved owner's interest. Austin makes labels insufficient in its own field but does not supply an automatic due-process answer here.
- Analyze takings independently within the reviewed question. Co-ownership alone does not yield an amount of compensation; conversely, calling the action a forfeiture cannot answer the antecedent claim that the State could not lawfully take this interest. A return, limitation of forfeiture, further hearing, and compensation are different remedies and require their own predicates.

The packet identifies due process and takings, not an Excessive Fines question. Do not replace the presented challenge with a new Eighth Amendment holding, presume state incorporation from Austin, or create a value/offense ratio from missing figures. Nor is this a claim that no hearing occurred: the record describes a contested forfeiture hearing. Good's seizure-process rule is relevant law but does not itself resolve this substantive owner-interest challenge.

## Date-eligible law and Justice-specific anchors

The following actual public positions provide distinct starting points for later independent analysis. They predict no vote in Bennis.

- **Austin (June 28, 1993):** Stevens, O'Connor and Souter joined the simulated majority's historical/punitive analysis and its reservation of truly innocent ownership. Scalia, Kennedy and Thomas accepted the defined Excessive Fines coverage proposition but did not join that full historical rationale; that distinction must be preserved. The controlling remand did not adopt instrumentality as the exclusive excessiveness test or decide incorporation against the States. Neither Ginsburg nor Breyer participated.
- **Buena Vista (February 24, 1993):** Stevens authored the statutory innocent-owner and relation-back holdings, joined by O'Connor and Souter among current associates. Scalia and Thomas accepted donee eligibility and the practical result on separate relation-back reasoning. This is a statute-specific opportunity to prove a defense, not a constitutional innocence finding or a rule that relation back overrides a defense before adjudication. Do not infer Kennedy's full rationale from the majority excerpt; it is not reproduced there. Ginsburg and Breyer did not participate.
- **James Daniel Good (December 13, 1993):** Kennedy's prior-process portion was joined by Stevens, Souter and Ginsburg among current associates. O'Connor, Scalia and Thomas joined the separate statutory-timing holding, not that prior-process portion. Movable-property exigencies remain distinct, and the case establishes neither automatic final return nor constitutional immunity for innocent ownership.
- **Libretti (November 7, 1995), public reading copy:** all current associates joined the limit that consent cannot enlarge statutory forfeiture authority or transfer an interest beyond the defendant's lawful power of disposition. O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Thomas and Breyer joined the bounded sentencing/jury classification; Souter and Ginsburg reserved that constitutional issue and stressed actual informed waiver. O'Connor, Kennedy, Souter, Ginsburg and Breyer joined the record-specific rejection of a wholesale new factual-basis proceeding. Stevens sought independent statutory assurance on remand. These distinct, date-eligible positions supply a concrete forfeiture-related anchor for Breyer and Ginsburg without attributing to them participation in Austin or Buena Vista. Libretti's criminal defendant, waiver and §853(n) framework cannot be transplanted as a Michigan civil innocent-owner defense.
