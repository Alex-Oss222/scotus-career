## Governing text and present law

Section 6512(b)(3)(B) makes a refund depend on payment within the period applicable under §6511(b)(2), (c), or (d), if a claim stating the overpayment grounds had been filed on the deficiency-notice date, whether or not one was actually filed. Section 6511(b)(2)(A) uses three years plus a qualifying filing extension when the claim was filed during the return-based three-year period prescribed in §6511(a); §6511(b)(2)(B) otherwise uses two years. Section 6511(a)'s no-return branch uses two years from payment. The hypothetical is not a new actual return or a deemed early filing of the later return.

The separately operative exceptions retain their terms. Section 6512(b)(3)(A) covers payments after the notice. Subparagraph (C) concerns qualifying pre-notice refund claims with the specified disallowance or timely-suit status. Section 6511(c) concerns an assessment-extension agreement and its stated time and payment conditions; subsection (d) contains special income-tax categories such as carrybacks and foreign-tax-credit claims. The parties agreed below that none of those alternatives applies here. No filing extension establishing a longer period is identified. Thus the present decision need not reconstruct or decide those exceptions. It must not describe every nonfiler in every tax category as necessarily limited to two years.

The packet labels its Bufferd excerpt “Burlington”; the authority actually supplied is the simulated Bufferd v. Commissioner, January 25, 1993. That decision ties the relevant assessment period to the statutory taxpayer return, not an unrelated corporate information return. It does not decide §6512's refund lookback.

The actual simulated Williams decision, April 25, 1995, expressly preserves the duly-filed claim and applicable §6511 timing requirements while construing the statutory refund consent to cover the lien-backed payer there. Ginsburg, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Souter and Breyer joined its express-waiver ground; Ginsburg, Stevens, O'Connor, Souter and Breyer joined the additional taxpayer-definition ground. Kennedy and Thomas did not join those grounds. Williams grants no equitable dispensing power and involved no alleged lateness. Reich v. Collins, December 6, 1994, protects an apparently available state postpayment remedy against subsequent withdrawal; its unanimous holding creates no general governmental-estoppel rule or exemption from an announced statutory requirement.

Pre-divergence [United States v. Dalm, 494 U.S. 596](https://www.law.cornell.edu/supremecourt/text/494/596), treats refund authorization and its statutory conditions together; Kennedy wrote the majority, joined by O'Connor and Scalia among the present Justices. Stevens's dissent urged the equitable-recoupment treatment appropriate to the government's inconsistent taxation of the same transaction. Neither writing decided this notice-date incorporation question. The supplied simulated law governs Dalm's continuing effect.

## Enhanced statutory-path comparison

**L1 — notice-date path:** Reverse. Section 6512(b)(3)(B) uses the same hypothetical claim date to identify the applicable §6511(b)(2) category and to measure the payment period. On September 26, 1990 no return-based three-year period had begun, so the hypothetical claim was not filed during that period. The two-year payment lookback therefore applies. The later actual return does not change the premise fixed by the statute. These withholding payments cannot be refunded by the Tax Court under the presented provision.

**Strongest objection to L1:** The language refers to an applicable “period,” not to an irrevocably fixed nonfiler status; the section protects a taxpayer forced into deficiency litigation, and the later actual return would normally open the return-based claim scheme. Freezing both the duration and endpoint creates forum-dependent recovery even though the underlying tax was overpaid. The lower opinion also quotes predecessor provisions and 1954 reports favoring continuity and describes prior administrative handling supporting the taxpayer. L1 nevertheless finds the express hypothetical and the cross-reference decisive: there is no textual instruction to use the real later date for half the incorporated operation while using the hypothetical earlier date for the other half. The absence of the word “deemed” does not remove the conditional operation expressed by “if.” The predecessor/report material is considered as the lower court's documented interpretive argument, not as a new finding that the IRS had an exception-free settled practice. An awkward result does not itself authorize revision of the enacted limit.

**L2 — later-return / preserved-window path:** Affirm the Fourth Circuit's selection of the three-year period. Read the notice-date hypothetical as protecting the endpoint of the lookback, while the later actual return and claim determine which §6511(b)(2) window applies. That gives substantive effect to both the actual return-based claim and §6512's earlier endpoint. The holding would concern a return and claim actually made within the ordinary return-based framework, not an equitable power to disregard a concededly expired limit or a license to leave Tax Court proceedings open indefinitely.

**Strongest objection to L2:** It splits the incorporated hypothetical and selects a period using a return that did not exist on the date Congress specified. Its practical-equivalence policy is not itself statutory text. The L2 supporters accept that this is the decisive issue; their response is that the reference to the applicable payment period, the structure of deficiency adjudication, and the preserved role of an actual refund claim permit the lower court's reading. They would treat practical forum disparity as confirmation of that construction, not as an independent equity holding. They would not direct a $3,537 refund without adjustment to the stipulated tax and addition.

Neither path reaches a new waiver, estoppel, settlement-enforcement or universal jurisdictional-label question. Neither uses a later statutory amendment. A general holding about the refund available in every district-court suit is unnecessary: the parties' agreement about a counterfactual forum is not itself Court law.
