## Exact grounds, barriers and publication functions

**Nonaggregation ground — O'Connor, Scalia, Souter and Thomas.** A six-month-or-less maximum is presumptively petty, but additional authorized statutory penalties, considered with imprisonment, can rebut the presumption if sufficiently severe to show legislative seriousness. The supported penalty package does not rebut it here. Two separately petty statutory offenses tried together do not become serious solely because their combined authorized imprisonment exceeds six months. The distinction from contempt does not abolish serious-contempt jury safeguards. These Justices reserve the cap question; they cannot be counted for its validation or invalidation. The concurrent-sentence default is not a finding that consecutive sentences were unavailable. No broad approval of prosecutorial evasion is necessary.

**Aggregate-liberty and cap ground — Kennedy and Breyer.** They reject a rule permitting unlimited cumulative imprisonment after a single nonjury prosecution merely because each count is petty. They nevertheless affirm where, as announced before this trial, aggregate imprisonment cannot exceed six months. Their constitutional analysis itself supplies the ceiling in a nonjury petty-offense prosecution: the announcement clarifies that limitation and has no independent force to reduce a legislatively serious offense. Thus no new fact that the magistrate issued an independently binding statutory sentencing order, no waiver and no legislative reduction is assumed. A genuinely serious individual offense still carries a jury right despite a promise of a short sentence. The result is not justified retrospectively by the concurrent probation actually imposed. Taylor supports their distinction between petty-count liberty exposure and an individually serious crime; the absence of a legislative contempt maximum does not, for them, dispose of aggregation. Their concern about cumulative regulatory prosecutions is explanatory, not a factual finding of charging abuse in Lewis's case. They join affirmance only on this separate ground and refuse the offense-specific nonaggregation proposition.

**Prosecution-wide maximum and ineffective cap — Stevens and Ginsburg.** Statutorily authorized aggregate imprisonment in this single prosecution exceeds six months, so the jury right attaches at prosecution. Legislative seriousness is not abandoned but measured across the joined prosecution. Codispoti supports aggregation; Taylor's actual sentence supplies a legislative proxy only where a contempt maximum is absent. A judicial pretrial promise cannot replace the existing statutory maximum or remove the attached right. They reject the stigma distinction used to allow a cap for multiple petty charges while disallowing it for a single serious offense. They also reject reliance on actual probation, implied waiver or automatic harmlessness. Reversal/remand permits lawful further proceedings with a jury; it does not direct an acquittal or determine guilt.

Each substantive position must remain visible if published. The cap rationale is a judgment-only alternative to nonaggregation, not a narrower logical subset of it; both have different premises and implications. No aggregation of votes on the result may be represented as assent to either rationale. The prosecution-wide position opposes the affirmance grounds on both entitlement and cap efficacy. Assembly must separately test the disposition and each proposition after the authorized Stone material enters. This handoff makes no assignment and does not require the historical authors to write.
