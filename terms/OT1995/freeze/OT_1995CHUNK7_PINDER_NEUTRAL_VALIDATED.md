## Verified question, channel and facts

The question is Johnson's individual qualified immunity on the asserted substantive-due-process claim concerning his own conduct and assurances preceding Pittman's attack. Review proceeds under the already granted federal appellate channel, 28 U.S.C. §1254(1). The interlocutory jurisdiction below rests on §1291's legal-immunity collateral-order category, not on a final damages judgment. Mitchell, the inherited Johnson/Kimberlin rules, and current-term Doe and Behrens permit the separable legal question on properly assumed facts; they exclude deciding what the evidence actually proves. Constitutional merits, historical notice and appellate jurisdiction are separate inquiries. The district court expressly used Rule 12(b)(6), while the appellate opinions describe summary judgment. That discrepancy is preserved; neither description licenses adverse factfinding.

The pleaded events occurred March 10, 1989. Johnson responded after Pittman broke into Pinder's home, assaulted her, threw objects and threatened violence against her and her children. Johnson was told of prior threats and the previous attempt to burn the house. After arresting Pittman, Johnson assured Pinder that he would remain in custody overnight; Pinder alleges that this induced her to return to work rather than stay with or arrange protection for her children. Johnson charged trespass and malicious destruction of property under $300. Commissioner Ames released Pittman that evening. He returned to the house, set it on fire, and killed Pinder's three children. No warning of release is alleged to have been given.

The assurance-induced change in private protective conduct is an alleged causal mechanism, distinct from mere knowledge of private danger, a general undertaking to help, or custody of the injured people. Johnson's alleged acquaintance with Pittman and suggested favoritism are not findings. The en banc majority and dissent differ over whether the commissioner representation concerned Pinder's ability to swear out a warrant or Pittman's release; the district and panel do not mention it. Knowing falsity is not found. Accounts also differ concerning probation versus recently completed imprisonment for the prior arson offense and the precise threat wording. The favorable legal-review assumption is not a judicial determination that an allegation is true.

Two omissions are consequential. District footnote 8 expressly leaves the culpability standard unraised and undecided; favorable pleading inferences might permit intentional or conscience-shocking conduct, but no such mental state is established. Footnote 13 expressly states that Pinder had not sufficiently shown how more serious charges would prevent release; undercharging alone was insufficient in the district court's main analysis. The en banc dissent's causal account does not establish a legal overnight-detention requirement. Any disposition must avoid making either missing proposition into a finding.

The reviewed claim does not include official-capacity/municipal liability, equal protection, §1985, or Maryland wrongful-death and survival counts. The panel dissent expressly identifies the official-capacity claim as outside immediate review. No new procedural-due-process claim, duty under a reconstructed state charging statute, or freestanding warning claim is added. A warning can be analyzed as the proposed means of correcting the danger allegedly created by the assurances, within the presented theory.

## Judgment below and competing reasoning

The district court denied dismissal and immunity, treating the allegations as a possible combination of an affirmative increase in danger and the Jensen relationship factors. The divided panel affirmed on an affirmative-danger theory and conduct-time notice; its opinion was vacated October 14, 1994 and is not controlling circuit law.

The en banc court reversed the due-process immunity denial, 9–4 in the judgment. Wilkinson's opinion, joined fully by Hall, Wilkins, Niemeyer and Williams, treats the conduct as failure to protect rather than creation of danger and emphasizes the absence of a clearly established duty. Widener excludes the three sentences recognizing a different affirmative-danger paradigm. Motz joins Parts I–IV but expressly identifies immunity as the sole holding and rejects Part V's broad policy dicta. Hamilton and Luttig concur only in the judgment on the notice ground. Russell, joined by Ervin, Murnaghan and Michael, regards the assurance-induced withdrawal of private protection, combined with the ensuing conduct, as an affirmative increase in a known danger; he relies on preconduct circuit law and Anderson's rejection of an identical-facts requirement. His final footnote accepts DeShaney's rejection of a relationship based on knowledge and willingness to help, while relying on Jensen only for the different danger-creation proposition. These lower judges' positions imply no Supreme Court Justice's position.

## Law entering the event: verified through the complete May 20 group

**Text and statutory roles.** The Fourteenth Amendment forbids a State to deprive any person of life, liberty or property without due process. Section 1983 supplies a civil remedy for a person who, under color of state law, subjects or causes another to be subjected to deprivation of federally secured rights; it does not itself create a protective duty or convert a state tort into a federal right. Individual responsibility, a federal deprivation, causation and personal defenses remain distinct. The ordinary legal-immunity appeal under §1291 does not bring other counts or factual disputes within appellate jurisdiction. Section 1254(1) permits certiorari review of a case in a court of appeals before or after judgment; the admitted grant, not a newly invented historical filing, supplies the established posture. No actual state charging or release command has been recovered that establishes the contested detention counterfactual, and none is used.

**Surviving preconduct law.** DeShaney, 489 U.S. 189 (February 22, 1989), generally rejects a due-process duty to protect against private violence. Custodial duties arise from state restraint of the injured person's ability to act, not official knowledge or expressions of willingness to help. The Court also expressly states that the State neither created the danger nor made Joshua more vulnerable and that returning him from temporary custody left him no worse off. It does not announce a complete affirmative-danger test. Footnote 9 reserves the foster-care analogy; footnote 10 reserves culpability, individual immunity and municipal responsibility. Its rejection of the asserted protective-duty argument is not a universal rejection of conscience-based claims involving affirmative state injury. February 22 is **sixteen**, not eighteen, days before March 10; the lower dissent's contrary interval is not adopted.

Anderson v. Creighton, 483 U.S. 635 (1987), requires particularized objective notice: the contours must permit a reasonable official to understand that the conduct violates the right; the very act need not already have been held unlawful, but unlawfulness must be apparent in pre-existing law. The official's subjective belief about legality is not the immunity test. A relevant subjective element of the underlying constitutional claim remains a different matter. Daniels, 474 U.S. 327 (1986), excludes merely negligent injury from due process; footnote 3 reserves whether recklessness or gross negligence suffices. Those reservations cannot be filled by pretending that every nonnegligent failure to protect is unconstitutional.

The strongest preconduct danger-creation authorities and their limits are part of both sides' legal landscape. Bowers, 686 F.2d 616, uses the snake-pit distinction but affirms dismissal because release of a dangerous person did not place this victim in danger. Archie, 847 F.2d 1211 (en banc), discusses incremental risk and replacement of withdrawn private protection, but holds that the dispatcher neither propelled the victim into danger nor hindered other aid; its recklessness discussion and contrary separate writings matter. Jensen, 747 F.2d 185, recognizes the developing affirmative-protection theory while granting immunity for much earlier conduct; DeShaney rejects the broad knowledge-and-promise relationship proposition, leaving disputed the continued application of its distinct affirmative-danger reasoning. The Pinder opinions additionally identify Wells, Ketchum and Escamilla on the danger-creation side and Wideman, Harpole, Gilmore, Washington and Walker on the restrictive side. A circuit quotation is not automatically a Supreme Court holding, a plaintiff's victory, or notice of this precise mechanism. Decisions after March 10, 1989—including later 1989 decisions—supply no notice retroactively.

**Opening simulated holdings.** Collins (February 26, 1992), in Stevens's Court opinion, separates federal wrong from municipal attribution, rejects custody based on voluntary employment, and rejects the pleaded workplace omissions. Its Parts B and C reserve actual compulsion, deliberate bodily injury and a different properly pleaded affirmative-danger claim. Elder (February 23, 1994), in Ginsburg's Court opinion, requires independent consideration of all relevant precedent on a preserved immunity question, even if uncited below, without admitting a new claim or record or allowing retrospective notice. Albright supplies no controlling general bar to a bodily-security claim. These later decisions govern current legal analysis at their actual scope but cannot themselves give March 1989 notice.

## Supported paths, concessions and remedial bounds

1. **Affirm on particularized notice alone**, reserving whether this affirmative-danger claim is constitutionally sufficient. The absence of injured-party custody, DeShaney's treatment of knowledge and promises, the distinct mechanisms in earlier danger cases, and the conduct-date landscape support this path. It must confront the specific alleged loss of private protection and Anderson's nonidentical-facts rule; the existence of judicial disagreement is evidence to analyze, not an automatic immunity test.
2. **Affirm on constitutional insufficiency**, with notice as a possible independent ground. This can characterize the conduct as omitted protection against Pittman's private crime. It must address the actual assurance/reliance mechanism and Collins's reservation instead of assuming custody exhausts every possible affirmative-deprivation theory. State tort duties, knowing state-law illegality, or reprehensible conduct alone do not establish the federal wrong.
3. **Recognize a bounded constitutional claim but affirm immunity** because the rule was insufficiently clear on March 10, 1989. Current merits and historical notice need not have the same answer. A new constitutional rule must state its affirmative-action, culpability and causal limits and explain how the properly assumed allegations meet them; it cannot acquire notice retroactively through Collins or Doe.
4. **Reverse or vacate and remand** if the particular affirmative-danger conduct violates a sufficiently established preconduct federal rule. This path relies on the State's alleged displacement of private protection, not merely its failure to supply protection. It must identify the source and specificity of notice, answer DeShaney's rejection of the broader relationship theory, and confront the preconduct cases' actual holdings and culpability limits. It does not establish liability or entitle Pinder automatically to trial, discovery or damages.
5. **A limited appellate remand or threshold disposition** remains possible if deciding the proposed ground would require appellate selection of facts rather than legal assessment of the proper favorable assumptions. Pure factual-sufficiency portions lie outside the collateral appeal. That limitation need not preclude a separable notice-only question on an adequate assumed record.

The special-relationship and affirmative-danger theories may receive different treatment. No disposition decides the criminal charges legally available, the detention that different charges would produce, knowing falsity, motive, actual reliance, causation or damages as historical facts. No disposition reaches excluded counts merely because they share facts.
