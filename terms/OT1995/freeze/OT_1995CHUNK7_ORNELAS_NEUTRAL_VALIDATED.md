## Corrected record and lawful review

The reviewed judgment is the Seventh Circuit's April 18, 1995 order in Nos. 94-3349 and 94-3350, reported at 52 F.3d 328 (table), following the February 10, 1994 opinion at 16 F.3d 714. The federal appellate/certiorari channel runs through §§1291 and 1254(1), following conditional guilty pleas under Criminal Rule 11(a)(2) preserving suppression. The source reader reports selected docket entries and orders confirming that mechanism, and the preserved independent-review contention in both appeals; the full written agreements and complete circuit briefs are not reproduced. No new waiver or additional plea reservation is inferred. The government conceded defendants could challenge the vehicle search. All nine seated Justices are included, with no established nonparticipation; six constitute a quorum and a majority of participating Justices controls judgment.

The search occurred December 11, **1992**. Isolated 1993 references conflict with the consistent record and offense date. Detective Pautz's investigation began with an older two-door General Motors car with California plates and motel-registration/travel circumstances. Two NADDIS entries were located through names thought related to the two men. The entries were not established to identify petitioners, and the underlying database files are unavailable. The first appellate opinion treated reliability as unproven and analyzed the purported mutual and circumstantial corroboration; that analysis is lower-court reasoning, not a new reliability finding.

Detectives approached as petitioners entered the car, obtained identification and consent to search. The government conceded that a reasonable person would not have felt free to leave. The officer's gun **was not visible**, as the district order corrected the original recommendation. Consent did not authorize dismantling the car; the government did not contend that the stop or consent independently justified removing the panel. Approximately two kilograms of cocaine were recovered after a vent cover and panel were removed.

Initially the magistrate found reasonable suspicion, disbelieved the testimony that rust in the screw groove indicated recent removal, found no probable cause, but recommended denial of suppression through inevitable discovery. The district court upheld the search on probable cause. The first appellate opinion expressly held inevitable discovery waived by the government, accepted reasonable suspicion under circuit clear-error review, and remanded the unresolved loose-panel credibility question. It did not hold that a credited loose panel necessarily established probable cause.

On remand, the parties stipulated to the existing record. The magistrate **credited the loose-panel testimony**, maintained the adverse rust credibility assessment, and found the scratches evidence inconclusive rather than additional probable-cause support. He nevertheless recommended suppression. The district judge **accepted that credibility finding** and reached the opposite ultimate probable-cause conclusion. The second appellate order affirmed for want of clear error. Thus the live standard question does not require choosing between unresolved conflicting loose-panel credibility findings. No new inspection, evidentiary hearing, or finding that officers intentionally falsified other facts is supplied.

## Supported positions and requested relief

Petitioners and the United States both request independent review of ultimate reasonable suspicion/probable cause while preserving clear-error treatment of historical facts and appropriate respect for credibility findings. Petitioners seek reversal and appellate remand; the United States seeks vacatur and appellate remand, maintaining that the stop/search would survive independent review while asking the court of appeals to apply that standard first. Their agreement on review classification does not end the underlying suppression dispute.

Isakoff's amicus brief and AELE/NDAA defend clear-error review and seek affirmance. The ACLU, ACLU of Wisconsin and NACDL support petitioners. These are authenticated source-reader descriptions of actual positions, not reconstructed filings. The strongest competing legal formulations remain:

- **Independent ultimate review:** constitutional standards gain content through applications; appellate examination promotes consistent national boundaries, coherent development, guidance and error correction. Historical credibility remains for the factfinder. Thompson's objective mixed-question reasoning and First Options' ordinary legal/factual allocation support the analogy without deciding it. Warrant review's substantial-basis rule serves the preference for prior authorization and need not extend to retrospective adjudication of a warrantless search.
- **Deferential ultimate review:** reasonable suspicion and probable cause involve practical probabilities and highly contextual assessments; trial judges have experience with testimony and physical evidence, and many applications yield little generally useful guidance. Clear-error review can coexist with correction of legal error. Miller's functional allocation, Pierce and Cooter & Gell supply the institutional argument, with their statutory/litigation-management contexts acknowledged. Deference is owed to the district court, not automatically to officers. The same standard would govern an authorized government suppression appeal; it does not authorize otherwise prohibited appeals.
- **Differentiated review:** independent ultimate constitutional judgment may coexist with a separately justified treatment of particular subsidiary findings or inferences. Any such formulation must identify exactly what receives weight, what receives clear-error review, and what the appellate court independently decides. Officer experience relevant to the substantive reasonable-officer inquiry does not by itself establish a new appellate deference rule. No precise intermediate formula is attributed to a party without source support.

The amicus expressly answers the magistrate-referral argument: institutional trial-court expertise and the district judge's opportunity to supplement the record support one standard even when the judge did not personally hear witnesses. Petitioners contest the amicus's asserted resemblance between Jackson sufficiency review and clear error. Neither that equivalence nor the claim that every mixed issue is reviewed independently is an established premise.

## Enacted law, actual qualifications and distinct functions

The Fourth Amendment protects persons, houses, papers and effects against unreasonable searches and seizures and permits warrants only on probable cause supported by oath or affirmation and particular description of the place and persons/things. It supplies the substantive constitutional boundary; it does not expressly prescribe the appellate standard here.

**28 U.S.C. §636(b)(1):** district review of a magistrate's recommendation is a different institutional relationship. Section (A)'s authority to hear and determine nondispositive pretrial matters and its clearly-erroneous-or-contrary-to-law review do not govern the final disposition of a criminal suppression motion. Section (B) permits referral of suppression to a magistrate for hearings and proposed findings/recommendations. For specifically objected-to portions, the district judge must make a de novo **determination**, may accept, reject or modify, may receive further evidence, and may recommit with instructions. Raddatz, 447 U.S. 667, 673–676, reproduces these operative provisions and holds that a fresh hearing is not invariably required. Its footnote 7 expressly leaves open the serious question of rejecting dispositive credibility findings without hearing the witnesses. Here the district judge accepted the remand credibility finding, so that reservation does not supply a reason to overturn it. The JA's quoted statutory text is verified by the safe source reader. No unverified statutory timing or objection default is used; no full current §636 reconstruction is claimed.

**18 U.S.C. §3731:** the recovered official 1994 text permits government appeal of a district order suppressing/excluding evidence or requiring return of property only subject to its own qualifications: the order must not be made after the defendant has been put in jeopardy and before verdict/finding, and the United States attorney must certify no purpose of delay and that the evidence is substantial proof of a material fact. Appeals must be taken within thirty days and diligently prosecuted. The distinct first paragraph permits appeals from dismissal or a new-trial grant but bars further-prosecution appeals prohibited by double jeopardy. The separate release-order paragraph does not expand suppression review. Liberal construction serves the section's purposes; it does not erase its conditions. The 1971 amendment's savings clause excludes district criminal cases begun before January 2, 1971; no such transition is implicated here. This is an analogy supporting symmetry in **authorized** prosecution appeals, not defendants' jurisdictional vehicle.

**Rule 11(a)(2):** the conditional-plea mechanism requires court approval and government consent to a written reservation of review of a specified adverse pretrial determination; a defendant who prevails on appeal may withdraw the plea. The original selected orders' recitation of reserved suppression supplies the current posture. Missing full agreements do not establish noncompliance, and adopting a standard/remanding alone does not determine ultimate entitlement to withdraw.

Civil Rule 52(a) is not the direct criminal rule. Its familiar clear-error/credibility allocation is an analogy, as is civil litigation-management deference. Section 2254(d)'s state-fact presumption, exceptions and burden are part of Thompson's habeas holding, not governing statutory text for direct review of these federal convictions. No §2254(d) presumption or clear-and-convincing burden is imported here. Neither parties' agreement nor the Fourth Amendment's objective test supplies an automatic answer to appellate allocation.

## Entering law and its limits, complete through May 21

**First Options (May 22, 1995):** Breyer's Part II, joined by all Justices, states ordinary independent review of legal conclusions and clear-error review of findings; arbitration adds no appellate deference to the district judge's own arbitrability determination. It does not classify probable cause or reasonable suspicion. The exact Standards and Tests entry is **Independent determination and delegation of arbitrability**, present operation effective May 22, 1995. No separate current-wording authority appears. The delegation/assent portions are not transplanted into criminal review.

**Stansbury (April 26, 1994):** the unanimous per curiam opinion supplies an objective, communicated-circumstances custody test: formal arrest or its equivalent, with no independent role for undisclosed police or suspect beliefs. Communicated suspicion matters only insofar as it affects a reasonable person's understanding and is not automatically custody. It resolves a substantive Miranda trigger, not this appellate standard.

**Thompson (November 29, 1995):** O'Connor's Court opinion, joined by Stevens, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Ginsburg and Breyer among the eight, independently reviews ultimate Miranda custody in habeas while retaining §2254(d)'s actual treatment of historical facts and credibility. Its functional analysis preserves the factual classifications in Patton and Witt and expressly does not make every mixed issue independent. Thomas dissents: the definition is legal but the concrete reasonable-person inference is presumptively factual; he distinguishes Miller's history and function. Neither side's Thompson view dictates this distinct question.

**Surviving pre-divergence law:** Miller, 474 U.S. 104, 113–117, gives independent federal review to ultimate confession voluntariness and respects subsidiary findings under the applicable statute; functional allocation depends on the nature of the inquiry and the comparative judicial advantages, not the mixed label alone. Gates, 462 U.S. 213, 236–239, supplies substantial-basis review for an **issuing magistrate's warrant decision**, explained through the warrant preference. Its observation that individual probable-cause determinations seldom supply useful precedent is contextual reasoning, not a holding choosing the standard for federal appellate review of a suppression judge in a warrantless case. Sokolow and White apply objective totality analysis and distinguish reasonable suspicion from the more demanding probable-cause showing; their substantive applications do not expressly settle the contested appellate allocation. Raddatz preserves the determination/hearing distinction and its credibility reservation.

Salve Regina, 499 U.S. 225, requires independent review of district state-law conclusions in diversity and explains appellate institutional advantages, while recognizing deference where the particular task calls for it. Pierce, 487 U.S. 552, Part II, reviews EAJA substantial justification for abuse of discretion using statutory context, comparative position, costs and limited generalizability; it does not create a universal test. Cooter & Gell, 496 U.S. 384, Part IV, applies abuse-of-discretion review to the Rule 11 determination under the rule then before the Court but treats an erroneous legal view or clearly erroneous evidentiary assessment as an abuse. Their litigation-management setting and particular enactments matter; neither is direct Fourth Amendment authority. These original opinions are checked for the identified passages and joins, not imported beyond their actual scope.

**Later issue-specific allocation authority.** [Markman](../entering-law/PUBLIC_Markman_v_Westview_Instruments_Inc_merits_1996-04-23.md), effective April 23, assigns patent claim construction to the judge, including genuine technical-meaning and credibility disputes embedded in that interpretive task. Souter authors Parts I–III; all eight non-Chief Justices join every Part. Part II expressly reserves appellate treatment of subsidiary findings: trial allocation does not establish universal independent factual review. The public legal-boundary and consistency rationale may inform the institutional comparison here, but neither the patent task nor its judge/jury allocation answers Ornelas's appellate classification. [Sheets](../entering-law/PUBLIC_Sheets_v_Salt_Lake_County_merits_1996-04-29.md), in Stevens's Parts I–II joined by O'Connor, Kennedy, Souter, Ginsburg and Breyer as well as the Chief, independently decides constitutional coverage and legal sufficiency while preserving supported trial findings under ordinary Rule 50 review. It expressly confines Markman to its actual task; its civil verdict setting is not this suppression standard.

**Completed May 20 law.** [Casarotto](../entering-law/PUBLIC_Doctors_Associates_Inc_v_Casarotto_merits_1996-05-20.md), Breyer's Part III joined by the seven other Justices supporting its merits judgment, preserves First Options' ordinary independent legal and clear-error factual review, actual antecedent assent and contractual limits. Thomas joins only Part I's reviewability holding; no Part III join is attributed to him. Casarotto neither classifies suspicion/probable cause nor extends its §1257 state-court finality route to these federal convictions. [Henderson](../entering-law/PUBLIC_Henderson_v_United_States_merits_1996-05-20.md) concerns a particular Rule 4/admiralty service conflict and preserves actual procedural authority and express pending-case transitions; it supplies no independent basis to change this appellate channel. Romer's equal-protection holding and BMW's punitive-damages holding provide no new suppression-review allocation or ground to expand the granted question. The complete May 20 group used its common May 13 baseline; all four decisions are effective law here.

**Actual Pinder, effective May 21.** O'Connor's Parts I–II are joined by all nine Justices. Part I independently determines the separable legal-immunity issue on properly assumed favorable facts, without reweighing factual sufficiency, disputed intent or causation or admitting separate excluded claims. Its preservation of Johnson, Kimberlin, Doe and Behrens is relevant to keeping legal review distinct from historical factfinding. Its favorable-assumption rule belongs to that civil interlocutory posture; Ornelas instead has accepted suppression findings and credibility determinations, not a license to substitute favorable pleading assumptions. Pinder does not classify ultimate reasonable suspicion or probable cause, require independent review of every mixed issue, or resolve the weight due subsidiary inferences here. Part III's five-Justice notice-only affirmance and Ginsburg's dissent, joined by Stevens, Souter and Breyer, concern a different constitutional attribution and historical immunity question. ## Lawful decisional paths and exact remedies

1. **Independent review with appellate remand:** hold the ultimate reasonable-suspicion and probable-cause determinations independently reviewable, preserve historical facts/credibility, and reverse or vacate the judgment as the chosen legal ground warrants. The court of appeals applies the standard first; no present suppression, acquittal, release or new trial follows automatically.
2. **Clear-error review and affirmance:** sustain the reviewed allocation on the supported institutional/classification ground while preserving independent correction of legal-rule errors. This path must answer the warrant/nonwarrant difference and the national-constitutional-boundary argument; it does not silently defer to the police or adjudicate NADDIS disclosure.
3. **An expressly differentiated rule with bounded remand:** identify the object and degree of deference for subsidiary inferences separately from historical facts and ultimate constitutional sufficiency. Explain how the rule changes the Seventh Circuit's analysis and what must be reconsidered; do not write an undefined compromise or decide the application outside scope.
4. **Separate treatment of reasonable suspicion and probable cause**, if their functions materially justify it, remains analytically possible because the granted question names both. It requires independent support and a component-specific remedy, not an inference from their placement on a continuum. The final order concerns probable cause; the earlier reasonable-suspicion determination remains part of the preserved question described in the safe record.

Unavailability of raw database files is not a ground to find party default or order production for the operator. Inevitable discovery is already waived and cannot become an alternative affirmance ground merely because a drug dog was present. Consent cannot be extended beyond its established scope. No identified jurisdictional or participation defect independently requires dismissal; no threshold disposition is selected merely because original papers remain outside this context.
