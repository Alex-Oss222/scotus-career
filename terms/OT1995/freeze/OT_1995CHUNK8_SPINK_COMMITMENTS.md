## Threshold, operative law and separate components

All eight Associates reach the submitted questions on certiorari. They accept the complaint's material allegations for the Rule 12(b)(6) posture, without turning those allegations into findings. No independent jurisdictional barrier or dismissal-as-improvidently-granted ground is established. The operative components are:

- **Design/adoption:** Whether the sponsor and board perform a section 3(21)(A) fiduciary function merely by amending the pension plan to offer enhanced benefits subject to release conditions.
- **Implementation and section 406:** Whether actual administration or disposition of plan assets is separately fiduciary conduct, and whether payment of the pleaded enhanced benefits conditioned on releases is itself a prohibited transaction under section 406(a)(1)(D).
- **Remaining Count III theories:** The unaddressed section 404 loyalty and section 403(c)(1) anti-inurement grounds, with their own elements and remedies. They cannot be disposed of merely by announcing the employer's sponsor capacity or rejecting a per se section 406 theory.
- **Counts I and II:** Whether the OBRA amendments require credit for the years when Spink was lawfully excluded from participation, notwithstanding their distinct temporal provisions.
- **Decree and fees:** The reviewed reinstatement of Counts I–III and its supporting grounds must be separated from the unpresented Count V and collateral-estoppel dispositions and from the dependent appellate fee award.

The controlling **Corporate procedures for ERISA plan amendments** Standard is effective March 6, 1995 (Curtiss-Wright). A sufficient reservation of amendment power is distinct from actual authorized adoption, and sponsor power is distinct from fiduciary administration. **ERISA guaranteed-benefit exclusions and insurer fiduciary functions** is effective December 13, 1993 (John Hancock): statutory asset status and the particular function are distinct from breach, causation, loss and relief. **ERISA equitable categories and nonfiduciary compensation** is effective June 1, 1993 (Mertens): appropriate equitable relief means traditionally equitable categories, with its antecedent-action reservation intact. Varity's March 19, 1996 holdings require function-specific analysis, permit appropriate individual equity for a proved section 404 violation, and preserve the qualified rule that an adequate statutory remedy normally makes further equitable relief unnecessary. Peacock requires an actual statutory or plan predicate; it supplies no immunity from a properly founded original fiduciary action.

**Statutory temporal reach and new consequences for completed conduct** is effective April 26, 1994 (Rivers, retaining Landgraf's sequential inquiry). Congressional temporal direction governs first; otherwise new legal consequences for completed conduct require clear congressional intent. A past fact alone is not a retrospective legal consequence. Stevens, O'Connor, Souter and Ginsburg adopted the general framework; Scalia, Kennedy and Thomas used the distinct relevant-activity approach. Breyer has no invented pre-appointment Landgraf join. His actual Smiley Part V join, June 3, 1996, distinguishes interpretation for antecedent transactions from unauthorized retrospective legislative change; it supplies no new-benefit entitlement here.

### Statutory boundaries all commitments preserve

Section 3(21)(A) attaches fiduciary status **to the extent** of the specified functions. Discretion qualifies plan management and administration; authority or control over disposition of plan assets supplies its own branch without an added discretion requirement. A person may be employer, sponsor and fiduciary in different acts. The investment-company provision in section 3(21)(B) is not implicated; its own-employees-plan qualification and preservation of other-law duties are not converted into a general sponsor exemption.

Section 406(a) requires a fiduciary causing a transaction with actual or constructive knowledge of its prohibited character, subject to section 408. Employer party-in-interest status supplies a different predicate. Section 406(b)'s self-dealing, adverse-representation and personal-consideration provisions are distinct; no unpleaded kickback or other new theory is adjudicated. No section 408 exemption is claimed for this arrangement. The structural recognition in section 408(c)(1) that a fiduciary may receive a participant benefit, computed and paid consistently with the plan's application to other participants, is not an adjudicated exemption for Lockheed or every release condition.

Surplus assets remain plan assets. Neither an employer's possible eventual reversion nor its ultimate funding responsibility authorizes free use of the surplus. Section 404's document-following requirement applies only insofar as plan terms are consistent with ERISA. Section 403(c)(1)'s benefits-and-reasonable-administration purposes and its actual statutory exceptions remain distinct from section 406's transaction categories. No exception for contribution correction, termination, qualified transfer or another unpresented arrangement is found; later amendment notes are not backdated to the May 1990 transactions.

## Shared enhanced comparison and bounded conclusions

### 1. Sponsor amendment versus actual fiduciary implementation

**Committed path, all eight:** A sponsor's decision to establish or change benefit terms is not, merely because it affects the plan's eventual expenditures, discretionary administration or disposition of existing assets within section 3(21)(A). Curtiss-Wright's recognition of amendment power, John Hancock's function-specific rule and Varity's insistence on identifying the challenged act support that classification. It applies to the pension plan's design decision without creating immunity from ERISA's substantive limits, funding/vesting rules or actual authorization requirements.

**Opposing path and its strongest ground:** The amendment directs existing surplus toward an employer advantage and thus could be characterized as control over plan assets. That account cannot distinguish the setting of any benefit schedule from its implementation and would make every sponsor amendment fiduciary by its financial consequences. Actual asset control by the Retirement Committee is a different act. All eight therefore reject a sponsor-only rationale for dismissing the implementation allegation: causing actual distributions may perform a fiduciary function even though setting the terms does not. They do not require an invented discretionary element for the asset-disposition branch.

**Scope:** No Justice commits to a finding that every actor complied, that the amendment was properly authorized, or that pension and welfare plans have identical substantive amendment limits. Those questions are not resolved by capacity alone.

### 2. The pleaded benefit payments and section 406(a)(1)(D)

**Committed path, all eight, at the following bounded level:** Assume for this legal question that the Retirement Committee acts in a fiduciary capacity when implementing the program and paying benefits. Paying genuine enhanced retirement benefits to eligible participants under a plan condition requiring employment-related releases is not, merely because the employer receives the release benefit, the forbidden transaction alleged under section 406(a)(1)(D). Sponsor advantage from a benefit design cannot itself turn payment of the plan benefits into a prohibited transfer for a party in interest. The rule concerns the described retirement program, not all transactions labeled benefits and not complete enforceability of any particular release.

**Explanation and strongest opposing path:** The statutory words reach indirect use for a party in interest, and a release is valuable to the employer. Keystone confirms that release from an obligation can constitute real consideration; calling an arrangement a contribution or benefit is not dispositive. These are substantial objections. But Keystone involved property exchanged with the plan in discharge of the employer's enforceable funding duty. Here the pleaded payments are retirement benefits to participants under the plan's new eligibility terms. The employer's receipt of an employment advantage from the program does not itself classify those distributions as the outsider exchange or diversion regulated by section 406. Pension benefits commonly serve recruitment, retention and retirement objectives alongside participant protection. Treating that employer advantage as sufficient would make the benefit purpose recognized in sections 403 and 404 defeat benefit payment itself.

This conclusion does **not** assume that the releases have no value, that surplus belongs to the employer, that participants consented to fiduciary misconduct, or that every payment described in a plan is lawful. It rejects the lower court's per se inference from the identified release condition and employer benefit. The terms and operation may still matter under the preserved loyalty and anti-inurement grounds or a properly presented release-validity claim. No statutory exemption is invented to excuse a transaction otherwise found prohibited.

**Narrower lawful alternative considered:** Decide sponsor capacity only and remand the entire implementation-based section 406 question. That would be necessary if the proposed decision turned on unreported release terms, an unknown payment recipient, a loss finding, sham benefits or additional conduct. The stated section 406 theory can instead be answered as a legal classification on the accepted allegations while expressly assuming fiduciary implementation. All eight accept that bounded answer. Souter, Ginsburg and Breyer in particular withhold assent from the materially broader proposition that benefit payment can never be fiduciary conduct or that no distribution labeled a plan benefit can ever be a prohibited transaction. Stevens likewise requires an express preservation of independently actionable administration. Those reservations are compatible with the bounded rule, not additional votes for an unrestricted immunity.

### 3. Preserved loyalty and anti-inurement grounds

**Committed path, all eight:** Return the unaddressed section 404 and section 403(c)(1) grounds for ordinary lower-court consideration to the extent preserved. Section 404 requires its own fiduciary-act and breach analysis; section 403(c)(1) is a distinct statutory limitation. The Court does not decide them adversely to Spink merely because the sponsor's design act is nonfiduciary or the particular per se section 406 theory fails.

**Strongest opposing path:** The same explanation that payment of genuine benefits is not a prohibited transaction may also defeat the alternative theories on these allegations. It may prove important to their resolution, but the appellate court expressly declined to adjudicate them, and their elements differ. Varity forbids substituting a sponsor power for examination of alleged administration. Mertens governs remedy classification rather than granting substantive immunity; Peacock does not foreclose an action alleging an actual ERISA violation. A wholesale Count III dismissal would exceed the sufficient grounds now established.

**Remedy limits:** No new finding of loyalty breach, inurement, deception, loss, release invalidity or entitlement to benefits without a release is made. Appropriate equity under section 502(a)(3), plan-centered relief under sections 409/502(a)(2), and any properly available benefits enforcement retain their separate requirements. Nothing here revives the withdrawn Count IV, supplies a new claim, or guarantees an award.

### 4. OBRA and previously excluded service

**Committed path, all eight:** Reverse the reinstatement of Counts I and II insofar as they demand pension credit for service during the period of Spink's lawful pre-participation exclusion. Section 9204(a)(1)'s covered-plan-year and one-hour provisions make the new accrual protections applicable to qualifying employees in the covered years; they do not command conversion of earlier lawful nonparticipation into credited plan service. Section 9204(b) separately confines section 9203's participation/normal-retirement-age amendments to covered plan years and service performed on or after January 1, 1988. Neither provision is rewritten to contain the other's exact words.

**Strongest contrary path:** Section 9204(a)(1) lacks subsection (b)'s express service-date phrase, and a current benefit formula can use past service without operating retrospectively. Spink therefore characterizes the refusal to credit the old years as a present age-based accrual reduction; the court below also relied on the different wording and legislative development. The answer is provision-specific: the present claim seeks new credit for years when participation and the resulting accrual duty were lawfully absent, not merely use of an earlier fact to calculate a newly earned current accrual. Applying the new protection to a participant in a covered year does not itself alter the legal status of all earlier service. The different wording performs different work for accrual protection and participation eligibility and supplies no clear retrospective credit command. The statute's anti-discrimination objective cannot substitute for that command.

Under Stevens's Landgraf framework, the new past-service credit would attach an additional pension obligation to the earlier lawful exclusion. Under Scalia's approach, the relevant activity is earning service and accruing the benefit during that period, not simply the later date of calculation or suit. Both lead to rejection of these Counts I–II theories. No holding that every use of an antecedent fact is retrospective follows. An independent reduction in accrual during a covered period, discriminatory administration, a different credited-service provision or some other unpresented claim is not decided.

**Transitions retained:** Section 9204(a)(2) applies its separate rule to qualifying pre-March 1, 1986 collective bargaining agreements: the first covered plan year begins on or after the earlier of January 1, 1990 or the later of January 1, 1988 and termination of the last qualifying agreement, disregarding extensions after February 28, 1986. No allegation invoking that transition is supplied. Section 9204(c) permits delayed formal amendment until the first plan year beginning on or after January 1, 1989 only if operation complies in the interim and the amendment covers that interval retroactively; it does not postpone substantive compliance. The December 25, 1988 participation date is the supplied relevant plan-year fact, not a new judicial date or finding of earlier noncompliance.

No proposed Treasury regulation is made binding law, and the lower court's retroactivity characterization is not treated as Congress's command. The holding rests on enacted terms and the controlling temporal rules, without using Smiley to backdate a new statutory duty.

## Decree map and aggregate boundary

All eight Associates support these component instructions, at the stated level of generality:

1. Reject the theory that sponsor/board adoption is itself fiduciary conduct merely because it changes prospective use of plan assets. This is a capacity holding, not the whole Count III disposition.
2. Reject the reviewed section 406(a)(1)(D) per se theory for the described genuine enhanced retirement-benefit payments, even assuming fiduciary implementation by the Retirement Committee. The lower per se ground cannot sustain reinstatement of Count III.
3. Remand the unaddressed, preserved section 404 and section 403(c)(1) alternatives. Do not direct unconditional dismissal of Count III or establish liability on those alternatives.
4. Reverse reinstatement of Counts I and II on their asserted OBRA entitlement to credit for the prior lawful nonparticipation period; the district court's rejection of those theories is restored. Do not adjudicate a new covered-period accrual or discrimination claim.
5. Leave the unpresented Count V dismissal and refusal of offensive collateral estoppel undisturbed. The lower opinion's inconsistent language about not reaching estoppel does not authorize a new estoppel judgment. Withdrawn Count IV remains outside the case.
6. Vacate the appellate fee award for reconsideration in light of the altered merits grounds. Its expressed basis included the now-rejected conclusion that Lockheed's section 406 position was meritless and its plan changes necessarily unlawful. No amount or new fee rule is supplied; any later fee decision requires its own lawful basis and actual remaining result.

This is an Associate-only eight-Justice commitment to a bounded common statutory ground, with material reservations preserved. It does not turn the positions into an issued opinion or supply authorship, a final Court tally, or a final join in language broader than the rule stated here.
