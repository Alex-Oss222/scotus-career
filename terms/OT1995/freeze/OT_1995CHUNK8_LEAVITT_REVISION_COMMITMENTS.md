## Concrete independent comparison

**Vera.** The Court now finally decides standing and merits, applies Shaw/Miller's territorial-design rule even on the strongest predominance premise, and removes relief dependent solely on that failed theory. It makes no conclusive appellate determination of disputed Dallas purpose findings. Independent claims, unappealed rulings and distinct collateral grounds remain separate; none actually sustained the three-district decree.

Leavitt differs at the decisive point: the Tenth Circuit expressly left §302(3)'s federal validity undecided. Correcting severability would therefore not establish that every independent ground for protection fails. Vera supplies no automatic-enforcement rule following rejection of one rationale and no duty to decide ungranted constitutional questions at the petition stage. It likewise does not prohibit a sufficiently supported summary correction confined to one ground. The original competing petition positions remain available.

The published separate positions were considered independently. Kennedy's common merits dissent proposes a different racial-classification trigger and addresses fit; O'Connor additionally treats valid §2 compliance as compelling while preserving practical latitude; Scalia and Thomas preserve broader classification and statutory objections. Those subject-specific positions do not change Casey or resolve Utah severability. Souter's merits opinion, joined by Stevens, Ginsburg and Breyer, preserves independent wrongs and grounds while ending wholly dependent relief. Every Associate joins the threshold opinion separating standing from merits; it creates no new Leavitt standing issue. A separate unadopted threshold proposal supplies no controlling rule.

**Egelhoff.** The Court accepts Montana's highest court's construction of the actual retained mental element, rejects the particular blanket restriction on otherwise admissible rebuttal evidence, and affirms the existing new-trial judgment while leaving other state assignments unresolved. It does not prescribe subjective intent for every crime, adopt automatic harmlessness, invalidate all evidentiary limits or decide abortion regulation.

Its central interpretive premise differs from Leavitt: the latter involves a federal court's disputed prediction of Utah severability law, not an authoritative Utah Supreme Court construction of this statute. Egelhoff therefore does not bar correction of the Tenth Circuit. Conversely, no Egelhoff writing authorizes an invented saving construction, medical fact or implied repeal. Its preservation of separate state assignments is consistent with leaving Leavitt's unadjudicated federal validity available after any Q1 correction.

Souter's concurrence emphasizes the binding state-law premise and leaves a genuine different culpability definition open. Breyer's concurrence, joined by Stevens, objects to substituting an uncertain behavioral proxy for the actual mental fact. Scalia's dissent, joined by Kennedy and Thomas, distinguishes substantive-definition and historically justified evidentiary grounds; it creates no general exception from governing constitutional law. Ginsburg's separate dissent identifies the enactment's operation as defining culpability and treats the state court's contrary view as affected by a restrictive federal premise, without joining Scalia's alternative evidentiary ground. Her willingness to correct that interpretive error is not a commitment to summary review of every disputed local-law question. These comparisons do not dictate a different Leavitt preference.

The complete Casey framework, Fargo's distinction between notice and adequate health protection, Miller's actual minor-notice application, and Dalton's conflict-specific remedy remain unchanged. Neither new decision turns state-law severability into constitutional validity, makes a fixed gestational week individualized viability, or imports HB206's section-specific viability method into §§307–308.
