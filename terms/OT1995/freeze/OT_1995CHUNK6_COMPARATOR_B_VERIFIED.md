## Carlisle v. United States, 517 U.S. 416 (1996), No. 94-9247, April 29

Sources: [syllabus](https://www.law.cornell.edu/supct/html/94-9247.ZS.html); [Scalia Court opinion](https://www.law.cornell.edu/supct/html/94-9247.ZO.html); [Souter concurrence](https://www.law.cornell.edu/supct/html/94-9247.ZC.html); [Ginsburg concurrence](https://www.law.cornell.edu/supct/html/94-9247.ZC1.html); [Stevens dissent](https://www.law.cornell.edu/supct/html/94-9247.ZD.html); [official report](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/USREPORTS-517/pdf/USREPORTS-517-416.pdf).

**Topology and judgment:** **Affirmed, 7–2**, Sixth Circuit 48 F.3d 190. Scalia for the Court, joined in full by Rehnquist, O'Connor, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg, Breyer. Souter concurs separately alone. Ginsburg concurs separately, joined by Souter and Breyer. Stevens dissents, joined by Kennedy. No partial Court-opinion joins; all separate concurrences also join the Court opinion.

**Historical rule:** The district court lacked authority to grant the postverdict acquittal motion filed one day after Rule 29(c)'s seven-day period, where no extension was fixed during that period. Rule 45(b) forecloses excusable-neglect enlargement; legal innocence, filing before sentencing, and attorney error do not alter the time bar. Rule 29(a) concerns pre-submission acquittal; Rule 29(b)'s reservation procedure does not establish an unreserved sua sponte power after guilty verdict. Rule 29(c)'s distinction between a guilty verdict and discharge without verdict matters: after a guilty verdict the court acts “on such motion.” Rules 2 and 57 do not authorize departures from a clear controlling rule. Inherent/supervisory authority cannot circumvent the Rules; the Court rejects the asserted historical sua sponte postverdict power. All Writs/coram nobis do not displace Rule 29; no developed due-process basis was offered for the proposed new right. The affirmed appellate judgment required reinstating the verdict and sentencing, **not** this Court's own sufficiency determination.

**Souter, 434:** Accepts an inherent acquittal power absent contrary law but is unpersuaded it includes sua sponte acquittal after verdict; inherent power generally is legislatively defeasible. He reserves possible Article III limits on legislative interference, unimplicated here because Rule 29(c)'s constitutionality on that ground is not seriously contested.

**Ginsburg/Souter/Breyer, 434–436:** Rule 29(c) is a time prescription, not properly labeled subject-matter jurisdiction. Its firmness does not make every rule of this kind utterly exceptionless. Thompson/Harris Truck Lines recognized a sharply confined exception when judicial misinformation misled a party who could and probably would have acted timely. Here counsel was **not** misled: he failed to follow plain instructions. Ginsburg emphasizes remaining avenues—the Government recognized plain-error direct appellate review and §2255 ineffective-assistance relief—as adequate correctives even for extraordinary delays of the sort the dissent posits. Do not replace this with a general equitable-tolling exception, a holding that all possible relief is barred, or adoption of the Sixth Circuit's jurisdictional nomenclature.

**Stevens/Kennedy, 436–455:** Agrees Rule 29 does not authorize an untimely defense motion but argues its permissive procedural text does not withdraw the court's preexisting inherent power, while it retains jurisdiction, to prevent conviction/sentencing unsupported by evidence. Relies on historical error-correction practice and Link/Sisson, distinguishes Smith's post-appeal setting, and says the filing deadline limits the defendant's right to demand reconsideration, not the court's discretionary sua sponte correction. The majority expressly rejects that reading. No Marks question.

## United States v. Armstrong, 517 U.S. 456 (1996), No. 95-157, May 13

Sources: [syllabus](https://www.law.cornell.edu/supct/html/95-157.ZS.html); [Rehnquist Court opinion](https://www.law.cornell.edu/supct/html/95-157.ZO.html); [Souter concurrence](https://www.law.cornell.edu/supct/html/95-157.ZC.html); [Ginsburg concurrence](https://www.law.cornell.edu/supct/html/95-157.ZC1.html); [Breyer partial concurrence/judgment concurrence](https://www.law.cornell.edu/supct/html/95-157.ZC2.html); [Stevens dissent](https://www.law.cornell.edu/supct/html/95-157.ZD.html); [official report](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/USREPORTS-517/pdf/USREPORTS-517-456.pdf).

**Topology and judgment:** **Reversed and remanded, 8–1**, Ninth Circuit 48 F.3d 1508. Rehnquist Court opinion joined by O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg; Breyer joins in part and concurs in the judgment. Three separate concurrences: Souter alone; Ginsburg alone; Breyer alone. Stevens alone dissents. The official report supplies **no numbered-part designation** for Breyer's partial join; preserve the stated substantive distinction rather than inventing one.

**Court holding and limits:** Rule 16(a)(1)(C)'s “defendant's defense” provision does not supply discovery for selective prosecution; such a claim attacks the decision to prosecute rather than guilt. Assuming discovery can be available on an appropriate showing (the same approach as Wade), defendants must offer some evidence tending to show discriminatory effect and discriminatory purpose. The issue actually resolved is the **effect** showing: credible evidence that similarly situated persons of a different race could have been prosecuted federally but were not. The sample of prosecuted black defendants, general news story and affidavits did not supply that comparator evidence. This is discovery-stage insufficiency, **not** adjudication that discrimination did not occur. “Clear evidence” describes rebutting prosecutorial regularity on the merits; do not silently elevate the discovery showing to conclusive proof or conflate the two stages.

**Express reservations:** Footnote 3 leaves open whether the similarly-situated requirement applies when prosecutors directly admit discriminatory purpose. Footnote 2 leaves undecided whether dismissal or another sanction is proper after proven racial selective prosecution; the Government itself suggested dismissal here to allow appeal of the discovery order. No general remedial holding can be inferred from that procedural vehicle.

**Souter, 471:** Joins Rule 16 discussion only as applied to the precise issue. **Ginsburg, 471:** Selective prosecution falls outside subdivision (a)(1)(C), but the case does not decide other contexts, including affirmative defenses unrelated to merits; she refuses a reading foreclosing unpresented issues. Those narrow reservations matter even though each formally joins the Court opinion.

**Breyer, 471–476:** Rejects limiting Rule 16 to the Government's case in chief; its three categories, drafting history, possible work-product qualifications and supplementation by other law support broader discovery. Nevertheless these defendants fail Rule 16's **materiality** threshold for the same absence of available similarly situated comparators identified by the Court. Do not portray him as joining the Rule 16 exclusion.

**Stevens, 476–483:** Agrees Rule 16 is not the source and defendants neither prove the defense nor establish a **right** to discovery. But the district court retains discretion/inherent supervisory power to inquire, and the factual showing and severe crack/federal-state racial sentencing disparities justified some response; the order might have been broader than necessary, but no inquiry at all is the wrong result. He criticizes dismissal of the affidavits as merely anecdotal/hearsay (one rested on professional experience; no evidentiary objection made), and the Government's inability to identify a white crack defendant. This is a **permission-versus-entitlement** disagreement; never summarize him as finding the merits of discrimination proven or an unconditional discovery entitlement.

## 44 Liquormart, Inc. v. Rhode Island, 517 U.S. 484 (1996), No. 94-1140, May 13

Sources: [syllabus](https://www.law.cornell.edu/supct/html/94-1140.ZS.html); [Stevens principal opinion](https://www.law.cornell.edu/supct/html/94-1140.ZO.html); [Scalia concurrence](https://www.law.cornell.edu/supct/html/94-1140.ZC.html); [Thomas concurrence](https://www.law.cornell.edu/supct/html/94-1140.ZC1.html); [O'Connor concurrence](https://www.law.cornell.edu/supct/html/94-1140.ZC2.html); [official report](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/USREPORTS-517/pdf/USREPORTS-517-484.pdf).

**Judgment:** First Circuit 39 F.3d 5 **reversed, 9–0 in judgment**. All nine historical Justices participate. No dissent.

Souter's simultaneous joins in III/V and the O'Connor opinion are real; do not count him twice. Thomas **does not join VIII**.

**Controlling and materially divided propositions:** Part VII holds the Twenty-first Amendment does not qualify the First Amendment; LaRue's reliance on an added Twenty-first Amendment presumption is expressly disavowed **without questioning LaRue's holding**, which police powers independently support. Part VIII invalidates the two statutory price-advertising bans and Regulation 32 because the State did not carry the burden of justification. The lawful, truthful, nonmisleading character of the ads is stipulated. The inside-premises exception requires prices not visible from the street; the media ban reaches advertisements for out-of-state sales and allows a trade-journal exemption.

Part V's four Justices find failure both of direct material advancement (third Central Hudson prong) and reasonable fit (fourth prong). They acknowledge likely higher prices and some demand response but find no evidence of a **significant** consumption reduction, with the trial finding contrary. O'Connor's four assume the third prong **arguendo** and invalidate under the fourth: obvious nonspeech alternatives such as minimum prices/taxes would more directly achieve the sole asserted goal; reasonable narrow tailoring is required, **not** a least-restrictive-means test. Therefore the generic comparator must not attribute the third-prong evidentiary conclusion to a majority. Seven distinct Justices expressly support fourth-prong failure across V and the O'Connor writing (Stevens, Kennedy, Souter, Ginsburg, O'Connor, Rehnquist, Breyer); this common holding needs no claim that all broader rationales are a logical subset of each other.

Part III's four discuss commercial-speech protection and special care for total bans unrelated to consumer protection. Part IV's three favor more rigorous review for truthful-speech suppression than for consumer-protective disclosure/deception regulation. Neither is a majority adoption of strict scrutiny. Part VI's four reject Posadas's highly deferential paternalism, “greater power to ban conduct includes lesser power to ban speech” reasoning, and a free-floating lawful-“vice” exception. O'Connor instead distinguishes Posadas in light of subsequent, more searching applications of Central Hudson. Do not collapse these writings into a majority adoption of every Part VI proposition or an undifferentiated wholesale overruling of Posadas; identify actual support and treatment.

**Scalia:** Shares doubts about Central Hudson and distaste for paternalism, but insists constitutional judgment needs historical practice. The record lacks adequate historical evidence to replace the test, so he follows existing doctrine for this case and joins I, II, VII, VIII. **Thomas:** Government interest in keeping lawful consumers ignorant to manipulate choices is **per se illegitimate**; he rejects balancing for that purpose rather than merely requiring better proof that suppression works. His footnote 5 expressly reserves balancing for other kinds of interests. Footnote 7 reserves reconciliation with Edge and advertising for lawful out-of-state purchases where the forum State has banned/regulated the transaction; he flags why his categorical theory can differ from fourth-prong analysis. **O'Connor/Rehnquist/Souter/Breyer:** Existing Central Hudson is sufficient; no new test is needed. Independently agrees the Twenty-first Amendment cannot save unconstitutional speech restrictions, but discusses LaRue by distinction rather than joining the Court's express disavowal language.

**Marks caveat:** Distinct majorities already support VII, VIII and the shared fourth-prong application. The writings do not establish an across-the-board replacement for Central Hudson. No automatic “O'Connor controls everything as narrowest” conclusion is supported without a proposition-specific logical-subset analysis. Historical structure is evidence only and does not dictate simulated joins.
