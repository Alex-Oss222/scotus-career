## Record and lower judgment

The complete May 18, 1994 reported opinion and order, 853 F. Supp. 212 (W.D. Va.), including five footnotes, were read. Judges Widener, Michael, and Spencer issue a per curiam with no separate writings. It dismisses Counts III (§5) and IV (§10) **with prejudice**, denies preliminary injunctive relief and expedited discovery, and leaves Counts I/II (Twenty-Fourth Amendment/equal protection) and V (§11 vote-payment allegation against the North campaign) for the single judge. It does not adjudicate whether this particular fee actually violates a poll-tax prohibition or has discriminatory purpose/effect. Later party materials/JA report voluntary dismissal of the North claim after the convention and a stay of the constitutional claims; do not restore those claims on this statutory appeal.

RPV issued its call in December 1993 for the June 3–4, 1994 senatorial nominating convention. Legal qualified voters accepting party principles and willing, if requested, to declare support for its nominees could attend local selection meetings. Under the plan, convention delegates were elected locally and could be slated out or instructed; the complaint alleges that actual practice admitted essentially every otherwise qualified fee-paying applicant with freedom to choose a candidate. The district court accepts that allegation at the dismissal stage. It is not a final finding abolishing the party's written rules.

The call, JA 61–64, provides a $35 fee where certification/payment is delivered or postmarked within fourteen days of the local meeting, $45 thereafter, final certification deadlines, and no refunds after May 20. Albemarle's call required a nonrefundable $45 from convention delegates but no fee to attend its local mass meeting. These distinctions matter to the alleged burden and proposed relief. No party waiver as of right is established; the affidavit describes private sponsorship of two other applicants without asking their candidate preference, which is different from a waiver.

Morse borrowed money, paid $45, received a North-campaign reimbursement under the described circumstances, and repaid that reimbursement. Bartholomew and Enderson allege deterrence from seeking delegate status. Enderson's Hampton local committee is not a defendant. The verified complaint, JA 4–13, requests temporary and permanent relief, declarations, reopening of delegate qualification, and a specific **$45 refund from RPV or Albemarle to Morse**. The continuing monetary request cannot be omitted from mootness analysis. No refund or ultimate statutory liability has yet been adjudicated.

The complaint alleges no fee on November 1, 1964. Johnson's affidavit says fees existed as far back as party records reach, including 1964, and increased over time. The district court proceeds on the no-1964-fee premise for dismissal. Preserve the difference between an assumed pleading fact and a resolved historical baseline; even a surviving earlier fee would not automatically validate a later increase. The affidavit also distinguishes conventions, committee nominations, and the canceled 1990 primary. RPV says the 1990 and 1994 Senate contests concern different seats and the seat at issue consistently used conventions/committee nomination. Do not convert an unheld primary in another election into an adjudicated change governing this seat.

## Operative federal text and exact limitations

The official 1994 Code texts of 42 U.S.C. §§1973c, 1973h, 1973l, 1973a and 1973j were fetched and read.

**Section 5 (§1973c):** a covered State/political subdivision may not administer a changed voting qualification, prerequisite, standard, practice or procedure without the prescribed review. November 1, 1964 is the applicable Virginia baseline; the statute separately includes 1968/1972 baselines for other coverage determinations. D.D.C. may declare that the change lacks prohibited purpose and effect; alternatively, submission to the Attorney General followed by sixty-day nonobjection permits enforcement. Good cause permits expedited affirmative nonobjection within that period; the Attorney General can reserve reexamination for additional information during the remaining sixty days. Neither nonobjection nor the declaratory judgment bars a later enforcement challenge. Three judges and direct Supreme Court appeal are specified. Coverage does not itself establish discrimination or an entitlement to permanent substantive invalidation.

**Section 14(c)(1) (§1973l(c)(1)):** “vote”/“voting” includes all action necessary to make a vote effective in a primary, special, or general election, including registration, listing, other legally required prerequisites, casting/counting, and appropriate totals for candidates for **public or party office** and propositions. The examples are nonexclusive, but “election” remains part of the enacted definition. Section 14(c)(2)'s subdivision definition is county/parish, or another registration-administering subdivision where those units do not supervise registration; Sheffield's construction must be considered rather than assuming every private organization is literally a county. Section 14(b) reserves specified preclearance declarations and restraints on federal enforcement to the designated channel; it does not abolish the recognized local coverage action. Section 14(e), not the miscited §14(f) in parts of the OCR briefs, permits a reasonable attorney's fee for a prevailing party other than the United States in an action enforcing Fourteenth/Fifteenth Amendment voting guarantees.

**Section 10 (§1973h):** subsection (a) finds that a voting poll tax excludes or severely burdens limited-means persons, lacks a reasonable relation to a legitimate state election interest, and in some areas denies voting because of race/color; it declares constitutional denial/abridgment in some areas. Subsection (b), invoking Fourteenth §5, Fifteenth §2, and Twenty-Fourth §2 powers, “authorized and directed” the Attorney General to bring necessary declaratory/injunctive actions, including against States/subdivisions, against a voting poll tax “or substitute therefor enacted after November 1, 1964.” Subsection (c) gives jurisdiction of “such actions,” three-judge procedure, direct appeal, and expedited treatment. It does not expressly name a private plaintiff. Former (d)'s late-payment provision for a tax upheld as constitutional was repealed in 1975; do not carry that exception forward. Whether (a)/(b) and the wider Act imply this private action is a legal issue, not a premise supplied by the heading “poll taxes.”

**Supporting provisions:** §3 (§1973a) expressly addresses proceedings instituted by “the Attorney General or an aggrieved person” under any statute enforcing Fourteenth/Fifteenth Amendment voting guarantees, with separate conditions for examiners, suspension of discriminatory tests/devices, and retention of jurisdiction. Section 12(a)/(c) includes rights secured by §10, (d) provides Attorney General preventive enforcement, and (f) supplies jurisdiction without exhaustion by a person asserting statutory rights. These textual links support the private-enforcement argument but must be interpreted; a jurisdiction or fees provision is not automatically an independent cause of action. No §1983 alternative should be invented without establishing its presentation and separate predicates.

**28 C.F.R. §51.7 (1993):** the complete text is reproduced in the lower opinion/JA and Virginia's amicus: a party change affecting voting requires preclearance if it relates to a public electoral function **and** the party acts under explicitly or implicitly granted covered-jurisdiction authority. Recruitment, political campaigns, and platform drafting are excluded examples; changes to primary elections selecting nominees, convention delegates, or party officials are covered examples. Where appropriate “jurisdiction,” but not “covered jurisdiction,” includes parties. Section 51.23(b), quoted by the United States and appellants, allows submission by an appropriate party official. The two conditions, the voting nexus, and the express exclusions must all be preserved. Whether the primary example is exhaustive is contested. These contemporaneous primary reproductions, not unsuccessful official-PDF requests, are the regulation-text source for this packet.

## Virginia law and its disputed significance

The briefs quote the 1993 provisions. Article II §4 directs the General Assembly to provide for candidate nomination and regulate election time/place/manner/conduct/administration. Section 24.2-101 defines the relevant “political party” by at least ten percent of the statewide vote at either of the two preceding statewide general elections, plus a state central committee and elected state-chair office continuously existing for six months. Section 24.2-508 expressly preserves making party rules, calling conventions, nominating candidates (including vacancies), choosing committees, and other functions “inherent” in party organizations. Sections 24.2-509(A)/(B) concern selection of nomination method with specified incumbent-primary constraints; the quoted (B) applies where the previous party candidate was nominated by primary or unopposed filing and was elected, absent all relevant incumbents' consent to another method. It did not itself compel RPV's 1994 convention.

Sections 24.2-510/511 govern nomination timing and certification, with qualifying party nominees receiving general-ballot access without independent petition signatures. The briefs describe the then-applicable nonprimary window of thirty-two days before the primary date and the 7 p.m. second-Tuesday-in-June deadline. Independent candidates use §24.2-506's support petition (then one-half percent of registered voters, at least 200 in each congressional district). A convention loser is not automatically barred from independent candidacy as a primary loser is under §24.2-520. Section 24.2-613 gives party nominees earlier ballot placement. These particulars support both the functional-delegation theory and the response that Virginia recognizes independently held associational rights rather than delegating nomination power. No claim is made that the complete official historical Virginia Code was independently read; obtain it before resolving a disputed omitted qualification or ordering relief against any of these provisions.

## Actual entering law and relevant public positions

- **“Voting Rights Act §5 coverage of reallocations of governing authority” — Present operation effective Presley, January 27, 1992.** Kennedy, O'Connor, Scalia, Souter and Thomas require a direct voting connection, preserve broad/nonexclusive electoral categories, and give substantial consideration to the Attorney General within the statutory boundary. Stevens's dissent, joined by White/Blackmun, gives Allen's functional protection a broader reach over substantial removal of elected authority. Stone separately advocates a bounded structural-removal rule. None of those public writings resolves convention fees, and no view is assigned to later-seated Ginsburg/Breyer from nonparticipation in Presley.
- **“The designated channel for preclearance review” — Present operation effective Shaw, June 28, 1993.** All nine then participating agree that local review of the Attorney General's preclearance merits is unavailable, while independent claims retain their own predicates. A local coverage/remedy judgment here must not grant or deny preclearance itself.
- **“Private enforcement of Adoption Assistance Act reasonable-efforts and reporting clauses” — Present operation effective Suter, March 25, 1992.** White, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter and Thomas require provision-specific congressional intent for the asserted implied actions and distinguish §1983. Beneficial purpose alone is insufficient; administrative enforcement does not universally foreclose an existing right. Stevens joins Blackmun's individual-right view; Stone's public position is narrower on the reporting claim, and the dissenters leave a redundant implied action undecided. Their §1983 views are not votes on a new §10 action.
- **“Compensatory remedies for intentional Title IX discrimination” — Present operation effective Franklin, February 26, 1992.** White with Stone, Blackmun, Stevens, O'Connor, Kennedy and Souter recognizes appropriate relief in an existing action, not a cause of action created from remedial usefulness. Scalia/Thomas give narrower judgment support. This distinction matters to restitution and §10.
- **“Culpable affirmative assistance to a securities fraud” — Present operation effective Central Bank, April 19, 1994.** The actual Court preserves a confined received private action, with Stevens, Stone, Blackmun, Souter and Ginsburg supporting its rationale. Kennedy, O'Connor, Scalia and Thomas dissent against adding a defendant whose own conduct lacks the statutory predicate. The majority's received-remedy reasoning and the dissent's distinction between development of an existing remedy and creation of new liability are relevant analogies. Neither supplies a universal rule for §10. Importing the historical Central Bank outcome would be erroneous.
- **“Section 2 challenges to governing-body size” — Present operation effective Holder, June 30, 1994.** Actual §2 coverage has Blackmun, Stone, Stevens, O'Connor, Souter and Ginsburg; O'Connor/Kennedy differ about the comparison required for liability; Scalia/Thomas urge a ballot-access rather than dilution reading. Stevens, Blackmun, Souter and Ginsburg separately emphasize reenactment/statutory continuity. This law preserves the distinction among §2 coverage, comparison, proof and remedy and the separate change-based §5 inquiry. Historical briefing that treats Holder as a different fractured result must not displace it.
- **“Special federal corporations as governmental actors” — Present operation effective Lebron, February 21, 1995.** Scalia's special-creation/permanent-majority-appointment rule has eight Justices; O'Connor declines the disavowed theory prudentially while conditionally recognizing its force. Ordinary regulation, subsidy, benefit or property alone does not satisfy that corporate rule. It cannot be substituted for the particular public-election-function analysis here. The associated remand holding separates actor status from constitutional merits and ultimate relief.
- **“Private parade composition and compelled messages” — Present operation effective Hurley, June 19, 1995.** Souter's unanimous protection concerns compelled identified expression, with a material expressive burden required outside that setting; no universal immunity from nondiscrimination law follows. RPV's Tashjian/Eu/La Follette association arguments deserve their own analysis, including the actual fee and proposed preclearance obligation, rather than dismissal because some functions are public.
- **“Write-in voting within the complete ballot-access system” — Present operation effective Burdick, June 8, 1992.** The White/O'Connor/Scalia/Souter/Thomas rule assesses actual burden under Anderson. Its particular write-in result does not decide convention control. Norman's January 14, 1992 public election-mootness holding recognizes continuing consequences and reasonably recurring disputes; it does not make every expired election automatically live.
- **“Equitable vacatur after settlement moots review” — Present operation effective Bancorp, November 8, 1994.** Scalia's unanimous opinion separates lack of merits jurisdiction from authority to dispose of the judgment. A genuine mootness conclusion cannot simply be called a merits affirmance, and §2106 does not compel vacatur in every setting. No settlement is alleged here.

The actual February 26, 1996 **Voting Rights Coalition v. Wilson** public decision unanimously sustains specified federal-election registration duties under separate congressional/presidential grounds and the Attorney General's express enforcement route. It decides neither a general private enforcement question nor this convention-coverage issue. **“Personal districting exposure and direct apportionment review” — Present operation effective Miller, June 29, 1995** — similarly distinguishes standing from success and a merits injunction denial from a nonmerits dismissal. Its statutory/constitutional racial-design reasoning supplies no automatic cause of action here. General reach rules in **“Presented questions, subsidiary arguments and exceptional Supreme Court review”** (Lebron February 21, 1995; X-Citement Video November 29, 1994; earlier components retained) require separate attention to the preserved claim, supporting legal argument, alternative judgment ground, and new claim. This is a direct appeal; do not mechanically treat its jurisdictional statement as a certiorari petition governed identically by Rule 14.

## Strongest competing positions

**Voters and United States:** Virginia makes nomination by qualifying parties part of the machinery placing candidates on the general ballot. Classic, Smith, Terry, Sheffield, Allen and the statutory “party office” language support functional coverage despite the convention label. The fee is either a delegate-candidacy qualification or a prerequisite to practical participation in selecting the nominee, directly related to voting under Presley. The Attorney General's regulation, established application and Congress's reenactments reinforce coverage, while campaigns/platforms/ordinary internal administration remain outside it. Section 51.23(b) removes the submission-mechanism problem attributed to Williams. For §10, Allen, the protected voting interest, §§3/12/14 and 1975 amendments support private declaratory/injunctive/restitutionary enforcement rather than sole dependence on federal initiative.

**RPV and Virginia:** Section 5 names States/subdivisions and §14(c) names primary/special/general elections, unlike federal statutes expressly adding conventions/caucuses. Regulation requires both an electoral function and delegated authority; Virginia's ballot-access rules recognize support and inherent political association, not state control of the fee. Private nomination does not become a governmental power merely through certification; competitive elections and available independent candidacy distinguish the alleged machinery from the white-primary settings. Williams/MacGuire support the lower boundary. Pervasive preclearance of rules adopted at conventions would impose serious timing, party-control and expression burdens under Eu/Tashjian/La Follette; constitutional avoidance favors a confined reading. Section 10 arose from a compromise authorizing Attorney General litigation rather than direct legislative abolition of every state poll tax, expressly specifies that officer, and was not amended to name private plaintiffs when other provisions were. Allen's §5 holding does not decide every section. Alternatively the private financing fee is not a state poll tax, and expired convention relief is moot.

Those are legal submissions, not established findings about racial intent, the actual constitutional validity of the fee, or the alleged impossibility of administering preclearance. The United States proposes a boundary covering nomination/voting rules while excluding platform, ordinary debate, recruitment, campaign and most administrative rules; RPV challenges the coherence and practical costs of that boundary. Both propositions must remain visible.

## Decision gates and lawful reach

1. Identify the live plaintiff/claim/remedy. Morse's refund demand remains pleaded; determine its legally cognizable foundation without presuming its merits. Assess ongoing prospective relief separately, including reasonable expectation of recurrence to these plaintiffs and whether full review can occur in the election cycle. RPV argues delay and no demonstrated future participation; voters rely on recurring fees and nomination cycles. If no live controversy remains, use the correct jurisdictional/judgment disposition rather than merits affirmance.
2. Keep the §5 fee-change claim distinct from a challenge to the switch between primary and convention. RPV says the latter was not raised below and compares different seats; reply cites the lower oral-argument proffer and complaint's nomination-process language, asserting a supporting theory/no prejudice. The lower transcript has not been independently read. Scope may be resolved without deciding the broader change-of-method theory.
3. Decide the statutory voting nexus and covered/delegated party function with §51.7's actual conditions. The alternative practical-primary and delegate-election theories require attention to the procedural posture and disputed written/actual practice. Do not resolve the contested 1964 amount as a fresh fact or require proof of racial purpose/effect merely to obtain a coverage ruling.
4. Assess any association/avoidance defense at the actual scope asserted. Distinguish fee eligibility and ballot nomination from platform/endorsement/member recruitment, and distinguish requiring submission from an imagined federal veto over every convention discussion. Neither the public-function argument nor the party label is self-executing.
5. Independently construe §10's private action. An affirmative answer removes the dismissal ground, not the need to determine an unlawful poll tax/substitute, proper defendants, and permissible relief. RPV's alternative non-poll-tax ground may defend its existing judgment, but cannot rest on factual findings absent at dismissal. Voters expressly request first-instance treatment of these merits; they also argue a §5 ruling may make §10 relief premature while the fee remains unprecleared.
6. If §5 covers the change, relief must enforce the preclearance requirement and the proper baseline while leaving purpose/effect merits to the designated channel. Do not order a new convention/election, select a nominee, decide the constitutional counts, or invalidate every party rule. If §10 is enforceable, no automatic refund amount beyond the pleaded payment, classwide repayment, damages, or universal prohibition follows. Separate the no-longer-available temporary requests from prospective and monetary relief.
