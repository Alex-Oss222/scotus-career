## Provisional result and exact shared ground

Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer each **affirm the California Supreme Court's judgment** on the federal statutory defense to the pleaded California liquidated-damages claim. All eight join propositions P1–P6 below. Their sufficient ground is the Court's construction of §85 as applied to these contractual late-payment charges, followed by the federal permission and interstate operation established by *Marquette*. The judgment does not depend on a determination that the 1996 rule satisfied §43, on giving that rule force as a new rule governing the earlier transactions, or on treating South Dakota's statutory definition as the federal definition.

This is an affirmative construction of the submitted statutory question. It is not a presumption of agency validity, a penalty for an inadequately recovered record, a ruling that a procedural objection was waived, or a decision to treat every interpretation the OCC promulgated as controlling. The Associates do not find that the older banking precedents already adjudicated the flat-fee question; P2 supplies the present application.

### P1. Federal classification precedes the home-State permission

“Interest” in §85 has a federal meaning. Home-state law supplies the permitted rate for a charge that falls within that federal category; it cannot enlarge the federal category merely by giving a charge an interest label. Citibank's South Dakota location and South Dakota's permission for the challenged charges are undisputed. The Court therefore need not decide a disputed bank-location or home-state-law question.

Section 85 makes the home-State allowed rate one express federal measure, with the statutory alternatives and qualifications preserved. *Daggs*, 177 U.S. 549, 554–556, establishes that a law permitting parties to agree to an interest rate can supply the rate “fixed” or “allowed” by state law; the law need not print a particular numerical maximum. It does not establish that every contractual exaction is federal interest. South Dakota's permission is used only after federal classification.

### P2. The challenged default charges fall within §85 interest

The contractual charges assessed upon failure to make the required minimum payment on these revolving credit accounts are interest under §85. A default charge for overdue credit does not fall outside the federal term because it is a fixed sum, is contingent when the credit agreement is made, accompanies continuing periodic finance charges, or is not calculated in proportion to the balance, days overdue, or the creditor's actual administrative loss. This holding covers the Classic Card's specified $15 charge and the Preferred Card's specified $6 and later greater-of-$15-or-0.65-percent charges as described in the packet. It does not adjudicate collection of a charge that the contract does not authorize.

The statutory permission concerns what a creditor may take or charge on a loan or evidence of debt, and “interest at the rate” does not confine every component of that compensation to a periodic percentage formula. The additional price attached by the agreement to continued nonpayment remains a charge on the credit. The fact that it is also intended to induce timely payment does not itself remove it from the category regulated by the federal interest provisions. A demand that every such charge correspond to proven loss would import a liquidated-damages ceiling into the antecedent federal classification.

Petitioner's actual distinction receives an answer: she accepts some flat upfront charges and time-and-balance-based default charges, but argues that a contingent late charge cannot be reckoned at inception by those measures. Neither §85 nor §86 makes calculability at inception a condition of interest. The amount becomes ascertainable when the agreed contingency occurs. Section 86's reference to twice the interest paid concerns the amount of the remedy, while its greater-rate predicate refers back to §85; giving each provision its own function supplies no additional requirement that all interest be fixed or annualized at the outset. The statutory alternative expressed as a Federal Reserve percentage rate likewise does not make that alternative's numerical form the exclusive form of the home-state-law allowance.

*Donnell*, 195 U.S. 369, 372–374, is limited supporting authority. It held that the bank could not avoid the applicable interest restrictions through its compounding method or its characterization of percentage overdraft charges as a penalty for failure to pay. Its actual charges were percentage charges, and its principal reasoning enforced Missouri's limit on compounding more than once a year. It did **not** decide flat credit-card late fees. The present holding draws the limited lesson that default and a penalty characterization are not categorical exclusions, then resolves the fixed and contingent form from §85's own operation. *Daggs* supports the allowed-rate mechanism, not this classification by itself; *Marquette* supports exportation only after classification.

The complaint's allegations about nonproportionality, additional finance charges, continuing contractual default and lack of relationship to processing cost are accepted for purposes of the pleadings judgment. The conclusion does not depend on finding them false. No finding establishes that the fees equal collection costs, reflect higher funding losses, benefit prompt payers, improve credit access, or result from a competitive market. The controlling connection is the undisputed contractual late-payment trigger on the credit extended, not an inferred economic study or an agency label applied without examining that trigger.

This proposition does not adopt the whole taxonomy of §7.4001. Attorneys' fees, litigation costs, prepayment charges, noncredit services, and every other default or breach charge are not decided. The mere name “late fee” does not establish the character of an unrelated charge. No new evidentiary burden, cost study, annualization formula, general compensation test, or fee-reasonableness proceeding is prescribed.

### P3. The federal permission defeats this contrary California restriction

For these federally classified interest charges, §85 permits Citibank to use the undisputed South Dakota allowance in its lending to this California borrower. *Marquette*, 439 U.S. 299, 307–319, establishes the interstate operation of §85 for a national bank located in its home State; lending to out-of-state credit-card customers does not by itself relocate the bank. A borrower-state law cannot withdraw that federal permission by imposing its different permitted amount or form of interest.

Section 1671 is invoked here to prohibit the agreed charges unless they satisfy California's liquidated-damages restriction. Once P2 establishes the federal-interest category and the undisputed home-state allowance applies, that claimed restriction conflicts with the particular federal permission. The conclusion does not depend on whether California calls the restriction a usury law, a penalty rule, or general contract law. It also does not depend on finding an empirical burden on Citibank's overall operations.

The actual *Barnett Bank* rule, March 26, 1996, protects lawfully conferred national-bank powers against state prevention or significant interference while preserving federal conditions and compatible state regulation. It reinforces the consequence of the particular §85 permission identified here; it is not a substitute for identifying that permission. *Cipollone* and *Gade* retain their attention to statutory scope and traditional state authority, and supply no rule permitting a State to negate this established federal option merely because simultaneous compliance is possible by abandoning it. Conversely, ordinary state contract law is not displaced simply because it affects a national bank. No banking-field immunity or general invalidation of §1671 follows.

The alternative contention that §24 incidental powers independently preempt California law need not be reached. No unsupported significant-interference finding is made for fees outside §85. The 1995 California credit-card provisions, their treatment of banks located in California, and petitioner's asserted twice-per-cycle issue supply no independent basis for declaring all present fees lawful under California law; they do not alter the federal ground resolving the pleaded challenge.

### P4. The State-definition premise does not require constitutional adjudication

The first two questions are addressed to the extent necessary to reject their premise that South Dakota is being allowed to define the federal term. P1–P3 apply Congress's own choice of a home-state-law measure to a federally identified category. They do not delegate the federal definition to South Dakota. The Court need not adjudicate an abstract Article I or Article IV objection to a State's supposed power to redefine federal interest for the nation. Petitioner's related statutory objection to the scope of the exported rate is answered, rather than dismissed merely because respondent describes only the third question as presented.

No general holding validates every federal incorporation of state law or decides an unpresented constitutional challenge to a different exercise of such authority.

### P5. The independent statutory judgment neither requires nor supplies a ruling on regulatory validity

The precise banking statutes refute a categorical claim that §86 leaves the OCC with no administrative responsibility relevant to §85. Section 93's Title 62 enforcement provisions include §85; §1818 supplies conditional administrative enforcement applicable to Comptroller-chartered national associations, including uninsured associations under (b)(5); and §1813(q)(1) identifies the Comptroller as the appropriate agency for national banking associations, without saying that an institution can never have another appropriate agency. Section 93a grants rulemaking authority to carry out the office's responsibilities, subject to its actual exclusions stated below. Those provisions distinguish regulation of substantive bank obligations from the attempt in *Adams Fruit*, 494 U.S. 638, 649–650, to alter a private judicial remedy outside the agency's delegation.

That distinction does not adjudicate the sufficiency of every OCC interpretation. *NationsBank*, January 18, 1995, supports reasoned construction within actual banking authority after judicial examination of enacted limits; it did not decide §85 or confer an independent power to preempt state law. *Good Samaritan* permits reasoned interpretive change, but preserves text, delegation and explanation. *MCI*, *John Hancock*, *Town & Country* and *Holly Farms* require independent respect for the statutory boundaries. Here the Court can resolve the submitted late-fee classification through P1–P3 without making controlling deference to the rule an independently sufficient ground.

The authorized rulemaking account establishes the OCC's published description of a March 3, 1995 proposal addressing late fees and a comment process, followed by the February 9, 1996 publication and April 1 effectiveness. It is more than a bare position first invented by appellate counsel. That account does not prove all §43 conditions. The statutory disposition does not turn on treating the rule as procedurally valid, treating a prior letter as controlling, finding essentially identical prior issues, or selecting an exception. Neither the factual applicability of §43 nor preservation of an independently dispositive procedural claim is inferred. No ruling annuls or validates the rule; no waiver or litigant default is pronounced. Even if the disputed rule supplied no controlling weight, the adopted statutory ground would produce the same judgment.

### P6. The judgment applies §85 to the transactions, without backdating the rule

The April 1, 1996 effective date is respected. The Court does not hold that the rule was legally operative when the earlier charges arose, authorize retroactive legislative rulemaking, or identify a nonexistent transition clause. It construes the statute governing the challenged transactions. *Bowen*, 488 U.S. 204, 208–216, forbids an agency's unauthorized retrospective legislative change; Scalia's concurrence, 216–225, further distinguishes rulemaking futurity from adjudication of past and present rights. Neither supplies a prohibition on deciding what §85 means in the private action already before the Court.

This distinction does not mean a court can always avoid statutory restrictions on retroactive rulemaking by calling a new agency policy an interpretation. The sufficient legal work here is done by the Court's stated statutory construction, not by assigning the later rule a retroactive effective date. No settled judgment is reopened or factual reliance interest adjudicated. The Court adopts no universal rule concerning retroactive use of later agency interpretations.

## Statutory qualifications and unresolved regulatory questions retained

The following qualifications constrain the commitments; none is converted into a present factual finding or enforcement remedy.

- **Section 85:** The home-state rate and the one-percent-above-specified-Federal-Reserve-discount-rate alternative retain the “whichever may be the greater, and no more” language and the special state-bank rate qualification. The no-state-fixed-rate provision retains the greater of seven percent or the specified discount-rate alternative, and advance interest is reckoned by the instrument's days to run. The foreign-branch local-law maximum and bona fide bill-of-exchange/sight-draft-exchange qualification remain. The old business/agricultural-loan savings provisions preserve their historical transactions only. No such alternative or exception is used to enlarge the allowance for these accounts.
- **Section 86:** Knowing taking, receiving, reserving or charging above the §85 rate triggers the stated interest forfeiture; payment of greater interest permits the payer or legal representative to seek twice the interest paid in an action commenced within two years of the usurious transaction. The classification here does not find those predicates, adjudicate a §86 cause of action, or award its remedy.
- **Section 43 trigger and duties:** The provision covers an agency opinion letter or interpretive rule, issued on request or on the agency's motion, concluding that federal law preempts application to a national bank of state community-reinvestment, consumer-protection, fair-lending or intrastate-branch law, and the specified §36(f)(1)(A)(ii) determinations. Subsection (a) requires advance Federal Register notice identifying the issue and describing each state law, at least thirty days for written comments, and consideration of comments received. Subsection (b) separately requires publication of the specified final action. The word “interpretive” is not an exemption.
- **Section 43 exceptions:** Subsection (c)(1) removes the entire section for essentially identical previously resolved court or agency preemption issues, or separately for a response to a request lacking a significant legal basis for a preemption determination. Subsection (c)(2) removes the entire section for materials prepared for judicial proceedings, submission to Congress or a Member, or intragovernmental use; litigation use of a separately promulgated rule does not itself establish that exception. Subsection (c)(3) permits exceptions only to (a), upon the specified written determination of a serious and imminent safety-and-soundness threat, or connection to a qualifying default/danger-of-default acquisition or FDIC-assisted acquisition under §1823(c). It does not excuse (b). Section 1813(x)'s institutional default and danger-of-default definitions require the specified official custodial appointment or agency determination/advice and financial conditions; a cardholder's delinquency supplies none of them. No exception is found applicable here.
- **Section 93a:** Authority expressly and exclusively granted to another regulatory agency remains excluded; the section's grant does not apply to §36 or national banks' Glass-Steagall securities activities. Its affirmative responsibility-based authority does not itself settle §85 meaning, §43 compliance or retroactivity.
- **Sections 93 and 1818:** The conditional administrative and judicial enforcement links support the limited responsibility conclusion in P5. They do not eliminate required notices, findings or consent, culpability and harm predicates, hearing and review opportunities, remedy limits, agency and institutional scope, effective dates, or time limits. Section 93(a)'s judicial forfeiture route is distinct from administrative civil penalties; §1818(b)(6)(A)'s restitution-type relief requires unjust enrichment or reckless disregard of the specified law, regulation or prior order. The thirty-to-sixty-day hearing rule, ordinary thirty-day order effectiveness, separate temporary-order predicates and ten-day review route, requested twenty-day penalty hearing, thirty-day appellate petition period and exclusions, and six-year post-separation notice limits remain. More specific use would require the complete saved provisions, including modified Rule 65 protections and the purpose-limited ancillary authority in §1818(n). No enforcement action against Citibank, an affiliate, director or other person is entered.
- **OCC interpretation:** The effective public entry preserves the nonexclusive included-charge examples and ordinary rather than absolute exclusions; highest home-state lender permission remains subject to material loan-class conditions, including loan-size restrictions without a state-license requirement. Paragraph (c) preserves solely state-law definitions and calculations, and paragraph (d)'s corporate-borrower provision does not govern this individual borrower. Non-interest charges are distinct. These commitments do not validate every one of those provisions or decide their every application. No late-fee retroactivity or pending-case transition command is supplied.
