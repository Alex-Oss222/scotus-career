## Reconciled result

**O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg, and Breyer retain the dismissal route.** The later action supplies neither the asserted original ERISA jurisdiction nor ancillary jurisdiction to impose the entire Tru-Tech judgment on Peacock. They reverse the Fourth Circuit's jurisdictional and personal-liability judgment and remand for the jurisdictionally appropriate dismissal of the later action on its asserted grounds. The dependent assessments against Peacock cannot survive; the original Tru-Tech judgment is untouched.

**Stevens retains ancillary enforcement jurisdiction and opposes dismissal, with an express remedial limit added at reconciliation.** He would vacate the unqualified personal-liability and derivative-fee affirmance and remand for possible relief restoring fraudulently diverted debtor assets, subject to governing enforcement law, a lawful personal-jurisdiction/service basis, and the available procedural scope. Ancillary jurisdiction on this ground does not authorize substituting Peacock for Tru-Tech as debtor for the whole pension judgment. His frozen remand already withheld affirmance of that judgment, but its open-ended reference to renewed liability is now confined: the presently asserted control-and-diversion ground supports restoration, not renewed unrestricted full-debt liability. No particular asset, value, recipient, or new judgment is adjudged.

This is a seven-to-one division among the associates over the subject-matter disposition. It is not the final Court tally. Agreement that the existing unqualified liability cannot remain intact does not erase the opposed dismissal and continuing-enforcement mandates.

## Historical comparison and intact premises

Thomas wrote the historical Court opinion, joined throughout by Rehnquist, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Ginsburg, and Breyer. Stevens alone dissented. There were no partial joins or other writings. Rehnquist's participation is historical information only and creates no ninth commitment in this timeline.

The historical majority separately decided original jurisdiction and ancillary jurisdiction. At 353–354 and notes 3–4, it rejected an ERISA basis because the subsequent complaint alleged no underlying ERISA or plan violation; veil piercing is a means of imposing liability, not an independent statutory cause. It expressly assumed without deciding that ERISA might permit veil piercing in an appropriate action. The reference to Mertens does not turn that earlier case into a decision of the antecedent cause-of-action question that the actual simulated Mertens reserved. Anderson v. Abbott involved an independent federal statute making bank shareholders liable; it did not supply jurisdiction here.

At 354–359 and notes 5–8, the majority treated the factual-interdependence and decree-enforcement branches separately. A later independent lawsuit cannot borrow the original suit's jurisdiction merely by alleging a connection, and these controversies in any event concerned materially different conduct. The court's power to execute or protect execution of its judgments remains substantial. But Beecher foreclosed this new obligation to pay a judgment, and Riggs and Labette required officials to carry out an existing duty rather than transfer the debt to them. Rule 69 and the then-effective Rule 62 supplied execution and security mechanisms; the majority did not promise that they ensure payment of every judgment. Its reference to possible extraordinary circumstances did not identify an established exception or find one satisfied here.

Majority note 6 expressly declined to decide the government's proposed characterization as a supplemental bill to void transfers or compel payment, relying on the actual full-debt judgment and its reading of the parties' characterization. Thus historical rejection of this judgment is not a holding that every later property-recovery proceeding lacks ancillary jurisdiction. Stevens's note disputed that characterization by citing the lower courts, briefs, and argument; it was part of a substantive disagreement about the enforcement character of this action, not a different historical vote on all veil-piercing claims.

At 360–362, Stevens invoked Wayman, Riggs, and Labette: continuing jurisdiction covers a claim that a person controlling the debtor fraudulently exercised that control to defeat satisfaction. He expressly agreed that the actual order requiring payment of the whole judgment was excessive and should be modified, distinguishing an order to restore diverted debtor assets. He distinguished Beecher's proposed prejudgment co-liability from later frustration of enforcement. He did not decide personal jurisdiction, statutory nationwide service, the governing state-law standard, a particular asset valuation, fees, or a freestanding ERISA collection cause.

The key historical premises remain intact: Peacock was a defendant in the original action but was not adjudged a debtor or fiduciary; the subsequent award imposed the whole corporate judgment; the later conduct was materially distinct from the original pension breaches; and neither a new ERISA violation nor a separable asset-recovery award was established. The validated neutral record more accurately distinguishes some prejudgment transfers from later conduct. Neither the majority nor Stevens may be modeled as depending on the false factual proposition that every transfer occurred after judgment. The relevant common distinction is original pension liability versus later collection conduct; transaction timing remains material to any permitted restorative remedy.

The report's aggregate transfer figure and its factual shorthand are comparator material, not additions to the frozen neutral facts. In particular, no aggregate dollar ceiling, finding that Peacock personally received each asset, or ownership finding is imported. The neutral record already establishes the legally sufficient difference between the unrestricted full-debt judgment and recovery tied to particular diverted property. No new factual premise is needed for the reconciliation below.

**D — Components adopted by each of O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg, and Breyer.**

1. **Original jurisdiction and ERISA:** This asserted later collection action identifies no fresh ERISA or plan violation that can support §§1331 or 502(e)(1), and veil piercing itself supplies none. The original nonfiduciary finding retains its scope. Section 502(a)(3) authorizes relief for the statutory or plan obligations it identifies, not equitable relief at large. The Court need not decide whether ERISA can support veil piercing in a properly founded original action. The frozen Mertens category analysis supports this confined conclusion; it is not a claim that the historical majority separately decided every damages/remedy issue, or that every unsuccessful federal merits theory defeats federal-question jurisdiction.
2. **Ancillary jurisdiction and narrower enforcement:** The separate full-debt action cannot borrow the earlier suit's original jurisdiction through factual relatedness and does not become lawful execution merely because collection would be improved. The decree-enforcement branch nevertheless exists independently of a common nucleus of merits facts. Ordinary attachment, garnishment, execution, and protection of debtor property are distinct. These Justices do not convert the property-recovery reservation into an affirmative holding that this Court can affirm a nonexistent severable award, resurrect rejected claims, or order a specified asset returned. A properly invoked different proceeding would require its own legal and procedural predicates.
3. **Personal jurisdiction:** Not reached after the subject-matter disposition. The preserved service/personal-jurisdiction objection is neither rejected nor deemed waived. Ancillary subject-matter jurisdiction is not nationwide-service authority. There is no finding about sufficient contacts, privity, or alter-ego attribution of contacts.
4. **State law and preclusion:** Not reached as independent merits adjudications. No federal rule is selected merely from ERISA uniformity; neither the Fourth Circuit's categorical preemption rationale nor its claimed equivalence of South Carolina law is endorsed. The original nonfiduciary adjudication remains binding within its scope, while the Court does not adjudicate away claims based on subsequent conduct or disturb the original Tru-Tech liability. Dismissal does not extinguish otherwise available state-law rights.
5. **Remedy and monetary consequences:** Reverse the Fourth Circuit insofar as it sustains jurisdiction and imposes the Tru-Tech debt on Peacock; remand for jurisdictionally appropriate dismissal of the later action on its existing grounds. Its personal liability, dependent interest, and derivative attorneys' fees and expenses against Peacock fall with that jurisdictional foundation. The appellate instruction to recalculate an assessment against Peacock under that theory cannot survive. The already vacated $205,182.01 combined fees-and-expenses amount is not reinstated. The original $187,628.93 judgment against Tru-Tech, and lawful rights against that debtor, remain. No new suit, amendment, transfer, award, or downstream court action is invented.

**S — Components adopted by Stevens.**

1. **ERISA and ancillary scope:** He need not decide the alternative ERISA cause to preserve the court's power to address deliberate frustration of its judgment. That jurisdiction supports consideration of relief restoring diverted debtor property, rather than a new obligation making Peacock answer for all Tru-Tech debt. He does not affirm the original full award, recognize a freestanding federal collection cause, treat Peacock as an original fiduciary, or identify every later alter ego as subject to full-debt ancillary liability.
2. **Property and procedural scope:** Vacate the unqualified personal-liability affirmance and remand for possible modification within the preserved enforcement controversy. The existing findings retain their lawful appellate status, but the record does not supply a ready-made separable asset award. The lower court must identify the legally available restorative relief, its factual and procedural predicates, and any supported amount. This is permission to examine whether appropriate relief remains available, not a direction to reinstate a dismissed count, order particular property returned, or find that Peacock personally received every asset. An unavailable remedy is not supplied by ancillary jurisdiction alone.
3. **Governing law:** Rule 69's ordinary state-law route must be considered; general uniformity and beneficiary protection do not alone justify displacement. O'Melveny's significant-conflict distinction and Travelers's bounded preemption rule constrain the choice without already deciding every judgment-enforcement rule. Stevens requires determination and application of the actual governing rule on remand. He does not decide that South Carolina law is satisfied or prescribe the federal rule used in a distinct contribution case.
4. **Personal jurisdiction:** Resolve the preserved statutory-service/personal-jurisdiction ground before entering renewed personal relief. This remand direction is a modeled treatment of an independently preserved issue historically unreached, not a purported historical Stevens holding.
5. **Fees, expenses, interest, and final stage:** Vacate the derivative fee-entitlement affirmance together with the unrestricted personal liability. Any permitted new monetary relief and related assessment require a lawful basis and compliance with the preserved objections. Neither ancillary jurisdiction nor the original ERISA judgment independently establishes fee shifting against Peacock. The $205,182.01 combined amount remains vacated; the original Tru-Tech judgment remains intact. Stevens opposes a mandate requiring dismissal for lack of subject-matter jurisdiction. He does not order immediate payment or establish a special mandate date.

**No supported material departure from historical judgment alignment:** The seven historical majority associates retain reversal for lack of subject-matter jurisdiction; Stevens retains continuing enforcement jurisdiction while rejecting the excessive whole-debt order.
