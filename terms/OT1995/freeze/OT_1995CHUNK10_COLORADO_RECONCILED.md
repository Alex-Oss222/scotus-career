## Historical comparison, with exact limits

Historically the judgment vacated the Tenth Circuit's adverse enforcement judgment and remanded, seven to two including the then-Chief Justice. The principal opinion had only Breyer, O'Connor and Souter. Kennedy's separate opinion was joined by the then-Chief Justice and Scalia. Thomas's Parts I and III were joined by those same two; Part II was Thomas alone. Stevens dissented with Ginsburg. These are historical facts only; historical authorship and joins do not assign a simulated writing.

- **Breyer, O'Connor, Souter, pp.608–626:** Actual independence defeats treatment as a contribution merely because of party identity; ordinary contribution and coordination rules remain distinct. The deposition was examined as summary-judgment evidence. The agency's categorical label was not an empirical finding and did not control constitutional classification. The opinion declined the broader challenge for prudential reasons, expressly acknowledging jurisdiction and the counterclaim, considering the separate §441a(a)(2) problem and severability before broader constitutional review. It did not decide the additional vagueness claim. Its historical Turner and IBM citations must be replaced by those decisions' actual simulated scope, not silently carried over.
- **Kennedy, pp.626–631, with Scalia:** Party-authored campaign speech remains the party's own expression when coordinated with its candidate. Ordinary contribution cases do not answer that distinct expressive-association claim. Kennedy accepted much of the objection to the conclusive presumption but considered it insufficient relief and would reach the presented broader challenge. He expressly left potentially regulable undifferentiated party contributions distinct, p.630. This is not a repudiation of all Buckley contribution doctrine.
- **Thomas I, pp.631–634, with Scalia:** The broad pleading, ordinary civil pleading rules and chilled-spending allegation supported deciding the §441a(d)(3) challenge. Absence of actual coordination in this advertisement did not prevent considering other statutory applications in a facial First Amendment challenge. Footnote2 expressly separated deciding the central constitutional issue from resolving every consequence concerning §441a(a)(2) or severability.
- **Thomas II, pp.635–644, alone:** He rejected the constitutional contribution/expenditure distinction generally and used strict scrutiny of both. He considered bribery prohibitions and disclosure less restrictive than broad prophylactic limits. Notes3–9 belong to that general analysis, not to the parts Scalia joined.
- **Thomas III, pp.644–648, with Scalia:** Independently sufficient under Buckley itself: a party's ordinary pursuit of shared electoral/platform goals was not the corruption that justifies a spending ceiling; diffuse membership and retained donor-to-party limits weakened the conduit theory. This alternative did not depend on Part II's wholesale framework rejection. Notes10–12 preserve that distinction.
- **Stevens/Ginsburg, pp.648–650:** They treated party electoral spending as constitutionally contribution-like because the party and candidate have a unique interdependent relationship, supported anti-circumvention regulation, and additionally invoked equalization/campaign-cost concerns and legislative deference. The first two grounds are not erased merely by rejecting the third. Their opinion did not award a penalty or adjudicate a separate statutory §441a(a)(2) violation.

## What the changed law does—and does not—support

**Day's actual contribution holding:** All eight affirmatively joined a functional rule preserving the contribution/expenditure distinction, meaningful pooling, and regulation of realistic candidate-contribution conduits. Its reviewed $100 intermediary ceiling expressly excluded political-party units. Its nonprofit holding concerned MCCL's actual funding and structure, not all party expenditure limits. Its responsive-financing result remains fractured: four votes for a burden rationale, one different concurrence, and a four-Justice dissent. No unified rule is manufactured.

**Thomas's supported framework departure:** Historically his Part II rejected the contribution/expenditure distinction for all donors and entities. In this timeline he has since affirmatively joined Day's controlling statement that the distinction remains and realistic candidate-limit circumvention may be regulated. That is a concrete earlier published position, not merely the continued existence of Buckley that he historically disputed. It supports retaining his frozen refusal to reopen the general framework here. It does not preclude his separate, party-specific conclusion under Buckley—the same logical alternative his historical Part III advanced. Day's party exclusion and express reservations are decisive limits on the inference.

**Kennedy/Scalia/Thomas scope:** Day's reservation of coordination and candidate transfers is not a holding that all coordinated party-authored speech is constitutionally equivalent to a cash contribution. Kennedy historically preserved ordinary contribution regulation while distinguishing the party's own expression, and Scalia did not join Thomas's Part II. Their acceptance of Day therefore supplies no contradiction requiring abandonment of that distinct party-speech position. Thomas's Day join changes his general framework but leaves the party-specific alternative available. Their published Morse positions supply additional continuity against treating unresolved, chilling party-activity boundaries as an adequate answer; Kennedy's IBM dissent likewise rejects treating an included argument as automatically outside review because the government emphasized another ground. Those cases do not compel broader adjudication here, but weigh against attributing a new uniform preference for narrowness without a material changed premise.

**Ginsburg's proposed outcome departure:** Her affirmative Day joins and the financing dissent's uncapped-speaker premise were evaluated against her historical institutional and anti-circumvention grounds, not just against the historical dissent's equalization language. Day changes the permissible equality rationale, but it preserves valid contribution/conduit controls and excludes the party recipient category. The actual party–candidate relationship is the regulatory distinction she historically regarded as material; it is not a new fact supplied by a legal label. Neither Day's protection of a differently situated intermediary and nonprofit nor her commercial-information positions in 44 Liquormart displaces that distinction. The current Morse position she joined with Stevens similarly treats the relevant electoral function as material while preserving ordinary expression. It does not decide campaign finance, but contradicts a categorical inference that every voluntary-party characterization must prevail over an institutional electoral justification. After this further source review, no sufficiently concrete changed premise supports changing her present enforcement vote. Her provisional outcome departure is therefore not adopted.

**Stevens and Ginsburg's supported rationale departure:** Their Day financing dissent expressly rejects equalization as a sufficient ground for directly suppressing the independent speaker. This materially differs from the third ground of their historical Colorado dissent. They therefore rely on the specific institutional corruption and circumvention grounds, not campaign-cost leveling, and must explain the accommodation of expression by this election-specific, inflation-adjusted allowance. Day also prevents treating every association as regulable merely because it pools money. Their result remains supported only because they find the party's candidate-electoral relationship supplies the concrete preventive mechanism, despite actual independence of this particular advertisement. This is a legal prophylaxis conclusion, not a factual finding of a bargain, earmark or candidate control.

**Other law:** McIntyre reserves organizational campaign finance; 44 Liquormart does not substitute commercial-speech tests or a general empirical-proof requirement here. Actual Turner permits genuine predictive judgment subject to independent review at its controlling level; it supplies no universal five-vote record-insufficiency method. Actual IBM preserves discretion to consider an included argument and does not create a mandatory scope bar. Doe's prudential disposition supplies no mandatory dismissal rule. None changes the factual independence premise.

## Reconciled commitments and join barriers

**Stevens.** Reach the merits without a magic-words-only disposition. Sustain the present party-expenditure allowance as applied to this independent ad because the continuing party–candidate financing relationship and evasion risk justify preventive regulation at this allowance's stated scope. Affirm enforcement with the penalty amount left to the lower court. He accepts actual transaction independence but does not regard it as sufficient constitutional immunity for a formal party. He will not join equalization as a sufficient direct-suppression ground, an Austin commercial-wealth rationale detached from the present institution, an actual-coordination finding, or a separate (a)(2) verdict. His own Austin/MCFL positions and Day's preserved regulatory interests support the result; Day supplies the express limits.

**O'Connor.** Protect the independent ad under Buckley's expenditure rule and vacate the adverse enforcement/penalty mandate. A legal party-status presumption cannot replace the missing statutory connection or establish adequate constitutional fit. Preserve direct transfers, genuinely coordinated candidate resources, earmarking, republication and other rules at their own scopes; reserve their constitutional applications. Remand for proper remaining counterclaim treatment without a compulsory factual retry. Her MCFL concurrence, Austin dissent join and Day commitments support this position. She will not join general invalidation of coordinated party limits or a rule demanding a proved bribe in every preventive-regulation case.

**Scalia.** Reject enforcement against the independent advertisement and support broader relief for the party's own Senate-campaign speech under §441a(d)(3), including communication produced in cooperation with its candidate. His Austin writings and Day's protection of political association remain consistent with distinguishing such expression from undifferentiated candidate transfers. His own Morse writing reinforces the need to confront chilling legal boundaries. He may agree with the narrower conclusion that this independent ad is protected, but refuses a rationale or remand premise that classifies all actually coordinated party speech as validly limited contributions. He does not adopt Thomas's historical general rejection of the contribution/expenditure distinction. Any remand he supports implements the broader party-speech conclusion and resolves remaining statutory/remedial consequences; it does not reserve that constitutional question afresh.

**Kennedy.** Reject the independent-ad application; additionally decide that a party's own Senate-campaign expression does not lose its speech character merely through cooperation with the candidate it exists to support. Reach that part of the same preserved party-spending claim as the supplied open scope question permits, without importing a historical grant. His Austin dissent, actual Day plurality and preserved contribution rule, and actual Morse/IBM positions support the distinction and reach. Undifferentiated party contributions may remain regulable; candidate cash, earmarks, sham conduits and presidential finance are not immunized. He can support the independent-ad protection but will not join the complete reserve-and-reconsider position on party-authored coordinated speech or any assumption that association with a candidate is itself corruption.

**Souter.** Retain the frozen as-applied rule, adverse-mandate vacatur and separate counterclaim remand. The lack of candidate direction in this transaction defeats categorical contribution classification; broader coordinated-party activity can present different mechanisms. Day's functional rules and his uncapped-speaker financing dissent remain material, not substitutes for the current facts. He will not join party-wide immunity, abolition of preventive regulation, an automatic preservation/default inference, or a purported unified Day financing test.

**Thomas.** Retain protection for the independent advertisement but reach the bounded §441a(d)(3) party-expression claim under the surviving Buckley framework. The party's pursuit of its own platform through its candidates is not itself corrupt dependence; ordinary party expression cannot be limited merely because the party and candidate work together. That conclusion is separate from extending immunity to donors using earmarked channels or to direct cash transfers. His Day join affirmatively preserves the contribution/expenditure distinction and realistic conduit regulation, so he does not propose the historical Part II's general merger or wholesale reopening of campaign-finance law. His Day expressive-burden position and actual Morse association/avoidance position support addressing the party-specific restriction. He refuses a remand that treats that legal question as inherently unanswerable from the existing facial claim, while leaving genuinely distinct statutory consequences for proper treatment.

**Ginsburg.** Sustain this enforcement application on the narrower institutional-corruption and anti-circumvention grounds, with lower-court penalty assessment and no fixed amount. The party's continuing candidate-selection/support relationship is the relevant preventive mechanism, not a fabricated finding that it actually consulted on this ad. Her Day joins demand fit and preserve real conduit regulation; they do not decide the expressly excluded party category. Her Day financing dissent bars reliance on leveling alone, and her 44 Liquormart positions require attention to the actual burden rather than deference by slogan. She will not join wholesale categorical equivalence of every association to a candidate, a separate (a)(2) violation without its predicate, or automatic invalidity based solely on this advertisement's independence. **Breyer.** Retain the as-applied independent-spending rule and separate counterclaim remand. ## Distinct contribution-channel guardrails

Section441a(d)'s invalidation does not automatically eliminate §441a(a)(2). For O'Connor, Souter and Breyer, the government's alternative fails in this transaction because a party-status presumption cannot supply the §441a(a)(7)(B)(i) cooperation/consultation/concert/request/suggestion predicate. Republication and earmarking remain separate. For Kennedy, Scalia and Thomas, even an established coordination link does not alone strip **party-authored campaign expression** of its constitutional character; this is their broader legal objection, not a declaration that (a)(2) is facially void. Undifferentiated transfers and genuine donor conduits remain distinct under this handoff.

Stevens and Ginsburg's sustain positions concern §441a(d)'s own application. They supply no independent (a)(2) liability finding. The Party's objection that the latter theory was new is not converted into a forfeiture judgment, and the government's assertion of noncontest is not treated as a factual concession. Remedial drafting must identify whether relief rests on the actual independence predicate or the broader constitutional protection of the party's expression; it cannot erase that difference with a general statement that all contribution restrictions fall.
