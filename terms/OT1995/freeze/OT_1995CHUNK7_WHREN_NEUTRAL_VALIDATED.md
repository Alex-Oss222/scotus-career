## Facts, findings, and procedural correction

The district judge acknowledged discrepancies but accepted Officer Soto's testimony. The credited account described a vehicle waiting unusually long at a stop sign, turning without signaling, and traveling at unreasonable speed. Officers approached the vehicle after it was already stopped at another intersection, ordered it to park, and then observed drugs in plain view. The traffic justification rests on the credited account, not an antecedent finding of drug probable cause. JA4–5, 8–10, 17–18 are the safe handoff's identified passages.

The D.C. Circuit sustained probable cause based on failure to give full attention, failure to signal, and unreasonable speed under 18 D.C.M.R. §§2213.4, 2204.3, and 2200.3. That does not settle every constitutional question about how the restraint was imposed. Petitioners challenge the initial stop rather than the later search; manner, scope, or unusual intrusiveness must be assessed only insofar as properly part of that initial-restraint argument.

The source copy's unqualified “affirmed the convictions” is superseded by this qualification: the court rejected the Fourth Amendment claim but remanded by agreement for correction of the lesser-included §841 convictions and resentencing relative to §860. ## Competing arguments and their actual limits

**Petitioners' position is an objective would-have test.** They ask whether reasonable officers would have made this stop for the traffic purpose absent the additional investigative aim. They do not simply contend that any mixed drug-investigation motive invalidates an otherwise lawful seizure. Departmental practice, enforcement norms, asserted danger, and arbitrariness are offered as objective evidence. Their strongest point is that pervasive minor traffic offenses permit virtually unconstrained selection if any technical violation invariably concludes the reasonableness inquiry.

General Order 303.1 ordinarily confines traffic enforcement to uniformed officers in marked vehicles or the designated unit, but expressly excepts violations **so grave as to pose an immediate threat to others' safety**. The identified text also includes written-warning requirements and a limited extreme-circumstances oral-warning exception. No unqualified prohibition on plainclothes traffic enforcement is established. Whether the evidence meets an exception is a separate application question if the rule is legally material.

**The government's position is objective probable-cause authority for an ordinary stop.** It argues that another investigative aim does not extinguish authority supplied by known traffic violations; departmental priorities vary and generally allocate duties rather than remove statutory police powers. It distinguishes inventory and administrative searches, where standardized limits justify action without individualized suspicion, from this probable-cause seizure. It preserves limits on subsequent scope and duration. Petitioners dispute the proposed confinement of anti-pretext and arbitrariness principles; the fork cannot be reduced to objective facts versus subjective motives.

Selective enforcement on a forbidden racial basis is a distinct constitutional concern. The briefs raise it, but Soto denied racial motivation and the district judge credited him. This validation makes no new finding of racial selection. The government's suggested equal-protection route proves neither discrimination nor entitlement to suppression or another remedy. Actual Armstrong applies within its federal charging/discovery field and may not be silently generalized to every police-stop claim.

The conflict description also requires correction: petitioners' merits brief 10–11 reports that *Botero-Ospina*, 71 F.3d783 (1995) (en banc), moved the Tenth Circuit to the could-have approach; identified Ninth/Eleventh decisions remained the contrary line. The source copy's “no material conflict identified” is underinclusive. The historical petition's earlier conflict account cannot displace the date-eligible update.

## May 28 actual Ornelas delta — appellate allocation

The exact `entering-law/PUBLIC_Ornelas_v_United_States_merits_1996-05-28.md` at **155b6fd** supplies the new controlling rule. Appellate courts independently determine ultimate reasonable suspicion and probable cause in the stated nonwarrant setting. Historical facts, credibility and genuinely factual subsidiary findings or inferences receive clear-error review. Grounded contextual inferences from resident judges and experienced local officers receive **genuine due weight**, including supported trial assessments of credibility and inferential reasonableness, without controlling the ultimate constitutional judgment. Due weight is substantive consideration of context, not a numerical weight, an officer-credibility presumption or an intermediate standard insulating the ultimate conclusion. The Court rejects deference to the ultimate constitutional application; clear error remains the factual-review term. Issuing-magistrate warrant deference, habeas presumptions, clear-and-convincing burdens and civil Rule 52 are not transplanted to this direct criminal review.

**Application to this packet:** Soto's credited observations and the district judge's credibility resolution remain historical findings protected by the proper factual standard. The present handoff already treats the observed traffic violations as supplying probable cause; Ornelas does not reopen a preserved or uncontested predicate merely to generate a dispute. To the extent ultimate constitutional sufficiency is at issue, independent appellate judgment cannot be replaced by asking only whether the lower court clearly erred.
