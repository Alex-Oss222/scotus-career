## Rules, statutory qualifiers and application

Part I-A construes former Rule 11(f)'s subject without turning every property interest into a CCE guilt element or dispensing with statutory authority. Rules 7(c)(2) and 31(e) retain distinct forfeiture functions. No later Rule 32.2 procedure is imported. Part II-A's conclusion concerns this §§848/853 property-extent determination, not every sentence-affecting fact; Gaudin's element protection and Austin/Alexander's limited punitive-forfeiture holdings remain. McMillan, Cabana and Spaziano are used only for the stated date-eligible distinction.

Part I-B correctly separates §853(a)(1) proceeds, (a)(2) facilitation/use and (a)(3) CCE interests/control. Lawful acquisition alone does not defeat every statutory route. All five substitute-property conditions are retained, including defendant act/omission, due diligence, third-party transfer/sale/deposit, extrajurisdictional placement, substantial diminution and commingling that cannot be divided without difficulty. The final text now correctly commands that the court **must order** forfeiture of **other property of the defendant**, up to the qualifying unavailable property's value, when the predicates are established. It does not declare those predicates established for every item here.

The rebuttable §853(d) presumption expressly occurs **at trial**, requires government proof by a preponderance, and retains both the acquisition-period and no-likely-other-source conditions. The all-assets promise proves neither condition by itself. Actual admissions may establish facts; consent cannot create power Congress withheld or dispose of another person's protected interest. Those distinctions reflect all eight Associates' common statutory ground, rather than adding a new independent-duty vote.

Part II-B rests on represented affirmative agreement, signed waiver and courtroom assent, with the reported prior notices reinforcing the context. No universal special-advice ritual follows, but affirmative misleading advice and evident confusion cannot be ignored; actual supported challenges to understanding, coercion, voluntariness and agreement scope remain. The difference between the broader five-Justice formulation and Souter/Ginsburg's actual-understanding grounds is preserved.
