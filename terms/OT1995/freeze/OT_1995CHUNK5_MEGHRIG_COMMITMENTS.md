## Reachability, record and common disposition

Each of the eight Associates reaches both submitted statutory questions on review of the Ninth Circuit's reversal of the Rule 12(b)(6) dismissal. Section 1254(1) supplies the review channel. The supplied roster establishes their participation at argument and decision; no case-specific nonparticipation is established.

The operative allegations are accepted favorably to KFC. They describe petroleum-contaminated soil, defendants' contribution, government-directed cleanup completed in 1989, and a May 1992 suit seeking at least $211,296.23. The amended complaint says the contamination **may have presented** a substantial endangerment. It alleges none remaining when suit commenced. Continuing off-site disposal expense does not itself allege continuing endangerment. The decision neither finds defendants responsible nor treats the later California summary judgment as federal pleading findings.

For each Associate, the judgment commitment is **reverse the Ninth Circuit and remand for disposition consistent with rejection of this pleaded RCRA claim**. ## The two grounds and their operative limits

**T — Endangerment at commencement.** Section 6972(a)(1)(B) requires waste that may present an imminent and substantial endangerment when the citizen action begins. Past generation, ownership, handling or contribution can produce a qualifying present threat; the repeated past-contributor language therefore has work to do without changing “may present” into “may have presented.” Imminence concerns a presently threatened harm, not a requirement that physical injury already exist or occur immediately. Complete prefiling elimination of the threat defeats this complaint. This is independently sufficient for reversal.

**R — No freestanding reimbursement action after completed prefiling abatement.** The authority to restrain a contributor and order that person to take other necessary action supplies prohibitory and affirmative abatement relief. It does not authorize this standalone action seeking only reimbursement after the entire abatement was completed before filing. The requested payment is not an order to perform remaining abatement. Calling it equitable restitution does not itself make it an authorized RCRA remedy. This construction of the remedial clause supplies an **additional, independently sufficient ground**: even if KFC's historical-danger reading satisfied the class of covered waste, that reading would not turn this completed-cost demand into the necessary-action relief Congress specified.

R is deliberately stated at the level of this submitted demand. It is **not** a holding that every payment order is legal damages, that all restitution lies outside equity, or that a proper RCRA action can never support a monetary equitable incident. Neither the traditional equitable-remedy presumption nor the contrary statutory-structure inference requires deciding those broader propositions here. KFC's concession that the statute does not authorize damages at law is preserved; its claimed equitable character receives a statutory answer without an unsupported factual finding that every requested dollar is compensation rather than restitution.

The grounds remain distinct. T concerns the condition for commencing the action; R concerns the particular relief Congress made available. CERCLA's express private cost-recovery provisions, necessity/NCP requirements and separate allocation arrangements reinforce R's statutory reading; they are not incorporated as new RCRA elements. The citizen notice and government-action provisions reinforce T's abatement setting, but no particular prosecution bar is found satisfied here. The absence of an express RCRA limitations period supports the statutory comparison; it is not converted into a new limitations rule or a ruling that laches is unavailable.

## Individual commitments and strongest competing positions

Each entry independently adopts the common judgment, T and R at precisely the scope stated above. The cited prior public opinions and joins are Justice-specific evidence; none purported to decide Meghrig in advance.

### Stevens

**Commitment:** Reverse on T and, independently, R. His opinion in **Key Tronic, June 6, 1994**, separates an existing environmental private action from authority for its requested expense, while distinguishing actual response work from litigation or liability-allocation work. That method supports attention to the function and statutory source of this reimbursement demand. Here the RCRA relief language directs action against an endangerment; it supplies no freestanding completed-cost allocation remedy.

**Strongest alternative and boundary:** Stevens joined **Franklin, February 26, 1992**, and his **Peacock dissent, February 21, 1996**, supports bounded monetary restoration within a court's legitimate enforcement authority. Those positions make the government's argument for equity in a properly begun suit substantial. They do not supply the missing commencement condition or establish authority for this independent action. His Peacock position required actual restorative authority and reserved liability and amount; it did not impose another person's entire obligation because reimbursement seemed fair. He can therefore join R without abandoning either prior position, provided the opinion reserves properly instituted actions and does not announce a general monetary-equity prohibition. No separate writing is required to preserve that limit if the common reasoning states it.

### O'Connor

**Commitment:** Reverse on T and R. Her joins in **DOE v. Ohio, April 21, 1992, Part II-A**, and **Key Tronic, Part I**, recognize that permission to bring an environmental action and authority for a particular financial remedy are separate statutory questions. Sovereign-immunity and fee rules from those decisions do not control this private reimbursement question, but their provision-specific treatment supports enforcing RCRA's present-endangerment and abatement language.

**Strongest alternative and boundary:** Her **Franklin** join and her join in **Breyer's Hercules dissent, March 4, 1996**, reject withholding otherwise authorized relief merely because it includes reimbursement or requires factual assessment. Hercules nevertheless required a genuine authorized undertaking and rejected fairness alone. The proposed Meghrig remand lacks an antecedent qualifying endangerment and an enacted standalone completed-cost remedy. She rejects a timing-only disposition as insufficient to answer the separately submitted remedial construction at this bounded level; she does not extend that answer to the availability of equitable costs after a proper commencement.

### Scalia

**Commitment:** Reverse on T and R. His **Mertens opinion, June 1, 1993**, requires the actual statutory remedial category rather than the entire historical power of an equity court. His **DOE v. Ohio, Part II-A**, join likewise respects distinct enacted liability and remedial provisions. RCRA's past-contributor terms cannot displace the present-tense threat condition, and “other action” must be read with the restraint/abatement function rather than as unlimited reimbursement authority.

**Strongest alternative and boundary:** His opposition to Key Tronic's litigation-fee exclusion demonstrates that express statutory language can support a monetary remedy; it supplies no premise that financial relief is invariably disfavored. Here there is no counterpart to CERCLA's response-cost authorization. Mertens itself preserved other monetary equity and the reserved antecedent-action issue. He adopts R because of this statute and this demand, not because the restitution label is always invalid. A categorical ruling against every equitable monetary incident would exceed his commitment.

### Kennedy

**Commitment:** Reverse on T and R. His joins in **Franklin**, **Mertens**, and **Key Tronic, Parts I–III**, support a coherent distinction: appropriate remedies follow an existing action, but statutory remedial categories and conditions retain independent force. KFC's completed prefiling abatement fails the condition for this action; the enumerated abatement powers separately do not create the requested completed-cost action.

**Strongest alternative and boundary:** Franklin and Porter support KFC's equitable-remedy argument after a proper invocation of statutory authority. They do not establish that a payment for a wholly completed cleanup falls within this statute's necessary-action clause. His **Lujan** joins do not require rejecting KFC's concrete financial injury or demand proof on a summary-judgment standard. He would withhold a join from a constitutional standing disposition or from an assertion that no equitable money can ever be awarded under RCRA. Neither is needed for the two adopted grounds.

### Souter

**Commitment:** Reverse on T and R. His **DOE v. Ohio opinion, Part II-A**, distinguishes access to a citizen suit from the particular financial liability incorporated by the enactment. His **Hercules opinion, March 4, 1996**, likewise requires an actual legal source of reimbursement without banning all payment undertakings. Here the present-tense trigger and necessary-action remedy, read in their notice and governmental-enforcement setting, provide the concrete statutory limits.

**Strongest alternative and boundary:** His **Franklin** and **Mertens** joins leave room for traditional equity where the cause and statutory remedy permit it. The Ninth Circuit's analogy to EPA enforcement cannot establish that condition: the cited governmental decisions did not conclusively decide this private cause, and §6973 has separate powers and qualifications. He joins both grounds without deciding §6973 reimbursement, imposing a sovereign-immunity clear-statement rule on private defendants, or treating every cost payment as outside equity. The government amicus's reservation for properly commenced suits remains compatible with this commitment.

### Thomas

**Commitment:** Reverse on T and R. His **Mertens** join supports the enacted remedial category rather than an unbounded appeal to equity; his **DOE v. Ohio, Part II-A**, join preserves separately enacted liability limits. His narrower **Franklin** judgment position also counsels identifying the statute's own basis for a financial remedy. The words about past contributors identify potentially responsible persons, while “may present” identifies the qualifying danger; neither supplies private allocation of all historical cleanup costs.

**Strongest alternative and boundary:** Like Scalia, he opposed Key Tronic's fee exclusion, so that decision cannot be used to attribute a categorical resistance to money awards. Its unanimously supported task-specific portions instead show that an actual statutory cost category matters. This demand has no corresponding RCRA completed-cost authorization. T and R suffice without importing Lujan's evidentiary posture, denying all restitution, or deciding costs incurred during a qualifying action. He requires no separate writing within those limits.

### Ginsburg

**Commitment:** Reverse on T and R. Her **Key Tronic joins, Parts I–III**, support separating an authorized environmental cause, the actual function of claimed expenditures, and the provision authorizing their recovery. Her **Hercules joins, Parts I–IV**, preserve genuine reimbursement authority while rejecting an obligation unsupported by its governing legal source. These positions support a statutory answer to KFC's completed-cost demand rather than a factual inquiry into fairness that could not cure the absent cause and remedy.

**Strongest alternative and boundary:** KFC's claim that it discharged defendants' public duty could matter to equitable entitlement in a legally available action. It cannot itself prove contribution, liability or unjust enrichment on this complaint, and it does not establish that RCRA authorizes the standalone claim. She accepts the government's distinction between partial emergency cleanup with danger remaining and complete prefiling abatement. Her commitment reserves the former remedial problem and does not use the later state judgment to deny the latter claim on disputed factual or preclusion grounds.

### Breyer

**Commitment:** Reverse on T and R. His **Hercules dissent, March 4, 1996**, supports examining actual reimbursement authority and foreseeable expenses rather than imposing a universal express-indemnity demand, but expressly rejects a fairness-based obligation without an authorized undertaking. His support for the **Peacock judgment, February 21, 1996**, similarly respects the boundary between lawful enforcement and a new independent action. RCRA's immediate remedial object supplies a sufficient legal distinction between an order addressing threatened harm and this later cost-allocation suit.

**Strongest alternative and boundary:** Prompt cleanup should not perversely defeat useful enforcement, and a mandatory waiting period makes the government's partial-abatement example significant. But that example involves a still-existing threat and therefore does not describe KFC's allegations. The statutory waiting exception for qualifying subchapter-III violations is also narrower than a general emergency waiver. Neither policy concern authorizes treating fully removed danger as present. He joins the additional R ground because it addresses only the standalone completed-abatement demand, while leaving courts' remedial powers in a properly begun suit open. He would not join a rule categorically excluding emergency expenses incurred before filing when a qualifying threat remains, or expenses incurred while a proper action is pending.

## Adversarial path test and disposition boundaries

**Affirm and remand for equitable restitution.** The strongest supporting case combines the express cause, past-contributor language, Porter/Hecht/Franklin remedial principles, §6973's similar language, and the benefit of prompt cleanup. A lawful affirmance on that theory would only permit further proceedings, not award the pleaded sum. The decisive objections are separately textual: historical contribution does not eliminate the present-threat condition, and equitable authority does not make every completed-cost demand necessary abatement relief. The governmental cases' assumed or distinct remedial premises do not resolve either private statutory question. All eight reject this path on the pleaded facts.

**Reverse solely on timing and reserve the entire remedy question.** The United States supplies the strongest version. T fully supports reversal, and the traditional-equity question is substantial in a properly commenced action. Each Associate nevertheless accepts R as a second ground at the complaint-specific scope stated above because both questions are submitted, both district grounds are before the Court, and the separate remedial text answers the actual demand without deciding the government's hypothetical. This is not an inference that judicial economy obliges resolution of every issue. Reservation of the broader remedy question is an express part of each commitment.

**Reverse on a categorical exclusion of monetary restitution.** Petitioners' structural argument and the comparison with CERCLA support denying this claim, but do not require that broader rule. Mertens does not equate all money with legal damages; Franklin does not furnish unrestricted relief; and the government's existing-action example has materially different statutory predicates. No Associate commitment here supplies that categorical holding. R is not a disguised decision about relief after a proper commencement.

**Reverse for unproved liability, notice, preclusion or mootness.** The state order, original pleading defect and voluntary-dismissal request do not supply preserved and established predicates for those alternative dispositions. All eight reject substituting them for the two pleaded statutory questions. No new factfinding follows from selecting the statutory grounds.

The following limits govern every individual commitment:

- Past conduct can cause present endangerment. A present threat can concern delayed harm and need not involve present physical injury. Partial cleanup does not defeat coverage if a qualifying threat remains.
- The decision leaves open equitable cost relief in a properly instituted action, including the government's example of emergency prefiling expenses with longer-term danger still present, and costs incurred while such an action is pending. It also leaves open EPA reimbursement under §6973. No Restatement restitution condition is adopted as a new RCRA element.
- RCRA's ninety-day notice rule, immediate-after-notification exception for a qualifying subchapter-III violation, and specifically conditioned federal/state enforcement bars remain distinct. No universal local-government-cleanup bar, excused notification or modern timing override is adopted.
- The §6972(e) discretionary fee provision is distinct from cleanup reimbursement. No fee entitlement or amount is decided. The saving clause preserves otherwise existing rights; it creates no alternative cause or guaranteed state recovery.
- No CERCLA petroleum rule is imported into RCRA, and no classification of every petroleum mixture is decided. No NCP burden or limitations period is invented for RCRA.
- No special mandate date, immediate payment, guaranteed discovery, or determination of actual contribution, responsibility, unjust enrichment or reasonable cost follows.
