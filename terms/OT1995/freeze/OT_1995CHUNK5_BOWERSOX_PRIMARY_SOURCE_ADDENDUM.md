## Barefoot: three distinct procedural settings

### Initial habeas appeal and certificate of probable cause

The Court's discussion at 888–895 concerns meaningful appellate consideration after issuance of a certificate of probable cause. At 893 it adopts the requirement of a **substantial showing of the denial of a federal right**, a showing greater than absence of frivolity and higher than the good-faith requirement for proceeding in forma pauperis. Footnote 4 explains that the petitioner need not show that he should prevail: the issues must be debatable among reasonable jurists, capable of resolution in a different manner, or adequate to deserve encouragement to proceed further. The nature of a capital penalty may be considered, but its severity alone does not warrant automatic certification.

Once a certificate issues, the petitioner must receive an opportunity to address the merits and the court of appeals must decide the merits of the appeal. The particular stay formulation at 893–894 expressly refers to the **initial habeas appeal**: where necessary to prevent execution from mooting the case, the court should stay execution pending disposition. The Court permits suitable expedition and, with adequate notice and opportunity to address the merits, simultaneous consideration of the appeal and stay. If time precludes a considered merits decision in that setting, the stay must be granted. A certificate ordinarily indicates a nonfrivolous appeal; before dismissing as frivolous, the appellate court should be confident that the claim is squarely foreclosed by statute, rule, or authoritative decision, or lacks any factual basis in the record. These conditions are not an invitation to substitute an unexplained likelihood-of-success assessment for the appellate disposition the Court requires.

The Court found the actual procedure in *Barefoot* sufficient because the merits had been addressed and decided with an adequate opportunity to present them; it did not treat formal failure to affirm as controlling. Its approval was qualified: this was not necessarily the preferred procedure. At 891 the Court also recounts the earlier solicitation of further party submissions in *Garrison* to determine whether the merits had been addressed. That is support for resolving an actual procedural uncertainty through information, not a present adjudication about Bowersox or a general entitlement to an indefinite pause.

### Second or successive federal habeas petition

At 895 the Court expressly states that second and successive petitions “present a different issue.” It identifies Rule 9(b)'s abuse inquiry and permits expedited consideration even where Rule 9(b) dismissal cannot be established. It then states:

> The granting of a stay should reflect the presence of substantial grounds upon which relief might be granted.

The certificate, the ability to pursue an appeal, and whether execution should be stayed are related but distinct questions. The initial-appeal stay formulation cannot be applied as though its express setting were immaterial. Conversely, classifying a petition as successive does not itself determine whether a particular claim is available, abusive, defaulted, exhausted, supported by an applicable gateway, or substantial. *Barefoot*'s abbreviated quotation of Rule 9(b) is not a replacement for the complete rule, including the prior-merits-determination condition for repeated grounds. This addendum neither treats a certificate as conclusive support for a successive-case stay nor treats its issuance as meaningless.

### Stay pending filing and consideration of certiorari

At 895–896, the Court separately addresses a stay while seeking this Court's certiorari review after the court of appeals has denied habeas relief. Such a stay is not automatic. The quoted requirements are a reasonable probability that four Members would find the underlying issue sufficiently meritorious for certiorari or probable jurisdiction, a significant possibility of reversal, and a likelihood of irreparable harm without a stay. The application must contain the information and materials needed for a careful assessment; a stay should first be sought in the court of appeals, whose decision ordinarily receives considerable weight **in these circumstances**.

Those certiorari-stage requirements are not a Rule-of-Four decision rule for the present referred application to vacate an existing appellate stay. Nor does the considerable-weight sentence, without its procedural setting, supply a newly controlling “flagrant abuse” prerequisite for every vacatur application.

## Delo: Court rule, separate authority discussion, and deference arguments

The *Delo* Court states at 321:

> A stay of execution pending disposition of a second or successive federal habeas petition should be granted only when there are “substantial grounds upon which relief might be granted.”

It applies that requirement to the actual fourth petition and holds at 322 that the District Court abused its discretion. Its reason is substantive: the claim could have been raised in the first petition, the asserted principles were not novel, and the petitioner's own reliance on earlier state dissenting opinions showed their availability. That is an application of Rule 9(b) and §2244(b), not a numerical rule that a third or fourth petition necessarily abuses the writ. The Court does not discuss a certificate of probable cause and announces no general stay duration. Its District Court stay, appellate refusal to vacate, and particular claim history remain facts of that source alone.

Kennedy's concurrence at 322–323, joined by the Chief Justice and Scalia, joins the Court's opinion and adds a discussion of prompt appellate procedures. His proposed All Writs avenue concerns appellate failure to act sufficiently promptly to preserve jurisdiction and protect parties against the consequences of an inadequately supported stay. He cites §1651 and *Maxwell v. Bishop*, 385 U.S. 650 (1967). This is a separate three-Justice explanation, not additional statutory reasoning adopted in the *Delo* per curiam. *Maxwell* was not independently read in this addendum, and no broader proposition from it is asserted.

Brennan's dissent at 323–327 is joined by Marshall throughout and Blackmun only in Parts I–III. It disputes the account of delay and the summary abuse determination, stresses the discretionary word “may” in Rule 9(b), and argues that the lower courts have better knowledge of the case. Its starred footnote, spanning 323–324, places the amendment before the setting of the then-current execution date; that dissenting chronology cannot be silently merged with the Court's account. Part IV adds Brennan's categorical capital-punishment position, without Blackmun's join. Stevens, joined by Blackmun, separately relies at 327 on the lower courts' superior position to assess abuse, particularly appellate consideration en banc.

The phrase **“flagrantly abused its discretion”** at 324 is in Brennan's dissent, quoting *Wainwright v. Adams*, 466 U.S. 964, 965 (1984). The complete retained *Adams* text identifies its source as **Marshall's dissent, joined by Brennan**. The *Adams* Court order grants the application without adopting that explanation. The dissent invokes exceptional-circumstances reasoning, individual-Justice writings, and a practice treatise; this addendum does not silently promote them into a Court holding. This source chain supplies a date-eligible argument for heightened deference, with its attribution preserved. It does not change the *Delo* Court's actual abuse-of-discretion formulation. Brennan's additional use of *Barefoot*'s considerable-weight sentence must likewise preserve the original certiorari-stage context.
