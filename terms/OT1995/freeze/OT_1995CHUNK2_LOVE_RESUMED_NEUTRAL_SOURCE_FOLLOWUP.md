## Lewis: material opposing primary authority and its precise limits

Lewis v. United States, 445 U.S. 55, 65–66 & n.8 (February 27, 1980), sustained the federal firearms disability arising from an extant felony conviction even when that conviction was allegedly uncounseled. The conviction had to be vacated, or the disability otherwise lawfully removed, before acquiring the firearm. Its rational-basis discussion addressed that enacted disability and Fifth Amendment equal protection.

Footnote 8 describes the legislative restrictions there as neither using suspect criteria nor burdening constitutionally protected liberties. It invokes Miller's militia-relationship reasoning and three lower-court decisions upholding §§1202(a)(1), 922(g) and 922(a)(6). This is meaningful support for regulation and militia-related analysis, not a holding about state incorporation, a qualified nonfelon's purchase, or an arrest-only policy unauthorized by the applicable statute. Love has no pleaded disqualifying felony conviction.

Blackmun authored the Court's opinion; Burger, Stewart, White, Rehnquist and Stevens joined. Brennan dissented, joined by Marshall and Powell. Stevens's actual join is relevant personal jurisprudential evidence, with those limits.

## Miller and Presser: what the actual historical accounts establish

Miller, 307 U.S. 174, 178–182 (1939), refuses to recognize protection without evidence or judicial notice of the short shotgun's relationship to militia preservation or efficiency, and expressly makes that purpose relevant to interpretation. Its historical account contrasts civilian militia with standing armies; describes a broadly drawn population of physically capable men, ordinary privately supplied arms in common use, and colonial arms obligations; and recounts Massachusetts, New York and Virginia enrollment/equipment provisions, including exemptions and public provision for indigent members.

An argument against a formal-membership prerequisite can point to its focus on the weapon and common defense, rather than an express demand that these defendants establish membership before invoking the Amendment. That is an inference from the opinion's treatment, not an express holding of a general self-defense right independent of militia purposes. Its remand is not a final conviction or exhaustive ruling on every firearm. [Full opinion and footnotes](https://www.law.cornell.edu/supremecourt/text/307/174).

Presser, 116 U.S. 252, 264–267 (1886), sustains control of unauthorized armed military association/parading and expressly states the Amendment's national-only limitation. Its reserve-force passage recognizes capable citizens as a resource for federal and state military needs and disallows state action disabling the federal resource through disarmament, even without relying on the Amendment. That qualification is not itself incorporation or an unrestricted personal self-defense holding. Neither opinion expressly resolves Love's precise formal-enrollment question. [Primary opinion](https://www.law.cornell.edu/supremecourt/text/116/252).

## Duncan and Malloy: incorporation method, not an already decided arms holding

Duncan, 391 U.S. 145, 148–150, 156 (1968), uses the right's fundamental character in the American scheme of justice and a historical/institutional inquiry to apply serious-offense criminal jury trial against the States. That method can support an inquiry into another guarantee but does not automatically incorporate every enumerated provision. [Primary opinion](https://www.law.cornell.edu/supremecourt/text/391/145).

Malloy, 378 U.S. 1, 6–11 (1964), recognizes the self-incrimination privilege against the States with the same governing standards. It undermines a categorical premise that enumerated rights can restrain only the Federal Government; it does not itself overrule the arms-specific state limitation. [Primary opinion](https://www.law.cornell.edu/supremecourt/text/378/1).

Thus the strongest supplied primary support for retaining the inherited state rule is Presser's express holding; the support for reassessment is the later rights-specific incorporation method and the earlier opinions' actual accounts of the arms interest. The packet supplies no primary Reconstruction evidence establishing the Fourteenth Amendment framers' arms-specific intent. The petition's scholarship may identify an argument, but cannot establish that historical fact. Its quotation of historical Casey is party advocacy and cannot substitute for the received Casey decision or establish a new arms holding.
