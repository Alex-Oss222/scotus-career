## What the full comparator establishes

**Majority.** At 421–422 the opinion accurately identifies the Federal Circuit's selected ground: a successful government-contractor defense would have prevented the tort liability, making the settlement voluntary for purposes of contract causation. It expressly affirms on different grounds. That is consistent with the validated neutral correction, which controls over the runtime's incomplete account of the judgment below.

At 422–425 the majority distinguishes express or implied-in-fact terms from obligations implied in law and asks what undertaking the contracting circumstances support. It reads Spearin's ordinary specifications implication as assurance of satisfactory performance; the act of furnishing specifications alone does not support the additional promise to bear these third-party settlement and defense costs. The military-tort-immunity discussion at 425 and note 6 provides added support; it is not a decision adopting the Federal Circuit's Boyle-causation ground. It cannot turn Hatzlachh's surviving contract/tort distinction into a rule that tort immunity itself invalidates a real contract.

At 426–430 the majority separately rejects Thompson's implied indemnification argument from compelled production, specifications, knowledge and control. Notes 4 and 7 address the contested reach of Hercules's claim and any supposedly distinct general claim. Notes 8–12 explain that the majority takes the asserted factual allegations favorably but regards them as legally insufficient to imply the claimed promise; it invokes authority and appropriations restrictions as evidence against readily inferring open-ended indemnification, rather than finding a particular appropriation after a trial. Note 10 expressly disclaims a rule invalidating every indemnity agreement. Note 12 describes the special statutory mechanisms as a caution against inference, not proof about what this particular officer actually did. Section 707 supplies immunity, not a reimbursement undertaking; note 14 expressly leaves the immunity's full scope undecided. Ryan is distinguished by the contractual undertaking and the supplier/customer relationship. The final equity discussion does not convert fairness into Tucker Act consent.

**Dissent.** Breyer and O'Connor require proof of an actual promise, breach and foreseeable consequential harm, 431. Their position is not a present indemnity award. They reject hindsight treatment of the 1984 settlement based on a defense definitively articulated by this Court in 1988, 433–436, and object to resolving the fact-dependent contractual inference as categorically impossible, 436–441. They regard specifications, alleged knowledge and control, compulsion, and the surrounding law together as potentially probative of an actual bargain. This is the strongest version of the whole-circumstances argument already supplied by the validated neutral packet and petitioners' reply; it is not merely a claim that §707 orders payment.

The dissent answers five propositions: later special-indemnity procedures do not establish what a 1960s officer could have done; the Anti-Deficiency Act does not by itself invalidate every possible indemnity; Spearin's consequences language does not itself establish an absolute postperformance/third-party boundary; §707 may inform contracting understandings even though it is not an affirmative indemnity; and Stencel's tort rule does not defeat genuine implied-in-fact contracts, as Hatzlachh confirms. The dissent's proposed capped-insurance possibility, its particular readings of historical regulations and accounting decisions, and its account of likely good-faith bargaining remain historical arguments. They are not adopted as new contractual facts, proven authorization, an actual cap, or an additional remedy in this reconciliation.

The majority's separate fee result and the dissent's remand must not be reduced to a settlement-only dispute. The Federal Circuit relied separately on Kania for expenses. The historical majority's absence-of-undertaking rationale covers both kinds of loss; the remand position must separately explain why the expense claim remains open. The dissent does not hold that any particular fee is recoverable.

### A — sufficient coverage ground for affirmance

Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas and Ginsburg conclude that supplying specifications for the procurement undertaking supports the ordinary assurance of satisfactory performance, but does not, without more, support the asserted promise to absorb these third-party tort settlements and defense costs. Spearin's consequences language does not establish that additional undertaking merely because a product was made to government specifications. This is the Government's properly available alternative legal ground, not a description of the Federal Circuit's selected reasoning.

Ryan and Weyerhaeuser demonstrate that a proved contractual duty can carry foreseeable third-party consequences without an express indemnity clause. Their particular service duty supplies the contractual predicate. They do not establish the promise claimed here solely from the Government's role as specification-giving purchaser. Hatzlachh prevents substituting tort immunity for contract analysis but does not itself create a term. The five therefore affirm both settlement and defense-expense dispositions without adopting an absolute express-clause requirement, a rule that all contract damages end at delivery, or a general immunity-based bar to contract recovery.

The opposing path correctly insists that third-party consequences can sometimes be recoverable and that the lodged instruments remain unavailable. The five's answer is limited: the asserted specifications implication does not provide this coverage even on the favorable allegations used to test it. Their conclusion does not depend on inventing a disclaimer in an uninspected contract or deciding disputed manufacturing and scientific facts. It leaves genuinely different, proven contractual undertakings outside this decision.

### B — bounded warranty and expense remand

O'Connor and Breyer would not hold the pleaded contract claim impossible simply because foreseeable economic loss took the form of postperformance third-party litigation. They read Spearin with Ryan, Weyerhaeuser and Hatzlachh as leaving a legally possible contract inquiry into the actual undertaking and its consequences. They require an actual authorized promise, breach, causal connection, foreseeability, reasonable settlement or expense, and proper allocation; neither conformity to a government order nor settlement approval proves those elements.

The Federal Circuit's hindsight equation between a successful warranty claim and a certainly successful government-contractor defense does not categorically eliminate the asserted 1984 settlement loss. Later defense rulings do not establish what was certain at settlement, and expenses can remain even when a defense succeeds. On remand, the actual contractual scope, material knowledge and specifications evidence, ex ante litigation risk, and relevant damages questions must be addressed to the extent necessary and procedurally available. The Government may renew its legal and factual defenses. This is not a finding that the settlement or any fee was actually reasonable, foreseeable or attributable to government breach.

Their expense position separately rejects using Kania as a universal bar. Kania involved a nonprosecution agreement, absence of demonstrated authority to bind the Treasury, and litigation expenses regarded as remote rather than directly contemplated. It does not eliminate the need to identify the procurement duty and apply the governing damages rules to third-party expenses here. Neither Justice creates a right to fees incurred prosecuting this contract action.

The historical dissent says it would reverse the appellate no-causation holding and remand, 434, 436, 441. The formal position here retains the frozen model's vacatur-in-part form: remove the judgment only as to the preserved contract components requiring further adjudication, reject the categorical causation ground, and leave other disposed claims undisturbed. This expresses the bounded remand without deciding that a warranty or indemnity actually existed; it does not award broader relief than the historical remand argument supports.

The strongest objection is that the absence of the claimed legal promise makes discovery pointless. O'Connor and Breyer answer that the pleaded specifications and contracting circumstances may bear on the real bargain and that the surviving authorities do not foreclose its scope by the categorical boundary asserted. They do not treat missing documents alone as an entitlement to trial or discovery without the ordinary procedural showing.

### D — Thompson's separate contracting-circumstances theory

**D-A, five affirming Associates.** Compulsion, control, specifications, alleged superior knowledge and the §707 shield, considered together, do not establish the asserted implied undertaking to reimburse these third-party losses. An obligation to obey a procurement command and a defense against liability do not themselves manifest assent to shift the asserted risk to the Treasury. Spearin and the service-duty cases remain distinct sources of particular contractual duties; their recognition does not establish this additional indemnity. The authority/appropriations setting reinforces caution against readily inferring an open-ended promise, but no contract-specific delegation or lack of appropriation is found here. Conditional special contracting authority is not proof that it was exercised. The conclusion rests on insufficiency of the asserted implication, not equitable disapproval of the claimants or automatic importation of Feres/Boyle into contracts.

**D-B, O'Connor and Breyer.** Thompson may attempt to prove an authorized implied-in-fact undertaking from this combined setting. Section 707's words are part of that setting even though they promise no reimbursement themselves. Specifications, alleged control and knowledge, and compelled acceptance may be assessed together as evidence of the allocation the parties actually made. This remains the preserved contextual contract theory described in neutral validation and reply pp.16–20; no additional general cause is created. Authority remains indispensable under Sutton, and neither §1431's existence nor a judicial label supplies it. The missing instruments and delegations cannot be replaced with an affirmative authorization finding. No capped-indemnity scenario or insurer-equivalent amount is adopted.

The strongest objection to D-B is that the proposed implication simply disguises an implied-in-law claim for fair treatment. The two distinguish the asserted factual bargain from a duty imposed regardless of agreement: a contractor must prove the former and loses if it establishes only the latter. The strongest objection to D-A is that rejecting every legal source separately ignores their possible combined significance. The five reject the cumulative asserted inference as well; they do not answer merely that §707 lacks the word indemnity.
