## Component I — excise-tax priority

**A — sustain denial of excise-tax priority.** The bankruptcy meaning of tax depends on the exaction's operation, rather than the Internal Revenue Code's label alone. Feiring establishes the federal characterization inquiry and examines the statutory incidents. United States v. New York applies that method to federal as well as state exactions: an ordinary wage-base tax supporting unemployment programs remains a tax despite incentives to contribute to a state system. Sotelo likewise looks beyond a penalty label when the liability represents taxes already withheld and not remitted. None makes every regulatory exaction a penalty or every IRC assessment a bankruptcy tax.

This assessment is imposed because the employer failed its independent pension-funding obligation; the amount is a percentage of that deficiency. The payment does not discharge the funding obligation or supply the benefits represented by the separate pension/PBGC liability. Its operation is a sanction for that default, not collection of a previously existing tax or an ordinary revenue measure whose credit provisions incidentally influence behavior. Thus the disputed assessment is a noncompensatory penalty for bankruptcy priority. Section 507(a)(7)(G)'s express priority for qualifying tax-related penalties compensating actual pecuniary loss does not cover this noncompensatory assessment. This characterization alone neither disallows the claim nor subordinates it.

The strongest contrary argument is that Congress expressly called §4971(a) a tax, placed it within federal excise-tax legislation, and provided a payable federal exaction; deterrent effect does not erase a tax. Path A accepts the last proposition, but finds the statutory trigger, measure and distinct underlying funding liability sufficient to identify this particular sanction. It does not adopt every part of the Tenth Circuit's Lorber formulation or a general test requiring an absence of private creditors. Minimum-funding waivers under §412(d) and 29 U.S.C. §1083(a) remove the relevant deficiency and can prevent either §4971 assessment; no distinction here rests on an assertion that only the additional hundred-percent assessment is avoidable.

**B — reject denial of priority on this penalty characterization.** Congress's express federal tax designation, the excise setting and the revenue payable to the Government give this assessment the tax character required by §507(a)(7)(E); a regulatory or deterrent object does not defeat that text. On this reading, the function-based precedents prevent a State's choice of label, or a collection provision's penalty label, from controlling a different federal bankruptcy category. They do not justify treating Congress's express federal excise tax as a non-tax merely because it is triggered by a funding failure. United States v. New York is substantial support that federal revenue provisions may use strong inducements without becoming bankruptcy penalties.

The strongest objection is that those precedents expressly require scrutiny of legal operation even for federal exactions, and this one sanctions breach rather than collecting an underlying tax. Path B accepts that scrutiny but finds no sufficiently enacted bankruptcy exclusion for this congressionally designated excise tax. It does not claim every charge collected by the IRS enjoys priority, or that the word tax overrides every express bankruptcy provision. The consequence is reversal of the penalty-based denial of §507(a)(7)(E) priority and remand consistent with tax treatment, not adjudication of the unreviewed assessments or a judicially fixed payment amount.

## Component II — equitable subordination

**C — reject the stated §510(c) subordination and remand.** Actual Noland, authored by Thomas and joined by all nine Justices on both holdings, is directly controlling. Its first holding preserves the actual administrative priority of the particular postpetition tax penalties there; that classification does not govern this prepetition assessment. Its second holding does govern: §510(c)'s equitable power cannot reclassify claims on grounds that substitute a judicial policy for Congress's distribution system. Noncompensatory character, the burden on innocent creditors, scarcity of assets and value-giving do not become claim-specific equity merely because the court calls its analysis balancing rather than an automatic rule.

The CF&I ground has those same defects. Denying tax priority does not establish a power to place every claim of this penalty class below ordinary unsecured claims. Noland applies to the permissible basis of §510(c) subordination, not only to claims that first receive a priority. Conversely, recognition of tax priority does not make a claim immune from all properly grounded equitable subordination.

**D — sustain the stated equitable result despite Noland.** The strongest alternative emphasizes the prepetition setting, the absence of administrative priority under A, the actual plan distribution and the lower court's asserted equitable balancing. Section 510(c) contains no express universal misconduct requirement, and the court may consider the actual bankruptcy effects of a claim. All eight rows reject D as sufficient on this record: those circumstances do not supply a ground distinct from the recurring class characteristics that Noland held inadequate. The lower court's express finding of no IRS inequitable conduct is preserved; no new misconduct is invented.

Neither C nor Noland imposes a universal misconduct condition, freezes equity to a catalog of 1978 outcomes, or orders a new misconduct hearing. The actual notice-and-hearing, distribution and claim/interest limits in §510(c) remain. A genuine legally sufficient claim-specific ground would require its actual support. None is established by the stated rationale. The independent §§1122/1129 arguments remain for lawful consideration on remand; their merits are not decided from paraphrases, and their availability does not sustain the present §510(c) holding.
