## Common record and statutory premises

Every commitment below incorporates these premises; no Justice supplies a contrary factual finding.

1. The plaintiff is the Tribe. Florida and Governor Chiles in his official capacity are distinct defendants. Alabama is not a party here. The review concerns immunity, not a finding that negotiations violated IGRA. The separate September 22, 1993 merits judgment for Florida and the Governor and the stayed appeal, No. 93-5256, remain distinct. An immunity reversal does not reverse that judgment or resolve game scope.
2. The complaint alleged a January 29, 1991 request and September 19 filing. The US brief instead describes a December filing. Both described intervals exceed 180 days; no amended-complaint history is invented. The actual prayer seeks a compact within sixty days, mediation if necessary, and a declaration concerning identified Class III games. The government's narrower declaration or good-faith-relief proposal is not substituted for that prayer as a record fact.
3. Negotiations through the Governor's representatives establish a concrete connection to the complained-of conduct sufficient to reject categorical dismissal for want of any connection. They establish neither waiver nor the Governor's unilateral power to bind Florida to every compact term. No affirmative order requiring such unestablished power is approved. His official-capacity designation alone is not the required connection.
4. Section 2710(d)(3)(A) imposes a good-faith negotiating duty upon receipt of a qualifying tribal request. Discretion over proposed terms and agreement does not confer discretion to violate that standard. It supplies no entitlement to the Tribe's preferred terms. The initial action under (d)(7)(A)(i) may be filed only after the 180-day period closes. State/tribal actions concerning violation of an effective compact under (A)(ii), and Secretary enforcement of prescribed procedures under (A)(iii), are different causes, not substitute authority for this plaintiff's requested remedy.
5. Under (B)(ii), tribal evidence of no compact and failure to respond or respond in good faith precedes the State's burden to prove good faith. Failure to agree alone is not bad faith. An actual adverse finding triggers (B)(iii)'s order to both State and Tribe to conclude a compact within sixty days. The court **may** consider public interest, public safety, criminality, financial integrity and adverse economic impact on existing gaming; it **shall** consider a demand for direct taxation of the Tribe or Indian lands as evidence of bad faith. No new burden, mandatory factor list or presumption of violation is added.
6. If that period produces no compact, (B)(iv) requires each side's last-best proposal to the court-appointed mediator. Selection must best comport with IGRA, other applicable federal law, and the court's findings and order. Submission to both sides under (B)(v) begins a second sixty-day opportunity for State consent. Consent produces an agreed compact under (B)(vi); nonconsent during that period leads to mediator notice and the Secretary's mandatory prescription under (B)(vii), in consultation with the Tribe and consistently with the selected proposal, IGRA and relevant state law. The direct findings/order wording in (iv) is not falsely quoted as language of (vii).
7. The State's failure to conclude a compact during the ordered period leads to the specified next stage. The selected construction does not add a coercive order compelling agreement or contempt merely for failure to agree or participate during that period. This conclusion rests on the particular substituted statutory consequence, not an express no-contempt clause. It does not adjudicate every hypothetical sanction for an officer's separate obstruction or disobedience. The mediator's selection obligation is not an automatic default award of the Tribe's proposal if Florida submits none. The US brief's “presumably” does not enact one.
8. A description of the duty as conditional means that maintaining the State's statutory negotiating opportunity carries a federal standard and that the enacted consequences follow violations through their actual predicates. It does not make initial good-faith negotiation optional, waive immunity by participation, preserve the State's entire role after disregarding conditions, or create a direct Secretary route. The Secretary's duty to prescribe after the statutory triggers is constrained, not a power to refuse all action at will.
9. Section (d)(4)'s absence of new state taxing authority permits the agreed regulatory-cost assessments in (d)(3)(C)(iii). Lack of other tax authority is no reason to refuse negotiations. That exception does not cancel (B)(iii)'s separate mandatory evidentiary treatment of demands for direct tribal/Indian-land taxation. Approved tribal legislation, the State-permits-such-gaming condition, the effective-compact requirement subject to the actual remedial provisions, and licensing remain separate. Section (d)(8) permits disapproval for IGRA violation, other federal law **not relating to jurisdiction over gaming on Indian lands**, or federal trust obligations. Forty-five-day inaction yields approval only to the extent consistent with IGRA; Federal Register publication remains necessary.
10. Section 2721 preserves remaining provisions if one is invalid. It does not satisfy the court-finding, period, mediation, selection and nonconsent predicates after immunity dismissal. No commitment endorses the Eleventh Circuit's suggested Secretary shortcut, confers a gaming license, finds bad faith, approves a compact, grants damages or grants new taxing power.

## Material alternative paths and reasons for their treatment

### Judicial function, binding relief, and later administration

**Commitment J answers the strongest Governors' objection.** The amici argue that even binding findings are insufficient if the judgment itself cannot vindicate either bargaining or gaming interests without later nonministerial executive action. The adopted answer is not simply that the Secretary cannot revise a bad-faith finding. A judgment establishes the federal obligation between adverse parties and legally changes their positions in the enacted process: the duty and limited opportunity to reach a compact during the judicial period give way, upon nonagreement, to mandatory mediation and ultimately to constrained federal prescription upon the further triggers. Access to that substitute process is concrete partial relief from the alleged negotiating obstruction. It does not require a judicial award of the ultimate gaming terms.

Aetna and Nashville establish that coercive execution is not indispensable to every conclusive judgment. Brimson establishes that judicial determination of an adverse legal duty can validly aid administration, but its actually enforceable witness order prevents treating it as dispositive by analogy alone. Ferreira involved a commissioner whose award created no debt without executive approval; IGRA gives legal effects to adjudication and constrains the next actors rather than submitting its judgment for approval. Hayburn's circuit communications and Taney's later-published Gordon paper illuminate that distinction without being misdescribed as delivered Supreme Court majority opinions. The actual Gordon disposition dismissed the appeal; the later reporter says the proposed Court opinion was never prepared.

Wisconsin reinforces the possibility of concrete redress through further executive action despite uncertainty of the ultimate outcome. It does not merge standing redressability with the judicial-function inquiry. Here the independent statutory effects just identified supply the additional answer. No automatic preclusion against the Secretary or every future claimant is assumed. The statutory constraint on the mediator, then on the Secretary through the selected proposal and governing law, performs its own work. Actual Morgan Stanley/Plaut concerns compelled reopening of the completed private securities judgment; its historical broader language is not imported.

**Competing Article III disposition:** Dismissal would be required if the statutory product were merely a recommendation or component findings with no binding relief until an executive chose whether to honor it. All eight decline that characterization of the enacted process. They do not save an otherwise advisory scheme by inventing contempt, an expanded injunction or a new cause. J also does not supply personal jurisdiction, defeat immunity, validate every possible declaration or establish that this Tribe has won the merits. The late amicus objection is examined because it genuinely challenges the judicial character of the assigned function; its consideration does not make unrelated late constitutional defenses reachable.

### Clear statement, congressional power, and precedent

**Competing narrower statutory disposition:** The Governors' Young-only reading is plausible enough to require an answer. States act through officers, prospective suits often need no direct-State abrogation, and Dellmuth rejects treating logical defendants as unmistakably named defendants. Nevertheless, each Associate concludes that IGRA's integrated litigation directions go beyond a general review clause combined with program references: the particular cause concerns the State's failure; the State must prove good faith after the statutory showing; and the court must order the State and Tribe into the specified process. Permitting an officer route does not make this direct-State design disappear. Legislative silence cannot negate clear enacted directions, and the assertion that Congress assumed Young is not a finding of legislative intent.

Scalia's Dellmuth concurrence and his Union Gas statutory join expressly permit clarity without naming the Eleventh Amendment. O'Connor's and Kennedy's Dellmuth positions require unequivocal text, not a ritual phrase. Stevens's Dellmuth dissent supports the same present result by a less restrictive approach. Souter's Blatchford join distinguishes §1362's general jurisdiction from this specified cause; Thomas's Suter approach likewise requires the actual statutory words. Ginsburg's and Breyer's recent Barnett joins concern the enacted grant rather than an added express-preemption demand, while leaving this distinct immunity rule intact. Dellmuth's former EHA result is not current immunity law for post-October 30, 1990 IDEA violations: enacted §1403 changed it. Smith's EHA exclusivity and fee result were separately altered in 1986. Neither superseded statutory result is treated as current general law.

**The constitutional fork is real.** Union Gas has an actual five-vote constitutional judgment: Brennan, Marshall, Blackmun, Stevens and White. Its statutory coalition instead includes Scalia and excludes White. White's disagreement with much of the plurality's reasoning prevents counting five for that reasoning, not counting five for the constitutional conclusion. The four P+ commitments apply that conclusion to the express Indian-Commerce remedy; the four P− commitments candidly displace it. No row treats it as already overruled by Blatchford, New York, Hess or the composition of the present Court.

The Indian/interstate distinction and the proprietary/governmental distinction are rejected as sufficient grounds by both groups, for different ultimate reasons. Blatchford's absence of reciprocal tribal consent does not itself decide Congress's power. Interstate and Indian commerce appear in the same clause; the federal Indian-affairs responsibility supplies no convincing reason to recognize less congressional regulatory authority. Union Gas's actual conclusion did not condition abrogation on the State acting as proprietor. Garcia's rejection of a general traditional-functions immunity bears on that proposed distinction, although it does not resolve a separate New York command objection. P− therefore requires express precedent correction instead of describing these distinctions as established limits; P+ requires an actual extension/application, rather than pretending IGRA was already adjudicated.

The government also proposes that Congress can at least authorize directly the prospective relief Young permits against officers. P+ accepts that as a reinforcing limit on this case's reach, not as the only source of congressional power. P− rejects it because the established officer fiction and direct-State immunity remain legally distinct; absence of a treasury payment does not eliminate the State's immunity from suit. Actual Hess supplies no contrary rule. The tribal plaintiff and federal protective responsibility do not make this an action by the United States. Fitzpatrick's later-amendment authority remains intact under every position.

### Officer relief, exclusivity, and avoidance

**Y+** recognizes an existing federal equitable officer route, not a newly implied damages action under IGRA or a §1983 holding. Suter supplies no prohibition here merely because a duty involves judgment: Congress expressly supplies a judicial action concerning this good-faith obligation. Young requires the responsible connection, a continuing federal violation and permissible prospective relief. The Governor's negotiating role provides the connection for the limited claim. Incorporating state gaming law into the federal duty does not convert the action into a demand to enforce state law under Pennhurst. The Court still cannot dictate discretionary terms, award a compact or assume the Governor has every power necessary to bind Florida.

Under Y+, the proposed officer action invokes the existing sequence rather than replacing it with harsher remedies. State-directed language does not expressly exclude connected officials, and the statutory findings, periods, mediation and Secretary constraints continue to govern. Sea Clammers and Smith concern implied additional actions and displacement of §1983; Wilder distinguishes rights, actions and remedy foreclosure. Extending their method to Young requires proof that this route conflicts with the enacted arrangement. Varity's express ERISA equitable grant is not transplanted here, but its provision-specific treatment refutes a mechanical inference from the existence of another remedy. Meghrig requires respecting the relief actually authorized, which the confined route does.

**Y−** answers that confined proposal, not an invented request for unlimited contempt. Congress selected the State as party in the specified judicial trigger, imposed the reciprocal compact order on State and Tribe, and used successive nonagreement/nonconsent consequences rather than an ordinary compulsory bargaining remedy. On this reading the identity and form of the statutory action are part of the compromise along with its relief. Recasting it as an officer action after the chosen State action fails constitutionally would alter that selected enforcement route even if the officer suit copied every deadline. A freestanding declaration of the same statutory violation would accomplish the same unauthorized substitution. The inference rests on these linked provisions, not complexity alone or the word “State” alone.

This is a **new, IGRA-specific application** of the remedial-intent method, not a representation that Sea Clammers, Smith, Wilder or Meghrig already held Young displaced. The contrary argument is substantial: an official acts for the State in every ordinary Young case, so party selection can become a circular invocation of immunity. The Y− Justices locate the additional reason in the expressly designed, limited cause and its mandatory reciprocal/substitute steps, not in the general real-party fiction. No remedy gap entitles a court to change Congress's chosen action. Y+ rejects that intent inference because substituting a connected officer while retaining every substantive remedial limit is consistent with the ordinary equitable background. Each group thus disagrees about this statute's exclusion of the route, not whether traditional Young exists or whether its remedies are unlimited. Barnett's ordinary prospective preemption action remains undisturbed under both positions.

**US Young-only avoidance:** All eight consider p19 n5 as a genuine alternative. The Y− group cannot use it because it rejects its officer premise. The Y+ group would accept a genuinely narrower disposition reversing the officer dismissal and leaving direct-State power undecided if the State claim could lawfully be left for consideration of complete relief and equitable necessity. They would not affirm State immunity without deciding it, deem the Tribe to have waived its claim, or declare the State claim moot. Their primary commitment resolves both presented defendants because the State's separately asserted immunity is still live and complete equivalence of relief through this Governor is not established: negotiating connection is not proof of every power to bind the State. The government's “presumably,” “might forgo,” and possible equitable denial establish a permissible alternative, not a fact compelling abandonment of the direct claim. This openness is a narrower join envelope, not an additional primary vote or an already adopted mootness holding.

## Justice-specific commitments and join limits

### Justice Stevens

**Result and grounds:** Reverse both immunity dismissals. Join J, I, P+, Y+, T and R. His independent direct-State ground follows his Union Gas concurrence, Dellmuth dissent and joined Blatchford dissent: the literal Eleventh Amendment does not describe this tribal federal-question action, and Congress may displace the judicially created immunity applicable beyond that text. A statute cannot amend the constitutional text. He does not need a wholesale overruling of every application of Hans to decide this expressly authorized suit, and agrees no waiver has been shown under inherited law.

His Sea Clammers separate opinion and joined Smith dissent distinguish a useful remedial-exclusivity inference from proof of Congress's intent. Wilder confirms that discretion within a federal standard does not make a right judicially unenforceable. His Varity and Meghrig joins require the actual route and relief, not judicial invention. Those commitments support Y+ only at the confined scope stated above. The strongest Y− objection is the deliberately limited statutory action; he answers that applying the same limitations to the connected officer preserves that design instead of replacing it. He refuses an officer opinion compelling agreement or granting all requested games.

His own Wisconsin opinion supports concrete partial redress, but he supplies J's separate binding-process analysis rather than a standing shortcut. His Casey stare-decisis join and published Hubbard concurrence require more than a new preferred constitutional reading; he rejects P−'s claim of sufficient doctrinal incompatibility. Congress's deliberate override can coexist with a default immunity, as White's Union Gas position demonstrates. He would join an appropriately bounded Young-only disposition but does not prefer leaving the live State claim unresolved where his own sufficient ground respects the constitutional text. His distinct immunity account is not attributed to the other P+ Justices.

### Justice O'Connor

**Result and grounds:** Affirm dismissal as to both defendants under P− and Y−, with J, I, W, T and R. Her Union Gas constitutional dissent, Dellmuth majority join, public Hess institutional-control dissent and Puerto Rico Aqueduct position treat the immunity from being sued as constitutionally significant beyond treasury protection. IGRA's integrated litigation language clears the stricter intent threshold notwithstanding her different reading of CERCLA in Union Gas. She will not preserve the result by a fictitious statutory ambiguity.

Her Casey join, Allied-Bruce concurrence and Hubbard dissent make mere renewed disagreement insufficient to overrule. Her selected special justification here is the unresolved structural conflict between treating immunity as a constitutional limitation and permitting the antecedent commerce grant to remove that limitation, combined with Union Gas's failure to supply a majority rationale defining and reconciling the exception. Subsequent actual Puerto Rico Aqueduct and Hess maintain that institutional constitutional premise; they do not themselves overrule Union Gas. Its five-vote outcome remains binding until expressly corrected. She accepts that the pro-abrogation conclusion is administrable as a clear-statement rule and that some legal reliance exists; she does not find the case empirically unworkable or claim Congress never relied on it. She nevertheless concludes that turning the constitutional immunity into a congressional option changes the underlying constitutional allocation, a defect not repaired by the lower court's ungrounded proprietary/Indian limitations. This structural and precedent-integration judgment, rather than changed membership or her original dissent alone, supplies her basis for correction. The P+ Justices dispute its sufficiency.

For Y−, her Smith majority and Wilder dissent, reinforced by her Varity dissent and Meghrig join, support close attention to who may invoke which process and what duty it judicially enforces. She acknowledges that Sea Clammers/Smith are not already Young holdings and that Smith's EHA result changed legislatively. Her present inference is confined to IGRA's deliberately selected action. She rejects the broader proposition that future federal officer relief always requires an express statutory officer cause. She also rejects a categorical lawmaking-function exception independent of statutory construction; actual New York and the preserved Tenth issue do not establish one here. J remains satisfied because the valid statutory form would bind legal relations and activate consequences even without compelling agreement.

She joins the Commerce-power correction at P−'s stated level. She requires separate justification before joining a disposition under another Article I clause or an unpreserved anti-commandeering judgment. She will not join a no-Young rationale resting solely on discretion, the State's real interest, or lack of money damages.

### Justice Scalia

**Result and grounds:** Affirm both dismissals under P− and Y−; join J, I, W, T and R. His Union Gas statutory join and Dellmuth concurrence foreclose a magic-words objection here. His Union Gas constitutional opinion supplies the direct structural opposition: the constitutional immunity affirmed by Hans is not merely a statutory drafting presumption, and Fitzpatrick's later Fourteenth Amendment authority does not prove the same power under the original Article I grants.

His published Hubbard concurrence, joined by Kennedy, requires an additional legal justification beyond an erroneous initial construction. Here he identifies the continuing inability to reconcile Union Gas's commerce exception with the constitutional-immunity premise retained in Hans and Blatchford without reducing the latter to a legislative default. The missing majority rationale increases that doctrinal defect but does not erase the five-vote judgment. Unlike Hans's long incorporation into statutory expectations discussed in his Union Gas writing, the narrower and more recent exception has not acquired comparable foundational status; that is a difference in the asserted institutional interests, not a finding of zero reliance or a rule that recent decisions may freely be discarded. He rejects the stronger P+ answer that express congressional action and automatic constitutional consent are categorically distinct enough to reconcile the decisions. He would support the broader structural proposition that Article I cannot enlarge the judicial power beyond constitutional state-immunity limits, while joining the narrower common Commerce holding without demanding adjudication of other Article I applications.

His Suter/Mertens positions, Varity dissent and full Meghrig join support Y−'s insistence on the particular enforcement choice. He considers the government's confined action carefully: matching the remedial sequence does not, for him, authorize replacing the statutorily selected defendant when direct-State jurisdiction is constitutionally unavailable. A declaration cannot do indirectly what this construction forecloses directly. He preserves ordinary Young, expressly recognized in his Union Gas opinion, including Barnett's preemption vehicle. He rejects J's opposing theory because a binding judicial trigger and mandatory legal consequences are not executive review of a recommendation; Nashville/Aetna prevent treating actual execution as universally essential. No newly invented contempt power is needed for that conclusion.

### Justice Kennedy

**Result and grounds:** Affirm both dismissals under P− and Y−; join J, I, W, T and R. His Union Gas constitutional dissent and Dellmuth opinion distinguish Congress's unmistakable intent from its constitutional power. His Hilton opinion preserves the statutory state-court FELA rule; it neither revives constructive federal-forum waiver nor decides this tribal action. His Hess majority join concerns actual arm status and does not negate immunity of Florida itself.

His Casey stare-decisis join and Hubbard concurrence with Scalia require a legal reason beyond original disagreement. He adopts P− because the five-vote Union Gas conclusion lacks a coherent limiting reconciliation with the structural immunity retained in the intervening public law, and because attempts to preserve it through this case's Indian/proprietary distinctions would add limitations the constitutional judgment did not announce. He recognizes the opposing coherent account—constitutional immunity subject to clear congressional override—and does not call it impossible to administer. His objection is to its alteration of the constitutional allocation, strengthened by the surviving institutional treatment of immunity, not a numerical lack of reasons alone. Constitutional error cannot be corrected by ordinary legislation as Hilton's statutory question could; that difference permits but does not automatically require correction. He finds the combined structural conflict and deficient precedential integration sufficient here, while the P+ group finds it a renewed disagreement insufficient under Casey. No claim of a newly discovered historical fact or quantified reliance finding is made.

His Varity join does not mechanically place him with the Varity dissenters here. ERISA expressly authorized appropriate individual equitable relief, and he joined giving that grant effect. IGRA, on his construction, chooses a particular State action and substitutes process for ordinary coercion without a separate residual equitable grant. His Wilder dissent and Suter join support that provision-specific examination; Meghrig confirms that the remedy cannot be enlarged merely to complete the statute's objectives. He accepts Y− only with its answer to the same-sequence officer proposal and its preservation of ordinary federal equitable relief. He refuses a blanket rule that all detailed statutes displace Young, that federally incorporated state law is a Pennhurst claim, or that negotiation requires judicial dictation of compact terms. He supports the P− Commerce rule and reserves broader Article I applications not needed here.

### Justice Souter

**Result and grounds:** Reverse both immunity dismissals under P+ and Y+; join J, I, W, T and R. His Blatchford majority join establishes no reciprocal tribal consent and no §1362 abrogation, while expressly leaving congressional power and possible officer relief open. IGRA supplies the unmistakable authorization absent there. He applies the inherited five-vote Union Gas constitutional conclusion without adopting all of Brennan's reasoning or Stevens's account of judicial immunity.

His Casey join, Hilton/Allied-Bruce positions and Hubbard dissent place a distinct burden on the proposed overruling. Union Gas's constitutional conclusion can operate without treating every plurality sentence as binding. A default constitutional immunity and Congress's specifically recognized commerce power are not made logically irreconcilable merely by repeating the Hans premise. The result has not been shown incapable of administration; neither actual New York nor Hess decided the abrogation question. He treats the missing shared rationale as a scope constraint, not sufficient special justification to remove the judgment. He rejects the Indian distinction because the absence of automatic tribal reciprocity does not settle federal legislative power over Indian affairs. The proprietary distinction is neither inherited law nor a persuasive constitutional line.

His own Varity opinion and Meghrig opinion require a lawful action and fit between enacted relief and requested remedy. Those decisions do not answer Young, but they support preserving an established route where it invokes rather than escapes statutory constraints. For Y+, the good-faith duty is specific and judicially actionable, unlike the Suter provisions; the officer is connected; and the requested terms must remain within IGRA. He answers Y−'s party-selection theory by distinguishing express authorization against a State from express exclusion of ordinary officer equity, especially where there is no incompatible relief. He would not imply a damages action, assume a §1983 right or allow an injunction that overrides the mediated progression.

He joins J because the judgment creates an operative entitlement to further constrained process, not merely a favorable advisory finding. Wisconsin and his Barnett joins reinforce partial relief and preservation of unresolved merits respectively, without supplying an automatic result here. He would join a proper Young-only avoidance disposition if the State claim were lawfully left undecided and no complete-relief or waiver fact were invented. His primary commitment decides the still-live State immunity question; his participation in the P+ rationale is bounded and supplies no wholesale Hans overruling.

### Justice Thomas

**Result and grounds:** Affirm both dismissals under P− and Y−; join J, I, W, T and R. No personal Union Gas vote is attributed to him. His actual Hess dissent join, Puerto Rico Aqueduct/Suter positions, Allied-Bruce dissent and Hubbard statutory correction supply date-eligible evidence of attention to the Constitution's allocation, State immunity and enacted authority rather than automatic retention of an unsupported extension. His position must still answer Union Gas as an inherited five-vote constitutional judgment.

He adopts the constitutional immunity account supported in Scalia's Union Gas writing and finds its incompatible commerce exception should be expressly corrected. ### Justice Ginsburg

**Result and grounds:** Reverse both immunity dismissals under P+ and Y+; join J, I, W, T and R. ### Justice Breyer

**Result and grounds:** Reverse both immunity dismissals under P+ and Y+; join J, I, W, T and R. His public Hess join is entity-specific; his Lopez dissent, joined by Stevens, Souter and Ginsburg, addresses the scope and review of commercial regulation, not automatic immunity abrogation. Those positions support careful attention to the federal scheme without replacing the separate immunity inquiry. Union Gas supplies the inherited constitutional conclusion that does that work here.

He regards the same-clause extension as more faithful to that conclusion than an Indian/proprietary limitation unsupported by the actual holding. Congress gave States a significant compacting opportunity while specifying judicial safeguards for Tribes. That arrangement is not a waiver, and federal responsibility toward Tribes does not make their action the United States' own suit. It does explain why applying the express commerce-power precedent fits this statutory design. His Hubbard concurrence with Stevens and Ginsburg requires an actual additional reason to unsettle precedent; doctrinal disagreement and a fractured rationale do not establish such a reason where the constitutional conclusion remains usable and later law leaves the question open. He joins P+ without importing the entire Union Gas plurality or erasing Hans elsewhere.

His Varity and Meghrig joins put the route and remedy, not remedial desirability alone, at the center. For Y+, a connected official can carry out the federal standard without a court selecting compact terms, while the same periods, mediation, selection and Secretarial constraints govern. He finds that operation consistent with Congress's action rather than an evasion of it. The strongest contrary contention is that defendant identity itself is an exclusive part of the compromise; he finds insufficient textual evidence for that added exclusion in a statute designed to make the State's negotiating standard judicially cognizable. He refuses to cure difficulty by creating broader contempt, automatic selection or administrative bypass.

His Wisconsin join is particularly relevant to the distinction between real partial relief and a guaranteed ultimate outcome. For J he nevertheless identifies IGRA's binding statutory consequences, not mere practical hope of later voluntary compliance. His Barnett join reinforces that eliminating one legal obstacle does not establish all predicates. He would accept a proper officer-only avoidance disposition leaving the State issue open, but his primary commitment decides the unresolved State claim because the record does not establish complete relief through this officer for every requested statutory step. He joins T and R without ordering a new technical or evidentiary proceeding merely to make the remand more elaborate.
