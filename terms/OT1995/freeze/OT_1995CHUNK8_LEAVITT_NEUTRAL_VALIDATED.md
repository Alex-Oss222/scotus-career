## The two petition questions and posture

The actual petition asks, in source-grounded paraphrase:

1. Whether the Eleventh Amendment permits a federal appellate court to invalidate a state statute in its entirety on severability grounds and enjoin remaining provisions asserted to be constitutional.
2. Whether the governing abortion doctrine permits reliance on Thornburgh to invalidate fetal-survival method duties subject to their maternal-health qualification and the State's claimed absence of an undue burden.

Respondents describe the first as refusing to rewrite §302 and the second as the constitutionality of §§307–308. The runtime's broad general question and focus on severability therefore cannot exclude the second petition question or treat the first as an already-granted merits question. A grant must specify its scope. Petition management, summary merits review, and a later plenary event remain separate actions; the Rule of Four does not establish majority support for summary reversal, and any public merits count cannot stand in for an undisclosed petition poll.

The District Court invalidated §302(2), sustained §302(3), and treated it as severable. The Tenth Circuit, 61 F.3d 1493, held subsection (3) inseverable from (2), without deciding subsection (3)'s federal constitutional validity. The State did not appeal invalidation of subsection (2). The method duties present the separate second petition question. The petition's label does not itself authorize review of every provision, an injunction against HB206, or all constitutional issues implicated by the statute.

## Statutory details that must be retained

The source supplement identifies the following 1995 text from petition pages 2–4:

- Section 302(2)(a) concerns abortion necessary to save the woman's life in the attending physician's professional judgment. Subsection (b) concerns pregnancy from rape or rape of a child under §§76-5-402 and 402.1, reported to law enforcement before abortion. Subsection (c) separately concerns incest under §76-5-406(10) or §76-7-102, likewise reported before abortion. Subsections (d) and (e) concern prevention of grave damage to the woman's medical health and prevention of birth of a child with grave defects, respectively, in the attending physician's professional judgment. The reporting conditions are part of the exceptions.
- Section 302(3) permits abortion after twenty weeks from conception only for the purposes and circumstances in (2)(a), (d) and (e). It does not textually adopt individualized viability or retain rape and incest as independent exceptions. The State's characterization of twenty weeks as postviability is advocacy, not an established finding of universal viability.
- Section 307 applies when the unborn child is sufficiently developed to have any reasonable possibility of survival outside the womb. It requires the procedure giving the child the best chance of survival in the physician's best medical judgment, and prohibits a procedure designed to kill or injure unless necessary, in the woman's physician's opinion, to prevent grave damage to her medical health.
- Section 308 requires the performing physician to use all medical skills to attempt to promote, preserve and maintain the life of a child sufficiently developed to have that reasonable possibility of survival, consistently with saving the woman's life or preventing grave damage to her medical health.
- Section 317 declares invalid provisions and applications severable and says each provision would have been passed irrespective of invalidity elsewhere. Section 317.2 separately conditionally reenacts the 1974 §302 if this Court holds the §302 amended by 1991 Senate Bill 23 unconstitutional. That condition is not the general severability clause.

The Tenth Circuit treated subsection (3) as an interdependent modification of subsection (2)'s comprehensive ban, relying on Utah decisions including Salt Lake City v. International Ass'n of Firefighters, State v. Salt Lake City, and Carter. It also read §317.2 as a specific contingency. Those are the federal lower court's disputed readings, not an authoritative Utah Supreme Court interpretation of this statute. Statutory commands, lower-court constructions and medical findings must remain separate.

## Intervening HB206: admitted text, contested effect

The [complete enrolled transcription](../sources/CHUNK8_C/Utah_HB206_1996_enrolled_text.md) and [April 29 admitted public source](../entering-law/PUBLIC_Utah_HB206_admitted_source_1996-04-29.md) are required inputs. The supplemental filing reports passage February 28, signature March 18 and effectiveness April 29. These retain their filing-recitation provenance; admission creates no judicial finding of implied repeal, mootness, constitutional validity or enforceability.

New §310.5 defines the covered dilation-and-extraction and saline procedures. Its first definition excludes dilation and evacuation involving dismemberment before removal, suction curettage, and suction aspiration. Its printed device word has a disclosed transcription issue; consult the original image if that exact word becomes material rather than silently substituting a word.

The prohibition applies after viability determined for this section by the physician's best clinical judgment from the pregnancy's known particular facts and reasonably available medical information and technology: a realistic possibility of maintaining and nourishing life outside the womb, with or without temporary artificial support. The defined procedures are forbidden unless **all other available abortion procedures** would pose a risk to the woman's life or health. The section's felony provision requires intentional, knowing and willful violation.

Amended §314 retains intentional unauthorized-abortion liability, expressly exempts the woman from criminal liability for seeking or obtaining her own abortion, preserves third-degree-felony treatment for willful violations of the listed sections including §§307, 308 and 310.5, and makes other violations class A misdemeanors. Amended §315 makes §§302, 304(2), 305(2) and 310.5 inapplicable when a serious medical emergency leaves insufficient time to comply. That list does not silently waive §§307–308. Section 4 expressly confines the new viability method to §310.5 and its purposes, disclaiming effects on other provisions or their litigation.

Respondents argue that the amendment informs severability and may impliedly repeal §§307–308, mooting the second question. The continued penalty references and section 4 are material countervailing text. Neither repeal nor no repeal is adjudicated here. No direct challenge to HB206 is added.
