**Good Samaritan Hospital v. Shalala, June 7, 1993.** White's six-Justice Court opinion permits the Secretary's reconciliation construction within lawful reimbursement methods and limits. A change of interpretation can reduce its weight without automatically invalidating a reasoned, otherwise permissible construction. Statutory meaning, actual delegation and the agency's explanation remain necessary; an unsupported litigation position gains no automatic controlling weight. Authorized exceptions and independent challenges to unlawful methods survive. *Bowen*'s unauthorized-retroactive-rulemaking rule is distinct.

Souter's dissent, joined by Stevens and Scalia, finds the text unambiguous: clause (ii) addresses methods producing inadequate or excessive aggregate reimbursement for the provider and fiscal period; §1395g already adjusts interim payments. Assigning both provisions that same work deprives distinct language of effect, while clause (i)'s no-cost-shifting requirement reinforces the final reimbursement obligation. The dissent requires correction of an inadequate aggregate result, not reimbursement of every isolated cost item. It rejects deference because its statutory reading leaves no ambiguity, **not because an agency changed position**. Its proposed reversal is not the Court's holding.

For Smiley, that disagreement supplies a concrete competing approach to the relationship between operative provisions, their distinct functions and the point at which interpretive discretion begins. It does not decide the relationship between §§85 and 86, establish OCC delegation, or make either an earlier agency position or a later notice-and-comment instrument automatically decisive.

**MCI Telecommunications Corp. v. AT&T, June 17, 1994.** The five-Justice Court opinion invalidates replacement of mandatory tariff filing throughout the nondominant long-distance sector with optional filing. Section 203(b)(2)'s power to modify operates within the statutory system: discretion, good cause and the particular-instance or qualifying-general-order terms remain, and the Commission cannot require more than 120 days' notice. Consequential adjustments, lawful class-wide technical changes and possible limited waivers remain. This is a statutory-boundary holding, not a new economic-importance exception to *Chevron*.

Scalia's concurrence, joined by Kennedy, Thomas and Ginsburg, supplies a separate objection even assuming the modification power otherwise reaches the rule: the general order lacks the required **special circumstances or conditions**. The nondominant class covered all long-distance carriers except AT&T and served roughly 40 percent of customers; treating that class as a unit describes ordinary sector regulation on this record. The label alone does not establish the limiting condition. The concurrence establishes neither a numerical cutoff nor a finding that each carrier possesses market power; genuinely qualifying conditions and smaller or differently conditioned waivers remain open. All four also join the Court, but their independently sufficient alternative has only four votes and is **not controlling**.

Stevens's dissent, joined by Blackmun and Souter, reads the express power to modify any requirement, including qualifying general orders, as reasonably accommodating dominant and competitive carriers. Special circumstances may be special in character rather than numerically rare. The agency's actual competition rationale matters: advance public filing can burden competition without the same protection against market power. Voluntary filing, just-and-reasonable rates, nondiscrimination, complaint enforcement and restoration upon changed conditions retain the statutory system. The 120-day ceiling limits an increased notice burden; it does not reduce every relaxation to insignificant detail. This position rejects both the Court's structural objection and the concurrence's separate-condition objection, while retaining good cause, actual qualifying conditions, discernible agency reasons and binding statutory limits. It would not authorize elimination of §§201–202, compulsory nonfiling or resolution of the distinct complaint-damages proceeding.

For Smiley, the concurrence makes actual enacted predicates independently material for its four joiners; the dissent supplies Stevens's and Souter's own contextual account of delegated choice and surviving safeguards. Neither position authorizes transplanting telecommunications predicates into banking law. The precise banking text and the legal character of the OCC instrument must supply the relevant conditions. Prior publication of these positions does not determine any current Justice's conclusion.

## Supplemental statutory validation — exact text and applicability

The [safe statutory provenance note](../sources/chunk7_b/SAFE_Banking_Statutes_1994_1996.md) and the following exact texts at `870950d` have been read: full [§43](../sources/chunk7_b/USCODE1994_12USC43.txt), [§93a](../sources/chunk7_b/USCODE1994_12USC93a.txt) and [§93](../sources/chunk7_b/USCODE1994_12USC93.txt); complete selected [§1818(b),(c),(d),(h),(i),(n)](../sources/chunk7_b/USCODE1994_12USC1818_selected.txt) and [§1813(q),(u),(x)](../sources/chunk7_b/USCODE1994_12USC1813_qux.txt); and the [§43 enactment excerpt](../sources/chunk7_b/PL103328_114_43_enactment_excerpt.txt). The source worker's official-edition comparison reports no intervening change to these operative provisions before June 3, 1996. Section 43 was enacted September 29, 1994, without a special delay: the separate §101(e) one-year delay applies to amendments made by that section, not §114's enactment of §43. Older conduct-dependent enforcement effective dates remain in the sources.

**Section 43 trigger and duties.** Before issuing an opinion letter or interpretive rule, on request or on the agency's own motion, concluding that federal law preempts application to a national bank of state law concerning community reinvestment, consumer protection, fair lending or establishment of intrastate branches, the appropriate federal banking agency must publish Federal Register notice describing the issue and **each state law at issue**, allow at least30 days for written comments, and consider comments received when developing the final action. Section 43 also covers determinations under §36(f)(1)(A)(ii). Subsection(b) separately requires Federal Register publication of the specified final opinions, interpretive rules and determinations. A federal-interest interpretation is not exempt merely because called interpretive; whether this particular instrument meets the statutory trigger remains an applicability question. The statute does not itself settle substantive preemption or deference.

All §43 exceptions remain distinct:

- Subsection(c)(1) excludes from the **entire section** an opinion or rule raising preemption issues essentially identical to those previously resolved by courts or addressed by an agency opinion or interpretive rule; it separately excludes a response to a request containing no significant legal basis for a preemption determination.
- Subsection(c)(2) excludes from the **entire section** materials prepared for judicial proceedings, submission to Congress or a Member of Congress, or intragovernmental use. The source permits distinguishing such materials from a separately promulgated public rule; later use of a rule in litigation does not by itself establish this exception.
- Subsection(c)(3) permits an agency exception **only to subsection(a)** upon a written determination of necessity to avoid a serious and imminent threat to a national bank's safety and soundness, or for an opinion/rule connected to an acquisition of one or more banks in statutory default or danger of default, or an acquisition receiving FDIC assistance under §1823(c). Final publication under subsection(b) is not excused by (c)(3). Section 1813(x)'s default requires an official appointment of a conservator, receiver or other legal custodian; danger of default requires the specified agency advice/determination concerning inability to meet demands or obligations, or depletion of substantially all capital, together with no reasonable prospect of recovery without federal assistance. An ordinary borrower's missed payment supplies neither condition.

**Section 93a delegation and exclusions.** The Comptroller may prescribe rules and regulations to carry out the office's responsibilities, **except to the extent** that rulemaking authority has been expressly and exclusively granted to another regulatory agency. The authority conferred by §93a **does not apply to §36 or national banks' securities activities under the Glass-Steagall Act**. Section 85 is not one of those express exclusions. These words supply actual rulemaking authority within the office's responsibilities; they do not resolve §85's meaning, the scope of delegated interpretive choice, satisfaction of §43 or judicial treatment of this instrument. The complete §85 and §86 readings and their exceptions remain as stated in the finalized packet.

**Enforcement link and its limits.** Section 93's Title 62 references include §§85 and86. Section 93(a) conditions franchise forfeiture on the directors' knowing violation or knowing permission of a violation and a judicial determination in the Comptroller's suit before dissolution; participating or assenting directors' personal damages liability is distinct. Section 93(b) authorizes Comptroller-assessed civil penalties for statutory/regulatory violations, with separate tiers, culpability/harm predicates, daily caps, mitigation/collection rules and an agency hearing upon a request within20 days. Section 93(c) preserves authority over separated institution-affiliated parties only if notice is served within six years. The final subsection, also numbered(d) in the official text, permits enforcement in the Comptroller's own name through the Comptroller's attorneys; it does not displace those substantive and procedural conditions. The separate conviction-based forfeiture provisions and good-faith-successor exception are not a basis for relief on this record. No penalty or forfeiture is adjudicated here.

Section 1818(b)(1) supplies administrative enforcement on its stated unsafe-practice or law, rule, regulation, written-condition or written-agreement predicates, including reasonable cause concerning an impending violation. It requires notice stating the alleged facts and ordinarily a hearing 30–60days after service (a different date may be set at a served party's request); a cease-and-desist/corrective order requires consent, including the specified nonappearance consequence, or findings on the hearing record. An order ordinarily becomes effective 30 days after service; a consent order uses its specified time, and stays, modification or termination remain possible. Section 1813(q)(1) identifies the Comptroller for national banking associations, while allowing that more than one agency may be appropriate; §1818(b)(5) expressly extends coverage to Comptroller-chartered national associations, including uninsured associations. The Federal Reserve limitation for specified holding-company/nonbank-subsidiary proceedings in (b)(3) is preserved; authority over a national bank is not unbounded OCC authority over every affiliate. Section 1813(u)'s institution-affiliated-party categories retain their participation requirements and, for independent contractors, knowing/reckless participation and the specified loss/adverse-effect threshold.

Corrective authority is conditional: §1818(b)(6)(A) restitution/reimbursement/indemnification/guarantees require unjust enrichment or reckless disregard for law, applicable regulations or a prior agency order. Other listed corrective actions and activity limits remain available within the order's lawful predicates; a deficient examination rating qualifies under (b)(8) only if uncorrected. Asset-restraint powers retain the modified Rule 65 protections in (b)(10) and (i)(4). Temporary orders under (c) require the stated threatened institutional/depositor harm or particular incomplete-record conditions and retain their limited duration and ten-day judicial-review route. Subsections(d),(h),(i) preserve court enforcement, APA hearing/review, the specified thirty-day appellate petition period and exclusions, no automatic stay, differentiated civil-penalty predicates and caps, a requested hearing within twenty days, mitigation and six-year post-separation notice limits. Section 1818(n)'s rulemaking and compulsory-process authority is tied to the identified proceedings, insured-deposit claims, examinations and investigations. These provisions establish administrative responsibilities relevant to the asserted §85 delegation; they neither supply an automatic deference conclusion nor transform Smiley's private case into an OCC enforcement proceeding. Their complete saved conditions, temporal notes and remedy limits control any more specific use.
