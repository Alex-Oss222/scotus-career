**Hawaiian Airlines**

**Current rule:** A state claim enforcing an independent, nonnegotiable right is not preempted merely because the workplace has a collective-bargaining agreement, the agreement supplies an undisputed wage figure, the same facts support a contract grievance, or a defense refers to the agreement. The claim’s actual legal elements and necessary interpretation govern, not its label. Section 301 does not displace a wage-penalty claim requiring only undisputed wage arithmetic. Under the Railway Labor Act, a claim turning on conduct, motive or another purely factual question likewise remains independent unless resolving it requires interpretation of disputed contract terms. Contractual disputes remain subject to their prescribed processes; parallel arbitration does not itself remove independent rights.

**Current rule:** Garmon and Machinists preemption do not bar a project-specific contracting condition when the State acts for its proprietary interests as owner and purchaser, analogous private conduct is lawful, and Congress has not expressly or impliedly displaced the proprietary conduct. ## Brown v. Pro Football — 95-388


The 1987 NFL/NFLPA collective bargaining agreement expired while bargaining continued. In 1989 the owners adopted Resolution G-2, allowing each club up to six developmental players beyond the regular 47-player roster. The program initially fixed no amount; the NFL later proposed $1,000 weekly pay. The NFLPA sought regular-player protections and individual salary negotiation. The parties discussed the proposal and rejected each other's wage position. The case proceeded on the assumption of lawful good-faith bargaining to impasse; no separate adjudication of an unfair labor practice should be invented.

The NFL implemented uniform developmental-player contracts and warned clubs that any pay above or below $1,000 could bring discipline, including loss of draft choices. In the 1989 season 236 players signed these contracts; the action was brought for 235 of them by nine named plaintiffs on May 9, 1990. Keep the 236 signers distinct from the 235-member plaintiff class.

The District Court rejected the labor exemption on three alternative legal grounds: expiration of the collective bargaining agreement; alternatively impasse; alternatively absence of union assent to a restraint previously embodied in a collective agreement. It granted plaintiffs summary judgment on antitrust liability in March 1992. A ten-day September 1992 jury trial concerned antitrust injury and damages, **not liability**. The trebled judgment was $30,349,642. The court denied the defense request for judgment as a matter of law/new trial, permanently enjoined uniform regular-season salary fixing for any player category, and awarded $1,744,578.41 in attorney fees.

The D.C. Circuit reversed solely on the nonstatutory labor exemption. It reasoned that lawful restraints imposed through collective bargaining and primarily affecting the labor market organized around that bargaining relationship were protected, including unilateral postimpasse imposition. It did not decide the defendants' remaining challenges. Judge Wald dissented from that appellate disposition. The appellate grounds and the unresolved other challenges matter to remedy if the exemption ground does not survive review; overturning that ground alone does not automatically adjudicate every remaining challenge to the damages, injunction and fees.
