### Stone’s Controlled Judgment

Affirm.

### Stone’s Disposition

The judgment of the Supreme Court of Alabama is affirmed.

The Court should not invalidate the $2 million punitive judgment on the ground that its magnitude is constitutionally excessive. Nor should this Court announce a federal ratio, numerical ceiling, or independent judicial measure of the punishment that would have been more appropriate.

This disposition does not hold that an appellate court possesses the same discretionary authority as a jury to select the amount of punitive damages. Alabama employed its established remittitur procedure, under which the plaintiff could accept the reduction or obtain further proceedings. Nothing in this case requires the Court to decide whether the original $4 million verdict could otherwise be reinstated, particularly where restoration would enlarge the respondent’s judgment beyond that entered by the Supreme Court of Alabama.

### Stone’s Proposed Holding

When state law entrusts assessment of punitive damages to a jury, judicial review protects the legality of that assessment but does not transfer the jury’s discretionary authority over punishment to the reviewing court.

A reviewing court may determine whether punitive damages were legally available; whether the jury was properly instructed; whether sufficient evidence supported punitive liability; whether the verdict rested upon legally impermissible considerations; whether statutory limits apply; and whether some recognized procedural defect requires a new trial or other lawful correction.

A court may also employ a lawful conditional remittitur procedure in which the prevailing party may accept a reduced judgment rather than undergo a new trial.

But where the party entitled to jury determination rejects that alternative, a reviewing court may not finally substitute its own preferred punitive amount for the amount entrusted by law to a jury. The lawful amount must instead be determined through the procedure that state law assigns to that task.

The magnitude of a punitive award, standing alone, does not constitute an independent violation of the Due Process Clause.

### Stone’s Reasoning

Punitive damages serve a function distinct from compensation. Compensation concerns the plaintiff’s loss. Punitive damages concern civil punishment and deterrence for conduct that the governing law has made punishable.

Where the law assigns that punitive judgment to a jury, the jury does not exercise unlimited power. Its authority exists within legal boundaries established before and during trial and remains subject to meaningful judicial review afterward.

The distinction is between reviewing a jury’s exercise of authority and assuming that authority for the court itself.

A court therefore may determine that the jury was permitted to award punitive damages at all. It may determine that legally insufficient evidence supported the award. It may correct an erroneous instruction. It may enforce an applicable statutory ceiling. It may conclude that the jury punished conduct outside the lawful scope of the case. It may identify prejudice, corruption, improper evidence, or another recognized defect requiring that the verdict be set aside.

Those are exercises of judicial authority because they determine the legal boundaries within which the jury must act.

The selection of a punitive amount within those lawful boundaries presents a different question.

Our prior cases do not establish a constitutional arithmetic of punitive damages. In simulated *TXO Production Corp. v. Alliance Resources Corp.*, the Court sustained a substantial punitive award after jury determination and meaningful judicial review, while no majority established a constitutional ratio or general substantive test governing the amount. The decision therefore supplies no rule under which an appellate court may derive the constitutionally correct punitive figure merely from the relationship between punitive and compensatory damages.

*Honda Motor Co. v. Oberg* likewise requires meaningful judicial examination of punitive awards. It does not establish a federal maximum, prescribe a permissible ratio, require remittitur, or authorize a reviewing court to choose the constitutionally proper amount. Its concern is that punitive awards remain subject to lawful judicial supervision rather than insulated from review altogether. The simulation’s existing rule expressly preserves meaningful review without establishing a fixed constitutional ratio or mandatory remittitur.

Those decisions can and should operate together.

The jury determines punishment where state law gives it that function. Courts determine whether the jury exercised that function lawfully.

Judicial review therefore remains meaningful without becoming a second assessment of punishment.

### Application to BMW

The original Alabama jury assessed $4 million in punitive damages.

The Alabama Supreme Court concluded that the verdict had been affected by the jury’s consideration of BMW transactions occurring outside Alabama and reduced the punitive judgment through Alabama’s remittitur procedure.

The distinction between evidence and punishment matters.

Conduct occurring elsewhere may in an appropriate case bear upon matters such as knowledge, deliberateness, intent, notice, or the existence of a common course of conduct. Its evidentiary relevance does not necessarily authorize Alabama to impose punishment for conduct that Alabama itself lacks lawful authority to punish.

A reviewing court may police that boundary.

If a punitive verdict actually rests upon conduct that the State may not lawfully punish, the court may identify that defect and require lawful correction. The court does not thereby acquire the jury’s discretionary authority to decide what amount of punishment would have been imposed had the forbidden consideration never entered the case.

Where an established remittitur procedure permits the prevailing party to choose between a reduced judgment and further jury proceedings, that procedure presents a materially different question from a court finally imposing its preferred amount over the objection of the party entitled to jury determination.

This case therefore does not require a categorical prohibition on remittitur.

It requires rejection of a different proposition: that the Due Process Clause itself empowers this Court to determine that the surviving $2 million judgment is simply too large and to constitutionalize our own assessment of proportionality.

### No Constitutional Ratio

The relationship between compensatory and punitive damages may provide useful information. It does not itself establish a constitutional fraction.

A large ratio may result from modest compensatory injury accompanying misconduct that the jury rationally regards as deserving substantial punishment. A smaller ratio may accompany grave compensatory loss arising from conduct warranting little or no punitive sanction.

The Constitution supplies no mathematical conversion between injury and punishment.

Likewise, legislatively prescribed civil or criminal sanctions may illuminate the legal treatment of particular conduct. But comparison with those sanctions does not transform them into a constitutional maximum unless governing law gives them that effect.

Reprehensibility, ratios, and comparable sanctions may therefore inform state-law review or other properly established legal inquiries. They are not independent sources of federal judicial power to select a different punitive amount.

### The Proper Division of Authority

The division is straightforward.

The legislature defines the substantive law and may establish statutory limits, ranges, prerequisites, or other lawful restrictions on punitive damages.

The trial court instructs the jury, controls the evidence, determines questions of law, and performs the post-verdict review required by governing law.

The jury, where state law entrusts the matter to it, determines the amount of punishment within those lawful boundaries.

Appellate courts review the legal integrity of that process.

None of those institutions possesses the authority of all the others.

A jury cannot disregard statutes or punish matters beyond the lawful case merely because it is the jury.

A judge cannot disregard the jury’s assigned function merely because the judge believes another number would be wiser.

### Conditional Remittitur

Nothing in this holding abolishes traditional remittitur.

A court may conclude under applicable law that a verdict cannot stand and condition denial of a new trial upon the prevailing party’s acceptance of a reduced judgment.

The important feature is the alternative.

If the prevailing party accepts the remittitur, the reduced judgment rests upon the lawful procedure governing the case and the party’s election to avoid a new trial.

If the party rejects the remittitur and remains entitled under governing law to jury determination of punitive damages, the remedy is further jury proceedings rather than a final judicial selection of the amount.

This distinction preserves judicial review without turning the reviewing judge into the factfinder entrusted with punitive assessment.

### Treatment of the Original $4 Million Verdict

This opinion does not order restoration of the original $4 million verdict.

Whether that judgment could properly be restored involves procedural questions distinct from BMW’s challenge to the judgment actually entered against it, including the scope of relief properly sought by the respondent.

The Court therefore has no need to decide whether the jury’s original award should have survived Alabama’s own review.

The question properly resolved here is narrower: whether the Federal Constitution requires this Court to invalidate the $2 million judgment because this Court independently considers that amount excessive.

It does not.

### Stone’s Operative Limit

This holding does not make punitive damages immune from judicial review.

It does not prevent courts from correcting erroneous jury instructions.

It does not protect an award unsupported by legally sufficient evidence.

It does not authorize punishment for conduct outside the lawful reach of the adjudicating State.

It does not prevent legislatures from establishing punitive-damages caps, ranges, predicates, or other substantive limitations.

It does not invalidate traditional conditional remittitur.

It does not decide the constitutional requirements applicable to punitive damages assessed directly by a judge, administrative tribunal, or another decisionmaker.

It does not establish a constitutional entitlement to jury determination of punitive damages in every State or every proceeding.

It does not prevent consideration of out-of-state conduct when that conduct is legally admissible for a purpose such as proving knowledge, intent, deliberateness, or a common course of conduct rather than imposing punishment for the foreign conduct itself.

And it does not establish that every punitive amount reached after adequate procedure is necessarily lawful.

The holding is narrower.

Where the governing law entrusts assessment of punitive damages to a jury, courts retain authority to police the legal boundaries of that assessment. They do not, merely by exercising review, acquire an independent constitutional authority to substitute their own judgment regarding the proper degree of punishment.

### Stone’s Character and Jurisprudential Disposition

Stone’s position is jury-respecting without making the jury supreme, and judicially restrained without making judicial review meaningless.

The jury must obey law.

The court must enforce law.

Neither institution should perform the other’s assigned function.

The position rejects two opposite extremes: unreviewable punitive verdicts on one side and appellate judicial sentencing of civil defendants on the other.

Its central principle is institutional rather than numerical.

Courts decide whether punishment was lawfully imposed. Where the law commits the degree of that punishment to a jury, the jury decides how much.
