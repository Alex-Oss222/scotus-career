## Findings and procedural boundaries

The trial court heard the case June 25–28, 1993 and signed its opinion October 1, 1993; the appended judgment was filed October 4, 1993. A different year in advocacy does not supersede the dated judicial document. The source descriptions also vary on the exact June 1988 resignation date; that day is not necessary to the controlled accommodation question and is not newly fixed here.

The district court found a sincere religious conflict with Friday-sunset through Saturday-sunset duty, notice to the employer and constructive discharge sufficient for the prima facie accommodation case. It found the release invalid; that distinct ruling is outside the submitted Supreme Court review, as the City acknowledges in its opposition. The pending merits question is the statutory accommodation/hardship judgment, not the validity of religion or the release.

The field-training program followed the academy and lasted sixteen weeks: an initial orientation and training phase, two further rotations and a final return. The program sought exposure to different officers, styles and geographic areas. The initial training-squad assignment was random despite known Sabbath needs. Apart from the first Saturday off, the district court found no accommodation other than the ordinary rotating schedule (Appendix 26a). The department's command officers testified that staying with a particular training squad without rotations would harm consistency and training; the City emphasized different trainers (19a). That testimony is not itself a judicial finding that every multiple-trainer alternative is infeasible or harmful.

The district court expressly could not find that transferring Beadle to another training shift would deprive another trainee of a neutral-scheduling benefit because the evidence did not establish a required swap at that stage (27a). It nevertheless relied on the continuous public-safety responsibilities of police work, its understanding that a permanent weekly exception need not be granted to the exclusion of other officers, and the neutral schedule itself as reasonable accommodation (26a–28a). The appellate opinion upheld the hardship ruling while recognizing that neutrality alone was insufficient. Its training rationale invokes Beadle's concession about losing exposure to different officers under a one-trainer proposal; the opposition/reply supplement establishes the limited scope of that concession.

The possible downtown assignment after training was a weekday assignment within commanders' discretion; the court described it as a possible accommodation, not a guaranteed vacancy or right (18a). The City's opposition says it was not a training squad. No immediate right to bypass training follows. The relevant collective agreement exempts assignments affected by training from seniority as a controlling consideration; the mere existence of the agreement does not establish a mandatory seniority obstacle here.

## Concrete proposed alternatives and their source status

The petition's **printed pages 25–26**, not Appendix 25a–26a, identify the following alternatives. The reply's shorthand citation to App.25a–26a does not itself establish the list; the actual appendix pages there concern legal standards and neutral scheduling. This resolves the citation uncertainty in the opposition/reply supplement.

1. Remain on Dawson's squad for sixteen weeks while receiving instruction from more than one qualified field-training officer. Petition pages 6–7 and 25, with Cook Dep. R2-66-43:22–44:10 and 126:18–129:20, claim that this would preserve training and not adversely affect the program. The reply expressly distinguishes multiple trainers within one squad from a single trainer throughout. Its concession that a single trainer loses educational benefit is not a concession concerning the multiple-trainer proposal.
2. Use qualified field-training officers assigned to non-training squads, including squads with Sabbath-compatible schedules, for additional training in relevant areas. The petition cites Cook Dep. R2-66-123:14–125:9, 117:7–119:7 and DX20. Its no-added-pay contention is advocacy, not a judicial finding that the proposal had no operational cost or was fully available.
3. Extend training to remedy actual deficits, using another trainer or area where needed. The petition cites existing four-to-eight-week extensions for other recruits, Cook Dep. R2-66-45:9–47:25 and Pegram Dep. R2-62-81:3–82:12. Comparable secular extensions bear on practice; they do not establish that every religious extension is costless or required.

These are supported party proposals and specified testimony references, not appellate findings of ultimate feasibility. Later adoption of a related program is cited in advocacy as evidence of feasibility. It is not proof that the later arrangement already operated in 1988, that every condition was the same or that this claimant had a guaranteed place. The appellate court's refusal to equate the later program with the original one does not resolve the separate contemporary multiple-trainer testimony.

Beadle accepts that tangible lost efficiency may be a cost and that proved inadequate training under the available alternatives can be hardship. The City acknowledges the arrangement arguably would not add money, while relying on training quality and safety. The dispute is not confined to cash. Ordinary fellow-worker resentment, hypothetical demands by unknown future claimants, and actual transferred burdens must not be treated as interchangeable.

## Source sufficiency

The actual lower opinions expose a legal-standard question independently of any unavailable transcript: whether the statutory burden was discharged by the general police-service rationale and the one-trainer concession without determining what the supported distinct alternatives would do to training or operations. The briefs supply the competing positions and precise concessions needed to evaluate that question. This permits a legally grounded remand if the model finds the lower inquiry deficient; it does not establish ultimate liability, mandate a proposed accommodation or authorize a remand merely to fill an operator's source gap.

The EEOC's March 30, 1990 finding favors accommodation; it is an agency charge determination, not a finding binding the trial court. The reproduced regulations are background agency guidance. Ansonia limits any suggestion that an employer must give the employee's preferred alternative after already providing an effective reasonable accommodation. No later hardship standard is available.
