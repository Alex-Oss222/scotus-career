## Good News — actual forum and appellate decree

The district court entered judgment for the district after a bench trial. The Eighth Circuit reversed and remanded for determination of a remedy consistent with its opinion (28 F.3d at 1511). The Court is reviewing that reversal and remedial remand; affirmance would preserve them, not affirm the district court's contrary judgment.

The 1992 policy permits community athletic activity and Scouts between 3 and 6 p.m., and forbids religious speech or activity even in the admitted Scout program. Other community groups, including this Club, may use the facilities after 6 p.m. and on the stated weekend schedule. The prior 1986 policy allowed community and student groups subject to application and space availability. An after-6 alternative is not the access the Club seeks.

The district court found both programs concerned with youth moral development. It also found the Scouts principally secular and skills-oriented and the Club principally concerned with Christian faith and values. Club meetings included prayer, Bible lessons, songs and activities. The appellate disagreement concerns the legal significance of those findings, including whether the comparison is an admitted subject or distinct activity. The Club's students were 11–15 years old. Parent volunteers led meetings, parental permission was required, and announcements disclaimed school sponsorship. A former board member had ceased involvement by adoption of the amended policy. The record does not establish school-directed worship or compulsory Club attendance. No proposition that all children always distinguish private from official speech is supplied.

The Eighth Circuit found the viewpoint claim presented in posttrial submissions and decided by the district court; it rejected waiver. The district court's no-hostility finding and the appellate prohibition on religious-viewpoint exclusion remain distinct. The Eighth Circuit did not decide the independent religion-clause claims. Relief sought is declaratory and injunctive, not an established damages award. The source supports nondiscriminatory access and a lower-court remedy inquiry, not a Supreme Court mandate reopening every activity throughout the day.

## Love — pleaded theory, conduct date and remedy

The alleged denial occurred in September 1990. The reported complaint truthfully disclosed the requested information; arrest entries lacked dispositions. The source reports that Love had one misdemeanor conviction, but that the denial was based on arrest history and the state court ordered approval. No disqualifying conviction is established by the packet. The appellate court affirmed dismissal of the federal action; Luttig concurred only under the circuit's entitlement precedent.

The pleaded claims are substantive due process, transactional liberty and a Second Amendment claim. The source expressly says no procedural-due-process claim was pleaded. The reported seven-day state review period and concern about incomplete arrest dispositions explain the challenged administrative practice; they do not make that practice lawful under Maryland law. Eventual approval does not erase a damages controversy. A state-created entitlement, a federal substantive right, an individual officer's responsibility, immunity, and the State's §1983 personhood remain separate questions.

The lower opinion's claim that every substantive injury must be beyond state-court rectification is a legal proposition for review, not an accepted fact or controlling simulated rule. Its collective-right account of the Second Amendment likewise is not imported as Court law. The damages action can be resolved without declaring a right to a particular firearm under federal regulation, adjudicating an unpleaded procedural theory, or invalidating Maryland's licensing system.

## SCLC — bounded challenge and independent lower grounds

The plaintiffs represented a statewide class, but this action challenged circuit-judge elections in ten judicial circuits: Fourth, Fifth, Sixth, Seventh, Tenth, Thirteenth, Fifteenth, Twentieth, Twenty-Third, and Twenty-Sixth. It also challenged district-judge elections in Jefferson, Mobile, Montgomery, and Russell Counties. The phrase "statewide challenge" in the original packet must not be read as review of every Alabama circuit or every state judicial office. The record identifies forty circuits in Alabama.

The district court rejected the claim after a bench trial. The en banc court affirmed on two independently stated grounds: failure of proof of unequal electoral opportunity, including the third Gingles condition, and lack of an appropriate feasible remedy even assuming dilution. The first rests on the trial court's treatment of judicial and nonjudicial races, actual minority preferences, party affiliation, incumbency and appointments; the second rests on jurisdiction/electorate linkage, candidate pools and practical judicial administration. Subsidiary historical findings were not challenged; the ultimate dilution conclusion and the remedy determination were challenged. Ordinary clear-error review of facts is distinct from review of their legal standard.

The majority's remedy discussion additionally infers racial partiality or damaged confidence from race-conscious subdistricting and invokes historical Shaw language. That reasoning is not governing Supreme Court law in this timeline. The actual simulated Shaw and Miller holdings supplied in the entering-law slice govern its examination. The source's absence-of-purpose findings do not add an intent requirement to §2.

The plaintiffs proposed recircuiting and subdistricting. Cumulative voting appears as a possible alternative in the dissent, expressly identified there as not proposed; it must not become a pleaded or preserved demand without further support. The dissent disputes the treatment of particular elections, special circumstances and practical alternatives, and would find liability. Those are competing analyses, not new Court findings. No all-circuit liability judgment, proportional seat entitlement, mandatory creation of offices, required adoption of a particular plan, or automatic injunction follows from this supplement.
