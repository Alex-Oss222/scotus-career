## Validated source framing

- **Case Name:** Auciello Iron Works, Inc. v. NLRB
- **Citation / Docket:** 517 U.S. 781; No. 95-668
- **October Term:** 1995
- **Entry Stage / Event Type:** Inventory-authorized merits review; decision pending in this simulation.
- **Question Presented:** As framed in the petition and Auciello’s merits brief: whether an employer is bound by a union’s “acceptance” of an earlier employer proposal for a collective-bargaining agreement when, at the time of the acceptance, the employer had a reasonable basis for a good-faith doubt of the union’s continued majority status and expressed that doubt within a reasonable period after being advised of the final erosion of employee support for the union, but less than one day after the union telegraphed its acceptance. As framed in the Board’s merits brief: whether the Board reasonably concluded that, after a previously certified union has accepted an employer’s offer for a collective-bargaining agreement, the employer cannot refuse to bargain with the union and withdraw recognition from it based on a good-faith doubt about the union’s majority status at the time of acceptance, when the asserted doubt is premised on events that occurred and were known to the employer before acceptance.
- **Authorized Review Scope:** The stated questions and the parties’ scope and preservation contentions govern the submitted merits review.
- **Legal Area:** Labor law — contract acceptance and union majority status
- **Court and Judgment Under Review:** The First Circuit (60 F.3d 24, decided July 21, 1995) granted the Board’s petition under §10(e) of the National Labor Relations Act, 29 U.S.C. §160(e), and enforced in its entirety the Board’s order requiring Auciello to cease and desist from the violations found, to put in writing, sign and effectuate, on the Union’s request and retroactive to November 27, 1988, the agreement the Union accepted on that date, and to make employees whole. After the Board’s supplemental decision on remand, 317 N.L.R.B. 364 (1995), the court, treating the Board’s policy judgment as entitled to substantial deference (citing *Fall River*, 482 U.S. at 42), accepted as reasonable the Board’s rule that, absent exceptional circumstances, an employer may not raise after the union’s acceptance of the employer’s outstanding offer a good-faith doubt of majority status premised on events before that acceptance, and it declined to condition enforcement on a Board-supervised election. Its earlier decision, 980 F.2d 804 (1992), sustained the Board’s findings that the November 17, 1988 offer remained open when accepted and that membership ratification was not required, but declined enforcement at that time, retained jurisdiction and remanded for the Board to explain its refusal to consider Auciello’s majority-status defense.
- **Requested Supreme Court Disposition / Relief:** Auciello asks the Court to reverse the First Circuit’s judgment and remand to the Board for a determination whether, when it received the Union’s telegram, Auciello maintained a good-faith doubt of the Union’s majority support (Pet. Br. 37). The Board asks that the judgment be affirmed (Board Br. 45), on its rule or, alternatively, because Auciello had a reasonable period to act in any event (Board Br. 42–45). Any decision must stay within the presented issues and source-authorized remedy.
- **Material Facts and Record:** The Board certified Shopmen’s Local No. 501 (the Union) in 1977 as representative of a unit including the production and maintenance employees at Auciello’s Hudson, Massachusetts shop (about 23 employees in 1988), and the parties made successive agreements. The most recent expired on September 25, 1988; the employees struck on October 14, 1988, and bargaining continued. On November 17, 1988, Auciello presented oral and written contract proposals; the Union neither accepted nor rejected them, and Auciello did not withdraw them. Picketing stopped on November 18. On Sunday, November 27, the Union telegraphed that its negotiating committee had ratified the November 17 proposal in its entirety and that the strike had ended. On November 28 Auciello replied that the offer had been rejected and was no longer open, that ratification by the committee alone was insufficient, and that it had reason to believe the Union no longer represented a majority; it disavowed any obligation to negotiate further and refused to sign. Auciello based its asserted doubt on events preceding the acceptance, asserting that some nine unit employees had crossed the picket line, that as many as 13 had submitted resignations to the Union, and that 16 had indicated that they did not support it (980 F.2d at 809–10); before the Board, the General Counsel contended that this evidence lacked the objective basis a good-faith doubt requires, and Auciello contended the contrary (Pet. App. 18a–19a). The administrative law judge and the Board (303 N.L.R.B. 562 (1991)) found that the offer was open when accepted and that no membership ratification was required, held that the binding agreement made it unnecessary to consider Auciello’s good-faith doubt, and made no finding whether the Union actually lacked majority support. On remand the Board adhered to its rule that where objective evidence supporting a doubt is known to the employer before the union’s acceptance but not acted on, the acceptance creates a valid agreement; it found it unnecessary to decide whether Auciello’s evidence sufficed to support a reasonable good-faith doubt, and it reserved case-by-case treatment for extraordinary cases in which the doubt arises simultaneously with acceptance. Relying on the testimony of Auciello’s vice president—who described a November 28 management review, after the telegram, of each employee’s union sentiment, but also acknowledged that management had prepared a list of employees likely to vote against union representation toward the end of the strike and took no action on it because negotiations were ongoing and the list represented a “worst case scenario”—the Board found that Auciello harbored its doubt while its offer was outstanding (Pet. App. 35a–36a). The parties read the timing differently. Auciello says that its earlier indications (some picket-line crossings and resignations, earlier expressions of dissatisfaction by one or two employees, and a few replacements) were an inadequate basis as of November 17, and that the final erosion of support surfaced on and after Friday, November 18, when the picket line came down, and during the Thanksgiving-shortened week of November 21—returning employees’ statements of disaffection, further resignations, and reports from apparent Union supporters that they had taken permanent jobs elsewhere—so that it was still assessing those events when the Sunday-evening telegram arrived (Pet. Br. 5–7, 29–31 & n.8). The Board says that Auciello had received most or all of its evidence by Monday, November 21: the picket line came down November 18, 12 of the 13 resignation forms are dated on or before November 21 and the other November 23, the vice president testified that many expressions of dissatisfaction came on or before November 18, and four employees reported taking jobs elsewhere by November 18 (Board Br. 43–44). Auciello argues that an employer should be entitled to raise its doubt as to the union’s majority status as of the date of acceptance; that a presumption of majority status must rest on a lawfully established relationship, so that an agreement accepted by a union lacking majority support cannot create one consistently with §8(a)(2), employees’ §7 rights and *International Ladies’ Garment Workers’ Union v. NLRB*, 366 U.S. 731 (1961) (*Garment Workers*), and that the presumption in the 1988 negotiations rested on the expired agreement rather than the 1977 certification, none of the 1988 employees having been employed in 1977; that because an employer acting prematurely on a doubt risks unfair-labor-practice liability, while one that keeps dealing with a minority union risks violating §8(a)(2), it should at least have a reasonable period to assess the erosion and voice its doubt, which it lacked before the acceptance; and that deference is unwarranted where the Board’s rule rests on an erroneous legal foundation or is inconsistent with the Act. The Board argues that its rule is a rational policy choice consistent with the Act and entitled to deference; that the rule keeps an employer from sitting on a known doubt and raising it only if the agreement later proves unfavorable, gives a newly formed agreement stability, and avoids post hoc litigation of an unarticulated doubt; that the employer controls whether and when to assert its doubt and may voice it, withdraw its offer or petition for an election before acceptance, while employees could have sought decertification between September 25 and November 27, 1988; that *Garment Workers* involved initial recognition of a union that actually lacked majority support, whereas this certified Union’s presumption was unchallenged at acceptance; and, alternatively, that even if an employer is entitled to a reasonable period, Auciello had one.
- **Preservation / Threshold / Vehicle Issues:** The petition’s question is framed in terms of good-faith doubt. The 1992 remand opinion spoke of an employer’s evidence that the Union lacked majority support at acceptance (980 F.2d at 813), but the Board’s supplemental decision (Pet. App. 17a–18a) states that Auciello never urged before the Board or the court an actual loss of majority support and that no finding addresses whether the Union actually lacked majority status at acceptance; the Board’s merits brief invokes §10(e), 29 U.S.C. §160(e), against consideration of any actual-minority claim (Board Br. 35 n.12). Auciello’s merits brief argues that the Board’s rule wrongly renders immaterial its evidence of the Union’s actual lack of majority status. The Board’s merits brief states that the Board takes no position on whether it would entertain a post-acceptance claim that a union actually lacked majority support when the agreement was formed, where that claim rests on pre-acceptance events known to the employer (Board Br. 26 n.7). The First Circuit agreed with the Board’s implicit conclusion that this case did not fall within the Board’s exception for doubt arising simultaneously with acceptance (60 F.3d at 27 n.2); the Board argues that Auciello does not, and could not here, challenge the finding that its evidence existed and was known before acceptance (Board Br. 41). Neither the Board nor the First Circuit decided whether Auciello’s evidence would support a good-faith doubt, and the Board does not concede its sufficiency (Board Br. 42 n.15). Otherwise, ordinary preservation, finality and remedy questions apply.

## Legal frame and distinctions that control the review

The lawful channel is §1254(1) review of the First Circuit's final §10(e) enforcement judgment. The renewed good-faith-doubt dispute does not reopen the sustained findings that the November 17 offer remained outstanding and that negotiation-committee acceptance required no membership ratification. A contract-law acceptance finding is not a finding of actual majority support or a resolution of the asserted NLRA consequence of known contrary evidence.

NLRA §7 protects employees' concerted representation choices and their right to refrain, subject to the Act's qualifications. Sections 8(a)(1), (2) and (5) respectively address interference with protected rights, employer support/domination and refusal of collective bargaining; §8(b)(1)(A) separately addresses union restraint/coercion. Section 8(d)'s bargaining duty requires good-faith meeting and conferring and, upon request, execution of an agreement reached, but does not compel agreement to a proposal or a concession. Section 9's majority-representation principle and §9(c)(1)'s employee/employer election routes inform the parties' arguments without establishing an actual election petition here. Section 10(e) prevents appellate consideration of an objection not urged before the Board unless extraordinary circumstances excuse the omission. The Board invokes that bar against actual-minority litigation; no qualifying extraordinary circumstance is supplied. An unpreserved actual-minority claim is not made preserved merely by litigating good-faith doubt.

Keep the temporal presumptions separate. Certification ordinarily carries a conclusive first-year presumption, subject to unusual circumstances. A valid collective agreement supports a limited contract-period conclusive presumption, ordinarily no more than three years, with the qualifications recognized by the governing Board/court law. After expiration the incumbent's presumption is rebuttable by the legally adequate routes then available. The 1977 certification does not independently make majority status conclusive in November 1988. The dispute is whether acceptance of the still-open offer initiates the protected contract period despite a doubt based on earlier known evidence. It is not resolved by assuming the very lawful-agreement premise the employer contests.

The earlier-known-evidence rule reserves extraordinary cases in which doubt arises simultaneously with acceptance. The First Circuit accepted the Board's implicit finding that this case is outside that exception. Neither court nor Board found adequate objective good-faith doubt or actual minority status; the Board expressly does not concede sufficiency. Membership resignation, picket-line crossing, taking other jobs and opposition to representation are not identical. The documents' dates support the Board's timing argument but do not establish what all employees wanted or conclusively answer the employer's claim about a reasonable assessment period during the holiday week. The scope of unusual circumstances during an already existing contract period remains distinct from the simultaneous-arising exception at initial acceptance. The authorized [bounded presumption extraction](../sources/chunk7_b/AUCIELLO_PRESUMPTION_SAFE_EXTRACT.md), ea321a0, supplies the Board's illustrative p.18 n.1 examples: a certified union dissolves or becomes defunct, or substantially all its members and officers transfer affiliation to another local/international because of a schism. The words “such as” prevent treating these as an exhaustive list. The brief's assertion that none occurred here remains advocacy; no new factual finding is created.

The Board's supplemental decision, Pet.App.23a, separately preserves the representation/decertification election window more than 60 but less than 90 days before expiration of an agreement of three years or less, with different treatment for healthcare and seasonal operations. That petition route does not itself authorize unilateral refusal to bargain during a contract. Its Pet.App.54a–55a reservation addresses employees first presenting signed anti-representation petitions virtually simultaneously with acceptance and calls for case-by-case treatment, rather than converting earlier-known information later assessed into contemporaneous evidence. The same passage states the agency's separate principle that an employer and union aware of an actual loss of majority may not continue bargaining, while expressly stating this case alleges no actual loss. This agency distinction preserves the employee-choice objection's possible lawful setting without deciding or finding that hypothetical setting here. Board merits p.19 n.2 distinguishes a numerical showing of actual lack of representational support from circumstantial evidence available to establish a sufficient objective good-faith doubt.
