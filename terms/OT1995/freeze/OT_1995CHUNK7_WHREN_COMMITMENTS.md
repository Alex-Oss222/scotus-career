These are common rationale commitments, not merely agreement in the judgment. None requires a categorical rule that every probable-cause seizure is reasonable, that police policy can never supply relevant evidence, or that motives are irrelevant to every constitutional claim. No separate writing is necessary if the eventual opinion stays within these propositions. Broader formulations require renewed consideration of the join limits below.

Scott's objective assessment supplies the principal legal approach. Prouse supplies the important qualification: discretionary vehicle restraint without an adequate individualized basis is constitutionally different from intervention founded on observed traffic violations. Robinson reinforces the distinction between an objectively justified intrusion and the officer's additional investigative interest, within its own lawful-arrest/search setting. Neither case itself eliminates execution limits.

Petitioners offer an objective would-have test, not a rule invalidating every mixed-motive stop. Their strongest argument concerns selection where ubiquitous minor offenses permit officers to choose nearly anyone. That concern is substantial. But making constitutional permission depend on customary enforcement propensity would substitute variable priorities for the established individualized justification. The test would require identifying the relevant practice and deciding how departures, assignments and exceptional danger alter the counterfactual, even though the observed conduct supplies probable cause. Its objective formulation does not establish why that additional condition follows from the governing ordinary-stop authorities.

Bertine and Wells do not already answer that question for petitioners. Standardized inventory criteria and purpose-related limits perform legal work where the inventory justification, rather than individualized probable cause of the offense, supplies the authority. An inventory conducted as an investigatory ruse cannot simply borrow that exception. Preserving those limits does not require transferring inventory conditions wholesale to this observed-violation stop. Prouse likewise constrains suspicionless discretion without making typical enforcement practice the measure of every individualized traffic seizure.

General Order 303.1 is therefore addressed without adjudicating compliance. Its ordinary assignment of traffic enforcement to uniformed officers in marked vehicles or the designated unit is qualified by violations so grave as to pose an immediate threat to others' safety; its warning provisions also have their actual qualifications. The order is not an unqualified prohibition. Neither departure from the ordinary assignment nor satisfaction of the safety exception is found here. The accepted unreasonable-speed observation does not automatically establish that exception. Because policy compliance is not the selected constitutional prerequisite, this unresolved application does not require remand.

Wilson remains fully effective. Lawful authority to act and constitutional execution are different questions. The accepted account describes officers approaching an already stopped vehicle, directing it to park and then observing drugs. The packet identifies no independently sufficient, preserved unusual-manner ground on which to invalidate that initial restraint. This conclusion does not turn an omitted duration or force detail into an affirmative factual finding. The later search remains outside the independently challenged question.

Ornelas does not itself settle pretext. Its historical-fact protection and genuine contextual due weight are real, while the ultimate constitutional judgment remains independent. The neutral already treats the credited traffic observations as supplying probable cause. Neither independent review nor police experience authorizes inventing another violation, antecedent drug probable cause, a safety-exception finding or a conflict requiring a hearing. Resolving the legal choice here under the controlling allocation supplies no reason to return the case merely to restate that allocation.

## Adversarial alternative-path assessment

### Path 1 — ordinary-stop probable cause with independent limits: selected by all eight

**Strongest opposition:** Because minor traffic violations are pervasive, an ordinary probable-cause rule can leave selection heavily discretionary. Petitioners' objective would-have proposal seeks an administrable external limit using practice, assignments and safety, rather than invalidating every mixed motive. Prouse and the inventory cases show that objectively lawful-looking conduct can require limits on discretion. Wilson shows that authority alone is not the whole reasonableness inquiry.

**Resolution:** All eight accept those descriptions but distinguish the legal work performed by the proposed limits. Prouse addresses restraint without the individualized basis present here. Inventory standards define use of a purpose-limited exception; they are preserved in that field. Wilson preserves actual execution review, which the selected rule also preserves. None makes typical enforcement propensity an additional prerequisite to the ordinary seizure on these established violations. The common rationale therefore answers the real objective proposal and confines its rejection to the submitted traffic-stop setting.

**Remedy and coalition:** The supplied probable-cause predicate and factual protections permit affirmance without a new safety-exception or motive finding. Each Justice directly supports the same sufficient rule and remedy. The result does not depend on aggregating an equal-protection rationale with a Fourth Amendment rationale.

### Path 2 — objective would-have restriction: legally developed but not selected

**Strongest affirmative case:** Enforcement norms can supply objective evidence that a traffic infraction is being used as an otherwise arbitrary entry point into a criminal investigation. Applying a reasonable-officer traffic-purpose standard could extend the anti-ruse concerns of Prouse, Bertine and Wells while avoiding an inquiry that turns entirely on an officer's denial of improper intent. Stevens's Wells position supplies the strongest individual reason to examine this extension; O'Connor's regulated-inventory joins give it an additional concrete analogue.

**Strongest opposition:** The test must explain why this additional condition governs an observed-violation stop and how constitutional authority is distinguished from local assignments and priorities. A practice-based standard cannot treat every departure as unconstitutional, because the General Order is qualified and enforcement circumstances differ. Constitutional concern about arbitrary selection does not itself establish the test's operative trigger, content or remedial result.

**Justice-specific resolution:** Stevens retains Scott and Prouse's individualized-basis distinction and does not generalize his Wells objection. O'Connor confines standardized inventory conditions to their justification. Scalia and Kennedy preserve the same authority distinction and independent execution limits. Souter and Ginsburg insist on addressing the selection concern but find no supplied rule making enforcement propensity dispositive. Thomas's Wilson rationale concerns actual entry execution rather than this additional prerequisite. Breyer finds the proposed variable counterfactual insufficiently grounded as a constitutional condition. None selects this path; its rejection rests on those legal distinctions, not merely a claim that petitioners are asking for a subjective test.

**Remedy if selected instead:** The present packet does not establish whether the credited conduct satisfies the General Order's immediate-safety exception or whether the proposed reasonable-officer test is otherwise resolved. Selection of this path would require a bounded determination of its actual unresolved application and an appropriately limited remand if necessary. It would not justify assuming a policy violation, ordering automatic suppression, vacating every charge, reopening the later search, or repeating the completed sentencing correction. No such factual premise is silently supplied to preserve an affirmance under this unselected path.

### Path 3 — distinct initial-manner ground: preserved in principle, unsupported as an alternative disposition here

**Strongest affirmative case:** Wilson defeats a categorical contention that probable cause alone settles every aspect of a seizure. A properly preserved initial-restraint objection based on supported unusual force, duration or manner would require a separate reasonableness assessment even if the traffic basis were sound.

**Strongest opposition and resolution:** The final neutral supplies no distinct, sufficiently established execution objection capable of changing this judgment. The officers' plainclothes assignment and investigative aim do not themselves establish unreasonable force, duration or manner. All eight preserve the legal possibility without inventing the decisive facts. Reliance on an exact disputed execution detail would require a bounded source extract before it could support a different commitment. This is not an automatic reopening of factfinding or an independently available favorable disposition on the present packet.

### Separate equal-protection route: reserved, not an alternative Fourth Amendment judgment

Purposeful racial enforcement remains constitutionally objectionable on its own terms. Soto denied racial motivation, and the district judge credited him; this handoff preserves that procedural status without making a new discrimination or neutrality finding. Kimberlin protects legally competent circumstantial proof where motive is an element. Armstrong remains an actual federal charging/discovery decision with a preliminary effect-and-purpose showing, attainable evidentiary alternatives and an unresolved remedy for proved discrimination. Its conditions do not automatically govern police-stop litigation.

All eight refuse to treat availability of a distinct constitutional theory as either proof of its elements or proof that suppression, discovery, damages or another remedy will follow. No separate equal-protection claim is adjudicated here, no burden is invented, and no supposed adequacy of another remedy is necessary to the selected Fourth Amendment ground. Stevens's Armstrong discretion theory and Breyer's different Rule 16 ground remain accurately separate; neither is converted into a controlling Whren procedure.
