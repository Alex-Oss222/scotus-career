## Exact scope, reservations and remedy

Rule501's contemporary first sentence gives federal courts the task of interpreting common-law privilege through reason and experience, subject to constitutional, congressional and duly prescribed rule requirements. Its second sentence continues to require state privilege law for an element of a civil claim or defense governed by state law. This case's recognition of a federal privilege makes it unnecessary to decide the disputed rule for evidence relevant to both federal and state claims when the two privilege laws conflict. That mixed-claim choice remains unresolved; the appellate treatment of this evidence is not a new universal federal-preemption rule.

The seven treat confidentiality as essential to frank diagnosis and treatment and identify public as well as private treatment benefits. State experience corroborates that judgment without supplying a uniform national list of providers or exceptions. Proposed Rule504 is evidence of considered professional judgment, not enacted governing text or an automatic answer to social-worker coverage. Clinical social work serves the same treatment function in this case, including access for persons unable to afford other providers. The holding is not a general physician-patient privilege, a privilege for all workplace counseling, or protection for a social worker's administrative conversations.

The strongest contrary case is the loss of reliable evidence concerning a disputed killing and the varied boundaries in state statutes. The seven answer that Rule501 permits a sufficiently justified confidential relationship and that uncertainty about later evidentiary need can deter the communications the relationship requires. They reject routine balancing within that category, while leaving future contours for concrete cases. Scalia finds that response insufficient, emphasizing both the general truth-seeking cost and the particular professional-coverage difficulties; his objection supplies no alternative controlling balancing rule.

Patient waiver remains possible, but partial responses and three redacted pages do not establish wholesale waiver here. No comprehensive waiver test or litigation-use exception is decided. The privilege is not exceptionless: the historical serious-threat qualification is retained as a reserved example—where a serious threat of harm to the patient or others can be averted only by therapist disclosure, the privilege must yield. No such threat is found here, and the decision establishes no general dangerous-patient procedure, retrospective evidentiary-need exception, or rule resolving criminal constitutional claims. Each of these reservations is distinct from the rejected routine balancing test.

Affirmance sustains the Seventh Circuit's reversal and new-trial remand; it does not reinstate the $45,000 federal and $500,000 state verdicts. The operative trial error was the adverse-inference instruction coupled with the assertion that withholding the notes lacked legal justification. The earlier exclusion of Redmond's shooting testimony had already been vacated. No new shooting finding, actual compelled disclosure of all notes, comprehensive waiver adjudication or reopening of the separately rejected deadly-force instruction challenge follows. Ordinary trial proceedings remain subject to the recognized privilege and proper independent grounds.
