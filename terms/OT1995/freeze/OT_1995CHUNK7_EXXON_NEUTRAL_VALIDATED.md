## Established findings, assumptions, and disputes

The tanker broke from its mooring during severe conditions. A connected hose remained a concern, but the district court found that by 1830 the ship had reached a safe position from which it could continue backing out. The master failed to plot fixes for an extended period, failed to use available personnel, and began the final shoreward turn without knowing the vessel's position. It characterized the subsequent navigation as grossly or extraordinarily negligent, independent and unforeseeable, and the sole proximate cause of the stranding. The Ninth Circuit sustained that analysis. JA143–160, 167–176, and 217–233 are the identified finding/review passages.

Exxon argues that grounding remained within the risk created by the breakout, that the legal consequences of these facts were mistaken, and that excluding pre-breakout proof impaired that assessment. Its argument must be stated; it does not replace the findings. Rule 52 clear-error limits apply to historical findings. Both lower courts' concurrence and HIRI's contention that Exxon's limited factual challenge was abandoned here matter. Legal causation, historical events, individual breach, and allocation of responsibility must not be collapsed into one question.

For the limited first trial phase, the parties assumed or conceded **collective cause in fact**. The now-read authorized `sources/chunk7_c/JUNE10_BOUNDED_RECORD_EXTRACTS.md` at **994a3b0** sharpens the premise: JA162 relieves Exxon from proving defendants' fault or strict liability for the breakout in Phase One and expressly treats the breakout as a factual cause. This does not prove that each respondent breached a duty or warranty, admit every respondent's negligence, or allocate breakout responsibility. A later phase could address those matters. JA72–75 provides the trial-phase context without enlarging review to a free-standing bifurcation challenge. No Supreme Court percentage allocation is presently established.

Contract content is independently material. The 5dc889b safe extract preserves this readable two-sentence quotation from the district court's finding 8, petition App.32–33, corroborated by petitioners' merits brief 10 n.11 (citing JA142–143):

> The terminal shall provide a safe berth to which vessels may proceed to or depart from, and where the vessel can always lie safely afloat. However, notwithstanding anything contained in this clause, the terminal shall not be deemed to warrant the safety of public channels, fairways, approaches thereto, anchorages, or other publicly-maintained areas either inside or outside the port area where the vessel may be directed.

Finding 9 records the April 4, 1988 amendment stating that the quoted provision includes single point mooring; that amended provision was in effect on March 2, 1989. **The express exclusion covers public channels, fairways, their approaches, anchorages, and other publicly maintained areas, inside or outside the port.** Its application to the particular alleged breach or grounding is not established merely by quoting it, and no finding that the reef or accident location satisfies an excluded category is invented. The district court introduces the quotation as the agreement “in relevant part.” It is not certified as the complete executed agreement, complete original paragraph, or every applicable risk-allocation term. It contains no express due-diligence wording and no express assignment of all later navigational losses.

The 994a3b0 and 5dc889b extracts establish that JA109–112 reserved which related corporation was bound, and held that possible earlier supersession did not forgive a subsequent breach: the duty remained operative after breakout. They do not adjudicate every defendant's individual contractual liability.

**The later no-breach conclusion has an essential legal qualification:** JA162–166 expressly considers Exxon's strict-warranty construction and HIRI's due-diligence construction, selects the latter, and then rejects claimed defects in mooring-master assistance, tug assistance, and current information under that selected standard. The no-breach conclusions therefore cannot silently defeat a challenge asserting a legally stricter promise. They also contain causal findings: one competent mooring master was available, failure to consult him and use available ship's officers mattered, and the vessel could reach safety without the additional tug. Any warranty disposition must distinguish those supported factual/causal conclusions from the disputed construction of duty. The Ninth Circuit expressly reserved the strict-warranty-versus-due-diligence choice, reasoning that even assumed strict warranty liability would not defeat its superseding-cause analysis. Its footnote states that Exxon did not dispute compliance with due diligence in all respects. That concession does not concede compliance with a stricter promise, and the reservation is not appellate adoption of the district court's standard.

The indemnification/pilotage terms come from a **separate instrument**, General Instructions, Discharging/Loading Orders and Indemnification, identified in JA166 conclusion 21 as **Joint Trial Exhibit 92**. ## Actual arguments on the two submitted components

**Tort/comparative fault.** Exxon argues that Reliable Transfer displaced all-or-nothing allocation and requires contribution from earlier negligent causes. Alternatively, even if superseding cause survives, the grounding is within the foreseeable risk created by the earlier breaches; Restatement §442B and Kinsman are invoked against treating intervening navigation as independent. Respondents distinguish factual contribution from legal cause and comparative allocation. Their best formulation is that genuinely independent extraordinary conduct can terminate the earlier legal causal chain while comparative fault allocates losses among causes that remain legally operative.

The degree of the captain's fault alone cannot silently answer independence, foreseeability, or the scope of the original risk. Reliable Transfer's criticism of the major-minor doctrine directly presses respondents to distinguish a true legal-cause cutoff from merely excusing a less negligent actor because the captain was worse. Conversely, comparative allocation's rejection of gross disparities does not by itself prove that every factual condition remains a legal cause indefinitely.

**Contract/warranty.** Exxon separately invokes strict liability, the express safe-berth undertaking, implied workmanlike service, Italia's allocation toward the party able to prevent the defect, and mitigation as reduction of damages rather than elimination of contractual liability. A warranty need not be identical to a negligence duty. Respondents answer within the actual contractual risk and causation setting; HIRI also invokes **avoidable consequences** and **foreseeability of contract damages**, arguing that the supported findings defeat stranding recovery even if earlier duty and breach are assumed. Those are independent contract arguments, not alternative labels for tort supersession. The appellate court's assumption of strict warranty supplies a narrower possible route: the standard-of-duty dispute may remain unresolved if a legally sound and contractually applicable causal or damage ground disposes of the submitted warranty claim. Conversely, due-diligence compliance alone cannot supply that disposition while the stricter contractual theory remains unanswered. Neither side is credited with an unquoted allocation of navigational risk; the express public-area exclusion remains part of any clause-specific analysis.

The safe handoff identifies Exxon's merits brief 19–22, 29–37; HIRI brief 32–42; Sofec brief 3 and 11 n.7; reply 15–17. HIRI argues the bifurcation/due-process issue is outside the submitted questions; Exxon answers that the exclusion of pre-breakout proof bears on the causation error. This dispute may inform whether the submitted issues are decidable on the supported record. It does not authorize a free-standing disposition of bifurcation.

## Entering law and authority limits

**Reliable Transfer, 421 U.S.397 (1975).** The unanimous pre-divergence decision requires proportional allocation where parties' fault contributes to maritime collision or stranding damage, with equal division if fault is equal or cannot fairly be measured. Its criticism of the major-minor escape from equal division means that a party's predominantly greater negligence does not, merely by degree, absolve another contributing wrongdoer. Whether a genuinely superseding cause remains distinct from that disfavored allocation device is the present dispute. Reliable Transfer did not itself decide the particular safe-berth or workmanlike-service warranty.

**Italia Societa, 376 U.S.315 (1964).** The identified official source concerns implied maritime workmanlike service and nonnegligently defective stevedore equipment in its own causal and warranty setting. It demonstrates that contractual responsibility can differ from negligence. Its loss-prevention rationale does not automatically assign every remote loss to a warranty defendant or remove actual contractual and causal limitations.

**Actual McDermott and Boca Grande, April 20, 1994.** Stevens's Court opinions, joined by O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, and Ginsburg among the then-full Court, require reliable proportionate settlement credit and define contribution protection accordingly. The Standards and Tests entry is **Maritime settlement credit and contribution protection**, present operation effective Boca Grande, April 20, 1994. Meaningful opportunity to litigate and reliable allocation are premises of contribution protection. Settlement risk remains distinct from insolvency and surviving joint liability. Those rules neither establish the respondents' legal responsibility here nor abolish all contractual protection or causation limits.

**Actual City of Milwaukee, June 12, 1995.** Stevens's eight-participant opinion, joined by O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, and Ginsburg, ordinarily compensates loss of use through prejudgment interest on an otherwise compensable amount; ordinary liability uncertainty and mutual fault already reflected in principal do not themselves justify denial. The Standards and Tests entry is **Compensatory maritime prejudgment interest**, present operation effective June 12, 1995. It presupposes a compensable loss and does not remove liability predicates. Breyer is not attributed a join in this eight-participant decision.

**Actual Yamaha, January 9, 1996, and Zicherman, January 16, 1996.** O'Connor's opinions, joined by all other non-Stone Justices in the material portions, respect actual statutory/remedial fields: supplementary territorial-waters remedies do not enlarge DOHSA's occupied field, and treaty liability conditions do not themselves supply every damages category. Yamaha expressly reserves liability standards and causation. Its §1292(b) rule concerns issues fairly within a certified order and supplies no expansion of Supreme Court certiorari question scope. Neither decision determines this superseding-cause or warranty question.

The 1995 Court Rules 14.1(a)/24.1(a), identified in the safe handoff's official Rules source, preserve submitted-question limits; permissive consideration of plain error is not routine authority to reopen an issue excluded by this task's supplied scope. No rule of factual deference shields a mistaken legal definition from legal review, and no legal review permits silently replacing supported historical findings.
