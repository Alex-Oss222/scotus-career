## Noland — May 13, 1996; No. 95-323

### Record and scope

The reviewed Sixth Circuit decision is *In re First Truck Lines, Inc.*, 48 F.3d 210 (1995), reproduced in Government petition Appendix A, printed 1a–22a. The entire opinion and Batchelder concurrence were read. Petition Appendices D–E reproduce the district and bankruptcy opinions; the parties' merits briefs quote the precise material rationale. First Truck filed Chapter 11 April 10, 1986; failed to pay postpetition FICA/FUTA taxes; its case converted to Chapter 7 August 1, 1988; Noland became trustee. Liquidation was insufficient to pay all creditors. IRS Claim 102 sought taxes, interest, and penalties; an amendment added a FUTA penalty. Taxes and interest retain administrative priority. The disputed nonpecuniary tax penalties are allowed administrative expenses under §503(b)(1)(C), but were subordinated to general unsecured claims. There is no IRS misconduct finding.

**Correction to the shorthand packet:** the Sixth Circuit expressly said its decision did not require automatic subordination of every tax penalty (20a), and purported to affirm case-specific equitable balancing. Its decisive reasons were the noncompensatory character of this claim, full priority for the Government's tax/interest losses, insufficient assets, and the loss borne by creditors who supplied value. The Government's challenge is that those are category-wide reasons that displace Congress's ordering, notwithstanding the lower courts' balancing terminology. A neutral presentation must preserve both that objection and the lower court's express disclaimer.

The Government seeks reversal of the subordination judgment. The trustee seeks affirmance. Merits briefing supports both a broad misconduct prerequisite dispute and a narrower question whether the reasons actually given lawfully subordinate this priority penalty. The question can be answered without declaring every form of no-fault equitable subordination valid or invalid. No factual misconduct theory may be invented as an alternative ground. A remand does not itself determine the final distribution or erase the admitted tax/interest priority.

### Text that performs legal work

Official source: 1994 Title 11 copies `code11_348`, `code11_503`, `code11_507`, `code11_510`, `code11_726`, downloaded from GPO/GovInfo. Case-era 1988 provisions also appear in the Government petition, Appendix F, 56a–58a. The prepetition-tax cross-reference then was §507(a)(7); the 1994 Code renumbered it §507(a)(8). Do not retroactively use the renumbering to change the claim treatment.

- Section 510(c), complete: “Notwithstanding subsections (a) and (b) of this section, after notice and a hearing, the court may— (1) under principles of equitable subordination, subordinate for purposes of distribution all or part of an allowed claim to all or part of another allowed claim or all or part of an allowed interest to all or part of another allowed interest; or (2) order that any lien securing such a subordinated claim be transferred to the estate.” The qualifications, permissive authority, allowed-claim requirement, and distribution limitation are part of the text. Section 510(a) enforces applicable nonbankruptcy subordination agreements; (b) prescribes securities-claim subordination. Neither supplies an independently established route here.
- Section 503(b)'s opening excludes claims allowed under §502(f) and requires notice and hearing. Section 503(b)(1)(B) covers “any tax—(i) incurred by the estate, except a tax of a kind specified in section 507(a)(7) of this title; or (ii) attributable to an excessive allowance of a tentative carryback adjustment that the estate received, whether the taxable year to which such adjustment relates ended before or after the commencement of the case”; (C) covers “any fine, penalty, or reduction in credit relating to a tax of a kind specified in subparagraph (B) of this paragraph.” Administrative allowance is distinct from equitable subordination.
- Case-era §507(a)(1): “First, administrative expenses allowed under section 503(b) of this title, and any fees and charges assessed against the estate under chapter 123 of title 28.”
- Case-era §726(a) begins “Except as provided in section 510 of this title, property of the estate shall be distributed—”; paragraph (1) first pays claims of the kind and in the order specified in §507. Paragraph (2) next pays qualifying allowed unsecured claims except (1), (3), and (4), subject to its timely-proof and no-notice/tardy-proof qualifications. Paragraph (3) addresses other tardily filed unsecured claims. Paragraph (4) fourth pays “any allowed claim, whether secured or unsecured, for any fine, penalty, or forfeiture, or for multiple, exemplary, or punitive damages, arising before the earlier of the order for relief or the appointment of a trustee, to the extent that such fine, penalty, forfeiture, or damages are not compensation for actual pecuniary loss suffered by the holder of such claim.” Paragraphs (5) and (6) next address legal-rate interest and return to debtor. The temporal qualification in (4) cannot be omitted to place all penalties there by statute.
- Section 348(d) treats post-order/preconversion claims as prepetition, **other than** §503(b) claims. Conversion therefore does not itself eliminate the administrative status established here.

### Supported alternatives and adverse arguments

1. **Enforce the priority against the reasons used below; reserve other no-misconduct cases.** Ahlers, 485 U.S. 197, 206, requires equity within the Code. Congress distinguished postpetition tax penalties from the temporally defined §726(a)(4) class. Subordinating because a penalty compensates no pecuniary loss merely repeats the classification Congress knew when granting priority. Strong contrary argument: §726 expressly makes all distribution subject to §510, and §510 refers to evolving equitable principles rather than expressly requiring misconduct. The narrow path must give that language real work instead of making administrative claims immune.
2. **Affirm the supported exercise of statutory equity.** Section 726's opening, §510(c)'s broad allowed-claim language, pre-Code treatment of penalties, and the Edwards/DeConcini legislative statements support susceptibility without government wrongdoing; here the lower courts identified nonpecuniary penalties and insufficiency harming actual-loss creditors. Batchelder relied on the unambiguous cross-reference while accepting inquiry into §510's undefined phrase. Strong contrary argument: susceptibility does not supply a lawful ground in every case, and the stated reasons recur whenever this statutory priority matters. Labeling that a balance cannot answer the statutory conflict.
3. **Adopt a general creditor-misconduct prerequisite.** Government briefing invokes Taylor v. Standard Gas, Pepper v. Litton, and Comstock, and argues the historic doctrine addresses wrongful acquisition/assertion of a claim. Counterargument: none held that creditor fault was indispensable in every possible claim category; Simonson's nonpecuniary-penalty treatment rested on former §57(j), not a holding about current §510(c). A broad prerequisite needs justification beyond the narrower flaw alleged here.

A fourth outcome of no immediate substantive judgment would require a concrete issue preventing lawful review; unavailable archival material alone is not one. The supported record permits the legal priority question without reconstructing a new filing, misconduct finding, waiver, or litigation default.

## United Food v. Brown Group — May 13, 1996; No. 95-340

### Scope and procedural facts

Reviewed opinion: 50 F.3d 1426 (8th Cir. 1995), with the district dismissal, postjudgment order, and appellate rehearing order reproduced in `brown_03._Appendix_*`, read in full. The district dismissed **without prejudice** under Rule 12 for standing; the appellate court affirmed. Allegations are accepted as true and construed for the union at this stage. No WARN violation, employee eligibility, or amount has been tried.

Brown Shoe's January 17, 1992 letter to an International employee announced closing of the Dixon, Missouri plant and displacement of 277 workers beginning March 20. Local 751 alleges notice should have been directed to it and layoffs began immediately. It sued June 16, 1992 as certified exclusive representative for employees' back pay/benefits and fees. Its status as an exclusive representative was undisputed below. Individuals' layoff dates, piecework pay, and potentially medical costs vary. The union's reply argues payroll records usually suffice and medical-expense claimants could be joined; that is an advocate's response to the lower court's individual-participation premise, not a finding of identical damages or no required participation.

The Eighth Circuit assumed for argument that inadequate statutory notice injured the union directly, but found that employees' damages did not redress that injury and a general prayer did not request an adequate union remedy. It found Hunt prongs one and two met, but treated the third as a constitutional bar because individualized proof was necessary. Separately it sustained denial of the union's Rule 59(e) request to add individuals/declaratory relief after a nine-month opportunity to amend. **That amendment issue was not included in the certiorari petition**, as the U.S. amicus expressly notes (brief pp. 8–9 n.8). Do not treat reversal on representational standing as adjudicating an amendment abuse-of-discretion question.

The actual merits question asks whether Article III precludes giving effect to Congress's express authorization of an exclusive representative to assert the represented employees' monetary WARN claims. The union principally presses direct statutory injury and authorized representation, describing voluntary associational standing as only one subcategory. The United States presses those arguments and the prudential character of Hunt's third condition. Respondent objects that the associational route is outside the question; union reply pp. 18–19 n.15 expressly defends its inclusion and points to petition pp. 14–20. Thus preservation is disputed but supported, not absent.

### Statute and remedy

Official complete copies: `code29_2101.txt`, `code29_2102.txt`, `code29_2104.txt` (1994 Code; GovInfo links in corresponding HTML). Section 2101(a)(4) defines representative by the exclusive labor-law representative. Section 2102(a) generally bars ordering a plant closing or mass layoff until 60 days after written notice to each affected employees' representative (or each employee if unrepresented), the state dislocated-worker unit, and the chief elected local official, with the specified choice for overlapping local units. Its statutory exceptions and reduced-notice conditions remain independent; no exception has been found satisfied in this standing appeal.

Section 2104(a)(5), complete: “A person seeking to enforce such liability, including a representative of employees or a unit of local government aggrieved under paragraph (1) or (3), may sue either for such person or for other persons similarly situated, or both, in any district court of the United States for any district in which the violation is alleged to have occurred, or in which the employer transacts business.”

The “such liability” reference matters. Section 2104(a)(1) makes the employer liable to each aggrieved employee suffering employment loss for back pay for each day of violation at not less than the higher of the prior three-year average regular rate and final regular rate; and ERISA-plan benefits, including qualifying medical expenses. Duration is capped at 60 days and half the employee's employment days. Paragraph (2) requires specified wage, unconditional-voluntary-payment, and third-party-benefit offsets, and permits defined-benefit service credit. Paragraph (3)'s distinct daily local-government civil penalty is subject to the three-week employee-payment exception. Paragraph (4) permits reduction for employer-proved good faith and reasonable grounds. Paragraph (6) permits prevailing-party fees in the court's discretion. Paragraph (7) defines aggrieved employee by the failure to receive timely notice directly or through the representative. Section 2104(b), complete: “The remedies provided for in this section shall be the exclusive remedies for any violation of this chapter. Under this chapter, a Federal court shall not have authority to enjoin a plant closing or mass layoff.”

Respondent's merits brief newly disputes statutory authorization, reading “aggrieved under paragraph (1) or (3)” to limit all listed plaintiffs and treating the representative reference as a vestige in a venue provision. The union/United States respond that this makes Congress's express representative language ineffectual; a representative enforces employee liability without becoming its beneficiary. This is a legal construction fork, not an undisputed concession in this Court. Standing, class certification, joinder, adequacy, merits, and damage computation remain distinct; no class action or assignment was established.

### Supported alternatives and limits

1. **Authorized representation with constitutional injury supplied by the represented members; third Hunt requirement prudential.** Warth, Hunt, and Brock permit genuine representative adjudication; injured employees and germaneness are established on the accepted allegations; individualized damages may raise management/protection concerns rather than eliminate the concrete dispute. Congress's express authority can remove that prudential bar. Strong contrary: cases allowing representation largely sought declaratory/injunctive relief, while determining each absent person's monetary entitlement creates adequacy, finality, and redressability concerns. This route must preserve actual member injury and case-management safeguards; it cannot treat Congress as creating jurisdiction over an abstract grievance.
2. **Direct union injury/redressability or expressly authorized legal-representative standing.** Statutory notice enables bargaining/readjustment work, and member compensation can redress the impairment of that legally protected function, comparable to third-party-beneficiary and fiduciary suits. Strong contrary: the pleaded prayer pays workers, not the union; its role may be only a notice conduit; asserted functional harm was not specifically pleaded; the collective agreement's reservation of closure decisions does not itself create a WARN injury. Deciding this path may require more about the pleaded union injury than the narrower associational route.
3. **Affirm because individualized recovery is constitutionally barred, or because the statute does not authorize this action.** Respondent relies on Warth's damages discussion and the need for an actual litigant entitled to redress, and separately its reading of §2104(a)(5). Strong contrary: Article III has long accommodated executors/trustees/authorized representatives, Hunt did not decide congressional override, and the statute specifically includes a representative. An affirmance cannot rest on missing archive pages or presume no injured workers when the complaint and lower decision establish the contrary at this stage.

The lawful reversal remedy is further proceedings on the WARN claim under the correct standing rule, with all statutory merits, individual entitlement, offsets, and procedural protections intact. It is not a judgment for back pay or an injunction against the closure. Affirmance preserves the standing dismissal; there is no basis to convert it to a merits dismissal with prejudice.

## BMW v. Gore — May 20, 1996; No. 94-896

### Preserved questions, judgment, and record

The petition states two questions: whether Alabama, having identified the jury's reliance on out-of-state transactions, provided a meaningful remedy; and whether the remitted $2 million punitive award (500 times $4,000 compensation) is grossly excessive under the Fourteenth Amendment. The case is not confined to selecting a constitutional ratio. A source-supported narrower process/remedy route must remain available alongside substantive excessiveness and affirmance.

The full substituted Alabama majority, Houston concurrence, trial postverdict order, and final certificate are in the Archive petition, Appendices A–C (printed 1a–31a), reviewed here. This is the August 19, 1994 substituted opinion, not the withdrawn October 29, 1993 text. Gore accepted remittitur August 26; the September 9 certificate affirms $2,004,000, with interest and costs. The lower judgment separately reverses and renders for German manufacturer BMW AG for lack of the required connection to Alabama and the suppression. Only distributor BMW NA's punitive challenge is the presented Supreme Court matter; do not rejoin the released manufacturer or reopen compensation.

The lower court recites these facts: Gore paid $40,750.88 for a 1990 BMW 535i; he dealt with the dealer, not directly with BMW NA or AG. He signed a form acknowledging possible damage/inspection/acceptance, but the particular refinishing was not disclosed. After about nine months, a detailer identified refinishing; the court describes acid-rain paint damage during transit and a $601 repair. BMW NA's 1983 policy did not disclose repairs below 3% of MSRP. The jury awarded $4,000 diminution in value and $4 million punitive damages on the suppression claim, finding gross, malicious, intentional and wanton fraud. Contract and other theories were not the BMW claims submitted. The state court found sufficient clear-and-convincing evidence for punitive entitlement under Alabama law; the federal review questions concern the punitive constitutional challenge, not an automatic reversal of that finding.

The parties stipulated 983 refinished vehicles. BMW estimated only 11–14 Alabama transactions, and a witness estimated about 60% of the repair orders concerned states with disclosure statutes (quoted testimony R.971–972). Gore argued that knowing nationwide nondisclosure was a pattern showing intent and that 35 states would have required disclosure when the policy was adopted. The lower court allowed evidence of similar acts as pattern/intent even if lawful elsewhere, but prohibited using other jurisdictions' numbers as a punitive multiplier because the record did not establish which out-of-state acts were unlawful. These are different uses of the evidence; the neutral shorthand “lawfulness disputed” must not obscure the Alabama court's own exclusion from its stated amount analysis.

Counsel's closing asked for $4 million based on about 1,000 cars times $4,000. The Alabama court found the jury substantially relied on out-of-state acts; it then said its own reasonable-relationship analysis did not consider those acts in setting the amount and fixed $2 million after Green Oil review and comparison cases. BMW argues the unexplained halving still inherits the forbidden starting point and requires a new punitive trial or reduction reflecting Alabama conduct. Gore's position, as recited by the court, stresses continuing deliberate nondisclosure, value loss, recurrence, profitability, and deterrence; the policy changed after the verdict according to Gore's argument, which should not become an independently verified company admission. Houston concurs in $2 million relying in part on the 14 Alabama instances and comparison awards, but also describes the award as punishing the overall pattern and warns about repeated punishment. His latter characterization is not the majority's express statement about what it included.

The trial judge held a postverdict evidentiary hearing, considered compensation, past similar acts, attempted correction, wealth impact, and Hammond/Green Oil; found no bias/passion/prejudice; and denied a new trial/remittitur. Alabama's review did reduce the amount. The record therefore does not establish the complete statutory bar to amount review found in simulated Oberg. Whether this actual correction suffices is a separate issue.

### Applicable text and law entering

The Fourteenth Amendment prohibits a State from depriving any person of property without due process of law. There is no enacted constitutional ratio. Alabama §6-11-20, as described and quoted by its supreme court, requires clear-and-convincing proof of conscious/deliberate oppression, fraud, wantonness, or malice for punitive entitlement. It is not a federal amount test.

The lower opinion quotes the subsequent 1993 Act No. 93-203 amendment to Ala. Code §8-19-5: material damage means damage, corrected or not, whose retail repair cost exceeds 3% MSRP or $500, whichever is greater; replacement of tires, glass, bumpers, and in-dash audio equipment with identical manufacturer equipment is excluded from that cost; failure to disclose below-threshold damage supplies neither sale revocation nor material misrepresentation/omission. The Alabama court declined to apply that later policy retroactively to the relevant conduct. Do not replace the adjudicated earlier disclosure duty with the subsequent threshold. The later enactment and other states' rules may be argued to bear on notice or blameworthiness without silently making this sale lawful.

Governing **Meaningful review of punitive-damage amounts**, present operation Oberg June 24, 1994, requires meaningful amount review or an adequate substitute, not a fixed ratio, mandatory written opinion, compulsory remittitur, specified standard, or prescribed tiers. Its eight-Justice opinion includes Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter and Thomas. Ginsburg's public dissent evaluates safeguards together and expressly reserves a properly presented gross-excessiveness claim. All those boundaries remain.

Simulated **TXO**, June 25, 1993, supplies only a five-Justice common procedural proposition: its actual jury/hearing/trial and appellate review sufficed, with no requirement for a separate trial explanation. Stevens/Blackmun adopt potential-harm substantive review; Kennedy's different rationale concerns rational punishment for established deliberate misconduct and rejects reconstructed potential-harm arithmetic; Scalia/Thomas reject substantive numerical review. These are not nested grounds creating a substantive Marks rule. A 500:1 or 1000:1 resemblance is no precedent validating this sum.

Pre-divergence **Haslip**, 499 U.S. 1, 18–24, remains governing: no universal mathematical line; actual jury constraints, state amount review, and reasonable relationship to deterrence/retribution sustained the award there. Its Court opinion (Stevens joined) did not approve every Alabama result. Scalia's separate writing emphasizes traditional process; Kennedy's separate writing also invokes traditional adjudication and the actual record; O'Connor's dissent challenges the adequacy of jury standards and meaningful constraints. Cornell full text and writing roster verified in `haslip.txt`. **Shutts**, 472 U.S. 797, 818–823, and **Allstate v. Hague**, 449 U.S. 302, supply the petition's choice-of-law/due-process analogy against imposing a state's law on disconnected transactions; they do not automatically prohibit relevant pattern evidence or set a punitive ceiling. Each must be applied to the particular use and state connection.

### Supported paths, counterarguments, and remedies

1. **Substantive excessiveness.** The combination of purely economic injury, modest repair, no discernible finish problem during use, the size of the remitted punishment, contested notice from variable state rules, and comparison to legally authorized sanctions/analogous awards supports a claim of gross excessiveness. The petition identifies the Deceptive Trade Practices Act's $2,000-per-violation maximum as a comparator, not an applicable exclusive punitive cap. Counterargument: deliberate repeated material suppression was found, potential and repeated harm need not be reduced to this one repair bill, deterrence may require more than compensation, and state judges actually corrected the sum. Any newly selected federal test must be justified from surviving law, not imported from the withheld case's later disposition. The record does not itself prove one exact lawful maximum.
2. **Procedural correction or remedy failure without a general substantive formula.** An express finding that the jury used legally inappropriate out-of-state multipliers requires a correction genuinely responsive to that error. Oberg and TXO make actual operation, not the mere existence of review, important. Counterargument: Alabama expressly excluded foreign acts from its own amount determination, applied several criteria, compared cases, and remitted; a federal court cannot equate a disagreement about the corrected amount with absence of review. Remand for lawful review/correction is distinct from commanding a new trial. A new punitive trial needs justification why appellate correction cannot cure the supported defect; BMW's proposed $56,000 is an advocate's arithmetic, not a finding of the constitutionally necessary award.
3. **Affirm on substantive reasonableness and sufficient actual safeguards.** Haslip permits discretionary punitive judgment bounded by meaningful review; this record contains an express state-law culpability finding and repeated practice, and the state court distinguished evidence of intent from punishment for foreign acts. Counterargument: the same recited factors do not explain why $2 million is lawful after rejecting the jury's predicate, especially where the comparable awards are much smaller and notice was uncertain.
4. **Affirm on the absence of federal substantive numerical control, while independently deciding process.** Scalia/Thomas's published TXO position is a lawful jurisprudential path, but it does not remove the procedural protection they joined in Oberg. It cannot become an automatic affirmance if the model independently finds meaningful correction missing. Conversely Oberg's procedural join is not a commitment to a substantive amount ceiling.

No path automatically alters the $4,000 compensatory judgment, the manufacturer dismissal, underlying state disclosure law, or imposes a ratio for all punitive cases. Remand can preserve the lawful punishment/deterrence authority while requiring application of the adopted federal limitation; affirmance leaves the remitted judgment in place. The Court need not award a chosen replacement sum itself.
