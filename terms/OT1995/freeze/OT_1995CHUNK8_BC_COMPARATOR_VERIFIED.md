## Jaffee v. Redmond — 518 U.S. 1; No. 95-266; June 13, 1996

Sources: [official full report](https://tile.loc.gov/storage-services/service/ll/usrep/usrep518/usrep518001/usrep518001.pdf); [Cornell syllabus](https://www.law.cornell.edu/supct/html/95-266.ZS.html); [Stevens opinion](https://www.law.cornell.edu/supct/html/95-266.ZO.html); [Scalia dissent](https://www.law.cornell.edu/supct/html/95-266.ZD.html). Argued February 26, 1996.

**Historical disposition and joins.** Affirmed, 7–2. Stevens wrote for O'Connor, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer as well as himself. Scalia dissented; Rehnquist joined only Part III of that dissent. Rehnquist's limited join does not establish his agreement with Scalia's general attack on recognizing any psychotherapist privilege in Parts I–II.

**Historical components.** The seven-Justice opinion recognizes under Rule 501 protection from compelled disclosure for confidential communications between a licensed psychotherapist and patient in diagnosis or treatment; extends it to licensed social workers providing psychotherapy; rejects the court of appeals' later, case-specific balancing of the patient's privacy against evidentiary need; and protects Redmond's conversations with Beyer and the notes of those counseling sessions. Affirmance preserves the Seventh Circuit's reversal and new-trial remand, not the original damages judgment. The opinion relies on trust necessary to effective treatment, private and public mental-health interests, state legislative consensus, and the proposed rules' treatment of psychotherapy. The absence of protection may prevent the useful communications from being made at all.

**Reservations that must remain distinct.** Footnote 14 acknowledges patient waiver but adjudicates no comprehensive waiver test. Footnote 15 expressly leaves unresolved the federal/state privilege rule when both sorts of claims are brought together; the parties did not raise it and resolution did not depend on it. The opinion leaves the full contours of the new privilege to later cases. Footnote 19 identifies circumstances in which it must yield, giving as an example a serious threat of harm to the patient or others avertable only by the therapist's disclosure; it does not prescribe a general future-danger adjudication or approve open-ended evidentiary balancing. The privilege covers psychotherapy communications, not every conversation with a social worker in any professional role.

**Separate writing.** Scalia's opening and Parts I–II stress the truth-seeking cost, lack of sufficient demonstration that privilege adds enough therapeutic benefit, and the difference between legislative choices and a judicially formulated federal privilege of unspecified scope. Part I objects to framing the case first as psychotherapy generally and treating social-worker coverage as automatic; the proposed rule covered doctors and psychologists, not social workers. Part III, joined by Rehnquist, focuses on social workers' varied training and functions, the difficulty of separating psychotherapy from other services, and the substantial variation and exceptions in state statutes. The state enactments do not establish agreement on a privilege's judicially manageable terms; in their view Congress should determine the protection. No dissent establishes an alternative controlling exception.

## Montana v. Egelhoff — 518 U.S. 37; No. 95-566; June 13, 1996

Sources: [official full report](https://tile.loc.gov/storage-services/service/ll/usrep/usrep518/usrep518037/usrep518037.pdf); [Cornell syllabus](https://www.law.cornell.edu/supct/html/95-566.ZS.html). Argued March 20, 1996. Full report read: Scalia plurality pp. 39–56; Ginsburg concurrence pp. 56–61; O'Connor dissent pp. 61–73; Souter dissent pp. 73–79; Breyer dissent pp. 79–80, including all footnotes.

**Historical disposition and topology.** Reversed, 5–4. Scalia announced judgment in an opinion joined by Rehnquist, Kennedy and Thomas. Ginsburg concurred in the judgment only. O'Connor dissented with Stevens, Souter and Breyer joining; Souter wrote separately alone; Breyer wrote separately with Stevens joining. No complete five-Justice majority opinion exists.

**Plurality.** Montana may prevent consideration of voluntary intoxication in determining the requisite mental state. Due process does not require every relevant item to be admitted. The defendant must establish a fundamental principle rooted in traditions and conscience; the older common-law exclusion, the incomplete and relatively recent acceptance of a contrary rule, and continuing state practice defeat that showing. The plurality invokes deterrence and responsibility for self-induced impairment, treats the ordinary reasonable-doubt instruction as intact, distinguishes burden reduction through valid evidentiary exclusion from burden shifting, and rejects broad readings of Chambers, Crane and Martin. Footnote 4 agrees that the legislature may extract voluntary intoxication from the mental-state inquiry through a substantive definition, but asserts that evidentiary exclusion can legitimately accomplish the same effect. Thus its rationale is expressly broader than Ginsburg's characterization-dependent ground.

**Ginsburg's separate ground.** The statute is best understood as an element-defining liability rule, given its placement among general principles of liability and its substantive operation. The State proves either actual knowledge or purpose, or circumstances otherwise establishing those states but for voluntary intoxication; intoxication is thereby irrelevant rather than relevant evidence suppressed. States have broad but not unlimited element-defining authority, and this choice survives fundamental-principle review in light of history and continuing state practice. She declines to categorize the law as simply an evidentiary rule and treats the Montana court as not having foreclosed a constitutional substantive construction. Her first footnote rejects Breyer's suggestion that this construction necessarily equates all intoxication with purpose and discusses the driving hypotheticals; it does not abolish the remaining mental-state requirement.

**Dissents.** O'Connor accepts that States can redefine offense elements, but reads the Montana Supreme Court as having authoritatively retained knowledge/purpose and recognized the relevance of intoxication. A blanket exclusion designed to improve convictions denies a meaningful defense and relieves the prosecution from meeting that defense. Ordinary reliability, privilege and trial-management exclusions are distinct. The involuntary-intoxication exception undermines the suggested theory that intoxication evidence is inherently misleading. Historical practices do not extinguish later fundamental fair-trial principles. Souter expressly accepts a substantive redefinition, would otherwise have read this statute that way, but regards the state court's contrary construction as binding. He does not categorically foreclose every justified evidentiary exclusion: Montana did not provide a convincing justification for keeping the relevant element while excluding the evidence; hypothetical confusion/capacity arguments might justify redefinition but are not shown to justify that different choice. Breyer, joined by Stevens, adds that the proposed constructive-knowledge reading creates anomalous dependence on external circumstances; he reserves whether an express law equating voluntary intoxication with knowledge/purpose would itself exceed constitutional limits.

**Comparator caution.** The runtime's compact statement of a substantive/evidentiary distinction cannot be attributed wholesale to a five-Justice Court rationale. The affirmative overlap is permission for the particular substantive treatment of voluntary intoxication; the plurality additionally permits the evidentiary route that Ginsburg does not adopt. Any proposed narrowest-ground analysis must identify this actual relationship rather than treating all rationale text as unanimously shared or transforming dissenting propositions into a Court holding.

## Koon v. United States; Powell v. United States — 518 U.S. 81; Nos. 94-1664, 94-8842; June 13, 1996

Sources: [official full report](https://tile.loc.gov/storage-services/service/ll/usrep/usrep518/usrep518081/usrep518081.pdf); [Cornell syllabus](https://www.law.cornell.edu/supct/html/94-1664.ZS.html). Argued February 20, 1996. Full report read pp. 81–119, including all three separate writings and their footnotes.

**Historical judgment.** Affirmed in part, reversed in part and remanded. Kennedy's Court opinion is unanimous except Part IV-B-1, which Stevens does not join, and Part IV-B-3, which Souter, Ginsburg and Breyer do not join. Rehnquist, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy and Thomas join throughout. Each dissenting Justice joins the remainder at the stated scope; a single 9–0 label must not erase the departure-factor splits.

| Component | Historical outcome and support |
|---|---|
| Part II and III: departure framework and appellate review | All nine. Abuse-of-discretion review incorporates correction of legal error; legal permission to consider a factor receives no deference. Encouraged, discouraged, already-considered and unmentioned factors retain their separate conditions. |
| IV-A: five-level victim-misconduct departure | All nine uphold the district court's departure. The relevant heartland is aggravated assault under color of law, including unprovoked official assault, not solely excessive-force arrests. The court's dynamic-arrest and provocation findings are more than mere but-for causation. |
| IV-B introductory discussion: categorical permissible-factor inquiry | All nine reject court-created categorical prohibitions beyond the Commission's scheme; Section 3553(a)(2)'s sentencing purposes do not independently forbid every factor failing to advance a listed purpose. Application must still satisfy the departure framework. |
| IV-B-1: loss of law-enforcement employment | Eight reject this factor on the facts as adequately contemplated by the guideline. Stevens alone would uphold its use here. Job loss is not automatically synonymous with prohibited socioeconomic status. |
| IV-B-2: especially low recidivism risk within category I | All nine reject the departure basis because the Commission expressly provided for the first offender with the lowest recidivism risk. |
| IV-B-3: exceptional prison vulnerability and successive prosecutions | Six uphold consideration here: Rehnquist, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Thomas. Souter, Ginsburg, Breyer reject both on the record, not through a complete refusal of the broader discretion/review rule. |
| Part V: remand | All nine join the stated remand disposition. The district court grouped the four considerations and said none sufficed alone. The remaining two did not establish that the same three-level departure would occur without the invalid grounds. The upheld five-level ground remains distinct. |

**Separate writings.** Stevens endorses everything except the employment ruling, regards these consequences as sufficiently unusual, and expressly understands the opinion to leave room for an aggregation of factors each insufficient alone. Souter, joined by Ginsburg, requires the unusual circumstance to warrant a different sentence within a rational normative sentencing order. Rewarding brutality because it generated publicity and prison risk is perverse on this record; successive federal prosecution after a facial state-system failure should not yield another advantage from that failure. His footnotes preserve unusual physical vulnerability as a potentially valid basis and recognize the rarity of successive prosecutions without treating rarity alone as dispositive. He leaves open a different case where comparison of verdicts and predicates yields no such incongruity. Breyer, joined by Ginsburg, separately regards federal civil-rights prosecution after inadequate state enforcement as contemplated by the guideline's purpose; simple successive prosecution is insufficient. Harsh prison treatment requires a truly unusual case, not shown relative to other imprisoned officers even with deference. He agrees with Souter's conclusion, not a formal complete joinder in Souter's separate opinion.

**Limits.** Convictions and the refusal to add serious-bodily-injury levels were not before the Court. Approval of particular grounds does not itself reinstate a 30-month sentence. The historical Williams discussion is comparator reasoning only; it does not replace the simulation's independently governing Williams rule or its burden allocation.

## Rise v. Oregon — 59 F.3d 1556; Ninth Circuit No. 93-35521; July 18, 1995

[Published lower judicial text](https://openjurist.org/59/f3d/1556/rise), cross-checked against [CAP report](https://static.case.law/f3d/59/cases/1556-01.json). Argued/submitted November 2, 1994. Fletcher wrote the majority with Rymer; D. W. Nelson dissented on the Fourth Amendment issue. The panel affirmed summary judgment. There is no supplied historical Supreme Court merits comparator, no Supreme Court docket or argument date, and no historical Supreme Court vote for the assigned June 14, 1996 event.

The majority independently upholds the scheme even assuming ordinary law enforcement is its sole purpose; it does not adopt the district court's special-needs/penal-administration rationale as necessary. It recognizes blood collection as a search, treats covered convicted persons' identifying privacy and medically conducted extraction as limited intrusions, and balances identification utility, recidivism evidence, safeguards and conviction-defined coverage. Its separate holdings reject ex post facto punishment and the general precollection-hearing claim. Milligan's separate claim fails because no evidence connects Knox or Pearce personally with the mistake. The majority expressly avoids deciding a hypothetical claim against the responsible actors; their absence cannot be converted into a holding that every mistaken demand or discipline is constitutional. The unappealed dismissals of the other defendants remain distinct.

Nelson disputes the fingerprint analogy, insists bodily intrusion ordinarily requires individualized justification for criminal investigation, distinguishes minimally burdensome collection methods from the legal predicate for searching, and finds neither a prison-security purpose nor a genuine special need. She invokes the separate privacy interest in genetic information and rejects statistical future-crime likelihood and uniform implementation as substitutes for the missing predicate. The dissent is confined in its stated disagreement to the search issue; no fabricated separate disagreement with the other components is supplied.

The lower opinion's description of one lifetime draw is qualified by the actual adequate-sample and repeat-sample provisions; its genetic-use language is judicial characterization, not a substitute for the full statute. These statutory distinctions and actual current Vernonia law belong to the sanitized neutral record. No later DNA case or historical Vernonia proposition becomes entering law by being cited in Rise.
