## Historical benchmark and material-premise test

Historically, Ginsburg’s opinion was joined by Stevens, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter and Breyer. Scalia’s concurrence, joined by Kennedy, expressly preserved Congress’s power to condition a sovereign-immunity waiver on strict compliance with procedure while finding no such condition in this scheme. Thomas dissented with Rehnquist and O’Connor. The historical judgment reversed and remanded. Rehnquist supplies no present participant or substitute vote.

The dissent’s important premise was more precise than “procedural provisions are jurisdictional” or “every sentence next to a waiver is substantive.” Thomas expressly rejected automatic jurisdictional treatment of ordinary procedural rules whenever the Government is a defendant, 517 U.S. at 676. He instead read Congress’s separate placement of the particular service requirement in §2, outside §3’s assimilation of ordinary private-party practice, as a decision to retain special service conditions for the sovereign. At least that reading was plausible; ambiguity concerning this consent limitation favored the Government. He also separately disputed displacement by Congress-enacted Rule 4(j), even apart from the technical inapplicability of §2072(b), id. at 674–679. O’Connor joined that reasoning.

The independent model’s H1 correctly demanded provision-specific classification, but that demand does not refute this provision-specific dissenting construction. H2 correctly recognized the need to answer coexistence, but the later general service regime and its extension provision were also before the historical dissent. Identifying those texts again is not a changed premise. The accepted fifteen-day extension and absence of a distinct Rule 4 dismissal ground do not remove the dissent’s statutory objection: the point of that objection is that Rule-authorized service still must satisfy the surviving special waiver condition.

## Reconciled propositions and exact joins

These are proposed decisional commitments for later assembly, not a declaration that the Court has adopted them. Every proposition states its own support. Common acceptance of a record limit is not counted as agreement on its legal consequence.

### R1. This particular postfiling service direction is not a condition of the SAA waiver

**Proposition:** Section 742 permits the specified nonjury in-personam action against the United States; its subsequent forthwith direction governs transmission of the filed complaint to designated federal recipients. Read with the section’s distinct consent, venue, service and transfer functions and §743’s private-party practice instruction, this direction implements an already authorized suit rather than independently restricting the class of claims or subject-matter jurisdiction. Its procedural placement and function must be assessed, not simply inferred from its proximity to the waiver.

**Necessary qualification:** Congress can condition a waiver on compliance with procedural requirements and make that condition jurisdictional. The conclusion is that this legislative scheme did not do so with forthwith service, not that Congress lacks the power. A provision’s procedural subject alone cannot establish nonjurisdictional status. Section 743 likewise contains distinct costs, interest and other prescriptions and is not a command erasing every difference between private and Government litigation.

**Joins:** Stevens, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Ginsburg, Breyer. **Refusals:** O’Connor and Thomas, for D1. Scalia and Kennedy’s unrestricted join includes the express qualification; no assertion that all procedural waiver conditions are impossible receives their assent.

### R2. The applicable Rule 4 timing scheme displaces the incompatible forthwith timetable

**Proposition:** In this domestic SAA action commenced within its limitations period, Rule 4 governs the time allowed for service, including its authorized enlargement. Its extendable 120-day scheme is an allowance that cannot coexist with a separate, shorter §742 timetable requiring dismissal even when service is allowed by that scheme. Physical ability to serve immediately under both texts does not reconcile their conflicting consequences for service later within the Rule-authorized period. Former Rule 4(a)’s prompt-service responsibility does not enact an additional independently fatal statutory deadline or turn this holding into permission to deliberately disregard service responsibilities.

**Enactment analysis:** Congress itself enacted former Rule 4(j) in Pub. L. 97-462. The legally sufficient route for that rule is Congress’s later procedural enactment, read as displacing the conflicting earlier procedural direction. Section 2072’s framework confirms the legitimacy of procedural regulation, the need to preserve substantive rights and congressional intent concerning conflicting procedure; Rule 82 remains respected because R1 leaves substantive consent and jurisdiction unchanged.

**Correction to provisional H2:** Section 2072(b) does not directly operate on former Rule 4(j) as though Congress’s own enactment were a rule prescribed by the Court under the Enabling Act. Its relevance is interpretive and structural on this route, not a second autonomous holding of direct statutory nullification. The Court-promulgated 1993 Rule supplies a potentially distinct later framework only under its just-and-practicable pending-case direction; this record does not require a separate finding making that framework an independently sufficient alternative disposition. All six withdraw the provisional characterization of these as two presently established independent enactment grounds.

**Scope:** This holding displaces the competing timing direction, not every service statute, every substantive SAA limit or the designated-recipient requirements. Registered-versus-certified-mail validity is not a presented independent controversy. Rule 3’s filing rule for federal rights does not displace a state-created right’s distinct state-law commencement condition under Walker/Ragan; the Government’s Walker analogy fails here because the rival provisions regulate the same postfiling service event, not a filing rule and an independent limitations question. Hanna/Murphree support procedure classification without replacing the necessary specific conflict analysis.

**Joins:** Stevens, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Ginsburg, Breyer. **Refusals:** O’Connor and Thomas, for D1–D2. None of the six treats R1 alone, administrative convenience or eventual actual notice as sufficient to resolve coexistence.

### R3. Remove the statutory dismissal without a newly invented service or merits proceeding

**Proposition:** Reverse the Fifth Circuit’s judgment affirming dismissal under §742’s forthwith direction and remand for ordinary proceedings consistent with R1–R2. Neither the 148-day United States Attorney service nor the Attorney General’s day-47 receipt can sustain that displaced timing ground. No separate ruling that either was forthwith, narrowing of the statute to one recipient, or finding of diligence is needed.

The existing fifteen-day extension and the Government’s reliance on §742 are retained. No independent Rule 4 dismissal defect was presented as the ground under review. The order’s absence of express good-cause findings does not itself invalidate it or authorize a compelled new hearing. Any genuinely available later objection must satisfy its own authority, preservation and procedural conditions; this remand neither invents an objection nor conclusively adjudicates every conceivable service dispute.

Former Rule 4(j) governed the April–September 1993 acts. The 1993 amendment became effective December 1 for new cases and, insofar as just and practicable, proceedings in pending cases. If a properly presented issue in the 1994 dismissal or later proceedings requires choosing the applicable Rule, that direction controls. Under Rule 4(m), good cause requires enlargement; without good cause enlargement is permitted, not required. A limitations bar may support discretion but does not compel relief. No automatic relabeling of the 1993 order, obligatory diligence hearing or present holding that amended Rule 4(m) necessarily supplies relief follows.

No injury liability, damages, maintenance/cure entitlement, immediate trial, constitutional notice holding, Clarification Act/PVA classification or general new refiling opportunity is decided. Section 745’s two-year limit and dated 1950 savings provision remain unchanged. The district Rule 12 waiver argument is not adopted as another sufficient ground because the actual transfer motion and appellate preservation are unestablished; those gaps need not be resolved for R1–R2.

**Joins:** Stevens, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Ginsburg, Breyer. O’Connor and Thomas agree that unsupported factual findings and new merits relief are unavailable but refuse the proposed reversal/remand.

### D1. The SAA specifically conditions its consent on forthwith service

**Proposition:** Congress can make a procedural requirement a strict condition of consent to suit. The structure here does so: Congress put a specific service command in §742, separately from §743’s instruction applying ordinary rules of practice between private parties. That arrangement gives the particular service requirement independent work despite generally applicable private-party procedure. This is more than a claim that every sentence of §742 is jurisdictional. At minimum, a plausible reading of this particular waiver as conditioned on the specified service defeats an enlargement of consent by procedural inference.

The provision’s subject is service, but its chosen role in this statutory scheme defines the scope of consent. Sherwood, Block and the actual simulated Nordic Village consent principles support respecting that choice; they do not decide every service provision by category. Meyer’s distinct broad agency clause and Carlisle’s unconditioned criminal procedural mechanism do not remove it.

**Joins:** O’Connor, Thomas. **Refusals:** Stevens, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Ginsburg, Breyer, who agree Congress has the power but reject this reading of its exercise here.

### D2. Neither judicial rulemaking nor the direct enactment establishes displacement of that special condition

**Proposition:** A Court-promulgated procedural rule cannot enlarge the sovereign consent fixed by §742; §2072(b)’s substantive-rights restriction and Rule 82 protect that boundary. Former Rule 4(j), however, was enacted by Congress, so its operation cannot be resolved by mechanically applying the Enabling Act’s language concerning rules prescribed under it. Congress had power to change the waiver, but the general 120-day dismissal regime does not sufficiently establish repeal of this separately retained special service requirement. Prompt service can satisfy both; the general extension mechanism need not supersede the special statutory condition.

This supplies two connected responses to the two claimed sources of displacement: no judicial-rule enlargement of this waiver, and no established legislative repeal by former Rule 4(j). Direct congressional provenance defeats the objection that Congress lacked power to change consent, but not the distinct objection that Congress did not make that change. Section 743 and Rule 4 uniformity do not by themselves supply the missing displacement. The qualified 1993 transition cannot transform a procedural amendment into substantive waiver enlargement either.

**Joins:** O’Connor, Thomas. The six reversing Associates accept the provenance and general limits on rulemaking but reject D2’s conclusion about this timing condition and its continued coexistence.

### D3. The 148-day service fails the surviving requirement, without additional factual adjudication

**Proposition:** Affirm the judgment dismissing this action on the surviving §742 condition. Service on the United States Attorney 148 days after filing is not forthwith even under a reasonable-dispatch construction and without fixing a universal number of days. The existing Rule 4 extension does not extend the separate condition. This suffices irrespective of whether day-47 Attorney General receipt independently met the statute, so no additional recipient-specific finding is required.

The disposition does not rest on rejecting counsel’s allegations, finding deliberate delay, creating a new Rule 4 defect or adjudicating the injury. The Fifth Circuit considered the delay insufficient even assuming good cause and reasonable-time interpretation; treating the condition as surviving does not require a new diligence hearing. Neither Pioneer’s rule-specific flexibility nor the proposed American Pipe/McCormick analogy establishes a general power to extend this consent condition. The unestablished Rule 12 waiver route does not supply an alternative reversal of a jurisdictional condition, and the separate due-process question is not presented for merits adjudication. Dismissal without prejudice does not establish that timely refiling remains possible or authorize extension of §745.

**Joins:** O’Connor, Thomas. The six reversing Associates refuse affirmance because the statutory premise fails under R1–R2, not because they have found the 148-day service prompt.
