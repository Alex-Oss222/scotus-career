## Operative propositions available for joinder

### H1. The particular postfiling service-time direction implements consent; it does not define the substantive waiver

Section 742 expressly permits the specified nonjury in-personam admiralty proceeding against the United States. Its later direction concerning delivery and mailing of the filed libel regulates bringing notice of that proceeding to designated federal recipients. The timing word “forthwith” is procedural, rather than an independent restriction on subject-matter jurisdiction or on the class of maritime liabilities for which Congress consents to suit. Its placement in the section containing consent does not give every sentence the same legal function.

This classification leaves Congress's actual consent conditions intact. Rule 3's commencement by filing, §742's grant and separate service language, and §743's private-party practice direction support distinguishing institution of the authorized suit from transmission of its papers. The disposition does not create a new monetary remedy, waive a previously unconsented class of claims, alter the two-year commencement period or decide compliance with every other statutory predicate. Rule 82 and §2072(b)'s substantive-rights limitation remain effective.

*Nordic Village* requires clear consent to the actual monetary remedy and distinguishes consent from jurisdictional labels; it does not classify every service instruction as substantive. *McNeil* enforces an express prefiling administrative sequence, and *Keene* enforces §1500's filing-time jurisdictional bar. Their texts and operative events differ. *Carlisle* expressly recognizes that an enforceable procedural limit can constrain a remedy without removing subject-matter jurisdiction. Those authorities require classification of this provision, not automatic extension of their results.

**Join scope:** Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer support H1, including its stated limits. H1 alone would not dispose of the Government's independent coexistence argument; H2 performs that additional work.

### H2. Rule 4's service-time regime displaces §742's separate forthwith deadline

For this domestic-service SAA action, the time for serving the United States is governed by the applicable Rule 4 time provision and its authorized extension mechanism. A separate §742 forthwith deadline cannot require dismissal because service took longer than that statutory direction would permit notwithstanding service allowed by the governing Rule. The holding concerns the competing timing direction; it does not erase the necessity of serving the designated federal recipients or decide an unpresented dispute over registered versus certified mail.

The governing instruments address the same procedural event: service after commencement. Former Rule 4(j) regulates that event through a 120-day period, dismissal without prejudice when the specified good-cause condition is not met, notice for sua sponte dismissal, and its foreign-service exception. Congress enacted that scheme itself in Pub. L. 97-462. Its operation cannot be reduced to an additional outer sanction while the earlier forthwith direction remains an independent, shorter, unextendable service bar. That would preserve a rival timing condition defeating the Rule's authorized treatment of the very service at issue. The retained former Rule 4(a) responsibility for “prompt service” does not enact such a second statutory dismissal condition; neither does it confer a general entitlement to deliberate delay.

The specific/general and implied-repeal objections are substantial. Physical compliance with both texts is possible by immediate service. But the question is which legal scheme determines the consequence of later service, including an authorized extension. Rule 4 is specific to that subject and includes service on the United States; the SAA's substantive and other special provisions can remain fully effective without the competing timing command. Section 743 supports that limited reading, not wholesale private/government equivalence. The packet's *Hanna/Murphree* service-procedure analogy supports it. The Government's *Walker* analogy does not resolve the case: the dispute here concerns the very service timing the federal Rule regulates, rather than treating a filing rule as an answer to a distinct limitations question.

Two enactment grounds support the same limited rule. First, after H1's classification and the actual timing conflict are established, §2072 permits genuinely procedural rulemaking and renders conflicting laws ineffective without enlarging substantive consent; Rule 82 is not violated. Second, Congress itself enacted former Rule 4(j), providing a direct legislative basis for the later service-time scheme even if §2072(b)'s phrase “such rules” does not technically describe that enactment. Direct enactment does not by itself prove displacement: the same subject-matter and incompatibility analysis is necessary on that route. The Government's concession about provenance is not treated as conceding repeal.

The 1993 Rule strengthens the express timing structure: Rule 4(c)(1) makes the plaintiff responsible for service within the time allowed by subdivision (m), and Rule 4(m) specifies dismissal or directed service, with required enlargement upon good cause. That later wording is not used to rewrite the August–September 1993 conduct. Its pending-case application retains the transition qualification stated under H3.

**Join scope:** All eight named Associates support H2 and both identified enactment grounds at this limited level. None joins a general rule that every service statute is displaced, that all government/private procedural differences are invalid, or that statutory deadlines may be disregarded whenever service eventually occurs.

### H3. Reverse the statutory dismissal and remand without adjudicating an additional service dispute

Reverse the Fifth Circuit's judgment affirming dismissal on §742's forthwith requirement and remand for further proceedings consistent with H1–H2. Neither day 148 service on the United States Attorney nor day 47 receipt by the Attorney General may sustain that displaced timing ground. Because the ground fails as to each recipient, no independent determination that day 47 was “forthwith,” no narrowing of the statute to one recipient, and no decision that day 148 was prompt is necessary.

The existing fifteen-day order and the absence of a distinct Rule 4 dismissal ground are retained as the record supplies them. Reversal does not require the lower court to revisit good cause, find clerk fault, conduct a diligence hearing, or treat a newly asserted Rule 4 objection as preserved. Any genuinely available objection must satisfy its own preservation and procedural requirements. Nor does the absence of an express good-cause finding invalidate the existing order by itself in this review.

Former Rule 4(j) governed the 1993 service conduct. The April 22, 1993 order made amended Rule 4 effective December 1, 1993 for newly commenced cases and, “insofar as just and practicable,” proceedings in then-pending cases. If a properly presented issue in the 1994 dismissal or further proceedings requires choosing the applicable Rule, that direction must be applied. Under amended 4(m), good cause requires an appropriate extension; relief without good cause is authorized but not compelled. A limitations bar may support discretion under the dated note, but does not itself require relief. No automatic retroactive relabeling of the 1993 order or new hearing follows.

The remand removes the service ground and permits ordinary further proceedings; it does not direct immediate trial, find negligence or unseaworthiness, award maintenance, cure or damages, or decide the Clarification Act/PVA coverage issue unnecessarily. Dismissal without prejudice below did not establish a viable new action. Section 745's two-year commencement limit and its narrowly dated 1950 savings provision are unchanged, with no new general refiling extension.

**Join scope:** All eight Associates support this judgment and bounded remedy. The transition qualifications are limits on the remand and any later Rule issue, not an independently sufficient holding that the district court necessarily must apply 4(m), grant an extension, or reconsider service.

**Alternative and strongest objection:** Affirmance through coexistence gives “forthwith” continuing work and is reinforced by his insistence in *McNeil* that actual statutory sequences matter. He rejects that result because the later service-time scheme, rather than a general fairness rationale, regulates the same postfiling act here. The competing substantive-waiver analogy fails at H1. An extension/reasonableness remand under a surviving statute would leave unnecessary factual and authority disputes that H2 resolves as a legal matter.

**Joins and limits:** Joins H1–H3, including the direct-congressional route. No separate writing is necessary to preserve his *Carlisle* position. ## Alternatives not adopted; precise remaining limits

**Affirmance through a jurisdictional waiver condition:** The SAA's express service sentence and sovereign-consent precedents make this a genuine path, but statutory location is insufficient to establish the asserted substantive function. H1 rejects that function after examining the distinct consent, commencement and service provisions. None of the eight joins a blanket relaxation of *Sherwood/Mitchell* or *Nordic Village* consent principles.

**Affirmance through nonjurisdictional coexistence:** This path survives H1 as a logical possibility and is separately rejected by H2. The strongest objection to H2 is that prompt service satisfies both schemes and Rule 4(j) affirmatively mandates dismissal only after 120 days without good cause. H2's answer is the actual overlap in regulating service timing and authorized enlargement, not physical impossibility of satisfying both. *Noland*'s coexistence method and the surviving SAA protections remain explicit limits. No Justice's agreement on H1 is counted as an automatic join in H2; each independently adopts H2 above.

**Surviving statute with reasonableness or extension:** If H2 were rejected, a court would have to determine a lawful source and scope of extension, address both recipients to the extent necessary, and review the actual delay without converting allegations into findings. The Fifth Circuit's assumption of good cause did not find it. Neither *American Pipe* nor the circuit *McCormick* analogy establishes a general power to extend this provision, and *Pioneer* applies only where the governing rule authorizes its neglect inquiry. All eight decline this alternative as unnecessary; no conditional extension result is entered or counted as a second sufficient ground.

**Rule 12 waiver:** Raised in the district court, but the actual earlier transfer motion and complete appellate papers remain unavailable. Its legal effect and appellate preservation cannot be supplied by a nonjurisdictional label. No independent waiver ground is adopted. That path-specific source gap does not block the independently presented supersession issue or require an in-world production order.
