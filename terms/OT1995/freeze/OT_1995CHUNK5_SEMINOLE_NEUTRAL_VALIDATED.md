## Corrected questions, posture, and record

The plaintiff is the **Seminole Tribe**, suing Florida and Governor Lawton Chiles in his official capacity; the question is not a suit by tribal officials. The petitioner's merits questions ask (1) whether the Eleventh Amendment prevents Congress from authorizing tribal suits against States for prospective injunctive relief under Indian Commerce legislation, and (2) whether Ex parte Young permits prospective relief against a state governor to enforce that legislation. Distinguish clear congressional intent, constitutional abrogation power, consent, officer relief, the underlying duty, and the relief a cognizable action permits.

The district court denied immunity dismissal on June 18, 1992, 801 F.Supp. 655. The Eleventh Circuit reversed that denial and remanded for dismissal. Its consolidation with the Poarch Band's Alabama appeals did not make Alabama a party to this Supreme Court docket. Tjoflat wrote; Johnson joined; Black concurred in the result without supplying a separate rationale. The route was an immediate collateral appeal of an immunity denial, not a judgment adjudicating the merits of Florida's negotiations.

An important separate proceeding remains: on September 22, 1993, the district court granted Florida summary judgment on good-faith negotiations; the appeal, Eleventh Circuit No. 93-5256, was stayed pending immunity review. A ruling permitting this suit therefore does not establish bad faith, entitle the Tribe to an immediate compact, or dispose of that separate merits appeal. This distinction is documented in petitioner merits brief pp.8–9 n.1 and the source receipt; the full September 1993 merits decision was not independently read in this validation.

The complaint alleged a January 29, 1991 request for negotiations, more than 180 days before suit on September 19, 1991. It sought an order that the State, acting through the Governor, conclude a compact within sixty days, followed by mediation, and a declaration that Florida's permission of identified games required good-faith negotiations over the asserted Class III games, including machine and casino gaming. It also requested costs and other proper relief. These are allegations and requested remedies, not established statutory entitlements. The answer denied bad faith, acknowledged negotiations by the Governor's representatives, and disputed both the games requiring negotiation and a duty imposed specifically on the Governor. JA pp.12a–19a and 45a–46a preserve the distinction between the actual prayer and a narrower proposed good-faith injunction.

The runtime assertion that no material circuit conflict exists is incorrect. The lower opinion identifies Cheyenne River Sioux Tribe v. South Dakota, 3 F.3d 273, 280–281 (8th Cir. 1993), as contrary on IGRA abrogation. Its participation-in-negotiations discussion must not be inflated into a separate definitive waiver holding without reading that opinion.

## The lower court's distinct grounds

1. **Consent:** No express consent was established. Blatchford rejected reciprocal tribal/state plan-of-convention consent. Negotiating under IGRA did not establish the voluntary proprietary participation the panel associated with Parden; neither refusal nor participation could automatically waive immunity merely because the statute created that choice.
2. **Clear statement:** The panel found congressional intent sufficiently clear. Although §2710(d)(7)(A)(i) speaks of jurisdiction, its cause of action concerns a State's failure to negotiate, and a reading leaving no suable State would deprive the provision of effect. That holding did not itself establish Congress's constitutional power.
3. **Power:** The panel classified IGRA as Indian Commerce legislation, rejecting the tribes' Fourteenth Amendment entitlement and interstate-commerce alternatives. It distinguished Union Gas on two grounds: Indian commerce differs from interstate commerce; and negotiation of a sovereign compact differs from the proprietary activity of a landowner or railroad operator. The latter was an independent limiting ground, including if Indian Commerce abrogation were otherwise possible. These are lower-court positions subject to review, not existing Supreme Court rules.
4. **Young:** The panel relied separately on (a) discretion over compact terms and, in its reading, participation itself, and (b) the claim being in reality against the State, to which IGRA directs its commands. Neither premise may be adopted merely by reciting the judgment below.
5. **Unreached Tenth Amendment:** Footnote 2 expressly declined the defense because first raised on appeal. Florida's merits brief argues it is fairly included in the officer-relief question; the Tribe's reply disputes both preservation and question scope. A new anti-commandeering disposition requires an independent lawful-reach determination. New York does not eliminate that requirement.
6. **Severability and proposed Secretary route:** Part V preserves the remainder under §2721 and says a Tribe may notify the Secretary after immunity dismissal. That shortcut must remain distinguished from the actual judicial finding, mediation, proposed compact, and nonconsent predicates of §2710(d)(7)(B)(vii). An immunity ruling alone does not supply those statutory predicates or establish independent administrative bypass authority.

## Enacted text and remedial sequence

Article I, §8, cl.3 grants power to regulate commerce with foreign nations, among the several States, and with Indian tribes. The Eleventh Amendment addresses the judicial power and suits against States by citizens of another State or foreign citizens or subjects. The dispute includes the inherited broader sovereign-immunity doctrine, not merely the Amendment's literal party descriptions.

Under the official 1994 Code, §2710(d)(3)(A) requires a Tribe with jurisdiction over the relevant Indian lands to request negotiations; upon receipt the State **shall negotiate in good faith**. That duty is distinct from an entitlement to agreement on the Tribe's preferred terms. Section (d)(7)(A) separately covers: tribal failure-to-negotiate actions; State or tribal actions enjoining gaming in violation of an effective compact; and Secretary actions to enforce prescribed procedures. The present action invokes the first category.

The statutory sequence matters to both abrogation and officer relief:

- A tribal action under (A)(i) may begin only after the 180-day period following the request closes.
- Upon tribal evidence that no compact has been entered and that the State failed to respond or respond in good faith, the State bears the burden of proving good-faith negotiations. This is a specified evidentiary trigger, not a presumption that every failed negotiation is bad faith.
- A **court finding of bad faith** triggers a command to the State and Tribe to conclude a compact within sixty days. The court **may** consider public interest, public safety, criminality, financial integrity, and adverse economic effects on existing gaming. It **shall** consider a demand for direct taxation of the Tribe or Indian lands as bad-faith evidence.
- Failure to conclude a compact within that court-ordered period requires each side's last-best proposal to a court-appointed mediator. The mediator selects the proposal best comporting with IGRA, other applicable federal law, and the court's findings and order, and submits it to both parties.
- State consent within the next sixty days treats the proposal as an agreed compact. Only nonconsent during that period requires mediator notice to the Secretary, who prescribes procedures in consultation with the Tribe, consistent with the selected proposal, IGRA, and relevant state law.

Section (d)(4)'s no-new-state-tax-authority rule excepts regulatory-cost assessments agreed under (d)(3)(C)(iii). Lack of other taxing authority cannot justify refusal to negotiate. That exception is not a general exception to the separate mandatory bad-faith evidentiary treatment of demands for direct tribal/Indian-land taxation.

Class III gaming also requires the approved tribal ordinance or resolution meeting subsection (b), location in a State permitting the gaming for some person, organization, or entity, and conformity with an effective compact, subject to the statutory remedial provisions actually applicable. Compact approval, Federal Register publication, and applicable tribal licensing and regulatory conditions remain separate. Under (d)(8), Secretarial disapproval is limited to IGRA violations, other federal law not relating to jurisdiction over gaming on Indian lands, or federal trust obligations. After forty-five days without action a compact is deemed approved only insofar as consistent with IGRA; publication remains required. No immunity disposition itself licenses gaming, approves a compact, or adjudicates the scope of games Florida permits.

Section 2721 expressly preserves remaining provisions when a provision is invalid. Severing an unavailable enforcement route does not itself draft a replacement route or establish the validity of an unpresented administrative program.

### Union Gas: actual force, not a label

Pennsylvania v. Union Gas Co., 491 U.S. 1 (June 15, 1989), remains inherited precedent within its actual scope unless expressly changed. The entire CAP opinion body, including all writings and notes, was read; the official United States Reports PDF independently confirms the critical divisions and White's Part II.

- **Statutory abrogation:** Brennan's Parts I–II supply the Court's treatment of CERCLA as amended by SARA. Brennan, Marshall, Blackmun, Stevens, and Scalia support the clear statutory authorization for private damages litigation against States. It rests on the combined enacted text, not a universal rule that mentioning States in any definition always suffices.
- **Constitutional authority and judgment:** Brennan, Marshall, Blackmun, and Stevens support Part III; White separately agrees that Congress has authority under Article I to abrogate immunity and would affirm. Thus five support the constitutional conclusion necessary to allow this CERCLA/interstate-commerce suit. The fractured rationale does **not** make the holding disappear.
- **Rationale limit:** White expressly disagrees with much of Brennan's reasoning and supplies no narrower comprehensive rationale. Do not assign five votes to Brennan's plan-of-convention account, the full reasoning comparing Commerce and Fourteenth Amendment powers, or every prospective application to all Article I powers. The case did not adjudicate IGRA or Indian-Commerce compact negotiations. Conversely, the lower court's proprietary/governmental limitation is not an express Supreme Court holding in Union Gas.
- **Hans and separate positions:** White's note 8 preserves Hans. Stevens's separate concurrence treats nontextual immunity as a judicial doctrine Congress may displace, while recognizing the constitutional text itself cannot be amended by statute. Scalia, joined by Rehnquist, O'Connor, and Kennedy on Parts II–IV, defends constitutional state immunity, Hans, and the distinction between later Fourteenth Amendment authority and original Article I power; O'Connor also writes separately. Scalia nevertheless joins the statutory clear-statement holding. These are date-eligible positions, with their actual scope; no current-case vote follows automatically.

The necessary present inquiry is whether to extend/apply the inherited constitutional conclusion to IGRA, distinguish it on a defensible ground, or candidly reconsider it. Neither treating the entire plurality as binding nor treating all five constitutional votes as legally irrelevant is accurate.

## Supported alternatives and their counterarguments

**Allow the State action under IGRA.** The Tribe argues Indian Commerce authority and the federal responsibility for Indian relations are at least as strong as the interstate-commerce authority recognized in Union Gas; IGRA's express State-directed remedy satisfies clear-statement doctrine. Florida argues consent to commerce regulation is not consent to this federal suit, Indian and interstate applications differ, and sovereign compacting is unlike proprietary activity. A decision applying Union Gas must explain why those distinctions do not govern; it may leave broader abrogation questions open. Relief would remove the immunity bar and permit the proper remaining proceedings, not adjudicate bad faith.

**Preserve Union Gas but distinguish IGRA.** Florida supports an Indian/interstate distinction and the lower court's governmental/proprietary distinction. The Tribe answers that the constitutional text grants commerce power together, federal power is especially strong in Indian affairs, and the distinctions do not follow from the actual controlling constitutional conclusion. A narrow distinction must be justified on its own terms; merely calling Union Gas fractured is insufficient. Officer relief remains a separate issue.

**Reconsider the inherited immunity precedents.** Florida expressly requests overruling Union Gas if it cannot be distinguished, invoking structural state immunity and constitutional stare decisis; the Tribe seeks preservation and also offers reconsideration or limitation of Hans for congressionally authorized tribal litigation. The Union Gas disagreement supplies both sides' date-eligible constitutional arguments, but any change requires candid precedent treatment and analysis of the actual statutory and constitutional scope. A decision on one precedent need not erase Fitzpatrick's distinct Fourteenth Amendment rule or decide every federal enforcement mechanism.

**Allow prospective relief against the Governor even if Florida is immune.** The Tribe argues a duty to negotiate in good faith is judicially enforceable although compact terms remain discretionary; federal statutory violations qualify under Young; the Governor's participation supplies the necessary enforcement connection; and reference to state gaming law gives content to a federal obligation rather than making this a Pennhurst state-law claim. Florida answers that the actual complaint seeks a mandatory compact and game-scope declaration, IGRA addresses the State rather than an officer, the required decisions are discretionary, and coercive relief reaches sovereign powers. Proper analysis must distinguish a narrower good-faith injunction from directing a particular compact or games and from retrospective relief.

**Reject or confine officer relief on an independently sufficient ground.** Possibilities requiring separate examination include the defendant's actual connection and authority, a genuinely state-law rather than federal claim, an impermissible demand to dictate discretionary terms, or incompatibility of the requested relief with IGRA's enacted remedial route. None follows solely from Florida's immunity or from the word “State” in the statute. The detailed judicial/mediator/Secretary sequence is relevant to the relief Congress provided; its existence does not by itself establish either a general exclusion of Young or a license to impose remedies outside that sequence. Any route based on implied-action/remedial exclusivity doctrine must receive provision-specific authority and adversarial testing; Suter alone does not decide that issue.

**Reach a preserved narrower ground, or preserve an unready ground for remand.** Constitutional avoidance does not authorize adding an administrative bypass or disregarding question scope. If any threshold or remedial path needs facts about negotiating authority, preserved game-scope claims, or events beyond the sourced record, identify those facts and obtain bounded record support before deciding that path. A Tenth Amendment judgment needs its own reachability analysis because the lower court refused to consider it.

## Remedy limits and remaining work

Affirming the immunity dismissal forecloses this action on the adopted grounds; it need not invalidate all of IGRA or determine independent Secretarial authority. No path establishes an immediate compact, a bad-faith finding, a gaming license, damages, a tax power, or an unconditional Secretary bypass.
