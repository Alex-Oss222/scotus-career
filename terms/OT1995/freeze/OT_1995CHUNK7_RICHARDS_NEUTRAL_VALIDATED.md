**Finality assessment on the supplied orders:** Cox Broadcasting, 420 U.S.469, 477–487, permits §1257 review in identified categories despite additional state proceedings. Its first category covers a finally determined federal issue where further proceedings are preordained and the matter is practically concluded; state procedural labels do not alone control. Here state Counts 3 and 4 had already been rejected, the challenged judgment conclusively bars the remaining Richards-Hill federal claims, and the remaining Buck Act claim belongs to the separate federal-employee group outside this review. On that record, implementing the bar against these petitioners supplies a supported first-category finality basis rather than an open merits trial that could make their nonparty-binding objection disappear. The distinct Buck Act proceedings do not establish unfinished merits work on Richards and Hill's claims. This is a legal assessment of the supplied orders, not an invented later judgment. Southland, 465 U.S.1, 6–8, illustrates Cox's separate fourth category; its arbitration rationale cannot be transplanted here because reversal of preclusion would permit, rather than terminate, litigation of these claims. No independent tax-merits adjudication follows from accepting review of the dispositive nonparty bar.

Bedingfield, 527 So.2d 1270, consolidated the City of Birmingham/finance director's challenge and individual employees' challenge. The earlier complaints pleaded federal and state constitutional theories. The appellate opinion discussed state-law authorization, population classification, and collection authority. The present Alabama majority reasoned that the federal theories could have been, and arguably were, adjudicated. Richards and Hill were not parties to Bedingfield, which was not a class action. Their current complaint challenges the exemptions and seeks relief against their own tax liability, including amounts already paid. Petitioners assert that the earlier taxpayers represented only themselves, neither side sought class treatment or additional joinder, and petitioners received no notice that their claims could be impaired. ## Entering law, presently established

The Fourteenth Amendment's Due Process Clause protects the opportunity to be heard before a person's legal rights are conclusively adjudicated, subject to constitutionally sufficient representative and other recognized binding relationships. The question is whether the asserted public-right representation supplies that relationship here, not whether every person must always appear individually.

*Hansberry v. Lee*, 311 U.S.32, and *Martin v. Wilks*, 490 U.S.755, supply the nonparty/adequate-representation framework identified by the safe handoff. Martin, 762 n.2, expressly retains adequate representation, control of litigation, and special remedial schemes consistent with due process. Its joinder principle is not an instruction that notice of another person's suit invariably imposes a duty to intervene. No categorical conclusion that absence of Rule 23 treatment alone invalidates every taxpayer representative judgment follows.

**Actual Matsushita, February 27, 1996:** O'Connor's Part IV-A, joined by all nine Justices, establishes that §1738 cannot bind an absent member through constitutionally inadequate representation; notice, objection, and opt-out opportunities do not independently cure inadequate representation. Part I distinguishes settlement capacity from a representative's authority to bind absent persons. Part IV-B, joined by the eight non-Stone Justices, reserves the complete scope of permissible collateral review, including the full-and-fair-litigation contention. The decision establishes neither actual inadequacy in every class judgment nor a universal license to retry state-law error. Its ordinary Delaware-recognition holding is distinct from the constitutional binding prerequisite. Section 1738's interjurisdictional recognition command is not an independent reason Alabama must bind these strangers to its own earlier judgment; the constitutional minimum bears on the asserted binding relationship.

**Actual Johnson v. De Grandy, June 30, 1994:** Souter's Parts I-A and I-B, joined by Stevens, O'Connor, Kennedy, and Ginsburg among the seven-Justice coalitions, give a state validation its actual state-law scope and distinguish an independent nonparty enforcement suit from appellate review of a judgment against that litigant. They establish no universal privity or reopening rule. Scalia and Thomas's different disposition is not a join in those propositions.
