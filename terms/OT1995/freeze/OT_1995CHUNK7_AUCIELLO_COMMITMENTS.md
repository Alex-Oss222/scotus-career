## Common sufficient ground and operative limits

The following propositions are independently acceptable to each Associate identified below, at this level of generality.

1. **The Act leaves this timing policy to the Board within statutory limits.** Sections 7 and 9 protect employee choice and majority representation; §§8(a)(1), (2) and (5) do not confer an employer's unconditional right to raise an unasserted good-faith doubt after an outstanding offer has been accepted. The established incumbent has a continuing, rebuttable presumption after the previous agreement expires. Neither that presumption nor the absence of a timely challenge establishes actual majority support, but a legally sufficient doubt is itself distinct from actual loss. *Brooks*, *Fall River* and the surviving presumption framework permit the Board to reconcile continuity with orderly challenges; they do not require uninterrupted proof of actual support as a condition of every further bargaining act. Section 8(d)'s duty to execute an agreement reached, upon request, fits a lawful acceptance cutoff and does not compel agreement to an unaccepted proposal.

   This conclusion does not reason backward from the existence of a contract to the union's authority. The antecedent question is whether the Act requires a latent doubt about an established incumbent to defeat acceptance. It does not. *Garment Workers*' initial recognition of an actually minority union does not answer that question. Its employee-choice and §8(a)(2) limits survive, and the Court decides nothing about a preserved actual-minority case. The 1977 certification supplies no new conclusive certification year in 1988.

2. **The explained rule is rational and consistent with that statutory range.** Where the employer knows the evidence underlying its asserted doubt before the union accepts its still-outstanding offer, the Board may ordinarily require the employer to act on the doubt before acceptance. A generally available post-acceptance assessment period would leave an accepted agreement contingent on later litigation about an uncommunicated assessment and permit selective disavowal after the agreement's consequences become apparent. Those are prospective policy reasons, not findings that Auciello acted dishonestly or opportunistically. A subjective bad-faith finding is unnecessary to this timing rule.

   The employer's risk of prematurely withdrawing recognition is substantial but does not compel the requested grace period. Leaving an offer outstanding is distinct from the factual assessment needed to establish a lawful withdrawal of recognition. The Board can take account of the employer's control of its proposal and the legally available means of raising a representation issue before acceptance. This is no general license to abandon good-faith bargaining, and invoking an election route remains subject to that route's requirements. The Board need not make an accepted agreement defeasible until management completes its assessment of previously known information.

3. **Policy review does not substitute for evidence review.** *MCI*, *Lechmere*, *Town & Country* and *Holly Farms* require independent respect for statutory boundaries before permissibility review. No enacted majority rule or binding judicial construction requires the particular post-acceptance doubt defense sought here. Within the remaining range, *Curtin Matheson* and *Fall River* support review for rationality and consistency with the Act. The supplemental Board decision expressly explains a legal timing rule; it does not purport to find that the employer's evidence proves no reasonable doubt. Its reasons are the agency's own. A court need not invent an evidentiary rationale, an agency position or a further explanatory remand.

   Neither *Curtin Matheson*'s presumption ruling nor *ABF Freight*'s remedial discretion makes all Board decisions conclusive. *ABF Freight* preserves statutory purpose, adequate explanation and review for arbitrariness; it supplies no majority-status rule. *Health Care* and *Holly Farms* permit a functional construction only within actual statutory predicates, not their replacement. Those principles are satisfied here without deciding that the statute compels this particular policy for every future Board.

4. **The received presumptions and separate exceptions remain intact.** The ordinary certification-year conclusive presumption, limited contract-period conclusive presumption ordinarily extending no more than three years, and post-expiration rebuttable presumption perform different work. The recognized unusual-circumstances qualification includes the illustrative dissolution/defunct-union and substantially-all-members-and-officers affiliation transfer through schism examples in the authorized extract. They are not an exhaustive list. The Board brief's statement that none occurred is advocacy, not a new judicial finding; no such claim is decided here.

   Separately, the Board reserves case-by-case treatment when new information and acceptance arrive virtually simultaneously, illustrated by employees first presenting signed anti-representation petitions at that time. The Court preserves that reservation without deciding its hypothetical outcomes or making a completed later assessment of earlier-known information equivalent to simultaneous receipt. The First Circuit's accepted treatment places this case outside that exception.

   The representation/decertification petition window is also separate: for an agreement of three years or less, the stated ordinary window is more than 60 but less than 90 days before expiration, with healthcare and seasonal operations treated differently. This election route is not permission for unilateral refusal to bargain during an agreement. No actual petition or employee election result is supplied here. The Board's separate principle concerning bargaining when both employer and union know of actual loss of majority is preserved as an agency position in a different setting, not applied to an invented finding in this case. The Board's express reservation of its position on a post-acceptance actual-minority claim remains undisturbed.

5. **This record falls within the upheld timing rule.** The supported lower determination is that the relevant evidence was known before acceptance and that the reserved simultaneous-arising exception does not apply. The vice president's earlier list and the accepted knowledge determination are not findings that each resignation, crossing, reported departure or expression of dissatisfaction opposed representation. The Board did not concede, and neither tribunal found, a sufficient objective good-faith doubt. No such finding is needed because the legal timing ground independently disposes of the preserved defense.

   Petitioner's Thanksgiving-week assessment account does not change the accepted sequence into contemporaneous receipt. Nor is it necessary to decide that a particular number of days was a reasonable assessment period. The Board's alternative reasonable-period contention is not an independently adopted affirmance ground. The Court makes no finding that petitioner had adequate doubt by November 18, 21 or 23, or that thirteen membership resignations establish a representational minority. Actual lack of support calls for its own numerical showing; sufficient objective doubt can rest on circumstantial evidence under the received framework. This decision alters neither evidentiary route where legally available and does not decide whether the Board could abolish good-faith doubt generally.

6. **Affirmance preserves the existing remedy without enlargement.** The First Circuit's enforcement judgment stands. On the Union's request, the employer must put the accepted agreement in writing, sign and effectuate it retroactive to November 27, 1988; the existing make-whole and cease-and-desist requirements remain enforceable. No amount, new liability finding or independent award is supplied. There is no remand to adjudicate doubt sufficiency, no election condition on enforcement, no automatic decertification and no newly prescribed compliance hearing. Any genuinely remaining implementation matter retains its lawful scope; no outstanding proceeding is invented. Ordinary mandate procedure applies without a special date.
