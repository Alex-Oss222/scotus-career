### Stone’s Disposition

**Affirm the judgment of the Supreme Court of Alabama.**

**Question One:** Yes. Once the Alabama Supreme Court identified the defect in the jury’s use of transactions occurring outside Alabama, it provided a constitutionally sufficient remedy by conducting judicial review and entering the remitted judgment through Alabama’s established procedure. The Federal Constitution requires a meaningful remedy for the identified defect. It does not require this Court to devise an additional remedy merely because another procedure could have been chosen.

**Question Two:** No. The $2 million punitive award does not violate the Due Process Clause merely because it is 500 times the compensatory damages. The Fourteenth Amendment requires lawful adjudication and meaningful judicial review against arbitrary or legally impermissible punishment. It does not establish a substantive federal ratio, numerical ceiling, or independent power in this Court to replace a State’s lawfully reviewed punitive judgment with the amount federal judges would have preferred.

The judgment is therefore **affirmed**.

### Stone’s Reasoning

BMW possesses a property interest protected by the Due Process Clause. Its corporate form neither deprives it of the procedural protections necessary to defend that property nor enlarges those protections into constitutional interests that their source does not supply.

That distinction is important here.

Legal personhood does not itself answer what constitutional protections an artificial entity possesses. Under Stone’s jurisprudence, an entity receives the protection necessary for its property, contracts, process, and lawful operations, while rights inseparable from human personality do not arise merely from corporate form. MASTER_JURISPRUDENCE -(4) BMW therefore may invoke due process to challenge the lawful basis and procedure by which Alabama imposes a monetary judgment upon it. It is entitled to notice, an opportunity to defend, lawful adjudication, and meaningful judicial review. MASTER_JURISPRUDENCE -(4)

But recognition of that protected property interest does not answer BMW’s second question.

A right to lawful process does not silently become a substantive right to a particular degree of punishment. Stone’s jurisprudence requires each legal consequence to obtain its own source. One valid constitutional protection does not generate another merely because the two concern the same property. MASTER_JURISPRUDENCE -(4)

BMW must therefore identify a constitutional source for the proposition that a punitive judgment, reached through constitutionally adequate procedures and surviving meaningful review, becomes invalid merely because its amount bears a large ratio to compensatory damages.

The existing simulated decisions do not supply that rule.

In *TXO Production Corp. v. Alliance Resources Corp.*, the Court sustained a substantial punitive award after jury determination, trial-court review, and state appellate review. No controlling constitutional ratio, potential-harm formula, or general substantive excessiveness test emerged from that judgment. HOLDINGS(20261004-030302)

*Honda Motor Co. v. Oberg* establishes a different proposition. A State may not eliminate meaningful judicial review of punitive-damage amounts without providing an adequate substitute. But *Oberg* expressly does not establish a permissible ratio, maximum award, mandatory remittitur, or federal formula for determining the proper punitive amount. Its concern is protection against arbitrary deprivation through adequate process, not creation of a federal civil sentencing code. HOLDINGS(20261004-030302)

Those decisions resolve the constitutional question presented here.

Alabama afforded BMW a jury trial, post-verdict review, and appellate review. The Alabama Supreme Court did not leave the original verdict untouched after concluding that the jury had improperly relied upon transactions outside Alabama. It addressed the identified defect and entered the remitted judgment now before this Court.

That distinction also preserves the proper treatment of out-of-state conduct.

Alabama may not use its courts to impose punishment for conduct beyond the lawful scope of its authority merely because similar conduct occurred elsewhere. A reviewing court may therefore determine whether a punitive judgment rests upon transactions the State lacks authority to punish.

But that rule does not mean all evidence of conduct occurring elsewhere is constitutionally forbidden. Such evidence may bear upon knowledge, intent, deliberateness, notice, or the existence of a common policy when governing law makes those matters relevant. The constitutional problem arises when the State uses foreign conduct as an independent object of punishment, not merely when evidence concerning that conduct appears in the record.

The Alabama Supreme Court identified that distinction and altered the judgment accordingly.

No further federal remedy follows automatically.

The first question presented concerns whether Alabama supplied a meaningful remedy for the defect it identified. It did. The Constitution does not require this Court to restore the original $4 million verdict, prescribe a new trial, or select another amount simply because one of those remedies might also have been lawful.

The second question is narrower still.

BMW asks whether the remaining $2 million judgment is unconstitutional because it is 500 times the compensatory award. That argument asks this Court to move from procedural supervision to substantive recalculation.

The Due Process Clause supplies no mathematical conversion between compensatory injury and civil punishment.

Punitive damages do not perform the same function as compensation. Compensatory damages measure loss. Punitive damages address punishment and deterrence. There is therefore no constitutional reason that one must increase in direct mathematical proportion to the other.

A large ratio may accompany a relatively small compensatory injury without establishing that the punitive judgment rests upon an unlawful basis. Conversely, substantial compensatory damages do not necessarily justify substantial punishment. Ratio alone cannot perform the constitutional work BMW assigns to it.

Comparable statutory sanctions and the reprehensibility of conduct may be relevant under state law or under an established review procedure. But their relevance does not convert them into constitutional elements unless the Constitution or governing precedent supplies that consequence.

The Court should therefore resist creating a new constitutional guidepost system merely because punitive damages require judgment.

Judicial review is necessary. Judicial substitution is not.

Courts may ask whether punitive damages were legally available, whether the jury was properly instructed, whether sufficient evidence supported punitive liability, whether impermissible considerations entered the verdict, whether a statutory limitation applies, and whether the State supplied meaningful review.

Those are legal questions.

The separate judgment about how much punishment is warranted, where state law assigns that function to a jury subject to lawful review, does not become an independent federal constitutional question merely because the number is large.

Nor does this conclusion immunize corporate defendants from unlawful punishment. BMW receives the same protection Stone’s jurisprudence gives any artificial entity with property at stake: full protection of the property, process, and legal operations the Constitution actually reaches. Corporate status supplies neither a discount in constitutional protection nor a premium unavailable from the constitutional source itself. MASTER_JURISPRUDENCE -(4)

The Fourteenth Amendment protects BMW against deprivation without due process of law.

It does not promise BMW that, after receiving constitutionally adequate process, this Court will independently decide whether Alabama punished it too severely.

### Stone’s Operative Limit

This holding does not make punitive damages unreviewable.

It does not authorize Alabama to punish conduct outside its lawful authority.

It does not prevent courts from correcting erroneous instructions, insufficient evidence, impermissible considerations, procedural unfairness, or violations of statutory limits.

It does not abolish traditional remittitur or require restoration of the original $4 million jury verdict.

It does not decide whether every form of judicial alteration of a punitive award is permissible.

It does not establish that corporations lack Fourteenth Amendment protection for property or process.

It holds only that, after constitutionally adequate adjudication and meaningful review, the Due Process Clause does not supply an additional substantive rule invalidating a punitive judgment merely because its amount, or its ratio to compensatory damages, appears excessive to a federal court.
