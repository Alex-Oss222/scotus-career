## Material corrections and additions before modeling

The prior packet correctly keeps State abrogation, officer relief, substantive duty, and available remedy distinct. The full government submission adds material specificity that must accompany that framing:

1. **A Young-only reversal expressly leaving State abrogation undecided is a briefed alternative.** The government proposes it in p.19 n.5; this is more specific than allowing officer relief only after deciding that Florida is immune.
2. **The government's Young theory operates through IGRA's own limited remedial sequence.** It supports a judicial determination of failure to negotiate in good faith and the prescribed negotiation, mediation, and, if necessary, Secretarial procedures. It does not present the sequence as incompatible with Young, authorize an immunity-dismissal shortcut to the Secretary, or advocate unrestricted judicial enforcement of an agreement.
3. **The conditional-duty construction and the asserted absence of contempt or more intrusive orders are substantive parts of that theory.** They must be tested together with the statute's mandatory language, rather than omitting either side of the government's argument.
4. **A narrower direct-State abrogation argument is also supplied.** The government argues that Congress may at least authorize against the State the prospective relief already available against its officer under Young, alongside its broader Union Gas/Indian-affairs argument.
5. **The government did not supply a separately developed New York/Tenth Amendment merits disposition.** Its explicit Tenth Amendment discussion uses Garcia to rebut the lower court's traditional-governmental-function distinction; its conditional construction addresses related structural concerns without resolving preservation or establishing that IGRA survives every anti-commandeering objection.

These are substantive completeness changes, not new controlling law. The packet's alternatives should remain open, with the following opposing arguments and qualifications.

## Young: duty, state action, and the limits of the officer route

The government's first Question Presented concerns a suit against the Governor arising from the State's failure to negotiate **through him** in good faith. Its second concerns constitutional authority to abrogate immunity from a tribal suit for declaratory and ancillary injunctive relief. This ordering supports its proposed officer-first disposition; it does not change the Tribe's own questions or enlarge review. [Questions Presented, safe lines 1–18.]

The United States invokes Young, Edelman, Green, Quern, and related pre-divergence cases for prospective relief ending a continuing federal violation, while retaining the prohibition on retrospective monetary relief. Its application turns on a federal construction of IGRA's “such gaming” and “permits” terms. If the Tribe's interpretation is correct, the government argues, a declaration and an order requiring negotiations under that construction can enforce federal law. It does not ask this immunity review to find the Tribe's interpretation correct. The brief notes that the district court ruled against the Tribe on the game-specific and charitable-casino-enforcement claims and that the separate merits appeal is stayed. Note 3 also reports that the courts of appeals squarely addressing the gaming-scope issue adopted Florida's game-specific position. These are the government's descriptions of the merits authorities and proceedings; this source is not an independent full reading of those decisions. [P.8 n.2; pp.12–15, nn.3–4; safe lines 305–319, 449–590, 611–624.]

The government separates a mandatory good-faith obligation from discretion to propose or accept compact terms. It argues that §2710(d)(3)(A)'s “shall negotiate” leaves no discretion to negotiate in bad faith, while expressly stating that a federal court has no authority to interfere with the State's discretion to propose or agree to particular compact terms. It relies on Young's distinction between lawful discretion and acts an officer has no legal right to undertake, with a labor-law duty-to-bargain analogy. An immunity reversal on that theory does not entitle the Tribe to its requested games, terms, or an immediate compact. [Pp.15–16; safe lines 592–645.]

On the State-as-real-party objection, the United States acknowledges that the State is the real party in interest. Its argument is that this is the usual premise of Young, not an exception defeating it. Although IGRA names the State, a State can act only through officials; the federal obligation therefore reaches officials vested with state-law negotiating authority. The Fourteenth Amendment/Home Telephone analogy explains why a duty addressed to the State can bind the officials exercising its power. It is **not** an assertion that Congress enacted IGRA under §5 of that Amendment, that this statutory dispute establishes a Fourteenth Amendment violation, or that every official is a proper defendant. [Pp.18–19; safe lines 707–763.]

The brief asserts that there is “no dispute” about the Governor's authority to negotiate. Attribute that assertion to the United States. The prior packet records Florida's acknowledgment of negotiations by the Governor's representatives and its dispute over a duty specifically imposed on him. Neither statement establishes that the Governor alone may finally bind Florida to every proposed compact term. Negotiating authority, capacity to execute an enforceable compact, the federal duty's attachment, and the permissible order remain separate. A path depending on contested state-law powers still requires bounded record support.

Florida's objections retained in the packet consequently remain substantial questions: whether this official has the necessary connection and authority; whether the actual prayer demands discretionary compact terms beyond the supported federal duty; and whether the particular statutory remedy is available against an officer. The government's State-action analogy addresses the categorical argument that naming only the State forecloses all officer relief. It does not independently answer every cause-of-action, remedial, or authority objection.

## The remedial sequence and the conditional-duty construction

The United States describes the judicial bad-faith determination, sixty-day compact period, mediator, and Secretarial procedures as a **single statutory progression**. It rejects the lower court's inference that having backup procedures makes initial good-faith negotiation discretionary. It then calls the duty “conditional”: a State wishing to preserve its opportunity to shape gaming must negotiate in good faith; refusal to participate leads to loss of its statutory opportunity and practical influence. Its Spending Clause/Quern analogy is that a State can decline a conditional benefit, yet prospective relief remains available while it retains the benefit. The brief does not characterize IGRA itself as a spending enactment. [Pp.16–17; safe lines 647–705.]

Read this construction in its complete form. The government simultaneously calls refusal to fulfill the statutory good-faith duty a federal violation and treats the ultimate consequence of refusal as transition through Congress's specified process. It does not say that mere unwillingness to negotiate makes bad faith disappear, that participation in negotiations waives immunity, or that a State may retain its entire statutory role while disregarding the conditions. Conversely, its construction does not by itself establish that compulsory bargaining backed by ordinary judicial sanctions is permissible.

The qualifications are material:

- On p.25 the government says there is “no indication” that failure to agree to a compact, **or even to participate in negotiations during the court-ordered period**, was intended to expose the State to a more intrusive order or contempt. It argues that nonparticipation instead leads ultimately to Secretarial procedures. This is its limiting statutory interpretation, not a judicial holding, an express statutory no-contempt clause, or Florida's concession. The passage describes the State's failure during that remedial period; it does not independently settle every possible sanction against an officer. [Safe lines 1023–1038.]
- The assertion that a mediator would select the Tribe's proposal if the State submits none is expressly “presumably” true. It is a proposed consequence, not a textual default-selection command or an adjudicated finding. The same qualification appears in the p.25 description of the Tribe's proposal being recommended. A model adopting that consequence must justify it consistently with the mediator's statutory duty to choose the proposal best comporting with IGRA, other applicable federal law, and the court's findings/order. [P.16, safe lines 651–660; p.25, safe lines 1032–1038.]
- The government retains Secretarial action subject to IGRA's conditions. Loss of state bargaining influence does not erase the requirements concerning the selected proposal, IGRA, or relevant state law. Nothing in the brief authorizes direct resort to the Secretary merely because a court dismisses on immunity grounds.
- The original packet's exact sequence remains controlling as statutory description: the 180-day suit prerequisite; the Tribe's evidentiary showing before the burden shifts; an actual bad-faith finding; the first sixty-day period; the mediator's selection and submission; and the State's second sixty-day consent opportunity before notice and Secretarial procedures. The amicus's compressed paraphrases do not dispense with those predicates.

This source therefore supplies an affirmative answer to a claim that the existence of a detailed remedy automatically defeats Young: the government regards the officer suit as the means of invoking that very remedy. It does not provide a full, distinct analysis of an implied congressional displacement of Young under remedial-exclusivity precedents. A model adopting displacement must support and test that inference provision by provision, including why an officer action confined to the statutory sequence nevertheless conflicts with congressional design. A model rejecting displacement must likewise explain why the State-directed wording and limited remedies permit this defendant and this relief; an available federal duty alone is not the entire analysis.

## Avoidance and narrower relief actually proposed

At p.19 n.5, the government proposes reversal on Young alone without deciding whether Congress can abrogate Florida's immunity. Its predicate is that a determination of the Governor's violation and an order implementing the statutory sequence would **presumably** provide complete relief. The Tribe **might** then forgo relief against the State, or the Court or lower courts **might** deny that relief in equitable discretion. This is a proposed disposition and possible later choice, not an actual waiver by the Tribe, established mootness, or a binding conclusion that State relief is unnecessary. A Young-only decision must state what happens to each defendant's claim and preserve lawful treatment of the separate merits appeal. [Safe lines 766–785.]

Note 4 proposes a still less coercive remedial possibility: declaration of IGRA's meaning may suffice. If Florida's interpretation prevails, no negotiation order would issue; if the Tribe's interpretation prevails, the Governor might comply without an injunction. That possibility does not authorize a declaration solely about past conduct without a live federal dispute, predetermine the game-scope question, or establish that discretionary withholding of an injunction is mandatory. [P.15 n.4, safe lines 611–624.]

These proposals are distinct from rewriting IGRA to create a Secretary bypass. They also leave Young's own applicability to be decided; invoking avoidance cannot assume away the defendant, duty, and remedial questions that make that route possible.

## Direct-State action and inherited immunity law

The United States separately supports unmistakable statutory abrogation intent based on the combined jurisdiction, burden, and court-order provisions repeatedly naming the State. That agrees with the lower court's clear-statement conclusion while disputing its conclusion about constitutional power. It is abrogation, not a demonstrated voluntary waiver by Florida. [P.20, safe lines 797–821.]

Its principal constitutional argument extends Union Gas to the Indian Commerce Clause: both commerce powers arise in the same clause; Indian-affairs authority is plenary; and the federal treaty structure, history, and protective responsibility reinforce that authority. Note 6 expressly acknowledges that White agreed with Article I abrogation power but not much of the plurality's reasoning and supplied no additional rationale. The new source therefore confirms the packet's distinction between the inherited constitutional conclusion and the plurality's broader reasoning; it does not turn the entire plan-of-convention rationale into a majority holding. [Pp.20–24, n.6; safe lines 824–986.]

Four additional arguments should accompany the broad extension argument: federal responsibility toward dependent tribal sovereigns; relief limited to prospective measures; the sovereign rather than individual plaintiff; and Congress's statutory arrangement enlarging the States' opportunity to regulate Indian gaming while giving Tribes an enforcement safeguard. The Senate Report is legislative-purpose evidence, not enacted text or a finding that Florida consented to suit. [Pp.24–28; safe lines 971–1151.]

The prospective-relief argument supports a narrower constitutional position: Congress may at least dispense with Young's legal fiction and permit the same relief against the State in its own name. The government presents that as a minimum even if the full scope of abrogation power remains uncertain. Opposing analysis must confront the distinction between officer relief and direct relief against a State, rather than assuming Young itself has already erased it. The argument also depends on what relief Young and IGRA actually permit; it supplies no damages authority. [P.25, safe lines 1008–1038.]

The government accepts the inherited broader immunity premise of Hans, Monaco, and Blatchford for purposes of its argument. It distinguishes absence of automatic reciprocal constitutional consent from Congress's affirmative power to authorize suit; its foreign-sovereign and mutuality discussion is offered as an analogy for that congressional power. It does not demonstrate a prior holding allowing all such foreign-sovereign suits, establish tribal/state reciprocal consent, or advocate a separate overruling of Hans in this submission. The Tribe's separately sourced Hans argument in the original packet remains its own. [P.12, safe lines 473–488; pp.26–27, safe lines 1046–1113.]

Against the lower court's proprietary/governmental distinction, the government invokes Fitzpatrick, observes that Union Gas did not make hazardous-site ownership's character a factor, relies on Garcia's rejection of traditional-function immunity, and argues alternatively that tribal compacting historically occurred under federal auspices. Those are distinct answers to the distinction; they do not supply an enacted waiver based on Florida's negotiations. [Pp.28–30; safe lines 1153–1220.]
