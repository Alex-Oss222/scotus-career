## Complete historical comparison

The official report, 516 U.S. 400–414, was read in full, including the single opinion and all eighteen numbered footnotes. Thomas wrote for a unanimous historical Court; there are no separate writings or partial joins. Its terminal disposition is **“Reversed,”** not an express remand command. This handoff's remand is the modeled instruction for lawful proceedings after reversal; it is not attributed to the historical report. The report supplies no express command to enter judgment for the railroad that would contradict the frozen refusal to issue one. Rehnquist's vote is not transferred.

The dispositive historical premises remain intact:

- Pages 407–409 recognize the absolute duty of **actual performance**, not merely installation of a coupler. Page 409 expressly rejects negligence and proof of a particular defect as necessary predicates to a failure-to-perform claim.
- Pages 409–410 apply Affolder's proper-setting qualification to steps needed to prepare a **nondefective** coupler, including drawbar alignment. The railroad may show that a failure resulted from absence of proper positioning. This is not an inspection or due-care excuse after performance failure is established.
- Pages 411–414 reject a universal prohibition of preparatory work between cars and an implied statutory command to develop automatic realignment equipment. The historical discussion of industry practice does not replace that textual and precedential ground with safety balancing or an agency-deference test.
- Footnote 14, pages 410–411, is limited to this case and record: a failed coupling attempt would not by itself establish Hiles's entitlement to judgment as a matter of law. It does not make attempted impact a universal prerequisite or hold that any failed attempt is immaterial in every SAA action.
- Footnotes 11–12 distinguish the employee's SAA-based remedy from an ordinary FELA negligence theory that Hiles did not assert. They do not turn the absence of a negligence count into an absence of the employee remedial framework, and they do not abolish performance liability merely because the claimant did not identify a broken component.

The safe JA independently confirms the record limits already frozen: Miller's inspection account and nondefect opinion were supplied through the affidavit and excluded proffer (printed JA 10–11, 21); the court directed liability under its legal rule (22, 25); Fauver described the difficulty of moving the bar and the other car's distance (15–16), and the uncoupling and proposed curve/slope evidence appears in the proffer (24). The merits record dispute over what those facts establish remains the validated one. The historical opinion's general discussion of ordinary misalignment does not convert this excluded proof into a finding about the cause or condition of this particular displacement.

## Alternative paths and retained limits

The strongest affirmance argument is that requiring work between cars recreates the danger the Act addresses, and that equipment need not be mechanically defective to violate its absolute performance duty. It fails to justify this directed verdict because it omits the proper-setting qualification. The distinction is not satisfied simply by the railroad's use of the word “preparation”: it concerns what the appliance and worker were doing under the actual evidence.

The strongest argument for immediate railroad judgment invokes the lack of any attempted coupling and the absence of a pleaded specific defect. That extension also fails. The model's issue is whether the per se liability direction was correct; Affolder and Carter permit performance-based proof without a named defect, and the safe record contains excluded evidence and disputed application. Neither the historical opinion's case-limited footnote 14 nor its account of the claimant's theory supplies a finding that resolves every remaining procedural or evidentiary issue for the railroad. The remand preserves only legally available issues, not a newly supplied negligence action or entitlement to another verdict.

Former §2 applies to the 1990 injury; the 1994 recodification does not create a new substantive rule for that accident. Once an SAA violation contributing to the injury is proved, due care, inspection, later successful operation, contributory negligence and assumption of risk do not erase that absolute-duty violation. Carter's footnote 4 preserves the distinction from comparative negligence in an ordinary FELA negligence count. The causal requirement remains separate from proving a violation.

No material historical departure is retained. The more explicit factual and remedial limits are grounded in the already-validated record and original H1–H3; no new fact or frame from the comparator is admitted as a neutral premise.
