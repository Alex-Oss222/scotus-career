## Component commitments

**M1 — §1738 and Delaware effect.** A state judgment approving and incorporating a class settlement falls within §1738. The statute does not categorically exclude consent judgments, class judgments, or judgments approving releases of exclusively federal claims. The inquiry begins with the effect the rendering State gives its judgment. A court may not substitute a federal common-law preclusion rule merely because the surviving federal action lies within exclusive federal jurisdiction. Marrese's competency rule for adjudicated claims is not automatically the rule for a judgment embodying an agreed release. Its footnote 2 expressly leaves room for state settlement law to produce a bar where ordinary claim preclusion would not.

Delaware permits approval of releases encompassing claims that were not pleaded and could not have been adjudicated in the state action, within the limits of its representative-settlement law. Nottingham permits a release of exclusively federal claims resting on the same operative factual predicate; its factual-predicate and representation limits are part of that rule. Prezant requires an actual, express determination of the Rule 23 requisites and adequate representation; substantive settlement fairness, notice and opt-out rights are not substitutes. The approved and affirmed judgment here expressly incorporates the acquisition-related release, declares its preclusive consequence and identifies the federal actions. It cannot be treated as merely a private agreement outside the order. Nor may an asserted error in approving a release simply be retried as ordinary state-law error in a collateral federal action. Determine the judgment's lawful binding effect under Delaware law and the applicable federal limitations; do not manufacture a rule that every claim that could not have been adjudicated also could not have been released.

**M2 — exclusive jurisdiction and judicial authority.** Exchange Act §27 does not supply a categorical exception from §1738 for a state judgment approving a release of related federal claims. Exclusive federal jurisdiction over adjudication does not make an individual securities claim incapable of settlement, and a properly authorized representative settlement is not itself an adjudication of federal securities liability. The required showing of statutory incompatibility/implied displacement is not established merely by the exclusive-jurisdiction language or the interest in consistent federal adjudication. This is a ruling about the claimed automatic exception, not a grant to state courts of federal securities jurisdiction or unlimited class authority.

The representative objection must be addressed as such: an absent member's binding participation depends on lawful representation and constitutionally adequate process, not a fictional finding that silence was an individually negotiated contract. Section 1738 presupposes a judgment capable of binding that person. The difference between adjudication and release therefore does not answer M3. Nottingham itself withheld an injunction against the pending federal action and left the federal court to determine release effect. The present state judgment includes an injunction; this decision does not approve its extraterritorial operation, decide any contempt power, or infer that state wording can remove federal jurisdiction. Federal recognition of the incorporated release and direct coercive interference with a pending federal case are distinct. Any properly preserved contention that the latter defeats the former must receive its own legally supported treatment; it is not settled by quoting the order.

**M3 — due process, scope and remand.** Hansberry and Shutts require adequate representation; Shutts's notice and opt-out protections do not replace that requirement. Prezant makes the same distinction in Delaware settlement law. The record contains express adequacy/due-process findings, notice describing the federal litigation and release, opt-out procedures, discovery and an adversarial hearing. It also contains disputed objections about interests, representation, notice, incentives and scrutiny of federal value. Neither the assertion that no meaningful inquiry occurred nor the assertion that the recital conclusively answers every collateral constitutional objection can be made an appellate finding here. The Ninth Circuit did not decide the fact-specific constitutional alternative. A remand must permit consideration of properly presented unresolved grounds, including the threshold extent to which those grounds may be raised collaterally, without granting unrestricted relitigation of issues already conclusively determined in a constitutionally adequate proceeding.

The mandate preserves valid opt-outs, including the separately identified opt-out litigants outside this review. It does not decide the underlying tender-offer claims, imply a new private right of action, reinstate an unpresented securities count or award damages. Nor does it disturb a state settlement wholesale based on an unmade inadequacy finding. If the remaining defenses fail under the applicable law, recognition may then require dismissal of affected claims. That consequence is conditional on resolving the remaining grounds, not a present Supreme Court direction to dismiss.
