## United States v. Noland, 517 U.S. 535 (1996), No. 95-323, May 13

Sources: [Cornell syllabus](https://www.law.cornell.edu/supct/html/95-323.ZS.html); [Souter Court opinion](https://www.law.cornell.edu/supct/html/95-323.ZO.html); [official report](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/USREPORTS-517/pdf/USREPORTS-517-535.pdf). Local source stems: `Noland_ZS`, `Noland_ZO`, `Noland_official`.

**Topology and judgment:** **Reversed and remanded, 9–0**, Sixth Circuit 48 F.3d 210. Souter delivers the opinion of a unanimous Court. All nine participate; no separate writings or partial joins. No Marks question.

**Historical rule, 538–543:** Section 510(c)'s equitable-subordination authority does not permit categorical alteration of Congress's bankruptcy priorities. Here the Government's postpetition, noncompensatory tax penalty normally had administrative-expense priority under §§503(b)(1)(C), 507(a)(1). The lower court's judgment that such penalties should yield to other creditors was a legislative judgment at the same level of generality as Congress's contrary ordering, rather than a permissible equitable exception.

The Court does **not** freeze equitable-subordination doctrine at its 1978 boundaries. It recognizes leeway for judicial development and particular equitable exceptions, while rejecting wholesale revision of the statutory hierarchy. Its formulation includes the qualification that, **absent a need to reconcile conflicting congressional choices**, circumstances warranting subordination cannot be defined at the same level of policy generality as the priorities Congress enacted. Footnote 3 explains why conversion to Chapter 7 does not make this administrative-expense claim an ordinary prepetition claim: §348(d) excepts §503(b) claims.

**Express reservation, 543:** The Court declines to decide whether creditor misconduct must **always** be found before equitable subordination. The Government lacked a finding of inequitable conduct here, but the Court resolves the impermissibly categorical rationale instead. Neither universal creditor-misconduct necessity nor categorical immunity of tax penalties is the holding.

**Generic-comparator correction:** Add the remand and unanimity. Describe a categorical priority alteration **contrary to the priority Congress actually prescribed**, not merely a rule Congress declined to enact; the latter obscures the operative conflict. Preserve the express misconduct reservation.

## United Food and Commercial Workers Union Local 751 v. Brown Group, Inc., 517 U.S. 544 (1996), No. 95-340, May 13

Sources: [Cornell syllabus](https://www.law.cornell.edu/supct/html/95-340.ZS.html); [Souter Court opinion](https://www.law.cornell.edu/supct/html/95-340.ZO.html); [official report](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/USREPORTS-517/pdf/USREPORTS-517-544.pdf). Local source stems: `UFCW_ZS`, `UFCW_ZO`, `UFCW_official`.

**Topology and judgment:** **Reversed and remanded, 9–0**, Eighth Circuit 50 F.3d 1426. Souter delivers a unanimous Court opinion; all nine participate, no separate writings or partial joins. No Marks question.

**Two actual holdings:** First, WARN Act §2104(a)(5) **does authorize** a union to sue for its members' damages. The text's express inclusion of a representative of employees, the absence of a union's own damages entitlement, and the legislative development defeat the employer's effort to treat that authorization as inadvertent. Second, Hunt's third requirement—that neither the claim asserted nor the relief sought require individual members' participation—is prudential and can be abrogated by Congress. Congress has done so here. The holding is therefore stronger and more concrete than merely saying Congress *may* authorize such an action.

**Article III boundary, 554–558:** Hunt's first prong, requiring at least one member with standing in the member's own right, has an Article III foundation. The third prong concerns practical convenience and efficiency, rather than an irreducible case-or-controversy requirement; longstanding representative damages actions support the distinction. This decision does not turn all associational-standing requirements into optional prudential rules.

**Material reservations:** Footnote 6 finds the second, germaneness prong satisfied but **does not decide whether it is prudential such that Congress can definitively declare a relationship sufficient**. Germaneness sufficient for standing need not establish adequate representation sufficient to bind an absent individual through preclusion. The union's representational adequacy is uncontested here. Footnote 5 leaves open whether a simplified damages claim could satisfy Hunt's third prong even **without** congressional abrogation. Footnote 4 does not decide the union's asserted direct organizational injury/redressability theory. Footnote 1 assumes the pleaded allegation on dismissal and does not adjudicate whether notice was deficient because it was directed to the International's employee rather than the Local. Footnote 2 excludes the denial of leave to add individual employees from the questions presented.

**Generic-comparator correction:** Add the remand, unanimity, and actual statutory authorization. Preserve the constitutional/prudential distinction among Hunt's prongs and the reservations. No merits liability or backpay amount was awarded by this decision.

## BMW of North America, Inc. v. Gore, 517 U.S. 559 (1996), No. 94-896, May 20

Sources: [Cornell syllabus](https://www.law.cornell.edu/supct/html/94-896.ZS.html); [Stevens Court opinion](https://www.law.cornell.edu/supct/html/94-896.ZO.html); [Breyer concurrence and appendix](https://www.law.cornell.edu/supct/html/94-896.ZC.html); [Scalia dissent](https://www.law.cornell.edu/supct/html/94-896.ZD.html); [Ginsburg dissent and appendix](https://www.law.cornell.edu/supct/html/94-896.ZD1.html); [official report](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/USREPORTS-517/pdf/USREPORTS-517-559.pdf). Local source stems: `BMW_ZS`, `BMW_ZO`, `BMW_ZC`, `BMW_ZD`, `BMW_ZD1`, `BMW_official`.

**Topology and judgment:** **Reversed and remanded, 5–4**, Alabama Supreme Court 646 So.2d 619. Stevens for the Court, joined fully by O'Connor, Kennedy, Souter, Breyer. Breyer concurs, joined by O'Connor and Souter, while also joining the Court opinion in full. Scalia dissents with Thomas; Ginsburg dissents with Rehnquist. No partial joins and no Marks plurality issue.

**Court holding:** The $2 million punitive award remaining after Alabama's reduction of a $4 million jury award is grossly excessive under due process. The $4,000 compensatory award reflected the diminished value of Gore's repainted car. The three guideposts are reprehensibility, the relationship to **actual or potential** harm, and comparable civil or criminal sanctions. State punitive interests must be identified before excessiveness can be assessed. The decision is about this award, including its 500-to-1 ratio and the particular conduct and notice of penalties; it announces no fixed constitutional ratio or dollar cap.

**Out-of-state boundary, 568–574:** Alabama may not punish conduct lawful where it occurred and having no impact on Alabama or its residents, nor use punishment to impose its preferred policy on other States. The Alabama Supreme Court correctly disclaimed punishment of lawful out-of-state transactions, yet its remaining award still fails constitutional review. Do not present the Supreme Court decision as resting solely on an unrepaired nationwide multiplier. Footnote 20 expressly leaves open whether a State may attempt to change a tortfeasor's **unlawful** conduct in another State. Footnote 21 permits evidence of out-of-state transactions to illuminate reprehensibility; this is not a categorical evidentiary exclusion.

**Reprehensibility and ratio limits:** The Court accepts the Alabama jury's finding of material nondisclosure fraud and recognizes Alabama's legitimate ability to impose compensatory and some exemplary relief. It finds the purely economic injury, lack of health or safety danger, absence of affirmative deception of the sort present in the comparison cases, and BMW's reasonable understanding of disclosure safe harbors insufficient to justify this enormous award. Economic misconduct can warrant substantial punitive damages, including intentional affirmative misconduct or targeting financially vulnerable victims. The opinion therefore does not make economic injury categorically ineligible. Higher ratios may be justified by small compensatory awards for particularly egregious acts, hard-to-detect injuries, or harm difficult to value. The potential-harm dimension from TXO remains part of the inquiry.

**Remedy, 585–586:** Whether relief should take the form of a new trial or independent appellate determination of an appropriate award is left for Alabama's court in the first instance. The opinion does not itself set the proper amount. The illustration using Alabama transactions and a multiple of compensatory damages is not a mandated $56,000 award. Wealth does not eliminate constitutional limits, and the available sanctions did not provide fair notice of a multimillion-dollar penalty on this record.

**Breyer/O'Connor/Souter, 586–598:** Explains why the ordinary strong presumption favoring a procedurally proper award is overcome: **both** the absence of meaningful constraints in Alabama's standards as interpreted and applied in this case **and** gross disproportionality to legitimate punitive objectives. He joins the Court in full, and does not hold Alabama's general procedures categorically invalid; Haslip had approved similar procedures. The seven Green Oil factors supplied insufficient constraints here. Economic theories might supply constraints and support deference, but no particular economic model is mandated and none sufficiently explained the state result. His appendix checks the historical inflation comparisons used to defend million-dollar awards, rather than creating a separate damages formula.

**Scalia/Thomas, 598–607:** Reject substantive due-process protection against an unreasonable punitive amount and regard a jury determination with state judicial reasonableness review as supplying the required process. They dispute the legitimacy and administrability of the Court's guideposts, view the out-of-state discussion as unnecessary after Alabama's disclaimer, and distinguish using conduct to assess character from punishing that conduct. This is the more categorical doctrinal dissent.

**Ginsburg/Rehnquist, 607–619:** Emphasize federalism, respect for state courts' application of the Court's earlier instructions, ongoing state reform, and the difficulties of federal supervision without a workable formula. Alabama had already corrected the impermissible multiplier; nationwide evidence was admissible for pattern and intent, and BMW had not timely objected to the multiplication argument or requested the appropriate limiting instruction. Do not collapse this institutional-restraint dissent into Scalia's categorical rejection of substantive review. Footnote 4 declines to address multiple punitive awards for the same conduct; only one award is before the Court and BMW did not properly raise that separate issue. The appendix catalogs enacted and proposed state reforms; proposed measures are not operative rules.

**Generic-comparator correction:** Add the remand and exact 5–4 structure, include potential harm, preserve the no-bright-line and out-of-state-evidence qualifications, and distinguish both dissents. No later punitive-damages rules should be inserted into this 1996 comparator.

## Romer v. Evans, 517 U.S. 620 (1996), No. 94-1039, May 20

Sources: [Cornell syllabus](https://www.law.cornell.edu/supct/html/94-1039.ZS.html); [Kennedy Court opinion](https://www.law.cornell.edu/supct/html/94-1039.ZO.html); [Scalia dissent](https://www.law.cornell.edu/supct/html/94-1039.ZD.html); [official report](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/USREPORTS-517/pdf/USREPORTS-517-620.pdf). Local source stems: `Romer_ZS`, `Romer_ZO`, `Romer_ZD`, `Romer_official`.

**Topology and judgment:** **Affirmed, 6–3**, Colorado Supreme Court 882 P.2d 1335. Kennedy for the Court, joined fully by Stevens, O'Connor, Souter, Ginsburg, Breyer. Scalia dissents, joined by Rehnquist and Thomas. No separate concurrence, partial join, or Marks problem.

**Holding and route:** Colorado Amendment 2 violates equal protection because its broad exclusion of gay and lesbian persons from specific legal protection against discrimination lacks a rational relationship to a legitimate government purpose. It both withdraws existing protections and prevents their restoration through ordinary state or local governmental action. The Court affirms **on a rationale different from Colorado's fundamental-right-to-political-participation/strict-scrutiny analysis**. It does not adopt that theory, declare sexual orientation a suspect classification, or purport to apply heightened scrutiny. The breadth of this status classification makes the asserted freedom-of-association and resource-conservation explanations impossible to credit; a bare desire to harm the group is not a legitimate end.

**Construction and reserved reach, 626–631:** The Court relies on the state supreme court's authoritative construction, including Amendment 2's effects on specific public and private antidiscrimination protections. It rejects the claim that those protections are merely special rights. It suggests that the amendment might also withdraw protection under laws of general application, but expressly **does not decide that question**, since the state court did not. Even assuming some general-law protection survives, the specific disability is unconstitutional. Do not convert the suggested broader reach into a definitive holding that all ordinary legal protection disappears.

**Precedent and scope, 631–636:** The majority states the conventional rational-relation test and links the extraordinary breadth of the disability to the inference of animus. Its treatment of the political process concerns this sweeping targeted exclusion; it does not announce that every statewide restriction on local relief or every disadvantage imposed at a higher decisionmaking level is unconstitutional. It discusses Davis v. Beason in distinct components: punishment of mere advocacy is no longer good law; status-based denial of voting rights would require strict scrutiny; ordinary convicted-felon disenfranchisement is unaffected. The majority does not discuss or expressly overrule Bowers. Do not import later sexual-orientation or substantive-due-process law.

**Scalia/Rehnquist/Thomas, 636–653:** Characterize the amendment as withholding special protection while preserving ordinary generally applicable rights, and argue the majority has converted inability to obtain preferential treatment locally into an equal-protection injury. They rely on Bowers and the greater-includes-lesser argument to sustain disfavor of conduct that could then constitutionally be criminalized; reject a decisive orientation/conduct distinction; and invoke Salerno against this facial challenge. Footnote 2 addresses the state court's nonseverability reasoning. Historical polygamy restrictions and Davis support their claim that statewide entrenchment of a moral judgment is not unprecedented. They also dispute the majority's animus characterization and defend democratic resolution of the policy dispute. Footnote 1 records that the plaintiffs did not appeal the trial court's rejection of suspect/quasi-suspect status. Those are dissenting arguments and procedural observations, not additional majority holdings.

**Generic-comparator correction:** The existing short holding is directionally sound but should carry the actual 6–3 topology and the separate rational-basis route, the general-law-protection reservation, and the dissent's distinct doctrinal arguments.
