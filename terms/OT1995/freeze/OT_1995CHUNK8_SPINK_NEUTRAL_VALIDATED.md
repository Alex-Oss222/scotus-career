## Event, participation and questions

Granted certiorari review of the Ninth Circuit, 60 F.3d 616. Chief Justice Alex-Lamar Stone-Zsela and Justices Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer are the event-date Court. No case-specific nonparticipation is established; all nine participate at argument/submission and decision under the supplied event premises. No vote is assigned here.

The submitted questions concern fiduciary conduct and prohibited transactions when an employer amends and implements a pension plan to pay enhanced retirement benefits conditioned on releases, and the separate OBRA claim for service credit before the new law's effective date. Lockheed seeks reversal of the adverse portions; Spink seeks their preservation. The judgment below is mixed, not a reversal of every dismissal. The decree and unresolved alternatives described below constrain relief.

Section 3(21)(A) makes fiduciary status attach **to the extent** of discretionary plan management/control; authority/control over management or disposition of assets; compensated investment advice or authority/responsibility for it; or discretionary plan administration. The separate investment-company exception in (B) prevents fiduciary/party-in-interest status solely from the plan's investment, with its own-employees-plan qualification and preservation of other-law duties. Nothing in this record makes that exception operative.

Section 406(a) begins with the §408 exception and requires a fiduciary causing the plan to enter a transaction with the specified actual/constructive knowledge. The text identifies sale/exchange/lease, lending/credit, goods/services/facilities, transfer/use of assets for a party in interest, and certain employer-security/property acquisitions; §406(b) separately addresses fiduciary self-dealing, adverse representation and personal consideration. Employer status supplies a §3(14)(C) party in interest, not by itself a finding that the employer performed a §3(21) fiduciary function. No §408 exemption is claimed in the reported controversy. The preserved implementation allegation must be separately identified before deciding any §406 scope issue.

OBRA §9204(a)(1) applies §§9201–9202 to plan years beginning on/after January 1, 1988 and employees with an hour of service in a covered year. Section 9204(a)(2) has a distinct transition for pre-March 1, 1986 collective bargaining agreements: the specified first plan year after the earlier of January 1, 1990 or the later of January 1, 1988 and termination of the last qualifying agreement, disregarding extensions after February 28, 1986. No fact invoking that exception is supplied here. Section 9204(b) separately applies §9203 to covered plan years **and service performed on/after** January 1, 1988. Section 9204(c) conditionally permits delayed formal plan amendment until the first plan year beginning on/after January 1, 1989 if the plan operates in accordance with the new requirements in the interim and the amendment covers that interim period retroactively. Formal-amendment delay does not itself postpone substantive operation. The lower opinion's disputed characterization of prior-service credit as retrospective is not the statute's command; apply actual simulated Landgraf to each provision's operation.

## Supported contrary paths and component consequences

1. Identify amendment/adoption as sponsor conduct and test whether that resolves only the employer's capacity or also the pleaded implementation theory. The strongest counterargument is that the Retirement Committee's payment of plan assets may independently be administration or asset disposition. Employer benefit and party-in-interest status cannot substitute for §3(21)'s function requirement, but sponsor classification cannot erase another actor's alleged conduct.
2. If the implementation actor performs a fiduciary function, test whether ordinary payment of plan benefits conditioned on a release is the prohibited transaction alleged under §406(a)(1)(D), or whether the provision's transaction categories target a different exchange. The opposing path treats the release as a benefit procured for the employer from plan assets. The existence of surplus, the participant's enhanced benefit, and the employer's benefit each require statutory analysis rather than a conclusive label. A narrower capacity decision with remand and a broader transaction construction are both live analytical paths; no §408 exemption is silently created.
3. Keep §404 loyalty and §403(c)(1) anti-inurement alternatives separate from §406 and from particular-release enforceability. Account for unaddressed preserved alternatives on remand without deciding their merits from the absence of original filings. A ruling on plan amendment or §406 cannot automatically dismiss every count.
4. Apply OBRA's separate textual transition provisions first. Spink's position that past service may inform present accrual must confront the actual covered-plan-year/service language; Lockheed's prospective-only argument must distinguish provision-specific operation from merely using a past fact. Compare the claim for accrual, the participation amendment, the collective-bargaining transition and the conditional amendment delay without conflating them. No invoking collective-bargaining fact is supplied.

The final decree must identify which Counts I–III and fee consequences follow from each legal ground. The unpresented Count V and collateral-estoppel issues are not revived; a fee amount, loss finding or complete release-validity ruling cannot be invented.
