## Wood — corrected packet and live alternatives

### Objective corrections and record

The Ninth Circuit **reversed the District Court's denial of habeas relief**, rather than affirming a habeas grant. The District Court denied relief after an evidentiary hearing on April 12, 1993; the Ninth Circuit, No. 93-35549, reversed September 6, 1994, 34 F.3d 870; rehearing was denied November 16, 1994. Its conditional remedy permitted a retrial on premeditation within a reasonable time or reduction to simple first-degree murder. A January 13, 1995 District Court order implemented that mandate. No subsequent compliance or new Supreme Court action is established.

The consequential guilt issue is premeditation and aggravated first-degree murder, rather than identification of the robber or an assertion that Bartholomew fired no shots. He admitted the robbery and shootings but described accidental discharge and denied an agreement to leave no witnesses. Aggravated murder exposed him to death or life without parole; after penalty proceedings he was serving life without parole. Simple first-degree felony murder carried life with parole. The original death-stage history does not make the present claim a penalty-only Brady claim.

Rodney and Tracy supplied the direct guilt-stage premeditation evidence identified in the lower opinion. Stanley Bell's testimony belongs to the penalty proceeding. The gun's single-action operation and other circumstances remain evidence supporting the State; the asserted assembly/testing circumstances are contested filing assertions, not new findings by this validator.

Rodney's negative answers concerning participation in the robbery and presence in the laundromat were reported deceptive. Tracy's result must not be described as a scientifically established truthful account: the Ninth Circuit and opposition describe a technically inconclusive result with an examiner's personal belief in truthfulness, while the petition characterizes the result differently. Do not invent a test question asking whether Rodney helped Dwayne. A failed polygraph is not an adjudication of perjury or proof that another person committed the crime.

Washington's general guilt-stage exclusion absent stipulation, including for impeachment, is the operative admissibility premise presented. Penalty-stage admission of polygraphs is a distinct rule. The Ninth Circuit did not finally determine all admissibility questions; its theory included possible lawful investigative fruits, depositions, inconsistencies, and later examination of Rodney. Neither direct admission nor a categorical conclusion that all derivative uses are unlawful may be silently substituted for that theory.

The State says counsel already believed Rodney a liar and testified that disclosure would not have changed investigation, strategy, or cross-examination. The present authorized digest does not supply counsel Anderson's complete testimony. This may be stated as the State's contention, not as a dispositive unqualified concession. Rodney's later five-page examination is described in the source digest: several tests, a return after drugs, no recollection of being told results, no prior contact with trial counsel, and maintenance of his account. That interview does not by itself establish either that no lawful lead could exist or that a particular favorable lead would have resulted.

The respondent also invoked ineffective assistance and false-testimony theories; the Ninth Circuit did not reach those alternatives. Preservation, presentation, and any request to affirm on an alternative ground must be identified before adjudicating them. The admitted Brady issue does not silently dispose of every alternative habeas claim. The State's nonretroactivity argument likewise remains a distinct defense, requiring its actual presentation and finality analysis rather than automatic application of a historical habeas standard.

## Tuggle — corrected packet and live alternatives

### Lower judgment, concession chronology, and record

The original runtime statements that the Ake violation **is conceded** and that the Fourth Circuit performed ordinary harmless-error review are materially inaccurate. The District Court granted habeas relief June 8, 1994. The Fourth Circuit, June 29, 1995, 57 F.3d 1356, reversed and directed dismissal. On the submitted Ake/Zant component it treated Virginia as a nonweighing State and the separate vileness finding as sufficient to sustain the sentence despite the invalidated future-danger predicate. It did **not** make a separate whole-record constitutional harmlessness determination addressing lost defense psychiatric functions. Its statement that future dangerousness was no longer in the case is appellate legal reasoning; the jury had heard the evidence.

The 1984 jury found both future dangerousness and vileness. On the 1985 Virginia reconsideration, the Commonwealth conceded Ake error and advocated resentencing; Virginia recognized denial of independent assistance as error but sustained the sentence on vileness. In the **1995 opposition, footnote 1, the Commonwealth expressly withdrew the earlier concession as mistaken** and asserted request, threshold, available-examination, and prejudice objections. Consequently, no live unanimous concession of a completed violation may be the premise of the present Court's decision. The earlier state ruling and litigation concession are historical lower proceedings; their binding or preclusive effect, if any, needs legal analysis and is not assumed away or automatically converted into a permanent concession.

The bounded respondent appendix establishes the following documentary chronology:

- October 13, 1983 motion requested competency and offense-time mental-condition examinations under §§19.2-169.1 and 19.2-169.5, citing prior evaluations and possible disorder. The November 18 hearing and order granted those examinations.
- A separate November request sought expenses for independent evaluation of scientific/physical evidence, identifying pathology, serology, ballistics, and odontology. The court allowed ordinary access to the Commonwealth's evidence but denied independent-expert authorization; counsel excepted. Do not silently convert this particular list into a sentencing-psychiatrist request.
- The December 1 hospital report, signed by psychiatrist Miller Ryans and psychologist Arthur Centor, reported competency and offense-time sanity, stated that a future-danger opinion had been formed but was not being reported because the court had not requested it, and asserted that Tuggle had received Miranda warnings concerning use at a capital hearing and signed a waiver. These are statements in the report, not this Court's adjudication of warning sufficiency, waiver, or counsel's advance notice.
- The December 27 renewed motion sought transportation for examination by Dr. Showalter and his group, describing offense-time condition, present capacity, and assistance in the defense. At the January 3, 1984 hearing, counsel referred to prior psychiatric records, the seriousness of the charge, their original request for independent evaluation, and arrangements already made after funding was denied. The State argued that the supplied psychiatrist/psychologist examinations met its obligation and that additional evaluation was not required. The petitioner asserts that the expanded examination proceeded without the requisite counsel notice and that assistance would have affected rebuttal, cross-examination, and mitigation. The Commonwealth asserts warnings/waiver, defense initiation of examination, procedural default of the Fifth Amendment theory, and Buchanan-related limits, as well as no prejudice. The complete warning instrument, notice history, use of psychiatric evidence, objections, and complete sentencing record have not been adjudicated in this validation. Ake assistance and Estelle warning/counsel claims are distinct; they must not be conflated into one conceded error.

The petition's first question includes both Ake and Estelle in challenging the surviving-aggravator rationale. The assigned packet emphasizes Ake and sentence review. Other petition questions concerning vileness instructions, voir dire, and rape sufficiency are not automatically admitted or decided. The jury's ability to choose life despite one or both proved aggravators matters to sentence selection; surviving eligibility is not itself a finding about what the jury would have selected with different evidence or assistance.
