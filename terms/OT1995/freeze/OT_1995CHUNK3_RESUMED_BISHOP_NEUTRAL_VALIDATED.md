## 2. The complete reported factual foundation

### Taking, pursuit, and vehicle history

Near midnight, Bradley was teaching Rollins to drive his newly purchased Dodge Shadow in an East Orange parking lot. When they got out to exchange places, one man put a pistol to Bradley's head and demanded the keys; another held Rollins from behind with a hand over her mouth. The opinion reports that both victims got a good look at the gunman. Bradley promptly flagged down police and described the taking, automobile, and assailants. The opinion reproduces only the general description of the occupants as two Black men, not the full descriptions he gave.

Rhodes saw a matching automobile, followed it, and confirmed the plate by radio before activating lights and siren. The car accelerated, collided with another car, and struck a building. Rhodes saw a man climb from the driver's window, fall, rise, and flee; he chased, stopped, handcuffed, and arrested that man as Stokes. Two guns were found on the car's floor. The other occupant was not found then. The description supports the reported firearm act and arrest sequence; it does not establish an unreported trial instruction or which recovered gun supplied each count.

The interstate history of the particular Dodge is not merely an invented inference from its make: Becker's opinion expressly refers to the fact that this Dodge Shadow once traveled in interstate commerce, 66 F.3d at 593. Neither side's reported Commerce argument disputes that factual premise. No manufacturing state, shipment route, stipulation text, exhibit, or current interstate journey is supplied. Do not confuse the undisputed reported history with proof of an independent substantial effect from this individual taking.

### Identification facts and findings

Within ninety minutes of the taking, Bradley and Rollins separately entered a station room and viewed Stokes through a one-way mirror. Before viewing, they had overheard police speaking about having apprehended the man who stole the car. Each identified Stokes as the gunman. They later testified to those identifications and identified him in court.

Footnote 2 acknowledges that the show-up was arguably unnecessarily and impermissibly suggestive. It nevertheless reports two district-court determinations: as facts, the identifications came from each witness's own recollection of the crime rather than the suggestion; as the legal conclusion, the identifications were reliable. The appellate court found no clear error in the former and no error in the latter, citing *Biggers*. It did not simply hold that any sincere identification is admissible.

Known indicators are the close encounter, the reported good look at the gunman, the prompt description, the separate viewings, and the short interval. Known corrupting circumstances are the single-suspect station procedure and police comments suggesting that the culprit had been caught. The opinion supplies no exact viewing duration, illumination, detailed prior description, certainty declaration, cross-examination, or suppression testimony. Do not invent those details or count their omission from the opinion as an adverse factual finding.

*Manson* requires consideration of opportunity to view, attention, accuracy of prior description, certainty at confrontation, and elapsed time against the procedure's corrupting effect. It rejects automatic exclusion for unnecessary suggestion. Those are considerations within the totality inquiry, not a demand that an appellate opinion contain five newly labeled findings. A supported challenge to the reliability determination remains possible; the available source establishes that the lower courts made and reviewed that determination. Other evidence of guilt, including Rhodes's pursuit and arrest, cannot substitute for reliability of the identification itself. *Manson*, 432 U.S. at 114–17, including Stevens's concurrence, distinguishes those inquiries.

### Booking: what is known and what is not

Rhodes took Stokes to the station and booked him while videotaping the procedure. Stokes was limping. The opinion describes **one of the officers** asking **what was the matter with his leg**. Stokes answered that he had hurt it in an accident. It does not quote a question asking how the injury happened, request a narrative of the car chase, identify the questioner as Rhodes, or attribute knowledge of the chase to that questioner. Rhodes's personal observations and the questioner's established knowledge therefore must not be treated as identical facts.

The statement was unwarned and was admitted at trial over objection. The district court found that the question was part of a booking procedure designed to fulfill the government's obligation to provide medical attention if necessary. The appellate court found no clear error in that factual determination and upheld admission under the routine-booking doctrine. This is an actual purpose-and-context finding, not merely counsel's untested assertion of a medical purpose.

The visible limp and question about the condition provide an objective administrative explanation for the inquiry. The strongest counterpoint is that an accident answer could connect Stokes to the just-reported crash, and a direct question cannot be immunized merely by placing it at booking or describing a benign intention. The source does not establish an actual request for incriminating accident details, a deceptive medical pretext, follow-up investigative questioning, a medical emergency, the severity of the injury, or whether care was ultimately provided. None may be supplied as fact.

*Innis* distinguishes express questioning from its functional equivalent and excludes conduct normally attendant to arrest and custody from the latter formulation; the inquiry is not exhausted by subjective police intent. *Muniz* Part III-C treats reasonably related administrative booking questions as excepted but is a four-Justice rationale, and its footnote 14 excludes questions designed to elicit incriminating admissions. The Court's other portions distinguish compelled testimonial assertions from physical evidence and narrowly confined legitimate procedural communications. No cited received holding establishes an unlimited exception for every medical question. A conclusion that this particular question was legitimate medical intake must address its content and setting, not just its label. Conversely, its later evidentiary usefulness does not by itself prove that the question was interrogation.

The prior addendum's suggestion that the fuller suppression record is necessary to settle **any** suppression disposition is too broad if read to foreclose deciding the narrow claim on these reported facts and findings. The report provides an affirmative factual basis for a bounded admissibility decision. Additional material becomes necessary if a decisional path depends on contradicting that account, attributing particular knowledge or purpose to the questioner, deciding an unreported exchange, or concluding that a factual finding is unsupported. Source scarcity is not affirmative proof of any of those propositions.

### Videotape: distinct constitutional and evidentiary questions

The source establishes that the booking procedure was videotaped and that the jury was shown the tape over objection. It does not establish the tape's duration, whether it was shown completely or edited, every audible statement, the camera's view, captions, repeated playback, prosecutorial argument, limiting instructions, or the specific reasons for either side's evidentiary position. Do not claim that the tape was silent, that its only content was the limp, or that the accident answer was necessarily played audibly rather than proved separately.

The appellate court states that Stokes supplied no good reason against showing the tape and that the government supplied several good reasons in favor; it finds no abuse of discretion. Those are the reported judicial conclusions. They do not permit the neutral packet to manufacture the missing reasons. Equally, their omission from a footnote does not establish that no relevance or prejudice inquiry occurred.

Under *Muniz*, a recording's physical observations and testimonial content must be distinguished. Merely recording appearance or movement is not compelled testimonial disclosure. A factual assertion about an accident remains testimonial in nature even if a legitimate booking ground permits its admission. Suppression of an unlawfully elicited answer would not automatically suppress every separable physical portion of the recording. Physical evidence is still subject to the applicable evidentiary rules; its nontestimonial character alone does not decide relevance or unfair prejudice. No specific Rule 403 balance or independent video error can be reconstructed from the unenumerated arguments.

## 3. Lawful decisional and remedial boundaries

These are available legal paths for later independent modeling, not recommended votes or findings of present error.

1. **Apply the actual entering law to the federal-power and cumulative-punishment components.** The supplied neutral materials identify completed *Harris* as the controlling original-§2119 economic-class and same-prosecution cumulative-punishment decision. 2. **Sustain a challenged evidence ruling on its supported, legally sufficient grounds.** The identification and medical-intake findings and the rejection of the videotape objection are actual adjudications. A concise appellate explanation does not itself require another hearing, a transcript-production order, or remand. Affirmance must remain bounded to the reported claim, not declare every unreported trial detail or possible objection sound.
3. **Correct an identified legal error.** A remand is available if the Court identifies a wrong governing rule or a necessary issue genuinely left undecided, and explains why the existing findings do not resolve that issue under the correct rule. Examples of legally material propositions would be treating medical intent as conclusively sufficient regardless of an objectively incriminating interrogation, or excluding or admitting an entire recording without making a legally necessary distinction. These are conditional examples of error; the present source does not establish that the lower court did either. Failure to cite *Innis* or *Muniz* by name, use of the phrase routine booking, and absence of lengthy appellate analysis are not independently such errors.
4. **Address prejudice only after identifying trial error.** For preserved constitutional admission error on direct review, *Chapman* places the harmless-beyond-a-reasonable-doubt burden on the beneficiary. For an ordinary nonconstitutional evidentiary error, *Kotteakos* asks about the error's effect in the whole trial rather than whether other evidence could support conviction. The pursuit, identifications, and recovered guns may matter to that inquiry but do not alone establish harmlessness. The unavailable trial presentation, objections, relevant recording, arguments, and instructions would be necessary for a new trial-specific prejudice determination not already supported by the source. No harmlessness finding is made here.
5. **Keep the remedy tied to the component.** If legal error warrants further appellate consideration, the remand must identify the question to be resolved and the effect on the affected Stokes judgment or counts. Suppression, redaction, harmless-error review, and a new trial are distinct consequences. A new trial does not follow simply because an answer should have been excluded; an acquittal does not follow from ordinary trial error. No current basis is supplied for reopening Bishop's plea because of Stokes's evidence claims.

The complete lower opinion supplies no affirmative showing of a deficient record in the lower courts, a refusal to hold a required hearing, or an overlooked material trial objection. It supplies a limited account of a fuller record. The present materials therefore support a real adjudicative choice about the reported booking question and findings, but do not support an **exploratory remand because researchers lack the underlying record**. Any later determination that the lower court's stated legal rationale itself is erroneous must say what the error is and why a bounded affirmance or application of the correct rule to the reported facts is insufficient.
