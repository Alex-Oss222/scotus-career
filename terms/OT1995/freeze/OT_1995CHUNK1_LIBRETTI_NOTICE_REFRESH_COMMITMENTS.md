## Reported notices and their exact weight

The primary judicial report describes a court/counsel exchange about a special-verdict form at **1 Tr.8**. The extract does not quote that exchange. It also reports that, at **1 Tr.188**, the judge told prospective jurors in Libretti's presence that the indictment sought forfeiture of property constituting or derived from CCE proceeds and that the jury would answer a specific question about it at the end of the case. Both are affirmative pretrial notice evidence. The trial transcript itself has not been directly obtained for these pages; this file attributes the facts to the validated primary judicial report and does not pretend to quote the entire underlying exchange.

The Tr.188 notice supports awareness that the jury had a forfeiture function. Tr.8 supports the separate proposition that counsel and court discussed the special-verdict mechanism. Neither may be dismissed simply because it preceded the plea. Conversely, neither reported passage states what counsel privately explained, records a personal waiver, describes each §853(p) condition, or explains the consequences of pleading guilty while disputing forfeiture. The notices concern the trial then underway and CCE-proceeds language. Absence of such additional wording from the bounded report is not proof it never occurred elsewhere.

## Adversarial effect on the actual waiver question

The **strongest affirmance path is now stronger**: counsel considered a special verdict; the defendant heard a judge identify the jury's forfeiture task; he later signed a broad property agreement, accepted it in court with counsel and ended the trial. This is affirmative evidence of relinquishment, not merely a failure to demand a jury. Knowledge need not be established by recitation of a rule number or by a second, formulaic warning. The six affirming Justices regard the original affirmative bargain and assent as sufficient for the statutory determination; these reported notices reinforce that application. They do not make a new finding about private advice or infer statutory eligibility from consent.

The **strongest inquiry path survives on a different, narrower proposition than no notice**: awareness that an ongoing jury would decide proceeds forfeiture does not necessarily resolve whether the later agreement and differently framed colloquy reflect an informed surrender of the separately retained property-verdict right. The broad all-assets term, statutory limitation, narrower drug-transaction explanation and actual sentencing objection make the nature of the relinquishment disputed in this record. Stevens and Souter weigh both notices as affirmative evidence favoring waiver but do not regard them as conclusive of that distinct question. Their remand does not find ignorance, demand a special ritual, require comprehension of every asset-tracing doctrine, or hold that the defendant was constitutionally incapable. It directs an actual right-specific inquiry that may find a valid waiver, including from the existing evidence.

The strongest objection to that remand is substantial: combined notice and assent ordinarily allow an inference of consent, and a special-verdict right does not require a separate explanation of every alternative procedure. Stevens and Souter do not adopt a universal hearing requirement for all sentencing stipulations. Their objection is confined to this separately retained verdict protection, the competing descriptions of what was conceded and the actual challenge. Their Godinez/Olano distinctions between capacity, knowledge, relinquishment and nonassertion remain separate from their Mezzanatto concern for the particular procedural rule's work. Mezzanatto's institutional-candor dissent is not itself a holding that this property-verdict right is nonwaivable.
