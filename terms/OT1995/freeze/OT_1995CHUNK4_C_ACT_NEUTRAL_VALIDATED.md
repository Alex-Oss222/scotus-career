## Operative statutory text and dates

Public Telecommunications Act of 1992, Pub. L. 102-356, §16(a), 106 Stat. 954, directs the FCC to promulgate rules prohibiting indecent programming:

> (1) between 6 a.m. and 10 p.m. on any public radio station or public television station that goes off the air at or before 12 midnight; and
> (2) between 6 a.m. and 12 midnight on any radio or television broadcasting station not described in paragraph (1).

The rules must use APA §553 procedure and be final within 180 days after enactment. Section 16(b) repeals the 1989 appropriations provision supporting the earlier all-day prohibition. Section 22 makes §16 effective on enactment, August 26, 1992; the Act's separate delayed effective date concerns §5(a), not §16. The complete nine-page official Act was read. Section 551(c) addresses television apparatus manufactured in the United States or shipped in interstate commerce with screens at least 13 inches diagonally. ## Record, competing positions, and lower reasoning

The challenged expression is indecent but nonobscene material constitutionally protected for adults. The challenged rule regulates content. The lower majority applied strict scrutiny in the context of broadcasting; it did not declare the rule content neutral. It rejected the vagueness challenge under the precedents before it and treated protection of youth and support for parental supervision as compelling. It did not reach an independent household-privacy interest as necessary to judgment.

The audience record reports substantial numbers of minors at late hours, declining further after midnight, and adults remaining in the audience. The majority cited approximately 4.3 million teenagers at 11–11:30 p.m., 3.1 million at 11:30 p.m.–1 a.m., and fewer than one million at 1:45–2 a.m.; it also addressed younger children, adult viewing and listening, channel changes, warnings, and supervision. These are the lower court's descriptions of the administrative record, not this Court's independently gathered social science or findings that every station has the same audience. The safe-harbor question concerns realistic adult access as well as declining child audiences.

The government's strongest supported account combines an independent interest in youth welfare, parental assistance, broadcast accessibility, and evidence that children remain in the audience while parental supervision cannot be assumed. Congress need not wait for conclusive scientific proof of harm before addressing protection of minors. A timed restriction preserves some access and differs from Sable's total ban. The lower majority considered warnings, household supervision, station-specific treatment, and alternative adult sources inadequate reasons to reject the rule altogether. But it found no adequate explanation for the distinction allowing some public stations a 10 p.m. safe harbor. It remanded for uniform 10 p.m.–6 a.m. treatment despite otherwise finding the midnight boundary permissible.

The petitioners' strongest account is that a broad restriction consumes most adults' waking hours and that aggregate child-audience figures do not establish adequate fit, the need for every station to follow the same boundary, or the inadequacy of materially less restrictive means. An invocation of parental support cannot replace support for suppressing speech to adults. Access through paid cable, recordings, or late-night schedules is itself contested as a practical substitute. Neither unproved general harm nor a claim that some child could always evade an alternative automatically supplies least-restrictive-means analysis.

Edwards challenges the continued breadth of broadcast exceptionalism and the premise that government assistance to parents justifies adult censorship. Wald's separate dissent, joined by Rogers and Tatel, is narrower: even retaining Pacifica, the record does not establish the necessity and tailoring of this broad safe harbor, meaningful adult access, or adequate testing of alternatives. These are materially distinct routes. The packet need not require overruling Pacifica as the price of holding this record insufficient. Conversely, Sable does not itself erase Pacifica's broadcast-specific holding.

The record's disputed examples of news, sexual-health discussion, and individual enforcement controversies illustrate the alleged breadth and chilling effect. They do not place individual sanctions before the Court in this event.
