## Material facts and their evidentiary status

The state committee was federally registered and unincorporated. In April 1986, before either party selected its Senate nominee, it spent $15,000 on the radio advertisement “Wirth Facts #1.” Wirth had registered for the Democratic nomination. The advertisement identified Republican Party sponsorship, attacked representations concerning Wirth's defense and balanced-budget record, and referred to his Senate candidacy. The lower courts accepted that it lacked the narrow express advocacy words discussed in Buckley and MCFL. The court of appeals nevertheless classified it as an electioneering message identifying a candidate.

The committee had assigned to the National Republican Senatorial Committee its authority to make all $103,248 of its permitted 1986 §441a(d)(3) expenditures for this Senate contest. Thus the additional $15,000 exceeded the available allowance if this advertisement fell within that subsection. $103,248 is the adjusted 1986 allowance, not the statutory base amount or a constitutional minimum. The committee reported the expenditure as voter-information advertising, not a §441a(d) disbursement. The FEC sued after administrative proceedings and unsuccessful settlement efforts.

The supplied case presents an independent-expenditure premise: the record does not establish candidate consultation, request, suggestion or coordinated control for this advertisement. That premise must be tested against the supported record rather than displaced by a party-status label. The lower courts' use of “coordinated” rested on accepting the FEC's categorical legal treatment of party spending; it is not an independent factual finding that a candidate actually participated in this transaction. The government's contrary legal inference remains an argument to assess.

## Operative statutory provisions

The official 1994 Code and the Joint Appendix pp.216–223 corroborate the terms. The October 1986 amendment affected Internal Revenue Code references elsewhere, not the quoted §441a(d) language. The relevant challenged terms are:

**2 U.S.C. §441a(d)(1):** “Notwithstanding any other provision of law with respect to limitations on expenditures or limitations on contributions, the national committee of a political party and a State committee of a political party, including any subordinate committee of a State committee, may make expenditures in connection with the general election campaign of candidates for Federal office, subject to the limitations contained in paragraphs (2) and (3) of this subsection.”

**Section 441a(d)(3):** “The national committee of a political party, or a State committee of a political party, including any subordinate committee of a State committee, may not make any expenditure in connection with the general election campaign of a candidate for Federal office in a State who is affiliated with such party which exceeds—

“(A) in the case of a candidate for election to the office of Senator, or of Representative from a State which is entitled to only one Representative, the greater of—

“(i) 2 cents multiplied by the voting age population of the State (as certified under subsection (e) of this section); or

“(ii) $20,000; and

“(B) in the case of a candidate for election to the office of Representative, Delegate, or Resident Commissioner in any other State, $10,000.”

Section 441a(c) separately adjusts the subsection (b) and (d) limits by the certified percentage difference between the preceding calendar year's CPI and the 1974 base period, for the calendar year of application. Section 441a(e) supplies annual Commerce certification and defines voting-age population as resident population eighteen or older. The presidential national-party allowance in subsection (d)(2) is distinct: two cents multiplied by national voting-age population, additional to expenditures by a national committee acting as a presidential candidate's principal campaign committee. It is background, not the spending allowance applied to this Senate dispute.

**Section 441a(a)(7)(B)(i):** “expenditures made by any person in cooperation, consultation, or concert, with, or at the request or suggestion of, a candidate, his authorized political committees, or their agents, shall be considered to be a contribution to such candidate”. Subparagraph (ii) separately concerns financing dissemination, distribution or republication of candidate-prepared campaign materials. Subsection (a)(8) separately attributes earmarked or intermediary-directed contributions to their original source and requires identification of that source and intended recipient. Their distinct triggers must not be fused. Their existence supplies no finding that this transaction satisfied them.

The government additionally invokes §441a(a)(2)'s contribution restrictions and characterizes subsection (d) as supplementary spending permission. The source supplement identifies that submission, but does not reproduce the complete subsection (a)(2). Before making that separate restriction the dispositive basis, inspect its operative terms in the official Code and its preservation/application in the briefs; do not infer a complete contribution judgment from this summary.

## Lower-court grounds and strongest supported positions

The District Court used Buckley and MCFL to construe “expenditures in connection with” narrowly and avoid the constitutional dispute. The Tenth Circuit rejected importing the express-advocacy limitation into party spending it regarded as coordinated, deferred to FEC advisory opinions 1984-15 and 1985-14, and used a clearly identified candidate plus electioneering-message standard. It relied on corruption and its appearance, the structural party–candidate relationship, and congressional electoral experience. The appellate opinion expressly recognized that Buckley's treatment of the party-expenditure provision addressed a Fifth Amendment challenge, not the First Amendment issue presented here. That distinction is material.

The Party's strongest supported argument is that paying for its own political message restricts its expression and the association of its members directly. A functional distinction between genuinely independent speech and candidate resources is not answered by calling all party activity coordinated. Electioneering about a registered opponent before a nominee is selected can be party expression without actual consultation. Equalizing voices or reducing campaign costs cannot alone justify restricting independent political advocacy. The Party also presses constitutional avoidance, express advocacy and limits on administrative deference. These are recorded submissions; none is adopted here.

The FEC's strongest supported response is that the party's continuing organizational mission, spending and candidate support may make party expenditures equivalent to contributions, even where a particular communication lacks express consultation. Subsection (d) can be viewed as permission above ordinary contribution restrictions. Categorical administration may protect the candidate-contribution system from circumvention through party intermediaries and corruption or its appearance. A message unmistakably directed at a Senate race may fall within the text even without “vote for” wording. The Court must evaluate that functional and statutory argument; it cannot reject it by overlooking the structural evidence or accept it by declaring actual coordination established.

Required concessions are equally bounded. Independent-spending protection does not automatically immunize candidate-directed payments, earmarked donations, republication or a demonstrated conduit. Legitimate corruption and anti-circumvention interests do not establish that every party message is actually coordinated, that equalization is sufficient, or that every dollar limit is fitted. An avoidance construction must remain a plausible reading of this statute; avoidance is not authority to rewrite the provision. A refusal to reach broader coordinated spending preserves the question, not an affirmative holding sustaining it.

## Law entering the event

The First Amendment protects political speech and association. Its resolution of §441a(d)'s Fifth Amendment issue is not an adjudication of the present First Amendment question. **Current rule:** First determine whether Congress prescribed the statute’s temporal reach. If it did, that direction governs subject to independent constitutional limits. ## Umbehr, No.94-1654

### Jurisdiction, judgment and actual contract

The petition invokes 28 U.S.C. §1254(1) after a §1983 action within 28 U.S.C. §§1331 and 1343 and an appeal under §1291. The Tenth Circuit judgment was entered January 4, 1995, with rehearing denied February 10. Petition pp.i–ii and 1–2 identifies the petitioners as Heiser and Spencer in their official capacities as county commissioners. The pending merits review does not reopen the individual-capacity immunity judgment or McClure's separate causation judgment.

The full appellate opinion, 44 F.3d 876, establishes these particulars:

- The operative 1985 contract, succeeding a 1981 arrangement, allowed Umbehr to haul residential refuse for cities that endorsed and ratified it. The county did not itself purchase trash-hauling services under that contract. Cities were not obliged to participate and could opt out on ninety days' notice. The agreement automatically renewed for one-year terms unless a party gave sixty days' notice of termination or ninety days' notice of an intention to renegotiate. The county and participating cities agreed not to contract with another residential-refuse provider while the contract operated. These are distinct notice provisions, not immediate termination at whim.
- Umbehr served six of seven cities from 1985 to 1991. He criticized landfill rates, charges for public records, possible Open Meetings Act violations, and alleged road-and-bridge mismanagement in commission meetings and newspapers. Those criticisms are described as allegations and speech; the courts did not adjudicate their truth in resolving this motion.
- The attempted 1990 termination was ineffective, and the contract renewed. The valid January 1991 termination followed another two-to-one vote. Heiser and Spencer supported termination; McClure opposed the earlier action and was no longer in office at the effective termination. Umbehr later made direct contracts with five of the six cities; the county made no replacement hauling contract. The successor arrangements do not by themselves establish zero injury.

The district court **assumed solely for summary judgment** that the speech would have been protected had Umbehr been an employee, that the comments motivated the termination votes, and that he suffered injury. It did not find those propositions after trial. It granted judgment on a categorical contractor-status ground and, alternatively, individual qualified immunity. It also found insufficient evidence of McClure's causation. The Tenth Circuit rejected the categorical constitutional bar, applied Pickering to contractor retaliation, affirmed individual immunity and McClure's disposition, and remanded the surviving official-capacity claims. The appellate court separately held standing supported by alleged monetary harm and loss of valuable contract benefits, including exclusivity, centralized negotiation and notice protection; a property entitlement is not a prerequisite to the speech claim. See 44 F.3d 877–879, 883–884 and nn.1–3, 6.

The lower court did not find damages, impose liability, or direct a particular award or reinstatement. Its opinion notes that damages may be small and difficult to prove. Personal legislative immunity and official-capacity liability are distinct: the appellate opinion invokes Kentucky v. Graham's entity-based treatment of the latter and does not allow individual immunities to become entity immunities by changing the caption.

### Actual competing submissions and record limits

Petition pp.i–ii asks whether Pickering protection extends to terminable government contracts, how the test should be adapted, and whether government affirmative defenses survive. Petitioners' merits brief pp.12–20 distinguishes retrospective criticism from patronage affiliation and broad prospective speech bans. It emphasizes the government's retained responsibility for services, the contractor's day-to-day independence, and termination as an important means of control. It invokes Mt. Healthy to oppose an automatic tenure rule and argues that the employee/contractor distinction can matter to managerial needs. Its assertion that protection makes services ungovernable is an argument, not a record finding.

Respondent's merits brief pp.13–19 argues that an independent contractor remains a citizen protected against economic retaliation, seeks demanding review of viewpoint-based retaliation, and argues that ordinary workplace disruption interests may be absent outside an employment setting. He asks for affirmance and a remand limited to motive and legitimate independent reasons, contending the speech is protected under any applicable standard. That is a litigant's requested limitation on remand, not a finding already supplied by the lower judgment. The difference between his stricter proposal and the appellate Pickering rule is material.

The commissioners' petition also describes disputes over landfill charges and related state litigation. Its description of arrearages or misconduct investigations is advocacy and does not establish an actual reason for the challenged votes. Motive, public-concern characterization, operational effects, same-decision proof and injury must retain their separate source status. Possession of a possible lawful ground is not proof that the decision would have occurred without protected speech.

The Tenth Circuit expressly acknowledged conflict with the Seventh Circuit's LaFalce/Triad/Downtown Auto Parks approach and decisions from other circuits. Its analysis also distinguishes public-issue retaliation from political affiliation. # Exact Waters standard

### Public-employee speech and the employer’s mistaken factual account

**Governing question:** Which employer beliefs about employee speech protect a disciplinary decision, and which do not.

**Current rule:** An employer acting on a genuine, reasonable account of speech that would be disciplinable under the governing public-employee-speech rules is protected even if that factual account is mistaken. Conversely, an unreasonable and incorrect account does not defeat a claim when the employee actually spoke protected speech and that speech caused the adverse action. The employee must show protected speech was a substantial or motivating factor; the employer may establish by a preponderance that it would have taken the same action absent the protected speech. These limited propositions do not impose liability for every investigation defect or adopt the plurality’s complete investigative procedure.

**Authority by component:**

- Seven-Justice genuine-and-reasonable-account safe harbor: [Waters v. Churchill — A genuine, reasonable account of disciplinable speech supplies a limited safe harbor](https://github.com/Alex-Oss222/scotus-career/blob/main/terms/OT1993/records/Waters_v_Churchill_merits_1994-05-31.md#public-projection), 1994-05-31. O'Connor's plurality opinion, joined by Stone-Zsela, Souter and Ginsburg, adopts this sufficient condition; Scalia's concurrence in the judgment, joined by Kennedy and Thomas, expressly accepts it under their broader genuine-ground rule. Seven Justices agree at this precise level; only four adopt the full objective-inquiry framework.
- Six-Justice unreasonable-and-false-account limit: [Waters v. Churchill — An unreasonable false account cannot defeat actually protected speech](https://github.com/Alex-Oss222/scotus-career/blob/main/terms/OT1993/records/Waters_v_Churchill_merits_1994-05-31.md#public-projection), 1994-05-31. O'Connor, Stone-Zsela, Souter and Ginsburg accept this proposition under objective factual care; Stevens's dissent, joined by Blackmun, expressly accepts it under their broader actual-speech rule. Six Justices agree on this proposition, but the two dissenters do not join the reasonable-error safe harbor or the judgment vacating the appellate rule.
- Motive and same-decision proof: [Waters v. Churchill — The motive dispute requires remand without restoring defendants' summary judgment](https://github.com/Alex-Oss222/scotus-career/blob/main/terms/OT1993/records/Waters_v_Churchill_merits_1994-05-31.md#public-projection), 1994-05-31. O'Connor's four-Justice plurality and Scalia's three-Justice concurrence in the judgment support this common vacatur and limited remand. Blackmun and Stevens would affirm the appellate reversal under their different actual-speech rule; their votes are not counted as support for vacatur.

**Present operation effective:** Waters v. Churchill, 1994-05-31.

**Open question:** Unreasonable belief where the actual speech was unprotected, and a universal investigation checklist, are not resolved. The four-Justice complete reasonableness framework is noncontrolling.

### Prospective restrictions on employees’ citizen expression

**Governing question:** Justification of a broad advance restriction on employees’ compensated speech as citizens.

**Current rule:** Under Pickering, the Government must supply a justification commensurate with the actual reach and burden of a broad prospective restriction on employees’ future compensated citizen expression, including the burden on listeners. A general concern about misuse of office does not by itself justify prohibiting protected expression unrelated to employment; the required connection must be adequately established. Narrower valid ethics restrictions remain available.

**Authority by component:**

- A broad prospective honoraria ban requires justification for its actual speech burden: [A broad prospective honoraria ban requires justification for its actual speech burden](https://github.com/Alex-Oss222/scotus-career/blob/main/terms/OT1994/records/United_States_v_National_Treasury_Employees_Union_merits_1995-02-22.md#public-projection), 1995-02-22. Justice Stevens's Part I, joined by Chief Justice Stone-Zsela and Justices Kennedy, Souter, Ginsburg and Breyer, supplies six votes for this rule and explanation. Justice O'Connor agrees with invalidity for unrelated expression on her separate, case-specific Pickering ground; her vote is not counted for this prospective-burden formulation.

**Present operation effective:** 1995-02-22; the component authorities identified above.

**Exceptions and limits:** This prospective-burden rule does not constitutionalize every payment or decide materially different senior-employee settings. The existing honoraria ban’s classwide remedy has its own five-Justice statutory-remedial ground; it creates no universal injunction for unrepresented employees or judicially rewritten employment-nexus exception.

### Public-employee speech and the employer’s mistaken factual account

**Governing question:** Which employer beliefs about employee speech protect a disciplinary decision, and which do not.

**Current rule:** An employer acting on a genuine, reasonable account of speech that would be disciplinable under the governing public-employee-speech rules is protected even if that factual account is mistaken. Conversely, an unreasonable and incorrect account does not defeat a claim when the employee actually spoke protected speech and that speech caused the adverse action. The employee must show protected speech was a substantial or motivating factor; the employer may establish by a preponderance that it would have taken the same action absent the protected speech. These limited propositions do not impose liability for every investigation defect or adopt the plurality’s complete investigative procedure.

## Challenged enactment in terms

**Section10(a), leased-access permission**, amends Communications Act §612(h) by inserting “or the cable operator” after “franchising authority” and adding:

Thus the new permission has a prospective, written-and-published-policy condition, a reasonable-belief condition and the stated substantive definition. **Section10(b), leased-access segregation and blocking**, adds §612(j):

> (j)(1) Within 120 days following the date of the enactment of this subsection, the Commission shall promulgate regulations designed to limit the access of children to indecent programming, as defined by Commission regulations, and which cable operators have not voluntarily prohibited under subsection (h) by—
>
> (A) requiring cable operators to place on a single channel all indecent programs, as identified by program providers, intended for carriage on channels designated for commercial use under this section;
>
> (B) requiring cable operators to block such single channel unless the subscriber requests access to such channel in writing; and
>
> (C) requiring programmers to inform cable operators if the program would be indecent as defined by Commission regulations.
>
> (2) Cable operators shall comply with the regulations promulgated pursuant to paragraph (1).

The voluntary prohibition clause limits the programming to which the mandatory segregation/blocking scheme applies. It does not make segregation and blocking optional when the operator carries covered programming. Programmer identification and notification are independent requirements.

**Section10(c), PEG permission**, provides:

> Within 180 days following the date of the enactment of this Act, the Federal Communications Commission shall promulgate such regulations as may be necessary to enable a cable operator of a cable system to prohibit the use, on such system, of any channel capacity of any public, educational, or governmental access facility for any programming which contains obscene material, sexually explicit conduct, or material soliciting or promoting unlawful conduct.

These are the enacted categories. Congress used “sexually explicit conduct” here, whereas (a) spells out sexual/excretory content and offensiveness. The FCC's construction linking those categories must be identified as agency interpretation. Section10(c) contains no mandatory segregation/blocking rule and no statutory written-request mechanism. Its governmental direction to issue regulations is distinct from the permission given to operators.

The lower court notes the FCC's interpretation limiting loss of immunity to constitutionally unprotected material. ## FCC implementation and material qualifications

The first order, 8 FCC Rcd998 (1993), 58 Fed.Reg.7990, implemented (a)/(b), formerly 47 C.F.R. §76.701. The definition adds the medium-specific phrase: material describing or depicting sexual or excretory activities or organs in a patently offensive manner measured by contemporary community standards **for the cable medium**. This is not Miller obscenity: it does not incorporate Miller's whole-work prurient-interest and lack-of-serious-value elements. A work's literary, artistic, political or scientific value does not categorically exempt every depiction under the indecency definition. The rules require provider identification, segregation and blocking of covered material not prohibited, subscriber notice, and written requests stating the requester is at least eighteen. The operator must satisfy a request **within thirty days**; this is an outside compliance period, not a mandatory thirty-day waiting period. The FCC permitted another blocked channel if the first lacked capacity, and an alternative involving lockboxes with operator-retained keys for qualifying small systems. Those implementation exceptions should not be converted into the statute's general rule. ## Lower judgment and competing paths, provision by provision

**(a), operator ban discretion.** The en banc majority treats Congress as restoring private editorial choice: permission, regulation, a franchise monopoly and a legislative preference do not themselves make each operator decision governmental under Blum, Jackson, Flagg Brothers and Lugar. It distinguishes Reitman's constitutional entrenchment of racial discrimination from ordinary statutory revision. It rejects an established coercive-cost premise because allocation of blocking costs remained for another FCC proceeding and the industry comments did not demonstrate inevitable censoring incentives. This is a record conclusion, not a holding that coercive incentives can never supply state action.

Wald treats (a) and (b) as an integrated governmental ban-or-block choice; both possible branches impose a speech burden, and retaining a nominal private choice does not end scrutiny. Alternatively she reads technical, personnel, administrative and financial burdens as encouragement of bans. Edwards shares the integrated-scheme objection, but would not find (a) standing alone necessarily governmental. Rogers would sever the invalid blocking scheme while preserving the operator permission. These are distinct alternatives: state action in the statute, attribution of the operator's choice, practical inducement, and independent private editorial rights. Avoid assuming one resolves all others.

**(b), compulsory segregation/blocking.** The majority applies Sable's least-restrictive-means formulation, relies on protection of children and the attributes of home television, and regards the mixed and unpredictable character of leased programming as impairing subscriber-controlled lockboxes. It reasons that a written opt-in leaves adult access available, that some children watch late, and that different treatment of premium, ordinary and PEG channels is justified by different editorial and access arrangements. Its privacy discussion cites §551 protections and distinguishes Lamont's request-to-government deterrence; it does not find an actual database disclosure here.

Wald, joined on these parts by Rogers, stresses the combined effect on adult speakers and viewers: initial denial, written request, up-to-thirty-day activation, concentrated adult content, inability to choose one program without opening the whole channel, and potential suppression of mixed serious programming. She challenges the agency's evidence for rejecting lockboxes, separation by time/channel plus subscriber control, notice and cost-spreading. She distinguishes telephone systems' technological limitations and alternative credit-card/access-code options. Those criticisms are not adjudicated universal facts about every system. Edwards agrees the combined scheme fails, while discussing a potentially different parent-facilitation rationale and structure; do not convert that conditional possibility into a holding sustaining the enacted system.

**(c), PEG operator power.** The majority's private-discretion rationale survives independently because no (b) compulsion attaches to PEG. Wald sees content-selective governmental withdrawal of access protection and a statutory source for discriminatory choices. She does not decide public-forum status; Rogers's rejection of the forum claim is expressly tied to what this facial record establishes. Edwards and Rogers do not join Wald's invalidation of (c). Distinguish any particular city's management of a PEG facility from a universal status for all PEG systems; distinguish operators' interests on their own programming from power over separately administered access programming.

**Vagueness, restraint and severability.** The lower majority sustains the generic standard based on existing indecency decisions and agency resolution; rejects government prior-licensing treatment of notification/certification and treats operator bans under its private-action conclusion. The opposing claim is that reasonable-belief bans, uncertain community standards and certification consequences deter protected speech beyond settled obscenity. These issues remain analytically distinct from whether the particular restriction is necessary. ## 3. Kilgore: location, access, and the arguments actually presented

**Respondent's brief in opposition**, read in full, makes the private location part of its question and argument: the automobile was on private land, its owner was in custody, officers controlled the situation and could obtain a warrant, no urgency required an immediate search, and the owner had not consented. Its factual account places the vehicle in a private driveway, describes other nearby structures, disputes how directly observed movements connected suspects to the truck, and says officers lost sight of them for a period. It also disputes any plain-view basis for the cocaine inside the cup. Its argument treats these circumstances as reasons the warrantless search did not fall within the automobile exception, invoking the absence of consent, a plain-view discovery, a sudden encounter or stop, or a search incident to arrest.

That submission affirmatively presents the private setting and officer control. It must not be reduced to a concession that the truck was on a public road, or to silence about location. At the same time, the opposition's location-based automobile-exception argument is not itself a finding establishing the property's constitutional boundary or an independent unlawful-entry claim. This note does not decide preservation or waiver of such a claim.

**Commonwealth's reply**, read in full, treats probable cause as established below and describes the disputed issue as whether police needed a warrant to search the vehicle. Those are the Commonwealth's characterizations. It answers through mobility and diminished expectations of privacy, maintains that no added exigency or sudden highway encounter is required, and discusses Carney, Texas v. White, Johns, and Ross. Its discussion of consent, plain view, search incident, and other exceptions treats them as independent grounds that do not define the automobile exception. These propositions report the argument made; they do not resolve the disputed doctrine or independently establish lawful access to this location.
