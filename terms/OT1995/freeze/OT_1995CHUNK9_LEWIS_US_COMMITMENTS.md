## Supported record and controlling entering law

Lewis faced two mail-obstruction counts, each permitting six months' imprisonment, a $100 fine and $10 assessment as described in the lower opinion. Before trial the magistrate promised no more than six months' imprisonment under any circumstances. The request for a jury was denied; convictions were entered February25 1994 and concurrent three-year probation terms imposed. The promise's legal effect is contested; it is not a legislatively reduced maximum, an established waiver or a finding that imprisonment above six months was legally impossible. The sentence actually imposed does not retrospectively cure a jury right existing at trial.

Actual Nachtigal preserves the complete Blanton rule: six months or less creates a rebuttable petty-offense presumption, and additional authorized penalties, considered with incarceration, can rebut it if sufficiently severe to reflect a legislative judgment of seriousness. Its particular DUI package did not rebut the presumption. It establishes neither an irrebuttable six-month rule nor a universal exclusion of fines and probation. Gaudin assigns an actual criminal element to the jury where a constitutional jury right governs; it does not determine which petty prosecutions require a jury. Bagwell classifies fixed noncompensatory contempt fines and requires serious criminal-contempt safeguards; it neither establishes a universal numerical seriousness threshold nor determines aggregation of statutory counts.

## Enhanced issue comparison

### A. One proceeding with aggregate authorized imprisonment above six months

**Path A — offense-specific seriousness.** Affirm because each legislatively defined offense is petty under the full Blanton rule and joining two such offenses does not itself change either offense's legislative character. Congress's authorization of separate punishments does not without more classify the offenses as serious. The possibility of more than six months from consecutive sentences is the aggregation issue, not an additional penalty attached to either offense. The supported fine, assessment and probation package supplies no independent basis overcoming the petty presumption here. This conclusion does not foreclose a different additional-penalty package.

**Strongest contrary case.** A defendant subjected to one prosecution carrying a year's imprisonment faces the same practical loss as a defendant charged with one serious offense. Codispoti rejects evasion by dividing a serious aggregate punishment into six-month contempts. The jury's protection against arbitrary punishment might seem no less necessary here.

**Resolution.** Codispoti's actual-sentence measure answers contempt without legislative maximums; it does not erase the statutory seriousness measure reaffirmed in Blanton and actual Nachtigal. Taylor likewise addresses contempt without legislative authorization of serious punishment. Both show why their sentences matter in that setting. The present holding concerns statutory petty offenses; it does not weaken serious-contempt safeguards, authorize circumvention of an independently serious offense or decide every possible multiple-prosecution arrangement. Congress's concurrent-sentence default is supportive context, not a finding that consecutive sentences are unavailable and not an independent categorical constitutional test.

**Path B — aggregate seriousness.** Reverse if combined authorized imprisonment in one prosecution triggers the jury right and the announcement cannot lawfully remove it. This path treats the single proceeding's aggregate loss as the relevant seriousness measure, extending Codispoti's protection beyond its contempt facts. Its strongest obstacle is that Blanton and Nachtigal identify the legislature's offense-specific punishment scheme, not the prosecutor's joinder choice, as the ordinary measure. No earlier actual holding has displaced that distinction. The individual conclusions below adopt Path A; they do not treat Path B as frivolous or already prohibited by a square holding.

### B. The pretrial cap

Under Path A the cap does no necessary legal work; its efficacy is reserved. Under Path B one must separately choose between an effective advance surrender of power to exceed six months and an unenforceable judicial attempt to alter the statutory jury trigger. The exact promise supports the former argument; the absence of an identified statutory reduction or waiver, and Baldwin/Blanton's authorized-penalty rule, support the latter. Taylor's acceptance of a reduced contempt sentence cannot by itself settle the statutory-offense question. The selected commitments do not resolve this branch merely to supply a second ground for affirmance.

## Compatible propositions, remedy and continuity

All eight provisional commitments support only this common route: the two offenses remain petty under the full rebuttable test; joinder alone supplies no constitutional jury entitlement; affirm the Second Circuit judgment sustaining the convictions.
