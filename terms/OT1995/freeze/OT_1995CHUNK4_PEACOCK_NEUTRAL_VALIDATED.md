## Review channel, questions, and judgment

No. 94-1453; argument November 6, 1995; the authorized event is argued merits review of the Fourth Circuit, 39 F.3d 493 (1994), through 28 U.S.C. §1254(1). The petition presents **two** questions: ancillary jurisdiction over a separate action imposing an earlier judgment on an individual not liable under that judgment, and the availability under ERISA of the asserted nonfiduciary/veil-piercing collection remedy. The petitioner reverses their presentation order in the merits brief. Both parties' merits presentations address both. The neutral packet's jurisdiction emphasis must not erase the ERISA cause-of-action/remedy question. This statement describes the filings, not an independently recovered historical grant order.

The first action, filed in 1987, produced the November 28, 1988 judgment for **$187,628.93 against Tru-Tech**, affirmed in 1990. Peacock was a defendant but was held not to be a plan fiduciary and was not made a judgment debtor. Accordingly, “new party” is imprecise: he is a proposed **new judgment debtor**, not a person absent from the original litigation. The present action was filed December 23, 1991 and amended February 7, 1992. It asserted conspiracy, fraudulent transfers, and veil piercing under ERISA and applicable federal law. Diversity was unavailable.

Following a bench trial, the district court imposed the whole earlier liability on Peacock under veil-piercing principles, with interest and fees. The Fourth Circuit affirmed jurisdiction and veil-piercing liability, rejected the preclusion objection, and affirmed fee entitlement but vacated the **$205,182.01 fee amount** for reconsideration of its scope across the two actions. It did not reopen the original pension judgment. Finegold was not held liable; conspiracy was not proved. The affirmed judgment is not simply an order returning identified transferred assets or a judgment capped at their value. Disposition must specify the consequences for this later judgment and its fees while preserving the original Tru-Tech judgment.

## Record distinctions that affect the forks

The original pension wrongdoing occurred in 1981–1985. Tru-Tech began as a legitimate business venture; later control, failure to observe corporate distinctions, and alleged depletion are the relevant veil-piercing circumstances. Peacock became the controlling shareholder and, in March 1988, sole director. The present findings concern conduct substantially later than the underlying benefit violations, including alleged transfers during and after the original litigation. The lower courts treated that conduct as designed to defeat collection. Peacock disputes the inferences, consideration analysis, valuation, and legal consequences; the appellate court upheld the factual findings under clear-error review. A new model must distinguish those upheld findings from the broader parties' allegations and from a fresh factual finding of its own.

The transfer history is not uniformly postjudgment. The Uhlman-note assignment was purportedly dated October 31, 1988; the district court found its asserted consideration deficient at that date or the assignment backdated. Other matters included a Brodsky note, a Marion receivable, a later $5,000 note, and transfers relating to Peacock, Williams & Co.'s asserted management debt. Peacock says the accounting writeoff did not extinguish a real obligation and that face values overstate what could have been collected. These disputes matter if relief turns on identifiable property, consideration, or the amount diverted. They do not establish that every dollar of the **$187,628.93** underlying judgment was transferred to Peacock.

The first court's nonfiduciary determination remains binding within its scope. The later veil-piercing claim does not automatically relitigate the same historical conduct, and the Fourth Circuit held the later facts defeated the res judicata objection. Conversely, later alter-ego conduct does not automatically establish that Peacock personally was a fiduciary when the original breaches occurred. ERISA §409(b)'s temporal limitation is part of petitioner's answer to the derivative-fiduciary theory. Preserve the distinction between personal fiduciary liability, identifying an obligor's alter ego, and a new liability for obstructing collection.

## Material competing legal positions

**Petitioner.** Beecher, the limits of Article III/statutory jurisdiction, and Kokkonen prevent a later, factually separate action from imposing the full judgment on a nondebtor without its own jurisdictional basis. No property was in federal custody, and no order directed Peacock personally to pay or prohibited the identified transfers. A federal court's interest in collection cannot alone authorize general jurisdiction over every collection dispute. ERISA's detailed remedial structure, Mertens, Mackey, O'Melveny, and Travelers do not authorize an additional federal cause of action and national veil-piercing law merely to improve collection. The requested full money liability remains compensatory despite the equitable label. This position does not deny that state-law collection, fraudulent-transfer, or properly founded equitable remedies may exist.

**Respondent.** The action implements an existing judgment and identifies the debtor's alter ego after deliberate disregard of the corporate form and obstruction of collection. Riggs, Labette County, traditional creditor's bills, Rule 69, and Kokkonen's decree-enforcement branch support ancillary power independent of a common nucleus of original merits facts. Alternatively, ERISA §§409 and 502, including §502(a)(3), permit the equitable identification of the true obligor; the relief is not a new damages claim of the Mertens kind. Federal common-law veil piercing serves uniform enforcement and beneficiary protection. The relation between this theory and the prior finding of no individual fiduciary status must be answered, not assumed away.

**United States amicus.** Its substantial independent route is ancillary enforcement jurisdiction, without requiring recognition of a new substantive ERISA collection cause of action. The government invokes the historical capacity to enforce judgments against property and closely related actors, the distinction between the two ancillary-jurisdiction purposes, and Peacock's alleged control and deliberate frustration. It distinguishes Beecher's attempted addition of independently liable persons rather than treating all third-party enforcement as forbidden. It acknowledges limits of attenuation and the relevance of fairness, comity, and judicial economy. Crucially, the government says **state law ordinarily supplies the enforcement rule under Rule 69**, absent a demonstrated conflict justifying displacement; affirming jurisdiction therefore need not approve the Fourth Circuit's categorical ERISA preemption/common-law reasoning. The lower court believed South Carolina law equivalent, but petitioner disputes that premise and the permissible amount. This creates a genuine choice-of-law/remand fork.

**Other amici, summaries actually reviewed.** The National Association of Real Estate Investment Managers supports petitioner, emphasizing the statutory functional distinction between fiduciaries and nonfiduciaries and the costs of extending fiduciary liability to shareholders. Central States funds, the Bricklayers and Trowel Trades International Pension Fund, and the National Coordinating Committee for Multiemployer Plans press federal alter-ego identity, contribution enforcement, statutory protections including nationwide service, and federal uniformity; NCCMP separately identifies §1331 federal common law, ancillary power, and §502(a)(3) as alternative jurisdictional routes. AARP and the National Employment Lawyers Association emphasize traditional equitable veil piercing and the difference between collection and a new Mertens remedy. The AFL-CIO favors ancillary enforcement and says the purported equivalence of South Carolina and federal standards can avoid deciding choice of law. These are advocacy positions, not holdings. Employer-contribution cases and a later action on a fiduciary-breach judgment are not necessarily interchangeable; the reply expressly urges preserving that distinction.

## Threshold issues and omitted alternatives

1. **Personal jurisdiction is separate.** Peacock preserved the objection in the answer and motion, petition appendix, and merits/reply presentations. The district court relied on ERISA's nationwide service provision, §502(e)(2), to reach the Pennsylvania resident. If the action has no ERISA basis but does have ancillary subject-matter jurisdiction, the personal-jurisdiction ground still requires attention. The government says the issue falls outside the questions for review and suggests alter-ego/privity contacts as an alternative; petitioner disputes both avoidance and the sufficiency of the existing ruling. Do not assert either that this separate issue was waived or that ancillary subject-matter jurisdiction itself resolves service and personal jurisdiction. Remand for a preserved, inadequately resolved basis is a possible consequence.
2. **Section 1367 is not a self-supplying original claim.** The original action predates the December 1, 1990 effective boundary. The later action's filing date does not alone establish the original jurisdiction to which supplemental claims could attach. The parties dispute the new statute's relevance; identify the relevant civil action and temporal rule before assigning it controlling force.
3. **The asserted circuit conflict is real but its scope is contested.** Sandlin, Berry, Manway and related decisions impose important limits; Argento, Skevofilax and Blackburn Truck recognize forms of ancillary enforcement against third parties. Indemnity, successor liability, transferred assets, and alter-ego liability differ. Respondent's opposition says there is no conflict on the narrowly framed ERISA-veil question. The original packet's categorical “no material conflict” formulation should not be carried forward as an agreed fact.
4. **Recovery of property and imposition of a new debt differ.** A ruling limiting full personal liability need not extinguish traditional garnishment, attachment, fraudulent-transfer recovery, or jurisdiction to protect property already within judicial control. Conversely, a doctrine supporting one of those remedies does not by itself support this full judgment. The district court did not award a separable asset-recovery judgment that the model can simply affirm without a source-supported procedural basis.
5. **Disposition must follow the ground chosen.** No original federal claim plus no ancillary jurisdiction points toward vacatur/reversal and jurisdictionally appropriate dismissal of the later action, without an adjudication extinguishing available state rights. Ancillary power plus erroneous federal choice of law can support a different remand. Recognition of an ERISA theory still requires its violation, remedy, temporal, preclusion, and personal-jurisdiction predicates. An affirmance limited to supported enforcement grounds must identify what is being affirmed and what remains unresolved.

## Entering law and Justice-specific anchors

These are eligible sources for later independent assessment, **not commitments**. Use the actual simulated public rules, not post-divergence historical descriptions in party briefs.

- **Kokkonen (May 16, 1994):** Scalia authored; Stevens, O'Connor, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas and Ginsburg joined the controlling distinction between factually interdependent claims and authority to effectuate decrees. Its settlement-enforcement holding is not itself a decision about postjudgment alter-ego liability. Breyer was not on that Court.
- **O'Melveny (June 13, 1994):** the same sitting participants joined Scalia in rejecting displacement of acquired state claims merely from federal financial interest or general uniformity; the particular significant-conflict requirement and surviving exceptional federal fields both matter. This does not automatically decide a federal judgment-enforcement claim.
- **Mertens (June 1, 1993):** Scalia, Kennedy, Souter and Thomas joined the controlling traditional-category limit on §502(a)(3) compensation. The actual simulated holding reserved the antecedent cause-of-action question and did not bar every equitable monetary remedy. The entering-law excerpt does not supply the full separate rationales of Stevens or O'Connor; do not infer their complete approach from the majority lineup. Ginsburg and Breyer did not participate.
- **Travelers (April 26, 1995):** Souter's unanimous simulated public decision includes every current associate Justice. Its actual state-law/ERISA distinction rejects automatic preemption from ordinary economic effects, while retaining genuine plan-administration and enforcement limits. It is a bounded source for both state-law and statutory-structure arguments.
- The briefs identify **Starrett Paving, 845 F.2d 23, 26–27 (1st Cir. 1988)** (Breyer) and **Connors v. P & M Coal, 801 F.2d 1373 (D.C. Cir. 1986)** (panel including Scalia and Ginsburg). Their distinctions between automatic employer/officer liability and alter-ego liability are useful research leads, but this validation has the parties' discussion rather than a independently retrieved full opinion. Do not elevate a party's characterization or a limited dictum into a settled personal position on the present postjudgment question.

The simulated Central Bank rule differs from the historical post-divergence rule quoted in some filings; no argument may silently import a historical no-aiding-and-abetting holding. No later ERISA or ancillary-jurisdiction decisions are admitted by a predecision filing's broad language.
