## Source-supported record, complete statutory qualifications and limits

Sources: [59 F.3d 1556](https://openjurist.org/59/f3d/1556/rise), including the complete majority, dissent and footnotes; official [1995 ORS chapter 137](https://archives.oregonlegislature.gov/ORS_Archives/1995-Chapter-137.htm), §137.076; and official [1995 ORS chapter 181](https://archives.oregonlegislature.gov/ORS_Archives/1995-Chapter-181.htm), §181.085. The section-history notes identify 1991 enactment and the intervening 1993 amendments. The supplied inventory authorizes assigned merits review of the identified Ninth Circuit matter; the source extraction invents neither a Supreme Court docket nor an argument date or separate grant event.

The challenged scheme is not restricted to violent sex crimes. Section 137.076(1) includes the specified rape, sodomy, unlawful penetration, sexual-abuse, public-indecency, incest and child-display crimes; burglary with intent to commit a listed sexual offense; promoting or compelling prostitution; conspiracy or attempt to commit the listed felonies in (a)-(c); and murder or aggravated murder. Attempted murder is outside that list. Rise, Durham and Rhodes had predicate convictions before enactment. Milligan's attempted murder and unlawful-weapon-use convictions were not predicates.

Section 137.076(2) requires the person to provide blood on request of the designated agency whether or not the judgment separately orders it; the sentencing court must enter the stated order, with cost reimbursement unless the person lacks ability to pay and a blood-sample probation condition where applicable. The agency must collect as soon as practicable after conviction and before release from incarceration. State Police notice that a sample is inadequate requires another sample. Thus the lower opinion's description of one lifetime draw is qualified by the actual adequate-sample and repeat-collection terms; it is not an absolute one-draw ceiling.

Section 137.076(3) requires medically acceptable collection by the listed licensed or qualified personnel or a person under physician direction/control, and compliance with prescribed procedures. The limited civil-liability protection applies to medically acceptable authorized collection. A procedural deviation that does not affect reliability does not alone render a test result or opinion inadmissible. Under (4), no sample is required when State Police notifies the court or agency of an adequate previously received sample, or when the court determines that collection would create a substantial and unreasonable health risk. Subsection (5) applies (1)-(4) to the specified persons incarcerated on September 29, 1991 and requires collection before release. Do not replace these predicates with a categorical statement that every prior convict is subject to collection forever.

Section 181.085 authorizes retention of samples and derivative physical material, identity/genetic-profile analysis, an identification database, and anonymous population-frequency databases. It permits accurate and reliable identification techniques, with examples. Disclosure is limited to the specified criminal-investigation/proceeding recipients and purposes; independently required discovery; and lawful court/grand-jury process for evidence not otherwise privileged. Recipient public agencies remain bound by the same limits. Subject access to database records is allowed on request, subject to a reasonable-likelihood-of-prejudice exception for pending investigations; the Department need not allow testing of physical material. Accuracy challenges and preservation procedures must be accommodated as stated.

Following reversal of the predicate conviction, judgment or order, a written destruction request plus a certified reversal order generally requires destruction of the person's sample, derived physical evidence and identification records. The obligation yields to another qualifying obligation; evidence relating to another covered person need not be destroyed, and a set-aside under ORS 137.225 does not itself affect the material. Retention therefore has stated rules; it is not established to be universally perpetual or immediately terminable at release.

The lower opinion characterizes the scheme as forbidding analysis for genetic predispositions. The official quoted section authorizes defined purposes rather than containing those words as a freestanding explicit prohibition. Keep that judicial characterization distinct from enacted language. The anonymous population-frequency permission is an actual additional statutory use; a statement that identity comparison is the sole permissible activity would be incomplete. No sample misuse is found merely because such misuse is technically possible.

The district court relied on special needs and penal administration. The appellate majority expressly sustained the program even assuming ordinary law enforcement was its only purpose. It used reduced offender privacy, the bodily procedure, identification utility, conviction-defined coverage, medical safeguards and limited uses. The dissent separately disputed equating bodily extraction and retained genetic information with fingerprints, stressed the ordinary investigatory purpose and lack of particularized suspicion, and distinguished Schmerber's permission for medically reasonable means from its predicates for initiating the intrusion. Those competing lower-court rationales supply supported paths, not an adopted Supreme Court exception. Actual Vernonia remains limited to its documented athletic program and non-law-enforcement use; historical language quoted in the lower opinion must be replaced with actual simulated law.

The due-process claims remain separate. The general claim sought a precollection hearing; the lower court regarded statutory predicate conviction as leaving little to contest. Milligan's claim concerned noncoverage and ensuing discipline. He refused at least three mistaken collection demands, underwent two disciplinary hearings without raising statutory noncoverage, and received a fine and disciplinary segregation. Tuttle's uncontroverted affidavit attributed the recommendation to reading the attempt language as reaching murder. The remaining appeal was only against Knox and Pearce; the dismissals of DOC, State Police, Madsen and John Doe defendants were not challenged. Neither Tuttle nor the disciplinarians was sued. The district court relied on at-most negligence; the appellate court instead found no evidentiary causal link to personal acts of Knox or Pearce, expressly leaving claims against responsible actors undecided. Supervisory title is not such a link. The source does not establish that Milligan's blood was actually drawn.

The ex post facto question concerns whether the retrospective collection obligation is punishment, not whether its operation is burdensome or constitutional under another clause. Collins v. Youngblood and the existing punishment/retroactivity authorities retain their separate functions. Actual Sandin must be considered if the disciplinary-segregation theory is reached, but no length or conditions of segregation have been established here.

## Supported contrary paths and component-specific consequences

The bodily draw is a search. One path sustains a conviction-defined, medically limited identification program using reduced offender privacy and the legitimate identification need, with actual use and retention controls. Its strongest objection is that ordinary criminal investigation ordinarily requires individualized justification and extracting/retaining biological material is materially more intrusive than taking fingerprints. The contrary path finds the supported law-enforcement purpose insufficient to excuse suspicion or a warrant; its counterargument is the objective conviction predicate, controlled means and claimed identification function. Special-needs and ordinary reasonableness paths are not synonyms: the district and appellate rationales differ, and actual Vernonia's nonpolice limitations must be retained. No future DNA precedent resolves the question.

The general process claim must identify the interest and what is genuinely contestable before collection, including statutory predicate and the enacted sample/health protections, then apply the proper due-process rule. Milligan's individual claim is separate: his predicates were not covered, but the surviving defendants' personal causal responsibility and the actual discipline matter. The appellate no-personal-link ground differs from the district court's negligence rationale. Neither his failure to raise statutory noncoverage at the hearings nor an unrecovered source automatically supplies a new waiver ruling. The record does not establish an actual blood draw from him, discipline duration/conditions, or liability of unsued actors.

For retrospective collection, distinguish punishment from a burdensome regulatory measure. A Fourth Amendment defect would not itself prove ex post facto punishment; nonpunitive classification would not establish search reasonableness. Any remedy must identify the claim, plaintiff and remaining defendant, respect unappealed dismissals, and separate collection, analysis, disclosure, retention and discipline. A hypothetical misuse or unproved universal perpetual-retention scheme is not adjudicated.
