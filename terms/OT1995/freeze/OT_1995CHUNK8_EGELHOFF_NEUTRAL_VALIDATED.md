## Source-supported record, enactment and qualifications

Source: [State v. Egelhoff, 272 Mont. 114, 900 P.2d 260 (July 6, 1995)](https://app.midpage.ai/document/state-v-egelhoff-883627), with the [published text cross-check](https://case-law.vlex.com/vid/state-v-egelhoff-no-889392159). The body gives July 6, 1995; a wrapper's October 1994 label is not the decision date. The state opinion supplies the statute, actual instruction, preservation ruling, competing evidence and remedy. The operative citation is **Mont. Code Ann. §45-2-203**, an unnumbered paragraph, not §45-2-203(3).

The complete challenged statutory paragraph reproduced by the Montana court is:

> A person who is in an intoxicated condition is criminally responsible for his conduct and an intoxicated condition is not a defense to any offense and may not be taken into consideration in determining the existence of a mental state which is an element of the offense unless the defendant proves that he did not know that it was an intoxicating substance when he consumed, smoked, sniffed, injected, or otherwise ingested the substance causing the condition.

The unknown-intoxicant exception is part of the enactment, not an exception for every involuntary or excessive intoxication. Instruction 11 gave the same rule with the shortened phrase concerning consumption; the state opinion prints that instruction separately. The earlier 1985 statute permitted use of an intoxicated or drugged condition to determine an element and separately excused responsibility if involuntarily produced incapacity prevented appreciation of criminality or conformity with law. Those superseded terms must not be imported into the amended paragraph.

Section 45-5-102's submitted deliberate-homicide theory required purposely or knowingly causing death. The published objective charge recitation states that purpose meant a conscious object to engage in conduct of that nature or cause that result; knowledge meant awareness of one's conduct, awareness that under the circumstances the conduct constitutes a crime, or awareness of a high probability that the conduct will cause a specified result. This is a bounded extraction of actual charge language, reported at 518 U.S. 40-41 from petition appendix 28a-29a; it supplies no Supreme Court conclusion about the challenged rule.

The state court found the constitutional objection preserved through the instruction-settlement objection and renewed new-trial motion. Egelhoff challenged consideration of evidence on the mental-state element, not a constitutional entitlement to an affirmative voluntary-intoxication defense. His intoxication evidence was admitted and could be used to explain memory and physical-capacity claims; the disputed instruction barred considering it for the mental-state element. An unqualified statement that the jury heard no intoxication evidence would be false.

The record recitation includes very high blood-alcohol readings, Egelhoff's asserted blackout and fourth-person account, and a doctor's testimony that someone experiencing an alcohol blackout can walk, talk and function. The prosecution pointed to directed behavior, coordination, speech and other circumstances bearing on his ability to commit the killings. Blackout, physical incapacity, absence of memory, and absence of purpose or knowledge are not interchangeable findings. The Montana court treated the restriction as reducing the burden on the State, reversed for a new trial, and did not resolve the remaining assignments concerning lay opinion, evidentiary sufficiency and dangerous-offender classification. Reversal of that constitutional rationale would therefore require attention to the unresolved lower-court issues rather than automatic Supreme Court findings on all of them.

Source-supported issues: legislative power to define culpability and treat voluntary intoxication as irrelevant, versus a restriction on proof of a mental element the State retains; historical acceptance of limiting intoxication evidence versus the right to contest every actual element; and the significance of the complete charge and other uses of the evidence. Winship, Patterson, Martin v. Ohio, Sandstrom, Chambers and Rock are distinct pre-divergence authorities for their actual rules. Actual Gaudin assigns enacted elements to the jury; Staples and Ratzlaf construe their particular federal statutes; Victor requires examination of the complete proof instruction. ## Supported contrary paths and component consequences

The substantive-definition path treats §45-2-203 as Montana's permissible choice of culpability independent of voluntary intoxication, emphasizing the State's authority and the history of limiting that consideration. Its strongest objection is that the State retained purpose or knowledge and expressly forbade using relevant evidence to determine that element. A label cannot resolve how the enactment and complete charge actually operate.

The evidence-exclusion path treats the instruction as disabling the defense from contesting an existing element, while leaving the State formally required to prove it. Its strongest objection is that Winship protects the elements a State lawfully defines and does not itself forbid the challenged rule or create a voluntary-intoxication defense. The State's historical-policy justification and the defendant's right to contest actual elements must each receive their actual constitutional analysis. Evidence of physical capacity, amnesia and directed conduct addresses different questions and cannot be substituted for a finding of purpose or knowledge.

The complete unknown-intoxicant exception remains in the rule; it is not every involuntary-intoxication doctrine. If the statutory restriction survives, the mandate must leave unresolved state assignments for appropriate treatment, not reinstate all convictions through Supreme Court findings on them. If it fails, the selected relief must follow the identified constitutional error; no unsupported harmlessness finding or automatic extension of Sullivan to every evidentiary error is supplied.
