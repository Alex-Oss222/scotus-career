## Provision, judgment and scope

The Colorado electorate approved Amendment2 on November3,1992, 813,966–710,151; certification occurred December16. Suit was filed November12. The operative provision added articleII, §30 to the state constitution:

> No Protected Status Based on Homosexual, Lesbian, or Bisexual Orientation. Neither the State of Colorado, through any of its branches or departments, nor any of its agencies, political subdivisions, municipalities or school districts, shall enact, adopt or enforce any statute, regulation, ordinance or policy whereby homosexual, lesbian or bisexual orientation, conduct, practices or relationships shall constitute or otherwise be the basis of or entitle any person or class of persons to have or claim any minority status quota preferences, protected status or claim of discrimination. This Section of the Constitution shall be in all respects self-executing.

The federal source is the Fourteenth Amendment's prohibition on a State denying any person within its jurisdiction equal protection of the laws. The challenge concerns the amendment's withdrawal of existing sexual-orientation nondiscrimination protections and its restriction of future protection through ordinary state and local government. The challenged text names state branches, agencies, municipalities and school districts; it extends beyond one city ordinance, a private expressive association, or an appropriation. Its reference to quotas/preferences is not the whole provision. Conversely, the source does not establish that every generally applicable tort, criminal, contractual or public-service protection disappears for gay people. The State characterizes the amendment as withholding special protection while respondents describe denial of ordinary equal protection; those characterizations must be tested against text and the authoritative state-law construction.

Colorado's 1993 **EvansI**, 854P.2d1270, sustained preliminary relief and required strict scrutiny for an identifiable class being fenced out of equal political participation. After a merits trial, the trial court permanently enjoined enforcement. The October11,1994 judgment now reviewed, Nos.94SA48/94SA128, 882P.2d1335, affirmed that permanent injunction. The majority adhered to EvansI, found no sufficiently tailored compelling justification, rejected proposed severance and rejected the claimed Tenth Amendment immunity from federal rights. Scott joined and separately proposed a Privileges or Immunities ground. Erickson dissented, rejecting the asserted fundamental right and sustaining the amendment under rational-basis review. These are lower positions, not current Supreme Court holdings.

At minimum the state court's construction establishes repeal of existing orientation-based nondiscrimination provisions and foreclosure of similar or greater protection unless the state constitution is changed. It describes the burden as affecting legislative, executive and judicial redress. One cannot reduce the amendment to simple local preemption while ignoring its restriction of the State itself. A decision may avoid speculative effects on every generally applicable law by resolving the minimum established operation. Federal interpretation must respect the state court's construction rather than invent a narrowing construction merely to obtain a preferred result.

## Preservation, record and remedies

The trial court rejected suspect/quasi-suspect classification on political-powerlessness reasoning. Plaintiffs did not appeal that ruling; respondents expressly do not concede its correctness. It is therefore neither an established new heightened tier nor a new issue automatically opened by this review. The political-process claim is distinct: it asserts a fundamental equality in governmental access independent of suspect-class status. The rational-basis alternative was pressed below and in the merits submissions, but the trial court and state majority declined to adjudicate it because they applied strict scrutiny. That alternative may support affirmance if the legal issue and sufficient record permit, without pretending the lower majority already made such a holding. Respondents' characterization that the State conceded inability to meet strict scrutiny is advocacy in the reviewed summary; no unconditional State concession is independently authenticated here.

The State offered interests in avoiding factionalism, preserving political functions, allocating limited enforcement resources, personal/familial/religious privacy, withholding support from special-interest political objectives, and child welfare. It **did not reassert child welfare on the merits appeal**. Morality was disputed as a separately preserved rationale, but the lower majority found the State's earlier submission sufficient to preserve it. Do not erase that actual ruling or manufacture renewed child-welfare litigation. The relevant interests and their proposed connection to this amendment must remain distinct rather than becoming an undifferentiated assertion of morality or animus.

The majority treated religious freedom as compelling but found the amendment's reach excessive. It identified religious exemptions, including Denver's, and an expert's concession that such an exemption could address the concern more narrowly. It did not adjudicate the adequacy of every existing exemption. The dissent cited particular claimed burdens in Aspen/Boulder and examples of conflicts involving religion or shared housing. Those descriptions support taking the asserted conflict seriously; they do not establish that all ordinary commercial transactions are intimate associations or that every cited municipal rule was unconstitutional. The majority identified existing owner-occupied housing exceptions and potential protections for intimate associations, while observing that Amendment2 covers impersonal commercial and public relationships as well.

On family teaching, the majority found no demonstrated connection between ordinary nondiscrimination protection and an inability of parents to communicate their moral convictions. The State disputed the effect of governmental messages on those values. That dispute does not establish a governmental obligation to endorse every private belief. On fiscal resources, the majority relied on findings from Denver and Wisconsin experience that protection did not increase costs or impair enforcement of other civil-rights laws; the dissent emphasized Colorado's limited enforcement budget, unmet work-share targets and potential future demands. Actual trial findings, predictive legislative judgments and legal conclusions under a selected scrutiny tier must remain separate. Failure to show a compelling, narrowly tailored policy does not by itself establish failure of ordinary rational-basis review.

The State asked to sever orientation from conduct, practices and relationships. The state court's severability test asked whether a remainder would be autonomous and consistent with enacting-body intent. It held these terms identify the same class and are not severable. A federal disposition may change the constitutional premise, but cannot simply decree severance under an invented state-law rule. Bowers's predivergence treatment of specified sexual conduct does not itself decide a prohibition on obtaining nondiscrimination protection across these settings; the State may argue relevance of moral regulation, while respondents may distinguish the actual legal burden without seeking an unpresented overruling.

Affirmance can preserve the permanent injunction against enforcement. Reversal of the political-process rationale alone need not dispose of the preserved rational-basis alternative. A remand can address that genuinely unresolved basis or a material state-law implementation issue if the Court declines to resolve it; missing original documents alone justify no remand or default. Respondents report that some First Amendment claims remain below. No complete disposition of those claims, new damages award, quota entitlement, required enactment of a particular ordinance or nationwide nondiscrimination code is authorized. Scott's Privileges or Immunities theory is a separate lower writing; it supplies neither a majority holding nor an automatically preserved independent question for this review.

## Entering law and alternative decisional paths

**Political-process strict scrutiny.** Hunter invalidated special political burdens tied to racial/religious/ancestral fair-housing protection; Seattle addressed racial restructuring in school integration. Respondents argue the equality principle prevents selectively making ordinary governmental protection unavailable to one identified class even if no one loses the vote. A bounded version distinguishes neutral allocations of governmental authority and substantive policy defeat from removal of one group's opportunity to seek a class of legal protection. If adopted, the breadth of this amendment and narrower religious, intimate-association and budget measures provide substantial support for affirmance under strict scrutiny.

The strongest contrary path reads Hunter/Seattle through their racial classifications and resists creating a fundamental entitlement for every identifiable group to pursue legislation at every governmental level. James upheld a referendum condition for public housing, Gordon a generally applicable supermajority rule, and Crawford a change in state-law desegregation remedies; those precedents require a limiting explanation. The State emphasizes continued voting, petitioning and the possibility of a new constitutional amendment. The reply is that formal ability to seek an amendment does not by itself answer discriminatory removal of ordinary channels. Neither side may equate every political loss with disenfranchisement or every referendum with constitutional immunity. This is a genuine scope dispute; the existing sources do not supply an already-adopted Supreme Court fundamental-right holding for this case.

**Rational-basis affirmance on the actual disadvantage.** Moreno and Cleburne remain limits on illegitimate purpose and irrational prejudice. Simulated **Heller** invalidates a specific unequal safeguard because the proposed differences do not justify the actual disadvantage, without creating a new scrutiny tier. It permits examination here of why asserted religious/privacy/resource concerns justify a class-wide, cross-context prohibition on access to nondiscrimination protection. The text and breadth may support an inference that the proposed legitimate purposes do not explain the selected disadvantage, independently of individual voters' private motives. This path can preserve the injunction without deciding suspect classification or extending Hunter to every identifiable group.

Its strongest counterargument is that breadth and available alternatives are not by themselves irrationality: ordinary review tolerates imperfect classifications and does not demand the least restrictive means. A reasoned decision must confront the State's uniformity, regulatory-restraint and finite-resource explanations, not merely repeat strict-scrutiny failures or announce that any law disapproved by a targeted group embodies impermissible animus. Neither campaign antipathy nor an assertion of legitimate policy alone automatically resolves the law's constitutional character. Findings about trial evidence do not amount to a finding concerning every elector's motive.

**Deferential rational-basis reversal.** Simulated **Beach** allows reasonably conceivable legitimate grounds for economic classifications without formal findings or empirical certainty; it preserves Moreno/Cleburne. A Justice could reject the new fundamental-right theory and find rational relations to limiting regulatory duties, protecting associational/religious choices, allocating finite resources or creating a uniform statewide rule. Heller's family-participation holding illustrates that a narrower alternative does not automatically invalidate a rational category. The State can distinguish removal of statutory benefits from denial of federal constitutional rights and emphasize the amendment process remaining open.

This route must identify an actual rational relationship to the amendment's full minimum operation. It must account for the restriction on state as well as local action, ordinary nonintimate settings, and the group-specific permanent constitutional barrier. Beach's specific economic holding is not blanket immunity for all group classifications, and the controlling Heller proof holding cannot be replaced by its historical counterpart. Rejecting heightened review still requires deciding or properly remanding the preserved ordinary-review argument. A mere “special rights” label cannot do that work.

**Private rights and federalism as bounded grounds.** Simulated **Hurley** unanimously protects a private parade's expressive composition; it expressly preserves ordinary nondiscrimination law and does not make customer identity a message. It supports carefully drawn protection against a concrete compelled-expression burden, but supplies no general exemption for all services or statewide withdrawal of legal protection. Religious and intimate-association concerns likewise require attention to their actual settings. Tenth Amendment/state-structure principles permit democratic institutional choices within federal limits; they do not validate an otherwise established Fourteenth Amendment violation. Gregory's respect for state choices is relevant to the State's argument, without treating the lower court's broad description of Gregory as a complete holding about every federalism question.

**No silent tier transfer.** Simulated **Adarand** retains the supported federal remedial classification standard under Metro/Fullilove, with meaningful purpose/fit review; it does not hold that any law called benign is valid or that orientation receives racial scrutiny. Its equal-opportunity injury distinction is analytically useful: access to a fair process differs from an entitlement to the desired substantive result. That analogy supports disciplined characterization, not automatic application of the contracting scrutiny tier. Similarly, a structural equal-protection argument that one class cannot be made categorically ineligible for governmental protection is supported by respondents' submission, but any adopted categorical rule needs its own defined scope and source. It is not established merely by an amicus's formulation.

## Justice-specific date-eligible sources, without modeled positions

- **Stevens:** Heller's proof majority and family-participation dissent require a relationship to the actual unequal burden. - **Scalia:** He joined Beach and Kennedy's Heller proof dissent/family-participation Court opinion, supporting deference to plausible generalizations in those cases. - **Kennedy:** His Heller proof dissent treats diagnosis, prediction and treatment differences as independently rational; his family-participation opinion sustains useful informational distinctions with safeguards.
