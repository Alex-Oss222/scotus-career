## 1. Verified case, inventory, counts, and preservation

The Eighth Circuit affirmed a jury's convictions on **thirteen counts under 18 U.S.C. §922(o)** and **three counts under 26 U.S.C. §5861(d)**, sixteen counts in all. The appellate opinion was submitted June 10, 1992, decided October 20, 1992, and followed by denial of rehearing November 20, 1992. These are lower-court events; the February 12, 1996 merits date remains the expressly assigned event. The lower opinion supplies no Supreme Court argument date. [H1]

The March 8, 1991 search of Hale's residence produced the following **described inventory**, which must not be replaced by general examples of NFA weapons:

| MAC-10 .45 caliber submachine gun | One |

The account mentions numerous weapons and parts and then identifies these items. It **does not map the thirteen possession counts and three registration counts to particular objects**, identify the numbered counts, establish whether each conversion kit was treated independently, or explain any overlap among counts. Inventory arithmetic cannot manufacture that mapping. No short-barreled rifle or shotgun, grenade, bomb, particular barrel measurement, magazine restriction, or semiautomatic-rifle offense may be added to Hale's record. Such categories can appear in statutory explanation only with that distinction clear. [H1]

The lower majority's footnote 4 characterizes Hale's weapons as military in nature and most of them as capable of killing or maiming groups. This is the appellate characterization of the described weapons, not a complete trial finding about each object's military issue, civilian prevalence, historical use, or constitutional status. Its express acknowledgment of military character must accompany its rejection of the asserted military-utility argument. [H1]

The constitutional-power argument was advanced pro se despite representation. The Eighth Circuit said its ordinary policy disfavored such briefs but considered this argument because it was presented as jurisdictional. That label does not itself decide whether the issue is constitutional authority, statutory coverage, or subject-matter jurisdiction. Hale's Second Amendment theory, negative-record confrontation challenge, and objections to exclusion and judicial notice of the 1979 Senate materials were actually considered below. The appellate sentence disposing of additional undescribed arguments does not supply their contents or preservation. [H1]

Beam concurred in the result, expressly agreed with the hearsay and confrontation discussion, and agreed that these particular weapons were not protected. He objected to treating *Miller* and the cited circuit cases as permitting Congress to prohibit an individual from possessing any type of firearm even for lawful purposes. His writing confirms that the lower result and a categorical collective-right account are analytically separable; it does not provide a developed substitute test for every weapon or regulation. [H1]

### Machinegun prohibition and its exceptions

Section 922(o) provides:

> (1) Except as provided in paragraph (2), it shall be unlawful for any person to transfer or possess a machinegun.
>
> (2) This subsection does not apply with respect to—
>
> (A) a transfer to or by, or possession by or under the authority of, the United States or any department or agency thereof or a State, or a department, agency, or political subdivision thereof; or
>
> (B) any lawful transfer or lawful possession of a machinegun that was lawfully possessed before the date this subsection takes effect.

The machinegun provision took effect on enactment, May 19, 1986, under Pub. L. 99-308 §110(c). The older-weapon exception requires the stated lawful-possession predicate, not merely manufacture before that date. Nothing in the reported Hale opinion establishes a count-specific governmental authorization or grandfather qualification. Nothing establishes a new interstate-commerce element in subsection (o). [S1, S3]

Section 921(a)(23) incorporates §5845(b)'s definition. That provision states:

> The term “machinegun” means any weapon which shoots, is designed to shoot, or can be readily restored to shoot, automatically more than one shot, without manual reloading, by a single function of the trigger. The term shall also include the frame or receiver of any such weapon, any part designed and intended solely and exclusively, or combination of parts designed and intended, for use in converting a weapon into a machinegun, and any combination of parts from which a machinegun can be assembled if such parts are in the possession or under the control of a person.

Whole weapons, receivers, single conversion parts, conversion combinations, and assemblable combinations have different statutory conditions. The weapons' names do not establish each condition or each conviction's theory. The amended parts language was effective before March 1991. [S1–S3]

### NFA possession, taxes, registration, and approval

Section 5861(d) makes it unlawful “to receive or possess a firearm which is not registered to him in the National Firearms Registration and Transfer Record.” It is not simply a requirement that every present possessor file a late registration application. [S2]

Section 5845(a) includes machineguns and silencers among its defined firearms, alongside specifically defined short weapons and destructive devices. It excludes antique firearms and qualifying collector's items under its terms; the collector-item administrative exclusion expressly does not cover machineguns or destructive devices. Section 921(a)(24), incorporated for silencers, includes a device reducing a portable firearm's report, the specified designed-and-intended combination of parts, and a part intended only for such assembly or fabrication. These definitions do not establish that every object named in the general NFA definition was found in Hale's residence. [S1–S2]

The relevant statutory scheme performs separate tasks:

- **Section 5811:** A transfer tax of $200 per firearm, with the express $5 rate for a firearm classified as an “any other weapon” under §5845(e), is paid by the transferor through prescribed stamps. Applicable exemptions elsewhere in the chapter are not adjudicated in this record.
- **Section 5821:** A $200 tax on making each firearm is paid by the maker through the prescribed stamp, subject to any applicable chapter exemption; the reported opinion establishes no Hale-specific exemption.
- **Section 5841:** The central registry covers firearms in the United States except those possessed or controlled by the United States. It records firearm identification, registration date, and the identification and address of the person entitled to possess it. Manufacturers, importers, and makers register what they manufacture, import, or make; a transferor registers a transferred firearm to the transferee. The provision specifies how notification or advance authorization effects registration, preserves the stated prior-registration category, and requires a lawful registered possessor to retain proof.
- **Section 5812:** Transfer requires the transferor's written application, payment of any tax payable, identification of the transferee, transferor and firearm, and approval of transfer and registration. An individual transferee's identification includes fingerprints and a photograph. Approval precedes taking possession. An application must be denied if transfer, receipt, or possession would put the transferee in violation of law.
- **Section 5822:** Making requires the prescribed application, payment of any tax payable, identification of the firearm and maker, the individual's fingerprints and photograph, and approval before making. An application must be denied if making or possession would put the maker in violation of law.

Thus tax liability, approval, registration to a possessor, statutory offense elements, and §922(o)'s exceptions are distinct. A general claim that post-1986 unlawful machineguns cannot receive approval does not establish **which of Hale's three registration counts** involved that condition or that Hale applied and was refused. The neutral input's registration-impossibility contention is a possible legal theory requiring those predicates if decisive, not a verified individualized refusal or a quotation from the reported appellate argument. [H1, S2]

*Sonzinsky* upheld the separate occupational dealer tax and its revenue-supporting registration requirement; it expressly did not decide the validity of every other NFA tax and regulation. Regulatory effects alone do not defeat a genuine tax. *Freed* upheld the revised registration scheme against the presented self-incrimination challenge and explained that a transferee cannot perform the transferor's registration function. Neither case licenses replacing an enumerated power with a general federal public-safety power. *Freed*'s hand-grenade scienter discussion also does not displace the supplied controlling *Staples* rule for a rifle prosecuted as a whole-weapon machinegun. [A2–A3; authorized neutral input]

## 3. Love governs; Miller retains its actual weapon-and-record inquiry

The supplied *Love* holding establishes a personal Second Amendment guarantee: **formal enrollment or current service in an organized militia is not a prerequisite to invoking it**. It preserves *Miller*'s inquiry into the particular weapon on the actual record, does not adopt an exhaustive weapons code or scrutiny tier, and leaves constitutional coverage and lawful regulation distinct. Necessary acquisition is protected when incident to constitutionally protected keeping; that proposition does not itself invalidate every transfer or possession restriction. Its incorporation holding is effective law, though Hale directly challenges federal restrictions. [L1]

Accordingly, the lower court's dependence on Hale's failure to establish a personal relationship to a military organization cannot simply be carried forward as an independent sufficient answer. The opinion's observation that Hale made no claim or showing of such affiliation remains a record fact; *Love* changes the legal use to which it can be put. A claimant cannot be denied a hearing on protected keeping solely by relabeling formal membership as required preparation for a military career. Conversely, removing that prerequisite does not prove that these particular weapons are protected against the particular restrictions. [H1, L1]

The authentic *Miller* opinion corrects an error in the opinion below. **Miller reversed a district court judgment sustaining a demurrer and quashing an indictment, and remanded for further proceedings. It did not affirm a conviction.** The charged conduct was interstate transportation of a described short-barreled shotgun without the required registration and stamp-affixed order. The Court found no evidentiary basis or matter of judicial notice establishing the weapon's reasonable relationship to preservation or efficiency of a well-regulated militia. It described citizens supplying arms of the kind in common use at the time. [A1]

This does not establish that a weapon with military utility is absolutely immune from manufacture, transfer, possession, tax, or registration regulation. Nor does *Miller* decide that military usefulness excludes a weapon from all constitutional protection. Hale's strongest supported argument is that the lower court itself recognized the military character of the actual arms and cannot reject the claim for lack of formal militia connection after *Love*. The Government's strongest supported answer separates a potentially covered weapon from whether a specific prohibition or regulatory duty infringes the guarantee. Neither answer is a complete merits test supplied by existing law. A decision adopting a new scope or regulatory rule must identify that judicial choice and its date-eligible support instead of attributing it to a holding that left it open. [H1, A1, L1]

The personal-right and limited-remand holdings in *Love* are controlling law. Its published separate positions remain noncontrolling at the limits expressly stated in the supplied projection. No separate position supplies a universal weapon classification or an automatic Hale disposition. [L1]

## 4. Registry proof and excluded Senate materials

Two affidavits signed by an ATF specialist stated that, after a diligent search of National Firearms Registration Branch records from 1934 to the then-present, the Bureau found no record of **an application by Hale to register his weapons**. Trial counsel objected on hearsay grounds. At appellate argument, counsel conceded that Rule 803(10) was firmly rooted in the common law and that the affidavits met Rule 902(1)'s self-authentication standard. These are the reported concessions, not a general waiver of every constitutional objection or concession that the registry was infallible. [H1]

The then-applicable Rule 803(10), reproduced in the opinion, permits proof of an absent public record or a matter regularly recorded and preserved by certification complying with Rule 902, or testimony, that a diligent search found none. Authentication, the proper scope of the search, the absence of a record regularly kept, substantive proof of nonregistration to Hale, and constitutional admissibility are different inquiries. Because the actual certificates and indictment are unavailable, the appellate shorthand cannot conclusively prove either that the search covered every legally relevant registration route or that the certificates were deficient. No new sufficiency or wording defect is inferred. [H1, S2]

*Ohio v. Roberts* supplies the inherited reliability inquiry and explains the significance of a firmly rooted exception. *Inadi* rejects reading *Roberts* as a universal requirement to produce every hearsay declarant or prove unavailability; its actual holding concerns statements made during and in furtherance of a conspiracy. Its reason that such contemporaneous statements have evidentiary value that cannot be reproduced at trial is not automatically a finding about a litigation-prepared registry certificate. The lower court's statement that availability is immaterial under Rule 803 accurately describes that rule's category, but the evidence rule's text alone does not decide the constitutional question. *Dutton*, as described in *Inadi*, involved a plurality's treatment of a state coconspirator rule and a distinct fifth vote, not a universal majority holding validating every official affidavit. [A4–A5]

The strongest admissibility path applies the conceded rooted exception and authentication, the described diligent search of regularly kept records, and the absence of a universal unavailability rule, at the scope the actual record supports. The strongest constitutional counterargument distinguishes a prepared assertion establishing an element from contemporaneous records and asks what the opportunity to test the actual search would add. The choice must be made under date-eligible law, without importing any later testimonial-hearsay formulation or a later notice-and-demand version of Rule 803(10). [H1, A4–A5]

Hale offered excerpts from a **1979 Senate subcommittee hearing transcript**, claiming they undermined the firearm registry's reliability. The district court excluded them as hearsay and too old to be reliable and refused judicial notice; the Eighth Circuit found no demonstrated abuse of discretion. This establishes the offered material and rulings, not the truth of every asserted registry defect, later correction, or continuing error rate. [H1]

Admissibility of a properly founded challenge to reliability differs from judicial notice of the truth of disputed assertions. Rule 201's requirement that an adjudicative fact not be reasonably disputable does not become satisfied merely because a statement appears in a congressional transcript. Notice that a hearing occurred is not conclusive notice that its witnesses' assertions were true or proved the state of this registry in 1991. Age alone is no general categorical rule excluding all criticism; relevance, foundation, hearsay purpose and exception, and any preserved right to present a defense require their own assessment. No particular transcript passage or offer of proof is supplied. [H1; authorized neutral input]

## 5. Lawful paths and remedy constraints for later modeling

These paths identify open legal choices. They do not select votes or promise a disposition.

1. **Commerce affirmance of §922(o).** The strongest ground examines possession control as part of regulation of machinegun transfer and supply, with the statutory exceptions and operative scheme actually before the Court. The supplied *Lopez* rejects a chain through generalized crime and productivity costs but does not categorically exclude every noneconomic intrastate activity. *Harris* supports its stated economic vehicle-taking class and enacted nexus; it does not supply a missing nexus element for this statute. The strongest objection is that a bare possession prohibition cannot be saved by invoking a market without demonstrating the legally sufficient connection of the regulated class. A valid defense of subsection (o) must supply that connection rather than repeat the lower opinion's generalized public-order reasoning.
2. **Commerce relief on affected §922(o) counts.** A conclusion that the enacted prohibition exceeds the power on the presented application could require reversal of the affected convictions. It would not automatically invalidate the NFA's separately supported taxes and registration duties. A missing element cannot be supplied judicially merely to save the prosecution. The scope of relief must match the actual constitutional conclusion and the counts it reaches.
3. **Independent NFA analysis.** The tax-registration structure and surviving *Sonzinsky* and *Freed* principles support a distinct authority argument; its strongest objection concerns application where approval and tax collection are legally unavailable. Deciding that objection as applied requires the relevant weapons, dates, statutory category and actual legal basis of the three counts. The supplied record does not establish those predicates. A facial attack on the entire NFA cannot be equated with an as-applied challenge to a particular forbidden machinegun. Avoiding the hypothesized application is possible; affirming or invalidating it as an established fact is not.
4. **Second Amendment affirmance on a valid independent ground.** It remains possible to reject Hale's absolute military-utility theory and sustain the particular prohibition or regulatory requirement under a reasoned scope or regulation analysis consistent with *Love*. The strongest objection is that dismissing absolute immunity alone may not dispose of a preserved narrower personal-right claim, and the abandoned affiliation prerequisite cannot supply the missing answer. Statutory classification alone also does not prove constitutional exclusion. The necessary independent ground must actually be supplied.
5. **Second Amendment vacatur and remand.** The material change in the lower court's personal-affiliation premise can support reconsideration of weapon coverage, the restrictions and count-specific application under *Love*. The strongest objection is that an adequate independent legal ground may permit affirmance on the existing record; a new right-holder premise does not invariably require remand. A lawful remand must name the unresolved issue and its affected components, without automatically ordering acquittal, a new trial, return of all seized items, or reconsideration of unrelated convictions.
6. **Second Amendment reversal of affected counts.** A merits determination that a particular restriction infringes the personal guarantee is a possible endpoint if supported by a complete operative rule and the established record. Military utility or lack of affiliation alone cannot perform all the required work. The relative burdens of the new-possession prohibition and registration of otherwise lawfully possessable arms must remain distinct. Any relief must say which statutory applications fail and why; missing count-to-object facts cannot be invented to enlarge it.
7. **Evidence affirmance or relief.** The two certifications and the Senate excerpts have separate admissibility questions. A constitutional error requires its own preserved-claim and harmless-error analysis; an ordinary evidentiary error requires the applicable nonconstitutional standard. The opinion alone supplies no complete trial-evidence basis for declaring an error harmless or for concluding the evidence was otherwise insufficient. Reversal for erroneous admission or exclusion does not by itself establish an acquittal remedy. No error or harmlessness is found in this preflight.

The controlling *Staples* rule in the supplied neutral input must remain visible for an established rifle-as-whole-machinegun component. The present sources establish no preserved knowledge-instruction claim, no instruction text, and no findings from which a defect or harmlessness can be inferred. *Gaudin* preserves the jury's role on elements; it does not make the jury the judge of statutory meaning, constitutional validity, or threshold evidence admissibility.
