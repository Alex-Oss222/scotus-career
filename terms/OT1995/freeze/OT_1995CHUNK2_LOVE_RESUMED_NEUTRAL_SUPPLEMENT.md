The following record statements are allegations or procedural facts, not adjudicated findings of individual liability:

- Love completed a handgun-purchase application on September 13, 1990. The licensing division received it September 18. Pletcher reviewed the application and state/FBI computer printouts on September 21; Pepersack made the disapproval decision, and Messick's September 21 letter notified Love and directed the seller not to complete the transfer. The complaint alleges a complete, truthful application and no disqualifying conviction or other disqualifying condition. It alleges knowing use of an arrest-only policy, not an accidental clerical error. [P, 3–7; App. 36a–42a.]
- The reported arrests were three Florida arrests in 1976 and an Illinois arrest in 1978. Only one Florida obscene-show misdemeanor resulted in a conviction and fine; the record does not describe a disqualifying conviction. The computer records lacked dispositions. Lack of dispositions and an actual disqualifying conviction are different facts. [L, 122 & n.1; P, 4 n.2.]
- Pletcher is alleged to have recommended denial without further inquiry; Pepersack to have reviewed the same printouts and denied; Messick to have transmitted denial and known and supervised the practice; Tippett to have known and consented to the practice and failed to stop it. The supervisory allegations include both actual-knowledge allegations and knew-or-should-have-known language. Those alternatives must not be collapsed into a finding of actual knowledge or into vicarious liability. [P, App. 37a–45a, paragraphs 11–34.]
- The complaint describes the November 8, 1990 administrative hearing and testimony concerning the longstanding arrest-only practice. In the district judge's oral ruling, counsel corrected the judge's initial description of an independent hearing examiner: the administrative appeal was heard by Pepersack, who had made the denial. That correction was accepted. This is record context; it does not add a procedural-due-process claim the litigation did not pursue. [P, App. 17a, 42a–44a.]
- Love obtained state judicial relief directing approval. The district judge expressly lacked the transcript of the state ruling and assumed for dismissal analysis that the state judge found the denial arbitrary and capricious under state law. The complaint identifies the order, but the packet does not contain the full state-court opinion or order. Do not invent its date, findings, damages remedy, preclusive effect, or a federal constitutional determination. [P, App. 17a–20a, 45a.]
- The amended federal complaint sought compensatory and punitive damages, costs and attorney's fees, and general equitable relief. It did not identify a continuing denial needing an injunction. The petition describes an individual-capacity action against four officers. The eventual permission to purchase does not itself erase the pleaded past deprivation and expense, but no award or amount of recoverable loss is established. [P, App. 35a–36a, 45a–47a; O, 5, 10–11.]

The district court's April 1, 1994 oral decision dismissed Maryland on sovereign-immunity grounds, rejected the officers' attempt to characterize the individual-capacity claims as official-capacity claims, and dismissed the federal theories for lack of a constitutional deprivation. Its substantive-due-process grounds were no protected entitlement under its reading of circuit law and, alternatively, insufficient constitutional arbitrariness. The judge accepted counsel's correction that the statute authorizes disapproval under specified conditions rather than requiring affirmative approval for every purchase. [P, App. 10a–24a.]

The Fourth Circuit heard argument December 7, 1994 and affirmed February 3, 1995. Hall and Currie assumed, without deciding, that Love had a property interest and that the circuit's land-use entitlement rule applied; they rejected substantive due process, treated contractual liberty as a component of that claim, and rejected the Second Amendment claim on nonapplication to the States and militia-related grounds. Luttig concurred in the judgment only because Gardner remained circuit law. Rehearing was denied March 2, 1995. These are lower-court events, not Supreme Court events. [L; P, App. 25a–33a.]

**Scope corrections:** Opposition page 3 footnote 2 states that Love did not appeal Maryland's dismissal and had abandoned a contract-clause claim and claims under the Maryland Declaration of Rights. The petition's four questions concern the individual/collective character of the Second Amendment, militia affiliation, Fourteenth Amendment incorporation, and the sufficiency of the alleged deliberate wrongdoing under §1983. The petition does not renew an independent Contracts Clause or state constitutional count. But it describes freedom to contract as part of the alleged Fourteenth Amendment deprivation, while the Fourth Circuit's footnote 4 had folded that liberty into substantive due process. Therefore:

1. Do not create a new State, official-capacity, Contracts Clause, or Maryland constitutional merits component. Their unreviewed treatment remains undisturbed.
2. The assigned reconstructed scope still permits analysis of ordinary economic/contractual liberty within the presented substantive-due-process theory. Opposition's loose reference to a contract-clause claim does not, without more, establish surrender of every legal argument about that same preserved due-process claim.
3. No procedural-due-process claim was pursued below. The Fourth Circuit expressly says only substantive due process was asserted. The Court may identify the difference but may not silently revive an unpresented procedural count.

## 2. Statutory mechanics and contested appellate inferences

Petition Appendix A reproduces Maryland Article 27 §§442 and 445. Its annotations identify the 1992 addition of an application fee and renumbering: the investigation/disapproval provision shown as §442(g) had been §442(f) in September 1990. Do not retrospectively impose the later fee or silently substitute subsection numbers.

The material mechanics, as reproduced and litigated, are these. The dealer must wait seven days before transferring. The Superintendent must promptly investigate the truth and completeness of the application and may request specified local law-enforcement assistance. A determination of false information or an improperly completed application triggers written disapproval to the seller within seven days of forwarding by certified mail, followed by notice to the applicant. The mailing day counts as day one; a seventh day falling on Sunday or a legal holiday extends to the next day that is neither. A timely disapproval bars transfer unless withdrawn or judicially overruled. An aggrieved applicant may request a hearing within thirty days of notice; the Superintendent must grant a hearing within fifteen days of the request; judicial review follows the administrative procedure statute. These requirements do not say that an arrest without a disqualifying conviction itself establishes falsity. [P, App. 3a–7a.]

The application addresses convictions for crimes of violence and specified drug, conspiracy and firearm offenses; fugitive status; habitual drunkenness; addiction or habitual use of specified drugs; more than thirty consecutive days of treatment for mental disorder, subject to a recent physician certificate that possession creates no undue danger; age twenty-one; and prior applications. Section 445's separate dealer restrictions include reasonable cause to believe a prohibited status exists; that provision is not identical to the Superintendent's application-disapproval condition. Its mental-health/possession provisions and the §442 certificate qualification must not be merged. No presently litigated fact makes those distinct prohibitions dispositive. [P, App. 4a–9a.]

The reproduced statute also preserves bona fide resale by a properly licensed dealer and transactions or use within specified law-enforcement, armed-forces, reserve and National Guard duties. This is not a facial challenge to every statutory restriction or exception. The source is the parties' reproduction of then-current text with conduct-date annotations, not a newly authenticated complete September 1990 official code. The source is sufficient to describe the disapproval controversy and its undisputed state-law correction; a broader definitive construction of a historically disputed provision would require the applicable official enactment.

Love disputes the appellate court's factual characterizations that extreme time pressure forced streamlined investigation and that caution explained the deliberate policy. Respondents defend those inferences from the September dates. The statutory seven-day period is law; necessity, actual motive, staffing, investigatory impossibility and good faith are not established findings. At dismissal, do not use the court of appeals' favorable inference to replace the complaint's alleged deliberate, knowing conduct. Conversely, describing deliberate state-law illegality does not itself establish the separate federal wrong. [P, 23–26; O, 4 n.3, 6; L, 122–123.]

## 3. Qualified immunity: exact preservation and lawful alternative paths

**Verified preservation fact:** Respondents expressly acknowledge in opposition page 11 footnote 6 that qualified immunity was not raised in the motion to dismiss. They say they would allege it in their answer if the Court held the complaint sufficient. Pages 10–11 invoke Anderson, Harlow and Davis as a reason the dispute is a poor vehicle: even if Love obtains a new constitutional rule, they contend existing law did not give the officials fair notice and they would assert immunity on remand. Neither the district court's full oral ruling nor the complete Fourth Circuit opinion decides qualified immunity. The available source packet supplies no later answer, merits brief, or other filing making it a preserved, adjudicated defense before this event.

This establishes neither that immunity was preserved below nor that it has been irretrievably waived. The case ended on a pre-answer motion. Gomez v. Toledo, 446 U.S. 635, 638–642 (1980), and Harlow v. Fitzgerald, 457 U.S. 800, 815 (1982), place pleading of the defense on the official. Love did not have to anticipate and negate it in her complaint. Failure to invoke immunity on this motion is not itself a finding that it could never be asserted in an answer after revival.

There are two genuine reach paths requiring independent consideration:

**A. Decline first-instance immunity resolution.** Apply ordinary preservation and presentation practice, decide the presented merits within the lawful scope, and leave an immunity defense for proper assertion if any claim survives. The strongest grounds are that neither court decided it, it is an affirmative defense not yet pleaded, respondents themselves describe a future answer, and Love's petition predates their immunity argument. No reply or adversarial merits briefing on that defense is supplied. Singleton v. Wulff, 428 U.S. 106, 119–121 & n.8 (1976), protects the opportunity to present law and evidence; Granfinanciera, 492 U.S. 33, 38–40 (1989), distinguishes record-supported grounds properly raised below from exceptional consideration of new grounds. This is a discretionary refusal, not a jurisdictional impossibility. Its strongest counterargument is that a purely legal, fully determinable immunity ground might avoid unnecessary constitutional adjudication and further proceedings without changing the favorable judgment.

**B. Consider a legally sufficient immunity alternative now.** The Court has power to make a justified exception to ordinary preservation practice. Singleton's examples of an answer beyond doubt or prevention of injustice are nonexclusive. Carlson v. Green, 446 U.S. 14, 17 n.2 (1980), illustrates an exception where a question absent below was squarely presented, fully briefed without objection, and important to recurring litigation; those circumstances must not be invented here. A Justice could assess whether the damages-only posture, alleged conduct accepted as true, existing September 1990 law and unchanged favorable judgment make this a sufficiently legal and conclusive exception. If so, the Justice must state that reach ground separately, identify the precise federal right and conduct-date notice problem, and apply it individually to the defendants and claims. Its strongest counterargument is the absent pleading, absent lower adjudication and incomplete adversarial treatment; avoiding constitutional difficulty or labeling an issue an alternative ground does not alone overcome them. If notice or the defense depends on additional facts, the current record cannot supply them.

Neither path is selected by this preflight. Taking the defense requires an actual legal justification for first consideration; declining it does not decide its eventual merits. A remand is lawful if the Court rejects a dispositive ground below without itself completing all remaining inquiries. No path automatically awards damages, commands a trial, or creates an injunction.

They establish no general immunity-first duty.

Deliberate state-law breach is not interchangeable with clearly established federal unlawfulness.

## 4. Substantive due process: competing merits routes

The law must separate a state-created entitlement, the actual constitutional prohibition, personal attribution, and relief. Roth can support the first inquiry; Hafer supplies individual-capacity coverage and preserves defenses; neither establishes the second. The mandatory disapproval conditions support Love's entitlement argument. The district court's contrary investigation-discretion analysis and Luttig's Gardner ground remain available for examination, but the Fourth Circuit majority declined to settle the entitlement question or extend Gardner beyond land use.

**A supported affirmance route** assumes the asserted interest and asks whether the pleaded conduct establishes a substantive constitutional deprivation. Collins's received alternative holding distinguishes breach of an assumed state duty from constitutional arbitrariness. It does not constitutionalize every erroneous or even deliberate violation of licensing law. Respondents can argue the purchase-screening function and review mechanism, while accepting the pleaded facts, do not describe the sort of substantive deprivation recognized by governing law. The ordinary economic/contractual liberty claim does not create an unconditional federal right to complete any transaction.

**A supported correction/remand route** rejects the premise that later state correction categorically cures substantive constitutional wrongdoing and insists on assessment of the actual deliberate-policy allegations. Zinermon v. Burch, 494 U.S. 113, 125–126 (1990), distinguishes substantive and incorporated-right violations, complete when the forbidden act occurs, from procedural claims where the total process matters. Daniels, 474 U.S. 327 (1986), concerns negligence; its holding does not relabel this complaint negligent. Stevens's concurrence in Daniels, 337–340, supplies the same categorical distinction. Love alleges a knowing departure from the statute, lasting denial of acquisition, expense, and a routine policy, not merely dissatisfaction with an adjudicative outcome. The counterargument remains substantial: defeating the court of appeals' cure rationale and its favorable factual inferences does not itself identify a sufficiently protected substantive interest or establish constitutionally forbidden arbitrariness. A remand may preserve that unresolved inquiry; an immediate liability judgment does not follow.

Collins is confined to its actual workplace-omission holding and alternative; its express reservation of deliberate bodily injury and different affirmative danger does not itself decide this licensing case. Albright supplies no controlling synthesized substantive-due-process test, no universal state-remedy bar, and no confinement or financial-loss prerequisite. No model may combine its separate rationales into an invented rule. Neither Zinermon's procedural result nor the administrative-hearing facts authorize adding Love's unpursued procedural claim.

## 5. Second Amendment: distinct forks and consequences

The source petition squarely seeks reconsideration of nonincorporation and of the collective-only/militia-affiliation reasoning. These are related but distinct questions. Recognition of an individual right against the Federal Government does not automatically apply it against Maryland; incorporation would not itself decide the full scope of a purchase right, the validity of all licensing, or damages immunity.

**Retain the inherited state-application rule.** Cruikshank, 92 U.S. 542, 553 (1876), describes the arms guarantee as restraining national rather than state authority. Presser, 116 U.S. 252, 265 (1886), expressly invokes that rule in sustaining Illinois's regulation of unauthorized armed military associations. The received baseline contains no subsequent incorporation holding. A decision retaining that rule can resolve the state-officer Second Amendment claim without adopting every statement below about collective rights. The strongest objection is that later incorporation cases rejected the broad premise that enumerated protections cannot bind States; the Court itself can reassess the specific arms holding rather than treat lower-court obedience as dispositive of its own inquiry.

**Reassess state application under received incorporation doctrine.** Duncan v. Louisiana, 391 U.S. 145, 148–150 (1968), examines the fundamental character of a right in the American system; Malloy v. Hogan, 378 U.S. 1, 6–11 (1964), recognizes that received incorporated guarantees apply against the States through the Fourteenth Amendment. Love argues that arms should receive comparable treatment and that the earlier cases did not conduct that later inquiry. Text and the historical accounts in Presser and Miller supply sources for assessing the protected interest. The counterargument is that incorporation of other provisions does not silently overrule the specific inherited arms precedents, and neither the constitutional label nor scholarship establishes the necessary historical and legal conclusion by itself. A new holding must explain the treatment of Cruikshank and Presser and identify the right incorporated; it cannot borrow later firearm holdings, tests or historical conclusions.

**Militia-related / collective reading.** Miller, 307 U.S. 174, 178–182 (1939), requires attention to militia preservation and effectiveness, refusing to recognize protection for the short-barreled shotgun on the evidentiary record before it. Johnson, 497 F.2d 548 (4th Cir. 1974), supplies the Fourth Circuit's collective-right construction. Love pleaded no militia relationship for this handgun. That supplies respondents' alternative account if the Court reaches scope. The strongest objection is that Miller addressed the weapon's relation to militia effectiveness and did not hold that only an organized state body may invoke the Amendment. It reversed and remanded the quashing of an indictment; do not convert it into a final conviction or an exhaustive rule for all firearms.

**Individual-right reading with a separately defined scope.** The operative text assigns the right to the people. Miller describes citizens bringing their own arms and a militia drawn broadly from the population; Presser acknowledges a reserve military resource while sustaining regulation of unauthorized military parades. Love argues that these accounts do not condition a person's invocation of the right on formal militia affiliation and that acquisition can be necessary to keeping arms. Verdugo-Urquidez, 494 U.S. 259, 265 (1990), gives a textual comparison of the people across amendments, but only as an expressly nonconclusive aid in a Fourth Amendment case. The strongest counterargument is that individual possession consistent with militia purposes does not establish an unrestricted self-defense or acquisition right, and the precise scope and incorporation steps remain necessary. Even an individual-right conclusion can leave nonincorporation dispositive; even incorporation can leave reasonable regulation and the effect of this alleged arbitrary policy unresolved.

A narrow disposal of one sufficient issue should identify other questions left open. An immunity-only resolution, if lawfully reached, would not establish any firearms merits rule. A new 1995 merits rule cannot itself establish September 1990 notice. The petition's reliance on Kellogg, 562 N.E.2d 685 (Ind. November 8, 1990), is relevant as a later state high-court analogy involving denied application forms, but the decision postdates the initial disapproval and cannot itself supply notice then. Its distinct state constitutional and statutory premises must not be imported as Maryland law. No present source establishes a later actionable event to which it supplied contemporaneous notice.
