## 1. Review, participation and the established legal burden

Review is of the Colorado Supreme Court's October 11, 1994 judgment, 882 P.2d 1335, Nos. 94SA48 and 94SA128, affirming a permanent injunction after a merits trial. The authorized packet supplies a state-court merits-review channel under 28 U.S.C. §1257. No supported jurisdictional, mootness, participation or preservation defect prevents review of the presented equal-protection question. No new finality defect is inferred from respondents' report that separate First Amendment claims remain below.

Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer were seated at argument and remain seated at decision. No recusal or nonparticipation is established. With the participating Chief Justice, nine constitute the Court and five votes would be necessary for a Court judgment or controlling proposition. This file models only the eight Associates.

The provision adds article II, §30 to Colorado's constitution:

> No Protected Status Based on Homosexual, Lesbian, or Bisexual Orientation. Neither the State of Colorado, through any of its branches or departments, nor any of its agencies, political subdivisions, municipalities or school districts, shall enact, adopt or enforce any statute, regulation, ordinance or policy whereby homosexual, lesbian or bisexual orientation, conduct, practices or relationships shall constitute or otherwise be the basis of or entitle any person or class of persons to have or claim any minority status quota preferences, protected status or claim of discrimination. This Section of the Constitution shall be in all respects self-executing.

The Fourteenth Amendment forbids a State to deny any person within its jurisdiction equal protection of the laws. The minimum authoritative state-law operation here is repeal of existing orientation-based nondiscrimination protections and foreclosure of similar or greater protection through ordinary state and local legislative, executive and judicial channels unless the state constitution changes. It covers the State itself, schools and public policies as well as local regulation of private conduct. It therefore cannot be treated as only a quota prohibition, rejection of one ordinance or transfer of local authority to an otherwise free state legislature.

That construction does **not** establish that gay, lesbian or bisexual people lose every generally applicable criminal, tort, contract or public-service protection. The commitments do not depend on that broader premise. They address eligibility for the identified antidiscrimination protection and the constitutional obstacle to obtaining it through ordinary government.

The trial court rejected suspect/quasi-suspect status; plaintiffs did not appeal that ruling while declining to concede its correctness. No Associate here reopens that classification issue. The political-process claim is separate from suspect status. Rational-basis invalidity was pressed below and in the merits submissions, but the trial court and Colorado majority did not decide it because they applied strict scrutiny. All eight Associates would resolve the preserved alternative in this review; none treats rejection or nonadoption of the lower court's strict-scrutiny reasoning as sufficient by itself to dispose of the appeal.

The State's asserted interests must remain separate: religious and intimate-association protection; familial teaching; finite enforcement resources; uniformity and regulatory restraint; avoiding factionalism; declining to support the political objectives of a special-interest group; and morality. The lower majority treated morality as preserved. Child welfare was not reasserted on the merits appeal and supplies no renewed defense here. Respondents' statement that the State conceded strict-scrutiny failure is not an authenticated unconditional concession.

## 3. Supported alternatives and the decisive objections

These are conclusions about legally available paths, not an account of private deliberations. A Justice's agreement with one outcome does not add support to a different sufficient rationale.

### A. Affirm under ordinary rational-basis review

The preserved alternative permits affirmance without adopting political-process strict scrutiny. The relevant disadvantage is the identified group's combined loss of existing antidiscrimination protection and constitutional ineligibility for comparable ordinary governmental protection across the amendment's covered contexts. Under Heller's actual proof holding and Moreno/Cleburne, that disadvantage must have a rational connection to a legitimate purpose.

The strongest objection is that this analysis can disguise a least-restrictive-means test: exemption possibilities, some nonintimate applications and failure of compelling-interest proof do not establish irrationality. Uniformity, deregulation and conserving resources can be legitimate, and an electorate need not leave every policy choice to ordinary legislation. A decision cannot invalidate the amendment merely because the Court prefers a different regulatory arrangement or because the category receives moral disapproval.

The six Associates adopting A answer that objection through the actual coverage, not a new burden of proving necessity. Uniformity explains a common rule but does not explain why this group alone is constitutionally disabled from seeking nondiscrimination protection from state as well as local authorities. Regulatory restraint explains opposition to particular private obligations but does not explain the simultaneous prohibition on ordinary public policies and protections across relationships bearing no religious, intimate or expressive character. Fiscal scarcity permits priorities; it supplies no rational explanation for forbidding protection without regard to the governmental body's resources or the nature of the protection. The trial findings about Denver and Wisconsin reinforce that conclusion without requiring empirical refutation of every conceivable saving.

Likewise, parental freedom to teach values survives nondiscrimination laws, while government need not communicate every parent's preferred moral judgment. A desire to stop one side's political objectives or avoid its participation because the issue is divisive is not an independent legitimate justification for this disability. Bowers's treatment of particular conduct does not explain barring access to protection for people by orientation and across ordinary public and commercial settings. The amendment's structure and minimum operation permit rejection of the supposed legitimate connections without finding that every supporter acted from hostility. The absence of such a connection, coupled with its class-wide disadvantage, places the amendment within Moreno/Cleburne's limit. This does not impose a general duty to enact an antidiscrimination law.

### B. Affirm by extending political-process strict scrutiny

Hunter/Seattle support an argument that a State cannot selectively close ordinary avenues for a category of protection to one identified class while leaving those avenues available to others. A defensible version would distinguish this closure from a neutral allocation of authority and from losing a vote on substantive policy. On that premise, the amendment's reach and narrower ways to protect religion, intimacy and fiscal priorities would support affirmance.

The strongest objection is the extension beyond the racial classifications that performed essential work in Hunter/Seattle, particularly in light of James, Gordon and Crawford and the unappealed suspect-status ruling. Every constitutional provision entrenches some choice and affects some identifiable interests; ordinary voting and amendment remain available. The six adopting A do not need to resolve that extension and commit to no additional independent strict-scrutiny holding. They may use the process authorities to describe the difference between a policy loss and the challenged disability, but do not turn that analogy into a new fundamental-right tier. Scalia and Thomas reject the broad fundamental-right ground urged below. No vote here rests on accepting respondents' claimed State concession.

### C. Reverse under deferential rational-basis review

Scalia and Thomas would hold that ordinary rationality sustains the amendment. Their ground treats the electorate's choice as a substantive, statewide decision to withhold a disputed statutory protection and the obligations it would create, implemented through constitutional reservation. Religious and associational concerns, regulatory restraint and finite enforcement resources rationally bear on that policy even though federal law does not require the State's chosen degree of accommodation. Uniformity reinforces the statewide choice. Imperfect coverage does not make a legitimate policy irrational, and narrower exemptions are not constitutionally mandatory under rational-basis review.

The strongest objection is that this account can erase the state-wide institutional disability by calling it ordinary preemption or special rights. Scalia and Thomas accept the full minimum construction: the amendment constrains state as well as local action and reaches public and nonintimate settings. They consider that reach part of preserving a single substantive policy at the constitutional level, rather than evidence that the policy can have no rational purpose. The continued capacity to change the constitution does not itself immunize the amendment; it demonstrates the form of the retained political choice after its substantive policy has separately survived their rationality analysis.

They preserve Moreno/Cleburne and do not regard every moral dislike as legitimate. Their contrary application rests on the supported dispute over antidiscrimination obligations and resource/association policy, not a right to injure people for its own sake. They would distinguish Heller's controlling proof holding as an inadequate relation between offered condition differences and a particular risk of mistaken confinement, rather than treat it as a general authority to require the least burdensome regulatory policy. They rely on neither universal religious unconstitutionality of the local ordinances nor Bowers as directly controlling this judgment.

### D. Reject strict scrutiny and remand without deciding ordinary review

A limited remand is legally available if the Court declines to resolve the preserved rational-basis alternative; rejection of the lower rationale alone cannot justify treating that alternative as lost. It could also be necessary if a genuinely material state-law implementation issue required resolution. The present record supplies the amendment, its authoritative minimum construction, the interests, relevant findings, adversarial rational-basis positions and state severability determination. All eight Associates reach a legal conclusion under ordinary review on that record and reject a new merits remand merely because the lower majority selected strict scrutiny. Missing filmed pages are not a reason to manufacture a factual inquiry or litigant default.

## 6. Remedy, severability and matters left open

The six affirming Associates preserve the permanent injunction against enforcement of Amendment 2. They do not require a new trial, a separate inquiry into each elector's motive, a new legislative record or reconsideration of a legal alternative they have resolved. The Court may affirm the judgment on the preserved rational-basis ground without affirming the Colorado majority's strict-scrutiny rationale.

The state court's autonomy-and-enacting-intent severability determination matters independently. Orientation, conduct, practices and relationships identify the same targeted class; deleting only orientation would leave the same constitutional disability through the other terms. Because A's defect reaches that shared operation, the existing inseverability determination supports the whole-amendment injunction.
