## A. Fourth Amendment: collection and authorized use

All eight recognize that compulsory blood extraction is a search. Skinner also treats analysis of a bodily sample as a protected examination; identifying purpose does not eliminate the initial bodily intrusion. Actual Vernonia's unanimous search/no-waiver component and Myers's protection of otherwise concealed information prevent a general declaration that a label such as identification removes all privacy. Myers concerns a home and supplies no automatic bodily-sampling result. Jaffee protects a defined confidential treatment relationship under Rule501; it creates no general medical privilege against offender identification.

**Individualized-justification path:** The ordinary police objective of obtaining a biological identification resource for investigating future crimes does not, on this record, justify suspicionless blood extraction merely because the person has a covered conviction. Schmerber distinguishes lawful grounds for entering the body from safe means of doing so. Winston confirms that medical reasonableness alone is insufficient. Skinner, Von Raab and actual Vernonia rest on their particular nonprosecutorial safety or supervisory needs; Bell's institutional-security rationale does not convert every investigatory database into prison administration. Sitz shows that not every law-enforcement seizure requires individual suspicion, but its initial brief roadside observation does not authorize extraction and retention of internal biological material. Statutory evenhandedness reduces discretion; it does not itself supply grounds for this bodily search.

The strongest contrary case is substantial: these are convicted persons, the State already has a legitimate identification interest, the physical procedure is modest, the selection rule is objective, and actual use/retention safeguards and record-supported utility make this unlike arbitrary testing of the public. The rejecting positions accept those features but find the proposed extension insufficient. Fingerprinting is an analogy to examine, not an answer that erases the bodily threshold. The ruling need not find misuse, permanent retention in every case, or medical danger to invalidate the justification offered. It does not decide a different prison-health/security program, consensual collection, a supported warrant/exception, every noninvasive identification method or every existing database's use.

**Convicted-offender identification path:** The covered convictions, lawful correctional relationship, limited medical intrusion, objective selection and defined uses together make this identification program reasonable without a fresh suspicion of a different unsolved crime. This is a new application of contextual reasonableness, not a claim that Vernonia already adopted a DNA exception or that convicted persons have no bodily privacy. Skinner's contextual assessment, Sitz's refusal to make ordinary law enforcement a universal bar to structured balancing, and the legitimate identification function support it. The law permits repeat sampling only on inadequacy notification, preserves an adequate-sample exception and judicial health-risk protection, and regulates retention/disclosure. Anonymous population-frequency use remains part of the actual scheme; no extra individualized medical or behavioral analysis is approved.

Its strongest objection is that a conviction does not ordinarily authorize new bodily evidence-gathering for unrelated future prosecutions, and the privacy loss extends beyond the brief needle procedure. The accepting positions answer only for this conviction-defined and medically controlled identification scheme and its authorized uses; they do not approve a general criminal-history exception, a regime for mere arrestees, unrestricted analysis, arbitrary resampling or harsher medical intrusion. The approved statutory limits matter to their agreement.

**Commitments:** Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Souter and Ginsburg select the individualized-justification path and would reverse the Fourth Amendment ground sustaining summary judgment and remand the covered plaintiffs' claims for appropriate relief. Kennedy, Thomas and Breyer select the confined identification path and would affirm this component. None supplies a direct damages award, automatic destruction order or liability finding for every named defendant. Relief must follow actual claims, capacities, immunities and participation; no hypothetical secondary misuse is adjudicated.

## B. General precollection process

The liberty interest includes bodily integrity and the substantive limits on who must provide blood. A final predicate conviction need not be retried in a new hearing. But conviction is not the statute's only operative condition: correct coverage, State Police notice of an adequate prior sample, and the court's substantial/unreasonable-health-risk determination remain distinct. Schmerber's rejection of a particular substantive-due-process attack on medically acceptable collection does not establish that no procedural protection can ever be required. Actual Albright supplies no controlling general displacement of all due-process claims by the Fourth Amendment.

**Remand path:** Vacate the general procedural-due-process summary judgment because the court below treated conviction as the only condition and therefore did not examine the actual opportunities to invoke the remaining substantive safeguards. Apply Mathews's private interest, error risk/value of additional process and administrative burden. A scheduled bodily invasion and potentially material coverage/health mistakes warrant meaningful consideration of available notice and objection channels. The current record does not establish those channels' adequacy or inadequacy. Remand orders no automatic oral evidentiary hearing, new trial of the conviction, fixed deadline or finding that these plaintiffs were denied an exception. Actual Sandin supports a genuine-constraint/process inquiry rather than an automatic inference from statutory commands.

The strongest objection is that the general demand for a precollection hearing presents no adjudicated health or prior-sample dispute for the covered plaintiffs, and record verification or ordinary judicial access may suffice. The remanding positions accept that less formal process may suffice but will not supply its actual availability from silence or accept the false premise that nothing besides conviction is contestable. They require ordinary lower-court examination, not a new factual presumption against the State.

**Narrow affirmance path:** Affirm rejection of a categorical entitlement to an additional hearing before every otherwise lawful collection. The predicate is objective, the final conviction was adjudicated, and the statute expressly retains its court/agency exceptions. A repeated hearing does not improve the established conviction determination merely because collection follows it. This path reserves any concrete claim that coverage was mistaken, a relevant exception could not be invoked, or available procedures were inadequate; it does not hold that bodily integrity lacks constitutional significance or that substantive exceptions may be ignored. Its strongest objection is the absence of a developed account of those available procedures and the actual coverage error illustrated by Milligan. Its answer confines the judgment to the generalized hearing demand, rather than endorsing the lower court's entire rationale.

**Commitments:** Stevens, O'Connor, Souter, Ginsburg and Breyer select remand. Scalia, Kennedy and Thomas select narrow affirmance. Where the Fourth Amendment claim also succeeds, the process remand is conditional on a live independent process issue requiring decision in the ensuing proceedings; it cannot authorize an otherwise unconstitutional collection. A hearing cannot cure the substantive search defect.

## C. Retrospective punishment

All eight affirm rejection of the Ex Post Facto Clause challenge to the collection obligation as presented. The scheme uses past convictions to identify the covered population, but its design and demonstrated operation serve identification rather than a new penalty for the earlier offense. The record establishes no increased sentence, reduced parole opportunity, retroactively enlarged crime or eliminated defense. Modest medical collection and qualified expense reimbursement do not, on this showing, convert the scheme into additional punishment. Costs are not imposed on those unable to pay. No ruling approves unspecified sanctions or detention beyond an otherwise lawful release date.

The strongest objection is that compelled bodily invasion, retention, probation conditions and deterrent effects impose new burdens because of an earlier crime. Those features require scrutiny rather than acceptance of a civil label. The limited identification purpose and actual safeguards nevertheless make the presented obligation different from additional penal severity. Actual Morales protects a meaningful avenue to earlier release from retrospective withdrawal; it does not make every new regulation of convicted persons punishment. A Fourth Amendment defect would not by itself establish punishment, and nonpunitive classification does not make the search reasonable. Milligan's noncovered discipline is considered separately; it cannot be treated as a lawful application establishing the program's general punitive character.

## D. Milligan, Knox and Pearce

All eight affirm summary judgment for Knox and Pearce on the lack of evidence connecting either official's own conduct to the mistaken demand and ensuing discipline. Actual Hafer requires the officer's own violation; supervisory title or general implementation responsibility does not establish personal causation. The record attributes the interpretive mistake to Tuttle, who is not a defendant; the disciplinarians are also unsued. Milligan's attempted murder and weapon conviction do not qualify under the statute. There is no established blood draw from him.

The strongest contrary path seeks remand because repeated unauthorized demands and segregation may deprive protected liberty under actual Sandin. That possibility is real and remains undecided as to responsible actors, but it does not supply Knox/Pearce's missing causal acts. The appellate basis differs from the district court's at-most-negligence rationale. No new finding that every actor was merely negligent is made, and Daniels need not dispose of all possible claims. Failure to raise coverage at the two disciplinary hearings is not declared waiver. Segregation duration/conditions, responsible-actor liability, damages and applicable defenses are not invented. The unappealed dismissals remain undisturbed.
