## Event

Planned Parenthood, Sioux Falls Clinic v. Miller. Merits decision, April 15, 1996; October Term 1995. On review under 28 U.S.C. §1254(1) of the Eighth Circuit's August 31, 1995 judgment, 63 F.3d 1452, in Nos. 94-3326SD and 94-3398SD. Those numbers identify the appeals below; no separate Supreme Court docket or oral-argument date is supplied.

The question is what facial-review standard governs and whether South Dakota may impose this compulsory parental-notification condition on unemancipated minors seeking previability abortions without a usable independent maturity or best-interests route. The clinic, Buck J. Williams, M.D., and Women's Medical Services, P.C., challenge the official-capacity administration of the law. The District Court resolved cross-motions for summary judgment, invalidating notice and separate penalty provisions while sustaining mandatory information and severability. The court of appeals affirmed. Only the minor-notice and facial-review component is before this Court.

Section 34-23A-7 ordinarily prohibits abortion until at least forty-eight hours after written parental notice. Personal delivery must be by the physician or an agent; certified mail requires return receipt and restricted delivery, with delivery deemed at noon on the next regular mail-delivery day after mailing. The period is not measured simply from mailing.

The exceptions require either a physician's good-faith clinical certification that immediate abortion is necessary to avert the minor's death or that delay will create a serious risk of substantial and irreversible impairment of a major bodily function, and that insufficient time exists to provide notice; written certification by the person entitled to notice that notification occurred; or the minor's declaration or information indicating statutory abuse or neglect, followed by the physician's required report. Recipient social-services, prosecutorial and law-enforcement authorities must protect the fact that an abortion was sought or obtained against parental disclosure. The abuse definition includes threatened substantial harm, an injurious environment and substantial emotional or mental impairment, as well as completed abuse. None of these provisions supplies an independent determination of maturity or best interests. The separate general information provision's twenty-four-hour period is not a new period imposed by the abuse exception.

## Holdings

### I. The complete Casey standard governs the actual notice duty

**Holding and operative rule:** The complete six-step previability standard of Planned Parenthood of Southeastern Pennsylvania v. Casey, as applied in Fargo Women's Health Organization v. Schafer, governs this notice challenge. Neither an independent no-valid-applications barrier nor a numerical facial threshold displaces its duty, causation, proof, substantive-protection and remedial requirements. The challengers must establish the triggering interference; a state justification burden does not arise merely because abortion is regulated.

**Authority:** Part I of Justice O'Connor's Opinion of the Court, with the six complete joins identified in Opinion Topology.

**Controlling explanation:** Casey requires identification of the enacted command and protected baseline, its function and purpose, the additional interference it causes, the applicable substantive protection, clinical and communication safeguards, and relief matching the violation. A baseline violation, proved obstructive purpose or substantial obstacle independently invalidates the requirement; no interest balancing rescues it. Remaining severe interference requires compelling justification and narrow tailoring. Remaining meaningful interference requires substantiated justification and fit, including a response to a concrete practicable less burdensome alternative. Below meaningful interference that heightened access inquiry ends without extinguishing independent constitutional protections.

Here the starting population is unemancipated minors seeking protected previability care who are subject to this notice condition. Compare their circumstances with the same system absent this duty and its interval, holding other law and relevant conditions constant. Within that population, examine supported circumstances of compelled disclosure and unusable exceptions before deciding injury. Neither all pregnant patients nor only successful constitutional claimants defines the population. The single-parent-household statistic answers an assumed safe second parent; it is not an injury rate.

The State may encourage useful parental advice, assistance and informed consideration. Legislative opposition to abortion alone proves no obstructive purpose, and this decision makes no such finding. But calling a command notice rather than consent does not end review of what it does. Fargo applies the full inquiry without an independent Salerno barrier. Correcting the court of appeals' abbreviated facial formulation requires that inquiry, not a remand for findings the supported record does not need.

**Precedent treatment:** Casey supplies the whole standard and proof allocation; Fargo applies them without a separate numerical or all-applications prerequisite. Salerno does not impose such a prerequisite in this setting; its operation elsewhere is not decided.

### II. The supported practical-control and obstruction applications are unconstitutional

**Holding and operative rule:** Compulsory notice is invalid where its demonstrated operation gives a parent practical control over the minor's protected decision or substantially obstructs timely care. The record establishes such applications of this notice condition, and the actual exceptions do not adequately protect them. A permissible parental-welfare interest cannot justify that established interference.

**Authority:** Part II of the same Opinion of the Court, joined by the same six Justices.

**Controlling explanation:** Formal consent and notice differ, and minors' welfare permits a parental role not imposed on an adult spouse. Those differences do not erase demonstrated practical prevention. The summary-judgment submissions recounted below include an honor-student minor whose father, after learning she was at the clinic, assaulted staff and forced her to leave; other accounts describe parental obstruction, newly threatened abuse after disclosure, and reporting reluctance causing delay. They support additional interference attributable to compulsory disclosure. Private hostility alone is not the constitutional injury; the State's added condition exposes the protected decision to the demonstrated obstruction.

The abuse definition reaches threatened harm and is not limited to an earlier assault. Its confidentiality command also matters. Yet the reporting requirement does not furnish a route for every mature or best-interests minor, and supported reluctance to report an abusive parent limits its practical use. The court of appeals' account of access to juvenile records, testimony and the provider's identity supports confidentiality concerns without establishing that every official will breach the command or that a particular later disclosure occurred. Written parental acknowledgment proves notice, not independent authorization. An emergency exception conditioned on insufficient time does not resolve the nonemergency problem. Casey's practical-control protection therefore applies while preserving the distinct permissible interests in minors' welfare.

**Precedent treatment:** Casey's spousal-notice holding supplies the distinction between formal notice and effective control; its application here rests on this minor-specific record. Matheson, addressing an immature dependent minor without an established mature or best-interests claim, does not decide these applications. No categorical equivalence of every notice and consent law follows.

### III. The State also fails to justify the remaining meaningful interference

**Holding and operative rule:** Independently of the established obstacle applications, the condition imposes demonstrated remaining meaningful interference through additional delay, forced disclosure and coercive consequences, and the State has not substantiated its fit or adequately answered the concrete alternative of a usable confidential maturity or best-interests route. That failure supplies a separate sufficient basis for invalidating this compulsory structure at the scope addressed in Part IV.

**Authority:** Part III of the same Opinion of the Court, joined by the same six Justices.

**Controlling explanation:** Meaningful interference triggers review; it is not by itself invalidity. Parental help can promote sound decisions, but general reliance on that benefit does not explain compelling disclosure where the supported circumstances make involvement harmful or inappropriate. The challenger must establish a concrete added burden, and the State may answer through reliable experience and reasonable predictions. Neither scientific certainty nor a new legislative study is required. Here the actual burdens and the missing independent route are established sufficiently to decide the question.

An effective maturity or best-interests procedure is a concrete alternative, illustrated by Casey's sustained consent structure and the independent-authorization authorities. It preserves justified parental involvement while addressing minors for whom compulsory disclosure frustrates the asserted welfare interest. The broad abuse route cannot substitute for a maturity inquiry and does not fully answer the supported best-interests, reporting and confidentiality gaps. The State offers no adequate showing of the alternative's unavailability, insufficiency or material countervailing consequences. This is not a demand to select the cheapest policy or disprove every imagined alternative.

Casey's severe-interference branch remains distinct: an established serious intrusion into protected confidentiality, health or clinical judgment requires its own compelling justification and narrow tailoring when the independent prohibitions have not already resolved the issue. The Court need not classify every remaining notice injury as severe; the supported obstacle ground and the separate failure of justification and fit suffice. Timely necessary care and genuine clinical judgment remain protected, without deciding an independent challenge to this emergency definition.

**Precedent treatment:** Casey and Fargo retain their full residual-interference inquiry. Bellotti's independent-authorization principle and Casey's sustained bypass establish a concrete alternative; they do not transplant every procedural feature of a consent statute into this notice law. Ohio v. Akron Center's approval of an available bypass did not itself decide its universal necessity.

### IV. Relief must remove the unconstitutional compulsory condition without deciding other applications

**Holding and operative rule:** The challenged previability minor-notice condition may be enjoined in its compulsory operation because the demonstrated defect is indivisible within that condition and the law supplies no usable independent maturity or best-interests route. Voluntary notice by other families does not cure that compulsion. Declaratory and injunctive relief must be conformed to the adjudicated minor violation, with independently lawful requirements and unreviewed rulings preserved.

**Authority:** Part IV of the same Opinion of the Court, joined by the same six Justices.

**Controlling explanation:** A nominal decree that mature minors are exempt would leave unanswered how a minor obtains that protection before disclosure and delay defeat it. The enacted condition supplies no independent route that a court can assume already exists. Retaining enforcement on that assumption would invent the missing safeguard. The broader protection of minors subject to this condition therefore rests on its indivisible compulsory structure, demonstrated interference and inadequate justification, not on one injured person or the premise that every family objects to consultation.

Casey permits relief against such a proved condition while preserving independent lawful operation. Dalton requires a decree corresponding to the violation, and does not impose a new facial formula or forbid adequate relief against an indivisible defect. Here its limit requires excluding an adjudication of adult incapacity and unreviewed postviability applications. It does not require leaving the established minor violation without an effective remedy.

The receiving courts must preserve the notice injunction at the stated scope, protect against dependencies resting solely on breach of that invalid condition, and conform any excessive declaratory or injunctive language. They must not reopen the separate information and penalty rulings or automatically revive enforcement those rulings independently prohibit. No new report-field or general scienter ruling is made. The State remains free to abandon the condition or pursue an adequate independent route through lawful means; the Court does not write that route into state law. Ordinary voluntary consultation remains possible. The remand implements these conclusions and requires no new merits trial or newly assigned proof burden.

**Precedent treatment:** Casey's tailored-relief qualification and Dalton's preservation of severable nonconflicting operation are applied together. Neither is narrowed, and no earlier precedent is overruled.

## Precedent Treatment

Matheson retains its limited immature-dependent-minor holding. Bellotti's mature and best-interests authorization principle informs the actual alternative; Hodgson's treatment of family circumstances and bypass operation is applied with its two-parent setting kept distinct. Ohio v. Akron Center retains its holding sustaining an available bypass and its reservation concerning necessity. Salerno supplies no independent facial barrier displacing the governing abortion standard here. Dalton, March 18, 1996, controls the bounded remedial inquiry without turning its Medicaid disposition into a parental-notice holding. No precedent is overruled or treated as authorizing a general prohibition on parental communication.

## Separate Writings

**Justice Scalia, dissenting, joined by Justice Thomas.** They would reverse the facial invalidation of the minor-notice condition and conform relief accordingly, leaving the unrelated rulings untouched. They retain their constitutional objection to Casey's heightened abortion framework and regard the actual one-parent notice duty, finite interval and emergency and abuse accommodations as reasonable means of protecting minors and parental responsibility. The abuse definition reaches threatened harm, and its confidentiality duty is a real statutory protection. In their view, the Federal Constitution does not require the additional maturity or best-interests mechanism the Court demands.

They recognize the Court's practical-obstruction evidence but reject the constitutional premise that places these supported notice burdens within the majority's heightened sequence. Approval of Pennsylvania's available bypass establishes its permissibility, not a general duty to enact one elsewhere. Their disagreement is with the governing constitutional rule and its justification burden; it does not authorize lower courts to disregard Casey or depend on an independent numerical facial bar. They do not decide every hypothetical emergency or wrongful-enforcement claim.

**Justice Kennedy, dissenting.** He would reverse this facial minor-notice invalidation and conform the decree to that result. He retains protected choice and the prohibition on effective control expressed in Casey, but rejects the general residual lesser-burden allocation that requires the State to answer the Court's independent-authorization alternative here. A parent's responsibility for a minor differs from a spouse's control over another adult. The actual law requires notice rather than consent and contains emergency, threatened-harm and confidentiality protections with more reach than a completed-assault-only description suggests.

The honor-student episode and the reporting and disclosure concerns are material. In his assessment, the supplied incidents and predictions do not establish that this entire notice structure is an effective veto or that all mature minors must be exempted. He would preserve relief for a separately established unprotected instance of practical prevention or denial of necessary care, with an identified application and effective remedy. That reservation does not lead him to join the facial injunction. He does not treat Salerno as displacing the Court's controlling method, accept all parental obstruction, or join Scalia's rejection of the underlying constitutional protection.

## Event

Cooper v. Oklahoma, No. 95-5207. Argued January 17, 1996; decided April 16, 1996. October Term 1995. On review under 28 U.S.C. §1257 of the Oklahoma Court of Criminal Appeals' January 10, 1995 judgment, 889 P.2d 293, No. F-92-533; rehearing was denied February 13, 1995.

The question is whether due process permits Oklahoma to require a criminal defendant to prove present trial incompetence by clear and convincing evidence. The court below affirmed Cooper's first-degree murder conviction and death sentence. The challenged competence ruling concerns his ability to participate at trial, not his sanity when the offense occurred or the truth of the murder charge.

Oklahoma Statutes title 22, §1175.4(B) requires the competency hearing to determine incompetence by clear and convincing evidence and presumes competence for burden allocation. The state court construes the rule to place that ultimate persuasion burden on the defendant. Sections 1175.1–1175.6 distinguish the present ability to understand charges and proceedings and rationally assist the defense; sufficient doubt initiating examination; the final competency hearing; and the separate treatment and restoration process. The defendant, counsel, prosecutor or court may initiate inquiry. Final-hearing opportunities include presence, cross-examination and a jury demand. The issue is the ultimate proof quantum after inquiry, not whether sufficient doubt initially required a hearing.

A psychologist found Cooper incompetent in 1990. Later examination and hearing evidence conflicted concerning improvement, continuing illness and malingering, and the judge found competence. Before the May 1992 trial, renewed concerns led to another hearing. Dr. Murphy diagnosed a major affective disorder preventing rational assistance, while acknowledging that manipulation could coexist with illness. Inmates described abnormal behavior; jailers reported ordinary communication. The State also relied on apparent understanding and Cooper's civil filings. The judge expressly concluded that Cooper had not established incompetence by clear and convincing evidence and directed trial. Further unusual conduct and renewed objections produced no determination under a different proof quantum. The appellate court upheld both the legal burden and findings assessed under it.

## Participation

Chief Justice Alex-Lamar Stone-Zsela and Justices Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer participate at argument and decision. All nine participate; no Justice is recused or otherwise nonparticipating.

## Public Action

The ruling approving clear-and-convincing defendant persuasion is reversed, the appellate affirmance is vacated insofar as the conviction and death sentence depend on that competency adjudication, and the case is remanded, 9–0.

## Judgment & Remedy

**Supporting:** Chief Justice Stone-Zsela and Justices Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer. The judgment is unanimous on the invalidity of the higher burden and the conditional remand.

The existing burden-dependent appellate determination cannot preserve the conviction and death sentence. The receiving court must assess whether a reliable retrospective determination of Cooper's competence at the relevant trial period is possible, considering the passage of time and available evidence. If reliable evaluation is possible, an appropriate factfinder may determine historical competence under the proper functional standard and a constitutionally permissible allocation, including defendant-preponderance persuasion. A reliable determination sustaining competence may permit the conviction and dependent sentence to be preserved through lawful further proceedings.

If reliable retrospective evaluation is unavailable, or a valid evaluation establishes incompetence at trial, the conviction and dependent sentence must be set aside. Any new trial may occur only when Cooper is competent. The State may elect lawful retrial instead of attempting to preserve the old judgment through retrospective evaluation.

This decision does not itself vacate the trial judgment unconditionally or order an immediate new trial. It orders no acquittal, automatic release or commitment, treatment, execution, stay, deadline or finding of current competence. Other claims retain their lawful procedural posture.

## Opinion Topology

Justice Kennedy delivers the Opinion of the Court, Parts I–III, joined in full by Chief Justice Stone-Zsela and Justices Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer. All nine directly adopt each controlling proposition and explanation. No Justice concurs only in the judgment, joins only part, or files a separate opinion.

## Holdings

### I. Clear-and-convincing defendant persuasion inadequately protects against an incompetent trial

**Controlling proposition:** Due process forbids requiring a criminal defendant to establish trial incompetence by clear and convincing evidence. The rule permits trial although the evidentiary balance favors incapacity to understand the proceedings or rationally assist counsel. Medina's permission to assign the defendant a preponderance burden at an adequate hearing remains intact, as does a competence presumption that merely states that allocation without adding evidentiary weight.

**Authority:** Part I of Justice Kennedy's Opinion of the Court, joined by all nine participating Justices identified in Opinion Topology.

**Controlling explanation:** The longstanding prohibition on trying an incompetent person protects the accused's ability to understand the proceeding and participate rationally in the defense. It does not eliminate all factfinding error or dictate every hearing procedure. Medina permits a defendant-preponderance allocation: when the evidence is evenly balanced, the defendant loses. Oklahoma goes materially further. Its higher quantum permits trial even after the greater weight of evidence supports incompetence. The additional risk falls on the person whose capacity to help establish incapacity is itself in question.

Counsel, psychiatric assistance, repeated examinations and renewed inquiry improve the evidence. They do not correct the allocation that governs each ultimate finding. Timely prosecution and the danger of feigning are legitimate concerns, but neither justifies this added risk. Ordinary examination and credibility assessment remain available, and lawful prosecution may proceed after adequate determination of competence. A mistaken incompetence finding can cause delay and raise separate treatment or confinement concerns; those consequences do not make an incompetent trial permissible or dispense with the independent legal predicates for treatment and confinement.

The burden's direction distinguishes Addington and other rules requiring stronger proof before imposing a deprivation. Increasing an accused's burden to avoid an incompetent trial serves the opposite function. Competence is not an offense element, and the Court does not import Winship's proof requirement or an insanity-defense allocation. The fundamental competence protection, Medina's actual limit and this rule's operation resolve the case without an exclusive due-process methodology, an empirical error rate or a universal historical count. Medina neither settled the higher quantum nor categorically excluded practical-fairness analysis.

**Precedent treatment:** Dusky, Pate and Drope supply the protected capacity and required inquiry; Medina's preponderance and allocation-only presumption holdings are preserved. Its divided broader methodology is not converted into a majority rule. No precedent is overruled.

### II. The existing findings do not establish competence under a lawful burden

**Controlling proposition:** Cooper's competency adjudication applied the unconstitutional burden, and the appellate affirmance rested on that rule. Those findings cannot alone sustain the challenged conviction and sentence. Neither conflicting evidence nor appellate sufficiency under the invalid standard establishes the result that a proper factfinder would reach under a permissible allocation.

**Authority:** Part II of the same unanimous Opinion of the Court.

**Controlling explanation:** The defect is established by the trial judge's express use of clear-and-convincing persuasion. It does not depend on this Court diagnosing Cooper or deciding that he was probably incompetent. The evidence presented both serious impairment and behavior the State viewed as understanding or feigning. Illness and manipulation can coexist; a diagnosis does not necessarily establish legal incompetence, and factual understanding alone does not establish rational assistance to counsel. These are matters for an adequate determination under the correct standard.

The appellate court regarded the defense evidence at the second hearing as stronger but held that the State's lay account allowed the result under Oklahoma's burden. That ruling supplies no independent finding under preponderance. Repetition of the same invalid standard also supplies no cure. Conversely, the judge's remarks about Cooper's ability to help counsel need not become a new, separately adjudicated substantive-competence claim to establish the presented burden error.

The Court therefore neither chooses an expert nor infers that the evidence was exactly balanced. It resolves the preserved ultimate-quantum issue, leaving historical competence to the proceedings required on remand. The abandoned preliminary-hearing characterization and distinct jury-waiver challenge do not displace that issue. No general harmless-error standard or structural-error classification is adopted, and sufficiency under the wrong burden is not itself a determination that the error made no difference.

**Precedent treatment:** Godinez preserves Dusky's functional capacity requirement and necessary renewed inquiry; it does not make earlier findings conclusive. Riggins's attention to supported trial impairment is consistent with this established adjudicative defect, without importing a medication claim or its particular remedy. Tuggle supplies no new universal error standard.

### III. Reliable retrospective adjudication must precede preservation of the challenged judgment

**Controlling proposition:** The case must return for assessment of whether trial-period competence can be determined reliably. If so, a proper factfinder may conduct that inquiry under a lawful allocation. If reliable retrospective adjudication is unavailable or establishes trial incompetence, the conviction and dependent sentence must be set aside; any retrial requires competence. The State may instead elect lawful retrial.

**Authority:** Part III of the same unanimous Opinion of the Court.

**Controlling explanation:** A retrospective hearing is not adequate merely because it is called a competency hearing. Passage of time, the nature of the disputed condition and the available contemporaneous evidence must permit a meaningful answer about capacity at the relevant trial period. Pate and Drope warn against assuming that past participation can always be reconstructed. The reports, hearings and testimony here nevertheless make feasibility a question to examine, rather than one this Court can resolve from their mere existence.

Petitioner requests a new trial. That remedy follows if reliable evaluation cannot be made or establishes incompetence, but ordering it now would decide an unestablished remedial predicate. The limited remand performs the necessary inquiry while preventing the old findings under the invalid burden from preserving the judgment automatically. A reliable determination of competence may permit preservation through lawful further proceedings; inability to make that determination requires setting aside the conviction and dependent sentence.

The Court prescribes no numerical proof burden for feasibility, mandatory universal hearing format or present medical finding. A lawful competency allocation may place preponderance persuasion on the defendant, consistent with Medina. The common functional standard remains unchanged. Treatment, restoration and periodic reassessment retain their separate legal requirements, and a finding of past incapacity does not establish permanent incapacity. Any retrial must await competence. The remand authorizes no acquittal, automatic release, compulsory treatment or new execution determination.

**Precedent treatment:** Pate, Drope and Godinez preserve adequate inquiry and the need to test retrospective reliability. Riggins's response to a supported unreconstructable trial risk does not establish that reconstruction is impossible here. No mandatory retrial rule or general harmlessness test is added.

## Precedent Treatment

Medina, June 22, 1992, remains controlling on defendant-preponderance persuasion and an equivalent competence presumption at an adequate hearing with counsel and psychiatric evidence. The present holding decides the higher quantum that Medina left open. Dusky's functional definition and Pate's and Drope's sufficient-doubt inquiry remain unchanged. Godinez's distinction between competence and an actual knowing, intelligent and voluntary waiver remains intact; no waiver claim is decided here.

Addington, Jones and A. St. P. C. address different deprivations and burden directions; they do not authorize shifting this additional risk to the accused. Leland and Patterson do not equate trial capacity with an insanity defense. Winship's offense-element rule is neither applied to competence nor narrowed. Ohio v. Akron Center's bypass procedure and Miller's parental-notice holding supply no criminal-competence quantum or procedure to transplant. Riggins and Tuggle retain their respective medication and psychiatric-assistance holdings without determining an automatic new-trial or universal error rule here. No precedent is overruled.

## Law After Decision

Effective April 16, 1996, Oklahoma may not require clear-and-convincing defendant proof of trial incompetence. The lawful defendant-preponderance allocation remains available with adequate inquiry and assistance. The substantive capacity standard, sufficient-doubt trigger and distinction between competence, insanity, guilt and waiver remain unchanged.

The new rule rejects this heightened quantum because it permits trial when the evidentiary balance favors incompetence. It does not require prosecution persuasion, a maximally defendant-favorable process or a single exclusive due-process method. Its protection is not limited to capital trials. The Court does not decide competence for execution, an independent medication or jury-waiver claim, the merits of a new commitment, or any unrelated guilt or penalty issue.

A conviction resting on the invalid competency determination cannot be preserved solely by those old findings. In this case, reliable retrospective evaluation under a lawful allocation may permit preservation; unavailable evaluation or established trial incompetence requires setting aside the conviction and dependent sentence, with retrial only upon competence. No conclusion concerning present capacity or reconstruction's feasibility follows from this decision alone.

## Event

Holly Farms Corp. v. NLRB, 517 U.S. 392, No. 95-210. Argued February 21, 1996; decided April 23, 1996; October Term 1995. On certiorari under 28 U.S.C. §1254(1) to the Fourth Circuit, which enforced the Board's order in 48 F.3d 1360 (1995). The question is whether chicken catchers, forklift operators and live-haul truckdrivers are agricultural laborers excluded from NLRA employee coverage.

Independent contract growers furnish facilities and raise Holly Farms' birds to marketable size; Holly retains ownership. Holly's crews catch and coop the birds, load them by forklift and haul them to its processing plant. Catching schedules follow processing shifts; the crews also handle trailers and holding arrangements before plant receivers take over. The Board included the workers in the unit, found unfair labor practices and imposed a bargaining order based on an earlier authorization-card majority and conditions preventing a fair rerun after a 95–95 election. The agricultural classification is the granted issue; Tyson's separate successorship dispute and unrelated violations are not independently before the Court.

## Holdings

### I. The unpreserved primary-harvesting theory is not considered

**Controlling proposition:** Under 29 U.S.C. §160(e), a court may not consider an objection not urged before the Board, its member, agent or agency unless extraordinary circumstances excuse the failure or neglect. Petitioners did not present their primary-agriculture harvesting objection to the Board, and no extraordinary circumstance excuses that omission; the Court therefore declines that objection while deciding the preserved secondary-agriculture question.

**Authority:** Part I of Ginsburg's Opinion of the Court, joined by all seven other Associate Justices. Stone-Zsela agrees with the disposition only.

**Controlling explanation:** Primary agriculture and secondary agriculture are distinct statutory routes. The earlier presentation invoked secondary agriculture; a new harvesting characterization would avoid that route's actor-or-place and farming-relationship conditions. Raising the general agricultural exclusion therefore did not preserve this separate objection. The Board identifies the omission before the agency, and the appellate and petition presentation does not supply the missing objection. Woelke & Romero applies §160(e)'s requirement that the agency receive the objection before judicial review, subject to the enacted extraordinary-circumstances exception. No such circumstance is established. The lower court's description of a concession need not be adopted: failure to raise this theory suffices. The Court neither decides that poultry catching can never be primary agriculture nor treats the preservation ruling as defeating the properly presented secondary claim.

### II. Farm location and the relation to farming are separate requirements

**Controlling proposition:** Secondary agriculture requires a practice performed by a farmer or on a farm and incident to or in conjunction with the relevant farming operations. A nonfarmer's employees may qualify through the on-farm alternative, but location alone is insufficient; preparation for market and delivery to storage, market or carriers remain subject to both requirements. Courts independently identify these statutory boundaries before testing an unresolved agency construction for permissibility.

**Authority:** Part II of the unanimous Opinion of the Court; all nine Justices directly adopt these predicates without agreeing on every application.

**Controlling explanation:** Congress supplied alternative actor and place gateways and a separate relationship to the farming operations. Making farmer employment indispensable would erase the on-farm alternative. Treating every activity occurring there as agricultural would erase the relationship requirement. Farmers Reservoir distinguishes farming from a separate business that serves agriculture; economic connection alone does not settle the statutory relationship. Chevron requires independent examination of the enacted limits, and Town & Country preserves express NLRA exclusions while permitting reasonable classification within the range the statute leaves. MCI likewise forbids an agency to remove a statutory condition through interpretation.

The definition does not make a processor's ownership of poultry conclusive, classify every employee in an integrated business identically or exclude every independent on-farm contractor. Practices may serve farming and processing purposes simultaneously; exclusive service to farming is not an added statutory condition. The relation of the actual work to the relevant farming operation must still be assessed. The Court's disagreement about catchers and loaders concerns that application, not whether Congress enacted both requirements. This holding creates no general occupational exemption or new evidentiary burden.

### III. The Board lawfully includes these live-haul drivers

**Controlling proposition:** The Board's classification of these substantially off-farm live-haul drivers as covered employees is lawful under the incorporated agricultural definition and this record. Their hauling for Holly's separate processing operation is not made agricultural merely because the birds were raised on farms, Holly continuously owned them or the journey begins there.

**Authority:** Part III of the unanimous Opinion of the Court; all nine directly adopt the stated application and limits.

**Controlling explanation:** The independent growers actually raise the birds. Holly's ownership and retaking possession for transport to processing do not themselves establish that Holly resumes poultry husbandry or that these drivers perform it. Bayside rejects attributing independent growers' farming to an integrated processor merely through ownership and control, and permits attention to the particular operation the employees serve. Its by-farmer analysis supports the Board's treatment of this hauling work.

The drivers' substantially off-farm transportation cannot satisfy the on-farm alternative merely by starting at a grower's premises. Nor does the express reference to delivery dispense with the gateway and farming-relationship requirements. The supported schedules, supervision and plant-side handling connect these drivers to the separate removal and processing operation. The Board's classification respects the statutory bounds and is sustained; the Court does not hold that the contrary classification on identical facts would also be permissible. No rule for every driver or mixed-duty employee follows, and the result does not depend on holding that a processing plant can never be a market. Bayside does not itself resolve the different on-farm question presented by the catchers and loaders.

### IV. The Board permissibly includes these catchers and forklift operators

**Controlling proposition:** The definition does not unambiguously exempt these processor-employed catchers and forklift operators. Although they work on farms, the Board reasonably classifies their catching, cooping and loading as part of a distinct removal operation serving processing, rather than as incident to or in conjunction with the growers' farming, on its actual and substantially supported integration rationale. That permissible classification sustains their coverage and affirmance; it is not a compulsory classification of every poultry arrangement.

**Authority:** Part IV of Ginsburg's Opinion of the Court, joined by Stone-Zsela, Stevens, Kennedy, Souter and Breyer; six Justices directly adopt this bounded permissibility holding.

**Controlling explanation:** Catchers coop the birds and forklift operators load them. Each works on a farm, so each satisfies that gateway. But the independent growers have completed raising the birds to marketable size; Holly separately organizes, supervises and times removal to supply its plant. Catching schedules follow processing shifts, and live-haul handling continues until plant receivers take over. These facts support the Board's stated functional-integration ground and its permissible conclusion about the separate farming relationship. Substantial evidentiary support for those facts remains distinct from judicial examination of statutory meaning.

Petitioners plausibly describe catching, cooping and loading as completing farm production and preparing poultry for market. The Labor Department's poultry-preparation examples support that description, but retain the surrounding secondary-agriculture conditions. The examples therefore do not compel the conclusion that this independently organized removal operation belongs to the growers' farming. An independent contractor can perform secondary agriculture, and simultaneous service to farming and processing is possible. Neither concession makes the Board's actual classification unreasonable here.

Farmers Reservoir permits examination of a separately organized operation. Bayside rejects attribution through poultry ownership but leaves the on-farm route open. Health Care and Town & Country support bounded functional classification after each statutory predicate receives independent effect; neither authorizes a new exclusion or substitute agency rationale. The Regional Director supplied both the farmer-status discussion and the actual integration ground. A remand premised on the absence of that ground would disregard the decision made.

The Court does not classify identical tasks performed by a farmer's own employees as nonagricultural, treat employer identity as conclusive, require an exclusive processing purpose or declare every post-growing activity nonagricultural. The Board remains confined to the incorporated definition. No broader classification or new procedure is necessary to affirm this order.

## Event

Markman v. Westview Instruments, Inc., No. 95-26. Argued January 8, 1996; decided April 23, 1996. October Term 1995. On review under 28 U.S.C. §1254(1) of the Federal Circuit's April 5, 1995 en banc judgment, 52 F.3d 967, No. 92-1049, affirming the Eastern District of Pennsylvania's judgment of noninfringement.

The question is whether the Seventh Amendment assigns construction of patent claims, including technical meaning informed by evidence, to the court or jury in a patent infringement action seeking damages. Herbert Markman owns Reissue Patent No. 33,054, and Positek holds its drycleaning license. They sued Westview, maker of the DATAMARK/DATASCAN system, and Althon, a user, under 35 U.S.C. §281, seeking damages under §284 among other relief. Infringement was tried separately from validity and damages.

Claim 1 describes a drycleaning inventory system with a data-input device, processor and memory maintaining an inventory total, transaction-associated indicia, a bar-code printer and at least one scanner. Its concluding function is to detect and localize spurious additions to and deletions from inventory. Claim 10 adds an alphanumeric keyboard. The claim, specification and prosecution explanation connect this function to articles entering and leaving the system. The prosecution amendment added the detection/localization language to overcome an obviousness rejection and explained input and output articles.

The accused system temporarily uses clothing descriptions to print tickets and then retains invoice numbers, dates and cash totals. The parties dispute whether the patented inventory may consist only of cash or invoices. They agree that the accused system does not infringe if the claimed inventory includes clothing. A jury instructed on claim meaning found infringement of claims 1 and 10, but not claim 14. The judge had reserved respondents' directed-verdict motion and entered judgment as a matter of law on October 1, 1991, holding that the claim requires clothing inventory. The Federal Circuit affirmed.

## Holdings

### I. A patent damages action's jury right preserves factual infringement work while the court states the claim's legal meaning

**Controlling proposition:** A patent infringement action seeking legal damages carries the Seventh Amendment jury right. The court determines the legally operative meaning of the public patent claim; the jury retains disputed infringement facts, external product identity and other properly factual work under that construction. An action's jury status does not make every subsidiary task a jury function, and technical subject matter alone does not remove otherwise protected facts from the jury.

**Authority:** Part I of Justice Souter's Opinion of the Court, joined by all nine participating Justices identified in Opinion Topology.

**Controlling explanation:** The Amendment preserves jury trial in common-law suits exceeding twenty dollars and restricts reexamination of jury-tried facts to the rules of the common law. Patent infringement for damages is a legal action. That identifies the action's constitutional protection, but the allocation question still requires examining the particular function performed.

A claim defines the public legal boundary of the patent. Judicial construction reads its words with the specification and prosecution history, rather than allowing a witness's desired coverage to determine the grant. Section 112's written-description and particular-and-distinct-claim requirements reinforce that public function; they do not decide every judge-jury question. Historical instrument-construction principles support the court's responsibility for legal meaning. Consistent scope across cases corroborates that function, but neither convenience nor technological complexity could override an established jury entitlement.

The distinction leaves substantial factual work to the jury. Determining what an accused product does, or whether external inventions are identical, differs from expounding the patent's legal boundary. Bischoff preserves that distinction, and Winans separates construction from factual novelty while allowing technical explanation. Tull shows that a legal action need not assign every issue identically; Terry and Wooddell preserve the actual historical and remedial inquiry. The rule is therefore neither that everything involving a patent is legal nor that every task in a damages action must be given to the jury. Part II addresses the narrower disagreement about evidentiary disputes within the assigned construction function.

**Precedent treatment:** Tull, Terry and Wooddell are applied to the action/issue distinction at their respective scopes. Bischoff and Winans preserve judicial construction and distinct external factual inquiries. No precedent is overruled or general allocation for other instruments decided.

### II. Claim construction remains the court's task when technical meaning or credibility within that task is genuinely disputed

**Controlling proposition:** The court construes patent claims, including terms of art informed by extrinsic technical evidence. The Seventh Amendment does not require jury resolution of a genuine technical-usage or expert-credibility dispute embedded in determining claim meaning. The judge evaluates that evidence against the claim, specification, prosecution history and coherence of the whole instrument. This trial allocation does not declare every underlying evidentiary proposition intrinsically legal or determine the appellate standard for all subsidiary findings.

**Authority:** Part II of Justice Souter's Opinion of the Court, joined by Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer: eight Justices. Chief Justice Stone-Zsela does not join this Part.

**Controlling explanation:** Petitioners correctly observe that technical explanation can involve real evidentiary disagreement. Merely calling the ultimate question construction would not answer their objection. The relevant inquiry, however, is whether resolving that disagreement must belong to the jury to preserve the substance of the historical right when the evidence performs the patent-interpretation task.

The cited historical qualifications do not establish such an indispensable allocation. Silsby's components of a combination and Bischoff's identity of external inventions concern application rather than construction strictly understood. Heald's and Singer's treatment of extrinsic explanation in comparisons likewise does not make every evidentiary input into claim meaning a jury question. Winans permits experts to explain the art without allowing their legal opinions to determine construction.

Technical meaning must be assessed for its fit with the instrument's internal structure and the grant's operative scope. The judge can perform that integrated interpretive task even when explanations compete or credibility matters. The evidence's factual underpinnings do not require dividing responsibility for that meaning determination. The patent's public and consistent scope supports the allocation alongside the historical construction/application distinction; uniformity alone would not justify it.

Useful technical evidence is therefore not categorically excluded, and distinct infringement facts remain for their proper factfinder. Nor does trial responsibility settle appellate treatment: assigning construction to a judge does not establish that every subsidiary observation must be reviewed anew. Gaudin's criminal-element holding and Thompson's habeas-review distinction concern different tasks and supply no contrary Seventh Amendment allocation here. The Court decides neither a universal construction-hearing procedure nor the allocation for every other type of instrument.

**Precedent treatment:** The historical patent cases are applied at their construction/application boundaries. Gaudin and Thompson are distinguished without altering their element, factual-protection or review rules. No universal de novo subsidiary-review rule is adopted.

### III. This patent requires clothing inventory, and the infringement verdict lacks a sufficient basis even with the technical proffer credited

**Controlling proposition:** This claim, specification and prosecution history require clothing as part of the inventory. Even accepting petitioners' supported technical evidence favorably and assuming their proposed jury allocation, no reasonable cash-only reading performs the claim's article-related detection and localization function. Ordinary judgment-as-a-matter-of-law principles independently support noninfringement because the parties concede that the accused system does not infringe under a clothing-inclusive construction.

**Authority:** Part III of Justice Souter's Opinion of the Court, joined by all nine participating Justices. This record-specific ground independently supports affirmance and does not narrow Part II's separately controlling allocation rule.

**Controlling explanation:** The conclusion rests on the actual instrument and evidence, not merely the lower court's classification of construction as law. Claim 1 integrates an inventory total with detecting and localizing spurious additions and deletions. The specification's article-related operation and the prosecution explanation of input and output articles establish a clothing component that cash totals alone cannot perform.

The Court credits petitioners' proffer at its supported significance. Industry participants may use inventory for cash or invoices, and Westview's advertising and president's testimony may reflect that usage. Such general usage does not erase this claim's integrated article requirement. The inventor's and patent lawyer's technical explanations are considered on their subjects; neither a witness's occupation nor interest makes the evidence automatically irrelevant. But subjective intended coverage and a legal opinion cannot expand the public instrument. Even the proposed broad usage leaves its article-related function intact.

The requirement of at least one scanner does not equate clothing inventory with cash totals. Batch or transaction processing need not reproduce every detail of the preferred embodiment, and the Court prescribes no particular scanning count, permanent item-by-item architecture or universal tracking technology. Those points still do not remove the clothing component or defeat the actual noninfringement concession.

Thus the result requires neither disbelieving a witness nor choosing between supported historical accounts. A jury right remains compatible with ordinary supported JMOL, and the reserved directed-verdict motion places that alternative before the Court. The competing request for remand identifies no material unresolved fact that could supply the missing permissible cash-only reading. This is not a general rule that any intrinsic evidence defeats any expert account.

**Precedent treatment:** Judicial construction of the public instrument and ordinary civil sufficiency operate together. The historically qualified treatment of technical evidence is respected, without converting this record into a universal evidentiary rule. No validity, damages or general §112 paragraph 6 question is decided.

The Court applies Seventh Amendment protection to the legal damages action while identifying the distinct construction function. Silsby, Bischoff, Heald, Singer and Winans preserve their distinctions among construing an instrument, technical explanation, comparison of external inventions and infringement. They do not establish that every dispute contributing to patent meaning must go to a jury.

Gaudin, June 19, 1995, preserves jury determination of criminal materiality and judicial statement of legal meaning; its criminal burden is not imported. ## Law After Decision

Eight Justices directly support that scope. The presence of extrinsic evidence does not transfer construction to the jury, but technical evidence remains available to inform the instrument's meaning. The jury retains disputed infringement, product-operation and other factual questions outside construction under the legal meaning supplied by the court.

The Court does not decide the appellate standard for every subsidiary finding, impose universal de novo factual review, mandate a separate pretrial construction hearing, exclude all extrinsic evidence or resolve all means-plus-function questions. Other documentary settings and broader law/fact classifications remain open. This case also establishes the independently supported sufficiency application to claims 1 and 10 and the parties' actual concession; it creates no presumption of noninfringement or authority to disbelieve a witness on JMOL.

Chief Justice Stone-Zsela's separate genuine-history and credibility limitation is noncontrolling insofar as it would require a jury to resolve such disputes within claim construction. His full joins to Parts I and III do not qualify the eight-Justice rule in Part II.

These seven adopt each controlling proposition and explanation. Justice Stevens dissents, joined throughout by Justice Kennedy. ## Holdings

### I. Rules 29 and 45 do not authorize this new, untimely postverdict acquittal

**Controlling proposition:** With the Government's timely objection preserved, the district court lacked authority to grant Carlisle's new postverdict motion outside Rule 29(c)'s period. Rule 45(b) excludes ordinary excusable-neglect enlargement beyond Rule 29's own conditions. This is a limitation on the requested motion remedy, not a loss of federal criminal subject-matter jurisdiction.

**Authority:** Part I of Justice Scalia's Opinion of the Court, joined by the seven Justices identified in Opinion Topology.

**Controlling explanation:** The applicable Rule 29(c) permits a motion within seven days after jury discharge or within further time fixed during that period. It authorizes setting aside a guilty verdict on such a motion and requires no earlier trial motion. Rule 29(a) requires acquittal, on a defendant's or the court's motion after the evidence closes, if the evidence cannot sustain conviction. Rule 29(b) permits reserving a timely trial motion and deciding it later on the evidence when reserved. These provisions supply different authorized paths; none was followed here.

Rule 45(b)'s general enlargement power expressly excepts action under Rules 29, 33, 34 and 35 except to the extent and under the conditions those Rules state. A one-day delay, attorney error or absence of Government prejudice therefore cannot itself supply power to extend this period.

The strongest contrary reading relies on Rule 29(a)'s lack of an express terminal phrase and the judge's responsibility not to enter judgment on insufficient evidence. But reading that provision to authorize the identical new acquittal without the postverdict conditions would make those conditions ineffective. The Court relies on the integrated text, not a caption alone. Sentencing supplies no substitute deadline in the Rule.

The decision leaves open a court-initiated action within the permitted period, reconsideration of a timely motion, valid reservation and Government forfeiture of its objection. Neither an earlier denial nor the judge's later changed evidentiary appraisal created a timely motion or reservation here. Enforcing this vehicle's conditions does not decide that the trial evidence was sufficient.

**Precedent treatment:** Smith's rejection of an unrestricted judicial substitute for a restricted new-trial motion supports the structural reasoning, although its postappeal posture differs. Pioneer permits excusable-neglect flexibility only where authorized and preserves specific enlargement restrictions. Neither case supplies a general extension here.

### II. General rules and inherent authority cannot supply the same act that the specific Rules foreclose

**Controlling proposition:** Rule 2, Rule 57 and inherent supervisory authority do not authorize this otherwise prohibited postverdict acquittal. The asserted separate pre-sentence power cannot circumvent Rules 29(c) and 45(b) by changing the label attached to the same relief. The holding does not abolish inherent judicial authority generally.

**Authority:** Part II of the same seven-Justice Opinion of the Court.

**Controlling explanation:** Rule 2 directs just determination, simplicity, fairness and elimination of unjustifiable expense and delay. It guides interpretation; it does not displace a specific restriction. Former Rule 57 allowed consistent practice where the Rules made no provision. This case presents a governing provision, not a procedural gap.

The December 1, 1995 amendment retains consistency with federal law and national and local rules. Its protection against nonwillful violations of local form requirements does not excuse a national Rule 29 deadline. Its actual-notice protection concerns requirements outside federal law, federal rules and local rules. The amendment governs pending proceedings only insofar as just and practicable; the Court neither applies it automatically to the 1993 conduct nor derives an enlargement power from it.

The dissent's distinct retained-power argument is substantial: a trial judge traditionally protects against legally insufficient convictions while the case remains before the court. But the asserted authority here would deliver precisely the relief the specific postverdict mechanism restricts. Bank of Nova Scotia requires supervision to remain consistent with governing rules. Williams's bounded supervision of a materially distorted grand-jury presentation survives; its prejudice-based, rule-consistent remedy does not authorize this conflicting exercise.

Fallen likewise does not supply an attorney-error exception. There the ill, effectively unrepresented prisoner did all reasonably possible to transmit a timely notice. That treatment of timely efforts differs from excusing counsel's admitted late motion. Existing inherent powers are not denied, and hypothetical Article III limits on withdrawing them are not presented. The Court decides the claimed power in this rule structure, not every judicial correction before judgment.

**Precedent treatment:** Bank of Nova Scotia and Williams preserve bounded, rule-consistent supervision. Fallen is distinguished on its timely-effort premise. Lonchar preserves process genuinely required by law without an additional equitable substitute; it establishes no still-owed proceeding requiring this late motion.

### III. The alternative writ and due-process theories do not require this acquittal or a second trial

**Controlling proposition:** Neither the All Writs Act nor due process supplies authority for the untimely acquittal on these facts. The Government may obtain restoration of the existing verdict without a second factfinding trial. The ruling preserves, but does not adjudicate or guarantee, independently available review of properly presented claims.

**Authority:** Part III of the same seven-Justice Opinion of the Court.

**Controlling explanation:** Section 1651 permits necessary or appropriate writs in aid of jurisdiction consistent with the usages and principles of law. Morgan's extraordinary coram nobis remedy is not an alternative deadline for counsel's late motion in an ongoing criminal case. No independent exceptional predicate is established here.

Nor is this a case of a predeadline judicial assurance inducing lateness. The judge's later reconsideration supplies no such assurance. The Court need not decide every judicial-misinformation case to reject ordinary counsel error as that different circumstance; Harris Truck Lines and Thompson do not establish the missing predicate.

Jackson protects against conviction without legally sufficient evidence, but does not itself require this additional late district-court vehicle. Carlisle had the opportunities provided by the Rules; a missed motion is not automatically informed waiver of every other claim. Remaining appellate and collateral channels depend on their own authority, preservation, proof and procedural conditions. Plain-error review is not declared equivalent to ordinary sufficiency review, and no ineffective-assistance remedy is promised. AEDPA's new conditions and unresolved application questions must be respected, rather than describing collateral relief as unlimited.

Finally, Wilson permits the Government's appeal because reversal restores the jury's existing verdict without subjecting Carlisle to another trial. That consequence does not decide sufficiency, which the Sixth Circuit left unresolved. Reinstatement and sentencing have already occurred, so affirmance orders neither repetition nor release. The dissent's requested sufficiency-review remand follows from its different authority construction, not from a sufficiency determination made by this Court.

**Precedent treatment:** Morgan's extraordinary remedy is distinguished; Jackson's substantive protection and Wilson's no-second-trial rule remain intact. Olano's forfeiture/waiver distinction and conditioned correction survive, without converting appellate power into district-court enlargement or supplying a new collateral holding.

## Precedent Treatment

The Court applies the specific-rule limits while preserving Williams's bounded supervisory authority and Bank of Nova Scotia's consistency requirement. Smith's rule-structure reasoning is applied with its later procedural posture distinguished; Fallen's protection of timely efforts is not converted into ordinary excusable neglect. Pioneer, Olano and Libretti preserve the separate inquiries into authorized correction, procedural default, actual waiver and lawful punishment.

Lonchar and Cooper require meaningful performance of legally required process but establish no additional Rule 29 route. Markman's reserved civil motion and Holly Farms' distinct statutory exception do not change this criminal deadline. Morgan, Jackson and Wilson retain the scopes explained above. No precedent is overruled; Manypenny's reconsideration of a timely motion is not decided to govern this new late motion.

## Law After Decision

On these facts, Rules 29(c) and 45(b) bar the new late acquittal, and general practice rules, inherent supervision, coram nobis and due process do not supply the same relief. The rule is not a subject-matter-jurisdiction holding or a determination of guilt or evidentiary sufficiency. Timely motions and reservations, possible timely court action, Government forfeiture and materially different judicial-assurance circumstances remain outside the decision.

Any separately available federal collateral proceeding must account for AEDPA, enacted April 24. Section 2255 now contains a one-year period measured from the latest specified event: finality; removal of an unlawful governmental filing impediment; a newly recognized Supreme Court right made retroactive on collateral review; or discovery of supporting facts through due diligence. A second or successive motion requires appellate certification for qualifying newly discovered evidence sufficient, if proved and considered with the whole record, to establish clearly and convincingly that no reasonable factfinder would have found guilt, or for a previously unavailable new constitutional rule made retroactive by the Supreme Court. The amended appellate-certificate provisions require the specified substantial showing of denial of a constitutional right and identification of qualifying issues.

These are statutory conditions, not a determination that a particular later Carlisle proceeding satisfies or is governed by them. Chapter 154's pending-case clause does not resolve every other transition question. The June 1995 dismissal of an early §2255 motion as premature establishes no subsequent-motion classification, deadline, custody predicate or entitlement here. No new collateral action or remedy is ordered.

## Separate Writings

**Justice Stevens, dissenting, joined by Justice Kennedy.** They would reverse the no-authority ruling and remand for appellate consideration of the Government's disputed sufficiency ground. Their source of power is the court's retained pre-sentence judicial authority to prevent judgment on legally insufficient evidence, informed by the acquittal duty recognized in Rule 29(a). They do not treat that subdivision alone as an unrestricted postverdict motion remedy.

They accept that Rules 29(c) and 45(b) foreclose the defendant's late-motion entitlement, but do not read those provisions as withdrawing the judge's distinct authority while the case awaits sentence. Smith's completed-appellate setting makes its finality concern materially different. Manypenny's timely-motion holding does not itself decide this further question, but recognizes judicial correction within a continuing proceeding. Williams supplies an example of bounded supervision; it is not a license to disregard a controlling rule. On their reading, no rule withdraws the particular power asserted here.

The Court's strongest objection is that this construction allows the same relief the time limit restricts and substitutes sentencing for the specified deadline. The dissent answers that the source and function differ: a lost party motion does not compel entry of a judgment the judge concludes lacks legal evidentiary support. The position is confined to that pre-sentence responsibility, not a one-day, no-prejudice or general fairness exception, and it does not reopen completed criminal judgments indefinitely.

The dissent does not decide that the evidence was insufficient or that Carlisle was factually innocent. The Government contested the sufficiency ruling, and the Sixth Circuit did not resolve it. Acquittal therefore could not finally be restored without that review. The dissent orders neither a new trial nor release and does not rely on an unrestricted collateral remedy or automatic judicial-assurance exception.

## Event

Sheets v. Salt Lake County. Merits decision, April 29, 1996; October Term 1995. Review under 28 U.S.C. §1254(1) of the Tenth Circuit's January 10, 1995 judgment, 45 F.3d 1383, in Nos. 93-4128 and 93-4134. Those numbers identify the appeals below; no separate Supreme Court docket or oral-argument date is supplied. The question is whether officials violated Gary Sheets's own constitutional confidentiality interest by giving a private author access to intimate material entrusted for a murder investigation.

After Kathy Sheets died in a 1985 bombing, Gary supplied her diary to investigators. Farnsworth testified to a confidentiality assurance and its communication to investigators; Bell could not recall an assurance, and George's notice was disputed. The diary described Gary's intimate marital and personal life. After the investigation ended in 1987, the investigative file, including the diary, was archived and publicly inspectable.

Investigator Michael George allowed his friend, author Lindsey, to examine his file box; other authors received answers without comparable access. George had copied intimate entries and kept detailed notes, which he destroyed before Lindsey's book appeared in August 1988. The jury inferred that George disclosed the material and caused its publication. The County's unwritten practice permitted discussion of closed cases with the press subject to evenhanded treatment. The action seeks redress for Gary's own injury, not his wife's surviving personality, copyright or ownership alone.

## Participation

Chief Justice Alex-Lamar Stone-Zsela and Justices Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer participate at consideration and decision. No Justice is recused or otherwise nonparticipating.

## Public Action

The Court affirms the constitutional-confidentiality determination and the judgment within the reviewed question.

## Judgment & Remedy

**Judgment:** Affirmed, 7–2. **Supporting:** Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Kennedy, Souter, Ginsburg and Breyer. **Opposing:** Scalia and Thomas.

The existing $650,000 compensatory judgment against Salt Lake County and George and the distinct $77,896.93 attorney-fee judgment remain undisturbed. No punitive award is established or reviewed. The Court makes no new award and orders no retrial, damages reassessment or immunity proceeding.

There is no partial join, judgment-only concurrence or fractured controlling rationale.

## Holdings

### I. Unjustified official dissemination of this intimate material violates protected personal liberty

**Controlling proposition:** The Fourteenth Amendment protects a person's objectively intimate marital and family information against intentional official disclosure causing public dissemination when the information was entrusted for a limited criminal investigation and the disclosure lacks sufficient governmental justification on this record. A confidentiality assurance informs the objective expectation but does not alone create the federal right; consent to investigative use does not surrender the information to general publication.

**Authority:** Part I of Stevens's Opinion of the Court, joined by the seven Justices identified in Opinion Topology.

**Controlling explanation:** Whalen distinguishes avoiding disclosure of personal matters from autonomy in important decisions. It upheld safeguarded collection and reserved unwarranted dissemination. Nixon considered legitimate personal privacy independently of ownership while sustaining protected archival screening. Those decisions supply the foundation, but neither resolved this disclosure. The Court now protects this confined intimate interest; it does not attribute today's application to an already complete confidentiality test.

Gary asserts exposure of his own marriage and personal life. Kathy's authorship does not make her the only person affected. Paul rejected reputation injury from an official arrest record; this claim concerns intimate information, not unfavorable statements alone. Collins's refusal to turn state duties into a federal workplace guarantee likewise does not decide this affirmative disclosure. Neither a promise nor a breach of Utah's confidentiality rule would suffice without the independently protected interest.

The file became publicly inspectable after 1987. That fact weighs against an expectation of secrecy, but does not itself establish prior public dissemination of these passages, Gary's voluntary exposure or the lawfulness of George's distinct facilitation of their publication. The Court does not invalidate the archive-access regime. Access by legitimate investigators also differs from transferring intimate material for a private author's use.

Trial counsel invoked public interest in the sensational crime and its subjects, although no compelling disclosure interest was advanced on appeal. General interest in the crime does not establish a sufficient governmental connection to publishing these intimate passages. Legitimate investigation, lawful retention, public accountability and legally required disclosure remain distinct; none supplies the missing justification for this transfer on the supported record. The decision regulates the official conduct at issue, not the private author's speech. It creates no general right to deletion or inherited claim, and imposes no universal strict-scrutiny, least-intrusive-means or fixed balancing test for every personal, business or financial record.

### II. Independent legal review and supported trial findings establish this application

**Controlling proposition:** The Court independently determines constitutional coverage and legal sufficiency while reviewing the denial of Rule 50 relief under the ordinary standard favorable to the prevailing party's supported evidence and reasonable inferences. The objective instructions and supported findings establish the confined right's application here; the unobjected instruction requires intentional acts causing deprivation, not specific intent to the author's ultimate publication or knowledge that the acts violate the Constitution.

**Authority:** Part II of the same seven-Justice Opinion of the Court.

**Controlling explanation:** Constitutional protection does not arise whenever a plaintiff wishes to keep something private. The jury was instructed on intimate home, marriage and family information, the expectation of a reasonable person in Sheets's position and his preponderance burden. Those objective criteria and the supported intimate subject permit the Court to decide legal coverage without adopting a wholly subjective preference. Markman's patent-construction allocation does not transfer these historical facts and credibility choices to appellate judges, or surrender the governing constitutional rule to the jury.

Farnsworth's testimony supports an assurance and its communication; Bell's failure to recall it and disputed notice to George do not become contrary appellate findings. Lindsey identified possible official sources. Farnsworth denied disclosure; Cannon lacked the diary and detailed notes; George acknowledged detailed excerpts and access to his files. Only Lindsey's book quoted the diary. Taken favorably to the verdict, that circumstantial evidence supports intentional disclosure by George and resulting publication. A direct admission or eyewitness account was unnecessary. Excluded writings unconnected to these passages do not prove that the diary's intimate contents were already public.

The defendants did not object to the intent instruction. The Court neither adds a purpose to violate a known right nor requires a newly found specific desire for Lindsey's eventual publication. It also does not treat lawful investigative sharing itself as the deprivation. The protected interest and supported causal disclosure distinguish this case from confidential retention.

No additional necessary criterion adopted here lacks factual support. A new trial is therefore unnecessary merely because the Court declines to adopt the circuit's comprehensive compelling-interest and least-intrusive-disclosure formulation. The judgment stands within the reviewed question; independent municipal requirements, conduct-date immunity and unreviewed award issues remain distinct.

### I. This record does not establish material advancement of temperance by the price-information ban

Saffer's theory supplies a real argument: suppressing comparison raises search costs and price dispersion, higher average prices tend to discourage purchases, and opposing experts acknowledged price responsiveness and a theoretically reasonable prediction. Their concessions cannot be omitted.

They nevertheless do not establish the necessary effect of this restriction on this record. The District Court credited contrary testimony and found no significant consumption effect. Saffer's international research concerned general advertising bans, with acknowledged control problems, rather than this price-information rule. The record lacked estimates connecting the projected Rhode Island price change to consumption, and market-share or profit gains could occur without greater total consumption. Smart's acknowledgment of the mechanism accompanied his criticism of its missing data; Pittman's theoretical concession likewise did not erase his contrary conclusion.

The Court separately assesses constitutional sufficiency while respecting the evidentiary character of findings and concessions. It neither decides credibility by counting experts nor treats the First Circuit's economic inference as a new trial finding. Supported prediction remains permissible; no state-specific numerical study, experimental certainty or universal study design is required. Here the whole showing fails the material-advancement requirement. The plaintiffs' expected private profits and the difficulty of measuring advertising effects do not discharge the State's burden.

**Precedent treatment:** Edenfield, Ibanez, Lanphere and Rubin require support responsive to the actual restriction. Edge's record-specific meaningful-reduction holding remains intact. Florida Bar's invalidation confirms scrutiny of the particular showing without prohibiting every predictive judgment.

### II. The complete price-information restraint independently lacks reasonable fit

**Controlling proposition:** Even assuming material advancement, suppressing the challenged category of truthful retail-price information is disproportionate to the asserted temperance objective on this record. Concrete direct price and tax alternatives expose the inadequately justified fit without becoming mandatory replacements or imposing a least-restrictive-means rule.

**Authority:** Kennedy's Part II, joined by Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Souter, Ginsburg and Breyer: seven. O'Connor and Breyer assume advancement without deciding it; Souter independently adopts both failures; Stevens's fit application is conditional and independently sufficient.

**Controlling explanation:** Fox requires a reasonable proportional relationship between the speech burden and the regulatory end. Rhode Island seeks to affect the amount purchased through the price consumers pay, yet withholds accurate comparison information continuously. Saffer's own acknowledgment that minimum prices or higher taxes could serve the consumption objective supplies a concrete comparison with measures addressing price directly.

The State need not select the least restrictive conceivable policy or exhaust every alternative. But it must justify this substantial informational burden in light of practical alternatives. Its interstate-shopping objection does not show why its own indirect price-raising mechanism avoids the same leakage or why the blanket speech burden is proportionate. Midcal and 324 Liquor identify defects in particular price arrangements, not the illegality of every state-imposed price measure. The Court neither designs a replacement nor adjudicates an antitrust dispute.

Permitting nonprice promotion and the specific merchandise and trade-journal exceptions does not by itself defeat or establish fit. Price information may have a distinct competitive effect, and incremental regulation is not automatically invalid. The decisive problem remains the unexplained breadth of suppressing accurate prices to reach an objective directly addressed by the identified controls. Calling the price category complete retains those exact exceptions; it does not describe a ban on all alcohol speech.

This ground suffices even taking the predicted consumption effect seriously. It creates no new heightened tier, universal alternatives checklist or all-sources-at-once requirement. Those who separately favor broader protection do not turn this conditional application into assent by the whole coalition to that broader theory.

**Precedent treatment:** Fox's reasonable-fit standard is applied; Discovery Network and Rubin supply responsive comparison without requiring the least restrictive means. Subsequent searching Central Hudson review prevents treating Posadas as automatic authorization for this restraint; the broader proposed repudiation in IV-C is separately noncontrolling.

### III-A. The Twenty-first Amendment supplies no added presumption validating this restraint

**Controlling proposition:** Rhode Island's alcohol-control authority supplies no additional presumption relaxing First Amendment protection for these off-premises truthful-price prohibitions. Authority over alcohol sales does not itself establish the validity of suppressing accurate information about lawful sales.

**Authority:** Kennedy's Part III-A, joined by all eight other participating Justices: nine. This common proposition does not attribute Central Hudson balancing to Thomas or either sufficient balancing ground to Scalia.

**Controlling explanation:** Section 2 of the Twenty-first Amendment addresses transportation and importation for delivery or use contrary to state law. It creates no express commercial-speech exception. LaRue and Bellanca involved regulation of conduct on licensed premises; those holdings do not decide this restraint on accurate off-premises information. The State's power to regulate the underlying product cannot alone carry its distinct First Amendment burden. Nor are constitutional speech rights surrendered simply by accepting a liquor license. The challenged bans fail without an added alcohol-based presumption; the Justices supporting the broader conclusion in III-B state that separate ground expressly.

**Precedent treatment:** LaRue and Bellanca's conduct holdings are preserved. The narrower distinction here commands nine votes independently of the broader treatment below.

### III-B. The Twenty-first Amendment does not qualify freedom of speech; LaRue's contrary rationale is disavowed

**Controlling proposition:** The Twenty-first Amendment does not qualify the First Amendment's protection of speech. LaRue's reliance on the Twenty-first Amendment to enhance deference is disavowed, while its holding remains supported by ordinary police powers over the conduct at issue.

**Authority:** Kennedy's Part III-B, joined by Stevens, Scalia, Souter, Thomas and Ginsburg: six. Stone-Zsela, O'Connor and Breyer do not join this broader treatment.

**Controlling explanation:** Constitutional authority over the traffic in alcohol is not authority to diminish a separate constitutional freedom. Giving a liquor-related restriction an extra speech presumption would permit the subject regulated to displace the protection owed to the means of expression. LaRue's result does not require that step: its licensed-premises conduct regulation can rest on the State's police powers. Removing the Twenty-first Amendment rationale therefore neither reverses that conduct holding nor invalidates Bellanca. The rule concerns the interaction with freedom of speech, not the validity of every direct alcohol restriction.

**Precedent treatment:** LaRue's identified rationale is expressly disavowed; its holding and Bellanca's conduct result remain undisturbed. This six-Justice treatment is broader than the case-specific distinction supported by the other three.

### V-B. The completed record supports an implementing remand preserving the constitutional declaration

**Controlling proposition:** Reversal of the operative First Circuit judgment requires implementation of the district court's constitutional declaration against the challenged price-information prohibitions. No new merits trial is required on any adopted sufficient ground. The Court does not issue an additional injunction directive.

**Authority:** Kennedy's Part V-B, joined by Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer: eight. Stone-Zsela separately would direct prospective injunctive relief.

**Controlling explanation:** The case was tried and the challenged provisions declared unconstitutional. The appellate reversal rested on legal treatment that cannot sustain these restrictions under the grounds adopted here. An implementing remand preserves the declaration rather than reopening resolved record development. It does not convert the paid fine or unappealed administrative order into a refund dispute, reach the waived Commerce Clause claim or require a substitute alcohol-control program. Stone-Zsela agrees with reversal but regards an express prospective injunction as necessary relief; that additional direction does not command the remedial majority.

## Event

United Food & Commercial Workers Union Local 751 v. Brown Group, Inc., No. 95-340. Argued February 20, 1996; decided May 13, 1996. October Term 1995. On review under 28 U.S.C. §1254(1) of the Eighth Circuit's judgment, 50 F.3d 1426, affirming a standing dismissal without prejudice.

Brown Shoe's January 17, 1992 letter to an International union employee announced closure of its Dixon, Missouri plant and displacement of 277 workers beginning March 20. Local 751 alleges that notice should have gone to the Local and that layoffs began immediately. It sued June 16 as the employees' certified exclusive representative for WARN back pay, benefits and fees. Its representative status is undisputed. The complaint's material allegations are accepted as true and construed favorably at this dismissal stage; no statutory violation, employee eligibility or amount has been tried.

The court below found the first two Hunt conditions satisfied but held that individual participation needed for damages made the representative action constitutionally impermissible. It separately rejected a direct-union-injury theory and upheld denial of postjudgment amendment. The petition presents representative standing, including Hunt's third condition; it does not present the amendment ruling.

## Participation

Chief Justice Alex-Lamar Stone-Zsela and Justices Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer participate at argument and decision. No Justice is recused or otherwise nonparticipating.

## Public Action

## Judgment & Remedy

**Supporting:** Chief Justice Stone-Zsela and Justices Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer.

The union's WARN claim may proceed under the correct standing rule. The decision removes the categorical individual-participation ground for the dismissal without prejudice. Statutory coverage, individual defenses and legally applicable procedures remain for further proceedings.

## Opinion Topology

Justice Souter delivers the Opinion of the Court, Parts I and II, joined throughout by Chief Justice Stone-Zsela and Justices Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer. All nine adopt both controlling propositions and their explanations. No partial join, judgment-only concurrence or separate writing is filed.

## Holdings

### I. WARN authorizes the exclusive representative to enforce employees' monetary claims

**Controlling proposition:** Section 2104(a)(5), read with WARN's representative definition and employee-liability provisions, authorizes the exclusive representative to pursue the represented employees' back-pay and benefit liability. The representative enforces the employees' entitlement without becoming the beneficiary of their recovery. The clause supplies enforcement authority as well as venue.

**Authority:** Part I of Justice Souter's Opinion of the Court, joined by all nine participating Justices identified in Opinion Topology.

**Controlling explanation:** Section 2101(a)(4) defines the representative as an exclusive employee representative under the specified labor statutes. Section 2102 gives that representative a notice function. Section 2104(a)(1) makes a violating employer liable to aggrieved employees suffering employment loss; paragraph (5) expressly includes a representative among persons who may enforce that liability for others. Reading these provisions together gives effect to both Congress's chosen enforcer and its chosen beneficiary.

Respondent argues that the requirement of being aggrieved restricts every listed plaintiff to someone personally entitled to payment, leaving the representative reference as obsolete language in a venue provision. But that reading deprives the expressly retained representative language of its natural function. A representative's enforcement of an employee's liability is not an award of damages to the union. The deletion of an earlier proposed union-specific remedy does not erase the enacted authority to enforce the workers' recovery.

The statute therefore authorizes the action actually pleaded, rather than a general implied cause of action. Its reference to employee liability does not permit the union to collect a local government's distinct penalty. Nor does naming a representative conclusively resolve Article III or the lawful handling of individual claims. Part II addresses the asserted constitutional obstacle while retaining the statute's limitations on who may recover and what may be awarded.

**Precedent treatment:** Warth and Brock did not decide this express congressional authorization of a representative damages action. Varity and Meghrig preserve the distinction between enforcement authority and additional remedies; applying that distinction does not import their different statutory relief into WARN.

### II. Congress has displaced Hunt's prudential individual-participation condition here without displacing Article III

**Controlling proposition:** Hunt's third requirement—that neither the claim nor the requested relief require individual-member participation—is prudential. Congress may displace it and has done so through WARN's express representative authorization. The represented employees must still have concrete injury fairly traceable to the challenged conduct and likely redressable by the requested relief, supported as the litigation stage requires. Individual proof is not made unnecessary; its potential necessity no longer categorically bars this authorized action.

**Authority:** Part II of the same unanimous Opinion of the Court.

**Controlling explanation:** Hunt ordinarily requires members with standing, interests germane to the organization's purpose, and a claim and remedy not requiring individual participation. The first condition secures an actual controversy through injured represented persons. The third addresses the practical suitability of representative litigation, including questions arising when individual entitlements must be determined. Those concerns do not become Article III requirements merely because the requested relief is monetary.

Warth's damages discussion and Brock's reservation of later individual determinations provide the strongest contrary argument: individualized recovery can raise serious proof, adequacy and binding-effect problems. But neither decision considered Congress's express authorization here. Representative standing already separates the named organization from the persons whose concrete interests are at stake. Congress can remove the prudential participation restriction without authorizing adjudication of an abstract grievance or dispensing with independent constitutional protections.

On the accepted allegations and undisputed representative status, affected workers suffered the employment losses and deficient notice for which the statute provides their monetary remedy; germaneness is also satisfied. These pleading conclusions establish no trial-level violation or amount. Different dates, wages and medical expenses may require individual evidence. That evidence may be handled under applicable law without treating its necessity alone as a constitutional bar.

Germaneness for standing does not automatically establish adequate representation for binding absent persons. Adequacy is not disputed on this record, and the Court orders no new adequacy proceeding, class certification, joinder arrangement or jury procedure. Applicable protections and defenses remain. The independent organizational-injury theory is unnecessary to this sufficient representative ground.

**Precedent treatment:** Hunt's third condition is identified as prudential and displaced by this statute; Hunt's first condition and Lujan's constitutional minimum survive. Northeastern Florida's application of Hunt did not decide this congressional-displacement question. Matsushita's protection of adequate representation concerns lawful binding and supplies no categorical bar to this authorized suit.

## Precedent Treatment

Warth, Hunt and Brock retain their representative-standing principles and their original limits. The Court does not treat earlier approval of common legal challenges as already deciding representative recovery of individual damages. WARN's express authorization supplies that further ground. Lujan's concrete injury, causation, redressability and stage-appropriate proof requirements remain unchanged.

Northeastern Florida remains effective on associational standing and pleading-stage support; it did not establish that every Hunt condition is constitutional. Rowland's statutory treatment of association applications is not an Article III holding. Varity and Meghrig preserve statutory enforcement and remedy boundaries, and Matsushita's actual representation protections remain distinct from permission to commence this action. No precedent is overruled.

## Law After Decision

WARN's exclusive representative may enforce employees' monetary claims despite the need for individual participation. Congress has displaced Hunt's third condition for that action, not the constitutional core. Without clear congressional authorization, the third condition remains operative. The Court leaves open whether a sufficiently simplified damages claim could satisfy it without abrogation. It also leaves open whether Hunt's second, germaneness condition is prudential and subject to congressional displacement; germaneness is satisfied here without deciding that classification.

The authorization enlarges no WARN remedy. Section 2104(a)(1) retains back pay for each violation day at not less than the higher of the employee's prior three-year average regular rate and final regular rate, plus covered employee-plan benefits, including medical expenses that would have been covered absent the employment loss. Liability lasts no more than 60 days and no more than half the employee's employment days. An aggrieved employee must have failed to receive timely statutory notice directly or through the representative.

Paragraph (2) requires reductions for wages paid for the violation period, voluntary unconditional payments not required by legal obligation, and employer payments to third parties or trustees on the employee's behalf attributable to that period. Defined-benefit liability may also be reduced by service credit for all plan purposes during that period. Paragraph (4) permits discretionary reduction when the employer proves good faith and reasonable grounds for believing its conduct lawful. Prevailing-party fees remain discretionary under paragraph (6).

The separate local-government penalty remains at most $500 per violation day and does not apply if the employer pays each aggrieved employee the amount owed within three weeks after ordering the shutdown or layoff. The union receives no right to that penalty. Section 2104(b) makes the statutory remedies exclusive and withholds authority under WARN to enjoin a plant closing or mass layoff. None of those amounts, predicates, reductions or exceptions is adjudicated here.

Actual individual proof, legally applicable joinder or certification, representation, duplicate recovery, binding effect and any properly presented jury question remain governed by their own law. No assignment, payroll-only proof rule or universal need for employee testimony is established. The remand prescribes no additional mandatory procedural framework.

## Holdings

### I. Rule 16 does not authorize discovery to prepare a selective-prosecution claim

**Controlling proposition:** Rule 16(a)(1)(C)'s provision for material preparation of the defense does not authorize the requested discovery to prepare a selective-prosecution claim challenging the decision to prosecute. This holding is confined to that claim; it decides neither all affirmative defenses unrelated to guilt nor whether a properly supported constitutional inquiry could have another source of authority.

**Authority:** Part I of O'Connor's Opinion of the Court, joined by Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas and Ginsburg. The six support the stated selective-prosecution scope; broader treatment of other defenses is not the Court's rule.

**Controlling explanation:** Paragraph (a)(1)(C) covers enumerated documents and objects in Government possession, custody or control through three alternative triggers: materiality to preparation of the defense, intended use in the Government's evidence in chief, or acquisition from or ownership by the defendant. The latter two triggers are not established here. For this purpose, preparation of the defense against the prosecution's criminal case differs from the independent constitutional challenge to the selection decision.

The Rule's structure reinforces that distinction. Paragraph (a)(2), except as provided in (a)(1)(A), (B), (D) and (E), excludes internal attorney or agent reports, memoranda and other Government documents connected with investigation or prosecution; it separately restricts Government witness statements through §3500. Paragraph (a)(3) preserves the grand-jury exceptions in Rules 6, 12(i), 26.2 and (a)(1)(A). These provisions protect distinct categories; aggregate information, criteria and internal deliberations are not automatically identical.

Respondents argued the Rule here, and the Government answered through materiality while denying that it sought a new categorical exemption. The lower courts did not decide the Rule's scope. The Court resolves the presented interpretation without finding waiver or attributing its construction to either the Government or the court below.

Protective and sanction powers regulate an established duty; they do not create the original entitlement. Rule 16(d)(1) permits protective relief on sufficient showing, including an ex parte written showing whose full text must be sealed and preserved if relief is granted. Paragraph (d)(2) permits discovery, continuance, exclusion and other just responses to noncompliance, without establishing that it supplied the actual sanction here. Neither Rule 17(c), Brady nor compulsory process supplies an established alternative entitlement on this showing. Rule 16's nonauthorization is not universal immunity from constitutionally warranted review.

### II. The existing showing does not support discovery of discriminatory federal selection

**Controlling proposition:** Before obtaining discovery on this indirect racial-selection theory, defendants must present some credible evidence tending to show discriminatory effect and purpose, including a reliable basis to infer that persons of another race relevantly similarly situated for federal prosecution could have been prosecuted but were not. The preliminary showing is less than a prima facie merits case; the present request fails on effect, without a separate determination that purpose evidence is independently deficient.

**Authority:** Part II of the Court's opinion, joined by eight Justices identified in Opinion Topology. Their shared holding assumes discovery could be available on an appropriate showing and establishes no additional source of authority.

**Controlling explanation:** Wayte preserves constitutional review of prosecutorial discretion but reserved discovery. Wade's meaningful sentencing-stage screening supplies an analogy, not a completed charging rule. A preliminary predicate must connect the evidence to the challenged federal selection; ultimate clear-evidence language does not set this discovery burden.

Klinger ties comparison to the actual treatment, while Kimberlin permits circumstantial proof without removing constitutional elements or lawful screening. Relevant similarity does not mean perfect identity. Grounded statistics and circumstantial comparisons are permissible, and a specifically justified alternative evidentiary basis may supply the required inference when comparator names cannot reasonably be obtained. Neither universal naming nor a fixed checklist is required. Whether direct admissions of discriminatory purpose dispense with similarly situated comparison remains open; none appears here.

Beller's selected closed-1991 sample supplies a numerator, not the racial composition of an eligible charging population or evidence of relevantly similar offenders passed over. Reed's state-practice experience and the relayed treatment observations do not sufficiently connect their subjects to the quantities, firearms, evidence and criminal-history circumstances relevant here. Considered cumulatively with the developed Government response, the submissions still lack that comparison. Missing particulars establish insufficiency of this showing, not the nonexistence of comparators. The Government's declarations identify relevant gaps but are not accepted as proven neutrality; its non-Black examples do not disprove Beller's bounded count.

Access difficulty is substantial, but discovery may complete a grounded inference rather than create its missing connection solely from hope. There is no finding that all state records are readily available or all subpoenas enforceable. General demographic assumptions, the discounted newspaper and an extra-record study cannot supply the denominator. The judge's consideration of all evidence does not cure this legal insufficiency. The present order therefore cannot stand, and neither can dismissal for refusing it. No ultimate discrimination finding follows.

### III. The case returns without a directed renewed discovery inquiry

**Controlling proposition:** The Ninth Circuit's affirmance is reversed and the case remanded for ordinary pretrial proceedings after setting aside the unsupported order and dependent dismissal. The mandate directs no renewed evidentiary inquiry or automatic discovery; it does not forbid a later lawful request supported under the governing requirements.

**Authority:** Part III of the Court's opinion, joined by the seven reversing Associates identified in Opinion Topology. Stone-Zsela agrees with setting aside the existing order and dismissal but rejects this remand without directed staged inquiry; Stevens would affirm.

**Controlling explanation:** The developed record permits resolution of the challenged order. No missing necessary finding, legally required separation of requests or proper showing establishes a present duty to undertake a new discovery stage. Reduced expense or an initial criteria-only request may affect the scope of otherwise authorized discovery, but cannot itself supply the absent selection comparison.

The Government obtained review by refusing the order, and the district court dismissed for that refusal. Once the order's predicate fails, the dependent sanction cannot stand. This conclusion does not decide whether the same sanction would have been appropriate following refusal of a valid order; that independent issue was not presented. Williams's different grand-jury duty, prejudice requirements and without-prejudice remedy do not assign a prejudice label here. Carlisle preserves lawful supervision within governing limits, not an automatic new production duty.

The case accordingly returns for ordinary prosecution. Any later supported request must satisfy its own source, threshold and protective conditions through ordinary procedures. The Court does not compel defendants to produce new evidence, guarantee another hearing or decide the sanction for proved racial selection. Nothing determines guilt, orders conviction or treats this dismissal as an acquittal.

- Wayte v. United States, 470 U.S. 598: applies the constitutional effect-and-purpose limits on broad charging discretion while preserving its express nondecision of discovery; the present threshold is resolved here.
- Wade v. United States, May 18, 1992: applies the distinction between constitutional review and meaningful preliminary screening by analogy from substantial-assistance proceedings; its sentencing rule did not already decide this charging comparison.

## Event

United States v. Noland, 517 U.S. 535, No. 95-323. Argued March 25, 1996; decided May 13, 1996; October Term 1995. On certiorari under 28 U.S.C. §1254(1) to the Sixth Circuit, which affirmed subordination of the Government's tax penalties in In re First Truck Lines, Inc., 48 F.3d 210 (1995). The question concerns the legal sufficiency of the equitable grounds actually used to subordinate these administrative claims.

First Truck commenced Chapter 11 on April 10, 1986, failed to pay postpetition FICA and FUTA taxes, and converted to Chapter 7 on August 1, 1988. The IRS penalties were allowed as administrative expenses under §503(b)(1)(C), but subordinated beneath general unsecured claims. Taxes and interest retained administrative priority; the estate could not pay all creditors. No Government misconduct was found. The Sixth Circuit expressly disclaimed automatic subordination of every tax penalty and required equitable consideration, while relying on the penalties' noncompensatory character, the losses of creditors who supplied value, insufficient assets and retained priority for tax and interest.

Section 503(b), after notice and a hearing and excluding claims allowed under §502(f), allows the specified administrative expenses. Section 503(b)(1)(B) covers a tax incurred by the estate, except a tax of the kind specified in case-era §507(a)(7), and taxes attributable to an excessive tentative carryback adjustment the estate received, whether the relevant tax year ended before or after commencement. Subparagraph (C) covers fines, penalties and reductions in credit relating to those taxes. Case-era §507(a)(1) gives first priority to allowed §503(b) administrative expenses and any fees and charges assessed against the estate under chapter 123 of title 28. Their allowance is distinct from possible subordination.

Section 510(c) provides:

> Notwithstanding subsections (a) and (b) of this section, after notice and a hearing, the court may— (1) under principles of equitable subordination, subordinate for purposes of distribution all or part of an allowed claim to all or part of another allowed claim or all or part of an allowed interest to all or part of another allowed interest; or (2) order that any lien securing such a subordinated claim be transferred to the estate.

## Participation

Chief Justice Alex-Lamar Stone-Zsela and Justices Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer participate at argument and decision. No Justice is recused or otherwise nonparticipating.

## Public Action

The Sixth Circuit's judgment sustaining subordination on the stated grounds is reversed, and the case is remanded.

## Judgment & Remedy

**Judgment:** Reversed and remanded, 9–0. **Supporting:** Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer.

The subordinate ranking cannot remain justified by the same class-based reasons relabeled as individual balancing. The receiving courts must treat the Government's claim under the Code while preserving equitable subordination on a lawful, supported claim-specific ground. The Court does not find such another ground, require a misconduct hearing or reopen every factual issue; any further consideration depends on the actual record and ordinary preservation.

No final distribution or payment amount is ordered. The Court neither disallows the penalty nor alters tax and interest priority, transfers a lien or determines another claim's rights.

## Opinion Topology

Justice Thomas delivers the Opinion of the Court, Parts I–II, joined in full by Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Ginsburg and Breyer. All nine directly adopt both controlling propositions, their explanations and the bounded remand. No partial join, judgment-only agreement or separate writing is present.

## Holdings

### I. Administrative priority and equitable subordination retain distinct statutory functions

**Controlling proposition:** These allowed §503(b)(1)(C) postpetition tax penalties retain case-era §507(a)(1) administrative priority after conversion because §348(d)'s ordinary treatment of post-order, preconversion claims expressly excepts §503(b) claims. They nevertheless remain susceptible to §510(c)'s authorized equitable subordination: §726 makes distribution subject to §510, and priority status alone supplies no immunity from that power.

**Authority:** Part I of Thomas's unanimous Opinion of the Court; all nine Justices identified in Opinion Topology join.

**Controlling explanation:** Section 726(a)(1) ordinarily distributes estate property first to claims of the kinds and in the order specified in §507. Section 726(a)(4) separately assigns fourth rank to an allowed secured or unsecured claim for a fine, penalty, forfeiture, or multiple, exemplary or punitive damages arising before the earlier of the order for relief or appointment of a trustee, and only to the extent it does not compensate actual pecuniary loss. That temporal qualification prevents treating every noncompensatory penalty as a fourth-rank claim. These postpetition administrative expenses retain their different statutory treatment; conversion does not erase it.

But §726 expressly excepts the operation of §510. Section 510(c) permits, after notice and a hearing, distributional subordination of all or part of an allowed claim or interest within its stated claim-to-claim or interest-to-interest terms, and transfer of a lien securing a subordinated claim. The Court cannot preserve priority by deleting that express authority. No agreement under §510(a), securities ground under §510(b) or lien-transfer basis is established here.

Susceptibility is only the first question. The exercise must rest on principles of equitable subordination, rather than an unrestricted judicial preference. Ahlers requires equity within the Code, although its reorganization holding did not decide §510(c). The priority provisions and express exception can both operate; their coexistence establishes no conflicting congressional choices requiring a new class ranking. No priority immunity or frozen catalogue of equitable principles follows.

### II. The stated reasons impermissibly replace Congress's priority choice

**Controlling proposition:** Section 510(c) does not authorize subordination of this statutory class merely because its penalties compensate no pecuniary loss and payment would reduce recovery by creditors who supplied value. The lower courts' stated reasons are legally insufficient despite their express refusal to announce automatic subordination of every penalty; lawful claim-specific equity remains available, but no other sufficient ground is established by this decision.

**Authority:** Part II of the same unanimous Opinion of the Court.

**Controlling explanation:** The Sixth Circuit said every tax penalty need not be subordinated and called for equitable consideration. The Court accepts that description of its announced approach, but examines the reasons actually supporting this result. Noncompensatory character, insufficient assets, the losses of value-giving creditors and preserved tax-and-interest priority recur whenever this penalty class competes with compensatory claims in an insolvent estate. They express a general preference for compensation over the very penalties Congress included among administrative expenses. Describing those reasons as balancing does not supply a different equitable circumstance.

The trustee correctly relies on §726's opening exception and §510(c)'s broad allowed-claim language. Legislative statements about penalty claims and continued equitable development likewise support susceptibility; they do not enact a replacement ranking. The express grant remains meaningful when applied on a lawful claim-specific ground. Ahlers's Code-confines principle preserves that distinction rather than abolishing statutory equity. The statutory method reflected in Things Remembered gives compatible provisions independent operation; its separate equitable-remand question does not determine this distribution power.

The Government's broader argument would require misconduct in every equitable-subordination case. Taylor v. Standard Gas, Pepper and Comstock establish misconduct-based applications, but this case needs no universal answer. The Court neither declares misconduct indispensable nor adjudicates a no-misconduct entitlement. Nor does it freeze permissible judicial development at 1978. Particular inequitable conduct or another recognized claim-specific ground remains addressable when its lawful predicates are proved.

Simonson applied a former statutory penalty prohibition and supplies no current §510(c) ranking. Boteler and Nicholas preserve distinct treatment of postpetition penalties and their compliance function. Innocent-creditor and financing concerns cannot alone displace the enacted order or bypass separate financing conditions. Reversal corrects the legal inadequacy of the stated grounds without changing historical findings or directing final distribution.

- Norwest Bank Worthington v. Ahlers, 485 U.S. 197: applies the requirement that equity operate within the Code; its §1129 holding is not treated as an independent construction of §510(c) or prohibition of all equitable relief.
- Taylor v. Standard Gas & Electric Co., 306 U.S. 307: preserves its misconduct-based equitable application without establishing a universal misconduct prerequisite.
- Pepper v. Litton, 308 U.S. 295: preserves its response to creditor misconduct; no necessary condition for every subordination case is inferred.
- Comstock v. Group of Institutional Investors, 335 U.S. 211: retains its treatment of the creditor relationship without resolving every possible no-misconduct application.
- Simonson v. Granquist, 369 U.S. 38: distinguishes its former statutory penalty prohibition from present allowance, priority and equitable subordination.

- Things Remembered, Inc. v. Petrarca, December 5, 1995: applies the method of giving compatible statutory provisions separate effect. Its remand-review holding and noncontrolling additional equitable-remand theory do not authorize a new distribution rank.

No precedent is overruled. The Court adds the present limit on class-based subordination without eliminating statutory equity, establishing new elements for every permissible application or deciding universal misconduct necessity.

## Law After Decision

The stated balancing grounds fail for that reason; the decision does not rest on a finding that the lower court expressly ordered every penalty subordinated.

The §726(a)(4) temporal and noncompensation qualifications, §348(d)'s administrative-claim exception, and §510(c)'s real distributional authority all remain effective. Priority claims are not categorically immune. Whether misconduct must always precede subordination remains open; no affirmative entitlement to relief without misconduct, mandatory new test or independently sufficient alternative ground is announced. Any lawful further application requires its own supported predicates and proper procedural presentation.

## Event

BMW of North America, Inc. v. Gore, No. 94-896. Argued October 11, 1995; decided May 20, 1996. October Term 1995. On certiorari under 28 U.S.C. §1257 to the Supreme Court of Alabama, 646 So. 2d 619. The questions are whether Alabama provided a meaningful remedy after identifying improper reliance on out-of-state transactions and whether the remitted $2 million punitive award is grossly excessive under the Fourteenth Amendment. The latter question includes, but is not confined to, the 500-to-1 ratio to compensation.

Gore paid $40,750.88 for a BMW sold as new. Acid-rain paint damage during transit had required $601 in refinishing, which was not specifically disclosed. BMW NA's policy withheld disclosure of repairs costing less than three percent of suggested retail price. A detailer discovered the refinishing after about nine months; no apparent finish problem during that use or physical injury was established. The signed damage/inspection acknowledgment did not defeat the adjudicated suppression claim. The jury awarded $4,000 for diminished value and $4 million in punitive damages. Alabama found sufficient clear-and-convincing evidence of the conscious or deliberate fraud required for punitive entitlement in this action.

The parties stipulated to 983 refinished vehicles; BMW estimated 11–14 Alabama transactions. Counsel proposed roughly 1,000 transactions multiplied by $4,000. Alabama found substantial jury reliance on foreign transactions but distinguished their permissible use to show pattern or intent from their use as a punitive multiplier. Because the record did not establish which foreign transactions were unlawful, the state court rejected that multiplier and expressly excluded foreign acts from its own amount analysis. The trial court had held an evidentiary postverdict hearing and found no bias, passion or prejudice. The Alabama Supreme Court applied its punitive-review criteria, compared awards and conditionally reduced punitive damages to $2 million.

The operative opinion is the August 19, 1994 substituted opinion, not the withdrawn October 1993 opinion. Gore accepted the remittitur on August 26. ## Holdings

### I. Grossly excessive punitive damages violate due process; fair notice includes severity

**Controlling proposition:** A punitive award grossly excessive in relation to the State's legitimate interests in punishment and deterrence violates due process. The inquiry begins with those interests. Fair notice encompasses both conduct subject to punishment and the possible severity of the penalty; wealth does not remove that constitutional constraint.

**Authority:** Stevens's Part I, joined by O'Connor, Kennedy, Souter and Breyer: five.

**Controlling explanation:** States may punish fraud and deter its repetition through damages exceeding compensation. The different functions of compensation and punishment preclude a rule requiring the two to move in exact proportion. They do not authorize every amount. Haslip sustained punitive adjudication bounded by reasonable constraints and a relationship to legitimate punitive purposes; it did not approve every later Alabama award.

The strongest objection is that traditional procedures and actual judicial review should leave the selected amount to state judgment. Those safeguards are indispensable, but the conclusion that review occurred does not dispose of a separately presented claim of gross excessiveness. TXO sustained its particular process without supplying a controlling substantive formula. Oberg requires meaningful examination and lawful correction of the amount, while reserving the substantive question answered here.

Notice of liability and notice of severity perform related but distinct work. Alabama's established disclosure duty remains valid, and the adjudicated suppression is not made lawful by disagreement among States or a later statute. Nonetheless, the punishment must remain within the constitutional protection against grossly excessive deprivation. Corporate wealth can bear on deterrence without replacing the required relationship to the wrong. The Court adopts neither all criminal procedural protections for civil actions nor a right to mathematical advance calculation of every permissible award.

**Precedent treatment:** Haslip's substantive and procedural protections are applied. TXO's record-specific procedural holding and Oberg's amount-review requirement remain intact; neither is recast as an earlier adoption of today's substantive framework.

### II. Foreign evidence and punishment are distinct; Alabama's stated correction is accepted

**Controlling proposition:** Alabama may not punish conduct lawful where it occurred and having no impact on Alabama or its residents, or use punitive deterrence to impose its policy on other States. Whether a State may seek to change unlawful conduct elsewhere remains open. Relevant foreign transactions may illuminate pattern, intent and reprehensibility; their evidentiary use does not itself impose punishment for those transactions.

**Authority:** Stevens's Part II, joined by Stone-Zsela, O'Connor, Kennedy, Souter and Breyer: six.

**Controlling explanation:** Alabama has a legitimate interest in protecting its purchasers from fraud. That interest does not give it authority to punish lawful transactions disconnected from the State simply because it would prefer a different disclosure policy elsewhere. Differences among state policies therefore matter to the permissible reach of deterrence. The holding is qualified by lawfulness and absence of Alabama impact; it supplies no universal immunity for conduct outside the State.

Evidence and punishment answer different questions. A recurring policy, including transactions elsewhere, can support knowledge, intent or the character of the Alabama wrong when governing law makes it relevant. The Court neither excludes all such evidence nor adjudicates the legality of every sale in the national sample. Nor does it decide whether another State could punish conduct unlawful within its own authority.

The original multiplier presented the identified problem. Alabama rejected it and expressly excluded foreign acts from its own amount determination. That statement is accepted; a separate state concurrence's description does not establish that the majority covertly retained the multiplier. The judgment under review is the accepted $2 million remittitur. Recognizing the correction neither reinstates the original verdict nor determines whether the remaining amount survives the independent substantive inquiry in Part III.

**Precedent treatment:** Shutts and Allstate v. Hague support attention to the State's connection with the transactions affected. Their choice-of-law principles are not transformed into an evidence exclusion or a numerical punitive ceiling.

### III-A. Three qualified guideposts structure constitutional excessiveness review

**Controlling proposition:** Review considers the reprehensibility of the defendant's conduct; the relationship of punitive damages to actual or potential harm to the plaintiff; and civil or criminal sanctions authorized or imposed for comparable misconduct. These are operative guideposts, with reprehensibility especially important, but not an exhaustive mechanical checklist, fixed ratio or dollar ceiling.

**Authority:** Stevens's Part III-A, joined by O'Connor, Kennedy, Souter and Breyer: five.

**Controlling explanation:** Reprehensibility connects severity to the wrong being punished. The nature of the injury, deliberate or affirmative misconduct, repeated behavior and vulnerability can distinguish more blameworthy conduct from less aggravated wrongdoing. Economic injury is not categorically immune from substantial punishment; intentional misconduct or exploitation can justify a substantial award without physical injury.

The harm comparison likewise informs rather than mechanically decides proportionality. Actual compensation need not measure the harm deliberately threatened. Supported potential harm belongs in the inquiry, while speculative loss cannot be invented to justify a chosen number. Small compensation, egregious conduct, injury difficult to detect or harm difficult to value can support larger ratios. The Court announces no bright line beyond which every award fails.

Comparable authorized or imposed sanctions inform the seriousness with which the State treats the misconduct and the notice of possible severity. They do not automatically become exclusive civil-punishment caps. State discretion, deterrence and differences among remedies remain relevant, but cannot make every comparison immaterial.

These guideposts supply reasons for constitutional judgment rather than an unexplained impression that an award seems large. Haslip's refusal to establish a universal mathematical boundary is preserved. TXO's divided potential-harm reasoning cannot be treated as binding arithmetic, and disparity among jury outcomes alone establishes no bias. The inquiry requires judgment applied to lawful purposes and supported facts.

**Precedent treatment:** Haslip's reasonableness inquiry is developed into the stated guideposts. TXO establishes no controlling substantive formula, and this decision adopts no fixed ratio.

### III-B. The remitted $2 million is grossly excessive on this record

**Controlling proposition:** After accepting Alabama's removal of foreign transactions from its punitive calculation, the remaining $2 million is grossly excessive in light of the established wrong, supported harm and comparable sanctions. Neither adequate power to review nor actual reduction conclusively establishes a constitutionally permissible amount.

**Authority:** Stevens's Part III-B, joined by O'Connor, Kennedy, Souter and Breyer: five.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court accepts the deliberate-suppression finding and entitlement to punitive damages. Recurrence and concealment support punishment beyond compensation. The injury nevertheless was economic, without an established health or safety danger, and the character of this nondisclosure does not equate it with the most aggravated fraud. A repair bill alone would understate Gore's loss; the jury's $4,000 diminution finding supplies the actual compensation.

No supported much larger injury to Gore supplies the missing scale. The estimated 11–14 Alabama transactions can illuminate pattern and culpability, but are not adjudicated individual claims or an automatic aggregate punishment base. Neither national loss nor a larger future injury is established in this record. The 500-to-1 disparity therefore raises a substantial concern on this record, though that number is not itself a universal rule.

Variable disclosure laws inform notice and blameworthiness without erasing Alabama's adjudicated duty. The identified $2,000 statutory sanction and the comparison awards provide no adequate notice or justification for this magnitude. The statutory figure is a comparator, not an exclusive cap, and BMW's proposed $56,000 calculation is not adopted.

The strongest argument for affirmance combines deliberate recurring conduct, deterrence and Alabama's actual hearing, review and halving. Those circumstances receive weight; they do not justify the extraordinary remaining amount under the guideposts. BMW's ability to pay cannot fill the gap. This is a substantive conclusion about the corrected award, not a finding that the reviewing judges were dishonest, that no review occurred or that the original multiplier remained secretly operative.

**Precedent treatment:** Haslip did not immunize every Alabama result. TXO's supported threatened harm is not presumed present here. Oberg's protection of amount review is preserved without treating its distinct procedural defect as established in this case.

### IV. Alabama chooses the lawful means of redetermining punitive damages

**Controlling proposition:** Reverse only the judgment sustaining the $2 million punitive award and remand for a lawful punitive disposition. Alabama initially determines whether a new punitive trial or an independent appellate determination is appropriate. The Court selects no sum and does not require a new jury merely because the original verdict used an improper multiplier.

**Authority:** Stevens's Part IV, joined by O'Connor, Kennedy, Souter and Breyer: five.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court has decided that the corrected amount is grossly excessive, not merely that Alabama omitted a hearing or failed to provide enough explanatory words. Its remedy therefore requires a disposition consistent with that substantive holding. Repeating the same $2 million on this record with fuller prose would not answer it.

The accepted remittitur establishes the operative judgment; it does not prevent further lawful correction after federal review. State procedures retain the first choice between a new punitive trial and an appropriate appellate redetermination. No constitutional maximum, mandatory reduction or universal rule governing every judicial alteration is selected.

The compensation and suppression liability supply no basis for reopening, and entitlement to a lawful punitive award survives. BMW AG's separate favorable disposition remains outside this challenge. Neither revival of the discarded $4 million nor judgment on other purchasers' claims follows. The limited remand respects Alabama's punitive authority while enforcing the constitutional constraint applied here.

## Precedent Treatment

Haslip remains governing: States may employ punitive damages with reasonable constraints, actual review and a relationship to legitimate punishment and deterrence. Its rejection of a universal mathematical line survives. The present guideposts and application develop substantive excessiveness review without facially invalidating Alabama's punitive system.

TXO's five-Justice procedural sufficiency proposition remains intact. Its distinct substantive writings establish no common ratio or potential-harm formula. Oberg continues to require meaningful examination and lawful correction of punitive **amount**, or an adequate functional substitute; review of entitlement or legal error alone does not necessarily suffice. The Court does not overrule that safeguard or impose its Oregon-specific result on Alabama's different process.

Shutts and Allstate v. Hague support territorial limits within their stated roles; neither creates a general prohibition on relevant foreign evidence. No precedent is overruled. The three-Justice supplemental constraints explanation and the dissenters' competing federalism and source-of-right theories retain their separate authority.

## Law After Decision

Due process forbids grossly excessive punitive awards in relation to legitimate state punishment and deterrence. Fair notice includes possible severity. Reprehensibility, the relationship to actual or potential harm to the plaintiff, and comparable sanctions are controlling guideposts, with their stated qualifications. They are neither optional observations nor a fixed ratio, exclusive checklist or dollar cap.

The qualified territorial rule has six votes. It excludes punishment of conduct lawful elsewhere and having no Alabama impact, preserves relevant evidence, and reserves the question of efforts to change unlawful foreign conduct. Alabama's stated removal of foreign transactions is accepted. Five Justices nevertheless invalidate the surviving $2 million on substantive grounds and require a lawful punitive redetermination.

Meaningful amount review remains required. The existence of process does not immunize every result, and this result does not establish absence of process. Compensation, punitive entitlement, BMW AG's dismissal, the validity of every form of judicial alteration and questions of repeated punishment for the same conduct remain within the limits stated above. No new general corporate-rights catalogue is adopted.

## Separate Writings

**Breyer, concurring, joined by O'Connor and Souter.** All three join the Court's opinion in full and supply an additional explanation. A procedurally proper award ordinarily deserves a strong presumption of validity. Here both the standards' failure significantly to constrain discretion as interpreted and applied and the gross disproportion to legitimate punitive objectives overcome that presumption. The existence of hearings, state criteria and actual appellate correction is acknowledged.

Clear-and-convincing proof of punitive entitlement does not distinguish every permissible amount. Harm, culpability and profit criteria must constrain the actual sum; wealth and litigation incentives do not supply a missing limit, and inapplicable mitigation factors do not do so. A supported economic theory can provide meaningful discipline, but a hypothetical model cannot replace the grounds and evidence before the state court. Historical comparisons illustrate the concern without creating a damages formula. The concurrence neither invalidates Alabama's system on its face nor requires written arithmetic, a compulsory economic model or an automatic new trial. Its conjunctive rationale is supported by three Justices, not an additional Court holding.

**Scalia, dissenting, joined by Thomas.** They would affirm. Their substantive/process distinction preserves traditional adjudication and meaningful judicial review enforcing state-law limits while rejecting an independent federal constitutional entitlement to a different punitive sum. They regard the guideposts as lacking a sufficient constitutional source and workable measure for federal supervision of amounts. Disagreement with a number does not prove the absence of a safeguard.

Alabama held a postverdict hearing, applied its review criteria, expressly removed the foreign multiplier and reduced the judgment. Assuming the identified error required correction, the correction occurred; they need not adopt the Court's territorial rule. Their position approves neither the original $4 million nor every possible remittitur. Both retain Oberg's amount-review protection; Thomas's agreement rests on his own TXO and Oberg positions, without adopting a separate opinion he did not join.

**Ginsburg, dissenting.** She would affirm from institutional restraint, federalism and respect for actual state correction. Alabama's courts addressed deliberate recurring fraud, considered their substantive criteria and comparison awards, and replaced the jury's national measure with a reduced judgment. The difficulty of supplying a workable federal scale counsels against displacing that state judgment here. Her concern is not answered simply by identifying a high ratio.

She does not join Scalia's categorical rejection of every substantive due-process protection or affirm by applying the Court's new guideposts. Her earlier reservation of a properly presented gross-excessiveness challenge remains meaningful. The present federal challenge does not justify additional intervention in light of the safeguards and correction actually supplied. State reform may address punitive practice, but proposed reforms are not treated as operative law, and a later disclosure amendment does not erase this adjudicated wrong. Her conclusion rests on the established review and correction.

**Stone-Zsela, concurring in Part II and otherwise dissenting.** He would affirm the remitted $2 million punitive judgment. His answer to the first question is yes: Alabama supplied a constitutionally sufficient remedy for the identified use of foreign transactions through meaningful review and its established, accepted remittitur procedure. His answer to the second is no: constitutionally adequate adjudication and meaningful review do not leave an additional federal substantive power to invalidate an amount merely because its magnitude or ratio appears excessive.

BMW's corporate form does not remove its property and procedural protections, but those protections do not themselves establish a substantive entitlement to the sum a federal judge prefers. Each claimed constitutional consequence requires its own source. TXO established no controlling ratio or general substantive formula; Oberg requires meaningful examination and lawful correction of the amount without supplying a maximum, mandatory remittitur or federal recalculation formula. That amount-review function cannot be reduced to checking punitive entitlement alone.

He agrees that Alabama cannot punish beyond its lawful authority and that relevant foreign evidence differs from foreign conduct made an independent object of punishment. Knowledge, intent, deliberateness, notice or a common policy may make such evidence relevant. Alabama expressly corrected its identified error; another potentially lawful remedy does not automatically become constitutionally necessary.

Compensation measures loss, while punitive damages serve punishment and deterrence. A large ratio alone cannot determine whether the latter rests on a lawful basis; nor does a large compensatory award justify any punishment. Reprehensibility, ratios and comparable sanctions may inform state-law or otherwise established review without becoming the Court's new constitutional elements. Courts retain meaningful amount review and correction of unlawful punitive availability, instructions, proof, considerations, procedural unfairness and statutory violations. Those examples are not an exhaustive checklist.

His conclusion neither makes punitive damages unreviewable nor authorizes punishment outside Alabama's lawful authority. It preserves traditional remittitur, restores no part of the original $4 million, and reserves whether every form of judicial alteration is permissible. He joins Part II's narrow territorial and evidentiary propositions, but not the Court's independent substantive framework, application or remand.

## Procedure After Action

The case returns to Alabama for a lawful punitive disposition, with the State initially selecting between a new punitive trial and an independent appellate redetermination. No replacement sum, automatic jury proceeding, special mandate date or retained Supreme Court merits proceeding is prescribed. The original $4 million remains displaced; compensation, underlying suppression liability, lawful punitive entitlement and BMW AG's separate dismissal are not reopened. Ordinary mandate procedures apply.

## Event

Romer v. Evans, 517 U.S. 620, No. 94-1039. Argued October 10, 1995; decided May 20, 1996; October Term 1995. On certiorari under 28 U.S.C. §1257 to the Colorado Supreme Court, which affirmed a permanent injunction against Amendment 2 on October 11, 1994, 882 P.2d 1335, Nos. 94SA48/94SA128.

Colorado voters approved Amendment 2 on November 3, 1992, by 813,966 to 710,151; it was certified December 16. The suit began November 12. The amendment added article II, §30 to the Colorado Constitution:

> No Protected Status Based on Homosexual, Lesbian, or Bisexual Orientation. Neither the State of Colorado, through any of its branches or departments, nor any of its agencies, political subdivisions, municipalities or school districts, shall enact, adopt or enforce any statute, regulation, ordinance or policy whereby homosexual, lesbian or bisexual orientation, conduct, practices or relationships shall constitute or otherwise be the basis of or entitle any person or class of persons to have or claim any minority status quota preferences, protected status or claim of discrimination. This Section of the Constitution shall be in all respects self-executing.

The authoritative state construction establishes withdrawal of existing orientation-based nondiscrimination protection and foreclosure of similar or greater protection through ordinary state and local legislative, executive and judicial channels unless the state constitution changes. The prohibition reaches the State itself, its agencies and schools, as well as local regulation in public and private settings. It is not confined to quotas, one ordinance or local preemption leaving the state legislature free to act. The Court assumes, without deciding, that generally applicable criminal, tort, contract and public-service protections remain available.

Evans I, 854 P.2d 1270 (1993), applied political-process strict scrutiny at the preliminary stage. After trial, the courts sustained a permanent injunction on that ground. The trial court rejected suspect or quasi-suspect status; plaintiffs did not appeal that ruling and do not concede its correctness. Respondents also pressed ordinary rational-basis invalidity below and here. The trial court and state majority did not decide that alternative because they applied strict scrutiny.

## Participation

Chief Justice Alex-Lamar Stone-Zsela and Justices Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer participate at argument and decision. No Justice is recused or otherwise nonparticipating.

## Public Action

The judgment of the Colorado Supreme Court is affirmed. Amendment 2 violates the Equal Protection Clause under ordinary rational-basis review.

## Judgment & Remedy

**Judgment:** Affirmed, 7–2. **Supporting:** Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Kennedy, Souter, Ginsburg and Breyer. **Dissenting:** Scalia and Thomas.

The permanent injunction against enforcement of Amendment 2 remains effective in full. The constitutional defect extends throughout the amendment's common class-specific operation, and the authoritative state severability determination supplies no independent remainder. No new merits hearing, damages award, quota entitlement or enactment of a particular nondiscrimination ordinance is ordered. The Court does not decide any independently preserved First Amendment claim.

## Opinion Topology

Justice Souter delivers the Opinion of the Court, Parts I–II, joined throughout by Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Kennedy, Ginsburg and Breyer. All seven directly adopt the same rational-basis ground, its complete explanation and qualifications, and the full injunction. Justice Scalia files a dissenting opinion, joined throughout by Thomas. No concurrence, partial join or judgment-only agreement is present.

## Holdings

### I. Amendment 2's class-specific disability fails ordinary rational-basis review

**Controlling proposition:** The Fourteenth Amendment forbids this combined withdrawal of existing nondiscrimination protection and constitutional foreclosure of comparable protection through ordinary government for the identified class. The amendment's established operation bears no rational relationship to the asserted legitimate interests. This conclusion does not depend on suspect status, political-process strict scrutiny, disappearance of generally applicable protection or a finding about every voter's motive.

**Authority:** Part I of Souter's Opinion of the Court; Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Kennedy, Souter, Ginsburg and Breyer, 7 Justices.

**Controlling explanation:** Ordinary rationality permits reasonable predictions, imperfect categories and conceivable legitimate grounds without formal findings or empirical certainty. It does not require the least restrictive means. But the justification must relate to the actual disadvantage. Heller's proof holding, Moreno and Cleburne preserve that inquiry; Heller's family-participation holding shows why a narrower alternative alone is insufficient.

Uniformity explains a common rule, not why this class alone becomes constitutionally ineligible for protection from state as well as local authorities. Regulatory restraint can explain rejecting particular private obligations; it does not explain prohibiting ordinary public policies and protection across nonintimate relationships. Scarcity permits priorities, but does not explain a blanket prohibition regardless of the authority's resources or the protection's nature. Denver and Wisconsin findings of no added cost or impaired enforcement reinforce that conclusion without requiring empirical refutation of every possible saving.

Religious and intimate-association burdens are real concerns. Hurley likewise protects actual private expression, not a general right to discriminate in ordinary services. Exemptions illustrate the particular settings these interests concern; their availability alone is not decisive, and no municipality's exemption is finally approved. Those concerns do not rationally explain the amendment's prohibition across public and impersonal commercial settings.

Parents remain free to teach their moral convictions; no demonstrated connection makes nondiscrimination protection prevent that teaching, and government need not endorse every private belief. Stopping one side's political objectives or avoiding its participation because the issue is divisive cannot independently justify this disability. Bowers's specified-conduct holding does not explain barring protection by orientation throughout ordinary public and commercial life. Morality was preserved, but this conclusion neither invalidates every conduct-based moral judgment nor overrules Bowers.

The amendment's structure and established operation, rather than a free-standing hostility finding, expose the missing connection. Its class-wide disadvantage therefore falls within Moreno/Cleburne's prohibition. The Court imposes no general duty to enact nondiscrimination legislation.

### II. The preserved ordinary-review ground supports the complete existing injunction

**Controlling proposition:** The Court may affirm on the fully presented rational-basis alternative without adopting the lower court's strict-scrutiny rationale. The established constitutional defect extends throughout the inseparable amendment, warranting preservation of the complete injunction without a new merits remand or a federal rewriting of state law.

**Authority:** Part II of Souter's Opinion of the Court; the same seven Justices join in full.

**Controlling explanation:** The amendment, authoritative construction, asserted interests, relevant findings and adversarial ordinary-review arguments permit legal resolution now. Failure of strict scrutiny below does not itself establish failure of rational-basis review; Part I independently decides that question. Nor does the Court rely on an unconditional State concession that it could not satisfy strict scrutiny.

The State sought severance of orientation from conduct, practices and relationships. Colorado's court applied autonomy and enacting intent, concluding that those terms identify the same class and are inseparable. Deleting one description leaves the same constitutional disability. Because the defect found here permeates that common operation, no independent lawful remainder is established. The injunction corresponds to the violation rather than disabling a separately lawful structure. No new federal severability rule or universal facial-challenge formula is announced.

States retain substantial authority over governmental organization and substantive policy. That authority does not immunize a state constitutional provision from the Fourteenth Amendment. The continued ability to seek a constitutional amendment does not cure the particular unequal barrier to ordinary protection. Yet a policy defeat, referendum, repeal or constitutional reservation is not unconstitutional merely because it affects an identifiable group.

The Court neither reopens the unappealed classification issue nor adopts a fundamental right of every group to every lawmaking level. Particular ordinances, religious exemptions and future expression conflicts remain for their own cases. Reported separate First Amendment claims are neither decided nor presumed abandoned; their existence requires no new hearing or remand in this disposition.

## Precedent Treatment

- FCC v. Beach Communications, 508 U.S. 307: preserves conceivable legitimate grounds, justified economic generalizations and the absence of a formal-findings requirement, together with its Moreno/Cleburne limit. It supplies no categorical immunity for every express group classification.
- Heller v. Doe, June 24, 1993: applies Souter's controlling invalidation of the unequal confinement-proof burden because the proposed differences did not justify the actual disadvantage. Kennedy's separate controlling family-participation holding preserves a relevant informational generalization with counsel, effective contest and independent adjudication; a narrower alternative alone does not defeat rationality. No new scrutiny tier follows from either holding.
- Department of Agriculture v. Moreno, 413 U.S. 528, and Cleburne v. Cleburne Living Center, 473 U.S. 432: apply the limits on bare harm and irrational prejudice while preserving relevant distinctions. Neither requires a finding about each elector's private motives.
- Hurley, June 19, 1995: preserves the private parade's expressive composition and ordinary nondiscrimination applications. Customer identity is not automatically a message, and ordinary service is not categorically expressive association. Swanner and American Life League retain their application-specific religious-liberty inquiries and actual RFRA burdens; no automatic exemption or transfer of those burdens results here.
- Hunter v. Erickson, 393 U.S. 385, and Washington v. Seattle School District No. 1, 458 U.S. 457: retain their racial political-restructuring settings. James v. Valtierra, 402 U.S. 137, Gordon v. Lance, 403 U.S. 1, and Crawford v. Board of Education, 458 U.S. 527, preclude treating every referendum, neutral voting rule or policy revision as unconstitutional exclusion. The Court does not adopt a new fundamental-right extension of those cases.
- Bowers v. Hardwick, 478 U.S. 186: remains in force on its specified-conduct question. The Court rejects the proposed justification for this wider eligibility-for-protection disability without deciding an unpresented overruling or declaring every conduct-based moral judgment illegitimate.
- Adarand, June 12, 1995: retains its supported federal remedial-classification standard and distinction between meaningful opportunity and entitlement to the requested benefit. It creates no sexual-orientation tier. Klinger preserves comparison directed to the actual challenged opportunity and distinguishes express classifications from purposeful discrimination in genuinely neutral decisions; Armstrong's federal-charging discovery threshold does not govern this express amendment.
- Dalton, March 18, 1996: preserves relief commensurate with an established violation and independent lawful operation. No general facial-review formula displaces the present inseparability analysis. ## Law After Decision

Effective May 20, 1996, Amendment 2 cannot be enforced. Its established combination of withdrawing existing protection and making the identified class ineligible for comparable protection through ordinary government fails rational-basis review. The full injunction remains because that defect extends throughout the inseparable measure.

Ordinary policy choices and reasonable generalizations remain permissible. The decision creates no suspect or quasi-suspect classification, universal political-access right, evidentiary checklist or entitlement to any particular ordinance. It assumes no loss of all generally applicable legal protection and decides no broader general-law effect. Specific religious, intimate-association and expressive claims retain their own governing rules. Bowers remains in force, and no nationwide nondiscrimination code, quota requirement or damages remedy is established.

## Separate Writings

**Scalia, dissenting, joined by Thomas.** The two Justices would reverse the reviewed equal-protection judgment and remove that foundation for the injunction, with remand bounded by any independently preserved unresolved claims. They reject an extension of Hunter and Seattle into a fundamental entitlement of every group to pursue legislation at every governmental level, and they would sustain the amendment under ordinary rationality.

They accept its full restriction on state as well as local institutions, including public and nonintimate settings. In their view, regulatory restraint, religious and associational choice, finite enforcement resources and statewide uniformity rationally support withholding the disputed antidiscrimination protection. Reasonable predictions and imperfect coverage suffice; narrower exemptions are not constitutionally mandatory. The breadth implements the substantive policy rather than defeating its connection to those purposes. Constitutional reservation does not immunize the policy from federal review, but neither makes an otherwise rational policy unconstitutional.

Bowers is a sufficient additional premise in their reasoning: constitutional power to criminalize the specified conduct supports the lesser choice of withholding the disputed protection. Conduct-based moral judgment is distinct from hatred of persons, and orientation may serve as a proxy in this noncriminal policy. They do not claim Bowers literally adjudicated Amendment 2 or that every moral dislike is legitimate. Moreno and Cleburne continue to forbid bare harm; the dissent finds legitimate policy grounds here. Heller's controlling proof holding is distinguished as addressing an inadequately justified confinement risk, not displaced by its opposing opinion.

Their conditional facial argument also respects the state construction. If orientation, conduct, practices and relationships identify the same class, the Bowers inference, on their view, reaches the common operation. If a distinct orientation-only application is instead posited, constitutionally permissible conduct applications would defeat wholesale invalidation under their Salerno argument. That contention depends on their rejection of the Court's defect throughout the actual class-wide operation; it does not independently answer that defect. They order no severance, find no conduct by every class member and rely on no unverified admission by a respondent.

Scalia's commercial-speech position supplies no general prohibition of conduct-based moral legislation. Thomas's rejection of suppressing independently protected truthful speech, and his separate rule against keeping lawful purchasers ignorant to manipulate their choices, remain confined to that speech setting; he does not extend them into a universal bar on the different Bowers inference. Neither Justice treats actual religious conflicts as automatic exemptions for every ordinance. Their proposed remand would not adjudicate surviving First Amendment claims, invent a new hearing or authorize enforcement despite any independent lawful restraint. Their grounds and disposition are noncontrolling.

Any independently preserved unresolved claim remains subject to its existing procedural requirements, without an opinion here on its merits or on whether further proceedings are needed.
