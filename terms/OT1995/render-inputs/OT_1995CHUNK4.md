## Event

Peacock v. Thomas, No. 94-1453, 516 U.S. 349. Argued November 6, 1995; decided February 21, 1996. On writ of certiorari to the United States Court of Appeals for the Fourth Circuit, 39 F.3d 493. The questions concern original ERISA and ancillary jurisdiction over a later action imposing Tru-Tech's earlier judgment on Peacock. He was a defendant in the original action, but was held not to be a fiduciary and was not made a judgment debtor. The later judgment imposed the whole $187,628.93 corporate obligation, with derivative assessments; it was not a separable award returning identified debtor property.

## Participation

Chief Justice Stone-Zsela and Justices Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer participated at argument and decision.

## Public Action

The judgment sustaining jurisdiction and imposing Tru-Tech's obligation on Peacock is reversed, and the case is remanded for dismissal of the later action for lack of subject-matter jurisdiction.

## Judgment & Remedy

**Reversed and remanded for jurisdictional dismissal, 8–1.** Supporting: Stone-Zsela, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer. Opposing: Stevens, who would vacate the unqualified personal-liability and derivative-fee affirmance but preserve ancillary jurisdiction for a bounded restorative-enforcement remand.

The later personal liability and its dependent interest, attorneys' fees and expenses against Peacock cannot stand. The direction to recalculate the derivative assessment falls with its jurisdictional basis; the already vacated $205,182.01 combined fees-and-expenses amount is not reinstated. The original $187,628.93 judgment against Tru-Tech remains intact. The dismissal does not extinguish otherwise available state-law rights or an action supported by an independent jurisdictional grant.

## Opinion Topology

| Writing | Author | Joined by | Relationship and scope |
|---|---|---|---|
| Opinion of the Court | Thomas | O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Ginsburg and Breyer throughout; Stone-Zsela on ancillary jurisdiction, its application and limits, and disposition | Seven Justices adopt the original-ERISA-jurisdiction analysis; eight adopt the ancillary-jurisdiction and disposition propositions. Stone-Zsela does not join the separate ERISA analysis. |
| Dissent | Stevens | No other Justice | Opposes subject-matter dismissal; supports vacating the excessive whole-debt and derivative-fee affirmance for possible restorative relief on remand. |

## Holdings

**The asserted ERISA basis does not supply original jurisdiction.** Thomas's opinion, joined by O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Ginsburg and Breyer, holds that this later collection action alleges no underlying ERISA or plan violation that supports the asserted federal jurisdiction; veil piercing is a means of imposing liability, not an independent statutory violation. Section 502(a)(3) supplies relief for the statutory or plan obligations it identifies, not equitable relief at large. Later control and collection obstruction do not themselves erase the original nonfiduciary adjudication or establish fiduciary responsibility during the earlier breaches. Calling an unrestricted compensation award equitable supplies neither a new duty nor a jurisdictional source. The Court assumes without deciding that ERISA may permit veil piercing in an appropriately founded action. It does not decide every possible equitable monetary remedy or treat every unsuccessful federal merits theory as a jurisdictional defect.

**Ancillary jurisdiction does not support this new full-debt action.** Thomas's opinion, joined on this proposition by Stone-Zsela, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Ginsburg and Breyer, holds that a court's power to identify, preserve and execute against its judgment debtor's assets does not authorize a later independent action imposing that debtor's whole obligation on another person without an independent jurisdictional basis. Kokkonen's decree-effectuation branch is distinct from factual interdependence; neither is unlimited. The later collection conduct differs materially from the original pension violations, and a new lawsuit cannot borrow the earlier action's jurisdiction merely through connection to its result. Making Peacock a new debtor requires adjudicating new facts, duties and defenses. Beecher's limit on creating a new obligation applies; Riggs and Labette County enforced existing official duties rather than transferring the judgment debt to officials. Rule 69 and §1367 do not independently create original jurisdiction. The original suit predates §1367's effective boundary, and the later filing supplies no original claim to which supplemental jurisdiction can attach. Remedial need does not replace statutory authority.

**The jurisdictional disposition preserves ordinary enforcement and leaves unresolved merits questions.** The same eight Justices require dismissal of the later action on its asserted grounds while preserving the original judgment. The holding does not restrict contempt, asset discovery, ordinary attachment or garnishment, fraudulent-transfer process directed to debtor property, or an independently founded federal action. It neither affirms a nonexistent separable property award nor adjudicates particular assets, recipients or values. The Court does not decide personal jurisdiction or service, the governing South Carolina enforcement rule, federal displacement of that rule, or the merits of later-conduct preclusion. The preserved service objection is neither rejected nor deemed waived. Ancillary subject-matter jurisdiction alone would not establish nationwide-service authority. No general prohibition on all alter-ego actions or all proceedings involving persons other than the debtor follows.

## Precedent Treatment

Kokkonen's independent-authority requirement and distinct decree-effectuation branch are applied to the later new-debtor action. Beecher is applied; Riggs and Labette County are distinguished by their existing-duty premise. Mertens's bounded equitable-category rule and antecedent-action reservation remain intact. Neither O'Melveny nor Travelers supplies a general federal collection rule, and the Fourth Circuit's categorical federal-preemption rationale is not approved. No earlier holding is overruled.

## Law After Decision

A federal judgment does not itself confer jurisdiction over a separate action creating another person's full liability for that judgment. Traditional execution against debtor property remains available, and an independently founded federal action remains possible. The Court adds this new-debtor limit without deciding the merits or governing law of a particular restorative property proceeding. It creates no new ERISA collection cause and no general bar to equitable monetary relief.

## Separate Writings

Stevens dissents from jurisdictional dismissal. A court's continuing authority to effectuate its judgment, he concludes, permits consideration of restoration when a person controlling the debtor deliberately diverts its assets to defeat collection. Wayman, Riggs and Labette County support that enforcement function; Beecher's attempted addition of independently liable persons does not resolve this distinct interference with execution. But the full corporate debt cannot simply be imposed on Peacock. Stevens would vacate the unqualified liability and derivative-fee affirmance and remand to determine whether restorative relief remains legally and procedurally available. The existing findings do not establish a ready-made asset award, that every transfer occurred after judgment, that Peacock received every asset, or a particular recoverable value. State enforcement law ordinarily supplies the Rule 69 rule; federal uniformity alone does not justify displacement. The preserved personal-jurisdiction and service objections must be resolved before renewed personal relief, and any fee or expense assessment requires its own lawful basis. He reserves the alternative ERISA cause and would not direct immediate payment, reinstate a rejected count or guarantee recovery. The original Tru-Tech judgment remains undisturbed under his approach as well.

## Procedure After Action

The case returns to the lower courts for dismissal of the later action for want of subject-matter jurisdiction and removal of the dependent assessments against Peacock. The original judgment against Tru-Tech remains enforceable through lawful proceedings. No special mandate date, new filing or particular asset-recovery order is directed.

## Source Notes

The Fourth Circuit opinion, 39 F.3d 493, the petition and its district-court appendices, the merits briefs and the Joint Appendix supply the reviewed posture and monetary distinctions. The transfer findings and disputed valuation questions do not establish an asset award equal to the pension judgment. The $205,182.01 vacated amount combines attorneys' fees and expenses. No quoted decisional language is used.

Jones v. ABC-TV, No. 95-7186, 516 U.S. 363. Filing-management order, February 26, 1996. The Court maintains the financial-leave motion under consideration and reserves the financial-eligibility determination. All eight participating Justices—Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas and Ginsburg—support the pending treatment and refusal of the proposed present and prospective restrictions. ## Opinion Topology

| Writing | Author | Joined by | Relationship and scope |
|---|---|---|---|
| Opinion of the Court | Ginsburg | Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter and Thomas throughout; Stone-Zsela on pending financial treatment and the present Rule 39.8 showing | Eight Justices adopt the financial and current-paper propositions; seven adopt the record-specific prospective-control ground. |
| Concurrence in part and in the result concerning prospective control | Stone-Zsela | No other Justice | Joins the financial/current-paper explanation; agrees that no future condition should issue on a broader individual-consideration ground. |

## Holdings

**Financial eligibility remains reserved.** Ginsburg's opinion, joined on this proposition by all seven other participating Justices, keeps the financial motion pending without finding eligibility or ineligibility. Rule 39.1 requires the prescribed motion and notarized affidavit or §1746 declaration, unless a qualifying appointed-counsel exception applies; no such exception is established here. The references to monthly benefits, copying loans and an accompanying affidavit do not establish the required financial particulars. Neither does an incomplete evidentiary showing establish that Jones can pay, failed to submit a required document or made a false statement. The pending treatment adds no refiling duty, deadline or default consequence. A later financial ruling requires its own supported basis.

**The present showing does not establish the Rule 39.8 predicate.** The same eight Justices decline a present denial for frivolousness or malice. The underlying media-agreement, compulsory-publication and derivative §1986 theories have substantial defects, and formal assignment and actual appellate review contradict broad allegations of self-assignment and no review. But the petition also identifies distinct objections to actual leave requirements affecting paid pro se filings and denial of requested appellate briefing. The defects in the media theories do not dispose of those objections. Martin, Anderson and Attwood require assessment of the particular paper; ordinary pleading failure is not automatically frivolousness. The Court finds neither that the access allegations are true nor that the lower restrictions, recusal ruling or appellate procedure were unlawful. A supported Rule 39.8 denial remains available; the Rule permits rather than compels that action. Refusing this proposed denial establishes neither indigence nor entitlement to review.

**The proposed future condition lacks its independent supporting showing.** Ginsburg's opinion, joined by Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter and Thomas, applies Martin, Day, Sassower, Anderson and Attwood: a prospective condition needs support tied to the applicant, the relevant filing category and the inadequacy of individual treatment. The lower courts' abuse findings do not themselves establish those predicates for future noncriminal certiorari submissions here. Preserving paid and criminal routes narrows the proposal but does not supply the missing justification. The Court declines the condition on this record without categorically invalidating all prospective controls, requiring a warning in every case, fixing a numerical threshold or demanding exhaustion of every conceivable lesser measure.

## Precedent Treatment

Martin's present-paper distinction and separate prospective requirement, together with Anderson, Day, Sassower and Attwood, are applied. Zatko continues to permit supported denial of a particular frivolous or malicious filing. McDonald and Sindram retain their bounded authority for targeted protection of the judicial process. No prior filing-control holding is overruled or enlarged into a categorical immunity.

## Law After Decision

Current-paper assessment, financial eligibility, justification for future controls and certiorari remain separate inquiries. This order applies existing filing law, leaves the financial determination and petition pending, and creates no merits holding concerning the underlying suit or its procedural restrictions. The Court does not adopt the concurrence's broader future-filing rule.

## Separate Writings

Stone-Zsela concurs in the financial and current-paper treatment and agrees that the prospective condition should be refused. He would require individual consideration of future ordinary civil-certiorari submissions by an otherwise financially eligible litigant. Rule 39.8 addresses a frivolous or malicious submission; past abuse does not establish the contents of an unwritten petition. Automatic fee denial based on identity and history risks excluding a later legitimate claim solely because its author cannot pay. Inherent docket authority should implement the Rules' current-paper inquiry, while permitting prompt rejection of a submission actually shown defective.

His position goes beyond the Court's record-specific ground. It concerns ordinary civil-certiorari fee treatment, does not decide every extraordinary-writ restriction, vacate unreviewed orders or establish unlimited fee-free litigation in every court, and preserves jurisdictional, timeliness, form and current-paper controls. Individual consideration requires no merits opinion, hearing or elaborate explanation for every filing. Financial eligibility must still be established; once it is established and current frivolousness or malice is not shown, he would grant financial leave and consider certiorari separately. The missing financial proof is not itself ineligibility, and the pending treatment grants neither review nor relief against the respondents.

## Procedure After Action

The financial-leave motion and petition remain pending. Financial eligibility is reserved for a supported later determination. No date for that determination, direction to refile, payment interval or terminal petition action is ordered. Future submissions remain subject to ordinary rules and governing law.

The alleged proof in nineteen separate appendix volumes is not established by the record described here. ## Event

Voting Rights Coalition v. Wilson, petition identifier No. 95-673; Ninth Circuit No. 95-15449, 60 F.3d 1411. Decided February 26, 1996. On review of the Ninth Circuit's July 24, 1995 judgment affirming a March 2 permanent injunction requiring California to comply with the National Voter Registration Act of 1993 and remanding for implementation. The district court consolidated the injunction hearing with trial under Rule 65(a)(2).

The questions concern Congress's authority to require federal-election registration through motor-vehicle and designated public agencies despite California's Tenth Amendment and retained-qualification objections, including assignment of duties to employees previously performing other work. Presidential-election coverage requires its own constitutional ground. The Governor had directed implementation only to the extent federal funding was available; the resulting noncompliance produced this enforcement dispute. The United States asserts its own §11(a) claim for prospective relief.

## Participation

Chief Justice Stone-Zsela and Justices Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer participate.

## Public Action

The Ninth Circuit's judgment is affirmed in full, including the permanent injunction, implementation remand and retained appellate jurisdiction.

## Judgment & Remedy

**Affirmed, 9–0.** Supporting: Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer.

California must comply with the Act's federal-election requirements. The existing remand for implementation and the Ninth Circuit panel's retention of jurisdiction over subsequent proceedings and appeals remain. The district court must impose no burden unauthorized by the Act that impairs California's retained conduct of state elections and must approach implementation with due sensitivity to federalism. No provision is severed, no new administrative process is required, and no new ten-day deadline is started. The lower judgment's denial of appellate costs to every party remains undisturbed.

## Opinion Topology

O'Connor writes the Opinion of the Court, joined throughout by Stone-Zsela, Stevens, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer. All nine adopt each controlling proposition and its limits. No separate writing issues.

## Holdings

**Congress may require the challenged congressional-election registration duties.** The unanimous Court holds that Article I, §4 permits the NVRA's actual registration commands, including distribution, assistance, acceptance and transmittal duties assigned to motor-vehicle, public-assistance and disability-service personnel. Smiley includes registration within the manner power. Siebold permits partial federal alteration through shared state administration; a wholly federal apparatus is unnecessary. California correctly identifies real commands on employees whose prior duties differed, with consequences for resources and accountability. But Congress's express authority to make or alter registration procedures cannot depend on which office a State previously used. Receiving and transmitting applications remains electoral administration when performed at multipurpose agencies. The Act's in-home requirement likewise concerns registration services where the designated disability office already serves the person at home. These duties are not upheld on a finding that they are costless, trivial or voluntary. New York's bar against the ordinary-commerce command to regulate private waste generators or assume their obligations remains intact; this distinct constitutional allocation supplies the authority absent there. California's preference for reimbursement does not excuse compliance with these valid federal duties. No general power to direct state administration follows.

**Presidential registration rests on a separate constitutional basis.** The same unanimous opinion sustains the challenged safeguards for California's chosen popular presidential election under the national protective authority recognized by Burroughs, implemented through necessary-and-proper legislation. Article I, §4 alone does not cover presidential elections. Burroughs upheld corrupt-practices regulation of political committees, not these agency assignments; its principle is applied here because registration opportunities, eligibility attestations and reliable rolls protect eligible participation in selection of the national executive. The Act neither chooses the State's method of appointing electors nor selects or allocates them. The holding concerns these registration safeguards for the chosen popular election, not an unlimited power to redesign presidential selection or a conclusion that the congressional and presidential grants have identical scope.

**Registration procedure is distinct from substantive voter qualifications.** The same unanimous opinion rejects the facial contention that these prescribed registration methods themselves redefine who is qualified to vote. Article I, §2 and the Seventeenth Amendment retain their distinct qualification rules. The Act requires eligibility statements, including citizenship, and signed attestations under penalty of perjury; state election officials continue assessing eligibility. That does not leave every procedural rule untouched: the statute regulates permissible form information and maintenance procedures, and its federal mail form may not require notarization or other formal authentication. Term Limits preserves the distinction between genuine election administration and additional personal qualifications, but its candidate holding does not decide every voter-qualification dispute. Concrete controversies about required information, eligibility decisions or list maintenance remain open.

**The injunction has an independent public-enforcement foundation and retains its existing limits.** The same unanimous opinion holds that §11(a) authorizes the Attorney General's declaratory and injunctive enforcement action against the established categorical noncompliance. Private §11(b) notice conditions do not govern that action. California's immunity objection cannot erase the United States' independently authorized prospective enforcement by treating this as a private damages suit. The Court decides no damages liability or general private-enforcement immunity question and makes no finding that separate private notice conditions were met. The complexity of implementation requires fidelity to the statute and the existing remand limits; it does not justify vacating a permanent injunction without an identified unauthorized command. No such command is established here. The Fourteenth/Fifteenth Amendment enforcement alternative is preserved but not reached because the distinct congressional and presidential grounds suffice.

**Operative statutory limits preserved by the affirmance.** These are the enacted limits of the sustained duties, not newly created conditions:

- The Act governs federal elections. Section 4(b) exempts a State with no federal-election registration requirement under law continuously effective on and after March 11, 1993; it also exempts a State permitting every voter to register at the polling place during a general federal election under law continuously effective on and after that date, or enacted by that date to take effect on enactment of the NVRA and continuously maintained thereafter. Those conditions are not a present opt-out for California.
- Under §5(a), a driver's-license or renewal application serves as a federal voter-registration application unless the applicant fails to sign the registration application; a submitted registration application updates prior registration. Under the distinct §5(d) exception, a driver's-license change-of-address form serves as a voter-registration address change unless the registrant states that it is not for that purpose. The license-registration portion may not demand duplicate license information except a second signature or information necessary for the required eligibility statement and attestation, and may require only the minimum information needed to prevent duplicates and permit eligibility assessment and administration.
- Section 7 requires designation of all public-assistance offices and offices providing state-funded programs primarily serving persons with disabilities, as well as other registration offices. Federal and nongovernmental offices may be designated with their agreement. Designated offices must distribute, assist with and accept forms for transmittal; a designated disability office providing services at home must provide those registration services there. An applicant may refuse assistance. Distribution with the public-assistance or disability-service application, recertification, renewal or address-change form may be omitted when the applicant declines registration in writing; applicants who do not decline receive the same degree of assistance as with the office's own forms unless they refuse it. Failure to mark either registration-choice box counts as declination for the assistance provision. Staff may not seek to influence or display political preference, discourage registration, or suggest that registration choice affects services or benefits; declination and source information retain their statutory confidentiality protections.
- Sections 5(e) and 7(d) ordinarily require transmittal to the proper state election official within ten days after acceptance. When an application is accepted within five days before the last registration day for an election, transmittal is due within five days after acceptance. These are statutory duties, distinct from the district court's implementation-plan direction. State officials retain registration and roll decisions within the statute; the decision does not require identical federal and state rolls or determine a particular implementation practice.

## Precedent Treatment

Smiley and Siebold are applied to registration and shared administration under the express congressional-election allocation. New York's ordinary-commerce structural holding remains effective and is distinguished by that allocation, without converting every federal interest into authority to command States. Burroughs's national protective principle is applied separately to the challenged presidential-registration safeguards while preserving the State's choice of appointment method. Term Limits supplies the administration/qualification distinction within its actual candidate-rule scope; it is not expanded into a comprehensive voter-qualification decision. No prior holding is overruled.

## Law After Decision

The challenged NVRA agency-registration duties are valid in their federal-election scope. Their congressional and presidential coverage rests on distinct constitutional grounds. California must perform the prescribed work despite its reimbursement condition; federal-election authority creates no general power to command state government. Substantive eligibility and retained state-election administration remain distinct from federally regulated registration procedure. The facial decision preserves concrete statutory and constitutional implementation challenges and does not decide the alternative Fourteenth/Fifteenth Amendment theory.

## Separate Writings

No separate writing issues.

## Procedure After Action

The permanent injunction stands and the case proceeds through the already ordered implementation remand. The Ninth Circuit panel retains jurisdiction over subsequent proceedings and appeals. The lower order's original ten-day plan requirement is not restarted, and no compliance with that requirement is presumed. Separately reserved coalition claims concerning implementation remain for their proper proceedings; dismissal without prejudice of the Federal Election Commission and its chair is not converted into a merits judgment. No new plan, costs award, state-election redesign or finding about a later administrative practice is ordered.

## Source Notes

The Ninth Circuit opinion, 60 F.3d 1411, and the district judgment reproduced in the petition establish the permanent-injunction posture, federal enforcement claim, implementation remand and retained appellate jurisdiction. Pub. L. 103-31, 107 Stat. 77–89, supplies the enacted duties and exceptions; California's applicable effective date was January 1, 1995. The State's estimates of $18 million and 58 additional employees were disputed and are not findings adopted by the Court. Enacted legislative findings remain distinct from findings about California. The decision resolves facial validity without adjudicating a later implementation controversy.

## Event

Matsushita Electric Industrial Co. v. Epstein, No. 94-1809, 516 U.S. 367. Argued November 27, 1995; decided February 27, 1996. On writ of certiorari to the Ninth Circuit, 50 F.3d 644. The question is whether §1738 requires recognition of a Delaware class-settlement judgment releasing securities claims within exclusive federal jurisdiction under Exchange Act §27.

The Delaware acquisition litigation began in September 1990; the federal Epstein action began December 3, 1990, before either proposed settlement. The first settlement was rejected. The second provided a $2 million gross fund reduced by fees and an opt-out opportunity; the final award for fees and expenses was $250,000. The state judgment expressly incorporated a release identifying the pending federal litigation, found adequate representation and due process, and was affirmed by the Delaware Supreme Court. Notice, discovery, objections and an adversarial hearing occurred; the constitutional adequacy and binding effect of that process remain disputed.

The Ninth Circuit refused recognition because the federal claims rested on different underlying facts and could not have been extinguished by adjudication of the state claims. It reserved releases sharing the same predicate facts and did not decide the fact-specific constitutional alternative. Petitioners request immediate dismissal of covered non-opt-out complaints; respondents defend the appellate judgment and invoke representative-authority, due-process and binding-operation objections. Valid opt-outs, including the identified opt-out litigants outside this review, remain excluded.

## Participation

Chief Justice Stone-Zsela and Justices Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer participate at argument and decision.

## Public Action

The Court reverses the Ninth Circuit judgment and remands. Ordinary Delaware recognition is established, but the Court does not direct immediate dismissal of the federal complaints.

## Judgment & Remedy

**Reversed and remanded, 7–2. Supporting:** Stone-Zsela, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas and Breyer. **Opposing:** Stevens and Ginsburg, who would vacate and remand for an initial determination of Delaware preclusive effect as well as the unresolved constitutional and collateral-review questions.

The lower federal court must give the judgment its ordinary Delaware effect, subject to properly presented unresolved constitutional and binding objections and the antecedent extent to which those objections remain available collaterally. ## Holdings

**Section 1738 governs this kind of judgment.** A state judicial judgment incorporating a class settlement falls within §1738 even when it releases claims subject to exclusive federal adjudicatory jurisdiction. The federal court begins with the effect the rendering State gives that judgment; class form, consent disposition and exclusive jurisdiction alone supply no categorical exemption. All nine Justices adopt this rule in Part I.

**Controlling explanation:** Marrese requires the rendering-state-law inquiry and expressly leaves settlement releases distinct from the competency limit governing adjudicated claims. The Ninth Circuit could not replace that inquiry with a federal rule equating inability to adjudicate a claim with inability to approve its release. Exclusive federal jurisdiction likewise does not by itself establish that a lawfully authorized representative can never compromise a related federal claim. But an owner's capacity to settle and a representative's authority to bind absent persons remain different. Silence following notice is not individually negotiated consent, and expansive release language cannot supply constitutionally adequate representation where it is lacking. This conclusion neither grants state courts jurisdiction to adjudicate Exchange Act liability nor validates every asserted release.

**This judgment receives ordinary Delaware preclusive effect.** Part II, joined by Stone-Zsela, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas and Breyer, determines the ordinary state-law recognition question in petitioners' favor. The incorporated release and affirmed judgment cannot lose that effect merely because a federal litigant alleges state-law error in approving the settlement; federal constitutional binding limits remain unresolved.

**Controlling explanation:** Nottingham permits an authorized release of exclusively federal claims within its identical-factual-predicate and representative limits. The judgment here does not merely acknowledge a separate private bargain: it incorporates the release and expressly covers the identified federal actions. Final approval and state affirmance have the ordinary force Delaware gives them. The objection that the lower federal court should first examine state law does not require treating an affirmed judgment as open to renewed collateral correction of alleged state-law errors. Marrese supplies the correct inquiry, not an inflexible requirement that every application begin below.

This holding does not replace Nottingham's identical-factual-predicate requirement with a rule permitting release of anything connected to the same acquisition. Prezant's actual Rule 23 and express adequacy requirements remain part of Delaware law. The Court makes no new finding about tendering shareholders, counsel's incentives, or full and fair determination of constitutional adequacy. Ordinary state-law error and a legally available objection that a person was never constitutionally bound remain distinct.

**Section 27 does not impliedly partially repeal §1738 here.** Part III, joined by Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas and Breyer, rejects the asserted statutory exception for this category of judgment. Exclusive federal authority to adjudicate Exchange Act liability does not prohibit a state court with jurisdiction over the state action from approving an authorized representative release.

**Controlling explanation:** Marrese and Kremer require a supported basis for displacing §1738. Section 27's exclusive-jurisdiction language and the interest in uniform federal adjudication do not establish incompatibility with recognition of a lawful settlement. A settlement court may assess federal claim value without adjudicating federal securities liability. The release/adjudication distinction therefore preserves both statutes.

Recognition is also distinct from direct coercive interference with a pending federal case. Nottingham's refusal to enjoin such litigation does not invalidate every release, and the present judgment's incorporation does not validate every injunction or contempt remedy. No state wording removes federal jurisdiction. An independently preserved objection to the order's coercive operation remains subject to its own legally supported treatment; the Court neither decides it nor guarantees that it defeats recognition. Ginsburg agrees that exclusivity alone does not negate settlement capacity but reserves this further statutory-exception determination.

**Constitutional representation and unresolved review questions limit the remand.** All nine Justices adopt Part IV-A: §1738 cannot bind an absent member through constitutionally inadequate representation, and notice, objection opportunities and opt-out rights do not independently cure that failure. The actual constitutional adequacy and binding application remain undecided, so no immediate dismissal is ordered. In Part IV-B, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer leave the complete permissible scope of collateral review, including the asserted full-and-fair determination of adequacy, for lawful consideration below; the Chief Justice states his own structural-failure standard separately.

**Controlling explanation:** Hansberry and Shutts make representation a constitutional prerequisite, distinct from notice and exclusion rights. The state court's express findings, discovery and adversarial hearing matter; they are not erased by respondents' criticism. But the Ninth Circuit did not decide the fact-specific constitutional alternative, and a recital does not itself answer every disputed constitutional dimension. Properly presented unresolved grounds and the antecedent extent of their collateral availability may be considered. This remand finds neither that representation failed nor that every adequacy question may be relitigated despite a full and fair prior determination.

The Court decides no underlying tender-offer liability, private right under §§14(d)(6)–(7), new federal class, damages or unpresented securities count. Traditional contract consent as an independent dispositive theory remains unadjudicated; any legally applicable state contract approach would operate through §1738, without a federal fiction of individually negotiated assent. Valid opt-outs remain outside the judgment's release.

## Event

Norfolk & Western Railway Co. v. Hiles, No. 95-6, 516 U.S. 400. Argued January 8, 1996; decided February 27, 1996. On writ of certiorari to the Illinois Appellate Court, Fifth District, 268 Ill. App. 3d 561, 644 N.E.2d 508, after the Illinois Supreme Court denied review. The question is whether the Safety Appliance Act makes the railroad liable as a matter of law for Hiles's injury while manually realigning an off-center drawbar before coupling.

Hiles injured his back on July 18, 1990 while he and a coworker moved the drawbar. The other car remained four or five car lengths away; no impact had been attempted. The trial court directed statutory liability and excluded nondefect evidence as irrelevant under its rule. A jury awarded $492,500, and the appellate court affirmed. The railroad seeks reversal and remand. No independent ordinary-negligence count under FELA was pleaded.

## Participation

Chief Justice Stone-Zsela and Justices Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer participate at argument and decision.

## Public Action

The Court reverses the Illinois Appellate Court's judgment and remands for proceedings consistent with the statutory preparation/performance distinction.

## Judgment & Remedy

**Reversed and remanded, 9–0. Supporting:** Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer.

The directed statutory-liability ruling and its dependent $492,500 damages foundation cannot stand. Further proceedings must apply the correct rule to statutory-performance and causation issues that remain properly presented and procedurally available. The Court orders neither judgment for the railroad nor a guaranteed new trial or recovery; it supplies no new negligence count and revives no forfeited issue.

## Opinion Topology

Justice Thomas writes the Opinion of the Court. Chief Justice Stone-Zsela and Justices Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Ginsburg and Breyer join in full. No separate opinion is issued.

## Holdings

**Common authority:** Thomas's opinion and all eight complete joins identified above directly support each following holding, its application and limits. No combination of separate rationales is required.

**Ordinary preparation is distinct from failure of required performance.** Former 45 U.S.C. §2 imposes an absolute duty that coupling equipment perform its statutory function when properly set; ordinary, nondefective lateral displacement requiring drawbar alignment does not alone violate that duty. The Act does not categorically prohibit every preparatory task between cars or separately require automatic-realignment equipment.

**Controlling explanation:** The governing text required couplers coupling automatically by impact and capable of uncoupling without workers going between the car ends. Affolder recognizes that automatic coupling presupposes proper setting; Carter preserves that qualification while rejecting an ordinary-care defense. The same distinction applies to preparing a nondefective drawbar as to opening a knuckle. O'Donnell's absolute-performance holding remains effective when an engaged coupler fails.

Hiles correctly emphasizes that the Act protects workers from manual-coupling dangers and that mechanically sound parts can still fail to perform. But that principle does not make every injury during preparatory work a statutory violation. Lateral play accommodates curves, and the enacted duty does not itself require a mechanism eliminating every manual alignment task. Neither customary practice nor a general appeal to safety decides whether this appliance was awaiting ordinary preparation or had failed its required function. The 1994 recodification at 49 U.S.C. §20302(a)(1)(A) adds no substantive requirement governing this 1990 injury.

**Performance-based proof and ordinary-setting proof remain distinct.** An employee may establish a violation by failure to perform the required function without proving negligence or identifying a particular mechanical defect. The railroad may establish that the complained-of condition instead resulted from ordinary nondefective misalignment before proper setting; once a statutory violation contributing to injury is proved, due care, inspection, later successful operation, contributory negligence or assumption of risk does not erase it.

**Controlling explanation:** Affolder and Carter make actual performance decisive within the proper-setting qualification. The railroad's permissible explanation concerns whether the statutory duty was breached; it is not a safe harbor for careful inspection after a breach. Nor can “preparation” excuse a malfunction simply by relabeling it. A statutory violation must have contributed to the employee's injury within the applicable FELA remedial framework, but causation does not supply a missing violation. No attempted impact is invariably required: the Act does not demand a futile or damaging collision before an otherwise provable failure may be shown. Conversely, an attempted coupling would not by itself establish Hiles's right to a directed liability ruling on this record.

**The per se liability direction requires reversal without an appellate finding for the railroad.** Injury during realignment does not establish statutory liability as a matter of law. The reason, extent and legal classification of this displacement remain for lawful consideration under the correct distinction, together with any properly preserved performance and causation issues.

**Controlling explanation:** The general need for lateral movement does not prove that this particular condition was ordinary or nondefective. The inspection account and asserted AAR compliance were excluded affidavit and proffer evidence, not findings. The difficulty moving the bar, the uncoupling evidence and the disputed curve inference also cannot be resolved merely from the trial court's erroneous rule. Wagner and Parker preserve assessment of the actual displacement and performance evidence. The absence of a prior impact and the absence of an identified broken component do not justify this Court's entering final judgment for the railroad. Reversal removes the directed-liability foundation of the damages judgment while leaving only procedurally available issues for further proceedings. It neither manufactures the unpleaded negligence claim nor guarantees another trial.

## Precedent Treatment

- Affolder v. New York, Chicago & St. Louis Railroad Co., 339 U.S. 96: applies its proper-setting qualification to ordinary nondefective drawbar alignment, retaining absolute performance once the appliance is properly set.
- Carter v. Atlanta & St. Andrews Bay Railway Co., 338 U.S. 430: preserves performance-based proof without negligence or identification of a particular defect and the separate causal requirement; ordinary FELA comparative-negligence principles do not excuse a proven statutory violation.
- O'Donnell v. Elgin, Joliet & Eastern Railway Co., 338 U.S. 384: leaves intact the absolute duty when an engaged coupler breaks; preparation supplies no defense to such an established failure.
- San Antonio & Aransas Pass Railway Co. v. Wagner, 241 U.S. 476, and Atlantic City Railroad Co. v. Parker, 242 U.S. 56: preserve consideration of the actual evidence bearing on drawbar displacement and failure, rather than making every realignment injury conclusive.
- Consolidated Rail Corp. v. Gottshall, 512 U.S. 532: its distinct negligent-emotional-injury rule remains unchanged; its negligence and zone-of-danger requirements do not govern this statutory-performance claim.

An injury during that preparation is insufficient by itself; a proven failure of required performance remains actionable without negligence or identification of a particular mechanical defect. Violation and causal contribution remain separate. The decision establishes no general exemption for misaligned drawbars, inspection safe harbor, universal impact prerequisite, new technology mandate or broader operating prohibition.

## Event

## Holdings

**Protected broadcasting and the applicable review.** Nonobscene indecent expression is protected. A restriction selecting that content must be carefully tailored to a compelling interest, with independent review of its necessity and means in light of broadcasting's actual characteristics. **Controlling explanation:** Pacifica permits the particular contextual regulation it sustained; it does not validate every general daily restriction. Sable requires protection of adult access and careful tailoring while preserving material differences between broadcasting and telephone services. Legislative predictive judgments receive substantial consideration, but asserted harm and the selected response remain subject to independent review. Neither scientific certainty nor a particular legislative report is indispensable. Turner preserves that judicial responsibility; its intermediate test for content-neutral cable carriage is not the test for this content-selected rule.

**The mandatory sixteen-hour schedule fails on this record.** The prescribed 6 a.m.–10 p.m. exclusion is insufficiently tailored to justify its broad burden on protected adult broadcasting. Ginsburg's Part II, joined by Stone-Zsela, O'Connor, Kennedy and Thomas, supplies five direct votes for final invalidity.

**Controlling explanation:** The record establishes substantial child access, including late audiences, and difficulties of supervision and rapid tuning. It does not adequately connect those circumstances to excluding this broad category of protected expression across sixteen hours, media and contexts. A child somewhere in the audience cannot by itself settle the necessary degree of adult exclusion. Late-night audiences, taping and purchased alternatives do not adequately answer practical access for adults relying on free broadcasting or the burden on parental choice. The FCC considered alternatives; the defect is their insufficient treatment and the sweep of the resulting rule, not the absence of any evidence or analysis.

The agency's contextual definition, attention to reasonable licensee judgment and clinical or bona fide news applications limit the burden but create no blanket news or serious-merit exemption. Its tentative defense permits market-wide evidence of no appreciable child audience at the relevant time; ordinary station/program ratings remain inadequate until convincing evidence establishes relevance despite grazing. Those qualifications do not cure the schedule's general fit, establish an automatic low-rating exemption or decide any individual defense.

Audience measures remain distinct. Receiver ownership shows opportunity; aggregate viewing does not establish actual indecent-program exposure. Radio quarter-hour audiences are not cumulative reach, and limited under-twelve radio data cannot be supplied by inference. Adult presence is not effective supervision; public radio's small teen listening share and weekly reach are not zero child audience. The holding requires neither proof of harm from every program nor flawless alternatives, and does not declare all broadcasting interchangeable with other media.

**The relief removes this rule while preserving lawful authority.** The appellate approval must be set aside and the D.C. Circuit must remove the corresponding rule and prevent §16(a)'s dependent command from requiring the invalid schedule. Ginsburg's Part III, joined by Stone-Zsela, O'Connor, Kennedy and Thomas, supplies five votes for that operative direction. The existing 10 p.m.–6 a.m. protection remains intact with all nine Justices' agreement.

**Controlling explanation:** Extending the 10 p.m. opening removed the unjustified classification but did not answer petitioners' independent breadth challenge. The Government's absence of a petition forecloses restoring the harsher decree through an alternative defense. The constitutional defect calls for removing the challenged mandate, not judicial selection of a new national opening hour. Section 16(b)'s repeal remains effective. Section 1464, Pacifica and ordinary obscenity enforcement retain their lawful scope; more confined FCC action must satisfy those authorities and today's ruling. No unexamined severability provision or statutory exception creates a substitute schedule. A renewed-record remand leaving validity open does not command five votes, and no Marks aggregation makes Souter's separate position the governing remedy.

The Court does not independently resolve facial vagueness, grant an automatic serious-merit exemption, adjudicate any enforcement sanction, overrule Pacifica or Red Lion, require station-by-station permission hearings, or prescribe a new multifactor test. Section 551 creates no present device-based factual ground for the judgment and does not automatically repeal §16, moot the case or require remand.

## Precedent Treatment

- Sable Communications of California, Inc. v. FCC, 492 U.S. 115: applies protection of adult nonobscene speech, careful tailoring and independent review; preserves the distinction between telephone services and broadcasting.

## Law After Decision

Effective March 4, 1996, the mandatory uniform 6 a.m.–10 p.m. indecency schedule may not be enforced, and the D.C. Circuit must implement its removal. The already favorable 10 p.m.–6 a.m. protection continues. The Court adds a controlling, record-based limit on the mandatory schedule; it does not withdraw all authority to protect children through lawful, more confined broadcast regulation. Adult speech remains protected, Pacifica retains its actual contextual force and ordinary obscenity enforcement survives. No new general definition or replacement safe-harbor hour is prescribed.

Section 551's parental-choice provisions remain subject to their enacted conditions. Its findings are legislative findings, not proof of a presently effective alternative. The rating amendment takes effect one year after February 8, 1996 only upon the FCC's determination, after consultation with appropriate public-interest groups and interested private-sector individuals, that acceptable voluntary ratings and voluntary transmission agreements have not been established. The rating paragraph does not authorize political or religious ratings. The advisory committee must comprise parents, television broadcasters, television programming producers, cable operators, appropriate public-interest groups and other interested private-sector individuals, fairly balanced in political affiliation, represented viewpoints and committee functions. The FCC must supply staff and resources necessary for efficient and prompt performance and require a final recommendations report within one year of initial appointments.

The manufacturing requirement applies on an FCC-selected date, after consultation with the television manufacturing industry, no earlier than two years after enactment to covered apparatus with screens at least thirteen inches diagonally manufactured in the United States or shipped in interstate commerce. Carriers merely transporting without trading are excepted. FCC rules must provide Commission oversight of industry's adoption of blocking-technology standards and require covered apparatus to receive line-21 rating signals conforming to industry specifications established under Commission supervision. An FCC determination that alternative technology enables parental blocking by identifying programs without ratings, is comparably priced and blocks broadly across a multichannel system as effectively and easily requires permitting either technology. No such determination, implemented nationwide ratings/blocking system, household penetration, cost/effectiveness finding or radio counterpart is established. These provisions neither repeal §16 nor independently resolve this challenge.

## Separate Writings

**Souter, concurring in part and dissenting in part.** Souter joins the content-based standard, independent review and Pacifica's continued limits. He would vacate approval of the actual schedule for renewed examination of its boundary, contextual reach, practical adult opportunities and alternatives. **Stevens, dissenting, joined by Scalia and Breyer.** The dissent would affirm the actual uniform decree under demanding review. Substantial child access, rapid tuning, multiple receivers, the meaningful 10 p.m. harbor and evidence-sensitive contextual defenses together justify this schedule. The dissent accepts protected adult speech and independent review, but finds the majority gives insufficient weight to the remaining opportunities and the practical difficulty of protecting children without timed channeling. It requires neither a formal legislative report nor scientific certainty, makes no finding that every program harms children, and adopts no general lower-value hierarchy. No foolproof-alternative condition, operational V-chip or automatic station-rating exemption supplies its conclusion. Its proposed affirmance preserves the favorable nighttime protection, actual defenses and properly supported future challenges.

## Event

**Bennis v. Michigan, No. 94-8729 — March 4, 1996.** Argued November 29, 1995. On review under 28 U.S.C. §1257 of the Michigan Supreme Court's final judgment, 447 Mich. 719, 527 N.W.2d 483 (1994), reversing the Court of Appeals and reinstating nuisance abatement of the jointly owned automobile. The questions concern due-process protection of an uninvolved co-owner's interest and whether the Takings Clause independently requires compensation. Bennis seeks protection of her interest or constitutionally required relief; Michigan seeks affirmance.

John Bennis used the car to convey the participants and as the place of the prohibited sexual act in an area with an existing prostitution nuisance. The state lead opinion assumes that Tina neither knew of nor consented to the illegal use, while treating ordinary shared use as voluntary. It does not establish all reasonably expected preventive action. Earlier encounters do not establish repeated prohibited use of this particular car. Officers observed no payment; the state lead opinion relies on lewdness associated with prostitution and alternatively circumstantial proof. The Court makes no additional state-law offense finding.

Riley's lead rationale had three votes; Griffin concurred in the result, providing the fourth vote for the Michigan judgment. The Court reviews that judgment without attributing all of the lead reasoning to a state-court majority. A contested hearing and equitable consideration of alternatives occurred. No precise ownership fraction, valuation or sale accounting is established for the relief ordered here.

## Participation

Chief Justice Stone-Zsela and Justices Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer participated at argument and decision. All nine participate in the judgment.

## Public Action

The Court vacates the judgment insofar as it sustains forfeiture of Tina Bennis's ownership interest, and remands for determination of the qualified due-process protection and appropriate relief.

## Judgment & Remedy

**Vacated in part and remanded, 5–4.** Supporting: Stone-Zsela, Stevens, Kennedy, Souter and Breyer. Opposing: O'Connor, Scalia, Thomas and Ginsburg, who would affirm rejection of both reviewed constitutional objections.

The state court must afford Tina a meaningful opportunity to establish her actual ownership, nonparticipation, lack of knowledge and all reasonably expected prevention, and must decide those predicates. If she establishes the defense, her proven interest must be preserved or restored through release, division, sale accounting or another lawful property remedy reflecting established ownership and value. No immediate return, half share, fixed gross or net payment, mandatory sale, particular expense allocation or independent compensation award is ordered. The Court does not finally sustain retention of her interest before the required determination.

## Holdings

### A qualified owner defense applies to this nuisance forfeiture

**Holding and operative rule:** Due process forbids forfeiture of a co-owner's separately held interest in an ordinarily lawful automobile on account of another's prohibited use when the claimant establishes that she neither participated in nor knew of that use and did all that reasonably could be expected in the circumstances to prevent it. Ordinary co-ownership and permission for shared lawful use do not conclusively defeat that protection; knowingly tolerated misuse, culpable entrustment and omitted reasonably expected precautions fall outside it.

**Authority:** Stone-Zsela's Opinion of the Court, Part I, joined by Stevens, Kennedy, Souter and Breyer. Five Justices directly adopt the rule, its claimant burden, application and limits; no combination of separate rationales is necessary.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that the owner's interest cannot be taken solely because she shared lawful use with the person who committed the wrong, if she establishes both uninvolvement and all reasonably expected prevention. Goldsmith-Grant and Van Oster sustain forfeiture despite ignorance in their unlawful-use and voluntary-entrustment settings. Calero-Toledo likewise sustains forfeiture against an unaware entrusting owner but reserves the materially different case in which the owner proves the additional preventive circumstances. The Court now adopts that reserved substantive protection for the interest at issue; it does not describe the reservation as an earlier holding.

The State's deterrence and anti-collusion interests support requiring the claimant to establish the protection. They do not justify treating ordinary shared use as conclusive against an owner who proves she could reasonably have done no more. The car's use to convey the participants and as the place of the act permits invocation of forfeiture law, but does not itself resolve the constitutional treatment of each owner's interest. A neighborhood nuisance and the owner's personal knowledge or preventability are different matters.

The claimant bears the showing. Reasonable prevention means practical precautions in the actual circumstances, not constant supervision, omniscience or prevention at any imaginable cost. Neither ignorance, marriage nor joint title creates a presumption that the defense succeeds; joint title also does not establish that an available reasonable precaution was omitted. Ordinary state factfinding rules apply, subject to a meaningful opportunity to establish the protection. The Court creates no numerical or heightened proof standard and no universal obligation on the State to disprove every owner's innocence.

Austin addresses punitive forfeitures under the Excessive Fines Clause; Good addresses seizure process; Buena Vista applies a statutory defense. None already decides this substantive question. The rule neither immunizes every unaware owner nor decides materially different contraband, maritime or involuntary-taking cases, or a comprehensive constitutional instrumentality test.

**Precedent treatment:** Calero-Toledo's reasonable-prevention reservation is adopted as a substantive due-process protection in this setting; its actual forfeiture holding survives. Goldsmith-Grant and Van Oster remain applicable outside the newly protected category. Austin, Good and Buena Vista retain their distinct constitutional or statutory scope.

### Lawful forfeiture and compensable acquisition remain distinct

**Holding and operative rule:** A constitutionally and statutorily lawful forfeiture does not automatically require an additional compensation payment under the Takings Clause. That proposition cannot establish the lawfulness of forfeiting this claimant's interest before the due-process defense is resolved, and it supplies no independent compensation judgment here.

**Authority:** Stone-Zsela's Opinion of the Court, Part II, joined by Stevens, Kennedy, Souter and Breyer, controls with five direct joins. O'Connor, Scalia, Thomas and Ginsburg independently accept the lawful-forfeiture premise in dissent but would sustain this forfeiture; they do not join Part II's application or the Court's opinion.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that valid forfeiture is distinct from acquisition requiring a separate payment simply because an owner loses property. Calero-Toledo rejects the uncompensated-taking challenge to the forfeiture it sustains. Fuller concerns value attributable to revocable federal grazing privileges; it does not exempt every exercise of governmental power from compensation requirements. Michigan therefore correctly rejects an automatic compensation rule for every forfeiture, but cannot establish the validity of taking Tina's interest by naming the proceeding a forfeiture. If she proves the due-process defense, her interest must be protected through lawful property relief because it cannot be forfeited on this ground. That is not an award of compensation after authorizing the taking. If she fails to establish the defense and the forfeiture is otherwise lawful, the forfeiture alone does not generate an additional payment. The Court fixes no amount and does not sustain retention of her interest before the required determination.

**Precedent treatment:** Calero-Toledo's lawful-forfeiture/takings distinction is applied within its premise of a valid forfeiture. Fuller's separate valuation holding remains confined to its stated setting and supplies no general immunity from the Takings Clause.

### Unresolved protection predicates require a limited remand

**Holding and operative rule:** The judgment is vacated insofar as it sustains forfeiture of Tina Bennis's interest without determining the newly recognized defense; the state court must afford a meaningful opportunity to establish her actual ownership, nonparticipation, lack of knowledge and all reasonably expected prevention, and decide those predicates. If she succeeds, it must preserve or restore her proven interest through a lawful limitation of forfeiture or corresponding property relief; if she fails, this defense does not prevent otherwise valid abatement.

**Authority:** Stone-Zsela's Opinion of the Court, Part III, joined by Stevens, Kennedy, Souter and Breyer, supplies five direct votes for the application, remedy and limits.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that the missing prevention determination must be made below. The state lead opinion assumes that Tina lacked knowledge of and did not consent to the illegal use; it does not establish every element of the newly recognized defense. Nor does the existing hearing establish that reasonable precautions were available and omitted. Recognition of the defense makes those unresolved matters legally material. The Court neither finds Tina entitled to return nor treats hypothetical control of joint property as a finding against her.

The court may use existing usable evidence and conduct further proceedings to the extent needed to determine the defense; the mandate does not require discarding the record or automatically repeating the entire hearing. Buena Vista distinguishes eligibility to invoke protection from proof of entitlement, although its particular defense is statutory. Good's prior-process holding supplies no automatic final title remedy, and Libretti distinguishes lawful forfeiture authority and protected third-party interests from consent or procedural labels without importing its federal statutory scheme here.

If protection is established, release, division, sale accounting or another lawful property remedy must reflect the ownership and value actually established. The Court prescribes no ownership fraction, gross or net sum, mandatory sale, particular expense allocation or eminent-domain award. It does not transfer John's interest to Tina, undo his conviction, or defeat lawful forfeiture of his own interest. Existing state equitable authority is a means of implementing lawful relief; its discretionary exercise cannot replace protection once the constitutional predicates are established. No new state-law statutory ruling or independent Excessive Fines adjudication follows.

**Precedent treatment:** Buena Vista's distinction between eligibility and factual entitlement informs the confined remand without converting its statutory defense into this constitutional rule. Good's process holding and Libretti's statutory-authority and third-party boundaries remain distinct and effective.

## Separate Writings

**O'Connor, dissenting, joined by Scalia, Thomas and Ginsburg.** The dissent would affirm the Michigan judgment on the reviewed due-process and takings objections. Goldsmith-Grant, Van Oster and Calero-Toledo sustain forfeiture despite ignorance where the owner voluntarily made property available and it was actually used unlawfully. Here the car both transported the participants and provided the place of the act; this is not a decision about a remote, fortuitous association with crime. Assumed lack of knowledge does not establish the additional reasonable-prevention category, and Calero-Toledo's reservation does not require its adoption as a new ground for remand.

Austin's treatment of punitive forfeiture, Buena Vista's enacted defense and Good's seizure-process protection do not already create the constitutional immunity sought. The dissent preserves those actual limits, statutory authority and constitutionally required process. It does not find that Tina omitted an available precaution, decide a proven all-reasonable-prevention case, or authorize forfeiture of every property loosely associated with wrongdoing. Because it sustains this forfeiture, the separate compensation claim fails; neither an invalid forfeiture nor every governmental acquisition is thereby exempted from constitutional scrutiny.

**Thomas, dissenting separately and joining O'Connor.** Settled entrusted-property forfeiture supports affirmance, but the absence of a personal-fault requirement makes historical limits on what counts as an instrumentality especially important. A government may not make those limits disappear simply by calling property a nuisance. This car's admitted transport and use provide a sufficient basis for applying Van Oster without relying on an instrumentality concession. Thomas does not establish a comprehensive nexus test, adopt Scalia's earlier exclusive excessiveness formula or characterize every historical forfeiture as personal punishment. Possible remedial costs do not supply a finding that this forfeiture was exclusively remedial or constitutionally costless to Tina.

**Ginsburg, dissenting separately and joining O'Connor.** The voluntary sharing of a jointly owned vehicle and actual equitable consideration distinguish this application from an unrestricted power to punish innocent third parties. The trial court considered alternatives; the qualitative cost concern and reference to another family automobile supply no verified expense calculation or finding of substitute transportation. She would apply the inherited entrustment rule to assumed ignorance here, while reserving the materially different owner who proves all reasonably expected prevention and different exorbitant applications.

A hearing does not cure every substantive constitutional defect, and low property value does not erase an owner's rights. Equitable discretion is material context, not a mandatory federal offset or an answer if constitutionally required owner protection is established. Her conclusion rests on the existing forfeiture rule in this setting, without a precise ownership fraction, an unverified concession about the vehicle or a newly found valuation.

## Holdings

**Authority for all holdings:** Justice Souter's Opinion of the Court, Parts I–IV, joined throughout by Chief Justice Stone-Zsela and Justices Scalia, Kennedy, Thomas and Ginsburg. These six direct joins establish the following rules and their stated limits; no combination of separate opinions is required.

**An actual authorized contractual undertaking is necessary.** Under 28 U.S.C. §1491(a)(1), the asserted contract claim must rest on an express or implied-in-fact undertaking that the Government was authorized to make. The Tucker Act supplies jurisdiction, not the substantive reimbursement promise; fairness alone cannot supply an obligation implied only in law.

**Controlling explanation:** Sutton v. United States applies limits on governmental contracting authority to implied as well as express agreements. Hatzlachh Supply Co. v. United States preserves the separate point that the absence of a tort remedy does not itself defeat an actual contract claim. Both rules apply: the Government cannot escape a genuine contractual obligation merely by invoking tort immunity, and a claimant cannot create the necessary promise by calling equitable reimbursement a contract. An ordinary authorized warranty does not become invalid merely because its possible breach damages were uncertain when the parties contracted. The decision rests on the asserted undertaking's scope, not a finding that a particular appropriation, delegation or required approval was absent from these contracts.

**The asserted specifications warranty does not itself cover these third-party costs.** The implication arising from the Government's furnishing specifications assures satisfactory performance of the procurement undertaking; it does not, without more, promise reimbursement for these manufacturers' settlements of veterans' tort claims or their expenses of defending those claims. Both petitioners' warranty-based settlement and defense-expense claims therefore fail on this legal ground.

**Controlling explanation:** United States v. Spearin ties the specifications warranty and the consequences of its breach to the performance the purchaser prescribed. Its protection against defective specifications does not by itself make the purchaser an insurer of the supplier's liabilities to persons outside the contract. Ryan Stevedoring and Weyerhaeuser v. Nacirema recognize that a particular contractual service duty can carry foreseeable third-party consequences without an express indemnity clause. They require identification of that duty; they do not establish this additional promise merely because the Government specified and purchased the product. Hatzlachh preserves contract remedies but supplies no missing term.

The manufacturers correctly distinguish contractual loss from tort liability. That distinction does not establish the coverage they claim. Even taking the material allegations favorably for this legal inquiry, the asserted specifications implication does not undertake these losses. Defense expenses receive a separate disposition: they can exist despite a successful tort defense, but the same absent undertaking defeats their recovery here. The Court does not hold that every contractual damage ends at delivery, that third-party losses always require express indemnity, or that every such expense is categorically barred by Kania. A different demonstrated, authorized undertaking remains possible. The Federal Circuit's government-contractor-defense causation equation is unnecessary to the judgment and is not decided.

**Compulsion and statutory protection do not establish the asserted indemnity.** Thompson's preserved claim that the contracting circumstances imply a reimbursement undertaking fails: compulsory procurement, specifications, alleged control and superior knowledge, and the Defense Production Act's liability protection, considered together, do not establish the asserted authorized agreement to bear these third-party settlement and defense costs. Section 707 itself supplies no affirmative reimbursement promise.

**Controlling explanation:** Section 101 authorizes priorities and compelled performance. Section 707 protects against specified damages or penalties arising from compliance. A command to produce and protection against liability are different from governmental assent to pay a supplier's later litigation costs. Thompson relies on the combined contracting setting, not merely a private statutory cause of action. But the cumulative asserted inference still lacks the claimed undertaking: control and knowledge do not turn the procurement command into an indemnity promise. To impose the obligation solely because the suppliers should fairly be relieved of the loss would exceed the Tucker Act's actual-contract boundary.

The actual-authority principle in Sutton, the appropriations limits and the existence of specific authorized indemnity mechanisms reinforce caution against readily inferring an open-ended commitment. They do not establish that every indemnity is forbidden, that any particular officer lacked a proven delegation, or that special contracting authority was exercised here. Genuine authorized indemnities remain enforceable on their terms. The Court does not decide §707's full immunity boundary, including particular product-injury or accelerated-production situations, and creates no separate statutory reimbursement action.

**Scope and questions not reached:** Both petitioners' preserved specifications-warranty claims are decided. Thompson's separate contextual implied-in-fact indemnity is decided; Hercules's compulsion allegations do not reopen a separate indemnity claim not preserved below. Knowledge, control and compulsion may be considered as subsidiary arguments within the preserved claims, but no freestanding superior-knowledge, purchaser-use, good-faith or takings cause is newly adjudicated. No finding is made concerning defective specifications, relative knowledge, veterans' injuries, an actual warranty breach, the reasonableness or allocation of claimed damages, or contract-specific authorization. No claim for fees incurred prosecuting this contract action is awarded. The absence of an express indemnity does not establish a universal express-clause rule.

## Precedent Treatment

- United States v. Spearin, 248 U.S. 132: applies the specifications warranty to its actual performance undertaking; the warranty alone does not establish the additional third-party-loss coverage asserted here. Its ordinary protection and damages for breach remain intact.
- Sutton v. United States, 256 U.S. 575: applies the requirement of actual governmental authority equally to express and implied-in-fact undertakings; no new finding about an individual contract's appropriation or delegation follows.
- Hatzlachh Supply Co. v. United States, 444 U.S. 460: applies the independence of genuine contract remedies from tort immunity and the distinction between actual contracts and obligations implied in law.

The Government's alternative may defend its existing favorable judgment, but no new factual findings or independent unpresented causes are added.

Its language is not adopted as a universal bar to third-party expenses resulting from breach of an authorized procurement contract. No earlier Supreme Court holding is overruled.

## Holdings

### The Town may specify disposal as part of the service it purchases

**Holding and operative rule:** A municipality purchasing residential waste collection and disposal may specify the disposal facility as part of that purchased performance. Payment through a hauler, separate price items and use of a privately owned facility do not make the disposal component an unrelated downstream transaction. The rule reaches the service actually bought; contractual form or participation in another market alone supplies no authority over unrelated business.

**Authority:** Kennedy's Opinion of the Court, Part I, joined by the eight other participating Justices identified in Opinion Topology. All nine adopt this purchased-performance application and its limits.

**Controlling explanation:** White permits conditions within the public performance a government buys and rejects an inflexible boundary at direct contractual privity. Reeves supplies the distinction between participation in a commercial transaction and regulation of others. Smithtown pays for final disposal as well as collection. Its purchased service remains incomplete when SSC merely collects the waste. The disposal allowance, retained volume savings and excess-volume risk do not erase that component.

SSC's strongest objection is that collection and disposal have separate suppliers and prices and that the destination term protects the same private processor favored by the ordinance. Those circumstances require examining the transaction's substance. They do not prevent a purchaser from buying services in both markets. The four-Justice downstream discussion in South-Central Timber warns against using a completed sale to control a purchaser's later, separate business. Its concern does not invalidate actual paid disposal still to be performed. The Court neither treats that plurality as a prior majority holding nor endorses every condition a government can place in a contract.

West Lynn Creamery requires attention to a linked program's operation rather than its labels. That examination reveals a genuine purchased component here; invalidity of the separate ordinance does not erase it. Boston Harbor reinforces the distinction for genuine purchases in its labor-preemption setting, but the present Commerce Clause ruling rests on the applicable participant authorities. Government need not own the incinerator or employ every performer of the service it buys. The Court decides no restriction on unpaid, unrelated subsequent transactions and no materially different public waste system.

**Precedent treatment:** White's purchased-performance and privity principles are applied; Reeves's participant distinction remains within its scope. South-Central Timber's downstream plurality is distinguished without enlarging its authority. Carbone's reservation of genuine purchasing receives this specific application; Boston Harbor retains its separate labor-preemption scope.

### The separate compulsory route remains invalid under Carbone

**Holding and operative rule:** The Town may not use the challenged ordinance to reserve independently arranged private waste-processing transactions to the designated local private operator. Under Carbone, that discriminatory reservation requires a legitimate local purpose that reasonable nondiscriminatory alternatives cannot adequately serve. Smithtown's financing commitments and sanitation interests do not establish that justification on this record.

**Authority:** Kennedy's Opinion of the Court, Part II, joined by all eight other participating Justices. O'Connor and Souter join this application of existing Carbone without withdrawing their prior classification objections. Nine Justices directly support the present application and invalidation; no combination of separate rationales is necessary.

**Controlling explanation:** Carbone governs compulsory reservation of private processing services, including where other local processors are excluded along with interstate competitors. Smithtown's ordinance reaches commercial and other covered waste beyond the residential disposal the Town buys. The Town's investment and capacity arrangements do not make those independent customers' purchases its own or make Ogden's facility publicly owned.

Criminal fines and imprisonment identify the sovereign mechanism compelling patronage here. They are not the entire constitutional test: ordinary lawful health regulation may also carry penalties. The defect is the discriminatory command over private transactions outside the purchased service. The reported exclusions for recyclables and unacceptable waste do not remove that defect from the covered private demand.

The Town's strongest defense is that dependable waste flow protects financing and ensures sanitary service. Those legitimate interests do not show why reasonable neutral health regulation, lawful purchasing and nondiscriminatory financing cannot adequately serve them. Carbone and Oregon Waste require that justification; ordinary Pike balancing does not replace it after the controlling discrimination rule applies. The Court creates no perfect-certainty requirement or duty to defeat every imaginable alternative, and selects no replacement program. Neither state encouragement of the project nor the contractual financial commitment supplies congressional authorization to suspend the Commerce Clause rule. The existing Carbone classification controls this case without reconsideration of its original theoretical basis.

**Precedent treatment:** Carbone's private-processing rule and justification requirement are applied without change, with Oregon Waste's distinction between discrimination and justification intact. West Lynn's scrutiny of linked arrangements does not make the Town's genuine purchases identical to the ordinance's compulsion. Pike remains available for genuinely evenhanded incidental burdens; no separate Pike holding is made here.

**Remedial scope:** Part III, joined by all nine Justices, affirms both appellate components while preserving only properly remaining ordinary proceedings. Invalidation of the ordinance neither rescinds the entire contract nor establishes damages. No antitrust immunity, actual CERCLA liability, general subcontract rule or treatment of a materially different public system is decided.
