## Components and common limits

**Recognition:** The affirmative path protects confidential communications in diagnosis or treatment by a psychotherapist under Rule 501's enacted reason-and-experience authority. State experience and the relationship's dependence on frank disclosure support a defined treatment privilege. The contrary path regards the asserted treatment benefit and uneven state boundaries as insufficient justification for suppressing relevant evidence. The draft chooses the first path for Stevens, O'Connor, Kennedy, Souter, Ginsburg and Breyer, and the second for Scalia and Thomas. These are independently selected positions, not a Court judgment or desired coalition.

**Professional coverage:** The affirmative path includes this licensed clinical social worker's actual psychotherapy, with her relevant training and treatment function. It does not cover every social worker, employer conversation or unlicensed confidant. Restricting protection to psychiatrists or psychologists would withhold it from the same therapeutic function without a demonstrated distinction material to confidentiality. The two nonrecognition positions do not independently decide every profession's eligibility under a privilege they reject.

**Balancing:** For a recognized confidential-treatment privilege, routine case-by-case weighing of evidentiary need against privacy would make its protection unknowable when disclosure must occur. The contrary path uses balancing to protect adjudicative access, invoking Nixon's resistance to absolute secrecy. The affirmative positions distinguish the executive/criminal-process setting and protect the defined communication category without deciding every possible exception. No dangerous-patient exception, litigation-use exception, complete waiver or criminal defendant's constitutional claim is adjudicated. Rejecting general balancing does not pronounce the privilege exceptionless.

**Instruction and remedy:** The affirmative positions sustain the Seventh Circuit's new-trial judgment, while rejecting its general balancing formulation. The actual adverse-inference/no-legal-justification instruction burdened withholding of protected communications; the earlier exclusion of Redmond's testimony had been vacated and is not the operative injury. The nonrecognition positions would reverse the federal-privilege basis and return the case for proceedings consistent with that conclusion, without creating an independent ruling that every note is discoverable or every remaining state-law question has been resolved. Rule 501's state-law sentence remains operative; the shared federal evidence question in this mixed federal/state action is the issue reviewed. The previously rejected deadly-force instruction claim is not reopened.
