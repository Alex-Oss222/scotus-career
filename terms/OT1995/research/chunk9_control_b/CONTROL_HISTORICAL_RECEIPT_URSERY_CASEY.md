## United States v. Ursery / United States v. $405,089.23 in United States Currency

**Historical identity:** 518 U.S. 267; Nos. 95-345 and 95-346, argued April 17, 1996; decided June 24, 1996. Certiorari to the Sixth and Ninth Circuits respectively. The reported judgments below are 59 F.3d 568 and 33 F.3d 1210 / 56 F.3d 41. Both were reversed.

| Historical writing | Author and exact joins | Relationship to the two judgments |
|---|---|---|
| Opinion of the Court | Rehnquist; joined in full by O'Connor, Kennedy, Souter, Ginsburg and Breyer | Six-Justice common opinion supporting reversal in both dockets |
| Concurrence | Kennedy alone, also joining the Court opinion in full | Additional explanation for reversal in both dockets; no withholding of the majority join |
| Concurrence in the judgment | Scalia, joined by Thomas | Reversal in both dockets on successive-prosecution ground; neither joins the Court opinion |
| Concurrence in the judgment in part and dissent in part | Stevens alone | Concurs in reversal in No.95-346, on proceeds rationale; dissents from reversal of Ursery's criminal-judgment relief in No.95-345 |

Thus the historical judgment coalitions are 8-1 for reversing the Sixth Circuit in No.95-345 and 9-0 for reversing the Ninth Circuit in No.95-346. Those tallies do not enlarge the six-Justice opinion's joins or make Stevens a joiner in its general rationale.

**Court writing:** The majority retains the civil-forfeiture line represented by Various Items, Emerald Cut Stones and 89 Firearms. It distinguishes Halper's fixed in-personam civil penalty, Austin's Excessive Fines inquiry and Kurth Ranch's unusual tax. It uses the 89 Firearms two-stage inquiry: congressional intent to create civil proceedings, followed by whether the clearest proof demonstrates a scheme so punitive in purpose or effect as to be criminal notwithstanding that intent. Procedural incorporation of customs-law in-rem mechanisms, summary administration, notice rules and burden rules supplies intent; remedial confiscation, prevention of illicit use and disgorgement of proceeds, coupled with the other factors discussed, supplies the second-stage application. Scienter, innocent-owner terms, deterrence and connection to criminal activity receive separate discussion; the relevant factors are neither exhaustive nor individually dispositive.

The opening's broad language about civil forfeitures must be read with note 3: civil labeling and in-rem form establish a presumption, not a per-se constitutional exemption. The clearest-proof exception remains. The Court does not predicate its conclusion on pretending that only sentient property, rather than its owner, is punished. Austin's excessiveness review remains distinct; a sanction may implicate that inquiry without being criminal punishment for double-jeopardy purposes.

Note 1 expressly leaves three Government alternatives undecided: whether the earlier civil action was a jeopardy even if punitive, whether the criminal charge and forfeiture are the same offense, and whether parallel contemporaneous actions may count as a single proceeding. Kennedy's or Stevens's discussion cannot be attributed to the majority as answers to these reserved questions. The operative judgment reverses both appellate decisions; no independent excessive-fines disposition is supplied.

**Kennedy:** Accepts that section 881(a)(7) instrumentality forfeiture can punish the owner, consistently with Austin and criminal forfeiture in Libretti, but distinguishes in-rem owner accountability for misuse from a second in-personam punishment for that owner's criminal offense. Proof that someone used property in a crime is different from charging the owner with that crime. His rationale is not reliance on a sentient-property fiction. He regards Blockburger as a poor fit because the forfeiture does not charge a second offense against the person, and doubts that the two-part test adds much beyond Various Items. He nonetheless accepts 89 Firearms as precedent and expressly joins the complete Court opinion. This is an additional explanation, not judgment-only agreement or a new majority holding on same offense.

**Scalia and Thomas:** The Double Jeopardy Clause forbids successive prosecution, not successive punishment. These civil forfeitures are not criminal prosecutions even under Mendoza-Martinez and Ward. Their concurrence is a full nonjoin to the Court opinion, not a partial refusal limited to a passage.

**Stevens:** Separates proceeds, contraband and lawfully acquired instrumentalities. No.95-346's funds are treated in his writing as unlawful proceeds the claimants have no right to retain; confiscating them imposes no loss of lawful property. Ursery's house is neither proceeds nor contraband, and taking it is punitive in his analysis. The distinction must not be converted into an independently verified finding that every item in the companion complaint was only proceeds: the majority describes alternative statutory grounds and various property.

He reads Halper as a general punitive/remedial inquiry plus a narrower disproportionate-application rule, and Austin and Kurth Ranch as consistent applications of the general inquiry. He rejects purely formal in-rem immunity, examines asserted remedial goals and the innocent-owner/criminal-predicate structure, and distinguishes genuinely remedial seizure of contraband or instruments primarily devoted to crime. He rejects the remaining Government grounds: punitive civil proceedings can be jeopardies regardless of sequence; the actual offense used to establish the house forfeiture includes the later criminal offense under his Blockburger/Vitale/Harris analysis; two parallel actions remain separate proceedings. His discussion of a unified criminal sentencing judgment and the proposed December 1996 rule is an argument, not an operative rule already in effect on June24. Note 16 preserves the possibility of remedial administrative licensing sanctions; his position is not that every civil sanction punishes.

**Record/source boundaries:** The $13,250 settlement is the actual forfeiture resolution in Ursery, not physical forfeiture of an unstated home value. The indictment preceded consummation of settlement; the criminal conviction followed. The companion civil case was deferred by agreement pending the criminal prosecution and reached summary judgment more than a year after trial. Stevens's statements about no sales, plants off the premises, no proceeds purchase and no nuisance argument are characterizations to validate separately against the lower record before using as new neutral facts. His presumption that contraband was seized is expressly a presumption, not a recovered seizure finding. None is newly supplied to a model by this receipt.

## Lewis v. Casey

**Historical identity:** 518 U.S. 343; No.94-1511; argued November 29, 1995; decided June 24, 1996. Certiorari to the Ninth Circuit, 43 F.3d 1261. The Court reversed and remanded.

| Historical writing | Author and exact joins | Scope |
|---|---|---|
| Opinion of the Court, Parts I and III | Scalia; Rehnquist, O'Connor, Kennedy, Thomas, Souter, Ginsburg and Breyer join | Eight-Justice opinion portions, including the remedial and deference grounds in Part III |
| Opinion of the Court, Part II | Scalia; Rehnquist, O'Connor, Kennedy and Thomas join | Five-Justice access-right, injury, standing and systemic-proof analysis |
| Concurrence | Thomas alone | Joins the Court opinion in full but separately questions Bounds's affirmative-assistance foundation and structural remedial authority |
| Concurrence in part, dissent in part, and concurrence in the judgment | Souter; Ginsburg and Breyer join in full | Joins Parts I and III, dissents from Part II, concurs in judgment |
| Dissent | Stevens alone | No opinion join; accepts that remand is appropriate for overbreadth/process reasons but opposes the Court's disposition and unnecessary constitutional rulings as explained in his writing |

The reported reversal-and-remand alignment is eight with Stevens formally dissenting. Stevens's express agreement that remand is appropriate must be preserved; it does not turn his dissent into a full judgment or Part III join. Likewise Souter's trio does not join Part II merely because it agrees that the existing systemwide injunction is unjustified.

**Scalia, Part II:** Bounds secures access capability, not a free-standing library or assistance entitlement. The claimant must establish that deficiencies hindered or presently hinder a nonfrivolous legal claim; injury can be actual or imminent. The Court disclaims Bounds language requiring grievance discovery or effective litigation after filing and confines required tools to direct/collateral sentence attacks and conditions claims. It rejects the State's position that formally adequate library access suffices even if an illiterate or non-English-speaking inmate cannot actually present a covered claim. It identifies Bartholic and Harris as the demonstrated examples, Bartholic as the named plaintiff, and draws distinct conclusions for illiterate prisoners and for other groups.

Do not turn those appellate sufficiency assessments into raw trial findings that only two people were injured. Note 7 expressly says the lack of a proved systemwide violation as to illiterate inmates is an evidentiary/merits conclusion, not a standing rule. Standing is not dispensed in gross; deficiencies affecting other groups require their own supported injury. Certification and standing are distinct. The opinion also distinguishes a procedurally defaulted claim from a frivolous claim and does not assert that all unsuccessful claims lack value.

**Part III:** Requires Turner deference to regulations reasonably related to legitimate penological interests. Delay attributable to such a valid regulation is not unconstitutional even if injury results; actual injury alone does not defeat the regulation. The order is also overintrusive, and the State did not receive the first genuine opportunity to devise a remedy. The process ground independently requires setting aside the order. The majority rejects treating the State's earlier Gluth conduct as enough to deny that opportunity here and regards its reservations as sufficient; those conclusions must not be recited as neutral trial facts. The ordinary final action is reversal and remand, not extinguishment of every individual access claim.

**Thomas:** Fully joins because the Court places needed limits on Bounds and federal remedial authority. Separately, he argues that Bounds lacks a constitutional textual, historical or precedential foundation for state-financed libraries or assistance. His analysis separates equal-access/transcript-fee cases from noninterference cases, criticizes a disparate-impact equal-protection foundation after Rodriguez/Davis, and preserves a due-process right not arbitrarily to be prevented from presenting federal claims. He challenges structural decrees on federalism and separation-of-powers grounds, criticizes ongoing supervision and untailored statewide commands, and insists that relief be no broader or longer than needed for a proved violation. These additional grounds have no other stated join and are not the Court's overruling of Bounds.

**Souter, Ginsburg and Breyer:** Would resolve the case by evidentiary failure to prove pervasive denial, excess of equitable discretion, Turner and the deficient remedial process. They can accept a standing description for unsupported non-English-speaking/lockdown claims, but the illiterate named plaintiff's standing already exists and absent-member representation is governed separately by Rule 23. They reject making nonfrivolousness of an underlying claim an additional standing prerequisite; ordinarily a concrete grievance plus inadequate means to investigate or present it suffices. They reserve complete systemic denial cases, where an underlying-claim prerequisite may be unnecessary, rather than establish an unconditional exemption. They defend meaningful access during litigation and reserve access for other fundamental rights, including marriage, family matters and possible deportation issues. They do not hold that every outside civil claim must receive assistance. An improved paging alternative, accepted conditionally by respondents, is material to the Turner/remedial discussion.

**Stevens:** Grounds meaningful access in retained liberty and the petition right, including affirmative protection needed in custody. He rejects the new merits-linked standing barrier, objects to the unsolicited limitations on Bounds and agrees that the remedy exceeds the findings. He accepts a remand for narrowing and improvement of the remedial process while disputing blame placed on the district judge: the prior Gluth history, invitations to submit proposals, accepted objections and declined appellate mediation matter to his account. His prediction that further injury evidence may emerge on remand is not an existing finding or proof. He expressly distinguishes merits defeat on an inadequate-library claim from standing to present it.
