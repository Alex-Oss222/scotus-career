## Baby Richard / Illinois No. 78101: exact claim and narrower implementation issue

The authentic Illinois opinion is *In re Petition of Kirchner*, 164 Ill. 2d 468, 649 N.E.2d 324, order announced January 25, 1995, explanatory opinion February 28, 1995. The complete text, including both dissents, is preserved from [Caselaw Access Project](https://static.case.law/ill-2d/164/html/0468-01.html). The OpenJurist page with this citation contains the dissents but omits the majority and is insufficient alone.

This was an original habeas proceeding in the Illinois Supreme Court seeking turnover after the earlier adoption judgment was vacated. The court issued an immediate writ requiring the Does to surrender Richard “forthwith.” It reasoned that the Does lacked statutory standing for a separate custody hearing; its opinion separately addressed the child's asserted federal liberty interest. At 499–501 it expressly identifies the final argument of **both the Does and the guardian ad litem** as Richard's liberty interest in the familial relationship formed with the Does. The court rejected an independent protected interest against a fit parent's claim and refused to require a hearing on that ground. That is a lower-court adjudication of the child's federal issue, not merely an argument invented for this review.

The original later certiorari petition, No. 94-1644, filed by the Does, is recovered from the Internet Archive's Records and Briefs collection, [item micro_IA40386012_1537](https://archive.org/details/micro_IA40386012_1537). Its questions concern (1) whether the Fourteenth Amendment mandates denial of a custody hearing following vacatur of adoption and automatic custody in the father, and (2) whether the four-year parent-child relationship formed under color of law requires particularized process before removal. Petition p. 7 expressly reports that the Does' state opposition at pp. 50–54 and Richard's state brief at pp. 18–22 argued that failing to consider their interests, needs and the consequences of changing custody violated federal due process. This is a verified representation in the recovered filing; the two underlying state briefs themselves have not yet been recovered.

**The narrower transition theory is expressly present in that petition.** At p. 26 it argues that the father's constitutional interest does not preclude state custody policies minimizing risks from abrupt transfers after vacated adoptions. At pp. 28–29 it invokes flexible due process and fact-finding directed to the particular relationships and needs. At p. 29 it says that even where transfer is appropriate, a hearing can provide information to mitigate trauma from a hasty, uncounseled transfer, citing *Nale v. Robertson*, 871 S.W.2d 674, 680 (Tenn. 1994), as a biological-father-custody case remanded for a least-disruptive transition over time. These are actual litigant contentions in the recovered petition, not analytical possibilities presented as filings.

The lower court's dissent likewise addresses the point: McMorrow's discussion at 540–543 concerns procedure before abrupt severance, and explains that a custody hearing can provide an orderly transition avoiding undue harm while considering Kirchner's rights. A dissent establishes the argument and contested issue, not a binding factual finding or a controlling rule. The published majority rejects a protected child interest before selecting procedural safeguards; it does not make a supported individualized clinical finding that an abrupt transition will be harmless.

**What is established and what is not.** The family relationship's duration, the child's having lived with the Does since four days old, the prior adoption and its vacatur, and the requested immediate turnover are supported. The need for process to consider serious transition harm is genuinely raised; a proposed narrow implementation remedy must remain inside that federal claim and preserve the accepted parental-custody entitlement. The sources do not establish a current 1996 diagnosis, a quantified risk, a necessary number of visits, a transition timetable, a particular professional's assessment, or an entitlement to indefinite nonparent custody. Those matters cannot be invented. The adopted pending-transfer reconstruction supplies present custody/live controversy, not these additional findings.

**Material source disagreement.** The Illinois majority sharply attributes deception and delay to the Does and their lawyer; both dissents dispute that record characterization and stress the absence of a hearing on their conduct. The Does' petition cites transcript passages asserting attempts to obtain the father's identity. Do not convert these disputed characterizations into an undisputed finding that the Does abducted the child or deliberately concealed him. The father's lawful custody entitlement in the accepted packet does not require adopting every accusation in the majority narrative.

## Doe / Illinois No. 76063: original adoption judgment and federal-question scope

The complete authentic opinion and rehearing writings are preserved from [159 Ill. 2d 347](https://static.case.law/ill-2d/159/html/0347-01.html). The merits opinion was filed June 16, 1994; rehearing denied July 12. The majority reversed the finding of unfitness under the first-30-day statutory provision as unsupported by the evidence of the father's efforts under the circumstances. It did not perform a best-interests comparison after finding the necessary predicate absent. McMorrow, joined by Miller and Freeman, concurred, treating unfitness as prior to best interests and observing that a separate custody petition was not properly before the court in this adoption appeal. The July rehearing writings do not convert the later turnover case into this earlier judgment.

The recovered original Does petition, No. 94-615, [IA item micro_IA40386012_0571](https://archive.org/details/micro_IA40386012_0571), was read through its entire 27-page substantive petition. Its two questions are whether *Stanley* prohibits termination under the Illinois 30-day provision when an unwed father fails to grasp the parenting opportunity, and whether *Stanley* requires suspension of that period until actual knowledge of birth. At pp. 10–11 it claims a federal question was passed upon and invokes *Michigan v. Long* against an independent-state-ground bar. It argues that *Lehr* permits prompt-assertion requirements and that federal law did not compel Illinois' result. Those are verified petition arguments; they are not a Supreme Court holding that the state judgment actually rests on federal law.

The child's original petition, No. 94-236, [IA item micro_IA40386012_0217](https://archive.org/details/micro_IA40386012_0217), identifies both the child and Does as petitioners in the caption, but its operative request is by the guardian. Its first question asserts **the child's** liberty interest in maintaining his filial relationship and a best-interests hearing before placement with the father. Its second question asks whether *Stanley* requires disregard of the policy of stable adoptions. A child's claim does not automatically prove the adults asserted their own independent constitutional entitlement.

**Remaining adult-scope limit.** There is positive evidence of a federal challenge to the original adoption judgment, so the matter is not merely a freestanding state-law dispute. But the recovered original papers do not yet establish that the adults' separate federal right to preserve adoption/permanent custody, as distinct from challenging the supposed federal necessity of the father's priority and advancing the child's rights, was presented or passed upon in No. 76063. The later No. 94-1644 petition expressly asserts adult and child liberty interests and documents their presentation in the later habeas proceeding; that positive evidence concerns No. 78101. It must not silently be moved back into No. 76063. No forfeiture or deliberate omission is inferred merely from this research limit. The operator must decide whether the supplied review scope permits the actually supported original federal challenge, or whether the independent adult claim requires a narrower disposition or the specific missing state presentation. Nothing here authorizes altering the assigned question or combining the judgments.

## Settle / Sixth Circuit No. 93-6207: complete lower-court scope

The full majority and concurrence in the result were read at [53 F.3d 152](https://openjurist.org/53/f3d/152). The actual opinion begins after modern metadata, which is not relied upon. The district court granted summary judgment for defendants and dismissed the action. The appeal challenges both the legal freedom-of-speech ruling and whether the teacher's actual reasons create a material factual dispute. The majority expressly says the plaintiff does **not** present Free Exercise or Establishment Clause claims. Assigned review concerns this free-speech summary judgment; do not add those claims.

During the week of March 15, 1991, Ramsey assigned ninth-graders a research paper intended to teach research, synthesis and writing, requiring four sources rather than mere exposition of existing ideas. Students could select and change topics on a sign-up sheet through March 22. The stated subject criteria were “interesting, researchable and decent.” After Ramsey removed the list for approval, changes required her permission; outlines were due March 26. Settle originally chose Drama, changed without prior approval to The Life of Jesus Christ, and later proposed A Scientific and Historical Approach to Jesus Christ on April 3. Both proposed outlines were rejected. Her father met with officials; Ramsey said she would accept a religion paper if not confined to Christianity or the Life of Christ. Settle declined a different assignment and received zero.

Ramsey's stated reasons, all recorded in her deposition or school-board testimony, are separately material: (1) failure to obtain advance approval for the changed topic; (2) concern that Settle's strong belief would prevent dispassionate research or cause ordinary grading criticism to be received as criticism of religion; (3) a view that personal religious beliefs were inappropriate classroom subject matter; (4) existing knowledge would let the student produce an outline without new research; (5) belief that law forbade religious issues in class; (6) belief that all sources on Jesus derived from the Bible, defeating the four-source requirement. The opinion also records Ramsey permitting secondary sources—two books and two encyclopedias or resources, with magazine/pamphlet substitutes—so a primary-source-only assignment cannot be assumed.

The majority (Merritt, joined by Weber) affirmed, granting broad curriculum discretion while recognizing that pedagogy cannot be pretext for punishing religion or other forbidden identity/viewpoint. It treated all six reasons as within that latitude, including possibly mistaken ones, and found no genuine record-supported motive dispute. These are the challenged lower reasons, not a binding Supreme Court standard or a conclusive fact finding made after trial.

Batchelder concurred only in the result. She rejected the majority's suggestion that every reason was permissible and disagreed that the record gave no basis to question the stated explanations. Her narrower position treated this as an assignment-topic control case rather than protected opinion expression, even if the poorly explained assignment produced after-the-fact excuses. She emphasized that the paper was solely for a teacher's grade, not public dissemination or perceived school endorsement, and that a religious opinion paper could not be rejected simply because religious. She also addressed the student's comparative treatment argument: other classmates wrote on Spiritualism, Reincarnation and Magic Throughout History. She treated those topics as potentially meeting the objective-research demand and rejected an equal-protection claim on these facts. That concurrence's discussion verifies the examples and claim existed; it does not enlarge the assigned free-speech question into a new independent grant.

The record accordingly supports review of legal deference and possible pretext at summary judgment. It supplies no trial finding of religious hostility, no license to deem all six reasons factually true, and no basis for a court to grade the paper. If the summary judgment is set aside, unresolved factual and remedial issues remain for ordinary proceedings; final damages or a required grade are not established by this source.
