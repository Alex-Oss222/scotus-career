# Sanitized lower-court grounds: In re Petition of Doe, No. 76063

Source: Illinois Supreme Court, 159 Ill. 2d 347 (June 16, 1994), with supplemental writings upon denial of rehearing (July 12, 1994). Source text is preserved at `doe_case_law.txt` from the complete published report. The following concerns the state judgment under review only. Quotations normalize typographic apostrophes. No inference about federal appellate jurisdiction is supplied.

## Majority opinion: Heiple for the court, 159 Ill. 2d at 349-351

The majority identifies the trial court's ground as failure to show sufficient interest in the first thirty days under section 1 of the Illinois Adoption Act, making the father's consent unnecessary under section 8. It then states, at 350:

> The finding that the father had not shown a reasonable degree of interest in the child is not supported by the evidence. In fact, he made various attempts to locate the child, all of which were either frustrated or blocked by the actions of the mother.

The same paragraph describes the mother's concealment, the attorney's failure to ascertain the father's name or address despite the mother's knowledge, and concludes:

> Under the circumstances, the father had no opportunity to discharge any familial duty.

The next paragraph, at 350, states:

> Since, however, the father's parental interest was improperly terminated, there was no occasion to reach the factor of the child's best interests. That point should never have been reached and need never have been discussed.

At 351, the majority states:

> The adoption laws of Illinois are neither complex nor difficult of application. Those laws intentionally place the burden of proof on the adoptive parents in establishing both the relinquishment and/or unfitness of the natural parents and, coincidentally, the fitness and the right to adopt of the adoptive parents. In addition, Illinois law requires a good-faith effort to notify the natural parents of the adoption proceedings. These laws are designed to protect natural parents in their preemptive rights to their own children wholly apart from any consideration of the so-called best interests of the child.

Its operative conclusion is:

> The law is otherwise and was not complied with in this case. Accordingly, we reverse.

The formal disposition is reversal of both the appellate and circuit courts. The complete majority opinion, 349-351, cites the Illinois Adoption Act and the appellate opinion; it contains no Stanley citation or express federal constitutional holding, and no express statement that its judgment is independent of federal law. Its positive grounds are the evidentiary insufficiency, the Act's parental-unfitness/consent requirements, and the consequent inability to proceed to best interests. This description identifies the actual text; it does not decide whether federal review is permissible.

## Separate concurrence: McMorrow, joined by Miller and Freeman, 352-361

This writing is a concurrence, not additional majority text. It elaborates the Illinois statutory requirements and In re Adoption of Syck, 138 Ill. 2d 255 (1990), as reasons to determine unfitness before best interests. It rejects treating section 20a's general best-interests policy as authority to disregard section 8(a)'s specific requirements. At 359 it also invokes federal authority:

> Because termination of parental rights is a permanent and complete deprivation and severance of the relationship between child and parent, legal precedent has demanded a heightened standard of proof, i.e., clear and convincing evidence, in order to prove parental unfitness.

The citations following that sentence include Santosky v. Kramer, Stanley v. Illinois, Lehr v. Robertson, Caban v. Mohammed and Quilloin v. Walcott. The concurrence then says the evidence did not establish unfitness under section 1(D)(l) by clear and convincing evidence and the trial court's determination was against the manifest weight of the evidence. It applies Syck's focus on efforts in actual circumstances, including obstruction by others, rather than the success of those efforts. It does not decide amici's proposed alternative custody petition under the Marriage and Dissolution of Marriage Act because that question is not properly before the court in this appeal (358-359).

## Rehearing: distinguish the denial from the individual explanations

Heiple writes individually in support of denial of rehearing, 362-368. At 362 he expressly characterizes the basis of the court's reversal:

> Our reversal was the result of the failure of the courts below to correctly apply Illinois law in terminating the natural father's parental rights.

At 364 his own explanation also invokes Stanley, characterizing it as prohibiting differential treatment of unmarried fathers, unmarried mothers and married parents in determining custody rights, and states:

> The courts of Illinois are bound by that decision.

He then describes Syck's unfitness-before-best-interests rule, the Illinois statutory methods of voluntary or involuntary termination, and Illinois notice law. At 365 he writes:

> The best interest of the child standard is not to be denigrated. It is real. However, it is not triggered until it has been validly determined that the child is available for adoption. And, a child is not available for adoption until the rights of his natural parents have been properly terminated.

Freeman's separate support for denying rehearing, 369-370, invokes the judicial obligation to apply the legislature's enacted law, describes the evidentiary review as showing a conclusion opposite the trial/appellate courts, and rejects the claim that this necessarily substitutes the reviewing court's judgment improperly. It identifies statutory application and evidentiary adequacy; it supplies no new constitutional majority holding.

McMorrow, joined by Miller, dissents from denial of rehearing, 370-372. She seeks further argument on the legal/factual issues, including protection of children, the relationship between the Act's policy and specific requirements, and treatment of the evidence. At 371 she describes her earlier concurrence as analyzing statutory, constitutional and case law and maintains that parental unfitness must be shown by clear and convincing evidence before proceeding to other adoption issues. She also says courts must follow existing Illinois termination law and statutory amendment belongs to the legislature. She does not withdraw her original concurrence or provide a new majority ground.

The state-law characterization and federal citations above coexist in distinct writings. They must not be flattened into either a courtwide express federal holding or a formal courtwide federal-independence declaration. The petition's separate argument concerning federal dependence remains an advocate's jurisdictional argument, not the state court's description of its own judgment.
