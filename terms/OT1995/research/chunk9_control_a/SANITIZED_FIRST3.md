## Lane v. Peña — 95-365

Lane entered the United States Merchant Marine Academy in July 1991 after satisfying appointment requirements including a Defense Department physical examination. A private physician diagnosed diabetes during the first year; Lane reported it. The Academy's September 1992 medical hearing reported insulin-dependent diabetes. In December 1992 the Academy separated Lane because diabetes disqualified him from the identified Navy/Merchant Marine Reserve or Naval Reserve commissions. The Academy is administered through the Maritime Administration within the Department of Transportation. These are objective published record recitations, not a fresh finding about medical fitness.

Lane unsuccessfully challenged separation administratively, then sued for reinstatement, compensatory damages, attorney fees and costs under Rehabilitation Act §504(a). The District Court granted Lane summary judgment on liability and ordered reinstatement. The Government did not contest the injunction. The court first permitted damages while deferring their amount; after the D.C. Circuit decided *Dorsey v. Department of Labor*, 41 F.3d 1551 (1994), it vacated that damages ruling for lack of an unequivocal federal damages waiver. The D.C. Circuit denied initial en banc reconsideration of *Dorsey* and summarily affirmed. Thus the reviewed damages issue does not reopen the discrimination determination or the unchallenged reinstatement.

The published record recites §504(a), 29 U.S.C. §794(a), as prohibiting exclusion, denial of benefits or discrimination against an otherwise qualified individual with a disability solely by reason of disability under a program or activity receiving federal financial assistance, or conducted by any Executive agency or the Postal Service. Section 505(a)(2), §794a(a)(2), makes Title VI remedies, procedures and rights available to a person aggrieved by an act or failure to act by a recipient of federal assistance or a federal provider of such assistance under §504. These are distinct enacted formulations. Complete official 1994 Code text, including all additional statutory clauses, is being obtained; these recitations are not a claim that the complete sections have yet been independently verified.

## United States v. Reorganized CF&I Fabricators — 95-325

CF&I and its related entities sponsored two qualified pension plans. On September 15, 1990 it failed to make approximately $12.4 million in required funding for the plan year ending December 31, 1989, then sought Chapter 11 reorganization on November 7, 1990. The IRS claim reviewed here is the ten-percent §4971(a) assessment, approximately $1.24 million, for that 1989 deficiency. The claim's basic allowability/liability is not contested; priority and subordination are separate questions. Other §4971(a)/(b) claims were disallowed and the Government did not seek review of those rulings.

The bankruptcy court denied excise-tax priority, characterizing the assessment as a penalty not compensating pecuniary loss; it later subordinated it under §510(c)(1) to general unsecured claims. The district court and Tenth Circuit affirmed. The Tenth Circuit applied its own *Cassidy* precedent, which treated the Internal Revenue Code label as nondispositive and used the functional *Lorber* analysis. It adopted substantially the bankruptcy court's priority reasoning. On subordination, the bankruptcy court expressly found **no inequitable conduct by the IRS**. The appellate court nevertheless held claimant misconduct unnecessary for nonpecuniary-loss tax penalties, relying on *Virtual Network*. Its stated equity was that ordinary creditors would receive only a small fraction and payment of the penalty would injure innocent creditors rather than punish the debtor. PBGC, which would pay terminated-plan benefits, was one unsecured creditor. This is the lower court's reasoning, not an established Supreme Court rule.

The plan placed the assessment with noncompensatory penalties in Class 13, which would receive nothing. It expressly provided alternative placement in Class 12, the general unsecured class receiving some funds, if subordination or separate classification failed. CF&I alternatively invoked §1122(a) substantial similarity and §1129(a)(7)'s Chapter 7 comparison (including §726(a)(4)); the lower courts did not decide those alternatives as independent grounds. Their availability must therefore remain separate from the §510(c) rationale. No automatic entitlement to a specified final payment follows merely from resolving §510(c).

The pre-1994 Code governs. The applicable priority citation is §507(a)(7)(E), not the later (a)(8)(E). Its actual recited wording covers allowed unsecured governmental claims for “an excise tax on—(i) a transaction occurring before the date of the filing of the petition for which a return, if required, is last due, under applicable law or under any extension, after three years before the date of the filing of the petition; or (ii) if a return is not required, a transaction occurring during the three years immediately preceding the date of the filing of the petition.” Section (a)(7)(G) separately covers a penalty related to a claim specified in the paragraph and compensating actual pecuniary loss. Full official pre-1994 Code verification remains in progress; do not substitute the modern Code.

## Brown v. Pro Football — 95-388

The NFL/NFLPA collective bargaining agreement expired in 1987, and the parties began negotiations for a new agreement. In 1989 the owners adopted Resolution G-2, allowing each club up to six developmental players beyond the regular 47-player roster. The program initially fixed no amount; the NFL later proposed $1,000 weekly pay. The NFLPA sought regular-player protections and individual salary negotiation. The parties discussed the proposal and rejected each other's wage position. The case proceeded on the assumption of lawful good-faith bargaining to impasse; no separate adjudication of an unfair labor practice should be invented.

The NFL implemented uniform developmental-player contracts and warned clubs that any pay above or below $1,000 could bring discipline, including loss of draft choices. In the 1989 season 236 players signed these contracts; the action was brought for 235 of them by nine named plaintiffs on May 9, 1990. Keep the 236 signers distinct from the 235-member plaintiff class.

The District Court rejected the labor exemption on three alternative legal grounds: expiration of the collective bargaining agreement; alternatively impasse; alternatively absence of union assent to a restraint previously embodied in a collective agreement. It granted plaintiffs summary judgment on antitrust liability in March 1992. A ten-day September 1992 jury trial concerned antitrust injury and damages, **not liability**. The trebled judgment was $30,349,642. The court denied the defense request for judgment as a matter of law/new trial, permanently enjoined uniform regular-season salary fixing for any player category, and awarded $1,744,578.41 in attorney fees.

The D.C. Circuit reversed solely on the nonstatutory labor exemption. It reasoned that lawful restraints imposed through collective bargaining and primarily affecting the labor market organized around that bargaining relationship were protected, including unilateral postimpasse imposition. It did not decide the defendants' remaining challenges. Judge Wald dissented from that appellate disposition. The appellate grounds and the unresolved other challenges matter to remedy if the exemption ground does not survive review; overturning that ground alone does not automatically adjudicate every remaining challenge to the damages, injunction and fees.
