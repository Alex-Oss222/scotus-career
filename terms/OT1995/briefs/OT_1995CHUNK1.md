**Kyles**

**Holding:** Suppressed favorable evidence is material when its collective lawful uses, considered in the whole record and in the defense’s preparation and presentation, create a reasonable probability of a different result sufficient to undermine confidence in the verdict. No item must independently satisfy that standard, and the defendant need not show acquittal more likely than conviction or that the remaining evidence is legally insufficient; the established suppression here meets the standard.

**Proposition-level authority:** Opinion of the Court (Souter), joined by Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Ginsburg and Breyer: six Justices.

**Limits and questions not reached:** The collective inquiry includes legitimate effects on preparation, corroboration, impeachment and examination of the investigation. It does not excuse lack of diligence about evidence already known to the defense, require open-file discovery or make every nondisclosure structural error.

**Holding:** A prosecutor must learn of favorable evidence known to police acting on the government’s behalf in the case and disclose it when Brady requires disclosure; personal ignorance does not remove that evidence from the materiality inquiry. This case-specific responsibility does not impute all knowledge of every government office or establish unrestricted investigative or open-file duties.

**Proposition-level authority:** Opinion of the Court (Souter), joined by Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Ginsburg and Breyer: six Justices.

**Holding:** Once this suppression is found material under Brady and Bagley, a second prejudice or harmlessness test may not defeat relief merely by reassessing whether the same nondisclosure affected the verdict. This holding rests on the prejudice built into the completed Brady violation and establishes no universal harmlessness rule for other habeas claims.

**Proposition-level authority:** Opinion of the Court (Souter), joined by Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Ginsburg and Breyer: six Justices.

**Stone’s reasoning and application:** Remedial discipline remains part of Stone's approach: relief requires a supported connection between the suppression and the verdict, and a new trial does not follow merely because the State withheld something favorable. Three other commitments weigh against rejecting this claim summarily. First, Brady, as Kyles applies it, protects lawful uses in the defense's preparation, so the Court must assess how timely disclosure could have strengthened investigation and questioning even if Washington law kept the results themselves from the jury. Second, the inquiry stays with the actual dispute and record. Dwayne Bartholomew admitted the robbery and the fatal shot; the contested issue was premeditation. As the Ninth Circuit described the trial record, Rodney's and Tracy's testimony that he said he would leave no witnesses was the only direct guilt-phase evidence of premeditation, the gun's single-action design was disputed circumstantial evidence, and Bell testified only at the penalty phase. That court also identified Rodney's later conflicting accounts of how many polygraph examinations he took. General evidence that he robbed the laundromat and shot the attendant does not resolve whether disclosure would have affected the premeditation finding. Third, existing law applies without added mandatory elements. Kyles assesses the collective lawful uses of suppressed information in the defense's preparation and presentation, including legitimate effects on corroboration, impeachment and examination of the investigation, so reasonable inferences about how timely disclosure would have strengthened the defense count; no item must suffice alone, and the defendant need not show that acquittal was more likely than conviction. A particular confession, a newly discovered witness or an identified new exhibit is therefore not a prerequisite for relief. Stone takes the investigative-use argument seriously while still requiring more than an imaginable benefit. The State's competing account, including its description of trial counsel's habeas testimony, Rodney's adherence at the hearing to his trial testimony and the differing descriptions of Tracy's result, deserves examination on the full record. Stone's substantive judgment is that the defense's account of what suppression cost it has enough grounding that the Court should not reject it through a summary disposition; full consideration follows from that judgment, not from a general preference for procedure.

**Mechanism, remedy and limits:** Grant only the presented materiality question; claims the Ninth Circuit did not reach remain outside it. Briefing and argument should address which lawful preparation, deposition and impeachment uses timely disclosure would have supported, assessed collectively against the whole guilt-phase record on premeditation, including trial counsel's complete habeas testimony, which the bounded materials reviewed here do not reproduce in full and the parties describe differently. No evidentiary lead, concession or gun-testing fact is invented or resolved at this stage. **Limit:** Inadmissibility alone does not eliminate a lawful investigative or preparation use, but an imaginable benefit alone does not establish materiality. Opposing summary disposition is not a merits vote; whether the supported uses, assessed collectively, undermine confidence in the premeditation verdict remains for decision after full consideration.

## OT1995-002 — Tuggle v. Netherland

**Event:** 1995-10-30 | **Category:** MERITS | **Action:** Summary review on certiorari petition

**Citation:** 516 U.S. 10

**Docket(s):** No. 95-6016

### SECTION I — NEUTRAL MODELING PACKET

- **Case Name:** Tuggle v. Netherland
- **Citation / Docket:** 516 U.S. 10; No. 95-6016
- **October Term:** 1995
- **Entry Stage / Event Type:** Certiorari petition before the Court with possible summary merits disposition; no prior plenary grant inferred.
- **Question Presented:** Whether a capital sentence may stand when the prosecution presented psychiatric future-dangerousness evidence after the trial court denied the indigent defendant access to an independent psychiatric expert.
- **Question Granted:** The stated question describes the submitted review scope; no additional question or plenary grant is inferred beyond the identified procedural stage.
- **Legal Area:** Capital sentencing — expert assistance, future dangerousness, and harmless error
- **Court and Judgment Under Review:** The Fourth Circuit, *Tuggle v. Thompson*, 57 F.3d 1356 (June 29, 1995), reversed the District Court’s June 8, 1994 grant of habeas relief and remanded with instructions to dismiss the petition. On the claims arising from the denial of psychiatric assistance and the State psychologist’s future-dangerousness testimony, it held that, Virginia being a nonweighing State, the jury’s separate vileness finding supports the death sentence under *Zant v. Stephens* after the future-dangerousness finding was set aside. It rejected the District Court’s finding that the future-dangerousness evidence prejudicially spilled over, following *Smith v. Procunier*, 769 F.2d 170 (4th Cir. 1985), which had held that asserted errors in psychiatric testimony directed to dangerousness did not require setting aside a death sentence also supported by unchallenged evidence of vileness. It held that this ruling disposes of those claims, and it did not revisit the state court’s Ake determination.
- **Requested Supreme Court Disposition / Relief:** The party seeking review asks for relief from the adverse judgment identified below; the opposing party seeks its preservation. Any decision must stay within the presented issues and source-authorized remedy.
- **Material Facts and Record:** Virginia used expert psychiatric testimony to portray Tuggle as a future danger, an aggravating consideration supporting death. Before trial, on a defense motion, Tuggle was examined for competency and sanity at Central State Hospital by a forensic psychiatrist, Dr. Miller Ryans, and a clinical psychologist, Dr. Arthur Centor; their report said they had formed an opinion on future dangerousness but were not reporting it because none had been requested (Resp. App. 18–26). The trial court then denied a defense motion for a further evaluation by Dr. C. Robert Showalter, a psychiatrist the defense had arranged, whose written motion sought a report on Tuggle’s condition at the time of the offense and his present competency; it ruled that any further motion for evaluation was denied (Resp. App. 27–34). At the penalty stage Dr. Centor testified for the Commonwealth that Tuggle showed a high probability of future dangerousness, and the jury found both future dangerousness and vileness and fixed death. On direct appeal the Virginia Supreme Court affirmed, noting that the need to rebut future dangerousness had not been advanced in Tuggle’s written motion or oral argument (citing its Rule 5:21) and holding that whether to allow a second evaluation lay within the trial court’s discretion. *Tuggle v. Commonwealth*, 228 Va. 493, 503 (1984). The Supreme Court of the United States vacated that judgment and remanded for further consideration in light of *Ake v. Oklahoma*. *Tuggle v. Virginia*, 471 U.S. 1096 (1985). On that remand, the Attorney General took the position, in brief and at oral argument, that the trial court had erred at the penalty stage and that resentencing was required. The Virginia Supreme Court held that Tuggle had not made Ake’s threshold showing for guilt-stage assistance, but that Ake required the conclusion that the trial court erred in denying him an independent psychiatrist once the Commonwealth presented psychiatric evidence of future dangerousness. In footnotes it noted that he had asked only for reevaluation of sanity and competency, never for a psychiatrist to rebut future dangerousness, and that his objection to Dr. Centor’s testimony rested on the absence of counsel at the examination, not on the denial of an expert. Declining to accept the resentencing suggestion, it held that the error “neither impairs nor invalidates” the jury’s vileness finding and, under *Zant v. ### SECTION II — STONE POSITION SUPPLEMENT

**Position status:** Approved by the user as revised on October 2, 2026; no Court vote or outcome entered.

**Stone’s disposition:** Vacate the Fourth Circuit’s judgment and remand. Accepting the lower courts’ Ake premise for this limited review, without independently deciding the disputed entitlement, Stone would hold that the surviving vileness aggravator does not automatically cure the assumed denial of psychiatric assistance, reject the Fourth Circuit’s automatic reliance on that aggravator, and direct consideration of the error’s consequences under the governing harmless-error law. Stone’s position, as an expressly proposed extension of the simulated O’Neal rule, is that Chapman should govern: the State must establish beyond a reasonable doubt that the denial did not contribute to the death sentence. Stone would neither order a new sentencing hearing now nor declare the error harmless.

**Stone’s reasoning and application:** A valid aggravating circumstance can establish eligibility for death without establishing that constitutional error did not influence the jury’s choice of death. Under Virginia’s instruction, a jury that found an aggravator still had to decide on all the evidence whether death was justified; the vileness finding answers eligibility, not that selection. Zant sustained a sentence where the evidence behind the invalid factor was properly before the jury and open to the defense’s explanation; it does not hold that a surviving aggravator excuses an error in the defendant’s means to answer the State’s case. An unrebutted psychiatric future-danger case can shape the entire sentencing choice. A defense expert serves investigation, cross-examination, rebuttal and mitigation; removing one aggravator does not reconstruct those lost functions. Review must therefore address how the error could have affected the defense’s rebuttal of the Commonwealth’s psychiatric evidence, its mitigation case and the jury’s ultimate decision to impose death rather than life; that is the ordinary harmless-error question, not a new test. On the standard, Stone would extend the actual simulated O’Neal preserved-error rule to an available Ake error of this kind because the State obtained an evidentiary advantage through the very denial that makes the counterfactual hard to prove. The risk of that uncertainty should not be placed on the defendant. This is an expressly proposed extension: the prior O’Neal holding did not already decide all Ake errors, and its broader harmlessness theory was not controlling.

**Mechanism, remedy and limits:** Assume, for this limited review, the penalty-stage Ake violation found by the Virginia Supreme Court in 1985 and left undisturbed by the Fourth Circuit; the Commonwealth’s withdrawn concession is neither relied on nor treated as binding. The assumption does not itself conclusively establish the violation, and it does not automatically reopen matters already settled below: any renewed challenge to the Ake determination, including the Commonwealth’s 1995 contentions about the scope of Tuggle’s request and the threshold showing, must be considered according to the existing rulings and applicable preservation rules. If the violation stands, the court on remand must assess the error’s consequences on the whole penalty record, including what competent assistance could have supplied for rebuttal and mitigation, without prescribing an expert's invented testimony. If the error is harmless under the governing standard, deny relief on this error; otherwise issue a conditional writ requiring a constitutionally adequate new penalty proceeding within a reasonable district-court deadline or replacement of the death sentence by lawful relief. Do not order an automatic new guilt trial, treat the error as structural, or decide an independent state aggravator issue. Permit briefing on the governing harmless-error standard, including Stone’s proposed Chapman extension, if the current submissions have not addressed it.

**Independent ground and principle:** Reliance on a surviving aggravator does not answer the lost expert-assistance injury. That holding independently requires vacatur and remand whatever harmless-error standard ultimately governs; Stone’s primary harmless-error position remains Chapman. Eligibility for punishment cannot substitute for examining whether the decision to impose that punishment was constitutionally reliable.

**Stansbury**

**Current rule:** Custody turns on the objective circumstances confronting the person questioned and whether a reasonable person in that position would understand freedom of movement to be restrained to the degree associated with formal arrest. An officer’s uncommunicated view of the person as suspect or witness does not enter the calculation. Beliefs conveyed by words or conduct may affect the objective setting to the extent they would affect that reasonable understanding. Communicating suspicion does not itself establish custody, and an officer’s private intent cannot substitute for the objective inquiry.
