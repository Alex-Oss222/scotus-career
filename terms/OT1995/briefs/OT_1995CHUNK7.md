## OT1995-064 — Henderson v. United States

**Event:** 1996-05-20 | **Category:** MERITS | **Action:** Argued merits decision

**Citation:** 517 U.S. 654

**Docket(s):** No. 95-232

### SECTION I — NEUTRAL MODELING PACKET

- **Case Name:** Henderson v. United States
- **Citation / Docket:** 517 U.S. 654; No. 95-232
- **October Term:** 1995
- **Entry Stage / Event Type:** Granted merits review; decision pending in this simulation.
- **Question Presented:** Whether Federal Rule of Civil Procedure 4’s period for service (120 days after filing, subject to a showing of good cause, Fed. R. Civ. P. 4(j) (1988)) supersedes the Suits in Admiralty Act’s direction that the libelant “shall forthwith serve” the United States, 46 U.S.C. App. §742, or whether compliance with that “forthwith” requirement is a condition of the Act’s waiver of sovereign immunity and therefore a jurisdictional prerequisite the Federal Rules cannot alter. The petition also asks, if Rule 4 does not supplant the requirement, whether district courts may extend the time for service the Act provides in appropriate circumstances; the United States’ merits brief adds whether the district court correctly dismissed the complaint for failure to serve “forthwith.”
- **Question Granted:** Certiorari was granted November 22, 1995 (116 S. Ct. 493). Henderson’s merits brief presents the petition’s two questions, and the United States’ merits brief presents the supersession question and whether the dismissal was correct; no additional question or plenary grant is inferred beyond the identified procedural stage.
- **Legal Area:** Federal procedure — service of process and suits against the United States
- **Court and Judgment Under Review:** United States Court of Appeals for the Fifth Circuit, *Henderson v. United States*, 51 F.3d 574 (5th Cir. 1995) (per curiam; No. 94-40697; judgment May 10, 1995), affirming the Eastern District of Texas’s June 16, 1994 order dismissing Henderson’s complaint without prejudice for lack of subject-matter jurisdiction under Fed. R. Civ. P. 12(b)(1). Following *United States v. Holmberg*, 19 F.3d 1062 (5th Cir. 1994), the court held that §742’s forthwith requirement is a condition of the Government’s waiver of sovereign immunity and therefore a jurisdictional prerequisite that Rule 4(j) does not supersede, so the district court’s fifteen-day extension did not affect jurisdiction or Henderson’s §742 obligations; that, even read for reasonableness and even assuming good cause for the delay, completing service in 148 days is not forthwith; and that “forthwith” applies both to service on the United States Attorney and to the mailing to the Attorney General, so service on the Attorney General alone could not satisfy §742. It did not decide whether the 47-day service on the Attorney General was forthwith, and it did not reach Henderson’s due-process argument because the dismissal was without prejudice and he did not argue that a new suit would be time-barred.
- **Requested Supreme Court Disposition / Relief:** Henderson asks the Court to reverse the Fifth Circuit’s judgment and remand for trial on the merits, holding that Rule 4’s 120-day period supplants §742’s forthwith requirement or, alternatively, that district courts may extend the time for §742 service in appropriate circumstances; the United States asks that the judgment be affirmed. Any decision must stay within the presented issues and source-authorized remedy.
- **Material Facts and Record:** Lloyd Henderson, a merchant seaman, alleges he was injured on or about August 27, 1991 (the Government’s district-court memorandum says August 29), aboard the M/V DELMONTE, a vessel owned by the United States through the Maritime Administration. After his administrative claim under the Clarification Act, 50 U.S.C. App. §1291(a), was disallowed, he sued the United States on April 8, 1993, in the Southern District of Texas, Galveston Division, invoking the Suits in Admiralty Act and the Public Vessels Act. The Government maintains that the claim lies only under the Suits in Admiralty Act and that the forthwith requirement applies to Public Vessels Act suits in any event through 46 U.S.C. App. §782. Henderson’s counsel attributes the delay in service to summons forms the clerk’s office was slow to supply, a summons issued without the court’s seal, and replacement forms lost in the mail. The Attorney General received the summons and complaint by certified mail on May 25, 1993, 47 days after filing. Rule 4(j)’s 120 days ran on August 6, 1993. Henderson received properly sealed summonses on August 25, 1993, and places the end of §745’s two-year period on August 27, 1993. On August 30 (day 144) he moved to postpone the scheduling conference because the United States had not been served. By order dated August 31 and filed September 1, 1993, the court granted the motion and ordered him to serve the United States within fifteen days; the order made no express good-cause finding, and Henderson has treated it as an implied finding of good cause and diligence. The United States Attorney was personally served on September 3, 1993, 148 days after filing. The case was then transferred, on the Government’s motion, to the Eastern District of Texas, Beaumont Division. The United States moved to dismiss under Rule 12(b)(1) on the ground that service was not “forthwith” under §742; its motions and briefs rest on §742 alone and do not contend that service failed Rule 4, and its district-court memoranda state that Henderson “properly effected service” on September 3, 1993, which they, like the district court’s dismissal order, count as 150 days after filing. The district court denied the motion on January 21, 1994. After *Holmberg*, it granted the Government’s renewed motion and dismissed without prejudice on June 16, 1994.
- **Parties’ Positions:** Henderson argues that §742’s forthwith requirement is a procedural provision adopted in 1920, before uniform federal rules existed; that the Civil Rules were extended to admiralty cases in 1966; that §2072(b) makes laws in conflict with the Rules “of no further force or effect,” and Congress itself enacted Rule 4’s 120-day period and good-cause exception (Federal Rules of Civil Procedure Amendments Act of 1982, Pub. L. 97-462, 96 Stat. 2527); that service is a procedural matter under *Hanna v. Plumer*, 380 U.S. 460 (1965); that §743 directs that suits proceed under the rules of practice obtaining between private parties; and that a jurisdictional reading defeats the Act’s waiver and yields inconsistent rulings on what “forthwith” means. In reply, he argues that the provisions conflict because Rule 4(j) allows more time than courts have allowed under “forthwith” and lets a court extend the time for good cause, while under §742 a court must dismiss once it finds that service was not forthwith; and that, under *Mississippi Publishing Corp. v. Murphree*, 326 U.S. 438 (1946), a rule governing the means of bringing a defendant before a court that already has subject-matter jurisdiction neither extends that jurisdiction contrary to Rule 82 nor abridges substantive rights contrary to §2072(b). Alternatively, he argues that a district court may extend the time for §742 service in appropriate circumstances, relying by analogy on *American Pipe & Construction Co. v. Utah*, 414 U.S. 538 (1974), and on the Fifth Circuit’s holding in *McCormick v. United States*, 680 F.2d 345 (5th Cir. 1982), that §745’s two-year limitation can be tolled in appropriate circumstances, and that, given the clerk’s-office and mail problems he encountered, the extension granted here should be honored; his reply adds, as a further alternative, that Rule 4 should be read with §742 so that the district court judges whether service was “forthwith” by Rule 4’s standard. The United States argues that §742 and Rule 4(j) can both be given effect, because Rule 4(j) sets only an outer limit and confers no affirmative right to take 120 days; that *Hanna* is distinguishable because it addressed whether a state procedural rule applies in diversity litigation, not whether a federal statute specifically addressed to Suits in Admiralty Act litigation should be enforced, and appears to have read the Rule there as conferring an affirmative right to serve process in the specified ways; that §743’s general direction yields to §742’s specific service command, which the legislative history ties to a need for prompt admiralty litigation; that, in any event, forthwith service is a term of the United States’ consent to be sued and so a jurisdictional condition that §2072(b) (the Rules “shall not abridge, enlarge or modify any substantive right”) and Rule 82 forbid the Rules to relax (citing *United States v. Sherwood*, 312 U.S. 584 (1941), and *United States v. Mitchell*, 445 U.S. 535 (1980)); that “forthwith” means immediate, prompt action with reasonable dispatch; that, even accepting Henderson’s declarations, his counsel made no effort to serve immediately, because counsel did not obtain or complete summons forms before filing and apparently did not file by hand, and the clerk’s-office mistakes that worsened the delay arose only after the forthwith obligation had already been breached, so the dismissal was correct; and that limitations-tolling principles are of little relevance to the requirement. Because Congress itself enacted Rule 4(j), the United States calls §2072(b)’s reference to “such rules” technically inapplicable to it, but agrees with Henderson that §2072(b) provides the best evidence of congressional intent on how the Rule interacts with other laws. Henderson replies that nothing in the rules forbids requesting summons forms by mail and that the clerk’s delay in forwarding the forms caused the delay in service.
- **Preservation / Threshold / Vehicle Issues:** Whether §742’s requirement is jurisdictional and whether Rule 4(j) supersedes it were raised in both district-court motions and decided below. Henderson’s separate due-process claim was not reached by the Fifth Circuit and is not presented as a question here; his merits brief invokes due-process and equal-protection concerns only in support of applying Rule 4’s uniform 120-day period. No court has decided whether the 47-day service on the Attorney General was forthwith or whether the August 31, 1993 order rested on good cause. Filing came within §745’s two years; personal service on the United States Attorney came after the end of that period as Henderson places it. Henderson’s merits brief states that, although the dismissal was without prejudice, the limitations period had already run when it was entered.
- **Relevant Dates:** Injury on or about August 27, 1991; complaint filed April 8, 1993; service on the Attorney General May 25, 1993 and on the United States Attorney September 3, 1993; dismissal June 16, 1994; Fifth Circuit judgment May 10, 1995; petition filed August 8, 1995; certiorari granted November 22, 1995; argument March 19, 1996; decision May 20, 1996.
- **Companion or Consolidated Matters:** None.
- **Known Participation Issue:** Stone, Stevens, O’Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg, and Breyer form the event-date roster; no case-specific nonparticipation is established.

**Keene**

**Current rule:** Section 1500 bars a Claims Court action when, at filing, a suit for or in respect to the same claim is pending elsewhere against the United States or a covered federal actor. - **Supporting Authorities:** Suits in Admiralty Act; Fed. R. Civ. P. 3 and 4; Rules Enabling Act.
- **Stone’s Proposed Holding for This Case:** When a statute requires an action against the United States to be brought within a limitations period but does not prescribe service timing, timely filing commences the action and Rule 4 governs service.
- **Stone’s Reasoning:** The waiver and limitations provision defines whether and when suit may be filed; the Federal Rules govern service after commencement unless Congress supplies a contrary command. ## OT1995-065 — Doctor’s Associates, Inc. v. Casarotto

**Event:** 1996-05-20 | **Category:** MERITS | **Action:** Argued merits decision

**Citation:** 517 U.S. 681

**Docket(s):** No. 95-559

### SECTION I — NEUTRAL MODELING PACKET

- **Case Name:** Doctor’s Associates, Inc. v. Casarotto
- **Citation / Docket:** 517 U.S. 681; No. 95-559
- **October Term:** 1995
- **Entry Stage / Event Type:** Granted merits review; decision pending in this simulation.
- **Question Presented:** Whether the Federal Arbitration Act preempts a Montana law making arbitration clauses unenforceable unless specially noticed on the first page of the contract. As framed in the petition: whether Section 2 of the Act, 9 U.S.C. §2—which makes written agreements to arbitrate “valid, irrevocable, and enforceable, save upon such grounds as exist at law or in equity for the revocation of any contract”—“preempts a state law that makes agreements to arbitrate, but not other agreements, unenforceable unless a specific notice of the arbitration agreement is placed on the first page of the contract.”
- **Question Granted:** The stated question describes the submitted review scope; no additional question or plenary grant is inferred beyond the identified procedural stage.
- **Legal Area:** Arbitration — FAA §2 preemption of a state arbitration-notice requirement
- **Court and Judgment Under Review:** Supreme Court of Montana, *Casarotto v. Lombardi*, 274 Mont. 3, 901 P.2d 596 (decided August 31, 1995), reaffirming and reinstating its December 15, 1994 decision, 268 Mont. 369, 886 P.2d 931, and remanding to the district court for further proceedings. The 1994 decision, by a 4–3 vote, reversed the district court’s order staying the claims against Doctor’s Associates, Inc. (DAI) and Nick Lombardi pending arbitration. On the first issue, it held under Montana conflict-of-laws principles (Restatement (Second) of Conflict of Laws §§187(2), 188) that Montana law governs despite the agreement’s Connecticut choice-of-law clause: absent an effective choice, Montana law would apply because Montana has a materially greater interest in the issue, and applying Connecticut law would be contrary to a fundamental Montana public policy established by the notice requirement of Mont. Code Ann. §27-5-114. The court derived that policy from legislative history reflecting concern that Montanans not unknowingly waive their right of access to Montana courts or be compelled to arbitrate at distant locations, and from what it described as the legislature’s determination that citizens are entitled to notice before agreeing to a procedure “as potentially inconvenient, expensive, and devoid of procedural safeguards” as arbitration under American Arbitration Association (AAA) rules, together with the terms of this contract (a hearing in Connecticut, with costs shared equally). On the second issue, it held that, under *Volt*, the inquiry is whether §27-5-114(4) would “undermine the goals and policies of the FAA,” and that it does not, because the FAA does not require parties to arbitrate when they have not agreed to do so and the requirement does not preclude parties from knowingly agreeing to arbitrate; it did not treat as controlling that *Volt* applied state rules the parties had chosen, whereas Montana law applies here under conflict-of-laws principles. It therefore held §27-5-114(4) not preempted by 9 U.S.C. §2 and concluded that, because the agreement did not comply with the notice requirement, it is not subject to arbitration under Montana law. Justice Weber and Justice Gray, each joined by Chief Justice Turnage, dissented in 1994 on both issues. Each concluded that §27-5-114(4), which by its terms addresses notice that a contract is subject to arbitration “pursuant to this chapter,” does not reach this agreement, which selects Connecticut law, and therefore cannot supply a fundamental policy overriding the parties’ choice of Connecticut law; each also concluded that the requirement is in any event preempted. Justice Gray distinguished *Volt* because the parties there had chosen California arbitration law and the California stay provision did not render their agreement unenforceable, and she would have affirmed the stay. The 1995 opinion recites that it issued on further consideration in light of *Allied-Bruce* after a June 12, 1995 order of the Supreme Court of the United States vacating the 1994 judgment and remanding; that recital identifies the source history only and does not import a historical grant or outcome. The four-Justice majority reasoned that, under *Volt*, state law is preempted only to the extent it stands as an obstacle to the FAA’s purposes; that a rule requiring only that parties be informed before they agree to arbitrate does not undermine those purposes; and that *Allied-Bruce* concerned a statute invalidating predispute arbitration agreements and the Act’s commerce coverage, not such a notice requirement. It stated that its 1994 decision had assumed that the transaction involved interstate commerce and that any state law frustrating the FAA’s purposes would be preempted. Justice Leaphart, specially concurring, added that the notice rule is part of Montana’s Uniform Arbitration Act, which specifically recognizes arbitration agreements, and that it furthers consensual arbitration by helping ensure that a consumer who signs a nonnegotiated form contract knowingly agrees to arbitrate. Justice Gray, joined by Chief Justice Turnage and Justice Weber, dissented. She objected that the majority decided the remand without acting on defendants’ request to brief and argue it; she read *Allied-Bruce*’s reaffirmation of *Southland* as bearing on the case and rejected the majority’s reading of *Volt* as a retrenchment from *Southland*; and, reinstating her earlier dissent, she concluded that the notice statute is preempted because its application renders the parties’ agreement to arbitrate unenforceable.
- **Requested Supreme Court Disposition / Relief:** Petitioners Doctor’s Associates, Inc. and Nick Lombardi seek reversal of the Montana Supreme Court’s judgment refusing enforcement of the arbitration clause; respondents Paul and Pamela Casarotto seek preservation of the judgment. Petitioners’ position (petition): §2’s savings clause permits only grounds that exist “for the revocation of any contract,” so a State may not condition enforcement of a covered arbitration agreement on a requirement that applies to arbitration agreements but not to other contracts. They rely on *Southland*, *Perry* (482 U.S. at 493 n.9: a state-law principle that “takes its meaning precisely from the fact that a contract to arbitrate is at issue” does not comport with §2), *Moses H. Cone Memorial Hospital v. Mercury Construction Corp.*, 460 U.S. 1, and their reading of *Allied-Bruce* as reaffirming that rule. They argue that *Volt* does not support the judgment, because there the parties chose California’s arbitration rules and the stay provision addressed a procedural matter without rendering the agreement unenforceable, whereas here the court applied Montana law over the parties’ choice of Connecticut law and refused enforcement; that Montana’s notice requirement departs from the 1955 Uniform Arbitration Act, which contains none; and that varying state notice and placement rules would subject multistate businesses to inconsistent conditions on enforcement. They acknowledge that States may apply general contract principles such as those against unconscionable agreements, but contend that a State may not single out arbitration agreements out of concern that those agreements in particular reflect unequal bargaining power. No respondent filing in this Court is among the packet sources. It may not determine only whether pretrial evidence creates a genuine dispute about the official’s participation, or choose between disputed motive, causation or factual accounts. A legal appeal uses the district court’s assumed facts; if not stated, the appellate court may identify likely assumptions favorable to the nonmovant without deciding truth or reweighing sufficiency. An abstract legal contention does not bring the factual dispute within immediate review.

### SECTION I — NEUTRAL MODELING PACKET

- **Case Name:** Ornelas v. United States
- **Citation / Docket:** 517 U.S. 690; No. 95-5257
- **October Term:** 1995
- **Entry Stage / Event Type:** Granted merits review; decision pending in this simulation.
- **Question Presented:** As framed by petitioners: “In non-warrant cases, should appellate courts conduct a de novo standard of review concerning reasonable suspicion to stop and probable cause to search?” The United States frames the question as whether the court of appeals, accepting as not clearly erroneous the facts the district court found, was required to review de novo the district court’s conclusion that those facts provided reasonable suspicion justifying an investigatory stop and probable cause to search. The invited amicus frames it as whether the court of appeals correctly reviewed the district court’s determinations of reasonable suspicion for the stop and probable cause for the automobile search under the clearly erroneous standard rather than de novo.
- **Question Granted:** Certiorari granted November 6, 1995, limited to Question 1 presented by the petition (J.A. 94). The petition’s second question, whether the trial court’s refusal of access to the raw data behind the NADDIS check violated due process, was not granted and is not before the Court. The United States notes that whether the stop and search would be sustained under de novo review was not presented in the petition, and petitioners do not ask the Court to decide that application. No additional question or plenary grant is inferred.
- **Legal Area:** Search and seizure — appellate standard of review
- **Court and Judgment Under Review:** United States Court of Appeals for the Seventh Circuit, Nos. 94-3349 and 94-3350, unpublished order and judgment of April 18, 1995 (Posner, C.J., and Flaum and Manion, JJ.), noted at 52 F.3d 328 (table), following the same panel’s earlier opinion in *United States v. Ornelas-Ledesma*, 16 F.3d 714 (7th Cir. 1994).

  Before the first appeal, the magistrate judge concluded that the stop was supported by reasonable suspicion and that petitioners consented to a search that did not extend to dismantling the panel; having disbelieved the rust testimony, he found no probable cause for the panel search but recommended denying suppression on an inevitable-discovery ground. The district court adopted the reasonable-suspicion and consent rulings and, finding inevitable discovery unnecessary to decide, found probable cause from the drug-courier profile, the two NADDIS hits and the loose panel.

  The 1994 opinion applied circuit precedent, *United States v. Spears*, 965 F.2d 262 (7th Cir. 1992), which had overruled earlier circuit decisions applying plenary review and adopted clear-error review of probable-cause determinations, and extended that standard to reasonable suspicion as an adjacent point on the same continuum. Because the record contained no evidence about NADDIS’s reliability, it treated each NADDIS entry as no more reliable than a tip from an informant not known to be reliable, but found the two entries mutually corroborating and corroborated by the circumstances that first drew the officers’ attention. Calling the question close, it held that the district court did not clearly err in finding reasonable suspicion for the stop. Noting that the Government did not argue that the stop or the consent justified dismantling the car, it treated the search as lawful only if supported by probable cause. It held that the Government had waived the magistrate judge’s inevitable-discovery ground, and that the district judge could not rely on the officer’s challenged loose-panel testimony, heard only by the magistrate judge, without resolving its credibility. It vacated the convictions and remanded for a determination whether that testimony was credible and, if so, whether the loose panel, with the NADDIS tips as corroborated by the other circumstances, established probable cause.

  On remand, the parties stipulated to decision on the existing record. The magistrate judge credited the loose-panel testimony but concluded that the loose panel, given the car’s age, together with a weak drug-courier profile and mismatched NADDIS reports, did not establish probable cause (he found the record on scratches on the screw inconclusive), and recommended that the suppression motions be granted. The district court accepted the credibility finding, sustained the Government’s objection and, describing the issue as close, found that the converging circumstances established probable cause. The 1995 order stated that its review was deferential and that the probable-cause finding could be set aside only if clearly erroneous; finding no clear error, it affirmed.
- **Requested Supreme Court Disposition / Relief:** Petitioners ask the Court to reverse and remand for determination of their suppression motions under de novo review. The United States agrees that the ultimate reasonable-suspicion and probable-cause determinations are reviewed de novo; it contends that the stop and search would be upheld under that standard, but because that application was not presented in the petition, it asks the Court to vacate and remand for the court of appeals to apply the correct standard. No party defends deferential review. Because both parties urge de novo review, the Court invited Peter D. Isakoff (order of December 4, 1995) to brief and argue as amicus curiae in support of the judgment; he defends clear-error review and asks that the judgment be affirmed. Other amici support each side: the American Civil Liberties Union, the ACLU of Wisconsin and the National Association of Criminal Defense Lawyers support petitioners, and Americans for Effective Law Enforcement and the National District Attorneys Association support affirmance. Any decision must stay within the presented issues and source-authorized remedy.
- **Material Facts and Record:** On December 11, 1992, Milwaukee County Sheriff’s Detective Pautz, conducting a routine survey of motels for drug trafficking, noticed a 1981 two-door Oldsmobile with California plates; based on his training and experience he regarded older two-door General Motors cars as often used to transport drugs and California as a drug “source” state. The dispatcher reported the car registered to a Miguel Ornelas of San Jose with the additional name “Ledesma”; Pautz was unsure of the order of the names. The motel registry showed that an Ismael Ornelas and another man had checked in at 4:00 a.m. without reservations. A NADDIS check through the DEA returned entries for a Miguel Ledesma Ornelas (a/k/a Miguel Ledesma-Lemus), a heroin dealer from El Centro, California, and an Ismael Ornelas, Jr., of Tucson, Arizona, a 1,000-kilogram-a-month cocaine dealer. No wants or warrants were outstanding, the officers did not obtain further descriptions, and they learned only later that neither entry concerned either petitioner.

  When petitioners entered the car to leave, two detectives approached, asked whether they had drugs (both said no), obtained identification (Ismael Ornelas-Ledesma’s California license listed a Martinez address) and received consent to search the car; the Government conceded that a reasonable person would not have felt free to leave. After a negative search of the trunk, Detective Luedke found that a right rear interior panel felt loose and testified that rust in the groove of a nearby vent-cover screw indicated recent removal. He removed the screw, the vent cover and then the panel and found about two kilograms of cocaine. The parties stipulated that the screw bore no rust, and the magistrate judge disbelieved the rust testimony. Before the first appeal, petitioners also disputed that the panel was loose, and the district court denied their request for a joint inspection of the car. The consent did not extend to dismantling the panel, and the Government did not contend that the stop or the consent justified it, so the search’s validity turned on probable cause.

  The magistrate judge heard the witnesses at evidentiary hearings in January 1993 and found the historical facts. The district court adopted those findings, except one concerning whether an officer’s gun was visible, and decided probable cause without hearing witnesses; on remand the magistrate judge resolved the loose-panel credibility question on the existing record by stipulation, and the district court accepted that finding. The United States describes the facts as undisputed. The dispute in this Court concerns how appellate courts review whether those facts amount to reasonable suspicion and probable cause.

#### Additional public case information

- **Conflict / Percolation:** The invited amicus states that the courts of appeals are divided on whether district-court probable-cause and reasonable-suspicion conclusions are reviewed de novo or for clear error. He contrasts the Seventh Circuit (*Spears*; *United States v. Adebayo*, 985 F.2d 1333) with the Ninth (*United States v. Normandeau*, 800 F.2d 953, relying on *United States v. McConney*, 728 F.2d 1195 (en banc)), and notes that the First Circuit (*United States v. Zapata*, 18 F.3d 971) and Sixth Circuit (*United States v. Padro*, 52 F.3d 120) have moved from clear-error to de novo review. *Spears*, as quoted by amici AELE and NDAA, cited the First, Eighth and Tenth Circuits as reviewing probable cause for clear error. Petitioners note that four Seventh Circuit judges dissented from denial of rehearing en banc in *Spears*.
- **Principal arguments:**
  - *Petitioners:* The standard of review follows whether an issue is one of fact, law or a mixed question; mixed questions are generally reviewed independently unless the trial court is better positioned, as with credibility, demeanor, competency or juror bias (*Townsend v. Sain*; *Miller v. Fenton*). This Court has reviewed probable cause and reasonable suspicion independently (*Carroll*, *Brinegar*, *Ker*, *Beck*, *Reid*, *Cortez*, *Sokolow*, *Alabama v. White*), and *Thompson v. Keohane*’s treatment of an objective reasonable-person standard applied to historical facts applies equally here. Probable cause is a legal conclusion; de novo review serves law development, uniformity, guidance to police and careful district-court findings (*Salve Regina*). *Gates*’ deference to issuing magistrates rests on the preference for warrants, which *Spears*’ uniform clear-error rule erodes, and *Stone v. Powell* presupposes full and fair consideration on appeal. Deference is especially inapt here because the district judge, who heard no witnesses, twice rejected the magistrate judge’s conclusion that probable cause was lacking. Petitioners assert that independent review would have led to suppression, but they ask only for a remand under the de novo standard.
  - *United States:* Reasonable suspicion and probable cause involve two steps: the historical facts known to the officers, reviewed for clear error, and an objective judgment whether those facts justify the stop or search, which is a legal or mixed question ordinarily reviewed independently under the usual fact/law allocation (*First Options*; *Salve Regina*). *Thompson* is closely analogous, and *Ker*, *Beck*, *Elkins*, *Carroll*, *Brinegar*, *Terry*, *Cortez*, *Sokolow* and *White* reflect independent review. Independent review lets appellate courts expound the standards, guide officers and courts, and secure consistent application of a constitutional right. *Gates*’ substantial-basis review reflects the preference for warrants and does not govern warrantless cases; its remark that one probable-cause determination will seldom be a useful precedent was not a holding on this question. The categories that receive deferential review do not fit: supervision of litigation (*Cooter & Gell*; *Pierce*), credibility-dependent subjective judgments such as competency and juror bias, and jury-administered negligence. Deference is equally unwarranted toward a trial court’s conclusion that probable cause was lacking, which the Government may appeal under 18 U.S.C. §3731.
  - *Invited amicus (Isakoff):* The allocation turns on which court is better positioned (*Miller*; *First Options*’ reference to the respective institutional advantages of trial and appellate courts). Probable cause and reasonable suspicion are non-technical, totality-of-the-circumstances judgments made from an experienced officer’s perspective, and *Gates* observed that one determination will seldom be a useful precedent for another. District courts are better placed to weigh physical and testimonial evidence, and clear-error review still corrects legal errors and unambiguously erroneous applications. This Court has never directly confronted the question: *Ker* and *Beck*, which reviewed state courts, did not state that they applied de novo rather than clear-error review; *Cortez* noted the clear-error formulation below with apparent approval; and *Brinegar*’s statement is hard to reconcile with *Gates*. Clear-error review would apply equally to government appeals from suppression orders, so it is neutral between the parties, and it leaves the advantages of warrants intact (*Leon*; doubtful cases resolved in favor of warrants). De novo review would subject district-court decisions made after adversary hearings to more scrutiny than ex parte warrant decisions, and to more than sufficiency review of guilt (*Jackson v. Virginia*). That the district judge here did not hear the witnesses does not change the analysis: deference rests on trial-court expertise, the judge could have reheard the evidence, and separate standards for magistrate-referred cases would be unduly complex.
  - *Other amici:* The ACLU, the ACLU of Wisconsin and NACDL argue that probable cause and reasonable suspicion are objective legal judgments, not historical facts, that this Court has traditionally reviewed de novo; that *Gates*’ deference concerns ex ante warrant decisions, not post hoc warrantless ones; and that clear-error review of consent (*Schneckloth*), which concerns a suspect’s state of mind, is not relevant. AELE and NDAA argue that the question is essentially factual and best resolved by suppression and trial courts under *Gates*’ totality approach, that one standard without de novo review should govern warrant and warrantless cases, and that de novo review would burden appellate courts and produce inconsistent rulings, as in Miranda cases.

**Stansbury**

**Current rule:** Custody turns on the objective circumstances confronting the person questioned and whether a reasonable person in that position would understand freedom of movement to be restrained to the degree associated with formal arrest. An officer’s uncommunicated view of the person as suspect or witness does not enter the calculation. Beliefs conveyed by words or conduct may affect the objective setting to the extent they would affect that reasonable understanding. Communicating suspicion does not itself establish custody, and an officer’s private intent cannot substitute for the objective inquiry.

- **Case Name:** Quackenbush v. Allstate Insurance Co.
- **Citation / Docket:** 517 U.S. 706; No. 95-244
- **October Term:** 1995
- **Entry Stage / Event Type:** Granted merits review; decision pending in this simulation.
- **Question Presented:** (1) Whether the court of appeals erred in holding that an order remanding a removed action to state court on abstention grounds is reviewable by appeal under the collateral-order doctrine of *Cohen v. Beneficial Industrial Loan Corp.*, 337 U.S. 541, rather than only by mandamus. (2) Whether the court of appeals erred in holding that the abstention powers of federal courts are limited to actions in equity or, alternatively, in limiting the exercise of *Burford* abstention solely to actions in equity.
- **Question Granted:** Certiorari was granted October 16, 1995, on both questions in the Commissioner’s petition (filed August 11, 1995), as stated above; no additional question or plenary grant is inferred. Allstate’s merits brief restates the questions as whether an order remanding a case on *Burford* grounds is reviewable by appeal and not merely by mandamus, and whether an action at law against a private party seeking money damages can present the exceptional circumstances necessary for a federal court to decline to exercise its jurisdiction under *Burford*; the Commissioner’s reply objects under Rule 14.1(a) that this restatement changes the substance of the second question.
- **Legal Area:** Federal courts — abstention, remand, and appellate finality
- **Court and Judgment Under Review:** The Ninth Circuit, *Garamendi v. Allstate Insurance Co.*, 47 F.3d 350 (No. 91-55855, decided February 2, 1995; petition for rehearing denied and suggestion for rehearing en banc rejected May 19, 1995; mandate issued May 31, 1995), reviewed the District Court’s July 1, 1991 order remanding the removed action to state court on *Burford* abstention grounds. It held that 28 U.S.C. §1447(d) did not bar review because the remand rested on no ground specified in §1447(c); that the abstention-based remand was a final collateral order reviewable on appeal, so it treated Allstate’s filing as an appeal rather than as the mandamus petition Allstate had requested in the alternative; and that a district court may not abstain under *Burford* when the plaintiff seeks only legal relief. It did not reach Allstate’s further argument that *Burford*’s requirements were not met. Its judgment reads: “The district court’s remand order is VACATED and the case is REMANDED for proceedings consistent with this opinion.” Neither the opinion nor the order denying rehearing rules on Allstate’s motion to compel arbitration or directs arbitration or a stay.
- **Requested Supreme Court Disposition / Relief:** The Commissioner (petitioner) asks that the Ninth Circuit’s judgment be reversed and the case remanded with appropriate directions, contending that under *Thermtron* a remand order is not a final judgment, that this order is not an appealable *Cohen* collateral order, and that it was reviewable only by mandamus (or by §1292(b) certification if both courts concur); and that federal courts’ abstention authority, including *Burford* abstention, is not confined to actions in equity under the merged federal system. Allstate (respondent) asks that the judgment be affirmed, contending that the remand was a final decision appealable under §1291 because, like an abstention-based dismissal, it surrendered federal jurisdiction, and alternatively was an appealable *Cohen* collateral order; that *Burford* permits a federal court to withhold only equitable relief that would restrain or interfere with state administrative proceedings or decisions and disrupt state policymaking on matters of distinctively local concern, and so affords no discretion to decline jurisdiction over this removed action at law for contract damages; and that, at a minimum, the District Court could not abstain without deciding Allstate’s pending Federal Arbitration Act motion. If review by appeal was unavailable, Allstate asks that the judgment be affirmed on the alternative ground that mandamus should issue, or that the case be remanded for the Ninth Circuit to address its mandamus request. Any decision must stay within the presented issues and source-authorized remedy.
- **Material Facts and Record:** California’s Insurance Commissioner, as liquidator and trustee of the insolvent Mission Insurance companies (ordered into liquidation by the Los Angeles Superior Court in 1987), sued Allstate and other reinsurers in that court in 1990 on the same causes of action as an earlier suit against Mission’s reinsurers (*Gillespie I*) that had been consolidated with the liquidation proceeding. The liquidation court’s orders assert sole and exclusive jurisdiction over the Mission companies’ assets and enjoin actions against those companies or the liquidator without that court’s leave; its conservation and liquidation orders also authorize the liquidator to bring legal or equitable actions in that or other states. Allstate filed claims in the liquidation proceeding in 1987; the Commissioner says they present the same setoff contentions as Allstate’s defenses here, while Allstate says the proofs of claim reproduced in the Joint Appendix were not before the District Court and have since been superseded. Against all defendants, the complaint seeks a declaration of the parties’ rights under the reinsurance agreements and of the amounts the defendants are obligated to pay, and, on a contract count, the sums due plus special damages; its conspiracy and tort counts, seeking damages according to proof and punitive damages, are pleaded against Doe defendants. Allstate removed the action on diversity grounds in August 1990 and moved under the Federal Arbitration Act to compel arbitration under arbitration clauses in the reinsurance agreements and to stay the litigation pending arbitration; the District Court deferred that motion until it decided the Commissioner’s motion to remand. On July 1, 1991, the District Court (C.D. Cal., No. CV 90-4713-WMB) held that it had jurisdiction, rejecting the Commissioner’s argument that the liquidation court’s in rem control of Mission’s assets excluded federal jurisdiction and noting that the liquidation court had authorized the Commissioner to seek redress in other forums; held that *Colorado River* abstention did not apply because there was no concurrent state litigation to which Allstate was a party; but abstained under *Burford*. It treated as critical Allstate’s anticipated defense that it may set off balances Mission owes it under other reinsurance contracts (Cal. Ins. Code §1031), a contested state-law question it understood to be before the California Supreme Court, and concluded that federal adjudication could undermine California’s interest in uniform and orderly insurance liquidations through inconsistent rulings. It rejected Allstate’s contentions, based on *New Orleans Public Service, Inc. v. Council of City of New Orleans*, 491 U.S. 350 (*NOPSI*), that *Burford* applies only to federal courts acting in equity and only to interference with state administrative proceedings or orders, reading *NOPSI* as a caution against extending the doctrine rather than as such a limit. It declined Allstate’s alternative request to stay the action pending a California Supreme Court setoff ruling, reasoning that it was unclear what would remain to adjudicate afterward, and remanded the entire action to state court without ruling on the arbitration motion. In 1992 the California Supreme Court decided *Prudential Reinsurance Co. v. Superior Court*, 3 Cal. 4th 1118, permitting some reinsurer setoffs and barring others. The Commissioner states that disputes over the application of that decision and of §1031 remain open. One such dispute, an appeal from a March 8, 1994 liquidation-court setoff ruling, was decided for the Commissioner in December 1995 (*Quackenbush v. Imperial Casualty & Indemnity Co.*, 41 Cal. App. 4th 828): the Court of Appeal held that *Prudential* barred the setoff at issue and that the parties’ settlement agreement foreclosed the reinsurer’s alternative single-entity theory; the Commissioner describes the decision as resting primarily on that settlement agreement. Allstate, which has not answered, states that it will dispute the claims and assert setoff, and that a federal court can apply §1031 and *Prudential* as ordinary state law.

Issue 2 — whether federal abstention authority, or *Burford* abstention specifically, is limited to actions in equity, and whether this action is one at law. The order reviewed below is the District Court’s abstention-based remand. Below, the Commissioner argued that §1447(d) barred review by appeal or mandamus; a Ninth Circuit motions panel denied his motion to dismiss the appeal, holding under *Thermtron* that §1447(d) does not apply to a remand not based on jurisdictional grounds, and referred to the merits panel whether the appeal should instead be treated as a mandamus petition; the merits panel likewise held §1447(d) inapplicable. The petition’s questions do not present the §1447(d) point. Allstate treats it as uncontested; the Commissioner’s reply states that he does not concede it and would view all remand orders as within §1447(d)’s bar, acknowledging that *Thermtron* holds otherwise on its facts but citing the Kennedy concurrence in *Things Remembered, Inc. v. Petrarca* as leaving the question open in other contexts. No federal court has ruled on Allstate’s motion to compel arbitration and to stay the litigation pending arbitration. The Ninth Circuit did not reach whether *Burford*’s requirements were otherwise met; the Commissioner says that whether the District Court properly exercised any discretion to abstain is not before this Court, while Allstate argues that no *Burford* basis exists here in any event. Allstate states that the Commissioner has not challenged the District Court’s *Colorado River* ruling, and objects that abstention grounds based on *Louisiana Power & Light Co. v. City of Thibodaux* or *Fair Assessment in Real Estate Ass’n v. McNary*, as urged by the Commissioner’s amici, were not raised below; the Commissioner cites those decisions in support of his position that abstention is not confined to equity. On rehearing the Ninth Circuit declined to consider, as waived because first raised in the rehearing petition, the Commissioner’s argument that the declaratory-relief count seeks more than legal relief. In this Court the Commissioner argues that the declaratory claim and Allstate’s setoff claims are equitable under California law, that Allstate’s own requested stay pending arbitration and setoff are equitable relief, and that this suit to marshal an insolvent insurer’s assets is part of quasi in rem receivership proceedings rather than an ordinary in personam action between private parties; Allstate answers that the declaratory request merely recasts the damages claim, that the argument was waived, and that the liquidator sues as trustee for the Mission estate on the companies’ contract claims. - **Question Presented:** Petitioner presents three questions: (1) whether Congress intended South Dakota’s legislative definition of “interest” to define the federal term “interest” in §30 of the National Bank Act, 12 U.S.C. §85, and thereby displace other States’ contract laws limiting the form or the amount of liquidated damages for late payments on revolving credit accounts; (2) whether Congress may constitutionally delegate to South Dakota the power to define the federal lending term “interest” for all fifty States so as to preempt other States’ contract laws; and (3) whether, as a matter of federal law, “interest at the rate” in §85 includes contingent, sum-certain penalty charges (late fees), so as to preempt state limitations on the form or the amount of contractual liquidated damages on revolving credit accounts. Respondent contends that only the third question is actually presented and would phrase it as whether “interest” in §85 includes charges by a national bank for a borrower’s failure to make timely payment of amounts due on credit card accounts.
- **Question Granted:** Certiorari was granted on January 19, 1996. The supplied sources do not show a grant limited to particular questions. Petitioner treats all three questions as accepted for review; respondent contends that the first two are not actually presented (see Preservation / Threshold / Vehicle Issues).
- **Legal Area:** Banking law, federal preemption and administrative law — meaning of “interest” in National Bank Act §30 (12 U.S.C. §85), with related nondelegation and agency-deference questions
- **Court and Judgment Under Review:** Supreme Court of California, 11 Cal. 4th 138, 900 P.2d 690 (judgment entered September 1, 1995), 5–2, affirming the Court of Appeal (26 Cal. App. 4th 1767, decided over a dissent), which had affirmed the Superior Court’s order granting Citibank’s motion for judgment on the pleadings. The Superior Court first denied the motion; on Citibank’s petition for a writ of mandate, the Court of Appeal directed it to grant the motion or show cause, and it then granted the motion. The majority (Justice Mosk) stated that “[t]he issue is not the existence of preemption under section 85,” because under *Marquette* a borrower’s State’s conflicting law “must, of course, give way to the federal statute,” and concluded that §85 “interest” “could include ... a late payment fee, payable contingently in the event of default after maturity,” relying on the legal usage of “interest” in 1864, the Act’s most-favored-lender purpose and the Comptroller of the Currency’s interpretations; it held that §85 permits a national bank to collect such charges if lenders in its home State may, so that contrary California law is preempted. The court also stated that Congress has “entrusted the question of the lawfulness of a national bank’s late payment fees to its home state and to its home state alone,” and that “Congress has made no such delegation” to the States (Pet. App. 30). The parties read the decision differently. Petitioner says it in effect let South Dakota law define “interest” and “rate” and thus the scope of preemption, applied the presumption against preemption only to the existence and not the scope of preemption, and reversed that presumption (“Had Congress intended to limit preemption, it would have doubtless made itself plain”); she also says the court presumably declined to defer to the Comptroller because of inconsistencies while noting agreement with its current position. Citibank says the court decided the federal meaning of “interest” and disclaimed any delegation. Two justices dissented; petitioner describes them as concluding that §85 “interest” does not include the sum-certain contract penalties at issue and that a national bank’s power to impose such charges rests on 12 U.S.C. §24 rather than §85, and Citibank describes the dissent as resting on a “plain meaning” of “interest” limited to periodic percentage charges.
- **Requested Supreme Court Disposition / Relief:** Petitioner asks that the California Supreme Court’s judgment be reversed; respondent asks that it be affirmed. Any decision must stay within the presented issues and source-authorized remedy.
- **Material Facts and Record:** Citibank (South Dakota), N.A. is a national bank located in Sioux Falls, South Dakota, with no branches elsewhere; from there it issues Visa, Mastercard and other credit cards to customers nationwide. Its card agreements generally provide for percentage finance charges on certain balances and for late-payment charges, which may be flat or percentage-based. Petitioner Barbara Smiley, a California resident, held two Citibank cards. Each agreement imposed a monthly finance charge on certain balances (a percentage of the average daily balance or a 50-cent minimum, whichever was larger), which she does not challenge. The Classic Card agreement imposed a $15 late charge if the minimum payment was more than 25 days overdue. The Preferred Card agreement imposed $6 if the minimum payment was more than 15 days overdue and, if still delinquent at 30 days, an additional charge of the greater of $15 or 0.65% of the current balance, assessed again for each succeeding month in which the minimum payment remained more than 30 days overdue. South Dakota law defines interest as “the compensation allowed by law for the use, or forbearance, or detention of money or its equivalent, including without limitation, points, loan origination fees, credit service or carrying charges, charges for unanticipated late payments, and any other charges, direct or indirect, as an incident to or as a condition of the extension of credit” (S.D. Codified Laws §54-3-1), and provides that, unless a maximum is specifically established elsewhere in the code, there is no maximum interest rate or charge when the parties establish it by written agreement (§54-3-1.1). Petitioner does not dispute that Citibank is located only in South Dakota or that South Dakota law permits these charges. On July 7, 1992, she filed a putative class action in Los Angeles Superior Court alleging that the late charges are unlawful penalties under Cal. Civ. Code §1671, which, for consumer contracts, makes a liquidated-damages provision void unless the parties agree on a presumed amount of damage in a case where it would be impracticable or extremely difficult to fix the actual damage; Citibank describes the California standard as limiting late charges to a “fair average compensation” for the lender’s anticipated loss, as estimated in a lender study, and says petitioner seeks a trial on whether the charges meet it. The complaint alleges that the fees bear no relation to the outstanding balance, the amount owed, the number of days late or the bank’s processing costs, are imposed in addition to continuing finance charges, and accompany a contractual default that the bank does not waive even if the fee is paid. Citibank describes them as charges for keeping the bank’s money past its due date that reflect delinquent borrowers’ higher funding, collection and loss costs. The case was decided on the pleadings; there are no findings on the fees’ purpose, relation to cost or market effects. California legislation effective January 1, 1995 (Cal. Fin. Code §§4000–4001) permits banks located in California to assess specified credit-card late charges; Citibank says its charges are consistent with those charges, while petitioner says certain Preferred Card fees are imposed twice per billing period in violation of §4001 and that under the decision below those limits would not apply to out-of-state national banks.
- **Preservation / Threshold / Vehicle Issues:** *Scope of the questions.* Both parties state that the meaning of “interest” in §85 is a question of federal law; Citibank adds that a State can neither enlarge nor shrink the federal definition. Citibank contends that petitioner’s first two questions are therefore not presented, because neither it nor the California Supreme Court claims that South Dakota law defines the federal term, and there has been no delegation. Petitioner replies that the first two issues “accepted for review” remain pivotal because Citibank’s reading still lets the home State’s law determine which charges are included in the exported “rate” and thus the preemptive scope of §85. *Posture.* Review is of a judgment on the pleadings; petitioner contends that Citibank’s economic and reliance assertions are outside the record. *The Comptroller’s regulation.* 12 C.F.R. §7.4001(a) (61 Fed. Reg. 4849, 4869) states that “interest” as used in 12 U.S.C. §85 “includes any payment compensating a creditor or prospective creditor for an extension of credit, making available of a line of credit, or any default or breach by a borrower of a condition upon which credit was extended,” including “numerical periodic rates, late fees, not sufficient funds (NSF) fees, overlimit fees, annual fees, cash advance fees, and membership fees,” but ordinarily not appraisal fees, premiums and commissions attributable to insurance guaranteeing repayment, finders’ fees, fees for document preparation or notarization, or fees incurred to obtain credit reports. According to the Comptroller’s preamble (61 Fed. Reg. 4858), paragraph (b) clarifies that one looks to state law to determine what lending charges are permitted for the most favored lender and thus for national banks, and paragraph (c), added in the final rule after comments from Arkansas commenters, confirms that the federal definition does not change a State’s definition of interest (or how it is used) solely for state-law purposes, so that a late fee allowed but not counted as interest under home-State law may be charged and exported but is not treated as interest in evaluating compliance with that State’s usury limits. The rule was proposed on March 3, 1995 (60 Fed. Reg. 11924, 11929), before the California Supreme Court’s September 1, 1995 judgment, which, according to the preamble, cited the agency’s recent interpretive opinions and the proposed rule as consistent with its reasoning. After notice and comment it was published in final form on February 9, 1996, with a stated effective date of April 1, 1996, after that judgment, petitioner’s November 30, 1995 petition and the January 19, 1996 grant. The preamble describes the final rule as substantially identical to the proposal apart from paragraph (c), as reflecting current case law and the Comptroller’s interpretive letters, and as reflecting positions the agency has taken as amicus curiae in pending litigation. *Deference.* Petitioner contends that the regulation, which she says was issued on the eve of argument, receives no deference: in §86 Congress committed private §§85–86 actions to the courts without delegating to the Comptroller authority to expand, contract or define their scope (*Adams Fruit Co. v. Barrett*, 494 U.S. 638), and the question is one of statutory construction that the plain meaning resolves. She argues that the Comptroller’s views have been too inconsistent to warrant deference (*INS v. Cardoza-Fonseca*), citing a June 25, 1964 letter from Comptroller Saxon that, answering an inquiry about charges on consumer auto financing, listed “[c]harges for late payments” among charges “which would not properly be characterized as interest”; a 1988 Deputy Chief Counsel letter treating the home State’s law as defining §85 “interest”; a February 17, 1995 Chief Counsel letter treating federal law as defining “interest” to include loan-related charges, which she says was issued without the notice that 12 U.S.C. §43 requires for preemption opinions; and the final rule’s own description, as she quotes it, of a “significant change.” She argues that the proposal was issued “[i]n direct response to this and related litigation” and that its timing raises the specter of manipulation of pending litigation. In her reply brief she adds that the agency does not justify its interpretation by expertise and rests its definition on lower-court decisions (“current case law”); that the regulation is not a permissible construction because it classifies fees by label rather than substance, draws no principled line between included and excluded fees, does not address prepayment penalties, attorneys’ fees or court costs, and through paragraph (c) treats the same fee as interest for exportation but not for a home-State ceiling; that 12 U.S.C. §93a confers no preemptive rulemaking authority, and the proposal did not describe each affected state law as §43 requires; that deference is inappropriate where preemption of nonconflicting state law is at stake; that any deference must be discounted because the regulation is a post hoc, litigation-inspired reaction to credit-card fee litigation in which the agency appeared as amicus for the banks; and that it cannot be applied retroactively to control this litigation without an express congressional grant of retroactive rulemaking authority (*Bowen v. Georgetown University Hospital*, 488 U.S. 204). These reply contentions postdate Citibank’s brief, and the supplied sources contain no response from Citibank to them. Citibank responds that, where language and purpose do not resolve the question, the Court defers to the responsible agency’s permissible construction and gives the Comptroller’s reasonable reading of an ambiguous National Bank Act term “controlling weight” (*Chevron*; *NationsBank*); that no express delegation of interpretive authority is required because the Comptroller, as exclusive supervisor of national banks, examines and enforces their compliance with §85, and *NationsBank* deferred on that basis (quoting *Clarke* and *Investment Co. Institute v. Camp*); that the agency’s expertise in lending mechanics and banks’ and States’ reliance on its views make deference especially apt; that the notice-and-comment regulation codifies longstanding positions, citing agency letters from 1946 onward on set-up, collection, service and delinquency charges, annual fees and late fees, including a 1988 letter to Iowa specifically addressing Citibank’s late fees; that an agency’s present views receive deference even if they have been revised (*NationsBank*; *Good Samaritan Hospital v. Shalala*); that the 1964 letter did not refer to §85 and, according to the Comptroller, concerned state law, as the California Supreme Court also concluded, and read naturally says only that such charges were allowed in addition to the state percentage rate; that the 1988 letter reached the same conclusion on late fees by a different analytical path, and the agency has never concluded otherwise; and that the “significant change” quotation is misleading because the proposal notice (60 Fed. Reg. 11929) shows the agency did not regard codification as a change in its legal views.
- **Petitioner’s Merits Contentions:** Petitioner argues that the presumption against preemption, grounded in federalism, applies to the scope as well as the existence of preemption and requires a clear statement before §85 displaces evenhanded state contract and consumer-protection law; §85 says “interest at the rate,” not “relating to” loan charges, and §1671 places no ceiling on the interest rate Citibank may charge, so its indirect economic effect on loan pricing creates no conflict. The most-favored-lender principle (*Tiffany*) concerns only the rate of interest, “and not ... the character of contracts” national banks may make (*National Bank v. Johnson*; *Evans v. National Bank*); a national bank’s power to impose late fees derives from its contract and incidental powers under 12 U.S.C. §24, which remain subject to nondiscriminatory state law that does not prohibit or significantly interfere with them (*First National Bank v. Kentucky*; *McClellan v. Chipman*; *Anderson National Bank v. Luckett*). In reply she adds that the Act separately bars a home State’s discrimination against national banks, so a home State could not favor other lenders on late fees in any event. If home-State law determined which charges are “interest,” §85 would give one State unlimited power to set lending law for the nation, raising Article I nondelegation and Article IV concerns that call for a narrow federal construction (*First National Bank v. Dickinson*; *Panama Refining Co. v. Ryan*; *Thomas v. Washington Gas Light Co.*). As a federal term, “interest at the rate” means compensation for the use or forbearance of money measured by time and the amount owed (modern and mid-nineteenth-century dictionaries; *Deputy v. du Pont*); “rate” in §85 refers to time-based charges, and §86’s “twice the amount of interest” shows that Congress distinguished a rate from a sum-certain penalty; and the common law distinguished interest from sum-certain contract penalties, which were governed by liquidated-damages law rather than usury (*Tayloe v. Sandiford*; *Lloyd v. Scott*; *Spain v. Hamilton’s Administrator*; *Loudon v. Taxing District*; *New Orleans Insurance Co. v. Piaggio*; *Merchants’ National Bank v. Sevier*), as she reads *United States v. Texas* to confirm. She also relies on the National Currency Act’s reference to “interest for delay in the payment of money,” the 1864 debates’ references to annual percentage rates, the 1933 discount-rate alternative in §85, DIDA legislative history disclaiming any intent to override state limits on “late charges,” Regulation Z’s separation of late charges from finance charges, and the rule that §§85–86 are strictly construed because they impose penalties (*Tiffany*). She accepts that interest may include periodic percentage rates, definite up-front charges that can be amortized over the loan term, and delay damages based on the unpaid balance and measured by time, and does not contend that interest must always be stated as a percentage. In reply she states that her objection is not to the flat form or the amount of the fees but that they cannot be reckoned at the outset by time and the amount owed, so a bank could not know whether a fee would exceed a percentage ceiling until it was imposed. She reads *Citizens’ National Bank v. Donnell* as turning on compounding and *Daggs v. Phoenix National Bank* as limited to time-based rates, argues that *Greenwood Trust* and the decisions following it erred, and contends that Citibank’s economic and reliance arguments are outside the pleadings record and belong to Congress.
- **Respondent’s Merits Contentions:** Citibank argues that the case presents a single question of statutory construction and does not implicate broader preemption or federalism theory. In 1864, as now, the ordinary legal meaning of “interest” included all agreed compensation “for the use or forbearance of money, or as damages for its detention” (*Brown v. Hiatts*; see *Redfield v. Ystalyfera Iron Co.*; *Shoemaker v. United States*), including new or increased charges triggered by failure to pay on time, as contemporaneous statutes, dictionaries and cases show; courts counted flat charges as interest against percentage ceilings because otherwise those ceilings could be evaded. “Rate” then and now included a “[p]rice or amount stated or fixed on anything” (Webster, 1828), and the discount-rate clause added in 1933 did not narrow the original language. Whether a charge is a “penalty” bears on its enforceability, not on whether it is interest: *Donnell* rejected a national bank’s argument that an overdraft charge was a penalty rather than §85 interest, petitioner’s authorities do not define interest, and *United States v. Texas* addressed only the common-law right to prejudgment interest. Under *Daggs*, which disapproved the reading of *Johnson* that petitioner advances, a national bank in a State that permits any agreed charge has the same federal right, so whether a charge is excessive or noncompensatory is for the home State’s law. The most-favored-lender purpose of protecting national banks from “unfriendly state legislation” (*Tiffany*) requires including such charges, since otherwise a home State could let favored lenders charge late fees while denying them to banks (California formerly allowed state-chartered credit unions a flat $5 late fee while subjecting banks to standards that might impose lower limits), and the 1864 Congress chose “allowed” over “established” to give national banks whatever charges private parties could contract for. Once late fees are interest, *Marquette* establishes that a borrower’s State’s contrary law “must, of course, give way”; the presumption against preemption is one guide to congressional intent, not a clear-statement rule, and deserves little weight because no significant historic police power of the borrower’s State is at stake, borrowers having long obtained credit from out-of-state lenders on terms governed by the lender’s law. Citibank reads petitioner as acknowledging that percentage charges, including increased default percentages, and up-front flat charges are §85 interest, and argues that this leaves no principled basis for reserving flat default charges to the borrower’s State, as the Preferred Card’s “$15 or 0.65%” charge illustrates, and that Congress did not intend to fragment regulation of a single lending package. DIDA, whose operative language parallels §85, and TILA’s disclosure regime are irrelevant to §85’s meaning. Citibank also points to intense competition among card issuers and to the benefits of differentiated charge packages for timely payers and borrowers with weak credit histories; petitioner responds that these assertions are unsupported in the pleadings record.

**MCI Telecommunications**

**Current rule:** Section 203(b)(2) permits the Commission, in its discretion and for good cause shown, to modify requirements made by or under §203 in particular instances or by general order applicable to special circumstances or conditions; no required notice period may exceed 120 days. That authority permits adjustments to filing form, contents, timing or place, including consequential and class-wide technical changes, but not replacement of mandatory filing throughout the nondominant long-distance sector with an optional system. The inquiry examines the obligation and whether its essential operation is adjusted or removed, not a numerical market-share threshold. Good cause alone cannot authorize the replacement. The Act’s exception for connecting carriers preserves their separate inspection duties; §203(c)’s allowance for otherwise lawful authority does not confer unlimited dispensing power.

**John Hancock**

**Current rule:** A guaranteed-benefit policy itself is a plan asset; the insurer’s underlying assets are not deemed plan assets solely because it issued the policy only to the extent the policy genuinely guarantees the amount of benefits. A nonguaranteed component does not gain the exclusion from general-account placement. Plan-asset status subjects the insurer to §3(21)(A) fiduciary duties to the extent it exercises the specified authority or control over management or disposition; breach, causation, loss and remedy remain separate. McCarran-Ferguson does not displace ERISA’s express insurance-related duties. Compatible state insurance regulation survives the saving clause, but a concrete conflicting obligation cannot control over ERISA itself. An agency interpretation cannot erase the exclusion’s component limitation.

**Citation:** 517 U.S. 781

**Docket(s):** No. 95-668

### SECTION I — NEUTRAL MODELING PACKET

- **Case Name:** Auciello Iron Works, Inc. v. NLRB
- **Citation / Docket:** 517 U.S. 781; No. 95-668
- **October Term:** 1995
- **Entry Stage / Event Type:** Granted merits review; decision pending in this simulation.
- **Question Presented:** As framed in the petition and Auciello’s merits brief: whether an employer is bound by a union’s “acceptance” of an earlier employer proposal for a collective-bargaining agreement when, at the time of the acceptance, the employer had a reasonable basis for a good-faith doubt of the union’s continued majority status and expressed that doubt within a reasonable period after being advised of the final erosion of employee support for the union, but less than one day after the union telegraphed its acceptance. As framed in the Board’s merits brief: whether the Board reasonably concluded that, after a previously certified union has accepted an employer’s offer for a collective-bargaining agreement, the employer cannot refuse to bargain with the union and withdraw recognition from it based on a good-faith doubt about the union’s majority status at the time of acceptance, when the asserted doubt is premised on events that occurred and were known to the employer before acceptance.
- **Question Granted:** Certiorari was granted on January 19, 1996 (Pet. Br. 2; Board Br. 2). The sources show no limitation or reformulation of the petition’s question; no additional question or plenary grant is inferred beyond the identified procedural stage.
- **Legal Area:** Labor law — contract acceptance and union majority status
- **Court and Judgment Under Review:** The First Circuit (60 F.3d 24, decided July 21, 1995) granted the Board’s petition under §10(e) of the National Labor Relations Act, 29 U.S.C. §160(e), and enforced in its entirety the Board’s order requiring Auciello to cease and desist from the violations found, to put in writing, sign and effectuate, on the Union’s request and retroactive to November 27, 1988, the agreement the Union accepted on that date, and to make employees whole. After the Board’s supplemental decision on remand, 317 N.L.R.B. 364 (1995), the court, treating the Board’s policy judgment as entitled to substantial deference (citing *Fall River*, 482 U.S. at 42), accepted as reasonable the Board’s rule that, absent exceptional circumstances, an employer may not raise after the union’s acceptance of the employer’s outstanding offer a good-faith doubt of majority status premised on events before that acceptance, and it declined to condition enforcement on a Board-supervised election. Its earlier decision, 980 F.2d 804 (1992), sustained the Board’s findings that the November 17, 1988 offer remained open when accepted and that membership ratification was not required, but declined enforcement at that time, retained jurisdiction and remanded for the Board to explain its refusal to consider Auciello’s majority-status defense.
- **Requested Supreme Court Disposition / Relief:** Auciello asks the Court to reverse the First Circuit’s judgment and remand to the Board for a determination whether, when it received the Union’s telegram, Auciello maintained a good-faith doubt of the Union’s majority support (Pet. Br. 37). The Board asks that the judgment be affirmed (Board Br. 45), on its rule or, alternatively, because Auciello had a reasonable period to act in any event (Board Br. 42–45). Any decision must stay within the presented issues and source-authorized remedy.
- **Material Facts and Record:** The Board certified Shopmen’s Local No. 501 (the Union) in 1977 as representative of a unit including the production and maintenance employees at Auciello’s Hudson, Massachusetts shop (about 23 employees in 1988), and the parties made successive agreements. The most recent expired on September 25, 1988; the employees struck on October 14, 1988, and bargaining continued. On November 17, 1988, Auciello presented oral and written contract proposals; the Union neither accepted nor rejected them, and Auciello did not withdraw them. Picketing stopped on November 18. On Sunday, November 27, the Union telegraphed that its negotiating committee had ratified the November 17 proposal in its entirety and that the strike had ended. On November 28 Auciello replied that the offer had been rejected and was no longer open, that ratification by the committee alone was insufficient, and that it had reason to believe the Union no longer represented a majority; it disavowed any obligation to negotiate further and refused to sign. Auciello based its asserted doubt on events preceding the acceptance, asserting that some nine unit employees had crossed the picket line, that as many as 13 had submitted resignations to the Union, and that 16 had indicated that they did not support it (980 F.2d at 809–10); before the Board, the General Counsel contended that this evidence lacked the objective basis a good-faith doubt requires, and Auciello contended the contrary (Pet. App. 18a–19a). The administrative law judge and the Board (303 N.L.R.B. 562 (1991)) found that the offer was open when accepted and that no membership ratification was required, held that the binding agreement made it unnecessary to consider Auciello’s good-faith doubt, and made no finding whether the Union actually lacked majority support. On remand the Board adhered to its rule that where objective evidence supporting a doubt is known to the employer before the union’s acceptance but not acted on, the acceptance creates a valid agreement; it found it unnecessary to decide whether Auciello’s evidence sufficed to support a reasonable good-faith doubt, and it reserved case-by-case treatment for extraordinary cases in which the doubt arises simultaneously with acceptance. Relying on the testimony of Auciello’s vice president—who described a November 28 management review, after the telegram, of each employee’s union sentiment, but also acknowledged that management had prepared a list of employees likely to vote against union representation toward the end of the strike and took no action on it because negotiations were ongoing and the list represented a “worst case scenario”—the Board found that Auciello harbored its doubt while its offer was outstanding (Pet. App. 35a–36a). The parties read the timing differently. Auciello says that its earlier indications (some picket-line crossings and resignations, earlier expressions of dissatisfaction by one or two employees, and a few replacements) were an inadequate basis as of November 17, and that the final erosion of support surfaced on and after Friday, November 18, when the picket line came down, and during the Thanksgiving-shortened week of November 21—returning employees’ statements of disaffection, further resignations, and reports from apparent Union supporters that they had taken permanent jobs elsewhere—so that it was still assessing those events when the Sunday-evening telegram arrived (Pet. Br. 5–7, 29–31 & n.8). The Board says that Auciello had received most or all of its evidence by Monday, November 21: the picket line came down November 18, 12 of the 13 resignation forms are dated on or before November 21 and the other November 23, the vice president testified that many expressions of dissatisfaction came on or before November 18, and four employees reported taking jobs elsewhere by November 18 (Board Br. 43–44). Auciello argues that an employer should be entitled to raise its doubt as to the union’s majority status as of the date of acceptance; that a presumption of majority status must rest on a lawfully established relationship, so that an agreement accepted by a union lacking majority support cannot create one consistently with §8(a)(2), employees’ §7 rights and *International Ladies’ Garment Workers’ Union v. NLRB*, 366 U.S. 731 (1961) (*Garment Workers*), and that the presumption in the 1988 negotiations rested on the expired agreement rather than the 1977 certification, none of the 1988 employees having been employed in 1977; that because an employer acting prematurely on a doubt risks unfair-labor-practice liability, while one that keeps dealing with a minority union risks violating §8(a)(2), it should at least have a reasonable period to assess the erosion and voice its doubt, which it lacked before the acceptance; and that deference is unwarranted where the Board’s rule rests on an erroneous legal foundation or is inconsistent with the Act. The Board argues that its rule is a rational policy choice consistent with the Act and entitled to deference; that the rule keeps an employer from sitting on a known doubt and raising it only if the agreement later proves unfavorable, gives a newly formed agreement stability, and avoids post hoc litigation of an unarticulated doubt; that the employer controls whether and when to assert its doubt and may voice it, withdraw its offer or petition for an election before acceptance, while employees could have sought decertification between September 25 and November 27, 1988; that *Garment Workers* involved initial recognition of a union that actually lacked majority support, whereas this certified Union’s presumption was unchallenged at acceptance; and, alternatively, that even if an employer is entitled to a reasonable period, Auciello had one.
- **Preservation / Threshold / Vehicle Issues:** The petition’s question is framed in terms of good-faith doubt. ### SECTION I — NEUTRAL MODELING PACKET

- **Case Name:** Richards v. Jefferson County
- **Citation / Docket:** 517 U.S. 793; No. 95-386
- **October Term:** 1995
- **Entry Stage / Event Type:** Granted merits review; decision pending in this simulation.
- **Question Presented:** (1) Whether the Due Process Clause of the Fourteenth Amendment (the petition also invokes equal protection) permits a state court to hold taxpayers’ federal constitutional challenges to a county occupational tax barred by res judicata (or, as the respondents alternatively urge, by issue preclusion) on the basis of the judgment in an earlier taxpayer suit to which they were not parties, where the state court held that the earlier taxpayer-plaintiffs adequately represented them and that the claims could have been, and arguably were, adjudicated in that suit. (2) Whether the tax’s exclusions — for domestic servants employed in private homes and for businesses, professions or occupations for which license fees are required to be paid under the County’s general license code or to the State or County under listed Alabama Code provisions — are consistent with the Equal Protection Clause (the petition also invokes due process), a question no court below decided. By its March 15, 1996 order (below), the Court removed question (2) from review; only question (1) remains before the Court.
- **Question Granted:** The petition was granted on November 22, 1995, on both issues it presented (Supreme Court docket No. 95-386, entry 11; respondents’ merits brief 24). On March 15, 1996, before argument, the Court ordered: “The writ of certiorari is dismissed as improvidently granted to the extent that it includes the question whether the occupational tax challenged by petitioners violates the Equal Protection Clause of the United States Constitution. The parties are directed to address at oral argument only the remaining question: whether the Due Process Clause of the United States Constitution prohibits the application of res judicate in this case.” (docket entry 21; local pre-argument docket copy `sources/A_micro_IA40385013_0653 01. Docket_pre-argument.txt`). The submitted scope is therefore the due-process question only; no broader plenary grant is inferred.
- **Legal Area:** Procedural due process — nonparty preclusion; equal protection — tax classifications
- **Court and Judgment Under Review:** Supreme Court of Alabama, *Jefferson County v. Richards*, 662 So. 2d 1127 (Ala. 1995) (decided March 31, 1995; rehearing overruled June 9, 1995). On a permissive interlocutory appeal from the trial court’s denial of summary judgment on the federal constitutional claims, it reversed and remanded, holding those claims barred by res judicata under the judgment in *Bedingfield v. Jefferson County*, 527 So. 2d 1270 (Ala. 1988). According to the respondents, the trial court had certified for the interlocutory appeal both whether the federal constitutional claims were barred by res judicata and whether Act No. 406 denies equal protection under the United States Constitution; the Alabama Supreme Court decided only the res judicata question and did not reach the equal-protection question.
- **Requested Supreme Court Disposition / Relief:** The petitioners ask the Court to reverse the Alabama Supreme Court’s decision and remand for trial, and invite the Court to decide that the exemption scheme cannot stand under equal protection and due process. The respondents ask that the judgment be affirmed “with such modifications as this Court deems appropriate” and that, regardless of the Court’s disposition of that judgment, the Court decide the federal equal-protection question and render judgment upholding the tax. Any decision must stay within the presented issues and source-authorized remedy.
- **Material Facts and Record:** *The tax.* Act No. 406 (approved September 7, 1967), applicable only to counties of 500,000 or more, authorizes the county governing body to levy a license or privilege tax on any person for engaging in any business (defined to include a vocation, occupation, calling or profession) for which the person is not required to pay a license or privilege tax to the State or county under specified state license provisions. Its stated purpose is “to equalize the burden of taxation,” and its §6 caps the county rate at the rate the county’s largest municipality levies on the same or similar activity. Jefferson County Ordinance No. 1120, adopted in 1987 (on September 29, 1987, according to the respondents) and effective January 1, 1988, imposes license fees measured by one-half percent of the gross compensation of persons working in the county, withheld by employers. Its definition of covered work excludes “domestic servants employed in private homes” and “businesses, professions or occupations for which license fees are required to be paid” under the County’s general license code or to the State or County under listed Alabama Code provisions. Petitioners Jason Richards, a courier employed by a law firm, and Fannie Hill, a domestic servant employed in commercial buildings, pay the tax. The parties dispute how far the exclusions reach. The petitioners describe the scheme as exempting persons employed “by or as” a long list of licensed businesses and professions, and allege that high-earning physicians and attorneys pay a license fee of $150 or less instead of the tax while ordinary wage earners pay it. The respondents state that only individuals who must themselves hold a state license (chiefly state-regulated professionals), proprietors paying listed license taxes, and private-home domestic servants are excluded, and that employees of license-paying business entities pay the tax; they support that account with a sworn declaration of the County’s Director of Revenue included in the appendix to their merits brief. In their answer, the County defendants attributed the private-home domestic-servant exclusion to the parallel exclusion in the City of Birmingham’s occupational tax, citing §6’s rate limit.

  *Bedingfield.* *Bedingfield* consolidated two earlier challenges to the tax: one filed October 19, 1987 by the City of Birmingham and Hoyt Bedingfield, Jr., its acting director of finance, who also sued individually as a taxpayer; the other filed (November 4, 1987, according to the respondents) by three persons suing individually as employees within the county. Their complaints alleged, among other grounds, federal and state equal-protection and due-process violations; the employees’ complaint alleged that the tax denied equal protection under the Fourteenth Amendment because the Act exempts professional occupations required by State law to be licensed but not other professional and nonprofessional occupations. The circuit court upheld the tax on December 11, 1987, before the tax’s January 1, 1988 effective date. The *Bedingfield* appellate briefs argued an equal-protection issue concerning the enabling Act’s application only to counties of 500,000 or more; the respondents state that the exemption-based equal-protection argument was also presented on appeal in an Alabama AFL-CIO amicus brief. The Alabama Supreme Court affirmed on May 20, 1988, holding that Act No. 406 authorized the tax, was a general rather than local law (its population classification bearing the requisite relationship to its purpose), and provided for the tax’s collection. Justice Maddox’s dissent below and the petitioners state that neither *Bedingfield* decision refers to the Federal Constitution, and the majority below described *Bedingfield* as decided “on other State constitutional grounds”; the respondents agree that neither decision discussed whether the exemptions violate federal equal protection, but characterize the population-classification ruling as resolving a federal equal-protection challenge to that classification. *Bedingfield* was not a class action. The later plaintiffs were not parties to it; the petitioners say its plaintiffs did not purport to represent anyone but themselves. The sources contain no finding on whether the later plaintiffs had notice of, participated in, or controlled that litigation; the respondents ground privity in identity of interest in the public question rather than in any such connection.

  *This suit.* After a federal-court suit raising the same claims was dismissed under the Tax Injunction Act in April 1992 (as the respondents report), Richards and Hill, with two federal employees, filed this action in state court on April 21, 1992 against the County and its Director of Revenue under 42 U.S.C. §1983, on behalf of a putative class of non-federal employees and a putative class of federal employees subject to the tax (the federal employees also asserting Buck Act claims). They alleged that the exemptions are arbitrary and irrational in violation of the Fourteenth Amendment’s Equal Protection and Due Process Clauses and parallel state provisions, and sought declaratory and injunctive relief and compensatory damages equal to the tax each had paid since January 1, 1988. The Birmingham-Jefferson Civic Center Authority intervened, citing $10 million a year of occupational-tax proceeds that the County pledged in 1989, for twenty years, to repay the Authority’s bonds. On the County’s motion invoking *Bedingfield*, the trial court on April 8, 1993 granted summary judgment on the state constitutional claims but denied it on the federal claims, finding that neither court in *Bedingfield* had determined whether the Act or Ordinance violates federal equal protection by its classification of those taxed and exempted.

  *The judgment under review.* The Alabama Supreme Court held the federal claims barred by res judicata. It reasoned that the same cause of action was presented, because the federal equal-protection and due-process claims pleaded in *Bedingfield* could have been, and arguably were, adjudicated there and the employees’ exemption allegation was the substance of the present complaint; and that the substantial-identity-of-parties element was met because the *Bedingfield* taxpayers adequately represented these taxpayers, whose interests were “essentially identical.” It added that the County and the Birmingham-Jefferson Civic Center Authority could have relied on *Bedingfield* when, in 1989, the County pledged occupational-tax revenues for repayment of bonds the Authority issued, and that a renewed challenge would undermine the security of those bonds. Justice Maddox dissented, concluding that the same-cause-of-action element was not met because *Bedingfield* adjudicated no federal constitutional claim and the present challenge to the ordinance’s exemptions was neither presented nor adjudicated there.

  *Contentions on preclusion.* The petitioners rely on *Martin v. Wilks*, and the *Chase National Bank v. Norwalk* passage it invoked, for the principle that joinder as a party, rather than knowledge of a suit and an opportunity to intervene, is how a person becomes bound by a judgment. They argue that *Bedingfield* was not a class action; that its plaintiffs represented only themselves and neither presented for decision nor obtained a decision on any federal claim or on the exemption scheme; that they could not have challenged the exemptions then because no one was yet paying the tax; and that the ruling forecloses any hearing on their federal claims. The respondents contend that, under Alabama precedent and the decisions of many other States, absent fraud or collusion a citizen’s judgment enforcing a public right in which no private interest is involved binds other citizens, privity arising from identity of interest in the public question; that the petitioners’ own class allegations (common questions, no conflict among class members) show they assert no private right distinct from that of the *Bedingfield* plaintiffs, who fall within the class the petitioners seek to represent; that the City of Birmingham’s resources and interest assured adequate, adversarial representation; that class notice and opt-out add no significant due-process value where no private right is at stake, while adequacy of representation can be assessed when a court later decides whether to give the prior judgment preclusive effect; and that Alabama law allowed a targeted taxpayer to seek a declaratory judgment before the tax’s effective date. They acknowledge that this virtual-representation doctrine departs from the general rule and, as a state decision they quote puts it, “demands the utmost caution,” and that full claim preclusion, which reaches theories never raised, “arguably goes too far” where privity rests only on identity of interest in a public question. They urge alternatively that issue preclusion applies because the exemption-based equal-protection issue was distinctly raised in *Bedingfield* and necessarily resolved by its judgment though unmentioned in the opinions (*Grubb v. Public Utilities Commission of Ohio*). They also stress the need for finality concerning public revenue sources, the County’s and the Authority’s reliance on *Bedingfield*, including the bond pledge, and the fiscal consequences they describe if the tax were invalidated.

  *Contentions on equal protection.* The petitioners, acknowledging that they are not members of a suspect or quasi-suspect class and must show that the exemption scheme fails minimum rationality (*Hooper*), argue under decisions including *Allegheny Pittsburgh*, *Cleburne*, *Metropolitan Life Insurance Co. v. Ward*, *WHYY* and *Zobel* that no rational basis supports exempting licensed professionals and the other listed occupations, many paying small license fees, while taxing ordinary wage earners, and that Hill pays the tax for the same work that exempt domestic servants perform in private homes. The respondents answer that tax classifications, particularly occupation taxes, receive highly deferential review and must be upheld on any reasonably conceivable basis, with the challenger bearing the burden to negate every such basis and no evidentiary record required; that the petitioners’ authorities turned on the right to travel, discrimination against out-of-state entities, irrational prejudice, or assessment contrary to state law; that Act No. 406 rationally reaches only persons not already subject to state or county license taxation, building on Alabama’s license code derived from 1927 and 1935 legislation and a long-standing state policy of denying counties power to tax state-regulated professions; that retaining state regulatory control over the professions and avoiding giving professionals a reason to leave the county are conceivable bases; that the private-home domestic-servant exclusion is justified by administrative cost and the burden on households; and that the petitioners misread the exclusions’ scope. The respondents acknowledge that the reasons for the exemptions “were not adequately developed in the proceedings below” because of the County’s reliance on *Bedingfield*.
- **Preservation / Threshold / Vehicle Issues:** The judgment arose on a permissive interlocutory appeal (Ala. R. App. P. 5) from the denial of summary judgment. No court below decided the equal-protection merits or made findings on how the exemptions operate. The respondents contend that the absence of a factual record is no barrier and that a judgment may be affirmed on any proper ground; they rely in part on materials reproduced in their merits-brief appendix, including the Director of Revenue’s declaration, to contest the petitioners’ account of the exemptions, which they say the petitioners did not advance below. The petition invoked 28 U.S.C. §1254; the respondents state that jurisdiction rests instead on 28 U.S.C. §1257. Only Richards and Hill, for the non-federal-employee class, seek review; the federal-employee class’s claims are not before the Court. Otherwise none identified beyond ordinary preservation, finality, and remedy questions.

**McDermott**

**Current rule:** The plaintiff’s claim against nonsettling maritime tortfeasors is reduced by the settling tortfeasor’s equitable proportion of responsibility, not by settlement dollars. A settler is protected from contribution when the nonsettler has a meaningful opportunity to litigate and obtain a reliable determination of that proportionate share. The nonsettler then pays its own adjudicated obligation and cannot recover the plaintiff’s voluntary settlement shortfall from the settler. A reliable proportionate credit is the premise for the contribution bar, not a fact established merely by identifying a settlement. Settlement risk belongs to the settling parties; the rule does not abolish surviving joint liability or transfer ordinary insolvency risk wholesale to the injured plaintiff.

#### C. ISSUE POSITIONS

##### Issue 1: Superseding cause under comparative maritime fault

* **Threshold and Merits Reach:** Reach the governing causation rule and its application to the sustained findings. Do not review the excluded bifurcation question or recalculate navigational facts.
* **Stone’s Legal Position:** Apply comparative allocation and proximate cause as complementary doctrines rather than replace one with the other.
* **Stone’s Proposed Holding:** Reliable Transfer does not abolish superseding cause. Where supported findings establish that a later independent act was the sole proximate cause of the grounding, earlier negligence does not require comparative allocation of that loss.
* **Essential Reasoning and Record Application:** The mooring failure explains the initial emergency, but the findings identify a later opportunity for safe navigation and a distinct extraordinary failure causing the grounding. Comparative fault apportions responsibility among actors legally causing the same loss; it does not decide whether each actor is such a cause. Contract and warranty theories likewise cannot recover this loss without their required causal connection. Exxon identifies no basis within the granted questions to disregard the sustained findings. [P1, 835–842]
* **Boundary or Reserved Question:** Ordinary foreseeable reactions to an emergency do not automatically supersede its creation. The holding does not excuse all suppliers whenever a captain is also negligent or approve every bifurcation practice.
