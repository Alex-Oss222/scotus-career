#### A. CASE, RECORD, AND POSTURE

* **Case Name:** Meghrig v. KFC Western, Inc.
* **Citation or Docket:** 516 U.S. 479; No. 95-83
* **October Term:** 1995
* **Legal Field:** Environmental law; RCRA citizen-suit remedies
* **Entry Stage and Event:** Argued merits review, on writ of certiorari to the United States Court of Appeals for the Ninth Circuit, of a RCRA citizen-suit ruling.
* **Simulated Event Date:** 1996-03-19; Historical Supreme Court event.
* **Question Presented:** Whether RCRA’s citizen-suit provision, §7002(a)(1)(B), 42 U.S.C. §6972(a)(1)(B), permits a private party to recover from prior owners the costs of a cleanup it completed before filing suit, where the contamination is alleged to have presented an imminent and substantial endangerment at the time of cleanup but is not alleged to have presented one when suit was filed. The question has two components, both decided below: (1) whether the endangerment must exist when suit is filed, or may instead have existed when the cleanup occurred; and (2) whether the district court’s authority under §6972(a) “to restrain” a contributor and “to order such person to take such other action as may be necessary” includes restitution of past cleanup costs. Source-grounded formulation, not a claim to reproduce a petition verbatim.
* **Question Granted / Submitted Scope:** The issue or issues stated above are reconstructed from the published case record. No additional question is assumed granted. A summary-review event does not imply a prior plenary grant.
* **Court and Judgment Under Review:** The district court dismissed KFC’s §6972(a)(1)(B) complaint under Rule 12(b)(6) on September 21, 1992, with leave to amend, and then dismissed the amended complaint with prejudice, ruling that the provision authorizes only injunctive or other equitable relief and only where an existing, imminent danger is present. A divided Ninth Circuit panel, 49 F.3d 518 (Mar. 1, 1995) (Pregerson, J.), reversed and remanded. It held that §6972(a)(1)(B) authorizes citizen suits with respect to contamination that posed an imminent and substantial endangerment in the past, though abated before suit, and that KFC’s claim for restitution of cleanup costs falls within the district court’s authority to order a contributor “to take such other action as may be necessary,” so that §6972(a)(1)(B) “entitles KFC to bring a restitution action.” Judge Brunetti dissented and would have affirmed the dismissal of the amended complaint.
* **Relief Requested:** Petitioners Alan and Margaret Meghrig, the former owners, seek reversal of the Ninth Circuit’s judgment, leaving the district court’s dismissal of the RCRA claim in place; the United States, as amicus curiae supporting petitioners, also urges reversal. Respondent KFC seeks affirmance, so that its §6972(a)(1)(B) claim for restitution of its completed cleanup costs may proceed on remand.
* **Material Record:** The case arises on a Rule 12(b)(6) dismissal, so the amended complaint’s allegations of material fact are taken as true. In September 1975 the Meghrigs sold KFC real property in Los Angeles on which KFC operates a Kentucky Fried Chicken restaurant. KFC alleges that the soil was then contaminated with elevated levels of refined petroleum products (lead and benzene) resulting from the Meghrigs’ operation of a gasoline station on the property. In October 1988, while improving the property, KFC discovered the contamination. The City of Los Angeles Department of Building and Safety stopped construction pending soil analysis and clearance from the County health department, and local authorities ordered KFC, which as alleged neither caused the contamination nor owned the property when it occurred, to clean it up. KFC spent over $211,000 to assess and remove the contaminated soil for off-site disposal, completed the cleanup in 1989, and in June 1990 asked the Meghrigs to reimburse those costs; they refused. KFC sued under §6972(a)(1)(B) on May 29, 1992, about three years after completing the cleanup. Its amended complaint alleges that the contaminated soil, at the time of cleanup, presented an imminent and substantial endangerment to public health and the environment by threatening surrounding groundwater and potentially the health of people expected to use the property, and characterizes the relief sought as “equitable restitution”; it does not allege an endangerment existing when suit was filed. The Ninth Circuit stated that CERCLA’s petroleum exclusion and a similar exclusion in California’s Hazardous Substance Account Act leave KFC without a statutory cost-recovery remedy under either scheme. In a separate state action, the California Court of Appeal reversed a dismissal to allow KFC to amend to plead possible common-law continuing-nuisance and continuing-trespass claims, KFC Western, Inc. v. Meghrig, 23 Cal. App. 4th 1167 (1994); the United States describes that action as pending.
* **Threshold, Preservation, and Vehicle Matters:** Review below was limited to the contents of the amended complaint. The Meghrigs raised both the timing-of-endangerment question and the remedy question in moving to dismiss, and the district court and the Ninth Circuit decided both. Compliance with the 90-day notice requirement of §6972(b)(2)(A) was not an issue on appeal (49 F.3d at 522 n.4), although the United States reports that the district court indicated KFC had not given the required notice (citing Pet. App. A25). All of the costs KFC seeks relate to the cleanup it completed in 1989, before suit. The cited sources raise no bar under §6972(b)(2)(B)–(C) based on EPA or State action.
* **Relevant Dates:** Sale: September 1975. Discovery: October 1988. Cleanup completed: 1989. Reimbursement demand: June 1990. Complaint: May 29, 1992. First dismissal: September 21, 1992. Ninth Circuit decision: March 1, 1995. Argument: January 10, 1996. Event: March 19, 1996, historical date.

#### Enacted and surviving authorities

RCRA §7002(a)(1)(B), 42 U.S.C. §6972(a)(1)(B), provides that “any person may commence a civil action on his own behalf ... against any person ... including any ... past or present owner or operator of a treatment, storage, or disposal facility, who has contributed or who is contributing to the past or present handling, storage, treatment, transportation, or disposal of any solid or hazardous waste which may present an imminent and substantial endangerment to health or the environment.” Section 6972(a) gives the district court jurisdiction “to restrain any person who has contributed or who is contributing to the past or present handling, storage, treatment, transportation, or disposal of any solid or hazardous waste referred to in paragraph (1)(B), to order such person to take such other action as may be necessary, or both.” Under §6972(b)(2), such an action may not be commenced before 90 days after notice of the endangerment to the Administrator, the State, and any alleged contributor, or if EPA or the State has taken specified actions under RCRA or CERCLA to address the endangerment. Section 6972(f) provides that “[n]othing in this section shall restrict any right which any person (or class of persons) may have under any statute or common law to seek enforcement of any standard or requirement relating to the management of solid waste or hazardous waste, or to seek any other relief (including relief against the Administrator or a State agency).” RCRA prescribes no limitations period for these citizen suits.

RCRA §7003(a), 42 U.S.C. §6973(a), authorizes the Administrator, “upon receipt of evidence that the past or present handling, storage, treatment, transportation or disposal of any solid waste or hazardous waste may present an imminent and substantial endangerment to health or the environment,” to sue any person who has contributed or is contributing to such handling or disposal “to restrain such person from such handling, storage, treatment, transportation, or disposal, to order such person to take such other action as may be necessary, or both,” and also to “take other action under this section including, but not limited to, issuing such orders as may be necessary to protect public health and the environment.” CERCLA §107(a)(4)(B), 42 U.S.C. §9607(a)(4)(B), expressly authorizes private recovery of response costs for releases of “hazardous substances,” a definition that excludes petroleum, 42 U.S.C. §9601(14); the Ninth Circuit identified a CERCLA time limit for cost-recovery claims in 42 U.S.C. §9612(d)(1). Whether the power to restrain a contributor and order necessary action includes a restitution remedy is contested.

Legislative materials invoked on both sides: The endangerment citizen suit was added by the Hazardous and Solid Waste Amendments of 1984, Pub. L. No. 98-616, §401, 98 Stat. 3268–3269. The House committee report describes citizens’ “limited right under Section 7002 to sue to abate an imminent and substantial endangerment,” exercised “pursuant to the standards of liability established under Section 7003” and only if the Administrator, after notice, fails to sue under §7003, and states that the expanded citizen suit “will complement, rather than conflict with, the Administrator’s efforts to eliminate threats as to public health and the environment, particularly where the Government is unable to take action because of inadequate resources.” H.R. Rep. No. 98-198, pt. 1, at 53 (1983). A 1979 House subcommittee staff report (the Eckhardt Report) states that imminence in §7003 “applies to the nature of the threat rather than identification of the time when the endangerment initially arose,” so that the section “may be used for events which took place at some time in the past but which continue to present a threat to the public health or the environment.” Staff of Subcomm. on Oversight and Investigations, House Comm. on Interstate and Foreign Commerce, 96th Cong., 1st Sess., Hazardous Waste Disposal 32 (Comm. Print 96-IFC 31, 1979).

Supreme Court authority invoked by the parties or the United States, as described in the cited sources: Gwaltney of Smithfield, Ltd. v. Chesapeake Bay Foundation, Inc., 484 U.S. 49 (1987), read the Clean Water Act’s authorization of citizen suits against persons “alleged to be in violation” to require allegations of continuous or intermittent violation rather than wholly past violations, and described one purpose of prefiling notice as giving the alleged violator an opportunity to come into compliance and render the citizen suit unnecessary; the Meghrigs and the United States invoke it, and the Ninth Circuit distinguished it. Hallstrom v. Tillamook County, 493 U.S. 20 (1989), treats citizen-suit prefiling notice as a mandatory condition precedent. S1 records Gwaltney as applied in United States v. Wilson (1992) for the principle that Congress’s choice of verb tense informs statutory timing, and Hallstrom as applied in McNeil v. United States (1993) to enforce a congressionally fixed prefiling sequence. Porter v. Warner Holding Co., 328 U.S. 395 (1946), and United States v. Moore, 340 U.S. 616 (1951), held that statutory authority to issue an injunction or “other order” included power to order restitution, because the full scope of a court’s equitable jurisdiction applies unless a statute “in so many words, or by a necessary and inescapable inference,” restricts it; the United States invokes them. Simulated law: Mertens v. Hewitt Associates (June 1, 1993), which the United States also cites, holds that ERISA §502(a)(3)’s “appropriate equitable relief” encompasses traditionally equitable categories of relief, not compensatory damages; it does not bar every monetary equitable remedy and leaves restitution untouched (S1; S2, “ERISA equitable categories and nonfiduciary compensation”).

Lower-court authority: The Eighth Circuit, construing §6973, held that its endangerment language “does not require the EPA to file and prosecute its RCRA action while the endangerment exists” and limits RCRA’s reach “to sites where the potential for harm is great,” and allowed the government reimbursement or an equitable award of abatement costs (United States v. Aceto Agricultural Chemicals Corp., 872 F.2d 1373, 1383 (8th Cir. 1989); United States v. Northeastern Pharmaceutical & Chemical Co. (NEPACCO), 810 F.2d 726 (8th Cir. 1986)); the dissent below read those decisions as assuming rather than deciding that reimbursement is available. The Third Circuit described §7003 as authorizing “the cleanup of a site, even a dormant one, if that action is necessary to abate a present threat,” and stated that “[r]eimbursement could thereafter be directed against those parties ultimately found to be liable.” United States v. Price, 688 F.2d 204, 213–214 (3d Cir. 1982). The Second Circuit read “may present an imminent and substantial endangerment” as not requiring a showing that actual harm will occur immediately, so long as the risk of threatened harm is present. Dague v. City of Burlington, 935 F.2d 1343, 1355–1356 (2d Cir. 1991) (reversed in part on other grounds). The Sixth and Fourth Circuits, construing the predecessor of §6972(a)(1)(A), treated RCRA citizen relief as preventive rather than compensatory. Walls v. Waste Resource Corp., 761 F.2d 311 (6th Cir. 1985); Environmental Defense Fund, Inc. v. Lamphier, 714 F.2d 331 (4th Cir. 1983). District courts had refused private reimbursement under §6972(a)(1)(B). Kaufman & Broad-South Bay v. Unisys Corp., 822 F. Supp. 1468 (N.D. Cal. 1993); Commerce Holding Co. v. Buckstone, 749 F. Supp. 441 (E.D.N.Y. 1990). After the decision below, a divided Eighth Circuit panel held that §6972(a)(1)(B) does not authorize a private action for past cleanup costs, stating that Aceto and NEPACCO “reflect no consideration of the jurisdictional issue,” and did not address whether a present endangerment is required. Furrer v. Brown, 62 F.3d 1092 (8th Cir. 1995) (as described by the United States).

Record sources: [Ninth Circuit opinion, 49 F.3d 518](https://static.case.law/f3d/49/html/0518-01.html) (local `sources/A_Meghrig_lower_CA9_49F3d518.txt`); Brief for the United States as Amicus Curiae Supporting Petitioners, No. 95-83 (Nov. 1995) (local `sources/A_micro_IA40385013_0633 11. #### C. DECISION-MATERIAL LITIGATION POSITIONS

##### Issue 1: Endangerment at commencement

* **Controlling Reasoning Below / Institutional Posture:** The Ninth Circuit permitted the suit despite completion of remediation before filing. It acknowledged that the Eckhardt Report and decisions describing an imminent hazard as a present risk (Dague; Price) support the Meghrigs’ reading, but followed the Eighth Circuit’s construction of the virtually identically worded §6973 in Aceto, which treats the endangerment language as limiting RCRA “to sites where the potential for harm is great,” not as limiting the time for filing, and which called a requirement that the government sue while the endangerment exists “absurd and unnecessary” in a reimbursement action. It distinguished Gwaltney as construing citizen-suit language that reaches only persons currently “in violation,” whereas §6972(a)(1)(B) expressly reaches persons who contributed in the past. Judge Brunetti, dissenting, read “may present” as “unambiguous” language requiring that the endangerment be occurring when suit is filed, observing that only “may or may have presented” would reach an endangerment already cleaned up, and found support in the 90-day notice requirement and the bar on citizen suits once the Administrator has sued, which together give the Administrator the first opportunity to act.
* **Petitioner or Applicant’s Position:** The enacted trigger concerns a threat existing at suit, not one removed years earlier. The Meghrigs argued below that no “imminent and substantial endangerment” existed because KFC completed the cleanup three years before suing, and invoked Gwaltney’s refusal to extend a citizen suit to wholly past violations. The United States, supporting petitioners, argues that “may present” requires a current threat, though not current harm; that Congress, which drew express past-and-present distinctions elsewhere in the same provision, could have reached activities “which have presented or may present” an endangerment but did not; that the provision’s venue, notice and governmental-action clauses likewise refer to an endangerment that “may occur” or “may present”; that Gwaltney, though addressing a different citizen remedy, calls for close attention to the statutory text; that the citizen suit was designed to supplement government enforcement, not to duplicate state-law remedies that §6972(f) preserves, so a plaintiff whose own cleanup has eliminated the threat must look to state law; and that a plaintiff who undertakes some abatement while pursuing its remedies does not lose the action so long as a threat exists when suit is filed.
* **Respondent or Competing Position:** The statute expressly reaches past contributors and past handling or disposal, and the endangerment requirement is satisfied when the waste presented an imminent and substantial endangerment at the time of cleanup, as KFC’s amended complaint alleges. KFC’s position, adopted by the Ninth Circuit, rests on the provision’s reach to any person who “has contributed” to “past or present” handling or disposal; on Aceto’s reading of the parallel §6973 endangerment language as identifying the seriousness of covered sites rather than when suit must be filed; on the distinction of Gwaltney as construing differently worded “in violation” language; and on the concern that a filing-time requirement would deny relief to owners who must clean up promptly under government order before they can sue.

##### Issue 2: Completed cleanup costs

* **Controlling Reasoning Below / Institutional Posture:** The Ninth Circuit read the power to order other necessary action as reaching reimbursement of past cleanup expense, rejecting the contention that citizens may obtain only an injunction or other equitable relief that is not the equivalent of compensatory money damages. Because Congress intended citizen suits to follow §7003’s standards of liability and worded the provisions almost identically, it read the available relief alike, relying on Aceto, NEPACCO and Price. It found the citizen notice requirement consistent with recovering cleanup costs from past contributors, since notice still informs EPA and the States, and noted that the Administrator’s own notice duty under §6973(c) has not barred governmental restitution; treated the absence of a limitations period as answerable through laches; distinguished Walls and Lamphier as involving the predecessor to §6972(a)(1)(A); disapproved the district court decisions refusing private reimbursement; and reasoned that innocent owners ordered to clean up must act promptly, cannot control the timing of cleanup, and, for petroleum, lack an adequate CERCLA or state-law remedy. Judge Brunetti, dissenting, agreed that §6973 and §6972(a)(1)(B) should be read alike, but concluded that Aceto and NEPACCO assumed rather than decided that reimbursement is available; that “such other action as may be necessary” does not contemplate reimbursement, relying on the House report’s “limited right ... to sue to abate” language and its stated goal of “the prompt abatement of imminent and substantial endangerments,” and on the Eckhardt Report’s description of §7003’s authority as including “both short- and long-term injunctive relief”; that the absence of a RCRA limitations period, in contrast with CERCLA’s express cost recovery and limitations period, indicates that Congress did not contemplate reimbursement actions; that §6972(f) preserves other claims without creating one; and that the policy concerns are for Congress, noting that nothing in the record shows KFC could not have sued before cleaning up.
* **Petitioner or Applicant’s Position:** Section 6972(a)’s remedial language provides abatement relief, not compensation for a completed cleanup. The Meghrigs argued below that the statute permits only an injunction or other equitable relief that is not the equivalent of compensatory money damages, so that KFC’s claim for its completed cleanup costs, though styled “equitable restitution,” falls outside it; that material differences between §6972 and §6973 preclude extending to private plaintiffs any reimbursement remedy available to the Administrator, pointing to the citizen’s 90-day notice requirement, which does not restrict the Administrator’s suits; that the absence of a limitations period for RCRA citizen suits, which would allow suits many years after cleanup, shows that reimbursement actions are unavailable; and that courts had treated RCRA citizen relief as preventive rather than compensatory (Walls; Lamphier).
* **Respondent or Competing Position:** Restitution can be equitable, and excluding past costs can discourage an owner from promptly removing contamination. KFC characterizes its claim as one for “equitable restitution,” not damages. Its position, adopted below, is that citizen suits carry §7003’s standards of liability, under which courts have allowed the government to recover cleanup costs; that the notice requirement remains consistent with recovering costs from past contributors; that laches can address stale claims; and that limiting relief to injunctions would leave the remedy to outsiders while denying it to innocent owners who must clean up promptly under government order, particularly where CERCLA’s and California’s petroleum exclusions leave no statutory cost-recovery claim and tort remedies face difficulties of proof and delay.
* **United States as Amicus (supporting petitioners):** The United States supports reversal only on the endangerment issue. On the remedy issue, it argues that §6972(a) confers the courts’ traditional equitable powers, including restitution under Porter and Moore, because RCRA contains no clear legislative command restricting them, so that a citizen plaintiff that has alleged a current endangerment, satisfied §6972’s other conditions, and spent sums to abate that endangerment may, in appropriate circumstances, obtain restitution, including a plaintiff forced to take emergency action before a longer-term cleanup is complete. It adds that restitution is not the equivalent of damages; that it generally requires a prior request to the party bearing the duty, a principle it finds consistent with the notice requirement, so that a contributor who receives notice and fails to abate may be liable in restitution; and that it may be unavailable to a plaintiff that is itself under a legal duty to perform the cleanup. It urges the Court not to decide the remedy question because KFC alleged no endangerment when it sued, contends that KFC’s remedies lie in state law, which §6972(f) preserves, and notes that EPA’s §7003 authority is broader than the citizen remedy and that the reach of §7003 is not before the Court.

* **Alternative or Unreached Grounds and Limits:** The United States urges reversal on the endangerment issue alone, without deciding the remedy question. Compliance with the notice requirement was not decided on appeal. Laches or other equitable defenses, and the measure of any restitution, were not resolved below. The United States notes that the reach of the Administrator’s own authority under §7003 is not before the Court. KFC’s state-law claims are not before the Court.

#### D. LAWFUL PATHS AND UNCERTAINTY

* **Lawful Paths and Consequences:** Both sides accept that, where an endangerment exists, §6972(a) authorizes relief restraining a contributor or ordering it to take necessary action; prospective relief is not sought on this record. (1) If §6972(a)(1)(B) requires that the endangerment exist when suit is filed, this RCRA claim fails on the amended complaint regardless of the remedy question; the Court may then decide or leave open whether restitution is available in a citizen suit properly brought during an endangerment, and the United States urges leaving it open. (2) If §6972(a)’s remedial authority does not include restitution of past cleanup costs, the claim fails regardless of the timing question. (3) If KFC prevails on both questions, the judgment is affirmed and the restitution claim returns to the district court, where notice, equitable defenses and the measure of any restitution remain open. No path resolves KFC’s state-law remedies, which §6972(f) preserves.

**Current rule:** Sections 107(a)(4)(B) and 101(25) do not shift private litigation attorney’s fees; a reference to enforcement activities is not the required authorization for that private award. Work performed by a lawyer can nevertheless qualify when the actual task independently constitutes a necessary response activity, such as identifying other responsible parties in a way that advances cleanup, and the private claimant proves necessity and consistency with the National Contingency Plan. **Department of Energy v. Ohio**

**Current rule:** The four statutory routes retain distinct limits:

- The Clean Water Act §505, 33 U.S.C. §1365, and RCRA §7002, 42 U.S.C. §6972, citizen-suit authorizations permit proceedings against the United States but do not unequivocally authorize punitive civil penalties incorporated from their separate enforcement provisions. Incorporation includes the penalty provisions' limits, whose generally defined liable persons exclude the United States.
- Clean Water Act §313, 33 U.S.C. §1323, does not unequivocally waive punitive fines for completed violations through its submission to requirements, process and sanctions. In that context, sanctions enforcing process support coercive compliance relief without clearly adding retrospective punishment.
- Section 313's qualified reference to civil penalties does not independently provide the missing punitive waiver. State-law penalties do not arise under federal law merely because EPA approved the state program, and the incorporated federal penalty definition still excludes the United States from liable persons.
- RCRA §6001, 42 U.S.C. §6961, submits facilities to substantive and procedural requirements, including permits, reporting, injunctive relief and sanctions enforcing that relief, without unequivocally authorizing punitive fines for past violations.

Enacted compliance duties and conceded prospective or coercive compliance remedies remain effective and distinct; no general immunity from every environmental remedy follows.

**Lujan**

**Current rule:** A plaintiff invoking federal jurisdiction must establish a concrete and particularized, actual or imminent injury; a causal connection fairly traceable to the defendant's challenged conduct rather than to independent third parties' own decisions; and a likelihood, not mere speculation, that the requested relief will redress the injury. Authorization for any person to sue does not supply standing for only the public's undifferentiated interest in lawful administration. A procedural right must protect the plaintiff's own threatened concrete interest; when that connection exists, the plaintiff need not prove that the required procedure will certainly alter the ultimate agency decision. Aesthetic, recreational or professional wildlife interests may qualify, but past visits and indefinite return intentions without concrete plans or another supported basis for imminent renewed exposure do not establish threatened interference with personal use. At summary judgment the plaintiff must support challenged standing elements with specific facts by affidavit or other evidence, with favorable reasonable inferences available; allegations cannot replace missing evidence.

**Authority by component:**
- Stage-specific evidentiary requirement and existing injury, traceability and redressability elements: [Lujan v. Defenders of Wildlife, “1. Article III elements must be supported at the litigation stage”](https://github.com/Alex-Oss222/scotus-career/blob/87e62b7612c15409039d524fd66f743d0ad75e2a/terms/OT1991/records/Lujan_v_Defenders_of_Wildlife_merits_1992-06-12.md), June 12, 1992; Scalia Part II, joined by White, Kennedy, Souter and Thomas (five).
- Prospective personal-use boundary: Lujan, “2. Indefinite return intentions and general species interests do not establish threatened personal injury here,” June 12, 1992; Scalia Part III-A, same five-Justice opinion; Stone separately supports the record-specific conclusion.
- Citizen-suit and procedural-right boundary: Lujan, “3. Citizen-suit authorization does not dispense with a threatened concrete interest,” June 12, 1992; Scalia Part IV, same five Justices.

## OT1995-044 — Wisconsin v. City of New York; Oklahoma v. City of New York; Department of Commerce v. City of New York

**Event:** 1996-03-20 | **Category:** MERITS | **Action:** Argued merits decision

**Citation:** 517 U.S. 1

**Docket(s):** Nos. 94-1614, 94-1631, 94-1985

### SECTION I — NEUTRAL MODELING PACKET

- **Case Name:** Wisconsin v. City of New York; Oklahoma v. City of New York; Department of Commerce v. City of New York
- **Citation / Docket:** 517 U.S. 1; Nos. 94-1614, 94-1631, 94-1985
- **October Term:** 1995
- **Entry Stage / Event Type:** Granted merits review; decision pending in this simulation.
- **Question Presented:** Whether the Secretary of Commerce’s July 15, 1991 decision not to make a statistical adjustment to the 1990 census violated the Constitution, including whether the Court of Appeals erred in imposing on the Secretary the heightened burden of showing that the decision not to adjust furthers a legitimate governmental objective and is essential to achieving it. The petitions frame the question as follows: the federal petitioners (No. 94-1985), whether the decision “violated the Constitution”; Wisconsin (No. 94-1614), whether the decision not to substitute statistically adjusted numbers for the totals previously reported by the President for apportionment and transmitted to the States for redistricting was consistent with the language of the Constitution and the constitutional goal of equal representation; and Oklahoma (No. 94-1631), (1) whether the Court of Appeals erred in imposing that heightened burden, (2) whether its opinion is inconsistent with *Department of Commerce v. Montana* and *Franklin v. Massachusetts* and conflicts with *Tucker* and *City of Detroit*, and (3) whether the decision is consistent with Article I, §2, cl. 3’s direction that “[t]he actual Enumeration shall be made . . . in such Manner as [Congress] shall by Law direct.”
- **Question Granted:** Certiorari was granted on all three petitions on September 27, 1995, and the cases were consolidated. The questions are those stated in the petitions, summarized above; the sources show no limitation of the grant, and no additional question is inferred. Whether Wisconsin and Oklahoma’s 13 U.S.C. §195 argument is fairly included is disputed (see Preservation / Threshold / Vehicle Issues).
- **Legal Area:** Constitutional structure, equal protection and administrative law — census methodology
- **Court and Judgment Under Review:** After a 13-day bench trial, the District Court, 822 F. Supp. 906 (E.D.N.Y. 1993), adhered to its ruling that the Secretary’s decision is reviewable, reviewed it under the APA’s arbitrary-and-capricious standard, found it not arbitrary or capricious, and dismissed the complaint. A divided Second Circuit panel, 34 F.3d 1114 (1994), agreed that the decision is reviewable and rejected Wisconsin and Oklahoma’s contention that 13 U.S.C. §195 bars statistical adjustment, but, while rejecting the plaintiffs’ request for de novo review, held the arbitrary-and-capricious standard inapplicable. Treating equal voting weight as a fundamental right and the differential undercount as disproportionately affecting minority groups, it held that the federal government, like the States, must make a good-faith effort to achieve equal representation as nearly as practicable, and it applied the burden-shifting framework of the congressional-districting cases (*Kirkpatrick v. Preisler*; *Karcher v. Daggett*) without requiring proof of discriminatory intent, reasoning that the apportionment cases impose no such requirement. Reading the District Court’s findings to show that the adjustment was feasible, would be more accurate for most purposes and would lessen that undercount, and relying on the Secretary’s acknowledgments, the priority he gave distributive over numerical accuracy, his concerns about future manipulation and state cooperation, his presumption against adjustment, and the foreseeability of the differential undercount, it concluded that the plaintiffs had shown that the Secretary did not make the required good-faith effort to achieve numerical accuracy as nearly as practicable. The burden therefore shifted to the Secretary to show that the refusal to adjust furthers a legitimate governmental objective and is essential to achieving it, under what the court called “the more traditional standard applicable to an equal protection claim that a fundamental right has been denied on the basis of race or ethnicity.” The court vacated the judgment and remanded for that determination. Judge Timbers dissented and would have affirmed on the District Court’s opinion.
- **Requested Supreme Court Disposition / Relief:** The federal petitioners ask that the Court of Appeals’ judgment be reversed and the case remanded with directions to enter judgment for the petitioners; Wisconsin asks that the Court of Appeals’ judgment be reversed and the District Court’s judgment affirmed; Oklahoma asks that the Court of Appeals’ decision be reversed and the Secretary’s decision upheld. The respondents ask that the Court of Appeals’ judgment be affirmed. Any decision must stay within the presented issues and source-authorized remedy.
- **Material Facts and Record:** The Census Bureau has measured a “differential undercount” in every census since at least 1940, with racial and ethnic minorities missed at higher rates than others; the federal petitioners note that other groups, such as men, young people and renters, are also missed at higher rates. The Department of Commerce announced in 1987 that the 1990 census would not be adjusted, and this suit followed in 1988. Under a court-approved July 1989 stipulation, that decision was vacated; the Secretary agreed to reconsider adjustment de novo, under published guidelines, and to decide by July 15, 1991; the Bureau conducted a post-enumeration survey (PES) of roughly 170,000 households designed to permit adjustment; and an eight-member Special Advisory Panel was appointed, four of its members selected from a list submitted by the plaintiffs. Guideline One provided that the census would be considered the most accurate count “unless an adjusted count is shown to be more accurate.” The enumeration produced an apportionment count of 249,632,692, on which the President’s January 1991 apportionment statement was based. As first calculated in June 1991, the PES indicated a 2.1% national net undercount (about 5.3 million people), with estimated undercounts of 4.8% for Blacks, 5.2% for Hispanics, 3.1% for Asian-Pacific Islanders and 5.0% for American Indians, against 1.7% for non-Blacks and 1.2% for non-Hispanic whites; the national estimate was later revised to 1.6% after correction of errors, chiefly a computer error. Used for apportionment, the June 1991 adjusted figures would have moved one seat each from Wisconsin and Pennsylvania to California and Arizona; the federal petitioners state that the corrected adjusted figures would move one seat, from Wisconsin to California, and the respondents state that every adjustment approach the federal petitioners cite moves that seat. The Secretary’s advisers divided: the Advisory Panel split four to four along the lines of its selection; the Bureau’s Undercount Steering Committee (seven to two) and the Census Director recommended adjustment; and the Under Secretary for Economic Affairs and the Administrator of the Economics and Statistics Administration recommended against it. In his July 15, 1991 decision, the Secretary acknowledged the differential undercount and that adjustment would likely make the national count more accurate, but treated distributive accuracy (“getting most nearly correct the proportions of people in different areas”) as controlling and concluded, applying Guideline One, that the adjusted figures had not been shown to be distributively more accurate and that “the evidence provided by the Census Bureau tends to support the superior distributive accuracy of the actual enumeration.” He relied in part on the Steering Committee’s report that the variances used in the Bureau’s loss-function analysis were understated. The respondents emphasize the Secretary’s acknowledgment that the Bureau’s analysis showed adjustment improving distributive accuracy for areas containing up to two-thirds of the population; the federal petitioners say that figure came from the uncorrected analysis, and the respondents answer that the increased-variance tests did not change the Bureau’s conclusion. The Secretary also cited incomplete evaluation studies, the sensitivity of seat allocations to reasonable methodological choices, the risk of actual or perceived manipulation of future adjustments (while expressing confidence that the 1990 PES had not been manipulated), possible effects on future census participation and state and local support, the two-hundred-year practice of unadjusted counts, and disruption of the completed apportionment and redistricting. Wisconsin and Oklahoma then intervened as defendants. The plaintiffs—cities, States, organizations and individuals—pressed claims under Article I, §2, Fifth Amendment equal protection, the census statutes, the APA and the stipulation. After trial, the District Court found that “for most purposes” the PES produced a more accurate count, described the plaintiffs’ challenges to the Secretary’s accuracy analysis as “a compelling attack on the Decision,” and said that on de novo review it “would probably have ordered the adjustment”; it also stated that the plaintiffs had failed “to illustrate affirmatively the superior accuracy of the adjusted counts either (1) at any level mentioned in Guideline One, or (2) for any reasonable definition of accuracy,” found the focus on distributive accuracy consonant with the constitutional goal of the most accurate census practicable and each guideline reasonably applied, upheld the decision, and ordered release of block-level adjusted data to the plaintiff States. The parties dispute whether those findings show an absence of good-faith effort. All plaintiffs except California and Hudson County, New Jersey, appealed, seeking de novo review.
- **Preservation / Threshold / Vehicle Issues:** The case concerns the constitutionality of the Secretary’s no-adjustment decision on this record and the standard for reviewing it. The judgment under review is interlocutory: neither lower court held the decision unconstitutional, ordered an adjustment or considered a remedy, and the Court of Appeals remanded for application of its standard; the respondents opposed certiorari on that ground. The federal petitioners have stated that they do not suggest an actual reapportionment of Representatives would be an appropriate remedy halfway through the decade even if constitutional error were found on remand; Wisconsin contends that the decision below threatens a mid-decade reapportionment and disruption of completed state redistricting. Neither lower court found a racially discriminatory purpose, and the federal petitioners state that none was alleged; the federal petitioners and Oklahoma contend that such a purpose is required for heightened equal-protection scrutiny, and the respondents contend that it is not required in apportionment cases. The federal petitioners note that on appeal the plaintiffs sought de novo review and did not challenge the District Court’s holding that the Secretary was not arbitrary or capricious in concluding that the adjusted figures’ superior accuracy had not been shown. Wisconsin and Oklahoma also contend in their merits briefs that 13 U.S.C. §195 prohibits statistical adjustment of the apportionment count, a contention the Second Circuit rejected; the District Court had earlier declared that statistical adjustment would not of itself violate the Constitution or federal law, and the Secretary stated that legal considerations did not provide a basis for his decision, as Oklahoma acknowledges. The respondents contend that the §195 argument was not presented in either State’s petition and is not fairly included in any question on which certiorari was granted (a point they say the federal petitioners also made in opposing the States’ motion to extend time for oral argument and for divided argument), and alternatively defend the Second Circuit’s ruling; the federal petitioners agree with the Second Circuit that the Constitution and the Census Act “do not bar the use of statistical sampling in conducting the decennial census.” In footnotes, the federal petitioners also question whether plaintiffs outside Arizona and California may contest the interstate allocation of Representatives or invoke heightened review on that basis, and whether municipalities or individual voters may do so after California declined to appeal, while acknowledging that many plaintiffs may have funding-based injuries; the respondents answer that Arizona is a respondent, that California voters have standing, that funding injuries support review, and that a single set of census figures serves all purposes. The District Court had earlier held that the plaintiffs had standing to bring their constitutional challenge.

#### Enacted and surviving authorities

12 U.S.C. §92; Supremacy Clause. As quoted by the district court, §92 provides that “any [national banking] association located and doing business in any place the population of which does not exceed five thousand inhabitants, as shown by the last preceding decennial census, may, under such rules and regulations as may be prescribed by the Comptroller of the Currency, act as agent for any ... insurance company authorized by the authorities of the State in which said bank is located to do business in said State ...” (839 F. Supp. at 838). The district court noted a 1963 Comptroller ruling, codified at 12 C.F.R. §7.7100 (1993), that a branch of a national bank located in a community of 5,000 or less may sell insurance even if the bank’s principal office is in a larger community (id.). #### C. DECISION-MATERIAL LITIGATION POSITIONS

##### Issue 1: Federal permission and the state prohibition

* **Controlling Reasoning Below / Institutional Posture:** The district court found that, on its face, §626.988 purports to prevent the insurance agency activities of the bank’s Belleview branch. It read §92 to place no geographic limit on where a national bank selling insurance from a place of fewer than 5,000 inhabitants may sell it, declined to read into §92 a further requirement, which it understood the Insurance Commissioner to seek, that such a bank’s activities be confined to that place, and decided the case under McCarran-Ferguson, which it held saved the apparently conflicting Florida statute from preemption (839 F. Supp. 835, 838–839, 843 (M.D. Fla. 1993)). The Eleventh Circuit affirmed on the McCarran-Ferguson ground alone and did not reach the cross-appeals contesting §92’s geographic scope and the bank’s small-place status (43 F.3d at 632, 637).
* **Petitioner or Applicant’s Position:** Section 92 authorizes a qualifying national bank to act as agent for insurers authorized in the State, and §626.988, which bars the Belleview branch’s agency, is in direct conflict with §92 and is preempted. Below, the bank sought a declaration that it “is authorized and empowered by federal law to act as an agent for any insurance company authorized by the State of Florida to do business in Florida” (43 F.3d at 633; 839 F. Supp. at 836–837, 839). It also maintains that §92 places no geographic limit on where insurance sold from a qualifying place may be marketed (839 F. Supp. at 837–838; 43 F.3d at 632–633).
* **Respondent or Competing Position:** Below, the defendants contended that McCarran-Ferguson saves §626.988 from preemption (839 F. Supp. at 839); the cited opinions do not record a separate contention that the two statutes are compatible. The Commissioner and Department cross-appealed the holding that §92 permits national banks to sell insurance nationwide, the district court having understood the Commissioner to ask that a qualifying bank’s activities be confined to the small place, and the agent intervenors cross-appealed the finding that the bank is “located and doing business in a place with a population of less than 5000” (839 F. Supp. at 838; 43 F.3d at 632). A competing reading of §92 is that it permits a qualifying bank to enter insurance only within the otherwise applicable state regulatory regime, so that no actual conflict arises; the cited opinions do not show whether respondents press that reading in this Court.

##### Issue 2: McCarran-Ferguson

* **Controlling Reasoning Below / Institutional Posture:** Both lower courts held that the Florida statute was enacted for the purpose of regulating the business of insurance, because it protects present and potential policyholders against coercion, threats to insurer solvency, and the loss of arm’s-length dealing when banks sell insurance (relying on Florida appellate decisions construing the statute, the trial testimony of the Department’s Director of Legal Services, and Part X’s purpose clause), and that §92, a 1916 banking provision enacted when insurance was thought to lie outside Congress’s commerce power, neither “specifically relates to the business of insurance” nor specifically requires preemption. McCarran-Ferguson therefore left the Florida statute in force (43 F.3d at 634–637; 839 F. Supp. at 839–843). The district court relied on Owensboro National Bank v. Moore, 803 F. Supp. 24 (E.D. Ky. 1992), for the view that §92 does not regulate the business of insurance, and distinguished that court’s further holding that a Kentucky restriction was not insurance regulation and was preempted by §92: Owensboro had no state caselaw on the restriction’s purpose, the Kentucky provision sat in the banking chapter, and Owensboro applied the Pireno test, which the district court, as the defendants noted, read Fabe to confine to McCarran-Ferguson’s second clause (839 F. Supp. at 843 & n.5).
* **Petitioner or Applicant’s Position:** Section 92 specifically authorizes insurance agency activity and therefore falls within the statutory exception to the preservation rule; as the district court acknowledged in addressing the bank’s argument, §92 “does contain plain language entitling national banks to act as insurance agents under limited circumstances” (839 F. Supp. at 842–843; 43 F.3d at 632). Independently, §626.988 is not a law enacted for the purpose of regulating the business of insurance: the bank argued below that it exists only to protect “independent insurance agents from competition by financial institutions” (43 F.3d at 636), that the Florida decisions relied on did not address McCarran-Ferguson, and that Michael M.’s deference to state-court readings, arising like Glendale in an equal-protection setting, is inapposite (839 F. Supp. at 841).
* **Respondent or Competing Position:** Section 626.988 is a law enacted for the purpose of regulating the business of insurance, and §92 is not, so McCarran-Ferguson saves §626.988 from preemption (839 F. Supp. at 839). The Florida statute sits in the Insurance Code’s unfair-insurance-trade-practices part, whose purpose clause invokes McCarran-Ferguson; below, the defendants urged deference under Michael M. to the Florida appellate decisions construing it and offered the Department’s trial testimony that it protects policyholders against coercion and threats to insurer solvency (839 F. Supp. at 841–842). Section 92 principally concerns banking and should not displace a law regulating insurance without a clearer congressional command (839 F. Supp. at 842–843; 43 F.3d at 636–637).

* **Alternative or Unreached Grounds and Limits:** The court of appeals did not reach the Commissioner and Department’s cross-appeal from the holding that §92 permits national banks to sell insurance nationwide, or the intervenors’ cross-appeal from the finding that the bank is located and doing business in a place of fewer than 5,000 (43 F.3d at 632, 637). The district court made no finding on the Commissioner’s allegation that only 35% of the bank’s business is in Belleview, holding that the figure would not deprive the bank of standing to assert that its activities are permissible under §92 (839 F. Supp. at 838 & n.2). The defendants’ counterclaims, which sought the opposite declaration that §92 prohibits the bank from, among other things, soliciting insurance in such localities, were held moot (839 F. Supp. at 837, 843–844 & n.6).

**Hafer**

### SECTION I — NEUTRAL MODELING PACKET

- **Case Name:** Morse v. Republican Party of Virginia
- **Citation / Docket:** 517 U.S. 186; No. 94-203
- **October Term:** 1995
- **Entry Stage / Event Type:** Granted merits review; decision pending in this simulation.
- **Question Presented:** Whether §5 of the Voting Rights Act required preclearance of the Republican Party of Virginia’s decision to hold a convention to nominate its 1994 candidate for United States Senator and to impose a registration fee on voters seeking to participate as delegates in that convention, and whether individual voters may sue under §10 of the Act to challenge that fee as a prohibited poll tax. Appellees contend that the convention-choice component was not raised below (see Preservation).
- **Question Granted:** The stated question describes the submitted review scope; no additional question or plenary grant is inferred beyond the identified procedural stage.
- **Legal Area:** Voting rights — preclearance, party nomination, and private enforcement
- **Court and Judgment Under Review:** A three-judge district court (W.D. Va.), *Morse v. Oliver North for U.S. Senate Committee, Inc.*, 853 F. Supp. 212 (May 18, 1994), granted, as to the §5 and §10 counts (Counts 3 and 4), the joint motion to dismiss of the Republican Party of Virginia and the Albemarle County Republican Committee, dismissed both counts with prejudice, and denied the preliminary-injunction and expedited-discovery motions so far as they relied on those counts. On §5, it stated as a general rule that political parties, to the extent they are empowered by the State to conduct primary elections for purposes of selecting national convention delegates, are subject to §5, but held that a registration fee imposed on candidates for delegate to a state party convention who are not chosen in an election and are chosen by local convention, mass meeting or party canvass is not subject to preclearance. It relied on the Attorney General’s regulation, 28 C.F.R. §51.7 (with the definition of “voting” in §51.2), and on *Williams v. Democratic Party of Georgia*, No. 16286 (N.D. Ga. Apr. 6, 1972), summarily aff’d, 409 U.S. 809 (1972). On §10, it held that the provision expressly authorizes actions by the Attorney General and does not on its face authorize private suits, distinguishing *Allen*’s recognition of private §5 suits. It declined to act on the Fourteenth and Twenty-Fourth Amendment counts and the §11 count against the North Committee as outside the three-judge court’s jurisdiction, leaving them to a single district judge.
- **Requested Supreme Court Disposition / Relief:** Appellants ask the Court to reverse the dismissal of the §5 and §10 counts. They argue that, under *United States v. Classic*, *Smith v. Allwright* and *Terry v. Adams*, party nomination processes that shape the general-election ballot are an integral part of the electoral process; that because Virginia places the Party’s nominee on the ballot without a petition and regulates when it may nominate by a method other than a primary, the Party performs a public electoral function under authority granted by a covered jurisdiction within 28 C.F.R. §51.7, whose reference to primaries is only one example; that *Williams* concerned national-convention delegates and predated the regulation’s route for submitting party rules; that §14(c)(1) defines voting to include all action necessary to make a vote effective in any primary, special, or general election with respect to candidates for public or party office, a definition whose legislative history states that an election of delegates to a State party convention would be covered; that, within categories recognized in *Presley*, the fee is a candidacy qualification for the elected position of delegate and changes the composition of the electorate choosing the nominee, and replacing the primary chosen in 1990 with a convention requiring the fee falls within the category of abolishing an elective office; that the convention operated as a de facto primary; that §5 reaches only party activities a State could regulate, leaving platforms and campaigns outside it, and preclearance within the statutory 60-day period is workable; and that voters may enforce §10 privately as they enforce §5 under *Allen*, relying also on provisions of the Act (including §§3 and 12(f)) that they read as assuming private suits and on §10(c)’s three-judge procedure. Appellees ask the Court to affirm or to dismiss the appeal as moot. They argue that §5 reaches voting in elections, not delegate filing fees or other convention rules, a limit they find confirmed by the Act’s definition of voting, *Williams*, the Attorney General’s regulation, *Presley*’s insistence on workable limits and Congress’s express coverage of conventions in other election statutes; that in nominating by convention the Party exercises no public electoral function delegated by Virginia, because ballot access for parties with demonstrated support is not a delegation, the cited statutes recognize rather than grant nominating authority, and no state law requires or affects the fee, so neither §5 nor the regulation reaches it; that preclearing convention rules, which conventions adopt when they convene, would be unworkable and would intrude on the associational freedom of a voluntary political association, raising serious constitutional questions the Act should be construed to avoid; and that §10, a compromise that did not itself abolish poll taxes, authorizes only actions by the Attorney General. Any decision must stay within the presented issues and source-authorized remedy.
- **Material Facts and Record:** Under Virginia law, the nominee of an organization meeting the statutory definition of a political party, as the Republican Party does, is placed on the general-election ballot without the petition required of independent candidates (Va. Code §§24.2-101, -506, -511). Sections 24.2-508 and -509(A) address such a party’s power to provide for its nominations by primary or by another method such as a convention; a §24.2-509(B) rule requiring a primary for certain offices held by a party’s incumbents, absent their consent, did not apply to the 1994 seat; and §24.2-510 sets the period within which a party may nominate by a non-primary method. The parties dispute whether these provisions grant the Party authority to nominate by convention or recognize authority it holds independently. The Party’s executive director averred that its State Central Committee selected the convention process for Senate nominations in 1964, 1966, 1970, 1972, 1976, 1978, 1982, 1984 and 1988, that the Committee itself chose the nominee in 1964 and 1978, and that in 1990 it voted to nominate by primary, which was canceled when the incumbent was unopposed; the 1990 nomination was for the seat held by Senator John Warner, and appellees note that the seat at issue in 1994 has always been filled by convention or by the Central Committee. On December 16, 1993, the Party issued a call for a June 3, 1994 state convention to nominate its candidate for United States Senator. Registered voters willing, if asked, to declare their intent to support the Party’s nominees could take part in local mass meetings, canvasses or conventions, but a voter seeking to become a delegate to the state convention had to pay a registration fee. Under the State Call, the fee was $35 per delegate if the certification and fee were postmarked or delivered within fourteen days after the local mass meeting, convention or canvass, and $45 if later; the Albemarle County call and pre-file form stated a non-refundable $45 fee, of which, the Party answered, $10 was imposed by the County Committee. Taking the facts favorably to the plaintiffs, the district court treated anyone who followed the registration procedure as able to become a delegate; the Party points to plan rules allowing local units to slate applicants out or instruct delegations, which it says competing campaigns have recently forgone as a party-unity measure. The Party admitted the complaint’s allegations that delegates are elected in county or city mass meetings, conventions or party canvasses and cast votes at the convention, and that as a practical matter delegates are not bound to support a particular candidate; it denied that, as a practical matter, any voter who wishes can qualify as a delegate, and its executive director averred that payment of the fee does not by itself make one a delegate. The Party admitted the complaint’s allegation that on November 1, 1964 it imposed or collected no fee for participation in its Senate nomination process, explaining that no nomination process was then in progress, but stated that it charged a registration fee for its June 1964 state convention; its executive director averred that the Party finances its state conventions with delegate filing fees (the 1993 convention cost approximately $373,373), that such fees had been required as far back as Party records go, including 1964, and had gradually increased to $35, and that certified delegates increased from approximately 550 in 1964 to 14,614 in 1994. The district court described the fee requirement as one the Party admits was not in effect on November 1, 1964 and treated it as authorized by the Party’s plan at least since 1987. The Party admits it has not obtained preclearance of its registration fee, maintaining that none is required. As alleged and taken favorably to the plaintiffs, Bartholomew and Enderson were deterred from filing as delegates by the $45 fee, and Morse, told the fee was mandatory when he said he lacked the funds, borrowed money and paid it; a North campaign coordinator then gave him $45, which Morse later repaid to the North Committee. The Party answered that its records show Morse was a delegate to the 1993 state convention and was certified as a delegate to the 1994 convention. The May 2, 1994 complaint sought declaratory and injunctive relief and an order requiring the Party or the County Committee to refund Morse’s $45 fee. The convention has since been held.
- **Preservation / Threshold / Vehicle Issues:** The parties dispute whether, in nominating by convention, the Party acts under authority granted by Virginia and performs a public electoral function within 28 C.F.R. §51.7, and whether the delegate fee falls within any such function or is internal party activity outside it. Appellees contend the appeal is moot because the convention has been held and concluded, the issues could have been litigated in time, and nothing shows the plaintiffs will again face the fee; appellants respond that the Party retains the $45 fee Morse paid, whose refund the complaint seeks, that the Party’s continuing practice of charging and raising such fees makes the controversy capable of repetition yet evading review, and that if the case were moot the judgment should be vacated under *United States v. Munsingwear, Inc.*, 340 U.S. 36 (1950), rather than affirmed. Appellees also contend that a delegate filing fee is not a poll tax and that any challenge to the Party’s choice of a convention rather than a primary, including its canceled 1990 primary, was not raised below. Appellants reply that whether the fee is a poll tax turns on disputed facts best left to the district court in the first instance, particularly because reversal on §5 would bar the Party from imposing any fee greater than the fee charged in 1964 unless and until precleared; and that their challenge to the fee embraces the Party’s 1994 change from the primary method it had chosen in 1990, an issue they say they raised at the district-court hearing and that the complaint’s challenge to the fee for full participation in the nomination process fairly presents. Appellants do not challenge the three-judge court’s refusal to hear the constitutional and §11 counts; they voluntarily dismissed Count 5, their only claim against the North Committee, when they noticed this appeal, and the constitutional counts were continued before a single judge pending this appeal.
- **Relevant Dates:** District-court judgment May 18, 1994; notice of appeal June 8, 1994; jurisdictional statement filed August 1, 1994, invoking direct appeal under 28 U.S.C. §1253; probable jurisdiction noted January 23, 1995; argument October 2, 1995; decision March 27, 1996. Apart from mootness, appellees raise no objection to this Court’s jurisdiction.

**Presley**

**Current rule:** Section 5 requires a direct relation to voting; a redistribution of governing authority does not qualify merely because it diminishes an elected official's influence over a function. **Event:** 1996-03-27 | **Category:** MERITS | **Action:** Argued merits decision

### SECTION I — NEUTRAL MODELING PACKET

- **Case Name:** Rutledge v. United States
- **Citation / Docket:** 517 U.S. 292; No. 94-8769
- **October Term:** 1995
- **Entry Stage / Event Type:** Granted merits review; decision pending in this simulation.
- **Question Presented:** Whether the Double Jeopardy Clause of the Fifth Amendment bars the entry of judgments of conviction and the imposition of concurrent sentences on two separate counts in an indictment where the conduct that forms the basis of the count alleging a conspiracy to distribute a controlled substance (21 U.S.C. §846) is identical to the conduct that establishes the “in concert” element in the count alleging a continuing criminal enterprise (21 U.S.C. §848). The United States restates the question as whether the §846 conviction and concurrent sentence must be vacated in light of the CCE conviction and sentence where the CCE “in concert with” element was based on the same agreement as the §846 conspiracy; Rutledge responds that vacating either judgment and its sentence would remedy the asserted violation.
- **Question Granted:** Certiorari was granted on Rutledge’s petition on June 26, 1995. The stated question describes the submitted review scope; no additional question or plenary grant is inferred beyond the identified procedural stage.
- **Legal Area:** Criminal procedure — double jeopardy; multiple convictions and concurrent sentences for CCE and §846 conspiracy in a single prosecution
- **Court and Judgment Under Review:** The Seventh Circuit affirmed, permitting separate convictions and concurrent life sentences for conspiracy and continuing criminal enterprise. It accepted that the conspiracy charge was a lesser included offense of the CCE charge but, citing its decision in *United States v. Bond*, 847 F.2d 1233, and *Jeffers v. United States*, 432 U.S. 137, held that double jeopardy is not implicated so long as the district court does not impose cumulative sentences, quoting *Bond*’s reasoning that one can both agree to run a drug business (§846) and strike the agreement and succeed on a grand scale (§848). Relying on *Bond* and *United States v. Bafia*, 949 F.2d 1465, it held that concurrent sentences may be imposed for conspiracy and CCE provided the cumulative punishment does not exceed the CCE maximum; because the concurrent sentences did not exceed the CCE maximum of life imprisonment, the sentence was proper (40 F.3d 879, 886). A petition for rehearing and suggestion for rehearing en banc were denied on January 3, 1995. Rutledge’s other trial and sentencing claims, rejected below, are not pursued here, and no question is presented on his other counts.
- **Requested Supreme Court Disposition / Relief:** Rutledge asks the Court to reverse and remand for the limited purpose of vacating one of the two judgments and its corresponding sentence, under either Count 1 (CCE) or Count 2 (conspiracy); he contends that the choice of which to vacate belongs in the first instance to the sentencing court. The United States asks the Court to affirm; alternatively, it asks that the CCE and §846 verdicts merge into a single judgment of conviction with a single sentence no greater than the sentence for the more serious offense. It prefers that approach to the Ninth Circuit’s practice of staying entry of judgment and sentence on all but one count, which it calls a partial accommodation of the interests at stake, and it opposes outright vacatur if the vacated conspiracy conviction could not later be entered should the CCE conviction be overturned. Rutledge replies that a combined judgment would still record convictions on both offenses and would equally violate the Clause, and that whether judgment and sentence on one verdict could be stayed for later entry is not presented in this case. Any decision must stay within the presented issues and source-authorized remedy.
- **Material Facts and Record:** After a jury trial in the United States District Court for the Central District of Illinois, Rutledge was convicted on all six counts of a second superseding indictment: CCE (Count 1, §848), drug conspiracy (Count 2, §846), a §841(a)(1) cocaine offense (Count 3), possession of a firearm by a felon (Count 4, 18 U.S.C. §922(g)) and two counts of using or carrying a firearm in relation to a drug-trafficking offense (Counts 5 and 6, 18 U.S.C. §924(c)). To convict on the CCE count, the jury had to find that he committed a felony drug violation as part of a continuing series of violations undertaken in concert with five or more other persons with respect to whom he occupied a position of organizer, supervisor or manager, and from which he obtained substantial income or resources. The parties agree that the Count 2 conspiracy rested on the same agreement, and the same conduct, that established the CCE count’s “in concert” element; according to the United States, it involved the same persons, time periods and overt acts. The District Court imposed concurrent terms of life imprisonment without possible release on the CCE, conspiracy and cocaine-distribution counts and other terms on the firearm counts; it imposed no fine but, under 18 U.S.C. §3013, imposed a separate $50 special assessment on each count. The parties dispute whether the second conviction carries practical collateral consequences for a defendant serving concurrent life terms.
- **Preservation / Threshold / Vehicle Issues:** Rutledge raised the double-jeopardy objection to both the dual convictions and the concurrent sentences in the Seventh Circuit, which rejected it on the merits. The United States notes that he did not challenge the two special assessments in the court of appeals or in this Court and contends that the question presented presupposes fully concurrent sentences, so the assessment question need not be decided; if it were reached, the United States contends that CCE and a §846 conspiracy are not, strictly speaking, the “same offense,” so separate assessments would be appropriate. Rutledge replies that his opening brief raised the assessments, that an additional judgment automatically carries an additional §3013 assessment and that the assessment’s validity is fairly included in the question presented. The United States does not contend that the concurrent-sentence doctrine bars review; it states that, because each count of conviction carries a §3013 assessment, that doctrine now has virtually no application in the federal system.
- **Relevant Dates:** Certiorari granted June 26, 1995; argument November 27, 1995; decision March 27, 1996.
- **Companion or Consolidated Matters:** None.
- **Known Participation Issue:** Stone, Stevens, O’Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg, and Breyer form the event-date roster; no case-specific nonparticipation is established.

#### Additional public case information

- **Conflict / Percolation:** The United States’ brief in opposition acknowledged that the courts of appeals are divided on the proper treatment of dual convictions on CCE and drug-conspiracy charges, while contending that, as in *Wingo v. United States*, No. 94-7980, it did not appear that Rutledge would suffer any prejudice from the conspiracy conviction and sentence. Rutledge states that the Seventh Circuit alone permits judgments and concurrent sentences on both counts, nine circuits prohibit entry of judgment and sentence on both, and two permit judgment but prohibit even concurrent sentences. The United States describes the circuits’ approaches in more detail: the Second Circuit combines the two verdicts into a single conviction with a single sentence; the Third Circuit permits two convictions with a single general sentence not exceeding the punishment for the more serious offense, an approach it calls somewhat similar to the Seventh Circuit’s; the Ninth Circuit’s general practice for offenses that may be tried together but support only one conviction stays entry of judgment and sentence on all but one count; the Eighth Circuit reviews both convictions on appeal and then remands for vacatur of one; and several circuits require vacatur of one conviction without saying whether it could later be entered if the other were overturned.
- **Parties’ Principal Arguments:** Both sides accept that the CCE and §846 counts could be charged, tried together and submitted to the jury; the dispute concerns entry of judgment and sentence on both verdicts. Rutledge contends that *Jeffers*, as reaffirmed in *Garrett v. United States*, 471 U.S. 773, establishes that Congress did not intend cumulative punishment under §§846 and 848 where the same conduct supports the conspiracy count and the CCE “in concert” element, a reading he says Congress left undisturbed when it later amended both statutes, and that under *Ball* a second judgment of conviction is itself punishment even with a concurrent sentence. He argues that whether a second judgment and concurrent sentence constitute punishment is a constitutional question rather than solely one of legislative intent (citing *Ball* and *United States v. Halper*, 490 U.S. 435), and that *Ball* sets a per se rule that does not depend on case-by-case proof of collateral consequences and spares sentencing courts from predicting future effects, while identifying the added §3013 assessment, stigma, impeachment and possible effects on any future parole or compassionate release. He attributes *Jeffers*’ limited relief to its posture—separate prosecutions at the defendant’s own insistence, with only the CCE conviction before the Court—and to its predating *Ball*; argues that the United States identifies no clear indication of congressional intent to permit dual convictions (*Whalen v. United States*, 445 U.S. 684); maintains that §846 conspiracy is in fact a lesser included offense of CCE because “in concert” signifies agreement; and contends that the CCE–conspiracy overlap here is complete. He calls the Government’s backup-conviction rationale speculative, since the Government may generally retry a defendant after a successful appeal or collateral attack and vacatur of a CCE conviction leaves the substantive drug conviction intact. The United States contends that in a single proceeding the Double Jeopardy Clause prevents only punishment greater than Congress intended (*Missouri v. Hunter*, 459 U.S. 359; *Garrett*; *Albernaz v. United States*, 450 U.S. 333) and that *Jeffers* did not resolve whether drug conspiracy is a lesser included offense of CCE. While adhering to its view that the CCE “in concert” element does not require proof of a conspiracy, it proceeds on the *Jeffers* plurality’s assumption that the §846 conspiracy may be treated as a lesser included offense where, as the parties agree is so here, both rest on the same agreement, and it maintains that congressional intent controls the outcome either way. It accepts that Congress did not intend “pyramiding”—consecutive terms or cumulative fines exceeding the punishment for the more serious offense—but reads *Jeffers* as addressing only that problem, noting that *Jeffers* left both convictions and the concurrent prison terms in place while reducing the CCE fine so that the combined fines did not exceed the CCE maximum. It acknowledges that *Jeffers* arose from separate prosecutions with only the CCE conviction before the Court, but contends that its result reflects congressional intent and that Congress’s later amendments imply no acquiescence in a rule no decision of the Court had adopted. It contends that the statutes’ distinct definitions and penalties, the CCE offense’s hybrid character and the need to ensure that a defendant whose CCE conviction is later overturned can still be punished for the §846 conspiracy show that Congress intended dual convictions with concurrent sentences. It distinguishes *Ball* on the grounds that firearm receipt and possession overlap almost completely and were enacted as alternatives, whereas many §846 conspiracies involve no CCE, and that reversal of one of the firearm convictions would rarely leave the other standing; and it argues that a CCE defendant faces little realistic collateral consequence from a concurrent conspiracy conviction, since federal parole has been abolished, the Sentencing Guidelines treat related concurrent sentences as one prior sentence and any incremental stigma is doubtful.

**Schlup**

**Holding:** A prisoner seeking to overcome otherwise applicable successive or abusive habeas barriers to independent constitutional claims through innocence of the offense must present reliable new evidence and show, considering the probative record as a whole, that it is more likely than not that no reasonable, properly instructed juror would find guilt beyond a reasonable doubt. **Gomez**

**Holding:** A court considering an execution stay may weigh the State's interest in enforcing its judgment and an applicant's unjustified delay in presenting a long-available claim independently of any habeas procedural bar. **Schlup**

**Holding:** A prisoner seeking to overcome otherwise applicable successive or abusive habeas barriers to independent constitutional claims through innocence of the offense must present reliable new evidence and show, considering the probative record as a whole, that it is more likely than not that no reasonable, properly instructed juror would find guilt beyond a reasonable doubt. This procedural gateway does not itself establish innocence, prove any constitutional violation or shift the ultimate burden on the underlying claims; Sawyer's clear-and-convincing rule remains confined to its distinct death-eligibility setting.

**Proposition-level authority:** Stevens's gateway and all-evidence portion, joined by Stone-Zsela, O'Connor, Souter, Ginsburg and Breyer: six Justices.

**Limits and questions not reached:** The court considers all relevant probative evidence, including evidence excluded at trial or becoming available afterward; trial-admissibility rules do not categorically delimit the gateway record, and even improperly admitted trial evidence may have probative force subject to reliability. The court must assess reliability and likely credibility, not accept every affidavit as true or treat the original verdict as a conclusive answer. Timing, delay and inconsistency may matter; no categorical rule excludes impeachment evidence or discredits a witness solely because the witness is a prisoner. A mere possibility of acquittal, a juror who might disagree, ordinary constitutional-merits prejudice or the reviewing judge's personal reasonable doubt is insufficient. The reasonable juror fairly considers the evidence and follows the beyond-a-reasonable-doubt instruction.

**Holding:** The courts' use of the rejected clear-and-convincing crime gateway and reliance on the original jury's credibility function require vacatur and renewed assessment on this record; §2244(b)'s permission to refuse a qualifying subsequent petition does not itself supply an adequate independent ground to sustain that legally mistaken refusal. The Court remands through the Eighth Circuit to the District Court for application of the probability rule and a reasoned determination of necessary factual development, without deciding whether every successful innocence showing compels review, choosing a general appellate standard, or ordering a hearing, writ, retrial, release or stay automatically.

**Proposition-level authority:** Stevens's statutory-refusal and remand portion, joined by O'Connor, Souter, Ginsburg and Breyer: five Justices. Stone-Zsela supplies a sixth judgment vote without joining the separate §2244(b) rationale.

**Limits and questions not reached:** Section 2244(b) provides:

> (b) When after an evidentiary hearing on the merits of a material factual issue, or after a hearing on the merits of an issue of law, a person in custody pursuant to the judgment of a State court has been denied by a court of the United States or a justice or judge of the United States release from custody or other remedy on an application for a writ of habeas corpus, a subsequent application for a writ of habeas corpus in behalf of such person need not be entertained by a court of the United States or a justice or judge of the United States unless the application alleges and is predicated on a factual or other ground not adjudicated on the hearing of the earlier application for the writ, and unless the court, justice, or judge is satisfied that the applicant has not on the earlier application deliberately withheld the newly asserted ground or otherwise abused the writ.

The prior-hearing alternatives are a factual evidentiary merits hearing or a legal merits hearing, each followed by a federal denial of release or other habeas remedy to a person held under a state judgment. Both unless conditions must be preserved: the later application must allege and rest on an unadjudicated factual or other ground, and the court must be satisfied that the newly asserted ground was not deliberately withheld or otherwise an abuse. New evidence is not automatically a new ground; absence of deliberate withholding does not eliminate every other kind of abuse. McCleskey requires consideration when both conditions are met and other barriers, including nonexhaustion and procedural default, are absent. The State must plead abuse with clarity and particularity by identifying prior writ history and newly raised claims and alleging abuse before the prisoner's burden arises. Cause and prejudice and the exceptional innocence route remain separate; cause and prejudice is not pressed in this review. Subsection (a)'s separate wording is not inserted into subsection (b), and no new subsection (c) predicate is established.

The videotape was trial evidence. Green's asserted prompt call, Faherty's movement account and other later identification accounts must be assessed with the existing record; the Court finds no witness truthful or false and does not find the gateway satisfied. Proper claim classification, exhaustion, default, abuse, retroactivity and a genuinely sufficient independent merits ground retain their separate operation. Remand is not mandatory constitutional-merits review. Any necessary live testimony is for a reasoned determination under the applicable rules, not an automatic consequence of affidavits or vacatur. The merits of the suppression and ineffective-assistance claims, a freestanding innocence entitlement and any ultimate guilt determination remain undecided.

**Gomez**

**Holding:** A court considering an execution stay may weigh the State's interest in enforcing its judgment and an applicant's unjustified delay in presenting a long-available claim independently of any habeas procedural bar. On this record, the unexplained postponement of the cyanide-gas challenge through four prior federal habeas proceedings leaves the April 20 stays without sufficient equitable support; those stays must be vacated even assuming the claim is cognizable under §1983 and not barred by abuse-of-writ doctrine.

**Proposition-level authority:** The per curiam opinion is joined by White, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter and Thomas. Six of nine participating Justices adopt this independent equitable ground. Stone, Blackmun and Stevens dissent. No other ground is necessary to the judgment, and no fractured-opinion rule is invoked.

**Limits and questions not reached:** The §1983 channel, the final Eighth Amendment method claim, the application of every habeas-default element, Younger abstention and any universal alternative-method requirement remain undecided. The decision creates no permanent injunction or direction barring another federal stay.

**Herrera**

**Independently majority-supported proposition:** Herrera's delayed and materially inconsistent affidavits, considered with the identification, physical and documentary evidence supporting his convictions, do not establish entitlement to the requested federal habeas relief or justify the district court's innocence-based stay, even assuming an exceptionally persuasive post-trial showing could constitutionally bar execution. This record-specific rejection establishes no uniform freestanding-innocence burden and does not decide whether such a constitutional claim exists.

**Proposition-level authority:** O'Connor's plurality opinion, joined by Scalia, Kennedy and Thomas, and White's express agreement with its reasons for finding this submission insufficient in his concurrence in the judgment: five Justices adopt this limited record conclusion. White does not join the plurality's broader discussion or an unspecified extraordinarily high threshold. No whole opinion commands five votes; no Marks synthesis establishes a common general test.

**Limits and questions not reached:** No controlling opinion recognizes or rejects a freestanding constitutional innocence claim or fixes its general burden. The undefined extraordinary threshold and White’s rational-factfinder minimum have no established logical-subset relationship under Marks. The hearing, exhaustion, abuse, Teague and distinct Sawyer capital-eligibility gateway rules retain independent operation.

### SECTION III — HISTORICAL COMPARATOR SUPPLEMENT

**Use restriction:** Introduce only at the comparator stage. This section is neither entering simulated law nor Stone’s position.

* **Historical Outcome and Vote:** Application granted and appellate stay vacated, 5–4.
* **Historical Opinion Structure:** Per curiam. Ginsburg dissented, joined by Stevens, Souter, and Breyer.
* **Historical Holding:** The historical Court held that the Court of Appeals abused its discretion by entering the stay on that record. Applying Delo v. Stokes’s rule, quoting Barefoot v. Estelle, that a stay pending a second or successive federal habeas petition should be granted only when there are substantial grounds upon which relief might be granted, and citing Delo v. Blair for the proposition that entering a stay absent such grounds is “particularly egregious,” it discerned no substantial grounds. It was persuaded by Magistrate Judge Hays’s report, which found each of Williams’s claims abusive, successive, procedurally defaulted or meritless, by the District Court’s order adopting that report and denying Williams’s motion to amend, and by the claims’ surface implausibility. While declining to say that a court of appeals must explain every stay, it reminded lower courts that a stay entered without explanation is disfavored (cf. Netherland v. Tuggle). The dissent would have denied the application, reading the certificate of probable cause and the scheduled argument as signals of reasons for a stay, and said that at the very least the Court might have invited prompt clarification of the Court of Appeals’ order before acting irretrievably.
