## Event

City of Cincinnati v. Kruse, No. 98-454. Merits decision, December 16, 1998. On writ of certiorari to the Sixth Circuit, reviewing 142 F.3d 907 and its affirmance of the judgment invalidating Cincinnati's mandatory candidate-expenditure ceiling. The question is whether the City's asserted corruption, fundraising-time and public-confidence interests, together with its other financing concerns, justify that ceiling. No oral-argument date is established.

## Participation

All nine Justices participate: Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer.

## Public Action

The Court affirms the Sixth Circuit's judgment invalidating Cincinnati's mandatory candidate-expenditure ceiling.

## Judgment & Remedy

**Affirmed, 9–0.**

**Supporting:** Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer.

The judgment invalidating the challenged ceiling and protecting against its enforcement remains in force. The separate contribution and disclosure provisions, voluntary-financing arrangements and any damages question are not adjudicated. No new ceiling or evidentiary hearing is directed.

## Opinion Topology

| Writing | Author | Joined by | Relationship to judgment | Scope joined |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Opinion of the Court | Kennedy | Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg, Breyer | Unanimous affirmance | Complete expenditure rule, accepted-record application, all limits and remedy. |

All nine Justices join the full opinion. No separate writing is filed.

## Holdings

### Candidate expenditures and the asserted municipal interests

**Holding and operative rule.** Cincinnati may not impose this mandatory ceiling on the total campaign expenditures of a candidate or candidate committee. A direct limit on the quantity of candidate advocacy receives the demanding First Amendment review applicable to expenditures; the accepted corruption, campaign-cost, equalization, official-work and public-confidence grounds do not justify this salary-linked, source-independent restraint.

**Authority.** Kennedy's opinion for the unanimous Court supplies the complete rule, application and limits. Every participating Justice joins it in full.

**Controlling explanation.** The Court holds that accepting the City's evidence does not establish the constitutionality of the means it chose. Buckley v. Valeo distinguishes a donor's contribution from the candidate's expenditure and protects the quantity of campaign expression. Spending contributed money remains expenditure by the candidate; spending personal or family funds does not become a contribution restriction merely because the City calls the law a campaign-finance measure. Reducing costs or equalizing resources cannot justify this limit. Prevention of actual or apparent corruption supports properly fitted controls, but the record does not connect the cutoff of all candidate spending to the contribution danger asserted here.

Fundraising's interference with official work is a distinct concern. The ceiling nevertheless reaches challengers and personally financed advocacy as well as incumbent fundraising, and its link to salary does not supply the missing fit. General public confidence cannot cure that defect or revive the rejected cost and equalization grounds. That a meaningful campaign can be conducted below approximately $140,000 does not permit government to forbid additional advocacy. The 1995 spending evidence preceded the new contribution controls and does not establish their inadequacy; the Court imposes no mandatory empirical waiting period.

Colorado Republican distinguishes actual candidate-resource transactions from political expression; Kentucky Right to Life likewise requires attention to the particular receipt or intermediary restraint. Neither converts this total-spending ceiling into a contribution limit. The Court accepts the City's asserted facts for summary judgment and decides their legal sufficiency, without finding those submissions false. It leaves materially different measures addressing official duties, separate contribution and disclosure provisions, voluntary public-financing conditions and damages outside this decision.

**Application.** The invalid ceiling is three times annual council compensation and governs candidate or candidate-committee election spending. Its exclusions for independent expenditures, uncompensated volunteer services and the described party slate or sample-ballot publications identifying endorsed candidates do not cure the restriction on candidate expression. The Sixth Circuit's invalidation is affirmed; no further evidentiary proceeding or replacement ceiling is ordered.

## Precedent Treatment

- **Buckley v. Valeo, 424 U.S. 1:** Applied to a mandatory candidate-expenditure ceiling; its contribution/expenditure distinction and rejection of cost and equalization grounds remain effective.
- **Colorado Republican Federal Campaign Committee v. FEC, June 26, 1996:** Applied for its actual-transaction distinction; its independent-party-expression holding is not enlarged into a ruling on every coordinated expenditure.
- **Kentucky Right to Life v. Stengel, September 14, 1998:** Distinguished because its receipt and intermediary controls regulate different transactions; their governing rules remain intact.
- **Austin v. Michigan Chamber of Commerce, 494 U.S. 652:** Its distinct corporate-treasury rationale does not authorize this all-candidate ceiling; no broader treatment of Austin is adopted.
- **NAACP, Los Angeles Branch v. Jones, November 5, 1998:** Its vacatur leaves the lower financing merits unavailable as a completed holding; it decides neither the validity of this ceiling nor a wealth-primary theory.

## Law After Decision

The existing distinction between contributions and expenditures governs this municipal ceiling. Cincinnati cannot enforce the challenged limit. No general contribution, disclosure or voluntary-financing rule changes, and the decision creates no compulsory empirical-testing requirement or categorical ruling on every measure addressing officeholder time.

## Separate Writings

No separate writing is filed.

## Procedure After Action

Merits review is complete. The affirmed judgment continues to bar enforcement of the challenged ceiling. Ordinary proceedings implementing that judgment remain governed by its terms; no further Supreme Court return or new nonroutine act is ordered.

The event uses the scheduled post-grant merits date; no argument date is supplied. ## Event

McClellan v. Cablevision of Connecticut, Inc.; Second Circuit No. 97-7156. Merits decision, January 7, 1999. On writ of certiorari to the Second Circuit, reviewing 149 F.3d 161 and its reversal of a pleading-stage dismissal. The question is whether section 531(e) authorizes the asserted implied private damages action after a cable operator's exclusion of public-access programming. The complaint also requested injunctions and asserted Connecticut law.

## Participation

All nine Justices participate: Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer.

## Public Action

The Court reverses in part and remands on the implied federal damages question.

## Judgment & Remedy

**Reversed in part and remanded, 9–0.**

**Supporting:** Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer.

The reversal reaches the asserted implied damages action only. The lower courts shall test any properly preserved remaining relief under its actual source and ordinary procedural requirements. No finding about the lawfulness of the exclusion or an affirmative alternative entitlement is made.

## Opinion Topology

| Writing | Author | Joined by | Relationship to judgment | Scope joined |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Opinion of the Court | Souter | Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Thomas, Ginsburg, Breyer | Unanimous partial reversal and remand | The entire damages holding, explanation, reservations and remedy. |

All nine join the full opinion. No separate writing is filed.

## Holdings

### The asserted federal damages action

**Holding and operative rule.** Section 531(e) of the Cable Act does not, by itself and in the statutory setting presented, create the asserted implied private damages action against Cablevision. A prohibition protecting public-access programming does not establish congressional intent to authorize that judicial damages remedy; independently authorized forms of relief must be identified and tested under their own sources.

**Authority.** Souter's unanimous Court opinion controls this rule, its damages-only review scope and the confined remand. All nine Justices join the full explanation.

**Controlling explanation.** The Court holds that the statutory command and the authority to recover damages present different questions. Cort v. Ash, Touche Ross and Transamerica require an inquiry into Congress's intent to authorize the particular action. Cannon permits contextual implication, so the concrete protection of programmers matters and an express remedy elsewhere is not automatically conclusive. But the differentiated provisions here and the limited judicial background do not support this new damages action. Section 532(d) expressly provides a particular leased-capacity action; section 531(c) addresses enforcement of franchise requirements. Neither is a general public-access damages authorization.

The strongest contrary argument combines the protected speakers, the direct editorial-control prohibition, the possible gap in franchise enforcement and Congress's later amendments. Those amendments followed only a few district-court recognitions, not the settled received-action background on which Curran and the existing securities action rest. The subsequent abandonment of one lower recognition is not attributed to Congress as foreknowledge. A gap in compensation does not itself supply the intended remedy, and the Court does not assume local enforcement is adequate or exclusive.

Suter preserves the separate action-creation inquiry. Franklin's appropriate-relief principle applies after a cognizable action exists; it cannot create its own antecedent. Central Bank and Musick, Peeler concern incidents of the received securities action and do not authorize importing it into the Cable Act. The supplied question concerns damages. The Court therefore decides neither every prospective remedy nor the legality of Cablevision's actual exclusion.

**Application, remedy and limits.** The Second Circuit's judgment is reversed only insofar as it permits the asserted implied federal damages action, and the case is remanded. The complaint also requested injunctions and asserted Connecticut law. Any surviving relief requires an actual authorized source and the ordinary preservation, jurisdictional and remedial requirements; no equitable action is created by the request itself. Viewer-claim status remains unresolved, no unpleaded section 1983 claim is created, and no damages are awarded.

Section 531(e) remains subject to section 544(d). Its separate permission to refuse public-access programs or portions containing obscenity, indecency or nudity is permission, not an obligation, and does not automatically extend that public-access wording to every educational or governmental use. The constitutionality and application of that permission, channel forum status, operator state action and whether the actual blanket exclusion was lawful remain undecided.

## Precedent Treatment

- **Cort v. Ash; Touche Ross v. Redington; Transamerica Mortgage Advisors v. Lewis:** Applied to the intent necessary for this asserted action and remedy; no universal express-text prerequisite is created.
- **Cannon v. University of Chicago:** Its contextual inquiry is preserved; beneficiary language and contrast with express remedies are considered rather than made automatically decisive.
- **Merrill Lynch v. Curran:** Distinguished because the limited public-access decisions do not establish its settled judicial-remedy background.
- **Suter, March 25, 1992:** Applied to the independent question whether Congress authorized the asserted action, without deciding section 1983 here.
- **Franklin, February 26, 1992:** Its appropriate-relief rule remains operative within a cognizable action and does not supply the missing authority to recover damages here.
- **Central Bank, April 19, 1994, and Musick, Peeler, June 1, 1993:** Their received securities action remains intact; its development is distinguished from creation of this Cable Act damages action.
- **Denver Area, June 28, 1996:** Its limited PEG judgment and fractured rationales neither establish a universal public forum nor create a statutory damages action; the separate 1996 permission retains its own unresolved constitutional questions.

## Law After Decision

The asserted implied section 531(e) damages route is unavailable. The substantive statutory restrictions remain, while any other relief depends on its actual independent authority. The decision neither validates the exclusion nor establishes a general immunity from suit, and it changes no received securities or other existing private action.

## Separate Writings

No separate writing is filed.

## Procedure After Action

The case returns to the lower courts. The asserted implied federal damages route cannot support relief; any remaining claim must satisfy its own authorization and procedural requirements. Supplemental jurisdiction and the reserved viewer questions remain for lawful consideration without a directed outcome.

The review resolves damages, not factual content or every possible enforcement route.

## Event

Buckley v. American Constitutional Law Foundation, Inc., No. 97-930. Argued October 14, 1998; decided January 12, 1999. On writ of certiorari to the Tenth Circuit, reviewing 120 F.3d 1092. The Court reviews the registered-voter qualification, the personal-name component of the circulation badge, and final and monthly reports identifying individual paid circulators and their compensation. Other petition-administration and reporting fields retain the limits stated below.

## Holdings

**Common authority.** Stevens's Court opinion is joined by Stone-Zsela, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter and Ginsburg throughout. Those six directly support Parts I, III-A and III-B. O'Connor and Breyer additionally join Part II, giving that component eight full rationale votes. Thomas concurs in every invalidity judgment on the distinct grounds described below; his judgment votes are not full joins in the Court's methodology. The rule's limits accompany each component.

### I. Registered-voter condition

**Holding and operative rule.** Colorado may not require otherwise qualified resident-adult initiative circulators to be registered voters on this record. Excluding otherwise qualified speakers because they are unregistered substantially burdens protected petition circulation, while the retained identification and enforcement mechanisms serve traceability more closely.

**Controlling explanation.** The Court holds that a person need not register to vote to engage in the protected political conversation involved in circulating a petition. Meyer v. Grant protects that interaction and the available pool of speakers. Colorado's condition excludes at least 400,000 otherwise qualified but unregistered voters. Easy enrollment does not eliminate that reduction or show that registration adequately measures residence, availability for process or honesty.

The State's strongest argument treats registration as an administrable qualification in a legally operative election process. Anderson and the governing Burdick rule permit appropriately fitted administration, but require attention to the actual burden and interest. Here the sworn identifying and residential-address affidavit, signature review, subpoenas and targeted fraud sanctions address traceability without the registration exclusion. Timmons's different candidate-nomination setting does not make this speaker restriction minor. The Court invalidates the registration condition, not the age floor, circulation period or residence rule whose validity is not presented. An affidavit cannot preserve the invalid condition merely by requiring a registration recital.

### II. Personal name displayed during solicitation

**Holding and operative rule.** Colorado may not compel every circulator to display a personal name on a badge during protected face-to-face petition advocacy when the retained post-circulation identification and enforcement measures serve the asserted interests with materially less speech burden. The personal-name component is invalid; standalone paid-or-volunteer and employer-information requirements are not adjudicated.

**Controlling explanation.** The Court holds that compelled personal attribution at the expressive encounter requires a justification fitted to that burden. McIntyre protects individual political advocacy against inadequately justified identification demands; Meyer protects petition circulation, and Riley prevents payment from stripping a solicitor of speech protection. The trial record shows deterrence from the personal badge, particularly when the subject is unpopular. That is a burden on the actual political conversation.

Even assuming fraud prevention and detection are compelling interests, Colorado has not shown the necessary fit. The identifying affidavit, signature assurances, review, protests and fraud penalties allow officials to identify and investigate circulators without exposing their names to everyone approached. The later public affidavit differs in timing and accessibility from a worn badge; the Court creates no anonymity from lawful investigation. A witness's interest in reporting misconduct does not establish the needed additional benefit of this blanket personal-name display. This holding does not decide standalone paid-status, sponsor or employer attribution, nor invalidate lawful post-circulation verification.

### III-A. Final individual-circulator reports

**Holding and operative rule.** The challenged final-report requirements publishing each paid circulator's identity, address and individual compensation fail the required substantial relationship to Colorado's asserted informational and enforcement interests on this record. Disclosure of initiative proponents and aggregate spending remains distinct; the decision creates no categorical exemption from initiative-finance disclosure.

**Controlling explanation.** Buckley v. Valeo permits campaign-finance disclosure substantially related to important governmental interests. That inquiry requires attention to what the information reveals. Proponent identity and aggregate expenditures reveal who finances an initiative and the extent of paid efforts. The individual messenger is not, for that reason, a financing source or a candidate contributor. Colorado has not demonstrated the added benefit necessary to justify publishing every paid circulator's identity and compensation, without a threshold separating consequential financing from any paid messenger.

The State argues that payee information permits tracing and checking spending beyond aggregate totals. That is a legitimate subject of inquiry, but the retained sponsor, aggregate, amount-per-signature and verification mechanisms make the asserted incremental need insufficient here. Final reporting is less immediate than a badge; it still requires its own disclosure justification. Kentucky Right to Life's payer-identification rule concerns a different financing function and does not answer this demand for every messenger's information. A narrower individual-reporting rule supported under the governing scrutiny remains open.

### III-B. Monthly individual-circulator reports

**Holding and operative rule.** Colorado's challenged monthly requirements publishing each paid circulator's identity, residential and business addresses and individual amounts paid or owed are likewise invalid on this record. The Court decides that individual-information package, not the independent validity of every other monthly-report field.

**Controlling explanation.** The Court separately considers publication during the circulation campaign. Timely information can expose abuse and inform voters before qualification; that argument gives monthly reporting work that a final report cannot always perform. But timing does not cure the insufficient relationship between identifying each messenger and the State's demonstrated financing or enforcement need. It also exposes the individual while the political effort is underway. The retained sponsor and aggregate disclosures and enforcement mechanisms remain material alternatives. The decision does not hold that referendum disclosure has no informational value or that every individual challenge requires proof of reprisals.

The Tenth Circuit invalidated the monthly subsection as a whole. The Court affirms the reviewed invalidity of individual circulator disclosures and leaves the independent validity of its other prescriptions, including proponents and measure identity, undecided. It neither orders those fields conclusively reinstated nor independently declares them unconstitutional. Any ordinary remedial or severability implementation must respect the existing judgment and the scope actually decided.

**Common limits and remedy.** The reviewed registration, personal-name and individual-disclosure invalidations stand. Age, circulation timing, verification affidavits, fraud and signature-review mechanisms are outside the reviewed invalidations and remain governed by their own terms. This decision does not invalidate standalone paid-status or sponsor attribution, create a replacement initiative code, grant damages, destroy public records, authorize fraud or decide every future disclosure rule. Unreviewed requirements receive no automatic new constitutional endorsement.

## Precedent Treatment

- **Meyer v. Grant, 486 U.S. 414:** Applied to the protected political interaction and reduction of available speakers; ordinary properly fitted administration remains possible.
- **McIntyre, April 19, 1995:** Extended to the personal-name demand during live petition advocacy at the stated narrow-fit scope; candidate-finance, organizational and independent post-circulation settings retain their distinctions.
- **Riley v. National Federation of the Blind, 487 U.S. 781:** Applied to protection of paid solicitation and the burden of compelled speech during the encounter; its discussion supplies no adjudication of the reserved badge fields.
- **Anderson v. Celebrezze and Burdick, June 8, 1992:** Their burden-sensitive administrative inquiry remains; the Court applies the actual petition-speech burdens rather than treating every election regulation as equally severe.
- **Timmons, April 28, 1997:** Distinguished as a candidate-nomination setting; it supplies no automatic lesser review for these speaker restrictions.
- **Buckley v. Valeo, 424 U.S. 1:** Its disclosure scrutiny and legitimate financial-information interests are applied to each report; no categorical initiative-disclosure immunity is adopted.
- **Kentucky Right to Life, September 14, 1998:** Its payer and source-identification distinctions remain; identification of the financer does not automatically justify identifying each messenger.

## Law After Decision

Registration cannot be used to exclude otherwise qualified resident-adult circulators; personal names cannot be compelled on the challenged solicitation badge; and the reviewed final and monthly individual-disclosure packages cannot be enforced. Governing law preserves distinct authority for lawful administration, verification, fraud controls, sponsor and aggregate disclosures. The independent validity and remedial treatment of unreviewed badge and monthly fields remain outside this judgment. No fractured-opinion or narrowest-ground synthesis is needed: each Court holding has at least six direct joins, and the badge holding has eight.

## Separate Writings

**Thomas, concurring in the judgment.** Thomas agrees that every reviewed restriction is invalid but applies traditional strict scrutiny to the direct speech and severe associational burdens. Registration reduces the Meyer-protected speaker pool without a narrowly fitted traceability justification. The personal-name badge directly intrudes on core political speech and fails narrow tailoring even assuming a compelling anti-fraud interest. The reports' effect on circulation is more attenuated; compelled disclosure independently burdens association and belief. Singling out paid circulators poorly fits general identification, and the need to expose each individual's identity and compensation has not been demonstrated. Monthly exposure is considered separately from final reporting. As an alternative, the challenged packages also fail Buckley's more forgiving substantial-relationship test. He does not displace Buckley's surviving disclosure holdings, adopt the Court's entire method, invalidate retained reports or decide reserved fields.

**O'Connor, joined by Breyer, concurring in part and dissenting in part.** They join Part II's complete personal-name-badge explanation: direct intrusion into political conversation requires narrow tailoring, and the retained affidavit and enforcement tools defeat this demand on this record. They would sustain registration and the challenged final and monthly reports. Under Anderson–Burdick, readily available enrollment provides an administrable connection to the formal petition process without the severe burden found by the Court; Meyer does not make every qualification equivalent to a ban on paid circulators. Reports are indirect financial-disclosure burdens rather than badges worn during persuasion. Identifying payees and individual disbursements can permit checking campaign activity beyond aggregate sums, and contemporaneous monthly information can expose abuse before the vote. They find the relationship sufficient even under more demanding disclosure review. They acknowledge the excluded unregistered speakers and possible exposure costs, but reach different applications. They propose no automatic admission rule, universal minimum scrutiny or independent disposition of reserved fields.

The reviewed invalidations and corresponding injunctive protection remain in force. The Court orders no replacement system or further Supreme Court proceeding. The lower judgment and ordinary remedial law govern any remaining implementation of unreviewed fields; their independent constitutional validity is not decided.

## Event
**El Al Israel Airlines, Ltd. v. Tsui Yuan Tseng, No. 97-475.** Argued November 10, 1998; decided January 12, 1999. On writ of certiorari to the Second Circuit, 122 F.3d 99. After a bench trial, the District Court dismissed Tseng's personal-injury claim; the Court of Appeals allowed local tort proceedings because it regarded her preboarding security search as no accident under Article 17. The question is whether local law permits personal-injury recovery within the Warsaw Convention's covered carriage when a treaty liability condition is unsatisfied. The separate $1,034.90 baggage award is outside review.

## Participation
All nine Justices participated at argument and decision.

## Public Action
The Second Circuit's judgment on the personal-injury branch is reversed, and the case is remanded for a conforming judgment restoring dismissal of that claim.

## Judgment & Remedy
**Reversed and remanded, 8–1.**

**Supporting:** Chief Justice Stone-Zsela and Justices O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer.

**Opposing:** Justice Stevens.

The New York personal-injury claim must be dismissed. The separate baggage award remains undisturbed; the Court orders no further tort trial or baggage relief.

## Opinion Topology
| Writing | Author | Joined by | Relationship to judgment | Scope joined |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Opinion of the Court, Part I | O'Connor | Stone-Zsela, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer | Supports reversal and dismissal | Exclusivity within Article 17's field and application to the accepted absence of bodily injury; reserves accident classification and Article 25's operation. |
| Opinion of the Court, Part II | O'Connor | Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer | Rejects the asserted Article 25 route on this record | Assumes availability without deciding preservation; leaves the no-willfulness finding undisturbed and reserves the provision's full operation where willfulness is established. Stone-Zsela does not join this part. |
| Dissent | Stevens | No other Justice | Would affirm permission for local-law proceedings | Distinguishes nonaccidents from accidents causing no bodily injury; accepts preemption in the latter category. |

## Holdings
#### Part I — Local law cannot avoid the Convention's conditions within its covered field

**Operative rule.** When a passenger's personal-injury occurrence falls within Article 17's temporal and substantive field, Article 24 subjects an action against the carrier, whatever its legal foundation, to the Convention's conditions and limits. Failure to satisfy the accident or bodily-injury condition does not itself remove the occurrence from that field or permit an independent local damages action; occurrences outside that field remain outside this holding.

**Authority.** Part I of O'Connor's Opinion of the Court has the eight direct joins identified in Opinion Topology and controls this rule and its application.

**Controlling explanation.** Article 24 makes the Convention's conditions apply to actions founded on local law as well as the treaty. Reading a failed condition as permission to escape that condition would deprive it of its limiting force. The reference to Article 17 confines the rule to the personal-injury field of covered international carriage; it does not make liability under the Convention a prerequisite to the operation of its restrictions.

Saks distinguishes the injury from the external event causing it. Floyd enforces Article 17's bodily-injury condition while leaving this exclusivity question open. Zicherman permits domestic law to identify compensable losses and beneficiaries subject to treaty conditions; it does not permit domestic law to create liability when those conditions fail. The absence of a uniform treaty damages code therefore does not establish a separate local source of recovery. Uniformity supports this reading but does not enlarge the treaty's field.

Tseng's search occurred in the operations of embarking for her international flight, not merely somewhere in an airport. The accepted absence of qualifying bodily injury defeats the reviewed personal-injury claim. The Court need not decide whether the search was an accident. It does not categorically exclude every physical manifestation of emotional harm, decide events outside embarking, carriage or disembarking, or alter the baggage award. Part I leaves Article 25's operation open.

#### Part II — The Article 25 argument does not change the result on this record

**Operative rule.** The asserted Article 25 route cannot alter the disposition where the trial's finding of no treaty willful misconduct remains undisturbed. Intentional touching alone does not establish that misconduct; this application decides neither contested preservation nor Article 25's full effect when its misconduct predicate is proved.

**Authority.** Part II has the seven direct Associate joins identified in Opinion Topology. Stone-Zsela does not join it.

**Controlling explanation.** Assuming that Tseng may present her Article 25 argument despite the disputed preservation objection, its factual predicate is absent. The trial court found no treaty willful misconduct, and this record supplies no basis for disturbing that finding. The deliberate character of a search does not itself prove the greater culpability required by the asserted treaty provision. The Court therefore need not determine how Article 25 affects exclusions or limitations when willfulness is established, and does not restrict that provision to monetary ceilings. It also does not infer that intentional conduct can never be an Article 17 accident.

## Precedent Treatment
- **Air France v. Saks, 470 U.S. 392:** Applied to distinguish the external event from injury; its flexible accident rule is unchanged and the classification of this search is not decided.
- **Eastern Airlines, Inc. v. Floyd, 499 U.S. 530:** Applied for Article 17's bodily-injury requirement. Its reserved local-law question is now answered within the treaty's covered personal-injury field.
- **Zicherman v. Korean Air Lines Co., Ltd.:** Preserved as allowing domestic loss categories and beneficiaries subject to treaty conditions; its reserved general-exclusivity issue is resolved in the stated field. It supplies no domestic liability despite a failed treaty condition.

## Law After Decision
Within Article 17's actual personal-injury field, a claimant cannot obtain local damages merely because the Convention's accident or bodily-injury condition is unsatisfied. Domestic law continues to supply losses and beneficiaries where permitted by Zicherman, subject to treaty liability conditions. Outside-field occurrences, baggage questions outside this review, and Article 25's full operation on proved willfulness remain unresolved here.

## Separate Writings
**Justice Stevens, dissenting.** Stevens reads Article 24's reference to Article 17 as covering injuries arising from an accident. He agrees that an accident causing only psychic injury remains governed by the Convention and cannot support a separate local recovery. He disagrees that the same displacement follows for a nonaccident: the text does not clearly extinguish local remedies for that residual category. Saks separates the event from the injury, Floyd leaves independent local claims open, and Zicherman's limited domestic supplementation does not settle the distinction. Uniformity cannot extend the treaty beyond the class its language identifies. Taking the Second Circuit's nonaccident determination as the premise of this review, without independently approving it, he would leave local proceedings available. He would award no treaty recovery and decides no battery, false imprisonment, loss or local defense.

## Procedure After Action
The case returns for a conforming judgment dismissing the personal-injury claim. The baggage judgment remains in place. No accident-classification inquiry or new willfulness hearing is ordered.

## Event
City of West Covina v. Perkins, No. 97-1230. Argued November 3, 1998; decided January 13, 1999. On writ of certiorari to the Ninth Circuit, 113 F.3d 1004 (May 9, 1997). The question is whether due process requires individualized instructions about state-law procedures to recover property seized under a criminal warrant. The Ninth Circuit held the California return procedures adequate, affirmed the individual officers' qualified immunity, and directed judgment for Perkins against the City on an additional-notice theory. Review resolves that instruction theory.

## Participation
All nine Justices participated at argument and decision.

## Public Action
The judgment requiring individualized instructions about published return procedures is reversed, and the case is remanded for further proceedings consistent with this opinion.

## Judgment & Remedy
**Reversed and remanded, 9–0.** Supporting: Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer. The lower courts must reject the federal individualized-instruction theory. Any distinct, preserved challenge to adequacy, accessibility, obstruction or operation remains subject to its own record and ordinary requirements. No new hearing or discovery is ordered, and the individual qualified-immunity judgment is not reopened.

## Opinion Topology
| Writing | Author | Joined by | Relationship to judgment | Scope |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Opinion of the Court | Kennedy | Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Souter, Ginsburg and Breyer | Reversal and remand | Entire opinion, including the procedural-due-process source explanation and both holdings |
| Concurrence in the judgment | Scalia | Thomas | Reversal and remand | Agrees with the negative no-instruction rule, record application and reservations; does not join the lead opinion's derivation of criminal-warrant notice duties from civil procedural-due-process cases |

## Holdings
### 1. Notice of the seizure does not require an individualized guide to published return procedures

**Holding and operative rule:** Due process requires timely notice reasonably calculated to identify the seized property and its responsible custodian so that an owner may seek its return. When the owner receives that notice, due process does not additionally require personalized instructions describing adequate state return procedures reasonably ascertainable from generally available statutes and judicial decisions; publication alone does not establish meaningful access to an inadequate, concealed, misleading, obstructed or practically inaccessible remedy.

**Authority:** Kennedy's opinion of the Court is joined in full by the other members of the seven-Justice coalition of Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Kennedy, Souter, Ginsburg and Breyer. They adopt both holdings and the procedural-due-process explanation. Scalia and Thomas agree with the judgment and the negative rule rejecting the additional legal-instruction duty, but withhold assent to deriving notice duties for criminal warrant execution from civil procedural-due-process decisions. The seven complete joins independently establish controlling authority; no fractured-decision synthesis is necessary.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that notice must inform the owner what the government has done and where to seek the property. Mullane v. Central Hanover Bank & Trust Co. supplies that practical notice principle. It does not require an official to provide a personal explanation of the law an owner may use. Memphis Light, Gas & Water Division v. Craft involved an operative utility-dispute procedure that a customer could not discover simply by consulting generally available law. California's judicial return procedures present a different question. Mathews v. Eldridge requires attention to the actual interests and procedures; the modest expense of adding instructions to a receipt does not alone make those instructions constitutionally necessary. United States v. James Daniel Good Real Property preserves an independent due-process inquiry despite a warrant, but concerned process before civil seizure of real property. It neither settles notice after criminal seizure of movable evidence nor requires a guide to judicial remedies. The receipt here identified the property, agency, issuing judge and court, relevant dates and officer contacts. The Court rejects the additional legal-guide requirement without independently adjudicating every aspect of California's procedures or their operation. Withholding information actually necessary to invoke a remedy, or misleading or obstructing the owner, would present a different properly preserved claim.

**Limits:** The holding does not validate an unlawful initial seizure, unreasonable retention, a failure to disclose the seizure or custodian, or an inaccessible return route. It adopts no comprehensive notice checklist, universal deadline or rule that a warrant satisfies every later process obligation. California Penal Code §1536's custody subject to court order, the nonstatutory return motion recognized in People v. Icenogle, and §1540's separate restoration conditions remain distinct; this case does not merge them or decide that every owner satisfies any one route.

### 2. The additional-notice judgment must be reversed without deciding an unproved obstruction claim

**Holding and operative rule:** The Ninth Circuit's requirement of individualized instructions about published return procedures, and its direction to enter judgment for Perkins on that ground, must be reversed. The omitted warrant number does not sustain that judgment because the District Court found that respondents had not established the number was necessary to bring a return motion; the reported difficulties do not establish a different obstruction ground.

**Authority:** The seven-Justice opinion of the Court supplies the controlling explanation. Scalia and Thomas also support this application and its reservations through their concurrence in the judgment; all nine support reversal and remand.

**Controlling explanation:** The Ninth Circuit held the available California procedures constitutionally adequate but demanded additional instruction about using them. This Court rejects that legal demand; it does not independently endorse every procedure or its practical administration. Perkins reported difficulty finding the warrant, the issuing judge's absence, a mistaken number and a sealed warrant. Some officer statements were disputed. A public index connected the searched address to the warrant number, and the District Court's unrefuted finding was that necessity of that number had not been established. These circumstances do not permit the Court to replace the lower finding with a determination that officials withheld an indispensable identifier. Nor does rejection of the instruction theory extinguish a distinct, properly preserved challenge to actual obstruction or accessibility. Such a claim must rest on its own record and procedural basis. Return of most property in June 1994 does not itself dispose of a supported claim concerning an earlier deprivation. The judgment on individual qualified immunity is not reopened, and the Court adjudicates neither entitlement to the reportedly stolen starter pistol nor damages.

**Remand limit:** The lower courts must reject the federal individualized-instruction theory. Any preserved adequacy, accessibility, obstruction or operation issue remains subject to ordinary procedural and substantive requirements; no new claim, fresh hearing or exploratory discovery is ordered.

## Precedent Treatment
- **Mullane v. Central Hanover Bank & Trust Co., 339 U.S. 306:** Applied to notice of governmental action that permits a meaningful response; it does not impose a general duty to teach published law.
- **Memphis Light, Gas & Water Division v. Craft, 436 U.S. 1:** Distinguished because notice of an operative utility-dispute procedure not reasonably discoverable in public legal sources differs from instruction in published judicial remedies. Its protection of meaningful access survives.
- **Mathews v. Eldridge, 424 U.S. 319:** Applied to the actual notice function and available procedures; low administrative cost alone does not establish an added constitutional duty.
- **United States v. James Daniel Good Real Property (December 13, 1993):** Its independent due-process protection is preserved. Its civil real-property, pre-seizure rule is distinguished from notice following criminal seizure of movable property; no warrant-based displacement of all due process is adopted.
- **People v. Icenogle, 164 Cal. App. 3d 620:** Its recognition of a nonstatutory return motion identifies an available state-law route. The Court does not convert that route into §1540's different statutory restoration conditions or decide a particular owner's entitlement.

## Law After Decision
An owner with timely notice of the seizure and custodian has no federal entitlement to personalized instruction about adequate, reasonably ascertainable published return procedures. Meaningful notice, actual access and a distinct supported obstruction claim remain separate questions. The ruling changes the Ninth Circuit's instruction requirement while leaving Good's civil real-property protection and California's separate return routes intact. It resolves no independent immunity, warrant-validity, damages or title question.

## Separate Writings
**Justice Scalia, joined by Justice Thomas, concurring in the judgment.** The concurrence agrees that officials need not give personalized instruction about generally available return law and that the missing warrant number does not sustain the judgment on this record. It withholds assent to the Court's use of civil procedural-due-process decisions as the source of notice requirements when officers execute a criminal search warrant. Mullane's notice setting and Good's civil real-property seizure do not, without further analysis, establish the duties attending execution of a criminal warrant. That source objection does not require approval of secrecy or obstruction, a finding that the Fourth Amendment affirmatively commands a particular inventory, or a holding that it displaces every later due-process protection. The concurrence leaves the constitutional source, timing and detailed content of any separate notice obligation unresolved and joins the limited reversal and remand.

## Procedure After Action
The case returns to the lower courts with directions to reject the additional individualized-instruction theory. Any properly preserved distinct claim remains governed by its own procedural posture and proof. The decision does not establish obstruction, entitlement to the reported stolen pistol, a damages award or invalidity of the original seizure.

## Source Notes
[The Ninth Circuit opinion and dissent](https://law.resource.org/pub/us/case/reporter/F3/113/113.F3d.1004.94-56365.html) supply the receipt, reported retrieval efforts, disputed officer statements, state return routes and judgment under review. The reported difficulties remain distinct from the District Court's finding that necessity of the warrant number had not been established. The opinion adjudicates no independent claim that requires resolving those factual disputes.

## Event
Marcus v. Iowa Public Television, No. 98-710. Merits decision, January 13, 1999. On writ of certiorari to the Eighth Circuit, No. 96-3645, 150 F.3d 924 (en banc, July 30, 1998). The question is whether a state broadcaster may exclude ballot-qualified minor-party candidates from a selectively composed debate on newsworthiness grounds. The District Court independently adopted advisory findings of genuine editorial selection and rejected viewpoint exclusion; the Court of Appeals affirmed. Review concerns the final common selection-policy challenge, rather than only the two broadcasts covered by the earlier emergency motion.

## Participation
All nine Justices participated in the merits consideration and decision.

## Public Action
The judgment rejecting the common selection-policy claim and its derivative equitable relief is affirmed.

## Judgment & Remedy
**Affirmed, 6–3.** Supporting: Stone-Zsela, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Thomas and Breyer. Opposing: Stevens, Souter and Ginsburg. The affirmance denies declaratory, injunctive and program-guide correction relief insofar as it depends on the rejected forum violation. It decides no independent guide claim, establishes no continuing guide injury, and orders no replacement broadcast, future admission or damages. The dissent would vacate rejection of the common policy claim and remand under adequately constrained standards, leaving the guide request's permissible remedial and jurisdictional basis for examination.

## Opinion Topology
| Writing | Author | Joined by | Relationship to judgment | Scope |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Opinion of the Court, Part I | O'Connor | Stevens, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer | Reaches the merits | Committee-based capable-of-repetition rationale; eight Associates. Stone-Zsela agrees to reach the merits but does not join this explanation. |
| Opinion of the Court, Part II | O'Connor | Stone-Zsela, Stevens, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer | Shared classification and governing rule | Actual nonpublic-forum classification, reasonable selection and viewpoint neutrality; nine. |
| Opinion of the Court, Part III | O'Connor | Stone-Zsela, Scalia, Kennedy, Thomas and Breyer | Affirmance | Iowa's standards and application satisfy Forbes; denial of the common claim and derivative relief; six. |
| Dissent in part | Stevens | Souter and Ginsburg | Would vacate and remand the common policy rejection | Joins Parts I–II; rejects Part III's sufficiency holding and corresponding remedy. |

## Holdings
#### 1. The Natural Law Party committee's policy challenge is capable of repetition yet evading review

**Holding and operative rule:** A challenge to completed pre-election broadcasts remains justiciable for the same continuing party committee when its established relationship to several excluded candidates, general electoral function, the broadcaster's general selection practice and its recurring program support a reasonable expectation of another short candidate-access controversy involving that committee. Both the period too short for complete review and the same claimant's reasonable prospect of recurrence are required; neither election timing nor an abstract organizational purpose alone suffices.

**Authority:** Part I of O'Connor's opinion is joined by Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer: eight Justices. Stone-Zsela agrees that the Court reaches the merits but does not join this threshold explanation.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that the Natural Law Party committee's common selection-policy challenge survives the 1996 broadcasts. The stipulated background distinguishes that general party organization from the separate committee formed for Marcus's 1996 campaign. Its participation with excluded candidates across congressional districts connects its electoral function to this broadcaster's actual selection practice. That concrete pattern, the general policy and the established recurring Iowa Press program support a reasonable expectation that this same committee will again confront the disputed practice. The opportunity for pre-election appearances is too short for complete ordinary appellate review. Spencer v. Kemna requires both facts and forbids a merely possible injury to others from supplying recurrence. Norman v. Reed supplies the comparison to continuing party interests, although its distinct party-qualification consequences are absent here. Chandler v. Miller established recurrence through an individual's stated intention to run again; it does not make that particular proof indispensable for a continuing organizational claimant. Honig v. Doe permits a grounded assessment of likelihood rather than requiring certainty of repetition. The unknown next candidate and unscheduled next debate make recurrence less certain, but do not erase the established relationship between this committee and the general practice. The conclusion is an inference from those facts, not a finding of a new candidacy, later exclusion, unchanged policy in 1999 or later campaign activity. The absence of contrary evidence does not shift the jurisdictional burden.

**Limits:** The surviving common claim does not separately preserve every individual's expired demand, establish future standing for every named plaintiff or supply an independent current injury from program-guide language. There is no general election exception, relaxed jurisdiction because review was granted, presumed reputational consequence or order to recreate a past broadcast.

#### 2. The actually selective debate is a nonpublic forum

**Holding and operative rule:** A state broadcaster's candidate debate is a nonpublic forum when the broadcaster actually retains editorial selection rather than opening the program generally to all qualified candidates. Restrictions must reasonably serve the program's journalistic purpose and remain viewpoint neutral; public ownership, a journalism label and ballot qualification alone decide neither access nor validity.

**Authority:** Part II of O'Connor's opinion is joined by all nine Justices: Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer. Agreement on classification and the governing requirements does not imply agreement that Iowa's standards satisfy them.

**Controlling explanation:** Arkansas Educational Television Commission v. Forbes supplies the governing rule because Iowa Press retained selection by invitation. The District Court independently adopted the finding that the broadcaster intended admission only for invited candidates, and the actual format paired selected candidates with a host and political reporters. Calling the programs debates, as the District Court did, does not transform them into an open microphone; calling them bona fide news interviews does not immunize them. Cornelius v. NAACP Legal Defense & Educational Fund and Perry Education Association v. Perry Local Educators' Association direct attention to actual access and permitted purpose. Rosenberger v. Rector and Visitors of the University of Virginia preserves the speakers' private viewpoints within the actual opportunity. The question is therefore whether this selective platform was administered reasonably and without viewpoint discrimination. It is not whether every ballot-qualified candidate must be admitted or whether the broadcaster's public status makes all candidate speech its own message.

**Limits:** A program genuinely opened to qualifying candidates presents a different access question. Reasonable selection is permitted; sham criteria, incumbent favoritism, inconsistent administration and unpopular-viewpoint exclusions remain subject to review.

#### 3. Iowa's adjudicated selection satisfies Forbes; the forum claim and its derivative relief fail

**Holding and operative rule:** On this record, the selective format, legitimate informing purpose, editorial-integrity policies, evidence of electoral engagement and public interest, and independently credited findings support reasonable and viewpoint-neutral selection. Forbes imposes no additional universal numerical formula, written-policy, advance-publication or prescribed investigative prerequisite; the affirmed absence of a forum violation defeats declaratory, injunctive and guide-correction relief sought as remedies for that violation.

**Authority:** Part III of O'Connor's opinion is joined by Stone-Zsela, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Thomas and Breyer: six Justices. Stevens, Souter and Ginsburg dissent from the sufficiency of Iowa's standards and would vacate the rejection of the common policy claim and remand.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that Forbes permits the editorial judgment established here. Instruction 15 described newsworthiness through sufficient audience and public interest. It operated alongside policies of fairness, objectivity, balance, independence from pressure and an informed citizenry, and testimony about campaign activity, organization, prior performance, fundraising breadth, media recognition and public engagement. Those considerations bear on journalistic purpose; they are not a binding checklist or findings that every candidate lacked each characteristic. The judge independently assessed credibility and adopted findings of genuine selective editorial judgment, rather than exclusion because of disagreement with political views. That finding matters, but does not alone settle constitutional reasonableness. Petitioners also asserted insufficient individual inquiry, missing candidate information, substantial publicity, invitations from opponents and fundraising comparisons. Those contentions do not establish clear error in the credited findings or compel a different constitutional judgment. The reported figures prove neither an adopted dollars rule nor materially equal electoral significance. The Court does not declare every allegation false or reduce the challenge to ballot qualification. Regan v. Time, Inc., which concerns governmental restrictions on another publisher's content, does not invalidate all content-sensitive selection in a nonpublic candidate forum. Nor does Forbes make professional sincerity conclusive. The actual record supplies the reasonable connection here without judicial substitution of a different editorial ranking.

**Limits and remedy:** The Court does not approve categorical exclusion of idea-centered campaigns, manipulation favoring incumbent parties, or rejection of a genuinely viable minor-party candidate simply because of party status. Offering another program does not cure viewpoint discrimination. The program-guide correction request fails only as derivative relief for the rejected forum claim; no independent cause of action, current guide dissemination, reputational injury or correction entitlement is decided. The judgment orders no new broadcast, future inclusion, damages or statutory access remedy.

## Precedent Treatment
- **Arkansas Educational Television Commission v. Forbes (May 18, 1998):** Applied to Iowa's actual selective format, governing reasonable and viewpoint-neutral choice, and supported adjudicated application. Its rejection of universal numerical, written or advance-publication requirements remains operative; its pretext, actual-comparator and open-access limits remain intact.
- **Cornelius v. NAACP Legal Defense & Educational Fund, 473 U.S. 788, and Perry Education Association v. Perry Local Educators' Association, 460 U.S. 37:** Applied to actual selective access and restrictions reasonably related to the forum's purpose.
- **Rosenberger v. Rector and Visitors of the University of Virginia (June 29, 1995):** Its protection of private viewpoints within an actual expressive opportunity remains applicable; it does not compel universal access to a genuinely selective debate.
- **Regan v. Time, Inc., 468 U.S. 641:** Distinguished because its private-publication restriction differs from the selective candidate-forum question governed by Forbes. It is not overruled or treated as banning every judgment of newsworthiness.
- **Spencer v. Kemna (March 3, 1998):** Its concrete-continuing-controversy and two-part recurrence requirements are applied. The committee's established organizational connection supplies the same-claimant basis absent from speculative injury to an unidentified future person.
- **Norman v. Reed (January 14, 1992):** Its continuing-party-interest analysis informs the recurrence inquiry. Its actual party-qualification consequences are not imported into this record.
- **Chandler v. Miller (April 15, 1997):** Its reliance on the candidate's stated future candidacy is distinguished from this continuing committee's established electoral activity; that individual proof is not made an exclusive method of demonstrating recurrence.
- **Honig v. Doe, 484 U.S. 305:** Applied for a supported likelihood assessment rather than certainty of repetition. No general post-grant relaxation of Article III is adopted.

## Law After Decision
Forbes continues to govern genuinely selective public-broadcast debates. Iowa's supported contextual editorial judgment satisfies that rule without a new universal formula or procedural prerequisite, while viewpoint, pretext, actual-comparator and forum-boundary review remain required. The committee-specific Article III holding permits review of this common policy challenge; it does not keep all former candidates' individual demands alive. The affirmed denial of the common forum claim defeats its derivative equitable relief, including the guide-correction request only in that capacity.

## Separate Writings
**Justice Stevens, joined by Justices Souter and Ginsburg, dissenting in part.** The dissent joins the committee-based jurisdictional holding and the nonpublic-forum classification, including the permission for reasonable selection and the prohibition of viewpoint discrimination. It maintains the standards objection stated in the Forbes dissent: allocation of a government-provided candidate platform requires sufficiently definite and reviewable restraints on selection. Iowa's fairness policies and illustrative factors describe professional judgment, but Instruction 15 still leaves officials to decide which candidacies interest the audience without adequate operative limits. The dissent accepts the adverse findings about viewpoint motive. Its objection is that sincere discretion and absence of hostility do not cure inadequate standards under Cornelius, Shuttlesworth and Rosenberger. The candidates' assertions about missing information, publicity and comparisons show why meaningful review matters; the dissent does not treat those assertions as findings of equal electoral significance or discrimination.

The dissent would vacate rejection of the committee's common policy claim and remand for consideration under adequately constrained selection standards. It would return the program-guide request for consideration of its connection to permissible equitable relief and any continuing injury the relief could redress, without establishing an independent guide claim or an entitlement to correction. It would order neither automatic ballot-based admission, a universal numerical threshold, a replacement 1996 broadcast, compulsory future programming nor damages. These views remain noncontrolling; they do not change the operative Forbes rule.

## Procedure After Action
The Eighth Circuit judgment rejecting the common forum claim and derivative relief stands. No new broadcast, particular future appearance or corrective guide statement is required. The decision does not create an independently live individual claim or resolve a separate guide cause of action.

## Event
Roberts v. Galen of Virginia, Inc., No. 97-53. Argued December 1, 1998; decided January 13, 1999. On writ of certiorari to the Sixth Circuit, 111 F.3d 405 (April 9, 1997). The question is whether EMTALA's stabilization requirement adds proof of improper motive. Wanda Johnson was admitted May 19, 1992 and transferred July 24, 1992. The Court of Appeals recognized a factual dispute about stabilization but affirmed summary judgment on the additional motive requirement; its separate state ostensible-agency disposition is outside the reversal.

## Participation
All nine Justices participated at argument and decision.

## Public Action
The judgment on the appealed EMTALA stabilization claim is reversed, and the case is remanded for further proceedings consistent with this opinion.

## Judgment & Remedy
**Reversed and remanded, 9–0.** Supporting: Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer. The reversal removes the improper-motive ground for summary judgment. The actual-knowledge and completed-screening defenses remain open, as do the statute's other predicates and available remedies. The separate state ostensible-agency ruling and other claims are not reopened; no liability judgment, damages award or mandatory trial is ordered.

## Opinion Topology
Per curiam opinion of the Court, joined in full by Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer.

## Holdings
#### 1. Stabilization has no additional improper-motive element

**Holding and operative rule:** A claim under 42 U.S.C. §1395dd(b) does not require proof that a participating hospital acted with an improper financial or other invidious motive. Liability nevertheless requires proof of the enacted stabilization or transfer duty and the applicable civil-action predicates; removing the extra motive requirement establishes neither breach nor injury.

**Authority:** The per curiam opinion is joined by Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer. All nine adopt both holdings and their stated limits.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that the stabilization provision means what its conditions say. When an individual comes to a hospital and the hospital determines that the individual has an emergency medical condition, subsection (b)(1) requires further examination and treatment within the available staff and facilities to stabilize the condition, or transfer in accordance with subsection (c). The text does not make that obligation depend on why the hospital acts. Subsection (a)'s separate requirement of an appropriate screening examination cannot insert an additional condition into subsection (b). National Organization for Women, Inc. v. Scheidler supplies the analogous principle that a court may not add a motive requirement while Congress's actual liability conditions remain intact. Concern that EMTALA should not become general federal malpractice law does not permit rewriting those conditions. Its determination, stabilization, transfer, injury and remedial requirements continue to distinguish a statutory violation from negligent care. Cleland's screening analysis therefore cannot sustain the additional motive element imposed on this stabilization claim. The Court does not decide whether subsection (a) contains a motive requirement, how long this hospital's duty continued, or whether the patient proves its violation.

**Operative qualifications retained:** Subsection (b)(2) deems the treatment requirement satisfied when the hospital offers the required examination and treatment, informs the patient or representative of their risks and benefits, and that person refuses; the hospital must take all reasonable steps to obtain written informed refusal. Subsection (b)(3) separately addresses refusal of a transfer offered in accordance with subsection (c), after risk-and-benefit information, with the same reasonable-efforts duty concerning written informed refusal. Neither provision is a finding about this patient.

For an unstabilized emergency condition, subsection (c)(1) separately requires either a written transfer request made by the individual or legally responsible representative after receiving information about the hospital's EMTALA duties and the risks of transfer, or a physician's signed certification, based on the information available when transfer occurs, that the expected medical benefits of appropriate treatment elsewhere outweigh the increased transfer risks, including risks to the unborn child in labor; it also requires an appropriate transfer. If no physician is physically present in the emergency department, a medical person qualified under the Secretary's regulations may sign after consultation with a physician who makes the required determination and later countersigns. The certification must summarize its supporting risks and benefits. Appropriate transfer requires risk-minimizing treatment within the transferring hospital's capacity; available receiving space, qualified personnel and acceptance with appropriate treatment; transmission of all emergency-condition records available at transfer, including the condition, observed signs or symptoms, preliminary diagnosis, treatment, test results and written request or certification, together with the name and address of an on-call physician who refused or did not appear within a reasonable time to provide necessary stabilization; qualified personnel and transportation equipment, including necessary and medically appropriate life support during transfer; and any further applicable regulatory conditions. None is declared satisfied or violated here.

Subsection (d)(2)(A) permits the person directly harmed by a participating hospital's violation to sue that hospital for personal-injury damages available under the law of its State and appropriate equitable relief. The separate limitation is two years after the violation. This decision creates no personal action against an individual physician and no automatic liability for later deterioration.

#### 2. The motive-based summary judgment must be reversed; other predicates remain open

**Holding and operative rule:** Summary judgment rejecting the stabilization claim because the guardian failed to establish improper motive rests on an element the statute does not contain and must be reversed. The hospital's actual-knowledge and completed-screening defenses remain open for lawful consideration on remand; neither is accepted or rejected on its merits by this decision.

**Authority:** The same unanimous per curiam opinion controls this confined reversal and remand.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court of Appeals recognized a genuine dispute about stabilization but nevertheless sustained judgment because the evidence did not sufficiently connect financial pressure to the physician's discharge decision. Eliminating that erroneous element removes its ground for judgment. Evidence concerning the patient's infection, the safety of transfer before the antibiotic response and culture results could be assessed, and the possible role of later care does not establish the remaining statutory elements in either party's favor. The hospital argues that it lacked actual knowledge of an emergency condition and that proper screening ended its duty. Those arguments must be assessed on their own legal and factual predicates. The Court neither converts the stabilization dispute into an undisputed absence of knowledge nor holds that screening necessarily ends every stabilization obligation. The preserved ability of a respondent to defend a favorable judgment on a supported alternative does not supply missing findings. The separate state-law ostensible-agency ruling and other claims are not reopened. No liability judgment, damages award or mandatory trial is ordered.

**Questions not reached:** Whether an emergency medical condition existed, what the hospital determined or knew, duration of any duty, stabilization, authorized transfer, causal injury, damages and ultimate liability remain undecided. The treatment-refusal, transfer-refusal and transfer-certification provisions remain distinct; no exception is presumed.

## Precedent Treatment
- **National Organization for Women, Inc. v. Scheidler (January 24, 1994):** Applied by analogy for refusal to add an omitted motive element while preserving every enacted liability condition. Its RICO construction remains distinct from EMTALA.
- **Cleland v. Bronson Health Care Group, 917 F.2d 266 (6th Cir. 1990):** The extension of its screening-motivation analysis to impose an extra element under subsection (b) is rejected. The Court does not decide subsection (a)'s screening-motive question.
- **United States v. X-Citement Video, Inc. (November 29, 1994):** Its favorable-judgment defense rule is respected without treating an unestablished alternative factual premise as proved. Neither alternative defense is conclusively adjudicated here.

## Law After Decision
EMTALA stabilization litigation proceeds without an additional improper-purpose element. The statutory determination, treatment or lawful-transfer conditions, direct personal harm, causation, permitted remedies and limitations period retain their independent force. Screening, stabilization and the separate state-law agency claim remain distinct. The judgment changes no general medical-negligence rule and awards no relief beyond reversal and remand of the appealed stabilization disposition.

## Procedure After Action
The case returns to the lower courts for further proceedings on the stabilization claim under the statutory requirements without the additional improper-motive element. The Court expresses no view on the factual truth or legal dispositiveness of the hospital's alternative defenses. The separate state-law disposition remains undisturbed.

## Event
**State Street Bank & Trust Co. v. Signature Financial Group, Inc.; Federal Circuit No. 96-1327.** Decided January 14, 1999, on merits review of the Federal Circuit's July 23, 1998 judgment, 149 F.3d 1368. The Court of Appeals reversed the District of Massachusetts' summary judgment of ineligibility, 927 F. Supp. 502, concerning Patent 5,193,056. The questions concern the Hub and Spoke financial data-processing system's eligibility under §101, a categorical business-method exclusion, and the relationship between actual claim construction and the mathematical-idea limitation. Six issued claims cover successive combinations of programmed processing, storage and financial-allocation routines.

## Participation
All nine Justices participated in the merits submission and decision.

## Public Action
The Federal Circuit's eligibility-based reversal is reversed. Issued claims 1–6 are ineligible under §101, and the case is remanded for proceedings consistent with that determination.

## Judgment & Remedy
**Reversed and remanded, 8–1.**

**Supporting:** Justices Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer.

**Opposing:** Chief Justice Stone-Zsela.

The District Court's invalidity result for issued claims 1–6 is sustained on the mathematical-calculation ground, without adopting its business-method exclusion. ## Holdings
#### Part I-A — Business use supplies no separate exclusion

**Operative rule.** An otherwise eligible invention is not excluded from §101 merely because its use is commercial or concerns financial administration. Rejecting that categorical exclusion does not establish eligibility, which remains subject to the mathematical-principle limitation and every other statutory requirement.

**Authority.** Part I-A of Stevens's Opinion of the Court has all nine Justices' direct joins.

**Controlling explanation.** Section 101 identifies statutory categories without excluding an industry. Chakrabarty applies those broad categories while preserving the exclusion of abstract principles. A business label therefore supplies no independent ground for invalidity, just as commercial usefulness supplies no independent ground for eligibility. The District Court's categorical business-method reasoning cannot sustain its judgment. The Court must examine the subject actually claimed.

#### Part I-B — Useful financial output is insufficient; the actual whole claim controls

**Operative rule.** A mathematical calculation does not become eligible solely because it yields a useful financial result, is confined to a named business arrangement, or is expressed as a programmed machine; the whole claimed combination must supply a patentable application rather than the calculation with its ordinary implementation. Conversely, mathematics or computer use does not itself make a claim ineligible, and means-plus-function limitations must be construed to include the corresponding disclosed structure and equivalents.

**Authority.** Part I-B has all nine Justices' direct joins at this level of generality; agreement on the standard does not establish agreement on its final application.

**Controlling explanation.** Benson involved useful computation, and Flook involved a useful numerical update limited to a particular setting. Those decisions prevent utility from deciding eligibility. Diehr requires considering the entire claimed application and forbids removing limitations merely because they were familiar; its curing process also shows the distinction between using mathematics in an application and claiming the calculation itself.

Section 112, paragraph 6, limits functional means to corresponding disclosed structure and equivalents. The issued combinations therefore cannot be treated simply as the cancelled methods or as every possible means of obtaining a number. Markman and Vitronics require actual claim meaning to be determined from the public instrument; Warner-Jenkinson preserves each material boundary. None makes a machine label dispositive. The Court adopts no universal physical-transformation requirement, independent noncomputer-contribution requirement, or rule that every program is abstract. The distinct requirements of novelty, nonobviousness, disclosure and infringement remain intact.

#### Part II — Each issued combination remains the financial calculation and its bookkeeping implementation

**Operative rule.** With each disclosed means and incorporated limitation retained, issued claims 1–6 cover the calculation, verification and recording of financial allocations rather than a qualifying application of their result, and are ineligible under §101. This conclusion sustains their invalidity without deciding the separate claims and counterclaims on their merits.

**Authority.** Part II has the eight Associates' direct joins identified in Opinion Topology; Stone-Zsela does not join.

**Controlling explanation.** The programmed computer and storage are real limitations and are considered together with every routine. The following table identifies each complete combination and why its added work remains accounting calculation:

| Claim | Whole combination considered and application |
|---|---|
| 1 | Processor/storage, initialization, fund-change and percentage processing, daily income/expenses/realized allocations, unrealized allocations and year-end data calculate and record the financial values. |
| 2 | Adds pricing-date/fund-asset storage, medium identification and investment, ratio, incremental, daily and breakage files: these organize inputs, intermediate values and results. |
| 3 | Adds correct-medium verification, date retrieval/correction, fund-change input/storage and prior-investment retrieval with adjusted-investment/ratio calculation/storage: these protect the calculation's intended input record. |
| 4 | Begins "A method as claimed in claim 3" and retains that dependency; adds medium/date checks/correction, incremental income/expense/realized retrieval and storage, investment/ratio retrieval and prepricing-asset computation: these refine the accounting values. |
| 5 | Incorporates claim 4; adds medium/date checks, unrealized input, investment/ratio/incremental/breakage retrieval, end-of-day calculation/storage and breakage/daily updates: these handle fractional discrepancies and maintain accurate allocations. |
| 6 | Incorporates claim 5; adds medium verification, daily-activity retrieval and year-end income/expense/capital-gain-or-loss aggregation and storage: these produce and retain the annual accounting result. |

The useful values affect ownership records, tax reporting and valuation, but the claims require no further operation applying their result comparable to Diehr's curing process. Flook's useful restricted calculation remains the relevant distinction. This conclusion assumes novelty and nonobviousness; no limitation is subtracted as prior art, and no disputed external technical fact is resolved against Signature. The accepted specification and scope reveal no material unperformed task requiring an eligibility remand. Claim 4's wording supplies no independent drafting-defect holding. A different computer operation or commercial application requires its own analysis.

## Precedent Treatment
- **Gottschalk v. Benson, 409 U.S. 63:** Applied to reject useful computation as sufficient eligibility; no general software prohibition follows.
- **Parker v. Flook, 437 U.S. 584:** Applied to the distinction between a restricted useful numerical calculation and its qualifying application; no element-by-element novelty subtraction is adopted.

- **Vitronics Corp. v. Conceptronic, Inc.:** Applied to the public instrument's meaning and permissible technical assistance; absent prosecution history supplies no disclaimer or mandatory hearing.
- **Warner-Jenkinson Co. v. Hilton Davis Chemical Co.:** Applied to preserve every material limitation; its infringement and prosecution-surrender rules are not replaced by this eligibility ruling.
- **Pfaff v. Wells Electronics, Inc.:** Preserved as governing the separate commercial-offer and readiness conditions of the on-sale bar; it supplies no mathematical-claim eligibility test.

## Law After Decision
Section 101 neither excludes inventions because they serve business nor establishes eligibility because they produce useful financial values. Courts must construe and assess the actual complete combination under the inherited calculation/application distinction. These six issued combinations are ineligible; that result establishes no categorical answer for all software, data structures or commercial inventions. Other patent requirements and the retained judicial/factual allocation continue to operate separately.

## Separate Writings
**Chief Justice Stone-Zsela, concurring in part and dissenting in part.** Stone-Zsela agrees that the business-method exclusion and the useful-result sufficiency rule are too broad. Benson and Flook show that a useful computation is not enough; Diehr requires the integrated application to be examined as a whole. Section 112 prevents treating the issued machine claims simply as cancelled methods. He would require the lower court to determine the actual corresponding structure and decide whether the integrated system claims a practical machine application or effectively the accounting mathematics itself. Correcting the appellate test does not, in his view, justify automatically reinstating the District Court's categorical approach. He would vacate and remand that inquiry under Markman without turning every technical finding into a universally de novo issue. He decides neither ultimate validity nor infringement and preserves the separate requirements of novelty, nonobviousness and disclosure.

## Event
California Public Employees' Retirement System and Florida State Board of Administration v. Paul Felzen et al., No. 97-1732. Argued January 11, 1999; decided January 20, 1999. On writ of certiorari to the Seventh Circuit, Felzen v. Andreas, 134 F.3d 873 (January 21, 1998). The question is whether shareholders who respond to Rule 23.1 notice, appear and object to approval of a derivative settlement must intervene to appeal. Both institutions were heard in opposition to the settlement but chose not to seek intervention. The District Court approved the settlement on July 7, 1997; their July 14 notice of appeal identified the settlement judgment and fee/expense order. The Court of Appeals dismissed on the appellate-status ground without reaching the settlement's merits.

## Participation
All nine Justices participated at argument and decision.

## Public Action
The judgment dismissing the institutions' appeal is affirmed.

## Judgment & Remedy
**Affirmed, 5–4.** Supporting: Stone-Zsela, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy and Thomas. Opposing: Stevens, Souter, Ginsburg and Breyer. The dismissal leaves the settlement undisturbed because this attempted nonparty appeal cannot proceed; the Court does not approve the settlement's substantive fairness or decide attorney compensation. No intervention application, denial, reopening, tolling or late-intervention entitlement is adjudicated. The dissent would reverse the status dismissal and remand for further appellate proceedings under a limited representative-objector rule, without directing rejection of the settlement or granting fee relief.

## Opinion Topology
| Writing | Author | Joined by | Relationship to judgment | Scope joined |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Opinion of the Court | Stone-Zsela | O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Thomas | Affirms dismissal | Both holdings, their complete reasoning, qualifications and confined remedy. |
| Dissent | Stevens | Souter, Ginsburg, Breyer | Would reverse and remand | Limited representative-objector status for review of the rejected settlement objections; no settlement or fee merits determination. |

## Holdings
#### 1. Rule 23.1 objection does not itself confer the party status needed for this appeal

**Holding and operative rule:** A shareholder who is otherwise not a party does not acquire appellate party status merely by appearing under Rule 23.1 notice and presenting objections that the district court hears and rejects when approving a derivative settlement. To appeal that settlement judgment, the shareholder must properly become a party through intervention; shareholder interest, notice and the recognized opportunity to object do not themselves supply the necessary status.

**Authority:** Stone-Zsela's opinion of the Court is joined by the other four members of the coalition comprising Stone-Zsela, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy and Thomas. All five adopt both holdings and their stated qualifications.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that an opportunity to assist the settlement court by objecting is distinct from authority to continue the corporate litigation on appeal. Marino v. Ortiz supplies both the general party-status rule and its application to persons actually heard in opposition to a settlement who had not intervened. Rule 23.1 recognizes shareholder participation in the district court's supervision of the corporate claim; that function does not itself confer the separate appellate role. Tryforos v. Icarian Development Co. supplies the strongest contrary rule. Its distinction between objecting to disposition and assuming prosecution expressly included an appeal by a shareholder who appeared to oppose a proposed settlement. The Court rejects that settlement-appeal proposition insofar as it permits this appeal without intervention: limiting an objector's appellate objectives does not remove the status prerequisite Marino applies. Izumi Seimitsu Kogyo Kabushiki Kaisha v. U.S. Philips Corp. reinforces the distinction between interest and status, although its undisturbed intervention denial and separately unpresented intervention question differ from this case's direct status challenge. Johnson v. Manhattan Railway Co. does not establish an independent route here. Its discussion of invitation, appearance and appeal at page 495 is followed at page 496 by an explanation why the applicable equity rule would not obstruct intervention, in a distinct receivership setting. Neither passage decides modern derivative-objector status by itself. These institutions were heard but sought no intervention. Their reliance on earlier circuit practice does not constitute admission as parties, and no denial of an application is before the Court.

**Limits:** This is a rule for the presented derivative-settlement appeal, not a categorical prohibition of every nonparty appeal under every procedural regime. Section 1291 supplies the final-decision framework, Appellate Rule 3(c) concerns identification of appellants, Rule 23.1 governs the recognized objection role, and Rule 24 supplies intervention; neither §1291 nor Rule 3(c) is treated as expressly deciding every party-status category. The decision rests on actual participation and the absence of intervention, not on a finding that the institutions violated a notice or service condition.

#### 2. Intervention review and nonparty due-process protection remain distinct

**Holding and operative rule:** Requiring intervention for this settlement appeal does not foreclose review by an actual party or an appropriate appeal from an actual denial of intervention. Nor does it impose a general duty to intervene in another person's suit to preserve an otherwise independent claim: constitutional limits on binding genuinely unrepresented persons remain, without establishing an appellate entitlement or a collateral remedy in this case.

**Authority:** The same five-Justice opinion of the Court controls this boundary and the confined affirmance.

**Controlling explanation:** Izumi preserves a lawful route for reviewing an intervention denial while distinguishing it from review of the underlying judgment by a party. No such denial exists here, so the Court decides neither entitlement to intervention nor the timing or result of an application never made. Richards v. Jefferson County protects a person's own rights against preclusion without constitutionally sufficient consent, control, genuine privity, adequate representative litigation or another recognized basis consistent with due process. Its rule does not make every affected person an appellate party. Matsushita Electric Industrial Co. v. Epstein preserves the independent requirement of adequate representation; notice, opportunities to object or opt out do not cure its constitutional absence. Those protections remain effective, but neither adequate nor inadequate representation is established by the derivative label or by these institutions' appearance. The claim settled below belongs to the corporation, while Richards involved direct personal claims. Keeping that distinction does not decide whether any particular independent shareholder claim exists or may proceed. The status ruling accordingly leaves the settlement's fairness, compensation terms and any properly presented intervention or due-process question undecided.

**Questions not reached and remedial limits:** The Court orders no reopening, tolling, late intervention, new hearing, discovery or review of a fictional denied motion. It does not independently decide the petition's requested pure-prospectivity question or adopt the Court of Appeals' broader assertion that every possible operation of Chevron Oil was eliminated. Harper's rule against selective prospectivity after application of a federal rule to the Court's litigants remains at its established scope; it creates no unsupported transition entitlement here. No settlement-fairness, fee-merits, collusion, representation-adequacy, personal-claim or collateral-preclusion judgment follows.

## Precedent Treatment
- **Marino v. Ortiz, 484 U.S. 301:** Applied to the distinction between being heard as a settlement objector and properly obtaining party status for appeal. Its separate equally divided collateral-action disposition supplies no general preclusion rule here.
- **Tryforos v. Icarian Development Co., 518 F.2d 1258, Part I and footnote 22:** Its express route for an appearing Rule 23.1 shareholder to appeal a proposed settlement without intervention is rejected to the extent inconsistent with today's rule. Its separate service, notice-of-appeal and fee rulings are not adjudicated or displaced merely by that conclusion.
- **Izumi Seimitsu Kogyo Kabushiki Kaisha v. U.S. Philips Corp. (November 30, 1993):** Its party-status boundary and route to review an actual intervention denial are applied; its different §1254(1), denied-intervention and question-presentation posture is preserved. It is not treated as having already decided the specific Rule 23.1 route presented here.
- **Johnson v. Manhattan Railway Co., 289 U.S. 479, 495–496:** Its invitation/appearance discussion and adjacent intervention analysis are read together in their receivership context. It establishes neither an unqualified modern nonparty-appeal right nor, by itself, a universal formal-motion requirement.
- **Richards v. Jefferson County (June 10, 1996):** Its protection of independent rights against unsupported nonparty preclusion remains intact. It supplies no automatic appellate status and no general duty to intervene to preserve an independent claim.
- **Matsushita Electric Industrial Co. v. Epstein (February 27, 1996):** Its adequate-representation component remains distinct from notice and objection opportunities. No broader collateral-review question is resolved.
- **Harper v. Virginia Department of Taxation (June 18, 1993):** Its applied-rule prohibition on selective prospectivity retains its actual scope. The Court does not adopt a blanket disposition of all prospective rulings or every possible Chevron Oil question.

## Law After Decision
Rule 23.1 continues to protect the opportunity to object to disposition of a derivative action, but an otherwise nonparty shareholder must use the intervention route to obtain the party status required for this settlement appeal. The contrary settlement-appeal proposition in Tryforos no longer governs this setting. Actual-party review and review of a genuine intervention denial remain distinct lawful routes. The decision neither converts notice into constitutionally adequate representation nor weakens the protection of independent rights against unsupported nonparty preclusion. It leaves the settlement undisturbed without deciding its merits or creating a new intervention or collateral proceeding.

## Separate Writings
**Justice Stevens, joined by Justices Souter, Ginsburg and Breyer, dissenting.** The dissent would recognize a confined appellate role for a shareholder who responds to the Rule 23.1 settlement notice, appears, presents objections, and has those objections heard and rejected in the disposition of the corporate claim. The procedural role, rather than mere ownership, financial exposure, receipt of notice or potential binding effect, supplies status to seek review of that disposition. It does not make the objector a plaintiff controlling prosecution or give a nonappearing shareholder a right to appeal.

Tryforos, which Justice Stevens authored as a circuit judge, supplies the direct distinction between objecting to disposition and undertaking prosecution. Its footnote expressly includes proposed settlements, although the actual objection there concerned dismissal with prejudice and the corporation's lack of service. Marino presents a serious contrary example because its objectors were heard too. The dissent distinguishes their adverse personal interests in another action from the Rule 23.1 shareholder's defined role concerning disposition of the represented corporate claim. Izumi involved an undisturbed intervention denial and a different presented question; it does not foreclose deciding that this recognized role supplies limited status. Johnson offers an equity analogy linking invitation, appearance, objection and appeal, but its adjacent intervention discussion and receivership setting prevent treating it as a universal nonintervention rule. Richards protects independent nonparty rights without itself creating appellate status.

The dissent would reverse only the status dismissal and remand for further appellate proceedings subject to ordinary preservation and appeal requirements. It would not invalidate the settlement, find collusion or inadequate representation, permit new claims, promise success on the objections or establish reviewability or entitlement on every fee contention. It proposes no independent prospective-only ruling, reopening, tolling or automatic late intervention. Its limited representative-objector rule does not control the judgment.

## Procedure After Action
The Seventh Circuit's dismissal stands. The settlement remains undisturbed as a consequence of that dismissal, without a Supreme Court determination of its substantive fairness or fee terms. No case is remanded for merits review, and no intervention application or other proceeding is ordered or deemed pending. An appropriate actual-party appeal or review of an actual intervention denial remains governed by its own lawful requirements.

## Event
**Department of the Army v. Blue Fox, Inc., No. 97-1642.** Argued December 1, 1998; decided January 20, 1999. On writ of certiorari to the Ninth Circuit, 121 F.3d 1357, which permitted an equitable-lien claim against the Army. The question is whether APA §702 waives immunity for that remedy. The separately rejected SBA claim is outside review.

The Army obtained no Miller Act payment or performance bond for Verdan's contract. Blue Fox remained unpaid $46,586.14; after notice of the debt the Army paid Verdan $86,132.33. The later $84,910.52 project balance partly financed the $126,772.78 replacement contract. No earned Verdan balance remains withheld. ## Participation
All nine Justices participated at argument and decision.

## Public Action
The Ninth Circuit's judgment permitting the Army lien claim is reversed, and the case is remanded with instructions to dismiss that claim for lack of an applicable waiver of sovereign immunity.

## Judgment & Remedy
**Reversed and remanded, 9–0.**

**Supporting:** Chief Justice Stone-Zsela and Justices Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer.

The Army lien claim must be dismissed. The Court orders no payment, recreated project balance, replacement lien or tracing hearing. The SBA judgment is untouched. | Writing | Author | Joined by | Relationship to judgment | Scope joined |
|---|---|---|---|---|

## Holdings
#### The requested lien supplies substitute compensation without an applicable waiver

**Operative rule.** Section 702 does not waive federal immunity for an equitable lien whose function is substitute payment from Government property for a private debtor's default when no identified retained property belonging to the claimant or specific statutory payment entitlement supports the requested recovery. The equitable form and identification of a project account do not establish consent; specific enforcement of an actual entitlement remains a different question under its own governing law.

**Authority.** Scalia's Opinion of the Court has all nine Justices' direct joins and controls the rule, application and limits.

**Controlling explanation.** Section 702's waiver excludes money damages. Bowen distinguishes substitute compensation from specific relief enforcing an actual entitlement, including a statutory entitlement to payment; it does not classify all monetary relief as damages. Nordic Village and Lane separately require consent to the remedy actually sought. A substantive duty, general jurisdiction or equitable description cannot supply missing consent.

Blue Fox expressly seeks contract funds and argues that its lien attached at notice, making later disbursements ineffective against it. But that attachment is the legal proposition to be established, not an accepted finding that the Army retained Blue Fox's property. No earned Verdan payment remains withheld, and the distinct completion balance is not shown to belong to Blue Fox. The requested recovery would replace Verdan's unpaid debt from Government property. Calling the Army a stakeholder cannot establish both the underlying entitlement and consent to that substitute obligation.

Henningsen and Pearlman concern surety or subrogation rights; Munsey Trust likewise supplies no general direct subcontractor right to recover against the Army free of immunity. The Miller Act's bond protection does not make the Government a guarantor when it fails to obtain the required bond. No bond exists here, and the Government's administration does not itself enact a payment mandate or waiver. That failure is not approved; it simply does not supply consent.

Immunity resolves this claim before independent equitable merits. The Court does not declare every equitable lien money damages or decide a genuine proceeding for claimant property under applicable authority. It also leaves the disputed agency-action, reviewability and adequate-remedy arguments under §§702 and 704, and the related preservation objection, unanswered. The money-damages limitation is sufficient.

## Precedent Treatment
- **Bowen v. Massachusetts, 487 U.S. 879:** Preserved and distinguished. Its specific statutory payment entitlement differs from compensation for this private debt; money alone is not the classification rule.
- **United States v. Nordic Village, Inc.:** Applied to require consent for the particular affirmative recovery and to reject equitable or property labels as substitutes. Genuine identified-property proceedings retain their own facts and authority.
- **Lane v. Peña:** Applied to separate a substantive governmental duty from waiver of immunity for the actual monetary remedy.
- **Henningsen v. United States Fidelity & Guaranty Co., 208 U.S. 404:** Distinguished as a surety/subrogation setting; it supplies no direct Army waiver for this subcontractor.
- **Pearlman v. Reliance Insurance Co., 371 U.S. 132:** Distinguished for its surety/property relationship; it does not establish Blue Fox's entitlement or consent here.
- **United States v. Munsey Trust Co., 332 U.S. 234:** Preserved in its governmental/surety setting; it creates no general right for an unpaid subcontractor to attach Army funds free of immunity.

## Law After Decision
Courts must identify the claimant's actual entitlement and the operation of the requested remedy before treating a monetary demand as specific relief under §702. A private-debt substitute does not become authorized merely through an equitable lien, a named project account, notice or the Government's failure to obtain a required bond. A genuine identified-property claim, enacted payment mandate, properly founded Tucker Act claim, recovery against an actual Miller Act surety or another unequivocal waiver remains governed by its own requirements. None is awarded here.

No separate writings.

## Holdings
#### Compatible federal fraud remedies do not impair the actual state insurance scheme

**Operative rule.** A generally applicable federal law impairs protected state insurance law when its operation contradicts the state law or frustrates an actual state policy or the administration of the state scheme; literal contradiction is not the only possible impairment. An additional private action or greater federal remedy does not itself establish impairment, and the Co-Payors' RICO claim may proceed because it targets fraud Nevada forbids while leaving the State's scheme intact and displacing no demonstrated exclusive remedial arrangement.

**Authority.** Ginsburg's Opinion of the Court has all nine Justices' direct joins and controls the rule, application and reservations.

**Controlling explanation.** The parties do not dispute here that Nevada enacted the relevant laws to regulate the business of insurance or that RICO does not specifically relate to that business. Those predicates frame the impairment question. Fabe protects policy performance and administration necessary to implement it, so the inquiry must examine the actual state policy that federal law would frustrate. Barnett Bank separately addresses an insurance-specific federal exception; RICO has no such subject matter and receives no exemption by that analogy.

The insurers correctly observe that enforcement machinery and remedial choices can form part of protected state policy. A common prohibition of fraud therefore does not by itself establish compatibility. Here, however, Nevada prohibits the alleged deception, and its regulatory authority and remedies are not established as an exclusive arrangement displaced by this Co-Payor action. RICO neither invalidates the state command nor replaces its regulator or defeats an identified policy forbidding this action or remedy. Larger federal damages alone do not establish the missing interference.

The allegations span 1985–1988. The 1987 addition of an express private remedy in Nevada Revised Statutes §686A.310 is not applied retrospectively, and the Court does not decide whether an earlier statutory action was implied. Even assuming no statutory private remedy for earlier conduct, that absence does not itself establish a policy making administrative sanctions exclusive or excluding independent federal relief. The result rests on the actual nonexclusive arrangement, not invented earlier private rights or a comprehensive state-remedy inventory.

A federal claim requiring conduct the State forbids, nullifying a state-approved practice or displacing an exclusive remedial structure established by enacted law would present impairment. No such operation, conflicting allocation of a finite insolvency estate or displacement of an administrative judgment is established here. The Court decides neither hypothetical exclusive schemes nor unrelated insurance conduct, criminal RICO or other federal laws. ERISA, antitrust, the rejected Premium Payor branch and ordinary RICO prerequisites remain unadjudicated in this review.

## Precedent Treatment
- **United States Department of Treasury v. Fabe:** Applied to preserve actual state policy-performance rules and necessary administration against conflicting nonspecific federal law. It does not insulate every insurer-related act or remedy from federal law.
- **Barnett Bank of Marion County, N.A. v. Nelson:** Preserved. Its insurance-specific federal exception remains separate from the interference question and does not extend to RICO by analogy; the actual relationship between the statutes must still be examined.

## Law After Decision
The McCarran-Ferguson inquiry compares the operation of the federal claim with the State's actual protected policy and administration. Neither overlap nor greater federal recovery alone bars the federal claim; actual conflict or frustration can do so even without a literal contradiction. On the relationship established here, the surviving Co-Payor RICO claim complements Nevada's fraud prohibition without displacing an exclusive state mechanism. No retrospective Nevada cause of action or broader RICO entitlement is created.

Rejected Premium Payor claims and the proved-overpayment injury limitation remain unchanged. Further liability and any authorized remedy require ordinary adjudication.

## Event

Lopez v. Monterey County, No. 97-1396. Argued November 2, 1998; decided January 20, 1999. On direct review of the three-judge Northern District of California's December 19, 1997 dismissal after the Court's November 6, 1996 mandate. The questions concern the County's administration of state-mandated judicial-election changes, the reach of the 1983 submission, prospective nonuse and the dependent judicial-tenure order. California defends dismissal; the County supports the appellants.

## Participation

All nine Justices participated at argument and decision.

## Public Action

The dismissal is reversed and the case remanded; the dependent tenure-order disposition is set aside to the extent stated below.

## Judgment & Remedy

**Reversed and remanded, 9–0**, with the dependent tenure-order disposition set aside insofar as based on the rejected exemption, also **9–0**.

**Supporting:** Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer.

## Holdings

**Authority and scope.** Thomas delivers the opinion of the Court. The eight Associate Justices join Parts I–V. Stone-Zsela joins Parts I, II and IV, and Part III only insofar as it rejects delay as authorization for prospective use and rejects the internal-administration characterization of a direct electorate change. He agrees in rejection of the collateral-designation defense and in the dependent tenure-order disposition without adding grounds to those parts. Each controlling part thus has at least eight direct joins. No separate constitutional justification for Section 5 is adopted.

### I. State compulsion does not exempt covered administration

**Holding and operative rule.** A covered political subdivision must obtain Section 5 preclearance before administering a postbaseline voting change within that subdivision, even when a noncovered State mandates the change and leaves no local discretion. The duty attaches to the County's administration; it does not require California to submit its laws in the abstract.

**Controlling explanation.** The Court applies its November 6, 1996 Lopez mandate. That decision expressly assumed complete state displacement of local discretion and nevertheless held that giving the change operative effect is administration. The lower court's renewed reliance on state compulsion cannot reopen the settled question. Neither the 1979 law nor the January 1995 elimination of justice courts supplies an exemption from the County's duty.

Young preserves the difference between the source of a policy and its actual administration, while reserving a different question about wholly federally compelled conduct. Foreman likewise rejects the proposition that an earlier authorization automatically clears subsequent local implementation. California's argument that the County cannot choose a different arrangement confuses authority to enact with authority to administer an unprecleared change. Practical difficulty affects lawful implementation, not whether the completed mandate governs. The Court decides no discriminatory purpose, retrogressive effect or entitlement to substantive preclearance.

### II. The actual submission controls the reach of approval

**Holding and operative rule.** Replacing district electorates with a countywide electorate for judges is a covered change in voting, and a submission does not preclear earlier operative changes it failed to identify. The District Court must determine the actual reach of the 1983 submission as to the final ordinance; its attachment and the Attorney General's nonobjection do not establish blanket approval of the preceding consolidations.

**Controlling explanation.** The prior Lopez decision fixes the direct electoral character of the change and the treatment of the earlier unidentified ordinances. Presley distinguishes internal allocation of governmental authority from changes in who votes for an official; this alteration concerns the electorate itself. Allen and Perkins preserve coverage of such practical electoral changes.

McCain and Clark require identification of what was submitted. Young and Foreman preserve that submission-specific boundary. The 1983 additional-judgeships submission, attached final ordinance and nonobjection are relevant evidence, but they do not establish the operative terms needed to decide conclusively whether the final ordinance itself was approved. That question remains for determination below. The Court does not invalidate an actual approval, review the merits of the Attorney General's decision, or treat an unavailable source as a party's failure to comply.

### III. Delay and a collateral designation challenge do not authorize prospective use

**Holding and operative rule.** Delay does not authorize prospective administration of an operative covered change that lacks preclearance, and the effective Section 4(b) coverage determination cannot be collaterally retried in this Section 5 enforcement action. Statutory bailout remains a distinct route; reliance, completed acts and practical timing may bear on legally available retrospective relief or a lawful transition, without supplying clearance.

**Controlling explanation.** California's remaining defenses must be separated rather than treated as another version of compulsion. Lopez already resolved prospective nonuse and the improper collateral challenge to designation. Clark and Hampton County preserve attention to remedial circumstances without making delay an authorization to use an uncleared practice. The direct change in judicial electorates likewise cannot be recast as merely internal administration under Presley. These applications enforce the existing mandate; they announce no new facial constitutional holding, decide no bailout request and create no general retrospective equitable rule.

### IV. Nonuse and the confined judicial-plan alternative

**Holding and operative rule.** The District Court must prevent prospective use of established operative changes lacking preclearance until approval is obtained through the Attorney General or the District Court for the District of Columbia. A necessary temporary plan wholly formulated by the federal court is distinct only if supported by independent lawful remedial authority, consistent with the Constitution and Voting Rights Act, no more displacing of state law than necessity requires, and limited to the period before a lawful precleared arrangement becomes available.

**Controlling explanation.** Connor distinguishes a genuinely judicial plan from governmental policy, and McDaniel prevents a governmental plan from becoming exempt merely through judicial adoption. Lopez and Young apply that distinction while preserving nonuse. The lawful alternatives include the lawful precleared baseline, completed preclearance, or an independently justified judicial plan meeting the stated limits. The Court selects none here and does not authorize a court to adopt the County's policy as its own.

Clark left open whether extreme circumstances could justify allowing an election to proceed while preclearance remained pending. No such circumstance is established here, and ordinary administrative difficulty supplies no exception. The earlier ordinary six-year at-large elections did not qualify under the prior mandate. Shaw preserves substantive purpose-and-effect determinations for the designated preclearance forums. The enforcement court's task is coverage, actual submission and lawful implementation, not its own substantive approval.

### V. The dependent tenure order requires lawful reconsideration

**Holding and operative rule.** The disposition of the judicial-term-extension order must be set aside insofar as it depends on the rejected exemption and reconsidered under the corrected legal premise and actual circumstances. Reversal does not automatically revive an expired arrangement, extend any judge's tenure, install or remove an officeholder, cancel a completed election or invalidate judicial acts.

**Controlling explanation.** The District Court vacated its September 25, 1996 extension order together with dismissal of the enforcement action. Once the exemption premise fails, that dependent disposition cannot stand on the same ground. Its correction nevertheless requires lawful authority and the actual tenure circumstances; the mandate did not select officeholders or create indefinite holdovers.

Prior preclearance of a four-division option resolves only its preclearance status. It does not settle independent state-law, electorate-and-jurisdiction linkage, population, divided-municipality, residence or remedial-authority questions. Johnson's rejection of a design-only racial injury likewise does not approve all features of a proposed arrangement. The District Court must apply the existing mandate to the actual remaining questions without treating preclearance as complete legal approval. No new submission deadline, compulsory hearing or automatic return to this Court is ordered.

## Precedent Treatment

- **Lopez v. Monterey County, November 6, 1996:** Enforced unchanged as the governing administration, direct-electorate, submission, nonuse and confined-remedy mandate; its state-compulsion question is not reopened.
- **Young v. Fordice, March 31, 1997:** Applied for actual administration, identified submission and the limits on judicial implementation; its wholly federal-compulsion reservation remains distinct.
- **Foreman v. Dallas County, June 27, 1997:** Applied to preserve actual baselines and submission-specific approval; authorization does not automatically approve later implementation.
- **Presley v. Etowah County Commission:** Distinguished because the electorate changes directly; its internal-governance boundary remains.
- **Allen v. State Board of Elections:** Applied to direct changes affecting electoral choice, without deciding discriminatory purpose or effect.
- **Perkins v. Matthews:** Applied to practical electoral operation rather than the label or source of the change.
- **McCain v. Lybrand:** Applied to require identification of the submitted change; related material does not establish blanket approval.
- **Clark v. Roemer:** Applied for specific submission and prospective nonuse; its reserved extreme-circumstance possibility is not established here.
- **NAACP v. Hampton County Election Commission:** Applied to distinguish prospective compliance from properly bounded remedial attention to actual circumstances.
