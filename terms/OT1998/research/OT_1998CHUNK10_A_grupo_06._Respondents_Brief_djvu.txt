STATUTES AND RULES INVOLVED 


Pertinent statutory provisions and rules are reprinted 
in an Appendix to this brief. 
STATEMENT 


The federal district court temporarily enjoined a foreign 
debtor from disposing of its principal asset pending the 
outcome of a civil action brought by investors to collect 
on notes issued by the debtor and guaranteed by its oper- 
ating subsidiaries. The district court made findings of fact 
that the investors were “almost certain” to succeed on the 
merits; that the debtor was “at risk of insolvency if not 
already insolvent”; and that the debtor planned “‘to satisfy 
its Mexican creditors to the exclusion” of the investors. 
Pet. App. 25a-26a. The district court concluded: “In light 
of [the debtor’s] financial condition and dissipation of 
assets, any judgment [the investors] obtain in this action 
will be frustrated” in the absence of a preliminary injunc- 
tion. Pet. App. 26a. 


The foreign debtor, despite admitting personal juris- 
diction, insisted that the district court lacked power to 
enjoin its dissipation of assets and contended that judg- 
ment must be allowed to “follow in the ordinary course.” 
Pet. App. 35a. The district court entered the preliminary 
injunction and the court of appeals affirmed. 


A. GMD’s Default On Guaranteed Notes And Its Dissipa- 
tion Of Assets 


Respondent Alliance Bond Fund, Inc., et al. (the “In- 
vestors”) are investment funds that purchased, out of a 
$250 million offering, more than $75 million in principal 
amount of 8.25% notes due 2001 (“Notes”) sold by 
Petitioner Grupo Mexicano de Desarrollo, S.A. (“GMD”), 
a Mexican conglomerate engaged in the construction busi- 
ness.! The Notes are unconditionally guaranteed by GMD’s

1. The Guaranteed Notes.—Although the Notes are 
unsecured obligations, they rank pari passu in priority of 
payment with all of GMD’s other present and future un- 
secured debt. See C.A. 235-36. The Note instrument pro- 
hibits GMD from incurring any encumbrance or other 
lien on any of its assets, whenever acquired, as: security 
for any indebtedness unless GMD secures the Notes equally 
and ratably with or prior to such indebtedness, with cer- 
tain exceptions (the “Negative Pledge Covenant”). See 
C.A. 128-29. Interest payments on the Notes were due in 
February and August of each year. See C.A. 235. GMD 
used a portion of the proceeds from the Notes to repay 
debts incurred as a result of its participation in the con- 
struction of an inter-city toll road system in Mexico. See 
C.A. 38, 56-58. 


2. The Failure Of The Mexican Toll Road Program 
And GMD’s Default On The Notes.—From 1990 to 1994, 
GMD participated in the Mexican government’s program 
to build an inter-city toll road system. As a result of 
economic uncertainty, currency devaluation, and other 


difficulties, the toll road program was a financial failure. 
See C.A. 236. 


For its part, GMD suffered substantial losses when its 
invoices for work in building the toll roads (the “Toll 
Road Receivables”) were not paid. See C.A. 264. In its 
Annual Report filed with the Securities and Exchange 
Commission for the 1996 fiscal year, GMD reported that 
its liabilities exceeded its assets and its independent audi- 
tors expressed “substantial doubt” that the company could 
continue as a going concern. See C.A. 222-23. 


In August 1997, GMD defaulted on the interest pay- 
ment then due on the Notes and the Guarantors defaulted 
on their obligations. See C.A. 4. Two months after its

default, on October 28, 1997, GMD issued a press release 
in which it reported revenues of approximately $119 mil- 
lion, a net loss of approximately $802 million through the 
third quarter of 1997, and a negative net worth of $214 
million. See C.A. 214. 


3. The Mexican Toll Road Rescue Program.—In re- 
sponse to the precarious financial situation facing the par- 
ticipants in the toll road program—including GMD—the 
Mexican government initiated a Toll Road Rescue Program. 
Under that program, the Mexican government promised 
to issue government-guaranteed notes (“Government 
Notes”) that could be exchanged for Toll Road Receiv- 
ables held by companies such as GMD. In return for the 
Government Notes, the Mexican government would as- 
sume control of the toll roads. 


In October 1997, GMD publicly disclosed that it ex- 
pected to receive at least $309 million in Government 
Notes in exchange for its Toll Road Receivables. See C.A. 
265. According to GMD, the Government Notes are its 
“primary” asset. See C.A. 213, 220; Pet. Br. 2. 


4. GMD’s Assignment Of Government Notes To Mexi- 
can Creditors.—Despite the Negative Pledge Covenant 
and the pari passu status of the Notes, GMD publicly 
reported that it had pledged $117 million in Government 
Notes to satisfy various of its Mexican creditors. See C.A. 
266-67. Because GMD did not possess Government Notes 
at the time of those pledges, it “irrevocably” assigned or 
placed in “trust” Tool Road Receivables that its Mexican 
creditors could later exchange for Government Notes. See 
C.A. 266-67. 


B. The District Court’s Entry Of A Preliminary Injunc- 
tion To Preserve The Status Quo And Its Ability To 
Enter Effectual Relief 


As a result of GMD’s default on the August 1997 
interest payment, the Investors accelerated the principal 
on their Notes. See J.A. 56-66. On December 12, 1997, 
they commenced an action for breach of contract. See 
J.A. 22-32. Because GMD had already pledged some of 


4 


the Government Notes to various of its Mexican creditors, 
the Investors moved for a temporary restraining order and 
a preliminary injunction to enjoin GMD from pledging or 
otherwise transferring its remaining interests in Govern- 
ment Notes, in order to maintain the status quo and pre- 
serve their damages remedy. See Pet. App. 22a-27a; J.A. 
21-32. The Investors also moved for an order that GMD 
deposit the Government Notes, when issued, into a trust 
to be established under Mexican law. See J.A. 31. On 
December 12, 1997, the district court granted the In- 
vestors’ motion for a temporary restraining order. See Pet. 
App. 56a. At the same time, it scheduled a hearing the 
following week on the Investors’ motion for a preliminary 
injunction and their request for a trust. See Pet. App. 
56a-57a. 


1. The Hearings On The Investors’ Motion For A Pre- 
liminary Injunction.—At the December 19, 1997 hearing 
on the preliminary injunction, GMD admitted that it was 
subject to personal jurisdiction in the district court and 
it did not challenge its liability on the Notes. See Pet. 
App. 33a.’ 


What is more, GMD disclosed for the first time that it 
had pledged between $214 and $258 million of Govern- 
ment Notes to its Mexican creditors—a fact that contra- 
dicted GMD’s prior public disclosures and the sworn affi- 
davit of its legal representative to the district court that 
it had pledged only $117 million of Government Notes. 
See C.A. 285-87; Pet. App. 40a. GMD also acknowledged 
its plan to pledge additional Government Notes to its 
Mexican creditors—a plan which would have left only 
$5.5 million of the original $309 million in Government 
Notes to satisfy the $75 million plus interest owed to the 
Investors. See C.A. 10, 228. 


In light of those revelations, the district court asked: 
“why should I delay entry of that judgment simply because

the debtor is going to try to use the process of the court 
to delay entry of a judgment as to which there is no de- 
fense?” Pet. App. 36a. Following the hearing, the district 
court extended the temporary restraining order by consent 
of the parties to December 23, 1997, and asked the parties 
to draft a preliminary injunction to “preserve the. issue 
status quo.” Pet. App. 53a-54a. 


After a hearing on December 23, 1997, the district 
court preliminarily enjoined GMD from further dissipat- 
ing, transferring, or otherwise encumbering its interests in 
the Government Notes. See Pet. App. 26a. The district 
court found that the probability that the Investors would 
succeed on the merits was “almost certain.” Jd. In addi- 
tion, the district court found that the Investors would be 
irreparably harmed absent a preliminary injunction and 
had no adequate remedy at law. See id. In support of 
that finding, the district court found that GMD had “a 
negative net worth of approximately $214 million” and “is 
at risk of insolvency if not already insolvent.” Pet. App. 
25a. The district court further found that GMD planned 
to use the Government Notes, which represented “72 per- 
cent” of its total current assets, “to satisfy its Mexican 
creditors to the exclusion of” the Investors and other hold- 
ers of the Notes and had already encumbered certain Toll 
Road assets. Pet. App. 25a-26a. “In light of [GMD’s] 
financial condition and dissipation of assets,” the district 
court concluded that “any judgment [the Investors] obtain 
in this action will be frustrated” absent a preliminary in- 
junction. Pet. App. 26a. Later that same day, the district 
court set a bond of $50,000 in accordance with Federal 
Rule of Civil Procedure 65(c). See Pet. App. 28a. 


At no time did GMD contend that the district court

SUMMARY OF ARGUMENT 


I. This interlocutory appeal is moot. In the court of 
appeals below, GMD contended that the district court 
lacked power to enter the preliminary injunction restrain- 
ing it from dissipating its assets pending the outcome of 
the litigation. While the interlocutory appeal was pending, 
the district court entered a permanent injunction into 
which the preliminary injunction merged. As a conse- 
quence, the preliminary injunction no longer exists and 
there is no live case or controversy regarding the district 
court’s power to enter it. 

Contrary to GMD’s contention, a claim for damages 
for being wrongfully enjoined does not save an interlocu- 
tory appeal from mootness. In University of Texas v. 
Camenisch, 451 U.S. 390 (1981), this Court held that 
although a damages claim prevents the case from becom-

ing moot, it does not save the interlocutory appeal from 
becoming moot. An interlocutory appeal—which is a nar- 
row exception to the strong federal policy against piece- 
meal review—presents the question whether the district 
court had discretion to enter the preliminary injunction 
and properly exercised that discretion. It does not present 
the question whether the injunction defendant is entitled 
to damages. That question arises, and GMD’s damages 
claim would have accrued, only if (and after) it had 
prevailed on the merits and all subsequent appeals. 


In this case GMD has no damages claim for being 
wrongfully enjoined because it lost on the merits and 
voluntarily abandoned its appeal from the permanent 
injunction. By acquiescing in the district court’s exercise 
of the very power it challenges on this interlocutory appeal, 
GMD forfeited any claim that it was wrongfully enjoined. 
Accordingly, the appeal is moot and the petition for a writ 
of certiorari should be dismissed. 


II. If the Court determines that it may reach the 
merits, it should affirm. The district court had personal 
jurisdiction over the parties and had power to issue a 
preliminary injunction against GMD to restrain it from 
dissipating its principal asset pending the outcome of the 
litigation, in order to preserve the status quo and protect 
its ability to render an effectual judgment. This Court’s 
decisions in Deckert v. Independence Shares Corp., 311 
U.S. 282 (1940), and United States v. First National 
City Bank, 379 U.S. 378 (1965), support the district 
court’s preliminary injunction in principle, and its deci- 
sion in De Beers Consolidated Mines v. United States, 
325 U.S. 212 (1945), is entirely consistent with it. 


Even if the district court’s power to enjoin temporarily 
the dissipation of assets presented a question of first im- 
pression in this Court (which it does not), the order 
granting the preliminary injunction should nonetheless 
be affirmed. This Court has consistently held that federal 
equity power is flexible and adaptable to new situations. 
Although GMD contends that federal equity power is con- 


11 


fined to circumstances in which there exists a “traditional 
predicate” for its use, the cases on which it relies for that 
proposition concern not equitable power, but inherent 
power. The concern posed by inherent power—i.e., the 
lack of democratic sanction—has no resonance with equi- 
table power, which Congress gave the federal courts be- 
ginning with the Judiciary Act of 1789. 


Nor does Federal Rule of Civil Procedure 64, which 
incorporates state prejudgment attachment law, impliedly 
limit federal courts’ power to issue preliminary injunctions 
under Federal Rule of Civil Procedure 65. The equitable 
power of federal courts incorporated in Rule 65 is not 
subject to limitation absent a “clear statement,” which 
Rule 64 plainly is not. Moreover, the issuance of pre- 
liminary injunctions under Rule 65 cannot circumvent 
Rule 64 because the separate origins of the two rules 
demonstrate that they operate independently of each other.

ing that the Rules of Decision Act, 28 U.S.C. § 1652, 
and Erie R.R. Co. v. Tompkins, 304 U.S. 64, require the 
application of New York law—is not properly before this 
Court and is without merit. The Erie issue is neither 
fairly included in the question presented nor pressed or 
passed on by any of the courts below. Even if it were,

entered in this case is consistent with New York law and 
this Court would have no occasion to address the Erie

In any event, federal law governs the issuance of pre-

decision in Hanna v. Plumer, 380 U.S. 460 (1965), state 
law does not govern the issuance of injunctions by federal 
courts because Rule 65 governs that procedure. In the 
alternative, even if federal law conflicted with state law, 
the federal interest in having uniform equitable remedies 
and entering effectual judgments outweighs the state in- 


terest in discouraging forum shopping between state and 
federal courts.

3. De Beers Consolidated Mines v. United States 


Between Deckert and First National City Bank, both of 
which approved of preliminary injunctions to enjoin the 
dissipation of assets, this Court decided De Beers. In 
that case, the Court reversed a preliminary injunction as 
inconsistent with the “principles of equity” (325 U.S. 
at 219), because the injunction restrained the use of 
property that “in no circumstances can be dealt with in 
any final injunction that may be entered” (id. at 220). 


In De Beers, the United States brought an equitable 
action against several foreign corporations and individuals 
in which it sought a permanent injunction prohibiting 
future violations of the antitrust laws. The government 
did not, and could not, sue for money damages. As the 
Court explained (id. at 219-20): 


Under the Sherman Act and the Wilson Tariff Act, 
the District Court has no jurisdiction in this suit to 
enter a money judgment. Its only power is to re- 
strain the future continuance of actions or conduct 
intended to monopolize or restrain commerce. 

Although the final relief sought by the United States 
could not affect the defendants’ property, the government 
nonetheless obtained an ex parte restraining order against 
banks in which the defendants had accounts and that 
order was continued in a preliminary injunction. See id. 


28 


at 216. In support of that relief, the government's coun-

abroad and required payment in advance (see id.) and

the final decree (see id. at 215). As the Court explained, 
“{iJn truth the purpose and effect of the injunction is to 
provide security for performance of a future order which 
may be entered by the court.” Jd. at 219. “Its issue,” 
the Court pointed out, “furthermore presuppose[d] or as- 
sume[d]” an eight-step hypothetical causal chain. /d.

granting of equitable relief” (id. at 218-19) do not au-

The Court concluded that a preliminary injunction may

C. Under This Court’s Decision In Hanna v. Plumer, 
Federal Law Governs The Issuance Of Injunctions

Procedure 


When a Federal Rule of Civil Procedure governs an 
issue, a court sitting in diversity should apply that rule 
even in the face of conflicting state law. See Hanna v. 
Plumer, 380 U.S. at 471. In Hanna, this Court prescribed 
the analysis for determining whether a Federal Rule of 
Civil Procedure should be applied. Erie plays no part in 
this analysis as long as the Federal Rule governs the situa- 
tion. See id. at 470-71. If the Federal Rule governs, this 
Court asks whether the Rule exceeds the authority granted 
by Congress in the Rules Enabling Act (“REA”) or vio- 
lates the Constitution. See id. at 463-64. As long as the 
answer to both questions is “no,” the Federal Rule applies 
regardless of contrary state law. See id. at 473-74.

not possibly “abridge, enlarge, or modify any substantive 
right” within the meaning of the REA.

Federal Rule of Civil Procedure 64 provides as follows: 


Seizure of Person or Property 


At the commencement of and during the course of an 
action, all remedies providing for seizure of person or 
property for the purpose of securing satisfaction of the 
judgment ultimately to be entered in the action are avail- 
able under the circumstances and in the manner provided 
by the law of the state in which the district court is held, 
existing at the time the remedy is sought, subject to the 
following qualifications: (1) any existing statute of the 
United States governs to the extent to which it is appli- 
cable; (2) the action in which any of the foregoing reme- 
dies is used shall be commenced and prosecuted or, if 
removed from a state court, shall be prosecuted after re- 
moval, pursuant to these rules. The remedies thus avail- 
able include arrest, attachment, garnishment, replevin, 
sequestration, and other corresponding or equivalent rem- 
edies, however designated and regardless of whether by 
state procedure the remedy is ancillary to an action or 
must be obtained by an independent action.
