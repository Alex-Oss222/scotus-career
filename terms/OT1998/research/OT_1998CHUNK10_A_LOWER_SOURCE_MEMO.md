## Schleifer v. City of Charlottesville, 159 F.3d 843 (4th Cir. 1998)

### Posture and supported record

The panel heard argument May 5, 1998 and decided October 20, 1998. Wilkinson wrote the opinion joined by Ervin; Michael dissented. This is the historical **lower-court** alignment, not a Supreme Court alignment. The panel affirmed the May 20, 1997 final judgment after trial, which rejected the remaining constitutional claims and denied permanent injunctive relief. The reported preliminary-injunction analysis at 963 F. Supp. 534 was adopted in the final ruling but is not itself the final judgment under review. Plaintiffs dismissed the Fourth Amendment claims at trial. Their equal-protection and due-process allegations were treated as Fourteenth Amendment claims despite the complaint's reference to the Fifth Amendment. See 159 F.3d at 846, 859 and dissent n.2.

The plaintiffs included five minors under seventeen, an eighteen-year-old who wished to associate with younger friends, and two parents. The minors alleged parental permission for lawful activities including late movies, eating out, playing in a band, socializing with older siblings, and returning from Richmond concerts through Charlottesville. These are allegations of desired activities, not findings that every such activity falls outside every exception. Parents claimed interference with their judgment about permitted activities and hours.

The ordinance was enacted December 16, 1996 and took effect March 1, 1997 at 12:01 a.m. It contains no sunset. It requires a City Manager review and report within one year concerning effectiveness and continued need, enforcement problems, costs, and citizens' fairness concerns including age, gender and race data. Do not infer from that reporting requirement that a report actually was made or that the law expired.

### Operative text, exceptions and enforcement

The full text is available in the official appendix, slip pp.20–26 / 159 F.3d at 855–58, and the separate ordinance extraction. These are legally consequential details:

- **Who and when:** an unemancipated person under seventeen, with emancipation defined by a court order under Va. Code §16.1-333. Hours are **12:01 a.m.–5:00 a.m. Monday–Friday** and **1:00 a.m.–5:00 a.m. Saturday–Sunday**. The latter means the early mornings following Friday and Saturday evenings.
- **Locations and conduct:** remaining in or upon a public place, in a motor vehicle operating or parked there, or on the premises of a covered establishment within the city. A public place includes places accessible to the public or a substantial group, with listed examples extending to common areas of apartment houses, schools, shops and other buildings. An establishment is a privately owned, profit-operated business inviting the public. “Remain” covers lingering/staying and failure to leave on request of an officer or person controlling the place. These are alternative definitions; the ordinance does not require a prior dispersal order in every case.
- **Parent definition:** qualifying biological/adoptive parents, including shared legal custody and the parent with whom the child regularly resides; a judicial guardian; and a person eighteen or older standing in loco parentis, shown by authorization from a listed parent/guardian or other circumstances. Thus the accompaniment exception is not limited to a biological parent physically present.
- **Eight exceptions:** (1) parent accompaniment; (2) involvement in an emergency; (3) employment or going to/returning home from it without detour or stop; (4) the sidewalk directly abutting the place where the child resides with a parent; (5) attendance at an adult-supervised activity sponsored by a school, religious/civic/public organization or agency, or similar organization/entity, including travel to/from without detour or stop; (6) an errand at a parent's direction with the specified signed writing in the minor's possession; (7) interstate travel through, beginning in, or ending in Charlottesville; and (8) exercising constitutionally protected First Amendment rights, with free exercise, speech and assembly given as examples.
- **Errand writing:** parent name, signature, address and telephone number; telephone number at which the parent can be reached during the errand; minor's name; brief errand description; destination(s); and authorized hours. General parental permission is not textually a substitute for an errand and the required writing.
- **Emergency:** unforeseen circumstances or their resulting condition requiring immediate action to safeguard life, limb or property; examples include fires, natural disasters, automobile accidents and similar circumstances. The examples are expressly nonexclusive.
- **Related prohibitions:** parents and motor-vehicle owners/operators may not knowingly permit, allow or encourage a subsection (b) violation. Establishment operators/employees may not knowingly permit, allow or encourage a minor to remain after curfew; prompt police notice that the minor is present and refuses to leave supplies a stated defense to this establishment offense. A separate subsection prohibits any person, including a minor, from giving an investigating officer a false name, address or telephone number. Do not transfer the adults' express knowledge requirement to the minor's subsection (b) offense.
- **Investigation and warning:** before enforcement against a minor, the officer must immediately investigate whether the presence violates subsection (b). A first violation receives a verbal warning followed by a written warning mailed to child and parent(s); an earlier warning for any such violation triggers a charge and summons. Warnings need not concern the identical activity involved in a later encounter.
- **Care alternatives:** as soon as practicable the officer must release the child to a parent or place the child in temporary care for no more than the remaining curfew hours. Temporary care must be nonlocked and nonrestrictive, under visual supervision, with no handcuffing or securing to stationary objects. If the child refuses to identify child or parents, no parent can be located before curfew ends, or a located parent fails to accept custody, the child may be taken to a nonsecure crisis center/shelter and/or a judge or juvenile intake officer under the applicable legal procedures. This process follows the violation finding and applies alongside the first-warning route; the ordinance is not merely a warning with no present liberty effect.
- **Others and penalty:** the first violation of subsections (c), (d) or (e) receives verbal and mailed warnings; after an earlier warning, a charge and summons follow. Subsection (h) makes each violation a Class 4 misdemeanor. No maximum fine amount should be invented without the term-effective Virginia code.

### Strongest supported lower-court paths and limits

**Majority path:** minors have constitutional rights but their rights and vulnerabilities can differ from adults'; the panel selected intermediate scrutiny for this curfew and additionally concluded that the ordinance survived strict scrutiny. Its asserted interests were crime reduction, protecting juveniles against victimization and recruitment into crime, and strengthening parental responsibility. It relied on limited age/hours and the eight exceptions. Its parental-right analysis distinguished intimate family custody and educational decisions from a limited overnight presence rule, invoked the State's protective authority, and relied on accompaniment/errand accommodations. A neutral packet must preserve the alternative strict-scrutiny ground, rather than make the judgment stand solely on the selected intermediate standard.

The majority credited public and local evidence of increasing juvenile crime, risk during nighttime hours, a late-night drug trade involving children, and examples from other cities. It recited a 25% increase in the city's juvenile delinquency caseload from 1991 to 1996; juvenile offenses between 11 p.m. and 6 a.m. rising 38% in 1995 and another 10% in 1996; serious-crime arrest data for ages ten to sixteen; and testimony concerning safety and possible curfew effectiveness. Plaintiffs contested the timing of crime, omission of seventeen-year-olds, whether the evidence established effective prevention, and the predominance of harm by acquaintances or family. These statistics are the published court's account of evidence, not independent reanalysis, proof of causation, or proof that every minor in public is dangerous. See 159 F.3d at 847–52.

On vagueness, the majority regarded the First Amendment exception as protecting clearly covered political protest and worship, with marginal cases suited to as-applied litigation. It treated the chief's hesitant hypothetical answers as insufficient proof of arbitrary enforcement and construed “civic” in context and “emergency” through its definition/examples. It did not hold that all conceivable juvenile curfews are valid. See id. at 853–55.

**Dissent path:** minors' fundamental rights receive strict scrutiny, with their particular vulnerabilities and parental relationships bearing on the strength of the asserted interests rather than categorically reducing constitutional review. Michael regarded universal nighttime restraint of law-abiding minors as insufficiently tailored and regarded displacement of fit-parent permission as weakening the asserted parental-support justification. He distinguished genuine emergency curfews and did not equate a state school's delegated custody with unrestricted statewide parental authority. On vagueness, he viewed the uncertain constitutional-rights exception as part of the definition of criminal conduct, stressing protected activity, chilling effects, fair notice and enforceable limits on police discretion. He would allow a facial challenge despite some clear applications. See id. at 859–75.

**Concrete enforcement evidence:** the chief's deposition treated political discussion by two fifteen-year-olds in a coffeehouse as possibly technically prohibited, though enforcement was doubted. He also regarded an unpaid fifteen-year-old musician at a restaurant as possibly in violation and left a decision to officer discretion. The chief separately testified that doubts would be resolved in favor of constitutional activity and legal advice sought. Majority and dissent evaluated the same testimony differently. Do not omit the favorable-to-expression assurance or promote any hypothetical into an actual arrest. See id. at 854, 873–74 & dissent n.14.

**Scope/remedy limits:** withdrawn search-and-seizure claims are not revived; statutory care provisions may still show the curfew's actual operation. No separate finding supplies a universal right of minors to remain out at night or an absolute parental veto over protective laws. A federal court's ability to adopt or rely on a narrowing construction is distinct from a benevolent officer assurance; no binding state narrowing decision was identified. The source does not establish a severability disposition. Any narrower invalidation or prospective injunction should state its precise source and scope rather than assume the entire ordinance survives excision of an exemption.

## Valdespino v. Alamo Heights Independent School District, 168 F.3d 848 (5th Cir. 1999)

### Posture, issue division and demographics

The official substituted opinion is by Edith Jones for a panel with Jerry Smith and Emilio Garza; no separate writing appears. The panel affirmed judgment for the school district. All appellate issues concerned proof of the first Gingles prerequisite. The plaintiffs challenged at-large, by-place, majority-vote elections to a seven-member school board under §2 of the Voting Rights Act. They raised three analytically distinct grounds: the asserted categorical more-than-50% citizen-voting-age requirement, replacement of presumptively accurate census data with updated evidence, and exclusion of the late survey. Slip pp.2–7.

The school district conceded that the plaintiffs' demonstration district had a Hispanic citizen voting-age majority under the **1990 census**. Bill Rives used the census baseline and housing-stock evidence for changed conditions in 1997. A large apartment complex within the proposed district closed, was renovated, and reopened with fewer residents and a changed ethnic mix. New residential development in Lincoln Heights, a former quarry/cement-plant area outside the proposed district, increased the overall school-district population. The proposed district no longer approached one-seventh of total district population.

Rives added a contiguous northern area that had appeared in some earlier plaintiffs' proposals. It reached the district boundary, supplied approximately the needed population, and otherwise would have required connection to another district by a mile-long narrow unpopulated strip. That adjustment yielded **47.9% Hispanic citizen voting-age population**; allowing the proposed district to be underpopulated by **8.1%** yielded **48.3%**. These are percentages of Hispanic voting-age citizens in the demonstration district. They are not Hispanic turnout, registered-voter proportions, vote shares, overall school-district ethnic percentages, or measured crossover rates. Slip pp.3–4.

### Census proof and review

The district court relied on Rives's **April 1997 report**, finding it documented and accurate enough to rebut the census presumption. Rives's **August 1997 report** used updated apartment rental rolls that modestly increased the Hispanic proportion at The Reserve, while correcting an earlier understatement of growth elsewhere; both reports still fell below a Hispanic citizen-voting-age majority. The trial court excluded reliance on the August update to accommodate the plaintiffs' complaint about its lateness. Describing the April report as “wrong” does not by itself identify a change sufficient to restore a majority; the panel examined the context of that admission. Slip pp.4–6, 13–14.

The agreed lower-court proof standard treated census figures as presumptively accurate, rebuttable by thoroughly documented, highly accurate, clear/cogent/convincing evidence of changed conditions. The panel rejected a purported separate requirement to prove that the original census itself was inaccurate. It reviewed the district court's demographic findings for clear error, not de novo. The recognized methodological issues concerned housing-stock estimation in small areas, projected imminent occupancy at apartments in lease-up, and electrical hookup records as evidence of housing growth. The district court could credit those explanations; this was not a universal endorsement of every population projection. Slip pp.11–14.

### Survey chronology, exclusion and preserved alternatives

Rives's general view was known by November 1996. The August report arrived in early August 1997; on August 22 the trial was reset for **Monday, September 15**. Korbel conducted the door-to-door survey during the preceding week in a small area south of the demonstration district. The plaintiffs faxed notice at **4:21 p.m. Friday, September 12**, and data at **4:13 p.m. Saturday, September 13**. They planned to rely on 1990 census data in their case in chief and use the survey only if the defense relied on updated demographics. The court initially barred mention pending the rebuttal-stage ruling; it then excluded the survey and accepted an offer of proof. Slip pp.5–6.

The exclusion rested on unfair surprise under W.D. Tex. Local Rule CV-16(e), which required expert summaries, opinion bases and exhibit identification; supplementation could be allowed for good cause. A deposition statement that Hispanic population existed to the south did not disclose the later survey's basis sufficiently for preparation or cross-examination. The panel also relied on the court's decision to use only the April defense report: a survey supposedly necessitated only by the August novelty was excluded along with that novelty. The court reviewed exclusion for abuse of discretion. Slip pp.14–17.

Keep two unadjudicated issues separate. First, the district court offered a footnote observation that the survey's methodology was too flawed to outweigh census figures, but the school district did not press that ground on appeal and the panel did not decide it. Second, because exclusion for surprise was sufficient, the panel did not decide whether the survey otherwise qualified as proper rebuttal. Its footnote noted a sometimes-used four-factor framework (importance, opposing-party prejudice, possible continuance, explanation for nondisclosure), but neither side addressed that framework on appeal; it was not four newly found facts. Slip pp.15–17 & nn.7–8.

### Competing legal paths supported by this record

**Defense path below:** the conventional first Gingles condition requires a numerical majority in the relevant proposed district; Fifth Circuit law used citizen voting-age population. Every prerequisite must be proved before the totality inquiry. The recognized ability to use other probative demographic evidence changes proof methods, not the numerical threshold. The substituted opinion therefore did not reach cohesion, majority-bloc defeat, or full totality once the first prerequisite failed. Slip pp.7–10.

**Plaintiffs' supported argument:** a nonmechanical approach should focus on electoral potential and ability to elect candidates of choice, coupled with the totality inquiry; census-presumption and disclosure rulings also warrant review. The panel rejected that legal threshold argument, distinguishing single-member packing cases and explaining why cases allowing alternative population proof did not establish a below-majority entitlement. This accurately records the parties' appellate positions but does not settle the simulated Court's governing statutory construction.

**Critical limit:** the opinion does not supply a factual finding that a 47.9% or 48.3% Hispanic population could reliably elect its preferred candidate through white crossover voting. Nor does it determine political cohesion, dependable cross-racial electoral cooperation, or majority-bloc defeat under an alternative framework. A proposed functional threshold cannot turn proximity to fifty percent into proof of those facts. Combining different protected minority groups is also a different issue; this case's asserted population is one Hispanic group. If the simulated Court changes the threshold, any instruction to assess actual electoral ability requires the preserved record or an expressly limited remand; immediate liability cannot be based on invented findings.
