STATUTES AND REGULATIONS INVOLVED 


Section 3(2) of the Americans with Disabilities Act of 
1990 (“ADA”), 42 U.S.C. § 12102 (1994): 


(2) Disability. The term “disability” means, with 
respect to an individual — 


(A) a physical or mental impairment that substantially 
limits one or more of the major life activities of such individ- 
ual; 

(B) a record of such an impairment; or 

(C) being regarded as having such an impairment. 
Section 102(a) of the ADA, 42 U.S.C. § 12112(a) (1994): 


(a) General Rule. No covered entity shall discriminate 
| against a qualified individual with a disability because of the 
disability of such individual in regard to job application 
procedures, the hiring, advancement, or discharge of 
employees, employee compensation, job training, and other 
terms, conditions, and privileges of employment. 


Section 1630.2(j) of the ADA Regulations, 29 C.F.R. 
§ 1630.2G)(1), (2) (1998): 


——ooeOeeeeeeeeeeeeeeeeeeeeeeeeeeeeEe eee 


2 


(j) Substantially limits — 
(1) The term “substantially limits” means: 


(i) Unable to perform a major life activity that the 
average person in the general population can perform; or 


(ii) Significantly restricted as to the condition, 
manner or duration under which an individual can perform a 
particular major life activity as compared to the condition, 
manner, or duration under which the average person in the 
general population can perform that same major life activity. 


(2) The following factors should be considered in deter- 
mining whether an individual is substantially limited in a 
major life activity: 


(i) The nature and severity of the impairment; 


(it) The duration or expected duration of the 
impairment; and 


(iii) The permanent or long term impact, or the 
expected permanent or long term impact of or resulting from 
the impairment. 


Excerpt, EEOC Interpretive Guidance for § 1630.2(j), 29 
C.F.R. pt. 1630, App. § 1630.2(h) & (j), at 347-48 (1998): 


(h) The existence of an impairment is to be determined 
without regard to mitigating measures such as medicines, or 
assistive or prosthetic devices. See Senate Report at 23, 
House Labor Report at 52, House Judiciary Report at 28. For 
example, an individual with epilepsy would be considered to 
have an impairment even if the symptoms of the disorder 
were completely controlled by medicine. Similarly, an indi- 
vidual with hearing loss would be considered to have an 
impairment even if the condition were correctable through the 
use of a hearing aid. 


(j) The determination of whether an individual is sub- 
stantially limited in a major life activity must be made on a 
case by case basis, without regard to mitigating measures 
such as medicines, or assistive or prosthetic devices.

STATEMENT OF THE CASE 


As alleged in their Amended Complaint,' Petitioners 
Karen Sutton and Kimberly Hinton (“Pilots”), identical twin 
sisters, are both experienced commercial airline pilots. J.A. 
17, 4 1. They have received the requisite licenses and medical 
certificates from the Federal Aviation Administration 
(“FAA”) to fly any class of passenger airplane. J.A. 17-20, 


qi 1, 16. 


Pilots’ uncorrected vision is severely impaired. Pilots 
have uncorrected vision of 20/200 or worse in the right eye, 
and 20/400 or worse in the left eye. J.A. 23-24, J 37(a), (b).* 
Without the use of corrective lenses, Pilots cannot conduct 
basic activities such as driving an automobile, watching tele- 
vision, or shopping in a public store. J.A. 24, J 37(d). When 
wearing glasses or contact lenses, however, Pilots’ corrected 
vision is 20/20. J.A. 23, | 36.

is their life-long goal to fly for a major global air carrier. J.A. 
17-19, 4] 1, 13. To that end, in 1992 both applied for pilot

SUMMARY OF ARGUMENT 


The Tenth Circuit's decision should be reversed for two 
independent reasons. Pilots are actually disabled within the 
meaning of the ADA, 42 U.S.C. §§ 12101-12213 (1994), and 
United regarded Pilots as disabled. 


Every available method of statutory interpretation con- 
firms the majority view among the courts of appeals that 
whether an individual is disabled should be determined with- 
out regard to available corrective measures. While the lan- 
guage of the ADA does not directly address this specific 
issue, it does support the majority view. The inability to 
perform a major life activity without the aid of corrective 
medications or devices is a substantial limitation. 


The EEOC has so construed the statute in its Interpretive 
Guidance. Because the EEOC’s construction was announced 
contemporaneously with the statute, at Congress’ express 
instructions, in accordance with the Administrative Procedure 
Act, it is entitled to the highest level of deference under 
Chevron, U.S.A., Inc. v. Natural Resources Defense Council, 
Inc., 467 U.S. 837 (1984). In the alternative, the Commis- 
sion’s view constitutes an interpretation of its own regulations 
and should be followed unless plainly erroneous. 


7 


The legislative history of the ADA speaks directly to the 
corrective measures issue, and demonstrates Congress’ inten- 
tion that corrective measures not be considered. Congress also 
implicitly approved this view by incorporating into the ADA 
language from the Rehabilitation Act that courts had consis- 
tently interpreted to permit a claim without regard to whether 
an impairment is correctable. 


This approach also best effectuates the policies underly- 
ing Title I of the ADA, by protecting from discrimination 
employees with a substantially limiting impairment that does 
not prevent them from performing the work they seek. In 
contrast, the lower court's view would remove from the scope 
of the statute a broad class of persons Congress plainly 
intended to protect, and would embroil courts in dictating to 
disabled persons nationwide what steps they must take to 
attempt to correct their impairments. 


The rule against consideration of corrective measures 
squarely applies to the circumstance of severe but correctable 
myopia. This condition is shared by only a small percentage 
of Americans. There is no principled or practical reason why 
the ADA should protect those who employ hearing aids, 
medication, prosthetic devices, and the like, but subject per- 
sons with severe but correctable myopia to continued discrim- 
ination. 


Even if severe but correctable myopia is not a disability, 
United regarded Pilots as disabled in rejecting them from 
employment based upon a false perception of their abilities. 
Because United refused to consider Pilots for any of its flying 
positions, and because United's pilot positions are indis- 
tinguishable from those of other airlines, United treated Pilots 
as unfit to work within the entire relevant class of jobs.

This Court has instructed that deference to an agency's

Congress has directly addressed the precise question at issue.

the agency's construction is controlling unless “arbitrary, 
capricious, or manifestly contrary to the statute.” Chevron, at 
843-44. Alternatively, if Congress has implicitly left an issue

the Court believes another reading would reflect a more 
natural or common usage of the statutory language. EEOC v. 
Commercial Office Prods. Co., 486 U.S. 107, 115 (1988) 
(citing Oscar Mayer & Co. v. Evans, 441 U.S. 750, 761 
(1979)); Chevron, 467 U.S, at 843 n.11.

(1991). Defining the limits of protections afforded by a reme- 
dial statute such as the ADA “is often more a question of 
policy than of law.” /d. Under the Chevron rule, Congress ts

3. Evaluating Disabilities Without Regard To 
Corrective Measures Best Effectuates The 
Purpose Of The ADA. 


As part of “an ongoing congressional effort to eradicate 
discrimination in the workplace,” McKennon v. Nashville 
Banner Publishing Co., 513 U.S. 352, 357 (1995), Title I of 
the ADA is intended to permit disabled persons to overcome 
employers’ unfounded preconceived notions of their capa- 
bilities. As demonstrated by the present case, including indi- 
viduals with correctable disabilities properly effectuates that 
Statutory purpose. In contrast, the lower court's approach 
would only undermine that purpose and needlessly require 
courts to dictate what treatments, medications, or other mea- 
sures each disabled person must undergo. 


Congress structured the ADA to define broadly who is 
disabled, but limited its remedies only to those who can 
demonstrate they are qualified to perform the work they seek. 
Arline, 480 U.S. at 285. This structure removes arbitrary and 
artificial barriers to employment, see Griggs, 401 U.S. at 431 
(1971), and ensures employees “even-handed treatment,” 
Traynor v. Turnage, 485 U.S. 535, 548 (1988), by requiring 
their abilities to be evaluated on the basis of medical evidence 
rather than mythology. Arline, at 285. That is exactly what 
Pilots seek in this case. Pilots merely request the opportunity 
to prove that they are qualified to perform the job for which 
they have been trained. They allege that they have been 
denied a lifelong employment goal on the basis of physical 
limitations unrelated to legitimate job qualifications. Their 
action illustrates that the majority rule against consideration 
of corrective measures directly advances the ADA’s remedial 
purpose of promoting and expanding employment oppor- 
tunities for those who can overcome significant physical 
impairments. See Darrone, 465 U.S. at 632-34 & n.13 
(emphasizing remedial purpose of Rehabilitation Act, which 
“cannot be read in isolation from its history and purposes”) 
(citations omitted).

4. The Language Of The Statute Supports 
Determination Of Disabilities Without 
Regard To Corrective Measures. 


Thankfully, the language of the ADA, although not 
directly addressing this precise issue, supports the majority 
view. Even if this Court were to disregard Chevron and 
interpret the statute anew, rather than simply confirming that 
the agency construction is a permissible reading of the ADA, 
it should still conclude that the existence of a disability must 


31 


be determined without regard to available corrective mea- 
sures. 


The majority rule reflects a superior construction of the 
statute for three reasons. First, the statute reaches any impair- 
ment that “substantially limits” a major life activity. The term 
“substantially limits” reasonably encompasses the inability to 
perform a major life activity except with the assistance of 
medication or a corrective device. Second, a contrary reading 
of this phrase is inconsistent with other key provisions of the 
ADA. Third, Congress’ failure to expressly limit disabilities 
covered under the ADA to uncorrectable conditions strongly 
counsels against judicial creation of such a restriction. 

The first flaw in the lower court's reasoning is its reading 
of the phrase “substantially limits” as restricting the Act's 
protections only to correctable impairments. This construction 
diverges sharply from this Court's unwillingness to read lim- 
itations into the operative language of the ADA not stated 
explicitly in the text. See Bragdon, 118 S. Ct. at 2205 (argu- 
ment that “major life activities” under the ADA are limited to 
matters of a public, economic, or daily character “founders on 
the statutory language”); Darrone, at 465 U.S. at 632 (reject- 
ing proposed limitation of § 504 to federal aid designed to 
promote employment where “§ 504 itself contains no such 
limitation”). The court below determined that Pilots’ vision is 
substantially limited without corrective measures, but is not 
substantially limited with corrective measures. To then 
emphasize that Pilots can see with corrective lenses begs the 
question. It is impossible to apply the “substantially limits” 
test without first deciding whether to examine the corrected or 
uncorrected state. 

The phrase “substantially limits” is more easily read to 
support the majority rule. As the Court instructed in Bragdon, 
the ADA “addresses substantial limitations on major life 
activities, not utter inabilities.” 118 S. Ct. at 2206. In Brag- 
don, plaintiff's major life activity of reproducing was 
restricted by the serious health risks her condition created. 
Here, Pilots’ major life activity of seeing is restricted in that 
they cannot perform it without the aid of corrective measures. 
The limitation in this case is thus even more direct. Pilots’ 


32 


limitation does not merely affect the wisdom or desirability of 
performing the major life activity, but the physical ability to 
perform it at all. Whereas the Bragdon plaintiff was at least 
physically capable of reproducing, with or without the use of 
corrective measures,*' Pilots essentially cannot see without 
corrective lenses. 


The Bragdon analysis is consistent with the EEOC’s 
definition of “substantially limited” as “significantly 
restricted as to the condition, manner or duration under which 
an individual can perform a particular major life activity.” 29 
C.F.R. § 1630.2(j)(1)(ii) (1998). The Commission reasons that 
a person who “cannot perform major life activities without 
the aid of medication,” is significantly restricted. 29 C.F.R. 
pt. 1630, App. § 1630.2(j), at 348 (1998). This applies to

B. The Rule Against Consideration Of Corrective

to exclude myopia but not other correctable impairments.

those who are regarded as impaired and who, as a

CONCLUSION 


United rejected Pilots’ applications for employment 
because Pilots are disabled or because United regards them as 
disabled. Pilots’ allegations thus state a prima facie case 
under the ADA. Of course, this does not mean they are 
necessarily entitled to relief. United will have the opportunity 
to meet its burden of showing a direct threat to safety, or 
some other defense. See McDonnell Douglas Corp v. Green, 
411 U.S. 792, 802 (1973) (once a prima facie case of discrim- 
ination is established, the burden shifts to the employer to 
demonstrate a nondiscriminatory justification). As it did in 
both Arline and Bragdon, the Court should remand this case 
for consideration of the direct threat issue.
