A. This Interlocutory Appeal Is Moot Because The 
Preliminary Injunction Has Been Converted Into 
A Permanent Injunction And No Longer Exists 


It is black-letter law that an interlocutory appeal from 
an order granting a preliminary injunction is moot if the 
preliminary injunction is made permanent: 


A preliminary injunction, if granted, lasts only until 
a final decree is entered; it is merged in and does not 
survive the entry of a final decree. If, therefore, an 
appeal is taken from an order granting or denying a 
temporary injunction, and before the appeal is heard 
a final decree is entered by the trial court, the appeal 
will be dismissed as moot. More obviously, where an 
appeal is taken from an order granting or denying a 
temporary injunction, and before the appeal is heard 
a final judgment is entered and an appeal is taken 
from the final judgment, the appeal from the inter- 
locutory order will be dismissed.

Damages from being wrongfully enjoined are not pre- 
sented on an interlocutory appeal from an order granting 
a preliminary injunction. An interlocutory appeal from 
an order granting a preliminary injunction is authorized 
by 28 U.S.C. § 1292(a)(1) (1993), which is an excep- 
tion to the general federal policy “against piecemeal 
appeals.” Switzerland Cheese Ass'n v. E. Horne’s Market, 
Inc., 385 U.S. 23, 24 (1966); see also GMD C.A. Br. 4 
(resting interlocutory appeal in this case on § 1292(a)(1)). 
The purpose of § 1292(a)(1) is to permit litigants “to 
effectually challenge interlocutory orders of serious, per- 
haps irreparable consequences.” Baltimore Contractors, 
Inc. v. Bodinger, 348 U.S. 176, 181 (1955). An inter- 
locutory appeal from an order granting a preliminary 
injunction thus presents the narrow question whether the 
district court had the discretion to issue the injunction 
and properly exercised that discretion.5 


By contrast to the narrow question presented on inter- 
locutory appeal from an order granting a preliminary 
injunction, a claim for damages arising out of being wrong- 
fully enjoined necessarily accrues only after the defendant 
prevails at the trial on the merits and all subsequent 
appeals therefrom. Federal Rule of Civil Procedure 65(c)
