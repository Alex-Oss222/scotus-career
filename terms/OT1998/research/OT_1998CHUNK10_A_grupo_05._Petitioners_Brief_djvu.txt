STATEMENT OF THE CASE 


This appeal addresses the question whether a federal 
district court, sitting in diversity, has the inherent equitable 
power to issue a preliminary injunction to preserve assets 
for a potential money judgment when such relief is un- 
available under the law of the state in which the district 
court sits. 


Petitioners, a consortium of Mexican construction com- 
panies (“GMD”), issued $250,000,000 of unsecured notes 
(the “Guaranteed Notes”), approximately $76,000,000 of 
which were purchased by respondents, the plaintiffs below. 
(C.A. 235, 240). GMD defaulted on an interest payment 
on those notes and respondents filed a complaint in the 
district court alleging breach of contract.? (J.A. 23-32). 
Respondents also sought a temporary restraining order 
and a preliminary injunction to restrain GMD from using 
its primary asset—the right to receive approximately 
$309,000,000 of unrelated notes to be issued by the Mexi- 
can Government (the “Toll Road Notes”). (J.A. 30-31). 


In seeking the injunction, respondents argued that injunc- 
tive relief was necessary to ensure that GMD, which had 
already assigned to certain creditors in Mexico its right 
to receive a portion of the Toll Road Notes, did not make 
further assignments that might prefer other creditors over 
respondents and might frustrate enforcement of a potential 
money judgment in respondents’ favor. (J.A. 30-31). 
Respondents also cited the possible insolvency of GMD

A. Background 


GMD, the fourth largest construction company in 
Mexico, is engaged in the construction of a broad range 
of public works and infrastructure projects in Mexico and 
throughout Latin America, including highways and toll 
roads, bridges, tunnels, water works, dams, airports, and 
port facilities. (C.A. 234). For example, GMD has con- 
structed toll roads throughout Mexico and operates the 
water purification and delivery systems for the City of 
Canctin. (C.A. 234). 


The Guaranteed Notes 


In February 1994, GMD issued to institutional invest- 
ors $250 million of Guaranteed Notes due in 2001, for 
general corporate purposes. (C.A. 235). Interest pay- 
ments, at the rate of 8% percent per annum, were sched- 
uled to be made on February 17 and August 17 of each 
year. (C.A. 235). The Guaranteed Notes constitute un- 
secured obligations that rank pari passu in priority of

The Mexican Toll Road Rescue Program 


From approximately 1990 through 1994, GMD partici- 
pated in the Mexican Government’s program to develop a 
modern intercity highway network. (C.A. 236). Under 
this program, the Mexican Government granted conces- 
sions to private companies (the “Toll Road Concession- 
aires”) to build and operate toll roads throughout Mexico. 
(C.A. 236). The Toll Road Concessionaires engaged 
construction companies, such as GMD, to build the roads. 
(C.A. 76-79). GMD and the other construction com- 
panies then billed the Toll Road Concessionaires for that 
construction. (C.A. 76-79). 


Commencing in late 1994, Mexico went through a period 
of severe economic dislocation leading to the devaluation 
of the peso. (C.A. 236). As a result of this economic 
downturn, toll road traffic and revenues fell below an- 
ticipated levels, causing the Toll Road Concessionaires to 
suffer substantial losses. (C.A. 236). These losses, in

companies. (C.A. 67). In an effort to facilitate the con-

terms of the Toll Road Rescue Program, the Mexican 
Government promised to issue government-guaranteed 
Toll Road Notes to GMD and other toll road construction 
companies to reimburse them for approximately one third 
of the unpaid construction receivables and expenses (the 
“Toll Road Receivables”) owed by the Toll Road Con- 
cessionaires. (C.A. 236-37). Certain of the Toll Road 
Receivables are eligible to be paid with Toll Road Notes 
when those notes are issued by the Mexican Government.

B. The Decisions Below 


The district court found that respondents “satisfied their 
burden for the issuance of a preliminary injunction be- 
cause: (1) they would almost certainly succeed on their 
breach of contract claims against GMD; and (2) without 
the injunction they faced an irreparable injury since 
GMD’s financial condition and dissipation of assets would 
frustrate any judgment recovered.” (Pet. App. 6a). The 


7 


preliminary injunction, which was in effect for four months 
(from December 23, 1997 through April 17, 1998), in- 
terfered with GMD’s efforts to restructure its debt and 
substantially impaired GMD’s ability to continue its oper- 
ations in the ordinary course of business. If the decision 
of the court of appeals is reversed, GMD will be entitled 
to damages to the extent that it can show harm resulting 
from the wrongful issuance of the injunction. See, ¢@.g., 
H.E. Fletcher Co. v. Rock of Ages Corp., 326 F.2d 13, 
17-18 (2d Cir. 1963).* 


On appeal, GMD, which has no assets or operations in 
the United States, challenged the district court’s authority 
to issue a prelim.u.ary injunction prohibiting GMD from 
transferring or conveying assets that are unrelated to 
respondents’ underlying claim and are held outside the 
court’s jurisdiction. GMD did not contest the district 
court’s findings of irreparable harm or the likelihood that 
respondents might ultimately prevail on the merits. 


The court of appeals affirmed, holding that a district 
court may exercise its “general equitable power” to 
issue a preliminary injunction “to ensure the preservation 
of an adequate remedy.” (Pet. App. 12a). The court of 
appeals explicitly recognized that other circuits had re- 
jected the notion that a federal district court may exercise 
its inherent powers to enjoin a defendant’s use of assets 
that bear no relationship to the plaintiff's underlying 
claim. (Pet. App. 13a). Since the decision of the court 
of appeals, the Fourth Circuit has joined the Fifth, Tenth, 
and Eleventh Circuits in holding that a preliminary in- 
junction may not issue in these circumstances. See United 
States v. Cohen, 152 F.3d 321 (4th Cir. 1998).

SUMMARY OF ARGUMENT 


The decision of the court of appeals expands the district 
court’s inherent power to issue injunctive relief beyond 
that recognized by this Court or authorized at common 
law. This Court has sanctioned the use of preliminary 
injunctive relief in equitable actions to restrain a defend- 
ant’s use of specific assets directly related to the plaintiff's 
claim in order to preserve those assets for an ultimate 
judgment. This Court has never condoned a district court’s 
issuance of a preliminary injunction to secure a potential 
money judgment. 


The inherent powers of a federal district court are those 
necessary for the performance of its duties and the ad- 
ministration of justice. They are rooted in the common 
law equity tools of the English Courts of Chancery to 
be exercised only in those exceptional circumstances in 
which congressionally authorized powers fail to protect 
the processes of the court. There is, however, no common 
law predicate for the preliminary injunction issued by the 
district court. In fact, the relief granted by the district 
court was expressly unavailable at common law. More- 
over, under the Rules Enabling Act, a mechanism has been 


provided for the district courts to preserve a defendant's 
assets to ensure satisfaction of a potential money judgment. 
That mechanism is encompassed in Rule 64 of the Fed- 
eral Rules of Civil Procedure which directs the district 
courts to rely on state law provisional remedies. Rule 65 
of the Federal Rules of Civil Procedure, by contrast, 
merely dictates the proof requirements for determining 
whether a preliminary injunction may issue in a particular 
case. Absent some other congressional mandate or inher- 
ent equitable power recognized at common law, a district 
court has no authority to issue preliminary injunctive relief. 


But even apart from the broad question of whether a 
federal district court ever may exercise some inherent 
power to enjoin a defendent’s use of assets to secure a 
potential money judgment, the preliminary injunction in 
this case was improper. This is a diversity action, and 
a district court sitting in diversity should be precluded 
from issuing an injunction when such relief is unavailable 
under state law. This Court’s decisions, beginning with 
Erie R.R. Co. v. Tompkins, 304 U.S. 64 (1938), dictate 
that a federal court exercising diversity jurisdiction should 
apply state law unless: (i) a federal statute or Federal 
Rule of Civil Procedure controls the issue before the court 
and that provision represents a valid exercise of Congress’ 
constitutional power or (ii) the application of federal 
judge-made law simply does not implicate the concerns of 
Erie—discouraging forum shopping and ensuring the 
equitable administration of the laws. 

In this case, the district court relied on its inherent 
powers, not a federal statute or Federal Rule of Civil Pro- 
cedure, to issue the preliminary injunction. There is no 


10 


court (and the court of appeals) gave respondents an 
advantage that a New York state court judge could not 


provide. The result is that plaintiffs able to comply with 
28 U.S.C. § 1332 can avail themselves of the more liberal 
provisional remedies available in federal district court. 
Under ths regime, a defendant in complete diversity with 
the plaintiff could be subjected to restraints on its assets 
that could not be imposed on a New York defendant in an 
action brought by a New York plaintiff. 


ARGUMENT 


I. A FEDERAL DISTRICT COURT DOES NOT HAVE 
THE POWER TO ISSUE A PRELIMINARY IN- 
JUNCTION TO RESTRAIN A DEFENDANT'S USE 
OF ASSETS IN ORDER TO PRESERVE FUNDS 
TO SATISFY A POTENTIAL MONEY JUDGMENT 


This Court has never held that a federal district court 
may issue a preliminary injunction to preserve assets to 
satisfy a potential money judgment. This Court has held 
only that a preliminary injunction is available when a 
plaintiff, in an action for equitable relief, seeks an injunc- 
tion with respect to the same res or thing that is the sub-

This Court first addressed the propriety of preliminary 
injunctive relief to preserve assets for a final judgment in 
Deckert v. Independence Shares Corp., 311 U.S. 282 
(1940). In Deckert, purchasers of securities brought suit 
under the federal securities laws to rescind contracts that 
they alleged were induced by fraud. The securities were 
held in a trust administered by a third party trustee. The 
plaintiffs sought and were awarded a preliminary injunc-

disposing of trust assets purchased with funds that the 
plaintiffs alleged they were fraudulently induced to pay. 
Id. at 285. This Court affirmed the issuance of the in-

preserve the status quo pending final determination” of the 
suit because the plaintiffs had shown that there was a 
danger that the defendant would dissipate or deplete the 
assets contained in the trust in anticipation of an adverse 
judgment. /d. at 290. Deckert stands for the proposition 
that a preliminary equitable remedy will issue to preserve a 
final equitable remedy of restitution or constructive trust 
when the plaintiff has an equitable claim to a specific res 
or thing in the possession of the defendant.

Deckert by holding that a preliminary injunction may not

restrained are not of the same “character” as the subject of 
the plaintiff's underlying action. In De Beers, the United

sought in the complaint was a permanent injunction pre-

commencement of the action, the United States sought a 
preliminary injunction restraining the defendants from “sell-

claims. Id. The government predicated its request for in-

A. The Erie Doctrine 


1. The Statutory Framework 


The issue of the application in the federal district courts 
of state or federal law has been the subject of much litiga- 
tion and many decisions by th’. Court. The analysis 
necessarily begins with the source of the federal courts’ 
power. It is axiomatic that the federal courts can neither 
be given nor exercise authority beyond that permitted by 
the Constitution. See, e.g., Sibbach v. Wilson & Co., 312

procedural rules governing litigation in those courts. The 
first Congress exercised this power in enacting the Judi- 
ciary Act of 1789. The current version of section 34 of

The laws of the several states, except where the Con- 
stitution or treaties of the United States or Acts of 
Congress otherwise require or provide, shall be re- 
garded as rules of decision in civil actions in the 
courts of the United States, in cases where they apply.

8. Under Erie, The District Court Should Have 
Relied On New York Law In Determining 
Whether A Preliminary Injunction Could Issue 


Where, as here, there is a conflict between federal judge- 
made law and state law, this Court should analyze the 
conflict with reference to the twin aims of Erie. See, e.g., 
Stewart Org., Inc. v. Ricoh Corp., 487 U.S. 22 (1988). 
If application of the federal judge-made law would pro- 
mote forum shopping or the inequitable application of the 
law, state law should govern. /d. at 27 n.6. This Court’s 
most recent decisions support the application of state law 
to a litigant’s request for preliminary injunctive relief in 
a diversity action. 


In Gasperini v. Center for Humanities, 518 U.S. 415 
(1996), this Court held that a federal district court sit- 
ting in diversity in New York was required to apply the 
New York standard in determining whether a jury 
award of damages was excessive. /d. at 438. C.P.L.R. 
§ 5501(c) dictates that the New York Appellate Division 
“shall determine that an award is excessive or inadequate 
if it deviates materially from what would be reasonable 
compensation.” By contrast, the federal courts tradition- 
ally have authorized remittitur only “if the jury’s verdict 
was so excessive that it ‘shocked the conscience of the 
court.’” Jd. at 422, citing Consorti v. Armstrong World 
Indus., Inc., 72 F.3d 1003, 1012 (2d Cir. 1995). 


Gasperini therefore presented the question whether to 
apply federal judge-made law or New York State law 
under Erie. Although on its face C.P.L.R. § 5501(c), 
which provides the standard of review to be employed 
by the New York Appellate Division in determining 
whether a damages award is excessive or inadequa.., 
is arguably “procedural,” this Court asked whether the 
application of C.P.L.R. § 5501(c) would be outcome de- 
terminative with regard to the twin aims of Erie. Id. at 
428. The Court concluded that “if federal courts ignore 
the . . . New York standard and persist in applying the 


29 


‘shock the conscience’ test to awards on claims 
governed by New York law, ‘substantial variations be- 
tween state and federal [money judgments]’ may be ex- 
pected.” Jd. at 429-30, citing Hanna, 380 U.S. at 467- 
68. The Court held that “[jjust as the Erie principle 
precludes a federal court from giving a state-created claim 
‘longer life . . . than [the claim] would have had in the 
state court,’ so Erie precludes a recovery in federal court 
significantly larger than the recovery that would have 
been tolerated in state court.” Jd. at 430-31, quoting 
Ragan v. Merchants Transfer & Warehouse Co., 337 
U.S. 530, 533-34 (1949). 


In this case, the preliminary injunction issued by the 
district court provided respondents with an advantage that 
could not have been provided by a New York state 
court in identical circumstances. Pursuant to the injunc- 
tion, GMD’s Toll Road Notes could be used only to 
satisfy a money judgment for respondents. Once the in- 
junction was in place, respondents simultaneously moved 
for summary judgment and the Turnover Order. (J.A. 
110-11). If the district court had adhered to New York 
law, however, the preliminary injunction could not have 
issued. GMD would have been able to assign its right to 
receive the Toll Road Notes, GMD’s primary asset, to 
restructure its financial obligations and pay debts owed to 
third parties. As both the district court and the court of 
appeals observed, respondents would have been unable to 
obtain the remedy that they ultimately obtained. Thus, 
as in Gasperini, the district court disregarded state law 
In preserving a damages remedy that could not have been 
preserved under state law. 


If the decision of the court of appeals is permitted to 
stand, both policies underlying Erie would ve tactiuied, 
First, the availability in federal district court of the pre- 
liminary injunction issued in this case undoubtedly would 
promote forum shopping. In any action in which the 
defendant's ability to pay an ultimate judgment is in ques- 
tion, a plaintiff able to comply with 28 U.S.C. § 1332 


30 


will choose to litigate in federal court solely because of 
the availability of preliminary injunctive relief to restrain 
a defendant from dissipating its extraterritorial assets. 
Second, affirmance of the court of appeals’ decision would 
contravene Erie’s goal of ensuring equitable administra- 
tion of the law. A defendant in complete diversity with 
the plaintiff could be subjected to restraints on its assets 
that could not be imposed on a New York defendant in 
an action brought by a New York plaintiff. As this Court 
has held, “the accident of a suit by a nonresident litigant 
in a federal court instead of in a State court a block away 
should not lead to a substantially different result.” York, 
326 U.S. at 108-09. Forum selection should not be predi- 
cated on the nature of the remedies available to secure 
satisfaction of the judgment ultimately to be entered. 


CONCLUSION 
The judgment of the court of appeals should be reversed.
