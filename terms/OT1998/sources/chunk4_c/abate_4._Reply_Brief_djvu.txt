The brief in opposition in this case takes issue with 
virtually none of the points made in the petition. Respondents 
do not deny that the issue presented in the petition is an 
important and recurring one. They do not deny that, in the 
absence of clear guidance from this Court, the lower courts 
are in disarray and have issued conflicting decisions on the 
constitutional _ rules governing consolidated trials. 
Respondents do not deny that many courts fail to offer 
meaningful safeguards against jury prejudice and confusion in 
the setting of consolidated proceedings. And respondents do 
not deny the applicability of the principle that “at times a 
procedure employed by the State involves such a probability 
that prejudice will result that it is deemed inherently lacking 
in due process.” Sheppard v. Maxwell, 384 U.S. 333, 352 
(1966) (citation and internal quotation marks omitted). 


Respondents also offer no reason to doubt that this case 
offers an appropriate vehicle with which to address this 
important question.They do not take issue in any respect 
either with our description of the trial Or with our account of 
the proceedings below. They do not deny that the various 
elements of the trial challenged in the petition are typical of 
the increasingly ambitious (and increasingly common) 
consolidations that are used in mass tort actions. And they do 
not challenge our submissien that the analysis used by the 
Court of Special Appeals is inconsistent with this Court’s 
holdings. '

A. Allowing Plaintiffs’ Counsel To _ Select 
“Tilustrative” Plaintiffs Without Any Involvement 
By The Defense Denies Defendants Due Process 


We showed in the petition (at 3-4, 10) that the method 
used to select the “illustrative” plaintiffs in this case was 
patently unfair: they were chosen by the plaintiffs’ lawyers 
with no participation by the defense, and included two 
atypical plaintiffs who suffered from a very rare and 
invariably fatal disease that did not afflict any of the common 
issues plaintiffs. We also explained (at 14) that the question 
of how to choose illustrative plaintiffs is a recurring one of 
growing importance. Respondents deny none of this. 
Instead, they assert that John Crane received due process 
because “there was no linkage between the verdicts in the 
trial plaintiffs [sic] cases and the other consolidated plaintiffs’ 
claims” (Br. in Opp. 5), and “the trial plan contemplates that 
each individual consolidated plaintiff must prove each and 
every element of his cause of action in a separate trial before 
a jury” (id. at 9). But this contention is wrong. 


First, respondents entirely miss the central point. Our 
principal contention for present purposes is nof that the 
presence of nonrepresentative “illustrative” plaintiffs 
prejudiced the cases of the common issues plaintiffs (although 
that surely also is true). Our actual submission is that 
presenting the cases of the nonrepresentative “illustrative” 


3 


plaintiffs along with those of thousands of common issues 
plaintiffs prejudiced the jury in the cases of the “illustrative” 
plaintiffs themselves. As we explained in the petition (at 11), 
telling the jury that the “illustrative” plaintiffs’ cases raised 
issues typical of those in asbestos suits, while simultaneously 
showing the jury that thousands of additional asbestos cases 
against John Crane are in the litigation pipeline, doubtless led 
jurors to believe that many of the plaintiffs in those cases 
would meet painful and lingering deaths from mesothelioma. 
The inevitable prejudice against the defendant that followed 
from this (erroneous) conclusion necessarily affected the 
outcome in the cases of the “illustrative” plaintiffs. This 
danger also was a concern in Jn re: Chevron U.S.A. Inc, 109 
F.3d 1016 (Sth Cir. 1997), in which the defendant “believed 
that a trial using such a skewed sample would * * * warp the 
jury’s perspective regarding each individual claim.” Faulk, 
Meadows & Colbert, Building a Better Mousetrap? A New 
Approach to Trying Mass Tort Cases, 29 TEX. TECH. L. REV. 
779, 785 (1998). The relationship of the “illustrative” 
verdicts in this case to liability in any future proceeding 
therefore is irrelevant to our argument. Respondents make no 
response at all to this point.?

2. Respondents also contend that the decision below is 
consistent with Malcolm v. National Gypsum Co., 995 F.2d 
346 (2d Cir. 1993), because Malcolm involved a larger 
number of trial plaintiffs and jobsites than does this case. Br. | 
in Opp. 10. We noted in the petition (at 22-24), however, 
that the Jegal principle announced in Malcolm is 
irreconcilable with the holding below: the Second Circuit 
found consolidation to deny due process when “there is an 
unacceptably strong chance” that the proceedings confused the 
jury (995 F.2d at 352), while the Court of Special Appeals in 
this case required a particularized showing that the jury was 
in fact biased or confused — a very different standard. See 
Pet. App. 20a. Respondents make no response at all to this 
point. 


In addition, respondents’ attempt to distinguish Malcolm 
on factual grounds is unavailing. To be sure, more individual 
plaintiffs were consolidated at the outset of the Malcolm trial 
than at the beginning of the trial in this case. But the liability 
trial in Malcolm actually was shorter than the one in this case 
(six months as against seven) (see 995 F.2d at 348); and only 
two trial plaintiffs remained in the case at the time the jury 
rendered its liability verdict in Malcolm (see ibid.), as against 
five who remained in this case. Moreover each of the 
considerations identified by Malcolm as leading to jury 
confusion — variations in workplace, occupation, time of 
exposure, and nature of disease, as well as the death of some 
of the plaintiffs — is present in this case as well. See id. at 
350-351. In fact, what this debate demonstrates most clearly 
is the need for guidance from this Court on the nature of the 
required due process inquiry when consolidation is challenged 
as unduly prejudicial and confusing. 


3. Finally, respondents assert (at Br. in Opp. 11-17) that 
review is inappropriate because John Crane has not 
established jury confusion or prejudice as a matter of fact.

But this contention again misses the point. The petition does 
not seek review of particular factual findings by the Court of 
Special Appeals (although many are highly questionable); 
instead, our submission is that this Court should provide 
guidance on how to determine whether the probability of jury 
confusion and of prejudice to the defendant renders the 
proceeding “inherently lacking in due process.” Sheppard, 
384 U.S. at 352 (citation and internal quotation marks 
omitted). Having said that, however, it may be added that 
respondents’ factual contentions are incorrect. 


First, respondents assert (at Br. in Opp. 12-15) that the 
reversal of many of the jury’s findings does not demonstrate 
jury confusion. We confess to finding respondents’ 
contentions on this point — which are offered without the 
benefit of any citation to the record — a little opaque. To 
offer just one example, the petition (at 23-24 n.20) notes that, 
so far as starting dates of liability are concerned, the jury 
found liability to attach years before many defendants began 
to manufacture the products in question (and, in one case, a 
decade before the defendant manufactured or sold any 
asbestos product). The court below set aside these 
determinations because they were inconsistent with the 
“uncontroverted evidence” (Pet. App. 36a) and announced 
that it “cannot explain” the jury’s reasoning, but speculated 
that “[ijt may well be * * * that the jury simply rejected the 
defendants’ evidence as to when they began manufacturing or 
using particular products.” Jbid. This observation hardly 
inspires confidence that the jury had a terribly clear idea of 
what it was doing. 


Second, respondents contend that the jury could not have 
been confused because it distinguished between John Crane’s 
gasket and packing products, holding John Crane liable only 
for its packing. Br. in Opp. 11-12, 15. In fact, however, 
this point demonstrates the irrationality of the jury’s verdict. 
Respondents assert that a document that was crucial to the 
jury’s determination was an air monitoring report describing

air quality at John Crane’s manufacturing plant (a document 
that, although labeled essential to the jury’s decision by 
respondents, was nowhere cited by the court below in its 135- 
page decision reviewing the verdict). Jd. at 11, 15. This 
same document was introduced into evidence in the Scribner 
case cited by respondents at Br. in Opp. 11. But the Scribner 
jury found John Crane liable for its gaskets — precisely the 
opposite of what the jury determined in this case. Moreover, 
although the jury below found that John Crane’s gaskets were 
not defective, the evidence indicated that the “illustrative” 
plaintiffs (to whom the jury awarded millions of dollars) were 
exposed only to gaskets and not to packing.* We suggest that 
this verdict was the understandable product of jury confusion.

This discussion of the record should not divert attention 
from the disagreement over legal principle that is at the heart 
of this case. Although respondents attempt to justify the 
jury’s verdict, they do not — and could not — deny that the 
jury was subjected to a wealth of confusing and prejudicial 
evidence. They accordingly are candid about the nature of 
their fundamental submission, contending that “practical 
resolution of multiparty litigation requires that the trial courts 
have unfettered discretion in formulating * * * trial plans.” 
Br. in Opp. 16 (emphasis added). The court below took 
essentially the same position. We disagree; in our view, the
