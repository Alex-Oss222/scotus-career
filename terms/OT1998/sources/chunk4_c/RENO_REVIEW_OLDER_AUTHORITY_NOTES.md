## What the older authorities establish

### Younger

The Court refused federal interference with a pending state criminal prosecution when the defendant could present his constitutional claims there and showed no bad faith, harassment or other extraordinary circumstance. Ordinary cost, anxiety and inconvenience of defending one good-faith prosecution are insufficient. Chill alone and possible facial unconstitutionality are also insufficient. Its reasoning combines traditional adequate-remedy limits with federal-state comity; the latter does not transfer unchanged to federal immigration administration.

The Court preserved relief for great and immediate injury that the ordinary defense cannot eliminate, including the supported harassment circumstances of *Dombrowski*. It did not exhaustively define extraordinary circumstances. Stewart's concurrence explained the injury arising from misuse of the very process expected to vindicate rights. Douglas's broader facial-invalidity approach was a dissent, not the rule. The Court left the anti-injunction statute's independent operation unresolved and addressed an already pending state prosecution. Thus *Younger* supports examination of the actual defense and review route, not a universal postponement rule for all enforcement or all constitutional claims.

### Dombrowski

The allegations and offered proof described raids, seized organizational records, invalidated warrants, continued threats, and prosecution used to discourage civil-rights activity without an expectation of valid convictions. Later success defending one prosecution would not necessarily end that continuing injury or resolve threatened enforcement of other provisions. The Court found sufficient irreparable-injury allegations, rejected abstention, held specified provisions facially invalid, and returned remaining relief and claims for further proceedings. It preserved modification of injunctions when circumstances changed and an acceptable limiting construction supplied lawful enforcement grounds with fair warning.

This is not authority that every selective-enforcement allegation warrants an injunction, that every First Amendment claimant must receive immediate review, or that an injunction permanently immunizes conduct. *Younger* expressly confined the broader language about overbreadth and chill. The government can distinguish enforcement supported by a valid substantive ground and a usable later defense; the claimant can identify ongoing coercion or suppression that success on deportability alone would not redress. Whether either description fits the current record remains for neutral modeling from the approved packet.

### Oestereich

The Court allowed pre-induction review when the Board withdrew a concededly satisfied, unequivocal statutory exemption for conduct unrelated to eligibility. It reconciled the review restriction with that explicit substantive mandate and distinguished disputed evidence or discretion in determining eligibility. The remedy still required proof of the allegations and satisfaction of §1331's jurisdictional requirements. The decision does not create a general exception for every allegedly unlawful or retaliatory administrative act.

Harlan's separate concurrence is particularly pertinent to the timing question: an agency unable to decide a substantial attack on its governing procedure could not supply the hearing otherwise used to justify delay, while post-induction habeas or risking prosecution was not meaningfully prompt on the described liberty claim. He nevertheless preserved administrative exhaustion that might produce relief on other grounds and required a strong merits showing for interim protection. Those broader procedural and constitutional propositions are his separate reasoning. Stewart's dissent treated the provision as a timing rule, distinguished substantive entitlement from procedural access, and opposed rewriting clear timing text because it seemed harsh.

### Webster

The Court separated statutory discretionary assessment from colorable constitutional claims, requiring clear congressional intent before reading §102(c) to exclude the latter. Denial of every judicial forum raised a serious constitutional question. It preserved tailored discovery and ordinary equitable limits, and left the actual claims and remedy for further proceedings. It did not hold that every constitutional claim succeeds, that review must occur at the claimant's preferred stage, or that every statutory bar is constitutionally invalid.

O'Connor's contrary view concerned the President's control of sensitive CIA staffing and security information; it was not a categorical rule for all executive action. Scalia rejected a universal judicial-remedy premise and treated traditional nonreviewability, statutory text and practical consequences as material. He insisted that avoidance chooses a permissible statutory construction rather than changes enacted terms. Those separate views cannot displace the Court's rule. Nor does *Webster* decide whether an actually adequate later judicial channel removes the concern about denying every forum.

## Two supported legal paths to test

**Ordinary later review may suffice.** A statutory scheme can channel a constitutional objection through agency proceedings to a competent reviewing court even when the agency cannot finally decide the constitutional issue. The required examination is whether this claim can be considered, the necessary evidence can lawfully be presented or developed, and effective judicial relief remains available. Ordinary litigation burdens and undifferentiated chill are insufficient by themselves. *Thunder Basin* supplies this method at its actual scope; *Younger* offers a limited equitable analogy. Neither permits assuming an unused factfinding mechanism works merely because the Government invokes it.

**Delay or a factual barrier may require earlier access.** A supported ongoing constitutional injury may be distinct from ultimate deportability. If later review cannot examine discriminatory selection, cannot obtain the evidence needed to decide it, or arrives only after the legally relevant injury has become irremediable, nominal availability of a final-order petition does not itself answer the review problem. *Dombrowski* supplies the continuing-injury analogy; *Oestereich* supplies a narrow statutory-reconciliation example and Harlan's separate timing analysis; *Webster* and the public *Catholic Social Services*, *Thunder Basin* and *Kolster* decisions require attention to actual judicial capability. This path must identify a permissible statutory reading and the established defect, not invent a categorical constitutional exception or assume that every preliminary chilling finding proves irreparability.

**Separate questions that remain separate.** Statutory jurisdiction, a serious constitutional doubt supporting permissible construction, the ultimate First Amendment merits, statutory defenses to deportability, entitlement to interim relief, and habeas/collateral jurisdiction are not interchangeable. A substantive statutory defense could avert an order without deciding unconstitutional selection; conversely, the existence of distinct injuries does not establish their merits or the proper jurisdictional channel. Existing injunctions require their own jurisdictional and equitable analysis; these sources create no automatic continuation, dissolution or new protection. No new charge, habeas petition, custody predicate, future factfinding opportunity or remedy is presumed.

## Additional bounded verification: McNary

**Source receipt.** *McNary v. Haitian Refugee Center, Inc.*, 498 U.S. 479 (February 20, 1991), [official report](https://tile.loc.gov/storage-services/service/ll/usrep/usrep498/usrep498479/usrep498479.pdf), was downloaded and mechanically extracted in full to [the complete text](reno_review_mcnary_official.txt). All 26 pages were read, including the Court opinion, every footnote and the Rehnquist dissent joined by Scalia. The browser fetch timed out; the direct official PDF download succeeded. Stevens authored the Court opinion. O'Connor, Kennedy and Souter joined it in full, as did Marshall and Blackmun; White joined Parts I–IV but not Part V. The current-law preservation of *McNary* is verified through the complete public *Catholic Social Services* and *Thunder Basin* holdings cited above.

**Text and relief.** Parts III–IV read §210(e)'s restriction on review of a determination respecting an application, together with its reference to review of such a denial, as covering individual SAW determinations rather than the general challenge to unlawful processing practices. The record-limited review provision reinforced that construction. The Court expressly observed that Congress could have used broader language. Part V distinguished *Ringer*: success would produce reconsideration using lawful procedures, not establish entitlement to SAW status. The jurisdictional holding did not review the merits or form of the lower injunction, which the Government had not presented for review. See pp. 490–495, 499.

**Actual barriers, not agency inability alone.** The administrative record comprised the application, medical report, agricultural-work and residence material, and any interview notes concerning one applicant. The process did not address the systemic procedural and constitutional claims. The lower court had found missing recordings, inadequate opportunities to present witnesses and evidence, and other defects that deprived review of a meaningful record. The pattern-and-practice proof included substantial material irrelevant to any one application. An appellate court ordinarily reviewing that record lacked the district court's record-development capabilities. These concrete defects, rather than merely an agency's inability to decide constitutional law finally, support the meaningful-review analysis. See pp. 493, 496–497.

**Access price.** Judicial review of an individual denial depended on a later exclusion or deportation order. Because the legalization application could not itself be used for enforcement and proceedings might never begin, most unsuccessful applicants could ensure access only by surrendering for deportation. The Court treated that price as practical denial for most undocumented applicants. This is a distinct obstacle from waiting for completion of proceedings already pending; the first must not be silently attributed to the latter. See pp. 484–486, 496–497.

**Competing application here.** The strongest distinction is textual and practical: a challenge directed at an executive act that the governing statute expressly enumerates cannot obtain jurisdiction merely by adopting a systemic or constitutional label; an already pending proceeding also changes the surrender-to-obtain-review problem. The strongest analogy remains independent: that distinction does not establish whether the actual final-order channel can receive selection evidence and decide the substantial constitutional objection, or whether delay itself defeats effective relief. The current packet must supply those facts and statutory capacities. The claim's target, the enacted text and the actual channel must all be confronted.

**Separate view and existing law.** Rehnquist's dissent, joined by Scalia, read the restriction to encompass both decisions and their antecedent procedures, rejected policy relabeling and considered the specified review scheme constitutionally sufficient in the amnesty setting. It questioned whether the administrative appeals, not exhausted there, would fail to provide statutory protection. That separate view is relevant to Scalia's date-eligible position but does not override the Court's holding or the actual later public *Catholic Social Services*, *Thunder Basin* and *Kolster* positions. It is not a finding that every untested immigration channel can develop every constitutional record.
