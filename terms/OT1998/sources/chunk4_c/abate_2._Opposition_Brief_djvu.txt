STATEMENT OF THE CASE 


The present proceeding is the second consolidated to 
asbestos case conducted in the State of Maryland. It was 
conceived as a continuation of the initial asbestos con- 
solidation, AcandS, Inc. v. Godwin, 340 Md. 339, 667 
A.2d 116 (1995). The purpose of this case was to re- 
solve the third party claims which had been severed from 
the Godwin proceedings. The resolution of the third party 
claims would require, as in every asbestos personal injury 
case, a lengthy presentation of state-of-the-art evidence, 
which includes medical articles, depositions and corporate 
documents to prove either actual or implied knowledge of 


2 


the defendants that their product’s use was hazardous in 
order to satisfy the knowledge requirements of negligence 
and strict liability. 


It became apparent that the resolution of the Godwin 
third party claims would represent a small return when 
compared to the cost and expenditure of judicial assets 
required by that trial. The Godwin court recognized that 
the necessity of the presentation of the lengthy state-of-the- 
art evidence in every asbestos case made asbestos cases 
particularly suitable for consolidation. The Godwin court’s 
reasoning was as follows: 


“Issues involving a plaintiff's burden on state-of-the- 
art in an asbestos products liability failure to warn 
case are particularly appropriate for consolidation. 
Absent unusual circumstances, it is senseless to repeat 
the presentation of the same evidence against the 
same defendants in successive, individual trials or 
mini-consolidations. After only a brief introduction 
to asbestos litigation one recognizes that the same 
medical studies, medical journal articles, workers’ 
compensation claims, third-party suits, depositions of 
witnesses, transcripts of court testimony, minutes of 
meetings, correspondence, and other exhibits are pro- 
duced against the same defendants in trial after trial 
throughout the nation. Indeed, the documents have 
been photocopied so many times for an ever-expand- 
ing distribution among members of the plaintiffs and 
defense bars that the copies introduced into evidence 
are nearly illegible.” 


Godwin, 667 A.2d at 146. 


It was determined that judicial economy would be 
served by using the same state-of-the-art evidence required 
to prove the third party claims to also resolve the same 
common issues in approximately 1300 asbestos cases which 
had been filed since the Godwin consolidated proceedings. 
The issues to be resolved in the third party cases and in 
the common issue plaintiff cases were the same; namely, 
whether the defendant manufactured, distributed or in- 


3 


stalled asbestos-containing products, whether the use of 
the product was hazardous, whether the defendant knew 
or should have known of the danger in the use of that 
product, and whether the product contained an adequate 
warning. Issues specific to a plaintiff's case would not be 
considered in the consolidation. These would be resolved 
in the future when groups of plaintiffs cases would be 
tried in mini trials where the jury would determine in each 
Plaintiff's case, whether a particular plaintiff sustained an 
asbestos-related disease, whether that Plaintiff was exposed 
to-a specific defendant’s asbestos product, whether that 
defendant’s product was a substantial contributing factor 
to that disease, and whether the plaintiff suffered damages 
and the amount thereof. 


It was also decided that trial plaintiffs would be utilized, 
as was done in the Godwin consolidation, to give the jury 
a better understanding of the issues involved in an asbestos 
case. Ultimately, five trial plaintiffs cases were tried to 
verdict against six defendants. The issues resolved in the 
trial plaintiff cases were the same as will be decided in 
each consolidated plaintiff's case when they are individu- 
ally tried to final verdict in a mini trial. 


The purpose of this consolidated case was to resolve 
defendant and product specific generic issues applicable to 
every Plaintiff's case in one proceeding, while affording 
each Defendant the due process protection of the opportu- 
nity to present a full defense as to its liability to each 
individual Plaintiff in a jury trial. 


The trial was separated into three phases, the first in- 
volved the trial of the trial plaintiff cases, which also re- 
solved the common issues involving the other 1300 con- 
solidated plaintiff cases. The second phase of the trial 
involved the trial of the Godwin third party claims. The 
state-of-the-art evidence presented in the phase I proceed- 
ing was equally applicable to the second phase. The third 
phase of the trial involved the resolution of punitive dam- 
age issues.

SUMMARY OF ARGUMENT 


A. There Is No Requirement That the Trial Plaintiffs 
Be Statistically Representative of All Consolidated 
Plaintiffs. 


When each of the consolidated plaintiffs cases are in the 
future called to trial, each plaintiff will have the burden 
of proving the elements of his cause of action, including 
the fact that a specific Defendant’s product caused that 
disease. The fact that a trial plaintiff may have proven in 
this case that one or more defendants were liable to him 
for his asbestos disease has no significance to any other 
consolidated plaintiff and will not lessen the burden of 
proof of any consolidated plaintiff in a mini trial. Crane’s 
argument that the selection of the trial plaintiffs must be 
the result of a scientific sampling would have some merit 
if the trial plaintiffs cases were tried for the purpose of 
setting parameters for the settkement amounts of all con- 
solidated Plaintiff cases, or if the verdicts in the trial 
Plaintiffs cases were by some mathematical formula ex- 
trapolated to set damages in all of the cases. The trial of 
the trial plaintiffs cases to full verdict is of no more signifi- 
cance than the result of any mini trial which is to follow. 
Crane’s argument that due process requires that the selec- 
tion of the trial Plaintiffs must be scientific in nature and 
representative of all consolidated plaintiffs, ignores the 
fact that Crane’s liability to any consolidated Plaintiff is 
conditioned upon proof by each consolidated Plaintiff that 
Crane is responsible for his injury. All of the benefits of 
due process are available to Crane under the trial plan, 
and no damages may be awarded to any consolidated 
plaintiff unless his entitlement thereto is fully proven in a 
jury trial at which Crane will have the opportunity to 
present a full defense to each Plaintiff's claim. 


B. The Consolidation Plan Afforded the Defendants a 
Fair Trial. 


Crane’s second contention that it was also deprived of 
its constitutional right to due process because of the com- 


5 


plexity of these consolidated proceedings is also not sup- 
ported by an honest review of the consolidated trial plan 
and the actual conduct of these proceedings. In this case 
five trial plaintiffs were tried to final verdict against six 
trial defendants. The trial of multiple plaintiffs is the 
norm in asbestos personal injury trials in State and Fed- 
eral courts. The issues raised and decided in this trial 
were no different than those required to be proven in any 
asbestos personal injury trial which is tried to full verdict. 
The first phase of this trial, which resolved the common 
issues in the consolidated Plaintiffs cases and also involved 
the determination as to Crane’s liability in the five trial 
plaintiff cases, afforded Crane the unfettered opportunity 
to present all defenses which it wished to raise. In fact, 
the very defense that Crane claims it could not properly 
present was not only presented, but was accepted by the 
jury which agreed with Crane’s assertion that Crane gasket 
products were not defective. A trial involving five plain- 
tiffs being tried to final verdict against six defendants is 
certainly not the complex consolidated proceeding de- 
scribed in Crane’s petition, and did not cause prejudice to 
Crane, and did not constitute a violation of Crane’s right 
to due process. 


REASONS FOR DENYING THE PETITION 


I. THE USE OF TRIAL PLAINTIFFS IN THE PRO- 
CEEDING WAS NOT A VIOLATION OF THE DE- 
FENDANTS’ RIGHT TO DUE PROCESS 


There was no violation of due process in the selection 
of the trial plaintiffs in these proceedings. Crane’s argu- 
ment that there must be statistical certainty in the selec- 
tion of the trial plaintiffs and that they be exactly repre- 
sentative of the common issue plaintiffs ignores the fact 
that there was no linkage between the verdicts in the trial 
plaintiffs cases and the other consolidated plaintiffs’ claims. 
No matter what verdict the jury reached in the trial plain- 
tiff’s cases, the common issue plaintiffs will, in the future, 
present the same issues to another jury in a mini trial. 


6 


In making this argument, Crane uses authority that is 
inapplicable here, since that authority relates to consoli- 
dated cases in which bellwether plaintiff cases are tried 
for the purpose of determining the rights of all consoli- 
dated plaintiffs. In cases utilizing bellwether plaintiffs, the 
obligations of the defendant and the rights of all consoli- 
dated Plaintiffs are determined by the trial of a representa- 
tive sample of the Plaintiffs cases, without the defendant 
having the opportunity to present defenses or to contest 
the other consolidated cases in a trial before a jury. It is 
conceded that there are due process implications when a 
defendant’s liability is established for an entire group of 
plaintiffs by the trial of representative plaintiffs cases 
which are selected as a reflection of the whole. In such 
cases, it is obvious that those results would be skewed if 
a Sstatisticaliy sound basis for the identification of the 
plaintiffs cases to be tried is not utilized. 


Under the trial plan in this case, each and every plaintiff 
will be required to try his case in an individual proceeding 
with the obligation and burden of proving the liability of 
each defendant as in every other civil case. The fact that 
there is no practical or legal significance as to which con- 
solidated plaintiff's case was tried in this consolidated pro- 
ceeding becomes apparent when the court’s trial plan is ex- 
amined. The purpose of this consolidated trial was limited 
in scope. The intent of the trial was to decide generic De- 
fendant and product specific issues which are required to be 
proven by the presentation of lengthy evidence in each indi- 
vidual Plaintiff's case. Since the proof required would be 
the same in every case, judicial economy is served by the 
resolution of these issues in one proceeding. Crane may 
believe that it is in its best interest that the plaintiffs be 
required to present the same lengthy evidence in each of 
the thousands of plaintiff cases which are to follow in order 
to litigate and re-litigate the exact issues that were deter- 
mined in this case, but the court’s refusal to do so is not 
a violation of any due process rights. This procedure will 
expedite the resolution of the mini trials, since only issues 
relating to a specific Plaintiff's cause of action will be 


7 


presented. When the trial court determined that judicial 
economy would be served if additional cases could be 
resolved in these procedings, 1,300 plaintiff cases which 
were filed since the Godwin case were selected for the 
purpose of resolving the same generic issues involved in 
the third party claims. The trial court then determined 
that trial plaintiffs, as in Godwin, would be tried to full 
verdict to give to the jury an understanding of the use of 
asbestos products, the manner that workers were exposed 
to asbestos, and insight into the development of asbestos 
diseases. The role of the trial plaintiffs in this case is a 
far cry from that described in Chevron USA, Inc. v. Peti- 
tioner, 109 F.2d 1016 (1997), which Crane claims as 
comparable to the instant case. Chevron involved thirty 
bellwether claimants whose cases were to be tried on a 
consolidated basis. The verdicts in these thirty cases 
would have a direct effect upon the remaining approxi- 
mate 3,000 claims. The fundamental difference between 
the trial plan in the Chevron proceedings and that found 
here is made apparent by the Fifth Circuit’s description of 
the Chevron plan. 


“On December i9, 1996, the District Court approved 
a trial plan. The trial plan provided for a unitary 
trial on the issues of ‘general liability or causation’ on 
behalf of the remaining plaintiffs, as well as the indi- 
vidual causation and damage issue of the selected 
plaintiff, and ordered the selection of a bellwether 
group of 30 claimants, 15 to be chosen by the plain- 
tiffs, and 15 to be chosen by Chevron. Chevron con- 
tends that the goal of the ‘unitary trial’ was to deter- 
mine its liability, or lack thereof, in a single trial and 
to establish bellwether verdicts to which the remain- 
ing claims could be matched for settlement purposes. 
It is this selection process which Chevron argues will 
not result in a representative group of bellwether 
plaintiffs.” Jd. at 1017. 


Under the circumstances found in Chevron, it is, of 
course, imperative that a statistically valid representative 
sample of the entire plaintiff pool be presented to the jury, 


8 


since the result of the verdicts in the bellwether plaintiff 
cases was intended to set the responsibility of Chevron 
to all consolidated plaintiffs. 


The trial plaintiffs in the present proceeding were not 
bellwether plaintiffs. A bellwether trial is held to answer 
issues common to the universe of the claimants, but the 
common issues resolved in the present case were directly 
the opposite, because they were issues that were common 
to the defendant. In the Godwin consolidation, the de- 
fendants challenged the constitutionality of applying these 
defendant and product specific common issue verdicts to 
each of the consolidated plaintiff cases. The Godwin 
court in rejecting that argument reasoned that even absent 
the consolidation of the common issue plaintiffs cases, 
issue preclusion would foreclose the defendants from re- 
litigating those issues. 


“Although the format of the subject consolidation 
involves 8,555 actions, the special verdicts on the 
Phase I issues produce a result that is not any more 
adverse to the defendants than if a same special 
verdicts had been rendered in the trial of the clairns 
of Leaf, Russell, and McNeal only. In that hypo- 
thetical the same Phase I verdicts when embodied in 
the final judgment, would give rise to issue preclusion 
or offensive, non-mutual, collateral estoppel. In that 
hypothetical any of the remaining 8.549 plaintiffs 
who claimed against one or more of the judgment 
defendants could use the Phase I jury findings that 
are part of the judgment to bar the judgment defend- 
ant from re-litigating the Phase I issues. (Citations 
omitted). The determinations adverse to defendants 
on the Phase I issues are no mc:* a denial of due 
process than is the law of preclus:_n.” 


Godwin, 667 A.2d at 146. 


Under this consolidated trial plan there is no reason to 
require the trial plaintiffs to be representative of the 
entire plaintiff pool. Due process is not implicated in the 


PR wae 


9 


selection of the plaintiffs which cases were tried when the 
trial plan contemplates that each individual consolidated 
plaintiff must prove each and every element of his cause 
of action in a separate trial before a jury. 


II. THE CONSOLIDATED TRIAL PLAN DID NOT 
VIOLATE THE DEFENDANT’S DUE PROCESS

In arguing that this consolidation plan was so complex 
that the defendants were unable to poperly present their 
defenses in a meaningful fashion, Crane compares the 
present consolidated trial to that described in Malcolm y. 
National Gypsum Company, 995 F.2d 346 (2d Cir. 1993). 
That comparison cannot be justified on any rational basis. 
In Malcolm, there were 25 direct defendants, numerous 
third and fourth party defendants and 48 plaintiffs whose 
cases were resolved on all issues. Malcolm involved 250 
jobsites worldwide, while the present case involved only 
five, all located in the Baltimore Metropolitan area.!_ The 
complexity of this case cannot be measured by the num- 
ber of consolidated plaintiffs to which the defendant spe- 
cific common issues were applied, since the proof neces- 
sary for that finding would be exactly the same no matter 
how many plaintiffs cases would be affected. The com- 
plexity of a case such as this is measured by the number 
of plaintiffs whose cases are tried to final verdict. This 
Same argument was raised by the defendants in the God- 
win consolidation and rejected by the Court of Appeals 
of Maryland. 


“The defendants’ principal argument for the un- 
constitutionality of the consolidation is that the num- 
ber of parties, the number of issues, and the volume

The Malcolm consolidation involved 48 selected plain- 
tiffs whose cases were tried to full verdict. In the present 
case, there were only five. The trial of 48 plaintiffs to 
full verdict required the jury to consider 48 specific med- 
ical histories and in each case to sift through the evidence 
for other factors which may have caused the condition, 
such as cigarette consumption, and to weigh and judge 
conflicting medical opinions in each case. In addition, in 
its liability findings concerning these 48 plaintiff cases, the 
jury would be required to consider the 250 sites where 
they worked, and to determine at each site what products 
were used in the Plaintiff's vicinity. The complexity of 
the present case bears no relationship to Malcolm, where 
the jury was literally deluged with evidence in support of 
the proof of 48 plaintiffs’ cases at the same time. 


In the present case, five plaintiffs went to full verdict 
and in proving their exposure to asbestos products of the 
six defendants testified as to only six jobsites. Crane. in 
support of its argument that its due process rights were 
infringed cannot articulate any specific prejudice which the 


11 


proceedings caused. The jury verdicts in this case are 
proof that Crane’s ability to present its defenses was not 
impaired and that the jury was not overwhelmed and 
confused. Crane, along with another manufacturer of 
asbestos gaskets, presented a spirited defense in these pro- 
ceedings that their gasket products were not hazardous 
because they were fully encapsulated and did not produce 
asbestos dust. The jury accepted Crane’s defense and, 
in its verdict, found that Crane’s gasket products were 
not defective. Any suggestion that these proceedings 
were so complex that meaningful defenses were foreclosed 
must be judged in light of the jury’s verdict. 


In its petition, Crane also infers that these proceedings 
were unfair, since, according to Crane, this is the first trial 
in which it has been found liable for asbestos injuries be- 
cause of the use of its products. Crane’s argument in this 
regard ignores the fact that this was the first case in which 
the plaintiffs were aware of and placed into evidence an 
air monitoring report which detailed air quality at Crane’s 
manufacturing plant. That report revealed that unsafe 
levels of asbestos fiber were released into the air during 
a number of manufacturing processes, one of which was 
the cutting of Crane’s sheet packing, which was the same 
operation that the trial plaintiffs in this case performed 
and which they alleged caused their injury. This docu- 
ment directly controverted Crane’s defense that sheet 
packing, when it is cut and manipulated, does not release 
harmful asbestos dust. Since that time, that same docu- 
ment was allowed into evidence in the case of James 
Scribner v. AcandS, Inc., Circuit Court for Baltimore City, 
November, 1998 trial cluster, consolidated case #98/ 
33701 /CX2561, Case No. 95284501. In that case, Crane 
was found liable to a single plaintiff who was awarded a 
total verdict of $5,241,500.00. Crane’s position that it 
lost this case because of the complexity of the proceedings 
and because of a confused jury, is actually explained by 
the fact that the plaintiffs now possess documentation 


ee 


that directly refutes Crane’s defense that its sheetpacking 
products do not release respirable asbestos fibers. 


In an effort to portray the jury in this case as confused 
by the complexity of the proceedings, Crane lists jury 
findings which it argues are incompatible with the evi- 
dence. These same allegations were fully briefed and 
argued by the parties in the Court of Special Appeals of 
Maryland. That Court in considering this argument held 
that: 


“Of all the allegations of jury error, only the Harbi- 
son Walker verdict sheets as to punitive damages and

dence presented at trial and could not be logically 
explained. The errors were corrected by the trial 
court.” 


The AC&S v. Abate, 710 A.2d at 969. 


The jury verdicts which Crane claims are not based 
upon the evidence are actually instances where the jury 
accepted the plaintiffs’ arguments and rejected the 
defendants’. 


A. Starting Dates of Liability 


Plaintiffs argued to the jury that the state-of-the-art evi- 
dence proved that by the 1930’s any manufacturer or 
asbestos products should have known that its asbestos 
products were unreasonably dangerous, and that, as such, 
the defendant should be responsible for any asbestos prod- 
ucts which they manufactured after that date without 
regard as to when a defendant claimed that it first manu- 
factured an asbestos product. It was the plaintiff’s position 
that the state-of-the-art evidence proved a date certain 
beyond which a defendant would be liable if it produced 
any asbestos-containing product. The jury accepted the 
plaintiff's argument and found dates of the beginning of 
liability consistent with the state-of-the-art evidence. The 


13 


Court of Special Appeals subsequently rejected plaintiff's 
theory of liability dates, but the verdicts by this jury in 
accepting plaintiff's theory of the case is certainly not 
evidence of jury confusion. 


B. The Ending Dates of Liability 


Crane also argues jury confusion is shown by the fact 
that the jury in the case of one defendant found that de- 
fendant liable for its product at a time after it had ceased 
to manufacture the asbestos-containing product. This argu- 
ment omits the fact that the jury in rendering this verdict 
apparently accepted the plaintiff's argument that a defend- 
ant has a continuing duty to warn and has a continuing 
responsibility for its defective product after it ceases manu- 
facturing that product. Crane obviously believes that re- 
sponsibility ends upon the date of last manufacture, but 
that difference of opinion does not support an argument 
that this jury was confused or overwhelmed by the 
evidence. 


C. Exposure Verdicts 


Likewise, Crane argues that jury confusion is evidenced 
by the fact that verdicts of the jury concerning trial plain- 
tiff exposures to certain defendants products were over- 
turned. This argument was rejected by the Court of Spe- 
cial Appeals of Maryland which found that those verdicts 
were understandable because of plaintiffs argument to the 
jury that, because of the aerodynamic properties of asbes- 
tos, exposure can occur over great distances and time from 
the point of use. The Maryland Court of Special Appeals 
explained: 


‘In most circumstances, there was evidence that the 
particular appellant’s product was used at the par-

It is apparent that the jury accepted plaintiffs’ argument 
that exposure to a defendant’s product may occur even 
though the plaintiff is not at the exact location of the use 
of the product or there at the exact time the product was 
present. There was a logical basis for the jury’s finding 
that trial plaintiffs were exposed to the defendant’s prod- 
ucts under such circumstance, and there is no evidence 
supporting an allegation that the jury was somehow con- 
fused. 


There is also no rational basis upon which Crane can 
argue that its rights to due process were somehow in-

that categories of products were defective rather than by 
trade names. Crane’s contention ignores the fact that the 
expert testimony in this case was that any product which 
released visible dust in its use was hazardous. No defend-

fense that the use of its product did not cause harmful 
dust. It is significant that the gasket manufacturers were 
relieved of liability concerning those products because of 
that defense. It would be completely redundant for the 
verdict sheet to list each individual product by trade name 
for each defendant when there was no allegation that 
those products did not release asbestos dust. This argu- 
ment also overlooks the fact that each individual plaintiff 
will be required to prove in a mini trial that the use of a 
specific defendant’s product in his vicinity caused respira-

Crane argues that the case was so complex and confused 
that it could not present a defense, but the record shows 
the opposite. Crane defended this action on the basis that 
its products were encapsulated and could not produce 
hazardous dust. The jury apparently accepted Crane’s 
argument that gaskets, which do not have to be cut or 
shaped by the workmen before application, do not cause 
dust and are not defective. Crane has filed this petition 
because it was found liable for its sheetpacking products. 
Its defense concerning sheetpacking was the same, but the 
evidence in this case refuted Crane’s encapsulation de- 
fense. During the course of these proceedings, Plaintiffs 
discovered a document from the Crane files which had not 
been produced in any other asbestos case. This document, 
a dust monitoring survey at the Crane manufacturing plant, 
showed that hazardous levels of asbestos dust were found 
in the plant, and that one of the manufacturing operations 
which was monitored was the cutting of gaskets from 
sheetpacking, the exact operation the plaintiffs in this case 
performed. Crane was found liable for its sheetpacking, 
not because the jury was confused or overwhelmed, but 
because the evidence refuted Crane’s defense. 


The issues presented in this case are no different than 
those found in any asbestos personal injury case tried to 
final verdict. The jury was charged with the resolution of 
five Plaintiff cases, which is not an unusual number of 
cases consolidated in asbestos litigation. When the exag- 
gerations concerning the complexity of this case are put 
aside, the real issue presented in the Petition is whether 
the trial of five consolidated Plaintiffs cases constitutes a 
violation of due process.

It is significant that Crane’s suggestion that this Court 
intervene to consider the constitutionality of the use of 
representative or bellwether plaintiffs in consolidated trials 
is not an issue which is presented by this case. Crane’s 
general allegations concerning the complexity of this case 
also does not support intervention by this Court. The 
consolidated trial plan found in this case reveals a con- 
sidered and practical approach to the resolution of com- 
mon issues which require the same lengthy proof in each 
case, and which can be resolved in a single proceeding 
without prejudice to the parties. Such practical resolutions 
of multiparty litigation requires that the trial courts have 
unfettered discretion in formulating such trial plans. 
Crane’s general allegations concerning the complexity of 
this case does not support intervention by this Court and 
is not an accurate description of proceedings involved. 
Crane is not liable to thousands of plaintiffs because of 
this case. It is liable to only five, and will not be liable 
to any other plaintiff until due process mandated trials 
are held in each plaintiff's case. This is not the mass con- 
solidation described in the Petition, and does not present 
issues which justify consideration by this Court. 


CONCLUSION 


For the reasons given above, the petition should be 
denied.
