Respondents! labor to divine (and wrongly belittle) 
what is “of importance to petitioners” (Resp. Br. at 1). The 
main issue is what is important to this Court. Where a 
GVR order is concerned, Lawrence v. Chater, 516 U.S. 613 
(1996), lays out those factors, and compels a GVR here. 
Respondents fail to show why this limited relief, or, for 
that matter, plenary review, is unwarranted in this case.

B. Because the Ninth Circuit Had No Notice of Act 
131’s Passage, Nor Even an Assurance that it 
Would Become Law, When It Acted on June 23, 
1998, There Is A Sufficient Chance of a New 
Result on Remand. 


Respondents oppose a GVR because the panel “was 
fully apprised of, and presumably considered, the poten- 
tial enactment of Act 131 long before it denied rehearing.” 
Resp. Br. at 11 (emphasis added). The answer is that there 
are lots of “potential” laws that could moot or alter cases; 
but a “potential” law does not count. When the panel 
acted it had no assurance H.B. 3130 would in fact become 
law or notice of Act 131’s enactment. 


On June 21, 1998, 88 days had passed from the initial 
panel decision. The Governor had not acted. He did not 
need to do so until July 8. An April 29 news report stated 
his view as lukewarm at best: “[t]he governor has said he 
is opposed to a Con Con but has also said he would not 
veto the bill.” Pet. App. 241a. Nothing had been heard 
from him since. His appointee, the Attorney General, did 
not notify the panel or Petitioners of any bill signing, and 
does not deny that it would have been impossible to stop 
the panel from acting in the hours between enactment 
and entry of the order on rehearing. No one contends the 
panel knew of Act 131, or the parties’ views of its effects, 
on June 23, 1998. 


The June 23, 1998, order, properly viewed, reflects no 
more than concern for expedition in an election case 
where the clock was ticking on the practical deadline for 
judicial resolution of equitable orders for the November 
election. See Williams v. Rhodes, 393 U.S. 23, 35 (1968). The 


Ninth Circuit had expedited this case (Pet. App. 102a), 
and may well have thought that it was appropriate to 
deny rehearing without waiting further for the Governor, 
so as to permit election-related stay proceedings here to 
be concluded in an orderly way. This is at least a case 
where new events occurred “so shortly before the lower 
court’s decision that the lower court had no ‘opportunity’ 
to [consider] them.” 516 U.S. at 169. The lower court(s) 
should have a full chance to consider the impact of Act 
131. A GVR is proper. 


C. A GVR Would Properly Allow the Panel to 
Revisit Its Liability Ruling In Light of Act 131; 
At a Minimum the Merits Issues Warrant 
Review Here. 


This case is about more than just fee issues — impor- 
tant as they are. The panel ruling conflicts with landmark 
election cases, from United States v. Classic, 313 U.S. 299 
(1941), to Burdick v. Takushi, 504 U.S. 428 (1992). Ignoring 
those conflicts, Respondents conclusorily claim that the 
broad panel ruling “applied the distinction between ‘fun- 
damental unfairness’ and ‘garden variety election irregu- 
larities.” ” Resp. Br. at 15. 


This superficial argument is perhaps the strongest 
reason to grant review. Liability here turns on what “fun- 
damental fairness” means. As Petitioners have shown, the 
panel ruling permitting retroactive application of an 
unexpected supermajority vote-counting rule defies Clas- 
sic’s holding that Americans are entitled “to have their 
votes counted as cast,” not “as retroactively interpreted.” 
It conflicts with Burdick’s holding that voters must have 


the chance “to act in a timely fashion” under election 
laws. Respondents have no, let alone a good, answer to 
this. 


Respondents cannot mask the conflict the panel 
engenders with Roe v. Alabama, 43 F.3d 574 (11th Cir. 
1995), and Griffin v. Burns, 570 F.2d 1065 (1st Cir. 1978). 
They paint Roe as involving “inclusion of improper 
absentee ballots [that] diluted proper absentee and in 
person ballots” (Resp. Br. at 16). But Roe, which agreed 
that inclusion of the at-issue ballots was not “improper” 
under state law, held that the state courts’ application of 
the “substantial compliance” doctrine was unanticipated, 
and, thus, federally void. See 43 F.3d at 577, 578 n.6, 581. 
Likewise, though Respondents describe Griffin as involv- 
ing a refusal to count “proper absentee and shut-in bal- 
lots [that] disenfranchised almost ten percent of the 
voters” (Resp. Br. at 16), at issue there was an authorita- 
tive, but unanticipated (and thus federally void) retroac- 
tive ruling by the Rhode Island Supreme Court that took 
“absence of express reference to party primaries in the 
statute authorizing absentee and shut-in voting as a clear 
indication that such ballots were not legally authorized in 
primaries.” 570 F.2d at 1068 n.4. 


What the Ninth Circuit here strained to, but could 
not, hide, is that Hawaii AFL-CIO v. Yoshina, 935 P.2d 89 
(Haw. 1997), is virtually identical to, if not worse than, 
the retroactive state court rulings in Roe and Griffin: In 
Hawaii, blank ballots “were simply ignored” and ” ‘Yes’ 
votes were counted against ‘no’ votes and the majority 


ruled.” Pet App. 7a. Yoshina radically changed that prac- 
tice. 


Respondents have no real response to this, other than 
to recite the panel's disparaging characterization of those 
returning blank ballots as “don’t care” voters, and their 
attempt to label this view, which conflicts with the view 
of the “right to vote” in Roe, Griffin, and elsewhere, as 
“common sense.” Resp. Br. at 16. Left to stand, the panel 
ruling leaves voters in the Ninth Circuit dramatically, and 
uniquely, unprotected against unexpected retroactive 
state court rulings on issues central to the right to vote. 


Now that the Act 131 vote has occurred, any justifica- 
tion for refusing to disturb the panel’s liability ruling has 
been eliminated. Both sides, as Petitioners show (Pet. at 
23; Supp. Br. at 3), and Respondents confirm (Resp. Br. at 
13 n.6, 17), have new arguments based on Act 131 that, if 
accepted, would very likely affect the panel judgment.® 
The panel should consider them. A GVR order should

D. A GVR Would Give the Panel the Opportunity to 
Consider Petitioners’ Important § 1988 Claims; 
Alternatively, Plenary Review Should be Granted 
to Review the Threshold § 1988 Issues Involved. 


Respondents do not dispute that this Court has long 
recognized that, under 42 U.S.C. § 1988, “ ‘parties may be 
considered to have prevailed when they vindicate rights 

.. without formally obtaining relief.’ ” Maher v. Gagne, 
448 U.S. 133 (1980). Nor do they deny that “whether the 
lawsuit was causally linked to the relief actually 
obtained”® is “a factual determination for the district 
court,”? and, as we contend, “should be resolved on a 
remand.” Pet. at 24 n.19. 


Even so, Respondents ask this Court to deny a 
remand on the § 1988 claim here because “petitioners lost 
across the board in the Ninth Circuit” and “obtained

accepted, would work radical changes in the dynamics of 
litigation under dozens of federal statutes and allied fee- 
shifting laws. First, as legislative relief is not granted “in” 
a lawsuit, Respondents’ position “negate[s] the possibility 
of ever granting attorneys’ fees when Congress [or a state 
legislature] amends or clarifies legislation and thereby 
secures plaintiff's requested [relief].” Paris v. HUD, 988 
F.2d 236, 241 (1st Cir. 1993). Second, under it, if officials 
survive the “tirst round” of appeal, they are free to sign 
mooting legislation, and avoid both further review and the 
payment of fees, irrespective of the strength of the under- 
lying claim, or the reasons for legislative action. By grant- 
ing a “free pass” for positions that survive initial review, 
Respondents’ view would discourage early settlement, a 
central goal of § 1988. It would also enshrine in law the 
disturbing idea that litigation “ends” with an initial panel 
ruling, and a benefit conferred thereafter is automatically 
“gratuitous.” Aside from ignoring FRAP 35 and 40, this 
view ignores the Constitution. Article II states there “shall 
be” “one supreme Court,” and, for better or worse, that 
Court is not composed of three-judge panels that sit in San 
Francisco. The possibility of review in this Court should 
not artificially be eliminated from the calculus for deciding 
whether “catalyst” fees are available. Unless the “catalyst” 
theory of fee recovery is dead, an issue this Court has not 
resolved, and may wish to review here, Petitioners are 
entitled as a matter of Due Process to prosecute their fee 
claim on the merits in the District Court. 


CONCLUSION 


For the reasons above, in the petition, and in the 
supplemental brief, the petition should be granted, the 
judgment vacated, and the case remanded to the Court of 
Appeals for further consideration of Petitioners’ claims 
for equitable relief, attorneys fees, and other costs, in 
light of Act 131, its history, and the vote had on Novem- 
ber 3, 1998, or with such other instructions as the Court 
tinds appropriate. In the alternative, the Court should 
grant plenary review as to the second question presented.
