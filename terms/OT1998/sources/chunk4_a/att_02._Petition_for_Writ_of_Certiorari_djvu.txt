QUESTIONS PRESENTED 


The Telecommunications Act of 1996 was enacted to 
“shift monopoly [local telephone] markets to competition 
as quickly as possible.” To that end, it adopts new federal 
requirements that allow new entrants to use essential 
facilities and services that are controlled by the incumbent 
local monopolists. This case concerns the authority of 
the Federal Communications Commission (“FCC”) over 
these new requirements of the Communications Act, and 
the substantive validity of two regulations that the FCC 
has determined are crucial to opening local markets to 
competition. The questions presented are: 

1. Whether, despite the explicit grants of authority to 
the FCC, the Eighth Circuit correctly held that the RCC 
has no “jurisdiction” either to enforce or to adopt regula- 
tions to implement myriad new federal requirements of 
the Communications Act, that the pricing and some non- 
pricing requirements of the Act are within the exclusive 
jurisdiction of state utility commissions to implement, and 
that the FCC’s authority over the remaining non-pricing 
en is limited to adopting narrow interpretative 


2. Whether, despite the FCC’s undisputed findings that 

the rule is essential to local competition and supported 
by the ordinary meaning of the terms of the Act, the 
Eighth Circuit lawfully invalidated the FCC regulation 
that allows new entrants to obtain and use existing combi- 
nations of network elements and that precludes incumbent 
monopolists from breaking those elements apart in order 
to impose additional costs and service outages on their 
competitors? 
3. Whether, despite its acknowledgment that the rule 
is supported by the Act’s terms, the Eighth Circuit law- 
fully invalidated the FCC’s most-favored-nation rule on 
the basis of its “belief” that the FCC was wrong in hold- 
ing that the rule would better foster negotiations and more 
effectively promote the Act’s goals?

STATEMENT OF THE CASE 


This case arises under the landmark Telecommunica- 
tions Act of 1996 (“1996 Act”), Pub. L. No. 104-104, 
110 Stat. 56 (1996), which was enacted to “shift monop- 
oly [local telephone] markets to competition as quickly

as possible.” * Congress thus adopted new “local com- 
petition” provisions of the Communications Act (47 U.S.C. 
$§ 251-61) that broadly preempt contrary state laws, that

local telephone markets, and that contain strict time limi- 
tations in order to implement the new federal requirements 
rapidly. Petitioners are new entrants in these local mar-

However, in the decisions under review, the Eighth 
Circuit eviscerated these requirements. First, the Eighth 
Circuit made “jurisdictional” rulings that it acknowl- 
edged to be unprecedented, that are contrary to deci- 
sions of other courts of appeals, and that will prevent 
the rapid implementation of the Act in accordance with 
its terms. Although the court recognized that the new 
local competition provisions are federal laws that preempt 
contrary state laws, the Eighth Circuit held that the 
Federal Communications Commission (“FCC”) has no 
“jurisdiction” to enforce or to adopt uniform national 
rules to implement the pricing and many nonpricing re-

ing, the implementation of these federal requirements is 
committed to several hundred separate state utility com- 
mission proceedings, followed only by actions in federal 
district courts. That would mean that the certainty and 
uniform national rules that Congress intended will emerge, 
if at all, only following years or decades of piecemeal 
litigation in federal district courts, courts of appeals, and, 
in some instances, this Court. 


Second, even in those areas where the Eighth Circuit 
upheld the FCC’s jurisdiction to adopt rules, the FCC 


has been prevented from implementing the Act’s require- 
ments. The Eighth Circuit limited the FCC’s authority

1. The Local Telephone Monopolies and the Historic 
State and Federal Regulation. Historically, all telephone 
services in the United States were provided by franchised 
local monopolists, which are known as “incumbent local 
exchange carriers” or “LECs.” The LECs were allowed 
to construct and operate lines (called “local loops”) that 
connect each home and business in their franchised terri- 
tories to switches and other facilities that allow each 
telephone in the area to place calls to, and receive calls 
from, every other telephone. These local telephone services 
are called “local exchange services.” 


In addition, these local facilities are connected to long 
distance (sometimes called interexchange) facilities that 
carry long distance calls between local calling areas. Thus, 
AT&T, Sprint, MCI, and other long distance carriers 
must use LEC networks to originate or terminate their 
long distance services. These are known as “exchange 
access” services. 

Regulation of LEC networks has always been divided 
between state and federal authorities. Section 2(b) of the 
Communications Act (47 U.S.C. § 152(b)) generally 
gives the states exclusive jurisdiction over “intrastate com- 
munications services,” so state commissions have exclu- 


4 


sively regulated the rates for local exchange services (and 
for exchange access for intrastate long distance calls). 


However, when LECs provide the “exchange access” 
services for interstate long distance calls, they are pro- 
viding interstate services, which the FCC has regulated 
under §§ 2(a) and 201-205 of the Communications Act. 
More than 20% of the LECs’ $100 billion in annual 
revenues are from interstate exchange access services. 


Because of the enormous economies of scale that char- 
acterize some or all of the components of local telephone 
networks and the “prohibitive capital investment” that 
would be required to duplicate them, local telephone service 
has historically been found to be a natural monopoly. 
See United States v. Western Elec. Co., 673 F. Supp. 
525, 538 (D.D.C. 1987). Accordingly, the FCC and 
states formerly prohibited competition with LEC services. 


2. The FCC’s and the Federal Courts’ Pre-1996 Efforts 
to Foster Competition with LECs. While LECs formerly 
had monopolies over all telephone services and facilities 
used in their franchised territories, the FCC has exercised 
its jurisdiction over interstate telephone services to open 
many of these services and facilities to competition. These 
efforts led to federal antitrust decrees as well. 


For example, the FCC exercised its jurisdiction over 
interstate services to require the competitive provision of 
telephone sets and other “customer premises equipment” 
that formerly was part of monopoly local telephone serv- 
ice. The FCC ordered LECs to allow the interconnection 
of other vendors’ equipment and to “unbundle” their cus- 
tomer premises equipment from their monopoly telephone 
services by stating separate prices for each and giving 
customers the option of purchasing only service from the 
LEC. The FCC adopted similar requirements of inter- 
connection, unbundling, and cost-based rates to foster 
competition in inside wire and enhanced services. Courts 
of appeals upheld all these measures. See p. 25, nn.8-11, 
infra. 

During this period, the FCC also sought to foster com- 
petition in the provision of interstate long distance services 


5 


by imposing a series of interconnection, unbundling, and 
related duties on LECs (and their long distance affiliates). 
These included requirements that LECs charge just, rea- 
sonable, and nondiscriminatory rates for exchange access 
services, and detailed interconnection, unbundling, and 
resale obligations. 


Because these measures were less than fully effective 
(and applied only to interstate long distance services), the 
Justice Department filed a suit under the Sherman Act 
charging the largest LECs (the Bell Operating Companies 
of the then Bell System) with using their local monopolies 
to impede long distance competition. This suit was settled 
when the Bell System consented to a Decree that (1) pre- 
cluded the Bell Operating Companies from providing 
interstate or intrastate long distance services so long as 
they had local monopolies and (2) required them to pro- 
vide exchange access under nondiscriminatory terms and 
conditions, on an unbundled basis, and at cost-based rates. 
It imposed duties that were fully applicable to intrastate 
as well as interstate long distance services and preempted 
inconsistent state regulation. United States v. American 
Tel. & Tel. Co., 552 F. Supp. 131, 153-160 (D.D.C. 
1982), aff'd sub nom., Maryland v. United States, 460 
U.S. 1003 (1983). A similar decree was entered against 
the nation’s next largest LEC, GTE. United States v. 
GTE, 603 F. Supp. 730, 737-46 (D.D.C. 1984). 


Finally, beginning in the late 1980s, the FCC sought 
to foster competition with the LECs’ local exchange access 
services. It certified other firms to provide competing 
interstate exchange access services under 47 U.S.C. § 214, 
and the FCC ordered LECs to unbundle their exchange 
access services and facilities, to permit competing exchange 
access carriers to interconnect with the LECs at specific 
points, and also to allow these new entrants to “co-locate” 
their facilities (either physically or “virtually”) with those 
of the LEC at FCC-approved rates. But the continuation 
of state law prohibitions on the provision of competitive 
intrastate services meant that these FCC orders created 
only limited opportunity for competition in the provision 


6 


of local telephene service, for it is not economical to pro- 
vide interstate exchange access services to anyone except 
the largest customers unless a firm can also provide intra- 
state exchange services. 


3. The 1996 Act and its Local Competition Provi- 
sions. In the 1996 Act, Congress sought to overcome all 
these obstacles and to establish a regime that would 
permit all the nation’s residential and business customers 
to have choices for local telephone service. Congress did 
so by enacting a new subchapter of the Communications 
Act (47 U.S.C. §§ 251-61) which contains new federal 
requirements designed rapidly to eliminate all legal and 
economic barriers to entry. 


These provisions expressly preempt all state laws, regu- 
lations, or orders that erect, maintain, or authorize legal 
or economic barriers to any form of local services. Section 
253(a) invalidates any state or local requirement that has 
the “effect” of prohibiting “any entity” from “offering 
any intrastate or interstate service,” and § 253(d) author- 
izes the FCC to preempt any state requirement found to 
violate § 253(a). See also § 251(d)(3) (standards for 
FCC to apply in “adopting” or “enforcing” regulations 
that preempt state requirements). And until the FCC 
adopts its “superseding regulations” under the new Act, 
§ 251(g) codifies as FCC regulations the provisions of 
the AT&T and GTE consent decrees that preempt con- 
trary state regulation of intrastate as well as interstate 
services. 

Further, Congress was not content merely to preempt 
such state requirements. In addition, §§ 251 and 252 
impose duties on incumbent LECs to allow new entrants 
to use the LECs’ essential or other facilities to provide 
competing service, and they further contain requirements 
designed rapidly to convert these duties into operational 
interconnection arrangements. 

In particular, in addition to duties that apply to new 
entrants and LECs alike (see § 251(b)), Section 251(c) 
of the Act imposes duties on all incumbent local exchange 
carriers (save those rural LECs who receive exemptions 


7 


under § 251(f)). These duties are designed to allow new 
entrants to provide local telephone service in any one or 
all of three separate ways. 


First, § 251(c)(2) requires LECs to allow new en- 
trants who construct their own facilities to offer com- 
peting “exchange and exchange access services” by “inter- 
connect[ing]” their networks with those of the LECs 
under “rates, terms, and conditions” that are “just, reason- 
able, and nondiscriminatory.” Interconnection is neces- 
sary to allow the new entrant’s customers to place calls 
to and receive calls from the LEC’s customers. 


Second, § 251(c)(3) requires the LEC to provide non- 
discriminatory access to “elements” of the LEC’s network 
on an unbundled basis and under “rates, terms, and condi- 
tions that are just, reasonable, and nondiscriminatory” and 
that comply with the requirements of § 252(d)(1) that 
the rates be based on cost. Because this access is to allow 
new entrants to provide any “telecommunications service,” 
it enables them to provide exchange access as well as 
exchange services. 


Third, § 251(c)(4) requires the LEC to offer each of 
its retail services at “wholesale rates” and to allow new 
entrants competitively to resell the LEC’s retail exchange 
services (but not to offer exchange access). The “whole- 
sale rates” are computed by subtracting from the retail 
rates the costs that the LEC avoids when it operates purely 
as © wholesaler. 


While these duties are subject to the general enforce- 
ment provisions of the Communications Act, the 1996 Act 
also adopts provisions designed te assure the rapid imple- 
mentation of these new duties. In particular, § 251(d)(1) 
required the FCC to adopt regulations to implement 
the requirements of § 251 by August 8, 1996, and 
§ 252 of the Act further establishes a procedure and 
strict time deadline through which competing carriers 
may obtain the arrangements that are required by § 251(b) 
and § 251(c). Section 252(a) provides that new entrants 
may, at any time, request that LECs negotiate and enter 


into agreements that implement these arrangements. Sec- 
tion 252,06) provides for compulsory arbitration of the 
arrangements before state commissions (or the FCC) if 
no agreement is reached within 135 to 160 days, and 
§ 252(e) requires the arbitration to be concluded within 
nine months of the request. §§ 252(b)(1), (b)(4)(C), 
(e)(4) & (e)(5). 


In arbitrating open issues and in establishing condi- 
tions, state utility commissions are required to ensure that 
their decisions “satisfy the requirements of § 251 of the 
Act, including the regulations prescribed by the [FCC] 
pursuant to section 251.” § 252(c)(1). When state com- 
missions make determinations under this section, the Act 
provides that an aggrieved party may bring an action in 
an appropriate federal district court to determine whether 
the arbitrated agreement “meets the requirements of sec- 
tion 251 and this section [252].” § 252(e)(6). Further, 
once an agreement becomes final, Section 252(i) permits 
other competing carriers to obtain individual intercon- 
nection, network elements, or wholesale services for resale

REASONS FOR ISSUING THE WRIT 


The questions presented in this case possess profound 
national importance. The nation’s telephone networks 
affect every home and business in the United States and 
are critical to the national economy. The Telecommuni- 
cations Act of 1996 represents the first comprehensive 
legisiative overhaul of national telecommunications policy 
since the enactment in the 19th Century of the Interstate 
Commerce Act (whose provisions were later codified by 
the Communications Act of 1934). While these Acts 
generally permitted local telephone systems to be operated 
as state-franchised monopolies, the 1996 Act establishes 
a new regulatory model in which today’s local monopolies 
are to be rapidly transformed into competitive businesses. 

To that end, the new local competition provisions of 
the Communications Act impose unprecedented fe eral 
obligations on incumbent local exchange carriers and on 
states to eliminate all economic and legal barriers to local 
competition so as to provide consumers with lower prices, 


12 


few matters that are more important to the nation than 
the effective implementation of this Act. 


However, unless reviewed and reversed by this Court, 
the decisions of the Eighth Circuit will prevent the 1996 
Act from achieving its objectives. The Eighth Circuit’s 
jurisdictional holdings will consign the development of the 
critical national rules that are essential to the Act’s im- 
mediate implementation to years of piecemeal litigation 
in federal courts across the country. It will immeasur- 
ably increase the uncertainty and costs imposed on new 
entrants and the time it will take them to enter, and 
will discourage some potential competitors from enter- 
ing at all. Even more fundamentally, in the narrow 
areas in which the Eighth Circuit upheld the FCC’s juris- 
diction, it invalidated two regulations that critically imple- 
ment the Act’s goals, and those holdings will prevent 
effectuation of the Act’s objective of widespread national 
competition to ithe incumbent monopolies even if the 
FCC’s jurisdiction is restored. Each of these holdings, 
moreover, is contrary to the terms of the Act and to the 
decisions of this Court and other courts of appeals.

infeasibility of constructing alternative telephone networks 
throughout the nation, the only entry vehicle that can 
permit the immediate, nationwide establishment of alter- 
natives to LECs’ exchange and exchange access services 
is for new entrants to obtain from the LECs essential 
components of telephone service at their economic cost and 
to use them to offer competing services. Thus, § 251(c) (3) 
reguires incumbent LECs to offer new entrants nondis- 
criminatory access to “network elements” on an “un- 
bundled” basis on rates, terms, and conditions that

based. See 47 U.S.C. §§ 251(c)(3) & 252(d)(1). This 
is the method of entry which would be used by AT&T, 
MCI, and other carriers who lack ubiquitous local facilities. 


The requirement of nondiscriminatory access was im- 
plemented by the FCC in Rule 51.315(b), which provides 
that “[e}]xcept upon request, an incumbent LEC shall not 
separate requested network elements that the incumbent 
LEC currently combines.” This rule was adopted because 
the FCC had specified that the LECs must offer seven 
components of their networks as “network elements,” and 
each of those components is physically and electronically 
connected to some of the six other elements and is used 
by the LEC in these combinations. For example, each 
“local loop”’—the wires that connect a customer’s home 
or business to the telephone network—is physically con- 
nected to the “network interface device”—a small box at 
the home or business that the loop serves—and those two 
elements are used by the LEC in their combined form. 
Rule 51.315(b)’s prohibition on separating network ele- 
ments implemented Section 251(c)(3)’s requirement that 
access to network elements be afforded on a “nondiscrimi- 
natory” basis: because the LECs use these elements in 
their combined form, their competitors must be afforded 
that same form of access. 


Thus, if a LEC sent a technician to the customer's home 
gratuitously to disconnect physically the loop and the 
network interface device (or any other elements) every 
time a competitor ordered those combined elements, it 
would be engaging in the very discriminatory and anti- 
competitive conduct that the Act is designed to prevent. 
The effect of such action—and its sole purpose—would be 
to force the LEC’s competitor to incur the expense and 
loss of goodwill generated by having to send its own 
technician to reconnect the elements while the competi- 
tor’s customer waits without any telephone service. Thus, 
as the FCC has found, the LEC would be vandalizing its 
network for the sole purpose of “impos[ing] costs on 
competitive carriers that incumbent LECs would not in-

cur,” contrary to “the requirement of § 251(c)(3) that 
incumbent LECs provide nondiscriminatory access to un- 
bundled elements.” Third Order on Reconsideration, 4 44.

willing to switch away from the incumbent if it meant 
suffering service outages of indefinite duration while the

physical connections between the elements. 

These facts were undisputed below. But the Eighth 
Circuit vacated Rule 51.315(b) on the basis of its un- 
explained assertion that “unbundled” must mean “sepa-

network elements “on an unbundled basis” within the 
meaning of § 251(c)(3) unless the LECs separated the 
network elements from one another before ishi 
them. The Eighth Circuit further stated that if the term 
“unbundled” were construed instead to permit new en- 
trants to receive combinations of network elements, it 
“would obliterate the careful distinctions Congress has 
drawn in subsections 251(c) (3) and (4)” between enter- 
ing the market through access to network elements and 
entering through resale of the incumbent LECs’ services. 
App. 71a. The Eighth Circuit did not attempt to explain 
how its ruling could be reconciled with the nondiscrimi- 
nation requirement of § 251(c)(3) or the accepted mean- 
ing of the term “unbundled.” 


Indeed, its holding is squarely contrary to the decisions 
of this Court, which required the Eighth Circuit to defer 
to the FCC’s interpretation of “unbundle” if reasonable. 
Chevron U.S.A., Inc. v. NRDC, 467 U.S. 837, 842-43 
(1984). And the reasonableness of that interpretation is 
decisively established by the fact that every judicial deci- 
sion and other source of which petitioners are aware— 
none of which was even acknowledged by the Eighth 
Circuit—rejects the Eighth Circuit’s interpretation of “un- 
bundled” and supports the FCC’s. They confirm that this 
term does not mean “physically separated.” Rather, to

provide something on an “unbundled” basis is simply to 
state a separate price for it and to give users the option 
of declining to purchase it as part of a package. 


Foremost, the FCC’s interpretation embodies the dic- 
tionary definition. “Unbundle” is uniformly defined as 
“to separate the charges for related products and services 
usually sold as a package,” with telephone services listed 
as an example.* That multiple dictionaries support the 
FCC’s interpretation is itself conclusive of its reasonable- 
ness. See National R.R. Passenger Corp. v. Boston & Me. 
Corp., 503 U.S. 407, 418-19 (1992). Indeed, the FCC 
cited these dictionary definitions in the Notice of Proposed 
Rulemaking that instituted this proceeding (Local Com- 
petition, 11 F.C.C.R. 14171, 14200 ¢ 86 &n.116 (1996)), 
and—with the support of the trade association of incum- 
bent LECs "—then adopted rules that define “unbundled” 
in precisely this way. See also 47 C.F.R. § 51.307(d). 


Further, that was the meaning of the term “unbundled” 
in the 20 years of prior regulatory decisions that had 
promoted competition in other components of telephone 
service and that had been the model for § 251(c)(3). 
In these decisions, the LECs’ duty was to state a separate 
price for the specified components of their networks and 
to give customers the option of declining to obtain the 
component in combination with other LEC facilities or 
services. But when customers decided to continue to 
obtain the component from the LEC as part of a package, 
the LEC was neither required nor permitted to disassemble 
the element from the rest of its network and make the 
consumer incur the inconvenience, delay, and expense of 
recreating the combinations that previously existed.

Section 252(i) requires an incumbent LEC to “make 
available any interconnection, service, or network element 
provided under an agreement . . . to which it is a party 
to any other requesting carrier upon the same terms and 
conditions as those provided in the agreement.” 47 
U.S.C. § 252(i) (emphasis added). This provision im- 
poses a conventional “most-favored-nation” mechanism 
that implements the Act’s nondiscrimination requirement, 
by ensuring that services or elements made available to 
one carrier be made available to other carriers on the 
same terms and conditions. The FCC applied § 252(i) 
in light of its language and self-evident purpose, and con- 
cluded that it required incumbents to make available to 
requesting carriers “any individual interconnection, service, 
or network element arrangement contained in an agree- 
ment . . . upon the same rates, terms, and conditions 
as those provided in the agreement.” See 47 C.F.R. 
§ 51.809(a); App. 261a-268a; 336a. 


The court of appeals vacated this regulation, concluding 
that the Act did not entitle new entrants to obtain in- 
dividual interconnection, service, or network element 
arrangements contained in other agreements, but merely 
entitled them to take an agreement in its entirety. The 
court could so hold only by declining to apply the plain 
language of the Act. Indeed, the words of the FCC rule
