STATEMENT 


On September 24, 1998, the U.S. District Court for the 
Eastern District of Virginia joined the U.S. District Court for 
the District of Columbia in ruling that 13 U.S.C. § 195 bars the 
Census Bureau from using any form of “sampling” to adjust the 
results of the decennial census to make it more accurate -- at 
least to the extent that the results are then used for purposes of 
apportionment of seats in Congress among the states. The 
Virginia court’s opinion, however, includes just a handful of 
pages discussing the merits. In that short section, the court 
pointed to no affirmative indication that Congress -- in enacting 
section 195 in 1957 or amending it in 1976 -- ever sought to 
prevent use of statistical methods to address the persistent 
problem of the undercount. Nor did the court attempt to square

Instead, the court purported to rely solely on the “plain 
language and structure of the Act.” J.S. App. 2la. As we 
discuss here, however, the plain language of the statute simply 
does not point to the conclusion that sampling is barred for 
purposes of apportionment. That is why the D.C. district court 
went to great lengths to deploy various presumptions and 
supposed principles of statutory interpretation in an 
unpersuasive effort to justify rejection of the obvious 
harmonizing reading of the two relevant statutory provisions, 
13 U.S.C. §§ 141(a) and 195. The Virginia court’s solution -- 
to rely on flat assertions about “plain meaning” that lack any 
valid basis in the actual meaning of the words Congress used -- 
is even less persuasive. 


SUMMARY OF ARGUMENT 


The Virginia court’s reading of the “plain language” of the 
statute was flawed in multiple ways. First, it suggested that 
section 141 would not, on its own, authorize use of sampling 
for purposes of apportionment. But there can be no doubt that 
section 141(a) is fairly read as authorizing such a use of 
sampling, as even the D.C. court acknowledged. Second, the 
Virginia court read the “except” clause of section 195 as a flat 
prohibition of use of sampling for apportionment purposes. 
But that is wrong as well. The court was obligated to try to 
harmonize sections 141(a) and 195, and that_is easily 


4 


accomplished if the “except” clause of section 195 is read in 
accordance with what it says -- not as a prohibition but simply 
as an exception from the mandated use of sampling for non- 
apportionment purposes. 


Such a reading is entirely fair. An exception to a mandate, 
like that contained in section 195, is not ordinarily understood 
to create a prohibition of anything. Indeed, this reality is 
illustrated by the two cases cited by the district court -- 
Richardson v. Ramirez, 418 U.S. 24 (1974), and Crosby v. 
United States, 506 U.S. 255 (1993) -- both of which involved 
“except” clauses that themselves prohibi' aothing. Finally, the 
district court’s reliance on the principle that a court should, 
where possible, read a statute so as to give each relevant 
provision some separate significance, was remarkable. It is the 
court’s own proposed reading that renders the reference to 


sampling in section 141(a) utterly superfluous. 
ARGUMENT 


The reasoning offered by the Virginia district court to 
support its decision to enjoin the Census Bureau from using 
statistical sampling for apportionment purposes in the 2000 
Census is even less persuasive than that contained in the earlier 
ruling of the D.C. district court. Like the D.C. court, the 
Virginia court held that section 195 of the Census Act, 13 
U.S.C. § 195, precludes the Census Bureau from using 
sampling for that purpose and thus trumps the apparent 
authorization of sampling in the decennial census for all 
purposes contained in section 141(a) of the Act, 13 U.S.C. 
§ 141(a). In so doing, the Virginia court, like the D.C. court, 
avoided the obvious possibility of applying both sections 
harmoniously -- .e., interpreting section 141(a) as authorizing 
sampling for all purposes and section 195 as requiring 


5 


sampling, wherever feasible, outside the apportionment 
context. But as explained in our earlier brief in No. 98-404, the 
law plainly requires a court to explore and adopt such a 
harmonizing reading, rather than assume that Congress (1) 
decided to create an exception to an ostensibly universally 
applicable provision and then (2) did so, not by including an 
exception in that section, but by enacting a separate section that 
makes no reference to the first. See Br. of Intervenors Gephardt 
et al. at 16-23, in No. 98-404. 


The district court began its statutory analysis by suggesting 
that section 141(a), read by itself, does not authorize the use of 
the Secretary to use “sampling procedures” in the “decennial 
census of population” -- the primary (and sole constitutional) 
purpose of which is collection of population information for 
use in apportionment of seats in Congress among the states. 
But the district court, although acknowledging that “Section 
141 of the Census Act generally authorizes the Secretary to use 
sampling in conducting various aspects of the census, without 
an express prohibition,” J.S. App. at 18a, pointed to section 
141(g), which defines a “census of population” as a “census of 
a population, housing, and matters related to population and 
housing.” Based on this slim reed, the court drew the 
remarkable conclusion that “the plain language of Section 141 
itself . . . establishes . . . Congress’ intent . . . to authorize 
sampling for numerous purposes of the census other than 
congressional apportionment.” Id. (emphasis added). 


That reading is utterly indefensible. To the contrary, 
section 141(g) makes clear that the authorization of sampling 
in section 141(a) extends to the census of population, the very 
census routinely used for apportionment. Indeed, section 
141(b) states that the tabulation of population used for 


6 


apportionment is to be based on the decennial census 
authorized by section 141(a). That is why even the D.C. 
district court concluded that section 141(a) “standing alone 
appears to permit statistical sampling in congressional 
apportionment.” J.S. App. at 6la, in No. 98-404. 


The Virginia court then went on to place greater reliance 
on the assertion that “[uJnder 13 U.S.C. § 195, Congress’ 
prohibition against the use of sampling is clear on its face.” 
J.S. App. 18a. It then asserted that section 195 is more 
“specific” and, relying on various decisions of this Court, 
concluded that this more specific provision must be read to 
trump the authorization of sampling for apportionment 
purposes in section 141(a). But for reasons already discussed 
in our prior brief, this analysis was severely flawed. 


First, as the Virginia court itself recognized, the principle 
that a specific provision controls a more general provision takes 
effect only when the two provisions cannot be “reconciled.” 
J.S. App. 20a; see Br. of Intervenors Gephardt et al. at 17-18, 
in No. 98-404 (citing cases). Second, it is hardly “clear on its 
face” that the “except” clause in section 195 constitutes a 
prohibition of anything. To the contrary, as the D.C. court 
acknowledged, an exception to a mandate does not always -- or 
even ordinarily -- create a prohibition. See id. at 20-22. There 
is thus no reason to avoid the obvious harmonizing reading of 
sections 141 and 195 as (1) authorizing use of sampling for 
apportionment purposes while (2) requiring such use, wherever 
feasible, for other purposes. 


The district court offered only two arguments to support its 
rejection of that reading. First, it cited two cases involving 
provisions with “except” clauses. Neither, however, is 
remotely apposite because neither interprets an except/shall 


_ es 


7 


sentence structure as itself creating a prohibition. Indeed, these 
cases provide further support for the proposition that when 
Congress uses the except/shall structure, it does not ordinarily 
-- if ever ~ intend to create a prohibition. At most it intends to 
allow other sources of law to govern the area that it has 
exempted, and these other sources of law determine whether or 
not there is a prohibition. Here, the relevant other source of 
law is Section 141 which, rather than prohibiting the use of 
sampling for apportionment, expressly authorizes the Census 
Bureau to do so. 


In Richardson v. Ramirez, 418 U.S. 24 (1974), the first 
case cited by the district court, this Court considered an equal 
protection challenge to a state’s disenfranchisement of a class 
of convicted criminals. In rejecting that challenge, the Court 
relied on Section 2 of the Fourteenth Amendment which 
imposes sanctions on states (a diminution of representation) if 
they disenfranchise their citizens but exemrts from that 
sanction states that disenfranchise criminals or rebels: 


Representatives shall be apportioned among the 
several States according to their respective 
numbers, counting the whole number of persons in 
each State . . . . But when the right to vote . . . is 
denied . . . or in any way abridged, except for 
_ participation in rebellion, o other crime, the basis 
~ of representation therein shall be reduced . . 


U.S. Const., amend. XTV, § 2. The Court concluded that the 
Amendment would not have exempted states that 
disenfranchise criminals from its automatic sanction of 
diminished representation if another portion of the same 
Amendment -- the equal protection clause -- simultaneously 


8 


forbade states altogether from disenfranchising criminals. 418 
U.S. at 55. 


Contrary to the unexplained suggestion of the district 
court, nothing in Richardson v. Ramirez in any way supports 
that court’s reading of section 195. The “except” clause in the 
Fourteenth Amendment is an exception from imposition on 
states of a penalty for abridging their citizens’ right to vote. 
The Court never suggested that this clause itself creates any 
sort of mandate or prohibition. It may well be that the 
Fourteenth Amendment does prohibit Congress from reducing 
the representation of states that disenfranchise criminals, but 
this prohibition is created by the first sentence of Section 2, 
which provides that apportionment is to be based on states’ 
respective numbers, not by the except clause in the second 
sentence. The except clause merely establishes that states 
which disenfranchise criminals are not subject to the automatic 
sanction of diminished representation and thus that 
apportionment for those states continues to be governed by the 
first sentence of Section 2. The analogeus provision of the 
Census Act is Section 141, which, rather than prohibiting the 
Census Bureau from using sampling, explicitly allows it to do 
so. 


Like the Fourteenth Amendment, the underlying statute at 
issue in Crosby v. United States, 506 U.S. 255 (1993), also 
cited by the district court, shows that when Congress exempts 
an area from a mandate, it does not ordinarily intend to create 
a prohibition. In Crosby, this Court was interpreting Rule 43(a) 
of the Federal Rules of Criminal Procedure, which requires that 
criminal defendants be present at trial, “except as otherwise 
provided.” The Court concluded that the “except” clause was 
exclusive; it leaves no room for creation of exceptions not 
“otherwise provided.” But the Court never intimated that the 


9 


“except” clause, where it applies, prohibits a defendant’s 
presence at trial. 


Nor could it have done so. For example, one of the 
exceptions “otherwise provided” is Rule 43(c)(2), which states 
that a defendant may choose not to attend a trial of a crime 
punishable by less than one year’s imprisonment. But the 
defendant obviously retains the option to attend. Thus, the fact 
that this situation is excepted from the requirement in Rule 
43(a) merely preserves discretion. Here, once again, an 
exception from a mandate is just that, and nothing more. 


Finally, the district court relied on the principle that a 
statutory interpretation should, where possible, give effect to 
every statutory provision. J.S. App. 19a-20a. But that 
principle, in fact, strongly undercuts the court’s preferred 
interpretation. The district court’s argument was that 
defendants’ reading of the statute “renders the ‘except for’ 
language [in section 195] devoid of meaning.” J.S. App. 19a. 
But that is clearly incorrect. The “except” clause in section 195 
exempts sampling for purposes of apportionment from the 


_conditional mandate established in the rest of section 195. By 


exempting apportionment from this requirement, Congress left 
the Census Bureau discretion to refrain from using sampling for 
apportionment even if it is feasible. 


Indeed, it is the district court’s reading that violates the 
canon that a statute should, if possible, be construed so that 
every word has substantive effect. Under that reading, section 
141(a)’s authorization of sampling is entirely superfluous, 
because section 195 controls the use of sampling both inside 
and outside the context of apportionment. Only under 
defendant’s reading does section 141(a) have independent force 


10 


-- providing the Census Bureau with affirmative authorization 
to use sampling for apportionment purposes. 


In sum, nothing in the Virginia district court’s opinion in 
any way strengthens the arguments in favor of reading the 
Census Act as prohibiting use of sampling for apportionment 
purposes. For all of the reasons stated here and in our earlier 
brief, the Act is best read as allowing the Census Bureau to 
make that choice. 


CONCLUSION 


The judgment below should be reversed.
