STATEMENT OF FACTS 
THE LEGAL FRAMEWORK OF THE CENSUS 


27. The United States Constitution requires that 
congressional “Representatives shall be apportioned 
among the several States according to their respective 
numbers, counting the whole number of persons in each 
State. U.S. Const., Amend. XIV, § 2; Art. I, § 2, 
el. 3. 


28. The Department of Commerce and its agency, 
the Bureau of the Census (collectively referred to as 
the “Department”), have been charged by Congress 
with the duty to conduct a decennial census in such 
manner as Congress “shall by Law direct” and in accord 
with the language and purpose of the Constitution. 
U.S. Const. Art. I, § 2, el. 3; Census Act of 1976, 13 
U.S.C. § 21. 


29. Following each decennial census, the President 
of the United States is required to transmit to Con- 
gress “a statement showing the whole number of per- 
sons in each State” and “the number of Representatives 
to which each State would be entitled” under a lawful 
apportionment on the first day, or within one week, of 
the first regular session of Congress. 2 U.S.C. § 2a(a). 

30. The Clerk of the House of Representatives is 


required to transmit to the executive of each State “a 
certificate of the number of Representatives to which 


15 


[each] State is entitled,” within fifteen days of receipt of 
the apportionment statement from the President, based 
on the apportionment of the population derived from 
the decennial census. 2 U.S.C. § 2b. 


31. After receiving the apportionment certification 
from the Clerk of the House, the States are required to 
establish congressional districts equal to the number of 
Representatives to which each State is entitled and 
“Representatives shall be elected only from districts so 
established.” 2 U.S.C. § 2c. 


32. The decennial census is the foundation of repre- 
sentative government under the Constitution because 
it provides the basis for apportionment of the number 
of congressional Representatives to which each State is 
entitled and the number of Electors from each State 
who, representing the people, elect the President of the 
United States. U.S. Const., Art. I, S 2, el. 2; Art. IT; 2 
U.S.C. S 2c, 3. 


33. The census also plays an important role in 
intrastrate redistricting because “State and local gov- 
ernments use census data to draw legislative districts 
of equal population to comply the with constitutional 
‘one man, one vote’ mandate.” REPOKT TO CONGRESS 
THE PLAN FOR CENSUS 2000, July 1997, as revised 
and reissued in August 1997, (the “Riche Report”) at 1. 
In fact, many state constitutions and statutes require 
the state to utilize the federal census in conducting 
their redistricting. Arizona, California, Connecticut, 
Florida, Georgia, Indiana, Montana, Nevada, New Jer- 
sey, Ohio, Pennsylvania, Virginia, and Wisconsin have 
such a requirement. 

34. The census also has important ramifications for 
state and local governments and their residents be- 


16 


cause census data are used “to determine allocation 
formulas for the distribution of billions of dollars of 
federal and state funds each year.” Riche Report at ix. 


35. The Constitution prohibits the intentional omis- 
sion of any person from the decennial census by man- 
dating that Congress, through the Department, conduct 
an “actual Enumeration” and engage in “counting the 
whole number of persons in each State” to implement 
the constitutional scheme of representative govern- 
ment, as envisioned by the Framers. U.S. Const., Art. 
I, S 2, el. 3; XIV Amend. For similar reasons, it 
prohibits the decennial census’ population figures to be 
based on a statistical estimate or anything other than 
an actual counting of real persons. 


36. Pursuant to the Census Act of 1976 (the “Census 
Act”), Congress has also expressly prohibited the 
Department from using the statistical method known as 
“sampling” in conducting the decennial census for de- 
termining the population for purposes of apportionment 
of Representatives among the several States. Census 
Act of 1976, 13 U.S.C. § 195. 


37. On June 5, 1997, Congress enacted the Supple- 
mental Appropriations and Rescissions Act (the “Sup- 
plemental Appropriations Act”) H.R. Cong. Report No. 
119, 105th Congress, Ist Session, June 4, 1997, which, in 
relevant part, prohibited the Department from utilizing 
any funds “to plan or otherwise prepare for the use of 
sampling or any other statistical procedure, including 
statistical adjustment in any determination of popula- 
tion for purposes of the apportionment of Representa- 
tives in Congress among the several States.” 


38. On June 9, 1997, Defendant Clinton vetoed the 
Supplemental Appropriations Act based on his opinion 


17 


that “the Constitution and relevant statutes permit the 
use of sampling in the decennial census.” Message of 
Cancellation, 1997 WL 307949. 


39. In November of 1997, Congress appropriated 
funding for the Commerce Department, including funds 
for conducting the 2000 Census, finding that “the sole 
constitutional purpose of the decennial enumeration of 
the population is the apportionment of Representatives 
in Congress among the several States.” 1998 Appro- 
priations Act, P. L. 105-119, § 209(a)(?). 


The Department’s Plan For Statistical Sampling and 
Adjustment 


40. Every census since the founding of the Republic 
has attempted to count each person. Each such census 
has actually enumerated the number of people. In other 
words, the number of persons has always been deter- 
mined by means of an actual head count. 


41. Given the inherent difficulties of actually enu- 
merating the population, each census has unintention- 
ally failed to count some people. 


42. For the first time in the Nation’s history, the 
federal government has decided that it will intention- 
ally fail to count at least 10 percent of the population. 
Thus, at least 26 million Americens will be deliberately 
omitted from the census if the Plan for Census 2000 is 
implemented. In addition, population figures produced 
pursuant to the Plan will not represent the sum of the 
omitted 10% and the 90% sought to be counted, but will 


‘be further adjusted upward based on estimates derived 


from statistical sampling and inference. 


43. Congress enacted the 1997 Emergency Supple- 
mental Appropriations Act for Recovery from Natural! 


18 


Disasters (“1997 Disaster Relief Bill”) which, in rele- 
vant part, required the Department to provide Con- 
gress with a written report containing a statement of 
the methodologies to be used in the conduct of the 2000 


44. As required by the 1997 Disaster Relief Bill, the 
Department issued the Riche Report which commits 
the Department to using statistical sampling in lieu of 
an actual enumeration for calculating the population in 
the 2000 Census. 


45. In July of 1997, the Department published its 
Census 2000 Operational Plan (the “Plan”), further 
confirming its intent to substitute statistical sampling 
for actual enumeration in the 2000 Census. The Plan 
further confirms that the Department will deliberately 
omit approximately 26 million people (ten percent of the 
population) from its actual count. 


46. The Department has specified that it will use the 
following methodology in conducting the 2000 Census: 
First, the Department will send questionnaires to 
households identified on its Master Address List. 
Second, the Department will conduct a non-response 
follow-up for some of those households which did 
not respond to the questionnaires. “The nonresponse 
follow-up operation in Census 2000 will incorporate the 
use of sampling to yield a 90 percent ec nipletion rate at 
the census tract level.” Riche Report at 18. Once the 
Department has attempted to count 90 percent of the 
population, no further effc will be made to count the 
missing persons. The Department will use statistical 
sampling to add computer-generated population num- 
bers to compensate for the intentional undercount of 
10% of all housing units. This estimate of virtual per- 
ons will be modeled after the composition of those per- 


19 


sons who were counted only as a result of the follow-up 
efforts. Thus, these fictional persons will not reflect the 
overall demographic composition of all the persons that 
were actually counted in that area. 


47. After using sampling to achieve a statistical es- 
timation of 100% of the population, the Department will 
further manipulate the population estimate by an addi- 
tional statistical methodology known as integrated cov- 
erage management (“ICM”). ICM relies upon dual sys- 
tem estimation (“DSE”) pursuant to which the Depart- 
ment will carefully count the actual number of people in 
a limited number of areas, a sample of 25,000 census 
blocks out of 7 million nationwide. Riche Report at 30. 
The Department will then compare the results of this 
careful head count to the numbers derived from the 
direct responses of the counting phase. It will then 
infer that demographic deviations from the initial cal- 
culation will occur throughout the rest of the state in 
question for each particular strata that is measured. 
“These strata will be based on the characteristics of 
each block in the 1990 census, such as the block’s state, 
racial and ethnic status, and proportion of homeowners 
to renters.” Riche Report at 30. 


48. According to the Department, “Compared to the 
undercount rate for non-Hispanic Whites, the 1990 
undercount rates were six times larger among African 
Americans and seven times larger among Hispanics.” 
Riche Report at 4. Thus, the computer-generated per- 
sons created by the Department will include a dis- 
proportionately high number of these minority groups. 
Any adjustments based on this methodology will have 
the effect of increasing the relative population of areas 
with a disproportionately large number of minorities 
under a virtual census as compared to an actual head 


20 


count and decreasing the relative population of areas 
with a disproportionately fewer number of minorities. 


49. The post-enumeration survey (“PES”) conducted 
by the Department after the 1990 census also utilized 
DSE. The Department has based its simulations of the 
2000 census on the premise “that the percentage under- 
counts (and overcounts) measured for each group in the 
1990 PES also would apply in Census 2000.” Riche 
Report at 45. 


50. In short, the census will utilize statistical sam- 
pling in at least three ways: 1) to conduct non-response 
follow-up and thus to arrive at an estimation of 90 
percent of the population; 2) to model that number to an 
estimation of 100 percent of the population; 3) to adjust 
that estimate to take into account demographic devia- 
tions that are discovered through the process of ICM. 


51. Congress has found that “the decennial enumera- 
tion of the population is a complex and vast undertak- 
ing, and if such enumeration is conducted in a manner 
that does not comply with the requirements of the 
Constitution or laws of the United States, it would be 
impracticable for the States to obtain, and the courts of 
the United States to provide, meaningful relief after 
such enumeration has been conducted.” 1998 Appro- 
priations Act, P. L. 105-119, § 209(a)(8). 


52. Federal law requires the Secretary of Commerce 
to report a tabulation of the total population by States 
to the President by January 1, 2001, and in turn re- 
quires the President to “transmit to the Congress a 
statement showing the whole number of persons in each 
state” by January 8, 2001. 13 U.S.C. § 141(b); 2 U.S.C. 
§ 2a. In light of the significant practical difficulties of 
conducting an actual head count of the population, the 


21 


Defendants will not be able to conduct an actual head 
count by January 1, 2001, unless they begin this process 
in 1999 at the latest. Thus, absent judicial intervention 
in the immediate future, the Defendants cannot provide 
lawful or constitutional census figures by January 1, 
2001, the date required by statute and the Constitution. 
Moreover, if the Department is required to conduct an 
actual head count at any time after January 1, 2001 it 
will be unable to complete an actual head count in time 
to provide the Congress and the states with the census 
data they need to create equally populated congres- 
sional districts for the 2002 elections. Thus, if the legal- 
ity and constitutionality of the Department’s Census 
Plan is not resolved in the immediate future, it will not 
be possible to use an actual head count, or any lawful or 
constitutional census, as apportionment or redistricting 
figures for the 2002 elections or other legally mandated 
purposes. 

53. The 1998 Appropriations Act provides that the 
use of any statistical method as part of a dress re- 
hearsal or other simulation of a census in preparation 
for the use of such method, in a decennial census, to 
determine the population for purposes of the apportion- 
ment or redistricting of Members in Congress shall be 
considered the use of such method in connection with 
that census.” 1998 Appropriations Act, P.L. 105-119,

30. The United States Constitution requires that 
congressional “Representatives shall be apportioned 
among the several States according to their respective 
numbers, counting the whole number of persons in each 
State. .” U.S. Const., Amend. XIV, § 2; Art. I, § 2, 
el. 3. 


31. The Department of Commerce and its agency, 
the Bureau of the Census (collectively referred to as 
the “Department”), have been charged by Congress 
with the duty to conduct a decennial census in such 
manner as Congress “shall by Law direct” and in accord 
with the language and purpose of the Constitution. 


39 


U.S. Const. Art. I, § 2, el. 3; Census Act of 1976, 13 
U.S. C. § 21. 


32. Following each decennial census, the President 
of the United States is required to transmit to Con- 
gress “a statement showing the whole number of per- 
sons in each State” and “the number of Representatives

The Department’s Plan For Statistical Sampling and 

Adjustment 


43. Every census since the founding of the Republic 
has attempted to count each person. Each such census 
has actually enumerated the number of people. In other 
words, the number of persons has always been deter- 
mined by means of an actual head count. 


44. Given the inherent difficulties of actually enu- 
merating the population, each census has unintention- 
ally failed to count some people. 


42 


45. For the first time in the Nation’s history, the 
federal government has decided that it will intention- 
ally fail to count at least 10 percent of the population. 
Thus, at least 26 million Americans will be deliberately 
omitted from the census if the Plan for Census 2000 is 
implemented. In addition, population figures produced 
pursuant to the Plan will not represent the sum of the 
omitted 10% and the 90% sought to be counted, but will 
be further adjusted upward based on estimates derived 
from statistical sampling and inference. 


46. Congress enacted the 1997 Emergency Supple- 
mental Appropriations Act for Recovery from Natural 
Disasters (“1997 Disaster Relief Bill”) which, in rele- 
vant part, required the Department to provide Con- 
gress with a written report containing a statement of 
the methodologies to be used in the conduct of the 2000 
Census. 


47. As required by the 1997 Disaster Relief Bill, the 
Department issued the Riche Report which commits 
the Department to using statistical sampling in lieu of 
an actual enumeration for calculating the population in 
the 2000 Census. 

48. In July of 1997, the Department published its 
Census 2000 Operational Plan (the “Plan”), further 
confirming its intent to substitute statistical sampling 
for actual enumeration in the 2000 Census. The Plan 
further confirms that the Department will deliberately 
omit approximately 26 million people (ten percent of the 
population) from its actual count. 

49. The Department has specified that it will use the 
following methodology in conducting the 2000 Census: 
First, the Department will send questionnaires to 
households identified on its Master Address List. 


43 


Second, the Department will conduct a non-response 
follow-up for some of those households which did 
not respond to the questionnaires. “The nonresponse 
follow-up operation in Census 2000 will incorporate the 
use of sampling to yield a 90 percent completion rate at 
the census tract level.” Riche Report at 18. Once the 
Department has attempted to count 90 percent of the 
population, no further effort will be made to count the 
missing persons. The Department will use statistical 
sampling to add computer-generated population num- 
bers to compensate for the intentional undercount of 
10% of all housing units. This estimate of virtual per- 
sons will be modeled after the composition of those per- 
sons who were counted only as a result of the follow-up 
efforts. Thus, these fictional persons will not reflect the 


overall demographic composition of all the persons that 
were actually counted in that area. 


50. After using sampling to achieve a statistical es- 
timation of 100% of the population, the Department will 
further manipulate the population estimate by an addi- 
tional statistical methodology known as integrated cov- 
erage management (“ICM”). ICM relies upon dual sys- 
tem estimation (“DSE”) pursuant to which the Depart- 
ment will carefully count the actual number of people in 
a limited number of areas, a sample of 25,000 census 
blocks out of 7 million nationwide. Riche Report at 30. 
The Department will then compare the results of this 
careful head count to the numbers derived from the 
direct responses of the counting phase. It will then 
infer that demographic deviations from the initial cal- 
culation will occur throughout the rest of the state in 
question for each particular strata that is measured. 
“These strata will be based on the characteristics of 
each block in the 1990 census, such as the block’s state, 


44 


racial and ethnic status, and proportion of homeowners 
to renters.” Riche Report at 30. 


51. According to the Department, “Compared to the 
undercount rate for non-Hispanic Whites, the 1990 
undercount rates were six times larger among African 
Americans and seven times larger among Hispanics.” 
Riche Report at 4. Thus, the computer-generated per- 
sons created by the Department will include a dis- 
proportionately high number of these minority groups. 
Any adjustments based on this methodology will have 
the effect of increasing the relative population of areas 
with a disproportionately large number of minorities 
under a virtual census as compared to an actual head 
count and decreasing the relative population of areas 
with a disproportionately fewer number of minorities. 


52. The post-enumeration survey (“PES”) conducted 
by the Department after the 1990 census also utilized 
DSE. The Department has based its simulations of the 
2000 census on the premise “that the percentage under- 
counts (and overcounts) measured for each group in the 
1990 PES also would apply in Census 2000.“ Riche 
Report at 45. 


53. In short, the census will utilize statistical sam- 
pling in at least three ways: 1) to conduct non-response 
follow-up and thus to arrive at an estimation of 90 
percent of the population; 2) to model that number to an 
estimation of 100 percent of the population; 3) to adjust 
that estimate to take into account demographic devia- 
tions that are discovered through the process of ICM.

8. The Plan relies upon statistical sampling 
methodologies to compute an estimated total population 
to be reported in the year 2000. First, the Department 
of Commerce (“the Department”) will deliberately 
exclude 10% of the population from its efforts to 
conduct a traditional head count of the population. The 
population that is not enumerated will be estimated 
from a sample of the nonresponding population. Riche 
Report at 27-29. Second, the Department will adjust 
the total population numbers obtained in the first phase 
of the census according to a statistical model in the 
Integrated Coverage Measurement phase of the Plan. 
The Department makes the assumption that people 
with shared demographic characteristics are equally 
likely to be missed, or counted more than once, in the 
census. Riche Report at 30. By assigning an 
“adjustment factor” to these demographic groups, the 
Department will adjust the number of people counted 
in the census to comport with a statistical model. Riche 
Report at 32. Typically, adjustment factors exceed 1 
because most demographic groups selected by the 
Department are estimated to have undercounts. See 
CAPE Report. However, many adjustment factors are 
less than 1; these groups are estimated to have been 
overcounted by the census. Id. 


9. Under the Department’s Plan, an actual head 
count of the actual population will not be conducted. 
Thus, even after the implementation of the Depart- 
ment’s Plan, there will be no population numbers 
available based upon a complete traditional enumera- 
tion. Indeed, the Department’s Plan intends to deliber- 
ately omit approximately 10% of the population from its 
actual head count.

18. I have denominated the difference between the 
adjusted and unadjusted populations as the “Shift in 
Share.” The Shift in Share for each state and county 
forms the basis for my analysis of relative impact of 
using adjusted data to redistrict congressional and 
state legislative districts and for the differential 
allocation of federal and state funding. The Shift in 
Share predicts the direction and degree of change of the 
area’s proportionate share of a finite resource, such as 
political representation or funding. Because the 
amount of federal funding appropriated for grants to 
states and counties is finite, the Shift in Share provides 
a means of assessing how a change in the population for 
any state or county will affect its share of the total 
resource. 


19. I have also calculated a second indicator which 
predicts the specific impact of adjustment for states and 
counties. This indicator is denominated as the “Local 
Differential.” The Local Differential is calculated by 
dividing the Shift in Share by the preadjustment Share. 
The Shift in Share assesses the relationship between 
the impact of adjustment on any one state or county 
with respect to other states or counties. In contrast, 
the Local Differential assesses the impact of 
adjustment on the size of the actual resources received 
by any particular state or county. 


20. Thus, the Shift in Share is the absolute dif- 
ference in the change of a state’s or county’s position,

A. Vote Dilution In Federal Elections 


21. In determining whether the statistical meth- 
odology described in the Plan is substantially likely to 
affect apportionment of congressional representation 
and the number of Electors to the Electoral College 
with respect to the states where Plaintiffs reside, I 
conclude that, as a result of the Plan, congressional 
apportionment could change. Specifically, it is a virtual 
certainty that Indiana will lose a seat, dropping from 
ten seats under a traditional enumeration to nine under 
the Department’s Plan, while Mississippi would gain a 
seat, going from four seats under a traditional 
enumeration to five under the Department’s Plan. This 
is true for both of the Preferred Series of projections 
for the 2000 population released by the Department. 


22. In addition, I have concluded that several other 
states are substantially likely to lose a seat due to the 
implementation of the Plan for Census 2000. The 
formula which is used to apportion the U.S. House seats 
to the states is the method of equal proportions. By this 
method, the proportional differences in the number of 
persons per representative for any pair of states is kept 
to a minimum. Because of the mathematical exactitude 
used in this formula, it can be subject to very small 
changes in population as between any pair of states. 
The contest for the last seat apportioned under the 
formula can be decided by only several hundreds of 
persons. In fact, the difference between the last seat 
could literally be decided by the addition of one person.

23. Although the NUR reported in the 1990 PES are 
relatively small, with a national rate of 1.584%, the 
NUR factors are of sufficient size to effect a shift of at 
least one seat in the U.S. House of Representatives 
from one state to another. In fact, if the 1990 NUR, as 
originally released by the Department in 1991, had been 
used to apportion representation among the states, the 
adjustment of the population numbers would have 
resulted in an entirely different apportionment than 
one using the corrected NUR, which was released in 
1992. Adjusting by the 1992 factors would have caused 
Wisconsin to lose a seat to California. Under the 1991 
factors, Wisconsin and Pennsylvania would have each 
lost a seat and California and Arizona would have each 
gained one seat. Clearly, the size of the adjustment to 
be implemented by the Plan is large enough to alter the 
apportionment of the U.S. House by at least one seat. 
Additionally, even if the adjustment does not cause a 
state to cross the threshold to lose a seat, the impact on 
its relative position, or rank, with respect to other 
states is clearly demonstrated. 


24. The impact of population adjustment on a state’s 
relative rank is that the rank may drop several 
positions without crossing the threshold of 435. From a 
review of the population projections for 2000, I can

numbers on the relative ranking of states for the last 
seat. Based upon all these projections, the average 
drop in rank is four positions for Connecticut, Indiana, 
Missouri, Ohio, and Wisconsin and five positions for 
Massachusetts, Minnesota, and Pennsylvania.

D. Failure To Timely Apportion 


31. As note above, the Department does not intend 
to conduct a complete traditional enumeration in con- 
nection with the implementation of its plan. Rather, it 
intends to deliberately omit 10% of the population from 
its actual head count. Thus, after the completion of the 
Department’s Plan, there will not be any population 
figures based on a complete head count. Thus, federal 
and state governments will be forced to continue to 
utilize the population numbers reported in the 1990 
census. Given the indisputable fact there is substantial 
variation in growth rates between counties and states, 
such continued reliance on the 1990 census result will 
cause severe malapportionment in federal and state 
elections and could change the congressional repre- 
sentation of the states in which plaintiffs reside.

An Explanation of Estimates and Projections 


4. As background, let me provide a basic explanation 
of how the Population Division of the Census Bureau 
prepares both estimates and projections of the popula- 
tion. The difference between an estimate and a projec- 
tion is that an estimate refers to the past, while a 
projection refers to the future. Estimates use a variety 
of existing sources to come to a reasoned conclusion 
about the population of a given governmental unit at 
some specified point in the past, while population 
projections make assumptions about which demo- 
graphic trends will continue in the future. Our popula- 


94 


tion estimates are built with administrative records; 
they are based on birth and death records, and tax 
return data, among other sources. Projections use the 
latest available national and state estimates as the 
starting point, and then project the population in the 
future based on assumptions about fertility, mortality 
and internal and international migration trends. 


5. The Census Bureau prepares annual population 
estimates by age, sex, race, and Hispanie origin for the 
nation, states, and eounties in the United States. Every 
other year we prepare population estimates for the sub- 
county level, for all funetioning governmental units. 
The population estimates which we released in March, 
1998 are the most recent population estimates down to 
the county level as of July 1, 1997. 


6. The Census Bureau prepares projections of the 
national and state populations on a periodic but irregu- 
lar basis. We have published state population projec- 
tions through the year 2025. The Bureau does not 
update its published projections every year after 
releasing updated estimates. Dr. Weber relied upon 
our most recent published population projections con- 
tained in the publication PPL-47, which was released in 
October, 1996. 


7. The data set on which Dr. Weber premises his 
work, PPL-47, contains three sets of state projections, 
the preferred or “A” series used by Dr. Weber, and the 
“B” and “C” series. The “A” series is based on a time 
series model of state-to-state migration while the B. 
series is based on employment projections. The C 
series lists population projections prepared by individ- 
ual states. While the specifics vary, the Census Bureau 
generally follows this format and publishes a variety of 


95 


different series of population projections in one publica- 
tion. The Bureau began to identify one of those series 
as preferred in 1994 in order to provide more guidance 
to naive users. Sophisticated users of Census Bureau 
data will typically use a variety of population projec- 
tions, depending on the use of data; unsophisticated 
users are aided by being pointed to a preferred series. 


8. PPL-47 is replete with disclaimers and explana- 
tions as to the number and variability of assumptions 
involved in population projections. The Census Bureau 
describes the preferred series thusly: 


Users are likely to choose the demographic series— 
the time series—due to the series stability, long- 
term inertia, and no need for economic assumptions. 
However, there is no definitive set of projections 
and the economic model is an alternative. Given the 
sensitivity of internal migration to changes in eco- 
nomic conditions, internal migration changes can be 
rapid and sizable. Identifying a preferred series 
along with an alternative series, reflects a process of 
evaluating State population projection models used 


in previous Census Bureau State population pro-

Even two years ago, when PPL-47 was published, there

Report. As described in that report, major design 
decisions for the non-sampling alternative remain to be 
made. Until these major design decisions are made, one 
cannot even begin to predict error in Census 2000. 
Additionally, while the basic structure of the plan for 
Census 2000 is in place, and is described in the Report 
to Congress, some details of the plan may be modified 


after the results of the on-going Dress Rehearsal are 
reviewed. 


Dr. Weber Ignores PES Sampling Error. 


11. As Dr. Weber correctly states, the 1990 Post 
Enumeration Survey, or PES, was a post census survey 
that estimated the undercount rate for the 1990 census. 
The Census Bureau believes that all decennial censuses 
thus far have undercounted the population. The PES 
quantified this undercount by using an established 
statistical method called Dual System Estimation. The 
1990 PES estimates are, in fact, the best evidence at 
the sub-national level of the undercount in the 1990 
Census. Like all survey estimates, however, the PES 
estimates have associated sampling error, otherwise 
known as standard error. Standard errors are 
measures of sampling error and are used to quantify 
the confidence interval about an estimate. A confidence 


106 


interval tells you in statistical terms how far an 
estimate could be from the truth. 


12. Contrary to standard accepted statistical prac- 
tice, Dr. Weber, has ignored the standard error associ- 
ated with each state’s NUR. I will illustrate, using the 
State of Indiana, which Dr. Weber erroneously states is 
“virtually certain” to lose a seat under the plan for 
Census 2000 (Weber Affidavit, para. 21). Indiana’s 1990 
NUR was 0.504% and its standard error was 0.399. 
This means that one can say with 90% confidence that 
Indiana’s true undercount in 1990 was between -0.152% 
and 1.160%. (A 90% confidence interval is constructed 
by multiplying the standard error by 1.645 and then 
adding and subtracting the resulting number to the 
point estimate.) Dr. Weber, however, has not ac- 
counted for this range, focusing inappropriately on a 
single point estimate. Standard statistical practice 
would have accounted for sampling error in preparing 
such a projection. This would have indicated a much 
greater degree of uncertainty about the effects of 
sampling than his estimates suggest. 


The Census Bureau Uses the 1990 NURs Properly 


13. Dr. Weber correctly points out in his affidavit 
that the Census Bureau itself has used the 1990 PES 
data in planning for Census 2000. These data are 
descriptive of the problems with the 1990 enumeration 
and thus are important planning tools. The Census 
Bureau used the 1990 NUR to help it estimate expected 
error rates for Census 2000. These error projections 
are used to make conservative estimates of the national 
NUR, and are used in the form of averages or ranges, 
not fixed numbers. Dr. Weber, in contrast, is using the 
1990 PES data to predict apportionment in 2000 by 
running the method of equal proportions. The 


107 


sensitivity of the method of equal proportions to small 
changes in relative population share between states is 
well known and is acknowledged by Dr. Weber himself. 
who states that “the last seat could literally be decided 
by the addition of one person”. (Weber Affidavit, para. 
22) Using the 1990 PES data to prepare conservative 
estimates is far different from using the numbers in a 
sensitive formula like the method of equal proportions. 


Dr. Weber’s Affidavit Contains a Number of 
Inaccuracies 


14. Dr. Weber makes a number of inaccurate asser- 
a in his affidavit. I will list the major inaccuracies 
Ow: 


— Dr. Weber asserts that Census 2000 will be 
less accurate using the Bureau’s plan than 
it would be were a traditional enumeration 
conducted. (Weber Affidavit, para. 37) 
This is not true. The Census Bureau has 
estimated that it can improve accuracy at 
the state level, the important level for 
Congressional apportionment, from 1.9% 
undercount to 0.5% error. Report to Con- 


gress, p. 44-45. 


— Dr. Weber asserts that the Census Bureau 
could count 100% of the population were it 
“willing to devote sufficient resources”. 
(Weber Affidavit, para. 37) The Census 
Bureau agrees with expert commentators 
who have concluded that traditional census- 
taking methods have never lead to a com- 
plete count and are increasingly unlikely to 
do so in the future. See, Report to Con- 
gress, p 5-6 and Modernizing the U.S. 


108 


Census, National Academy of Sciences, 
(1995) at 3 (“It is fruitless to continue trying 
to count every last person with traditional 
census methods of physical enumeration.”). 
Additionally, both the Census Bureau and 
its outside observers are seriously con- 
cerned about the Bureau’s ability to recruit, 
hire and train sufficient enumerators to 
conduct 100% nonresponse follow-up. 


Dr. Weber’s statement that “errors which 
exist at the census block level will be mag- 
nified when aggregated to the level of a 
federal Congressional district” (Weber Af- 
fidavit, para. 38) is egregiously wrong. Ele- 
mentary statistics teaches that a larger 
sample size will yield a smaller sampling 
error. While sampling error may be high at 
the block level under the plan for Census 
2000, that error will drop dramatically once 
blocks are aggregated together to form any 
sort of meaningful representational unit. 
On the other hand, systematic bias (non- 
sampling error), as measured by the 1990 
undercount, will not diminish as smaller 
areas are aggregated to larger ones. 


The Census Bureau is not going to “deliber- 
ately exclude 10% of the population”. (We- 
ber Affidavit, para. 9) As explained in the 
Report to Congress, pp. 26-29, the Census 
Bureau will go to extraordinary lengths to 
elicit voluntary responses from 100% of the 
population and sampling will be used only 


109 


to complete the nonresponse follow-up op- 
eration and as a quality check. 


Dr. Weber's projections ignore overseas Americans. 


15. My final contention with Dr. Weber’s work is 
that Dr. Weber has failed to take into account overseas 
Americans in making his 2000 projections. The Census 
Bureau’s population projections and estimates do not 
take the overseas Americans into account because this 
data was included only for apportionment purposes. 
The data on overseas Americans is public, however, and 
any statistician could have included it in his or her 
projections. Dr. Weber failed to do so. The history of 
the count of overseas Americans makes clear that a 
decision to include overseas Americans can and does 
affect apportionment. The Census Bureau included 
Department of Defense overseas employees in the 
official 1990 count based on their “home of record.” 
This decision added an additional 922,819 individuals to 
the official count, shifted a seat from Massachusetts to 
Washington and culminated in a Supreme Court deci- 
sion upholding the inclusion of overseas Americans. 
Franklin v. Massachusetts, 505 U.S. 788 (1992). The 
Census Bureau has recently decided that it will include 
overseas Americans again in the 2000 count. Clearly, 
overseas Americans have the potential to shift seats 


and a prediction that fails to take these individuals into 
account is not reliable.

98-564 CLINTON, PRESIDENT, ET AL. V. GLAVIN, 
MATTHEW, ET AL. 


The joint motion to expedite consideration of the 
jurisdictional statement and to set an expedited 
briefing schedule is granted. In this case, probable 
jurisdiction is noted. The brief of the Solicitor 
General is to be filed with the Clerk and served 
upon opposing counsel on or before 3 p.m., 
Tuesday, October 20, 1998. The briefs of appellees 
are to be filed with the Clerk and served upon 
opposing counsel on or before 3 p.m., Wednesday, 
November 4, 1998. A reply brief, if any, is to be 
filed with the Clerk and served upon opposing 
counsel on or before ‘Tuesday, November 17, 1998. 
Rule 29.2 does not apply. The case is consolidated 
for oral argument on Monday, November 30, 1998, 
with No. 98-404, Department of Commerce v. 
United States House of Representatives, and a total 
of one and one-half hours are allotted for oral 
argument.
