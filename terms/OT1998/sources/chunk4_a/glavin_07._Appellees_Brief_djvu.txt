The Legal Underpinnings of the Decennial Census 


The Constitution provides that Representatives shall be 
“apportioned among the several States . . . according to their 
respective Numbers.” U.S. CONST. art. 1, § 2, cl. 3. 
Originally, these “Numbers” were to be determined not by 
including all members of society in a numerical assessment, 
but rather, “by adding to the whole Number of free Persons, 
. . . excluding Indians not taxed, three fifths of all other 
Persons.” /d. The Census Clause then provides that “(t]he 
actual Enumeration [of the states’ respective Numbers] shall 
be_made within three Years after the first Meeting of the 
Congress of the United States, and within every subsequent 
Term of ten Years, in such Manner as [the Congress] shall 
by Law direct.” /d. The Fourteenth Amendment subsequently 
recognized the status of former slaves as equal “persons” 
under the Constitution, and requires that the “respective 
Numbers” of the states for the purpose of apportionment 
include “the whole number of persons in each State, 
excluding Indians not taxed.” U.S. CONST. amend. XIV. 


Congress has delegated to the Secretary of Commerce 
the responsibility for taking each decennial census “in such 
form and content as he may determine, including the use of 
sampling procedures and special surveys.” 13 U.S.C. 
§ 141(a). Congress has also provided that sampling must be 
used whenever feasible, “[e]xcept for the determination of

population for purposes of apportionment of Representatives 
in Congress among the several States.” 13 U.S.C. § 195. 
The statute does not say that sampling cannot be used for 


purposes of apportionment.

SUMMARY OF THE ARGUMENT 


1. The plain text of sections 141(a) and 195 together 
demonstrate Congress’s intent to permit sampling for a 
number of purposes, including the purpose of apportionment 
of Representatives in Congress among the several states. In 
section 1-11(a), Congress provides that sampling procedures 
and special surveys are permissible ways in which to 
undertake a decennial census of population. Section 195 
requires the Secretary to use sampling if he deems it feasible. 
The introductory clause of section 195, however, exempts 
sampling for apportionment purposes from the general 
requirement that sampling be used. Contrary to the holding 
of the court below, this exception from the mandatory 
language of the statute does not prohibit the Secretary from 
using sampling if he believes it appropriate to do so. The 
words of the statutory provisions speak for themselves, 
stating clearly that the Census Act allows the use of sampling 


in the apportionment process. 


2. The Constitution does not prescribe a particular 
methodology by which to conduct the census for purposes of 
apportionment, but rather, indicates that the form of such 
census should not be given priority over its accuracy. The 
words of Article I alone explicitly give Congress the power 
to conduct the decennial enumeration of the population “in 
such Manner as they shall be Law direct.” Moreover, both 
the language of Article I and the records of the Federal 


9 


Convention demonstrate that the Framers meant only to 
mandate that the numbers for apportionment be calculated 
decennially according to a formula that took into account the 
entire free population and three-fifths of the slave population. 
No evidence suggests that the Framers ever considered 
precisely what methodology should be used to determine the 
total population number to which that formula would be 
applied. Finally, the Census Clause of the Constitution must 
be interpreted in light of the subsequent ratification of the 
Fourteenth Amendment, requiring that former slaves be 
counted in the census equally with whites, and the 
constitutional goals of equal protection and equal 
representation. An interpretation of the Census Clause to 
require a methodology known to perpetuate a differential 
undercount of racial and ethnic minority populations would 
Clearly be inconsistent with those constitutional principles.

A. The District Court Gave Incomplete Consideration to 
the Provisions of Section 141 


The district court erred in its “plain” reading of the 
Census Act, as that reading was based on a conclusory and 
erroneous decision that “ine only plausible interpretation of 
the plain language and structure of the Act is that section 195 
prohibits sampling for apportionment and Section 141 allows 
it for all other purposes.” J.S.2la. The district court's 
interpretation of these two sections flies in the face of the 
language and the structure of the Act. 


The lower court interpreted section 141 to be a provision 
that “generally authorizes the Secretary to use sampling in 
conducting various aspects of the census, without an express 
proLibition,” and then concluded that “[a] reading of the 
plain language of Section 141 itself further establishes that 
Congress’s intent was to authorize sampling for numerous 
purposes of the census other than congressional 
apportionment.” J.S.18a. In deciding that the “plain 
reading of the Census Act prohibits sampling for the purpose 
of congressional apportionment, however, the court 
disregarded both sections 141(a) and 141(b), citing without 
explanation only to section 141(g). J.S.18a. Section 141(g) 
merely states: “As used in this section, ‘census of the 
population’ means a census of a population, housing, and 
matters relating to population and housing.” J.S. 18a. 


Read together, sections 141(a),(b), and (g) manifest the 
clear intent of Congress to give the Secretary the discretion 
to use sampling for a number of purposes, including the 
purpose of congressional apportionment. Even standing 
alone, section 141(a) expressly provides the Secretary with 
such discretion. United States House of Representatives v. 
United States Department of Commerce, 11 F. Supp. 2d 76, 
103 (D.D.C. 1998) (stating that “section 141(a) . . . standing

apportionment).

determine, including the use of sampling 
procedures and special surveys. 


13 U.S.C. § 141(a) (emphasis added). On its face, this

“decennial census of population,” including for the purpose

the constitutionally required decennial census for

C. The District Court Erred in Its Application of the 
Rules of Statutory Interpretation 


In reaching its conclusion that “[sjection 141 in no way 
undermines the definite prohibition of sampling for purposes 
of apportionment contained in Section 195,” J.S.2la, the 
district court also erred in its application of the rules of 
statutory interpretation. While it properly recognized that 
“*fijt is the cardinal principle of statutory construction . . . 
to give effect, if possible, to every clause and word of a 
statute ... rather than to emasculate an entire section,’” 
J.S.17a (citations omitted), the court violated this rule with 
a strained reading that ignores significant provisions of 
section 141. See, e.g., Clark v. Uebersee Finanz- 
Korporation, A.G., 332 U.S. 480, 488-89 (1947) (“Our task 
is to give all of it [the entire statute] . . . the most 
harmonious, comprehensive meaning possible... . To do 
otherwise would be to impute to Congress a purpose to 
paralyze with one hand what it sought to promote with the 
other.”). Section 141 plainly permits the use of sampling in 
the decennial census for congressional apportionment. 


The lower court incorrectly asserted that “[rjeading the 
general authorization for sampling in Section 141 as 
somehow negating the prohibition of sampling in Section 195 
would render the language of Section 195 meaningless.” 
J.S.20a. The purpose of Section 195 is to limit the 
Secretary’s discretion in using sampling for purposes other 
than apportionment, while allowing complete discretion under 
the statute to use sampling for purposes of apportionment. 
The “except for” language that the lower court found would 
be rendered “devoid of meaning” by such a reading, /.S. 19a, 
still has a very precise meaning: that the limits on the 
Secretary's discretion set forth in section 195 do not apply to 
apportionment.

Il. THE CONSTITUTION DOES NOT PROHIBIT THE 
USE OF _ STATISTICAL SAMPLING _IN 
ENUMERATING THE POPULATION FOR 
PURPOSES OF APPORTIONMENT 


If the lower court erred in interpreting the Census Act to 
prohibit sampling, the question of whether the language of 
the Constitution precludes the use of statistical sampling in 
the 2000 census for purposes of apportionment must be 
considered. 


The lower court stated that it was not considering the 
constitutional question presented by the plaintiffs, as it found 
the statutory question dispositive. J.S.17a n.2, J.S.2la. In 
dicta, however, the district court asserted without reasoning 
that “the plain language” of the Constitution in both Article 
I, section 2, clause 3 and the Fourteenth Amendment 
“mandates a head count of the population.” J.S.17a n.2. 
National Korean American Service & Education Consortium 
and the other parties submitting this brief respectfully refer 
the Court to their brief filed as appellees in United States 
Department of Commerce, et al. v. United States House of 
Representatives, et al., 98-404, on October 6, 1998 for a 
refutation of the district court’s assertion and a full discussion 
of the constitutional issues in this case, which is summarized 
below. 


Nothing in the Constitution prohibits the use of sampling 
for purposes of apportioning representatives. Neither the 
language nor the history of the Constitution offers any 
indication that the Framers intended either to require or to 
prohibit any particular procedure for determining the 
numbers of the entire population. The records of the Federal 
Convention, in fact, demonstrate that the words “actual 
Enumeration” in the Constitution mandated the act of 
calculating a total based on the formula set forth in Article I 


21 


that took into account the whole free population and three- 
fifths of the slave population, not a specific means by which 
to arrive at the total population number to which the formula 
would be applied. 


With the ratification of the Fourteenth Amendment, the 
Constitution for the first time required that the former slaves 
be counted equally in the decennial “enumeration” for the 
purpose of apportioning representatives. Any reading of the 
words “actual Enumeration” that would require adherence to 
a methodology certain to result in less-than-equal counting, 
therefore, would undermine the fundamental principles of 
equality embodied in the Fourteenth Amendment. 


Although various types of household surveys have 
historically been the mainstay of the decennial census, past 
history is not determinative of the limits of Congress’s 
discretion as set forth in the Constitution, which directs 
Congress to take the census “in such Manner as they shall by 
Law direct.” It does not preclude the use of newly 
developed methodologies that better account for the entire 
population. Furthermore, in light of the differential 
undercount of racial and ethnic minorities that will inevitably 
result if only traditional methods are used, considerations of 
equal representation and equal protection militate for the use 
of statistical sampling in the 2000 census.
