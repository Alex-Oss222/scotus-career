STATEMENT 


A. INTRODUCTION 


In order of importance to petitioners, the issues 
presented by the petition are: (1) petitioners’ quest for attorneys’ 
fees for losing a case in the Ninth Circuit; and (2) the merits of 
the Ninth Circuit opinion. 


In their lawsuit, petitioners asked for the alternative 
relief of an order either directing a re-vote of the 1996 
constitutional convention question or directing — the 
re-certification of the 1996 vote as having approved the 
constitutional conv: .ition question. 


Petitioners obtained neither of the alternative forms of 
relief requested because the Ninth Circuit held that the 
challenged 1996 election did not violate the United States 
Constitution. Afier the Ninth Circuit ruled against petitioners, 
the State of Hawaii gratuitously enacted Act 121, placing the 
constitutional convention on the ballot in the November 1998 
election. Petitioners then represented to the Ninth Circuit that 
the re-vote alternative was the principal relief sought. “[Act 131] 
grants Plaintiffs the principal relief sought in this litigation--a 
new election on the convention question.” (Pet. App. 179a) 


In November 1998, the constitutional convention . 
question was again rejected. 


Petitioners ask this court to grant a writ of certiorari on 
two grounds. They ask this Court to GVR this case--to grant 
certiorari, vacate the judgment of the Ninth Circuit and remand 
to the Ninth Circuit for consideration, in light of Act 131, of 
petitioners’ claims for attorneys’ fees premised on the theory 
that their lawsuit was the catalyst for Act 131. In fact, the Ninth 


2 


Circuit was fully apprised of the pctential enactment of Act 131 
throughout the pendency of petitioners’ unsuccessful request for 
rehearing. 


The petition in this Court is premature because 
_ petitioners’ fee request is pending in the Ninth Circuit. 


The equities, including petitioners’ lobbying in support 
of Act 131, do not support vacating the judgment. 


The catalyst ¢laim is insubstantial both chronologically 
and on the merits. The Ninth Circuit rejected petitioners’ 
constitutional claims in toto three months before Act 131 was 
enacted. And, no court has awarded attorneys’ fees on a catalyst 
theory for a total, across-the-board litigation defeat. 


There is no basis in the procedural facts of this case for 
issuance of a GVR order, and there is particularly no basis for 
issuance of a GVR order when, as here, the decision below does 
not merit review. 


In order to eliminate their loss in the Ninth Circuit to 
pursue their claim for attorneys fees, petitioners ask this Court, 
if the GVR request is rejected, to grant plenary review of the 
merits of the Ninth Circuit decision to afford petitioners the — 
“alternative” relief of an order re-certifying as approved the 
1996 election result now certified as rejected. | 


The Ninth Circuit’s decision that there was no 
constitutional violation is correct and accords with the law of all 
other circuits on the narrow circumstances in which federal 
courts may take the drastic step, fraught with implications for 
federalism and state sovereignty, of intervening in state 

_ elections. The law lies evenly over the land. The application of 
the law to vastly differing fact situations of course results in 
differing outcomes from case to case--sometimes plaintiffs 
prevail, sometimes defendants--but the law is constant.

Moreover, the “alternative” relief of re-certification of 
the 1996 election results is not available to petitioners, because, 
under their own theory of having obtained the principal relief 
sought of a new election, re-certification relief would be 
cumulative and duplicative rather than alternative. In addition, 
the retrospective undoing by a federal court of a certified state 
election would violate the Eleventh Amendment and would 
r2quire, completely contrary to the decision in Yoshina of the 
Hawaii Supreme Court as the final arbiter of the Hawaii 
Constitution, that blank bailots not be counted as ballots cast 
upon the question. 


B. FACTS 
: The 1996 General Election 


Article XVII, Section 2 of the Hawaii Constitution 
provides two different methods for submitting to the electorate 
the question whether to convene a constitutional convention. 
Under the first, the Hawaii Legislature may submit the question 
‘as often as it pleases at any general or special election. Under the 
second, the constitutional convention question must be 
automatically submitted to the electorate no less pomeny Y than 
every tem years at a general election. 


The constitutional convention question was included in 
the November 5, 1996 general election as an automatic ten-year 
submission. -Of the total 369,357 ballots: 


163,869 were marked yes, 
160,153 were marked no, 
45,245 _ were left blank, and. 
90 were marked both yes and no 
(overvotes) _

The Hawaii Constitution provides that a proposed 
constitutional convention is approved if “a majority of the 
ballots cast upon such a question” are in the affirmative. (Haw. 
Const. art. XVII, § 2). 


In the thirty-seven years of Hawaii statehood preceding 
the 1996 election, no Hawaii court had ever interpreted or been 
asked to interpret this language. There had been no need to 
interpret it because the blank ba'lots were irrelevant to the 
outcome of the three prior votes {all of the automatic ten-year ° 
variety) in 1966, 1976, and 1986 on convening a constitutional 
convention. 


The 1996 vote was sufficiently close that the outcome 
turned on whether the constitutionally-required calculation of “‘a 
majority of the ballots cast upon/such a question” was based on 
only the yes or no ballots or whether the calculation included the 
blank ballots as well. 


The Chief Election Officer requested the opinion of the 
Hawaii Attorney General on how to calculate a majority under 
Article XVII, Section 2. The Attorney General’s response, not 
formed in an adversarial context, was that the blank ballots were 
not ballots cast upon the question and that the constitutional 
convention question had been approved. 


yB Hawaii Supreme Court | 


The Hawaii State AFL-CIO and others filed a timely 
original adversarial election contest against respondents in the 
Hawaii Supreme Court, requesting judgment that the 
constitutional convention question had been rejected. The 
Hawaii Supreme Court is “the ultimate judicial tribunal in the 
state” and has “final unreviewable authority to interpret and 
enforce the Hawaii Constitution.” State v. Fields, 686 P. 2d 1379, 
1390 (Haw. 1984) (citation omitted). 


7 


5 


The Hawaii Supreme Court held that the convention 
question had been rejected because a majority of ballots cast on 
the question--including the blank ballots--were not affirmative 
votes. Hawaii AFL-CIO v. Yoshina, 935 P.2d 89 (Haw. 1997) 
(Yoshina). Its opinion was based on: (1) the singular language of 
Article XVII, Section 2; (2) the intent of the framers of the 
Hawaii Constitution, based on the court’s diligent review of the 
proceedings of the 1950 constitutional convention; and (3) the 
purposeful distinction in Article XVII, Section 2 between the 
“majority of the ballots cast upon such a question” required for 
convening a convention and the “majority of all the votes tallied 
upon the question” required for approving convention-proposed 
amendments {emphases added). Yoshina, 935 P.2d at 91-98. 


The final sentence of Yoshina directed the Chief Election 
Officer to certify that the question had been rejected, and he 
immediately did so. 


a. United States District Court 


Petitioners filed their federal complaint three weeks after 
the Hawaii Supreme Court decided Yoshina. The primary theory 
of the complaint was that Yoshina was unforeseeable and 
rendered the election on the constitutional convention question 
fundamentally unfair in violation of the Due Process Clause of 
the Fourteenth Amendment to the United States Constitution. 


Petitioners requested from the court the alternative relief 
of either: (1) ordering respondents to certify, precisely contrary 
to the holding in Yoshina, that the voters had approved the 
constitutional convention question at the 1996 general election; 
or (2) ordering respondents to hold a special election to re-vote 
the precise question that the Hawaii Supreme Court held in 

Yoshina was rejected. 


6 


Respondents defended on the grounds that Yoshina was 
neither unforeseeable nor unfair. The Hawaii Supreme Court 
ruled on a question of first impression and interpreted 
constitutiona’ language that has no counterpart in Hawaii or 
elsewhere. Second, the past practice in Hawaii was to include 
blank ballots in the vote count. They were counted as cast as 
blank ballots, along with the other categories of yes, 20, and 
spoiled. 


There was no past practice concerning whether in 
calculating a “majority of the ballots case upon such a question,” 
the blank ballots should be assigned along with the separate no 
ballots to the denominator of ballots cast, as Yoshina held, or 
should just be disregarded as though they had never been cast, 
as petitioners contended. 


The sum total of “past practice” evidence preceding the 
1996 general election on the issue comprised the conflicting 
facts that: (1) in 1/966, voters were forewarned that blank ballots 
would be counted as ballots cast in the denominator of the 
calculation in the same manner as would the no votes (CR 22 
S.J. Opp’n Ex. B); and (2) in 1996, two business days before the 
election, the Office of Elections printed up but did not distribute 
its fact sheet stating that blank ballots would not be included in 
the calculation of a majority on the constitutional convention 
question (CR/ER 8 Ex. 6). 


There is no evidence during the thirty years between 
1966 and 1996 of any fact sheet or similar pronouncement by 
any State election official concerning the treatment of blank 
ballots in calculating the constitutionally-required majority for 
convening a constitutional convention.

(2) denied respondents’ motion, (3) enjoined the application of

4. Ninth Circuit Opinion 


Respondents appealed the judgment entered against 
them, and petitioners cross-appealed as to the relief granted. The 
Ninth Circuit stayed the special election ordered by the district 
court. On the merits, the Ninth Circuit found that the Hawaii 
Supreme Court’s decision in Yoshina, applying its “blank votes 
count as ballots cast” rule to the 1996 vote, did not violate 
petitioners’ substantive due process rights. The Ninth Circuit 
reversed the judgment in favor of petitioners, remanded with 
instructions to enter judgment in favor of respondents, and 
dismissed petitioners’ cross appeal seeking an order certifying 
as approved the rejected 1996 constitutional convention 
question. 


The Ninth Circuit opinion reflects a straightforward 
application of the law, uniform from circuit to circuit, that 
federal courts will not intervene in state elections in the absence 
of egregious wrongdoing.

2. GVR Could Not Reasonably Result In A 
Redetermination | 


GVR is not appropriate because the Ninth Circuit had 
full opportunity to consider petitioners’ claim of mootness based 
on Act 131. As petitioners themselves acknowledge, the Ninth 
Circuit panel “was told of the potential enactment o“ Act 131 on 
May 5, 1998 (Pet. at 7)” and was specifically advised of 
petitioners’ theory of mootness. Thus, the Ninth Circuit had 
fully seven weeks to consider the impact, if any, of enactment of 
Act 131 on their opinion. 


While the facts that the Ninth Circuit was fully apprised 
of and had ample time to consider the impact of Act 131 do not 
absolutely prohibit GVR, see Lawrence v. Chater, 516 U.S. at 
169-70, they strongly counsel against it. This Court’s “GVR 
power should be exercised sparingly.” Lawrence v. Chater, 516 
_US. at 173. There is no compelling reason to return this case to 
the Ninth Circuit for consideration of Act 131 when the Ninth 
Circuit was fully apprised of, and presumably considered, the 
potential enactment of Act 131 long before it denied rehearing.’ 


3. Petitioners’ Lawsuit Was Not the Catalyst for 
Act 131 


The driving force behind the petition for a writ of 
certiorari is the claim that petitioners’ lawsuit was the catalyst 
for enactment of Act 131, thereby transforming petitioners to 
prevailing parties and entitling them to attorneys’ fees and costs. 


*Furthermore, Act 131 was enacted into law on June 22, 1998, one 
day before the Ninth Circuit issued its denial of the petition for rehearing. 
Thus, not only did the Ninth Circuit have seven weeks to consider the impact 
of the potential enactment of Act 131, the Ninth Circuit denied the petition 
for rehearing «fter Act 131 was in fact enacted. 


12 


The catalyst claim does not justify granting the writ. 
First, petitioners did not obtain any injunctive relief or 
settlement or consent judgment or compzrable relief in their 
lawsuit. Exactly the opposite is true; petitioners lost across the 
board in the Ninth Circuit. They obtained nothing in their 
lawsuit. They owe costs. 


If anything, it was the complete failure of petitioners’ 
lawsuit that served as the catalyst for Act 131. To those Hawaii 
citizens in favor of convening a constitutional convention, Act 
131 would have been unnecessary if petitioners had succeeded 
in their federal lawsuit. Act 131 was “necessary” only because 
of petitioners’ complete litigation failure. Petitioners are 
illogically saying that they are entitled to attorneys fees because 

of the total failure of their lawsuit. 


Second, petitioners’ catalyst claim is chronologically 
unsustainable because Act 131 was enacted three months after 
the Ninth Circuit ruled against petitioners. 


Third, petitioners assume that the catalyst theory of fee 
recovery is still viable after Farrar v. Hobby, 506 U.S. 103 
(1992).* Even if true, that assumption does not detract from the 
fact that no circuit, including catalyst circuits, recognizes a 
totally adverse litigation outcome as a fee catalyst.

The role of the federal courts in state elections is 
exceedingly narrow. First, the correctness of the Hawaii 
Supreme Court in Yoshina is beyond the purview of this or any 
federal court. “Neither [the United States Supreme Court] nor 
any other federal tribunal has any authority to place a 
construction on a state statute different from the one rendered by 
the highest court of the state.” Johnson v. Fankell, 520 U.S. 911, 
__»117 S.Ct. 1800, 1804 (1997). 


Second, extreme caution is required when state election 
results are challenged. The federal appellate courts uniformly 
recognize that federal intervention in state elections “necessarily 
implicates important concerns of federalism and_ state 
sovereignty . . . . Indeed, the voiding of a state election is a 
“drastic if not staggering” remedy.” Soules v. Kauaians For 
Nukolii Campaign Comm., 849 F.2d 1176, 1180 (9th Cir. 1988) 
(citations omitted). Invalidation is “reserved for instances of 
willful or severe violations of established constitutional norms.” 
McMichael v._County of Napa, 709 F.2d 1268, 1273 (9th Cir. 
1983) (Kennedy J., concurring) (citing Griffin v. Burns, 570 
F.2d 1065, 1080 (1st Cir. 1978)). 


Third, the Ninth Circuit in this Case recognized and 
applied the distinction between “fundamental unfairness” and 
“garden variety election irregularities” (Pet. App. 16a-17a) that 
is uniformly recognized and applied by the circuit courts of 
appeals.’

Unattainable 


Not only is there no reason in the law for this Court to 
grant plenary review of the merits of the Ninth Circuit decision, 
but the purpose for which petitioners seek such review is 
unattainable and frivolous. 


Specifically, petitioners, who claim to have previously 
obtained the principal relief sought, now seek review of the 
merits for the purpose of obtaining the “alternate” relief sought 
of a federal court order re-certifying as approved the 1996 
constitutional convention question that stands certified as 
rejected. This retrospective re-certification is unavailable. First, 
the “alternate” relief is cumulative and duplicative to the 
“principal relief” that petitioners claim to have obtained from 
Act 131. Second, a re-certification order could only be based 
upon not counting the blank ballots as ballots cast upon the 
question, in direct violation of the decision of the Hawaii 
Supreme Court, the final arbiter of the Hawaii Constitution, that 
the blank ballots are counted as ballots cast upon the question.‘ 


“In contrast, if a federal court decided that the 1996 election on the 
constitutional convention question was fundamentally unfair and ordered a 
re-vote, the re-vote order would not violate Yoshina by consigning the 1996 
blank ballots to the “do not count” category. 


In Roe, the Eleventh Circuit made the unremarkable observation 
that the decision of the Alabama Supreme Court construing an Alabama 
Statute did not address the issue whether constitutional rights had been 
iniringed. 68 F.3d at 409. The Roe court did not address the question of the 
propriety of a remedy in contravention of the construction of the state 
supreme court.
