PETITIONERS’ SUPPLEMENTAL BRIEF 


Petitioners Citizens for a Constitutional Convention, 
et al., file this Supplemental Brief to call the Court’s 
attention to intervening matters not available at the time 
the Petition for Certiorari was filed, and the effect of 
these new matters on the proper disposition of this case. 


These intervening matters are: (a) the results of the 
November 3, 1998, general election vote upon whether 
there shall be a constitutional convention — the vote 
directed by Act 131, 1998 Haw. Sess. L.; and (b) the 
continued inaction by the Ninth Circuit upon Petitioners’ 
July 7, 1998, Motions (1) to Transfer Plaintiffs’ Claim for 
Attorneys’ Fees to the District Court, or, in the Alterna- 
tive, for Award of Attorneys’ Fees on Appeal, (2) to Recall 
the Mandate, and (3) to Vacate the Judgment and Remand 
With Instructions.?

SUPPLEMENTAL REASONS FOR 
GRANTING THE WRIT 


Although the results of the November 3, 1998, vote 
do not fully moot this case, they point out, in a critical, 
added way, why this case has been fundamentally 
. reshaped by Act 131, and why a GVR order, or, alter- 
natively, an order granting plenary review, should issue. 
The lower court’s complete inaction on Petitioners’ July 7, 
1998, motions also demonstrates forcefully why certiorari 
should be granted. | 


First, if they show nothing else, the results of the 
November 3, 1998, vote demonstrate just how important 
the advance knowledge of the supermajority rule 
announced in the Yoshina decision is to Hawaii voters - 
and would have been to those voters in 1996. Compared 
to the more than twelve percent who turned in blank 
ballots in 1996, less than seven percent of voters turned in 
blank ballots in 1998. Forced by the Yoshina rule to 
choose, Hawaii voters overwhelmingly voted “yes” or 
“no.” They were not “don’t care” voters, as disparagingly 
described by the court of appeals. This fact alone under- 
mines the rationale of the judgment below. 


Second, the fact that the 1998 vote occurred, without 
more, furthers the claim that Petitioners are, at a mini- 
mum, at least partially “prevailing parties” within the 
meaning of 42 U.S.C. § 1988. Any past argument that 
Petitioners’ claim for attorneys fees and costs was prema- 
ture has been overtaken by the November vote itself. 
Petitioners prayed alternatively in the District Court for a 
new vote, and that vote has now occurred. Though the

makes painfully obvious, unless this Court grants the

refusal to decide those important claims, which have
