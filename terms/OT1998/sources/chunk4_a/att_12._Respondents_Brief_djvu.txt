QUESTION PRESENTED 


Whether the Federal Communications Commission (FCC) 
may preempt the State public service commissions’ dis- 
cretion to implement key provisions of the Communica- 
tions Act of 1934, as amended by the Telecommunications 
Act of 1996 (the Act). Specifically, did Congress intend 
the FCC to direct how the States set prices for local 
telephone services and facilities in those instances where 
incumbent local providers and competitors are unable to 
agree upon prices.

STATEMENT OF THE CASE 


The statute at issue is the Communications Act of 
1934, as amended by the Telecommunications Act of 1996. 
fhe controversy surrounds the Federal Communications 
Commission action preempting the State public service 
commissions’ discretion to implement key provisions of 
the Act, including the right to set prices for the services 
and facilities necessary to bring competition to the local 
market. 


The telephone network is used predominantly for 
calls that originate and terminate in the same state (intra- 
state calls). Because this network also carries calls that 
cross state boundaries, Congress chose to provide for 
federal regulation of interstate transactions. However, the 
1934 legislation, and the more recent amendments, con- 
tinue to protect States’ police power authority over intra- 
state calls through an express reservation in section 2(b) 
of the Communications Act, 47 U.S.C. § 152(b). This 
Court has strictly enforced this jurisdictional reservation 
of authority finding section 152(b) acts as both a substan- 
tive jurisdictional limitation on the FCC’s authority and 
also a rule of statutory construction. See, ¢.g., Louisiana 
Pub. Serv. Comm'n v. FCC, 476 U.S. 355 (1986). 


Congress, in the 1996 amendments to the Act, speci- 
fically chose to preserve this historic reservation of State 
authority. The 1996 legislation provides a framework to 


open all local (intrastate) telephone markets to competi- 
tion. This framework + specifically designed to eliminate 
regulatory barriers tc tocal phone competition, subject to 
competitively neutral protection of public welfare and 
consumer interests by the States. It also assigns a broad 
range of new responsibilities to incumbent and competi- 
tive telecommunications carriers, the FCC and the States. 


The Act contemplates that incumbent local (intra- 
state) phone service providers will privately negotiate 
agreements to interconnect with other competitors. These 
negotiations are targeted at assuring competitors are able 
to compete in the local service market. The agreements 
include prices that incumbents charge for resale of local 
service and “leasing” arrangements.? Only if negotiations 
fail will government regulation play a role. When agree- 
ment cannot be reached, the Act, in section 252, specifies 
pricing standards and other requirements for arbitration. 
Section 252 expressly directs State commissions to set 
prices and arbitrate according to those requirements. 
Only in the instance that a State fails to take action does 
the Act authorize the FCC to fulfill the State role of 
setting prices and arbitrating disputes in local competi- 
tion matters. In spite of this clear statutory mandate, the 
FCC action, reversed by the Eighth Circuit decision under

A. The Regulation of Telephone Service Prior to 
1996 


Although the first telephone patent was issued in 
1876, telephone service was virtually unregulated until 
the Twentieth Century. “State of New York Investigation of 
Telephone and Telegraph Companies — Proceeding of Joint 
Committee,” Vol. 1 (1910). The need for regulation became 
apparent in the early 1900s as the number of telephone 
service providers mushroomed and numerous problems 
arose. State legislatures throughout the nation established 
public service commissions to regulate the provision of 
telephone service. The Regulation of Interstate Telephone 
Rates, 51 Harv. L. Rev. 846, 847 (1938). 


The Mann-Elkins Act of June 18, 1910, extended the 
jurisdiction of the Interstate Commerce Commission 
(ICC) to interstate telephone service, but the ICC had 
scant resources and recognized that over 90 percent of all 
telephone calls were local in nature, a fact that remains true 
today.» Finding “[t}he great bulk of telephone business is 
of strictly local concern, and the State commissions are 
much better informed and equipped than we are to pass

B. The Telecommunications Act of 1996 


In February 1996, Congress passed the Telecommuni- 
cations Act of 1996. Pub. L. No. 104-104, 1996 
U.S.C.C.A.N. (110 Stat.) 56, codified at 47 U.S.C. § 151, et 
seq. (1996). This amendment to the 1934 Act adopts a 
framework to open all local telecommunications markets 
to competition and eliminates regulatory barriers to com- 
petition in the telecommunications industry, subject to 
the States’ competitively neutral protection of public wel- 
fare and consumer interests. A critical component of the 
Congressional scheme is found in sections 251 and 252 of 
the Act. These sections explain how Congress expects 
new entrants to interconnect with the incumbent local 
phone service providers. 


In lieu of active regulation, Congress sought to rely 
primarily on private negotiations between competitors to 
resolve the broad range of technical and pricing issues 
associated with interconnection and access to the facili- 
ties and services of the incumbent provider, the local 
exchange carrier. 


The 1996 Act sets forth a specific set of local 
exchange carrier duties. With great particularity, Con- 
gress identified those functions, features and services that 
incumbent local exchange carriers must make available to 
competing local exchange carriers, as well as the mutual 
duties of the carriers. See 47 U.S.C. §§ 251(b) and 251(c).

In the event private negotiations are unsuccessful,

Section 251(d)(1) does not even imply, much less

stopped nor delayed competition. It is the function of the
