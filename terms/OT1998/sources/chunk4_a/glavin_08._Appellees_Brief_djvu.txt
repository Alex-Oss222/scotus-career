STATEMENT OF THE CASE 


An inaccurate census is a boon to the plaintiff-appellees. 
For the last eight years, they have enjoyed the windfall 
produced by flawed 1990 census data: enhanced voting 
power and a more than fair share of federal funds. The 
plaintiff-appellees received this windfall because they reside 
in areas immune to the invidious problem of differential 
undercounting, namely, the predominately white, affluent, 
suburban regions of our country. And like all unjust 
enrichment, this windfall comes at the expense of others -- 
minorities, immigrants, children and the rural poor, the 


2 


people least able to afford vote dilution or the diminution of 
federal assistance. 


Conversely, an accurate census will cause grave 
"injury" to the plaintiff-appellees, or so they contend. 
Because statistical sampling would reduce or eliminate 
differential undercounting, the plaintiff-appellees would no 
longer receive an inequitable share of federal funds or 
greater representational value for each vote cast. In other 
words, their "injury" flows from being deprived of their 
windfall, and they are unwilling to relinquish those advantag- 
es. Such an incredible claim stands the notions of justice 
and redressable injury on their heads, and should not be 
countenanced by this Court. 


The Census Bureau’s plan for conducting the 2000 
census, which includes the use of statistical sampling in 
conjunction with traditional data collection techniques, will 
result in the most accurate and cost-effective census ever 
taken. See The United States Department of Commerce, 
Bureau of Census, Report to Congress -- The Plan for 
Census 2000, ix (revised and reissued August 1997). 
Reapportionment and redistricting based on census data 
adjusted using statistical sampling would more closely 
approach the constitutional objective of one-person, one- 
vote. And census data adjusted by statistical sampling also 
would facilitate a more even-handed distribution of federal 
funds to local government entities. Neither the Census Act 
nor the Constitution precludes the use of statistical sampling 
for the purpose of Congressional reapportionment, and 
Section 195 of the Census Act actually requires its use for all 
other purposes -- such as redistricting and allocation of 


3 


federal funds -- where, as here, the Secretary of Commerce 
deems it feasible. 


The plaintiff-appellees essentially claim an entitlement 
to the advantages they have enjoyed in terms of political 
power and federal funding. They, like all Americans, 
however, are only entitled to equal representation and an 
equitable distribution of federal funds, both of which depend 
upon accurate census data. 


SUMMARY OF ARGUMENT 


This case has been consolidated for oral argument with 
the United States Department of Commerce, et al. v. United 
States House of Representatives, et al., No. 98-404. On the 
merits, the legal issues raised by the two cases are virtually 
identical. These issues were addressed in the Brief of 
Appellees City of Los Angeles, et al. in Support of Appel- 
lants, filed in No. 98-404 on October 6, 1998, and need not 
be repeated here. The lone exception is plaintiff-appellees’ 
contention below that the phrase "counting the whole 
number" in Section 2 of the Fourteenth Amendment requires 
census takers to engage in a “head count," and prohibits the 
use of statistical sampling. That contention is nonsense. 
The quoted passage refers to a rule of apportionment, not a 
mode of gathering census data. 


4 
ARGUMENT 


Section 2 of The Fourteenth Amendment Does Not 
Prohibit The Use of Statistical Sampling For The Purpose 


of Apportionment 

In the district court, the plaintiff-appellees argued that 
the phrase “counting the whole number of persons” in 
Section 2 of the Fourteenth Amendment constitutes a 
"constitutional mandate that population be determined by 
means of an actual head count." Plaintiffs’ Reply Brief in 
Support of their Motion for Summary Judgment at 27. The 
district court did not reach this -- or any other -- constitu- 
tional issue. And, in any event, plaintiff-appellees’ argument 
has no support in either the text or the drafting history of the 
Fourteenth Amendment. 


Ratification of the Thirteenth Amendment abolished the 
ignominious “three-fifths rule” embodied in article I, Section 
2 of the Constitution: 


Representatives and direct Taxes shall be appor- 
tioned among the several States which may be 
included within this Union, according to their 
respective Numbers, which shall be determined by 
adding to the whole Number of free Persons, 
including those bound to Service for a Term of 
Years, and excluding Indians not taxed, three fifths 
of all other Persons. 


U.S. Const., art. I, § 2, cl. 3 (emphasis added). 


Because they were no longer slaves, the freedmen 
moved from the category “all other Persons” to the "whole 
Number of free Persons." However, because the Thirteenth 
Amendment had not guaranteed freedmen the vote, the ironic 


5 


result was to increase the political power of white Southern- 
ers. See William E. Nelson, The Fourteenth Amendment: 
From Political Principle to Judicial Doctrine, 40-43 (1998). 


The purpose of Section 2 of the Fourteenth Amendment 
was to adjust the rule of apportionment. The first part of 
Section 2 reflects the emancipation of ihe slaves, and the 
elimination of the three-fifths rule, by providing that: 


Representatives shall be apportioned among the 
several States according to their respective num- 
bers, counting the whole number of persons in each 
State, excluding Indians not taxed. 


U.S. Const., amend. XIV, § 2. 


And, as reflected in the balance of Section 2, the 
framers of the Fourteenth Amendment selected a rule of 
apportionment that would reduce Southern representation in 
Congress to the extent the freedmen were systematically 
denied the right to vote: 


But when the right to vote at any election for the 
choice of electors for President and Vice President 
of the United States, Representatives in Congress, 
the Executive and Judicial officers of a State, or the 
members of the Legislature thereof, is denied to 
any of the male inhabitants of such State, being 
twenty-one years of age, and citizens of the United 
States, or in any way abridged, except for partici- 
pation in rebellion or other crime, the basis of 
representation \therein shall be reduced in the 
proportion which the number of male citizens shall 
bear to the whole number of male citizens twenty- 
one years of age in such State. 


Id. 


The phrase “counting the whole number of persons" 
simply means that every inhabitant (with the exception of 
"Indians not taxed"), including the former slaves, shall be 
counted as a whole person -- not three-fifths of a person -- 


for the purpose of apportionment. 


Plaintiffs’ contention that the phrase demonstrates that 
the framers of the Fourteenth Amendment were exclusively 
committed to conducting the census through the use of a 
"head count" is wholly unsupported by the records of the 
debates of the first session of the Thirty-Ninth Congress that 
drafted and debated the Fourteenth Amendment. As was 
also true with the debates among the framers of the Census 
Clause in 1787, these debates were concerned solely with the 
substantive rule of apportionment, not the mode of gathering 
census data. 


CONCLUSION 


For all the reasons stated above, the Court should 
reverse.
