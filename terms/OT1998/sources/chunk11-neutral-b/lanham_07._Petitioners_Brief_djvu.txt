QUESTIONS PRESENTED 


1. Did the United States Congress have the power 
under Section 5 of the Fourteenth Amendment to enact that 
portion of the Trademark Remedy Clarification Act of 1992, 
Pub. L. No. 102-542, 106 Stat. 3567 (1992) (the “TRCA”), 
which abrogated the states’ Eleventh Amendment immunity for 
unfair competition suits alleging that a state engaged in false 
and misleading advertising of its product in violation of 
Section 43(a) of the Lanham Act ? 


2. Did Florida Prepaid Postsecondary Education 
Expense Board waive its Eleventh Amendment immunity 
under the constructive consent doctrine set forth by this Court 
in Parden v. Terminal Ry. of Ala. State Docks Dep't, 377 U.S. 
184 (1964), by continuing to engage in the interstate marketing 
of its products after Congress’ enactment of the TRCA by 
which Congress made it unmistakably clear that the states 


would be subject to suit in federal court for cases arising from 
violations of Section 43(a) of the Lanham Act?

STATEMENT OF THE CASE 


A. THE LANHAM ACT 


This case involves College Savings Bank’s (“CSB”) 
contention that the Florida Prepaid Postsecondary Expense 
Board (“Florida Prepaid”), has engaged in false advertising in 
violation of Section 43(a) of the Lanham Act. Section 43(a) 
provides in part that persons subject to the Act shall be liable 
for damages caused by a competitor's misrepresentation of “the 
nature, characteristics, [or] qualities” of that person’s goods or 
services. 15 U.S.C. §1125(a)(1). The pertinent portions of 


Section 43(a) are: 


(1) Any person who...uses in commerce any 
word, term, name, symbol, or device, or any 
combination thereof, or any . . . false or 
misleading description of fact, or false or 
misleading representation of fact, which— 


* * * 


(B) in commercial advertising 
or promotion, misrepresents 
the nature, characteristics, 
qualities, ... of his... goods, 
services or commercial 
activities, 


shall be liable in a civil action by any person 
who believes that he or she is or is likely to be 


damaged by such act. 


The congressional purpose in enacting Section 43(a) is 
explained in Section 45. 15 U.S.C. § 1127. As here pertinent 


the section states: 


3 


The intent of this chapter is to regulate 
commerce within the control of Congress... to 
protect persons engaged in such commerce 
against unfair competition . .. . 


15 U.S.C. §1127. 


: In enacting the Lanham Act, Congress recognized that 
“[t}here is no essential difference between trade-mark 
infringement and what is loosely called unfair competition. 
Unfair competition is the genus of which trade-mark 
infringement is one of the species .. .. All trade-mark cases 
and cases of unfair competition involve the same legal wrong.” 
S. Rep. No. 1333, 79th Cong. 2d Sess. ( 1946), reprinted in 
1946 U.S.C.C.A.N. 1274, 1275. 


B. po TRADEMARK REMEDY CLARIFICATION 


In 1992, Congress adopted the Trademark Remedy 
Clarification Act, Pub. L. No. 102-542, 106 Stat. 3567 (1992) 
(“TRCA”). The congressional purpose was to ensure that the 
States would be held liable for violations of the Act in the same 
manner as any other entity. Accordingly, the amendments 
included a specific abrogation of the Eleventh Amendment 
immunity of the states: 


Any State, instrumentality of a State or any 
officer or employee of a State or 
instrumentality of a State acting in his or her 
official capacity, shall not be immune, under 
the Eleventh Amendment of the Constitution 
of the United States or under any other 
doctrine of sovereign immunity, from suit in 
Federal Court by any person, . . . for any 
violation under this chapter. 


aes se se ee eS ee 


15 U.S.C. § 1122(a). 


As a part of the TRCA, Congress adopted other 
conforming amendments to the Lanham Act to ensure that the 
states would thereafter be held responsible for violations of the 
Act. Those included amending Section 43(a), 15 U.S.C. 
§ 1125(a), to expand the definition of “person” to include the 
states: 


As used in this subsection the term “any 
person” includes any State, instrumentality of 
a State or employee of a State or 
instrumentality of a State acting in his or her 
official capacity. Any State, and any such 
instrumentality, officer, or employee, shall be 
subject to the provisions of this chapter in the 
same manner and to the same extent as any 
nongovernmental entity. 


15 U.S.C. § 1125(a)(2). 


Section 1127 of Title 15, which sets forth the 
construction, definitions and intent of the chapter also was 
amended to provide that the term “person” includes any state or 
instrumentality of a state. 15 U.S.C. § 1127. 


The legislative history of the TRCA reinforces the fact 
that the sole purpose of the Act was to demonstrate Congress’ 
intent that “States and State entities” would not be immune 
from suits under the Lanham Act. S. Rep. 102-280, 102d 
Cong., Ist Sess. at 2 (1992), reprinted in 1992 U.S.C.C.A.N. 
3087. Congress enacted the TRCA to satisfy this Court's 
requirement that the congressional intent to permit the states to 
be brought into federal court be made explicit in the language 
of the statute itself. See S. Rep. 102-280, Jd. at 4-6, discussing 
the relationship of the TRCA to Afascadero State Hosp. v. 
Scanlin, 473 U.S. 334 (1985) (pertaining to the requirements 


5 


that must be met by Congress in exercising its powers under 
Section 5 of the Fourteenth Amendment), and Welch v. Texas 
Dep't of Highways & Pub. Transp., 483 U.S. 468 (1987) 
(pertaining to the requirements that must be met by Congress to 


apply Parden v. Terminal Ry. of Ala. Docks Dep't, 377 US. 
184 (1964)). 


The Senate Report states that the statutory language 
was deliberately adopted to provide “the necessary clarity to 
ensure that Congress intended to override the Eleventh 
Amendment as an appropriate way to enforce the provisions of 
the Fourteenth Amendment.” S. Rep. 102-280, Jd. at 4, 5 
(footnote omitted). The Report also states that the provisions 
of the TRCA are “justified under . . . the Fourteenth 
Amendment.” /d. at 8. The report concludes: “Based on 
previous court decisions, §. 758 [TRCA] and S. 759 will 
constitutionally abrogate State Sovereign Immunity, providing 
a uniform system of protection.” Jd. at 11.

provides in relevant part that the Congress shall have power: 


To regulate Commerce with foreign Nations, 
and among the several States . . . 


The Fourteenth Amendment provides in relevant part 
that no State shall: 


Section | [Djeprive any person of life, liberty or

The Eleventh Amendment provides: 


The Judicial power of the United States shall 
not be construed to extend to any suit in law 
or equity, commenced or prosecuted against 
one of the United States by citizens of another 
State or by citizens or subjects of any Foreign 
State. 


There are two constitutional issues involved in this 
case. First, petitioner CSB maintains that the Court of Appeals 
erred by concluding that Congress lacked the power under 
Section 5 of the Fourteenth Amendment to enact the TRCA 
making the states subject to suit for false advertising under 
Section 43(a) of the Lanham Act. . 


Second, the Court of Appeals erred in concluding that 
the activities of Florida Prepaid are beyond the reach of the 
Parden doctrine. CSB maintains the Court of Appeals applied 
the wrong test. The Court of Appeals has confused the 
difference between education, as an important state function, 
and the entry by the states into the commercial financial arena 
by offering investment vehicles to assist private citizens in 
meeting the costs of a college education. Under the broad 
doctrine enunciated by the Court of Appeals, virtually any 
activity that could be undertaken by a state would be beyond 
the reach of the Parden doctrine.

SUMMARY OF ARGUMENT 


Article 1, Section 8 of the Constitution provides 
Congress with a wide array of powers including the power to 
“regulate commerce . . . among the several states... .” In 
Seminole Tribe, this Court held that Article 1 does not provide 
Congress with the power to abrogate the immunity of the states 
provided for by the Eleventh Amendment. This holding was 
principally based upon the fact that the Eleventh Amendment 
was adopted subsequent to Article | and therefore served to 
limit the power of Congress under Article I. 


In Seminole Tribe, this Court also recognized that 
Congress does have power under Section 5 of the Fourteenth 
Amendment to enforce its provisions by appropriate legislation, 
including the right to abrogate the Eleventh Amendment 
immunity of the states. 


Pursuant to its Article | powers, Congress enacted the 
Lanham Act providing an amalgam of protections for 
businesses, including the right to seek damages resulting from a 
competitor's false advertising of its products and services. In 
1992, Congress enacted the TRCA, which by its explicit terms 
serves to abrogate the Eleventh Amendment immunity of the 
States and to treat the states as “persons” in the same manner as 
all other entities made subject to the Lanham Act. The 
legislative history of the TRCA makes it clear that the statutory 
provisions were designed specifically to comply with this 
Court’s requirement that abrogation of the Eleventh 
Amendment immunity must be unmistakably clear in the 
language of the statute itself. Congress also made it clear that 


10 


the language of the TRCA was designed to meet this Court's 
requirement of explicit language for the Parden doctrine to 


apply. 


CSB maintains in answer to the first Constitutional 
issue raised by this petition that the TRCA’s abrogation of the 
states’ immunity under the Eleventh Amendment, as it applies 
to the false advertising of a competitor's own products and 
services, is a constitutional exercise of Congress’ Section 5 
Fourteenth Amendment power. The position of Florida 
Prepaid is that, under Seminole Tribe, no property or liberty 
rights created by Congress pursuant to Article I of the 
Constitution can be made subyect to Congress’ Section 5 
Fourteenth Amendment enforcement powers; and therefore, the 
TRCA is unconstitutional. That, CSB maintains, represents a 
fundamental misreading of this Court’s decision in Seminole 
Tribe. Seminole Tribe did not involve any liberty or property 
rights. Therefore, it offers no authority for the proposition that 
Congress’ Section 5 powers cannot be used to enforce against 
the states the guarantees of Section | of the Fourteenth 
Amendment with respect to any and all property rights, 
including those which Congress has created under its Article | 
powers. 


The district court in this case implicitly recognized that 
property rights created by Congress pursuant to Article | are 
subject to Congress’ Section 5 Fourteenth Amendment power 
by upholding the constitutional validity of Congress 1992 
legislation abrogating the immunity of the states for the 
purpose of enforcing patents.’ 


’ Patent and Plant Variety Protection Remedy Clarification Act, Pub. 
L. No. 102-560, 106 Stat. 4230 (1992). See College Savings Bank v. 
Florida Prepaid Postsecondary Educ. Exp. Bd., 948 F. Supp. 400 
(D.N.J. 1996). 


Both the district court and the Court of Appeals 
expressed the fear that a finding that the TRCA is 
constitutional with respect to the issues in this case would serve 
to undermine this Court’s holding in Seminole Tribe. They did 
so with no explicit discussion of the relationship between 
Congress’ Article | and Section 5, Fourteenth Amendment 
powers. 


Both the district court and the Court of Appeals failed 
to recognize that “property” is not created by the Constitution. 
Rather, property has been created by the common law and the

Similar to the district court, the Court of Appeals failed

The Court of Appeals found that the language of the

The essence of the property rights protected by

The Third Circuit’s concerns are misplaced, and its

4. The Cases Relied Upon By Tne Court Of

The Court of Appeals placed principal reliance for its

The Court of Appeals’ reliance on Paul v. Davis, 424

A. THE PARDEN DOCTRINE REMAINS VALID

The Court of Appeals « orrectly declined to affirm the

More importantly, CSB contends that Parden should

of the Eleventh Amendment was to protect the states from

their governmental capacity. There is no diminishment of the 
Eleventh Amendment by applying the Parden doctrine

clear that states will be deemed to have waived their immunity

compete with the private sector.

C. FLORIDA PREPAID’S ACTIVITIES ARE NOT 
BEYOND THE REACH OF PARDEN. 


The Court of Appeals erred in finding that Florida 
Prepaid, by offering investment vehicles, was engaged in 
education as a core function of state government. The finding 
confuses “education” with financial investments used to meet 
the costs of college, clearly a private, not a governmental 
function. 


Florida Prepaid, a “body corporate”, Fla. Stat. § 
240.551(5), J.A. 48, of the State of Florida, was created to 
manage the prepaid expense program with the power to engage 
in commercial transactions with the public and the power to

49. Florida Prepaid’s product is marketed outside of the State

Florida Prepaid is authorized to collect administrative fees and 
penalties for delinquent payments. Jd. § 240.554(5)(c)(13), 
J.A. at 50. Florida Prepaid controls the price of its contracts. 
As of June 1995, it had assets of almost $1.5 billion and a 
surplus of $184 million. College Savings Bank, 948 F. Supp. at 
410, Pet. App. at 49a. Florida Prepaid’s earnings cannot be 
used by the State of Florida. Fla. Stat. § 240.551(10), J.A. at 
66-67.

In Parden, this Court found that Congress had the 
power to enact legislation governing conduct in areas not 
committed exclusively to the public domain and to deem a state

immunity if, after passage of the legislation, a state engaged in

leaves the sphere that is exclusively its own and enters into 
activities subject to Congressional regulation, it subjects itself 
to that regulation as fully as if it were a private person or

Providing investment instruments to assist in meeting 
the cost of college is neither a sphere committed exclusively to 
the states nor a core governmental function, and is therefore 
within the scope of Parden. The Court of Appeals, however, 
concluded that the Parden test was changed by this Court’s 
1973 decision in Employees. The Court of Appeals interpreted 
Employees as modifying Parden by creating an “important 
government function exception to Parden.” College Savings 
Bank, 131 F.3d at 362, Pet. App. at 19a. The Court of Appeals 
concluded “a state cannot be deemed to have waived its 
immunity if it is engaged in an important or core government 
function.” /d., at 131 F.3d 363, Pet. App. 19a. 


Employees was decided prior to this Court’s 1987 
decision in Welch, in which this Court stated that the 
application of the Parden doctrine “required an unequivocal 
expression that Congress intended to override Eleventh 
Amendment immunity.” Welch, 483 U.S. at 478. Employees 
was a forerunner of the Welch decision, relative to the needed 
clarity of the intention of Congress. In Employees, this Court 
concluded that “Congress desired to bring under the Act 
employees of hospitals and related institutions.” Employees, 
411 U.S. at 283. The significant finding by the Court, 
however, was that there was “not a word in the history of the 
1966 Amendments to indicate a purpose of Congress to make it 
possible for the citizens of that State or another State to sue the 
State in the federal courts.” Jd at 285. Because of the 
sweeping implications of concluding that Congress intended to 
impose a constructive waiver, the Court held that “we cannot 
conclude that Congress conditioned the operation of these 
facilities on the forfeiture of immunity from suit in a federal 
forum.” Jd. 


In summary, Employees does not stand for the 
proposition that this Court has changed the basic test as set 
forth in Parden, i.e., that the Congress has authority to impose

a constructive waiver “when a State leaves the sphere that is 
exclusively its own and enters into activities subject to 
congressional regulation ... .” Parden, 377 U.S. at 196 
Rather, Employees is a forerunner of this Court’s decision that 
Congress must clearly express its intent for the Parden doctrine 


to apply. 


The Court of Appeals’ discussion of the overall 
importance of education and the importance of education to the 
states is immaterial to the issue before this Court. College

Court of Appeals has confused the distinction between the role 
of the states in providing educational institutions with the quite 
different function of providing investment vehicles for assisting 
parents and others to meet the costs of college. That certainly 
is not a sphere of authority exclusive to the states, nor indeed, a 
core function of the states. See Genentech, Inc. v. University of 
California, 143 F.3d 1446, 1453 (Fed. Cir. 1998), petition for

The Court of Appeals’ interpretation would deprive the 
Parden doctrine of meaning, since virtually any activity
